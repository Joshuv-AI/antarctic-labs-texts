PCK1 and ALDH1A1 are identified as biomarkers for inherent drug resistance in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Wei Chen, Cheng Zhou, Gao Liu, Xinyu Wang, Yong Yi, Yang Xu, ShuangJian Qiu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a major cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, often diagnosed at advanced stages with limited treatment options. Despite advancements in systemic therapies, including the use of lenvatinib, the survival rate for advanced HCC remains low due to drug resistance and tumor heterogeneity.MethodThis study employed single-cell sequencing and spatial transcriptomics to investigate intra-tumor heterogeneity and identify subpopulations of malignant cells with inherent drug resistance. Tumor samples were obtained from patients undergoing lenvatinib, and differential analysis was performed to identify biomarkers associated with drug resistance.ResultsAnalysis revealed significant differences in treatment response between different HCC nodules within the same patient, indicating intra-tumor heterogeneity. Single-cell sequencing and spatial transcriptomics identified distinct cell clusters within tumors, with specific subpopulations showing inherent resistance to lenvatinib. Differential analysis identified 17 common upregulated genes, including PCK1 and ALDH1A1, which were significantly associated with drug resistance. Pseudotime analysis further supported the role of PCK1 and ALDH1A1 as potential biomarkers of inherent drug resistance in HCC. This study highlights the importance of tumor heterogeneity and identifying biomarkers associated with drug resistance in HCC.ConclusionsThe findings suggest that PCK1 and ALDH1A1 may serve as potential biomarkers for predicting treatment response and guiding therapeutic strategies in HCC patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03481-4.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the third leading cause of cancer mortality globally [1]. One noteworthy universal trait of HCC is the advanced tumor stages at diagnosis, with about 70% of patients not amenable to radical treatment [2]. Palliative treatment strategies, including transarterial chemoembolization, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy, have limited efficacy, with an overall 5-year survival rate of only 12.1% [3]. Despite recent curative improvements in HCC therapy, the survival rate of HCC remains dismal [4].

Since sorafenib became the first systemic treatment for HCC in 2007, a series of targeted therapies, including lenvatinib, donafenib, regorafenib, and apatinib, have been employed in the treatment of advanced HCC. Tumor immunotherapy, especially anti-PD1 antibodies, is emerging as a viable treatment. However, substantial clinical research has indicated that systemic therapies either exhibit high rates of drug resistance or demonstrate limited efficacy [5]. From real-world studies, it is observed that the efficacy of systemic therapy for HCC is limited, with less than half of the patients benefiting from treatment and an overall survival extension of less than six months [6]. Undoubtedly, lenvatinib stands out as one of the most pivotal and prominent drugs in the current systemic treatment for HCC. As of February 2024, there are a total of 56 ongoing Phase III clinical trials registered on clinicaltrials.gov for hepatocellular carcinoma, with 26 studies focusing on lenvatinib. Nevertheless, according to the modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (mRECIST) criteria, 72% of HCC patients receiving lenvatinib treatment failed to achieve an objective response [7]. In a real-world study involving 378 individuals, approximately 80.4% of HCC patients showed tumor stabilization or progression following lenvatinib treatment [8]. Furthermore, multiple clinical studies have demonstrated that over 70% of HCC patients did not significantly benefit from lenvatinib treatment [9, 10]. Nevertheless, lenvatinib currently stands as the preferred therapeutic approach for advanced HCC. Systemic treatments, however, only elicit a response in a subset of patients, with the majority demonstrating inherent resistance. Ongoing research has unveiled various mechanisms contributing to drug resistance in liver cancer, encompassing immune microenvironment remodeling [11], metabolic reprogramming [12], anti-apoptosis [13], and enhanced stem cell properties [14]. Presently, investigations predominantly focus on understanding the mechanisms of lenvatinib resistance, yet there is a notable absence of research on potential biomarkers for HCC resistance. Identifying such biomarkers could aid in promptly discontinuing futile treatments for patients, facilitating the transition to more effective therapeutic strategies. This sequential approach involving different drugs holds the potential to enhance long-term survival for liver cancer patients and mitigate adverse reactions associated with targeted therapies.

The drug resistance and heterogeneity of tumors are closely related. In clinical practice, differences in drug resistance are observed not only between patients but also within multiple lesions or different regions within a single tumor in the same patient. This phenomenon is attributed to the high degree of heterogeneity within HCC [15]. HCC is a metabolically driven tumor, and the high heterogeneity of the tumor manifests in different modes of metabolic reprogramming. It has been found that glycolysis, fatty acid metabolism, and bile acid metabolism show significant changes in tumors and determine tumor classification and prognosis [16]. By utilizing higher-resolution sequencing techniques, such as single-cell sequencing, we can analyze the differences between tumor cells more accurately and identify subpopulations of tumor cells that possess drug resistance.

In this study, we utilized single-cell sequencing and spatial transcriptomics to uncover the homogeneity and heterogeneity within multiple lesions of HCC. By examining the differential responses to systemic therapy, we aimed to identify subpopulations of cells with inherent drug resistance. Through differential analysis and pseudotemporal analysis, we sought to identify biomarkers of inherent drug resistance in HCC.

Materials and methods

Patients

This study was approved by the institutional ethics committee of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (B2022-401). Written informed consent was obtained before surgery or pharmacological treatment. The dose of lenvatinib was 8 mg/day (weight < 60 kg) or 12 mg/day (weight ≥ 60 kg). The dose of apatinib was 850 mg/day. The anti-PD1 antibody administration protocol was applied according to the manufacturer’s instructions (once every 21 days). Lenvatinib or apatinib was discontinued one week before surgery, while the anti-PD1 antibody was discontinued one month before surgery. Two distinct cohorts of 203 HCC patients were included in this study. Fifty-two patients received treatment with lenvatinib or apatinib plus an anti-PD1 antibody before surgery (cohort 1, n = 52). Paraffin-embedded tissues from cohort 2 were randomly obtained from HCC patients who underwent curative resection from April 2012 to December 2012 at the Liver Cancer Institute, Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (cohort 2, n = 173). Patients in cohort 2 were followed up for eight years after surgery. In the preoperative treatment with lenvatinib, two tumors in patient P131 exhibited a marked differential response; thus, these two tumors were used in subsequent high-throughput sequencing.

Sample Preparation and sequencing

Tumor samples were washed with PBS immediately after resection and photographed to record the precise site of tissue sampling. The detailed process of sample preparation for single-cell sequencing was the same as in our previous study [17]. The samples for spatial transcriptomics were harvested from the junction of cancer tissue and adjacent carcinoma tissue. Samples were treated with a frozen embedding agent (Invitrogen) and stored at −80 °C in a freezer. Spatial transcriptomics was performed using the Visium Spatial Gene Expression platform (10X Genomics) by Shanghai Crystal Energy Biochip Co., Ltd. The permeabilization step lasted approximately 15 min. The samples were quantified, and their quality was tested using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer.

Bioinformatics analyses

The process of data quality control and analysis was the same as described in our previous study [17] and is not repeated here. The spot-filtering parameters for spatial sequencing were 200 < feature RNA < 4000. Data analysis was performed using R software (version 4.2.1). Genes with a Bonferroni-adjusted p-value (p_val_adj) < 0.05, fold change > 1.5, and CT > 0.25 were considered relevant. Parts of the statistical analysis and biplots were generated using GraphPad Prism 9.0. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed for dimensionality reduction, followed by clustering using the Louvain algorithm. The Harmony package was applied to remove batch effects across different samples [18]. The clustering results were visualized using the UMAP algorithm. CopyKAT was used to predict malignant cells [19]. In the dimensionality reduction analysis of malignant cells, to avoid confusion, we designated the clustering results of single-cell sequencing as “sc-cluster X” and the clustering results of spatial transcriptomics as “sp-cluster X.” Subclusters with proportions below 0.05 in the sample and fourfold lower than their corresponding proportions were deemed non-representative of the sample’s characteristics.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Tissues were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C overnight after washing with PBS. After dehydration, permeabilization, wax dipping, and embedding, 5 μm sections were cut. The slices were processed in the following order: dewaxing, hydration, antigen retrieval, and BSA blocking. Afterward, slices were incubated with primary antibodies (PCK1 (Abclone, A2036), 1:200; ALDH1A1 (Abclone, A22351), 1:200) overnight, and secondary antibodies were added for 1 h. Finally, the chromogenic substrate tetramethylbenzidine was added for color development. The immunohistochemistry results were independently analyzed by two pathologists.

Modified response evaluation criteria in solid tumors (mRECIST)

Tumor progression was evaluated using the mRECIST criteria. The mRECIST criteria aim to select liver lesions suitable for accurate and reproducible measurement. In cases where multiple lesions are present, target lesions are selected based on size and reproducibility, with a maximum of two target lesions allowed, while the remaining lesions are considered non-target lesions. The imaging follow-up method involved contrast-enhanced MRI. A complete response was defined as the absence of enhancement in all lesions during the arterial phase. A partial response was defined as a decrease in the sum of the diameters of target lesions by at least 30%. Progressive disease was defined as an increase in the sum of the diameters of lesions by at least 20%. Stable disease was assigned to cases that did not meet the criteria for partial response or progressive disease.

Results

Discrepancies in treatment response indicating the heterogeneity of HCC within the tumor

Given the substantial heterogeneity among hepatocellular carcinomas (HCCs), the disparity between resistant and non-resistant tumors primarily stems from inherent heterogeneity rather than a correlation with drug resistance. In this study, we encountered a unique case of multifocal HCC in a patient exhibiting marked variations in treatment response among tumors (P131-1 exhibited pronounced necrosis post-treatment, whereas P131-2 demonstrated treatment resistance). The patient, diagnosed with two HCC nodules, underwent a treatment regimen consisting of two months of lenvatinib and three cycles of anti-PD1 antibodies prior to surgery. The larger nodule was designated P131-1, and the smaller one was designated P131-2. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed distinct treatment responses between P131-1 and P131-2. T1-weighted images acquired during the arterial phase demonstrated that anticancer treatment led to decreased intratumoral blood flow and substantial necrosis in P131-1, whereas P131-2 exhibited no response (Fig. 1). Following this observation, the patient underwent surgical resection, and the excised tumor specimens corroborated the imaging findings. As such, P131-2 was considered to have inherent resistance to the treatment. To identify malignancies with inherent drug resistance and unravel their molecular characteristics, we conducted single-cell sequencing and spatial transcriptomics analyses.

Intra-tumor heterogeneity revealed through high-throughput sequencing

Single-cell sequencing

To investigate the heterogeneity between different nodules from a patient, we conducted single-cell sequencing on cells obtained from four samples (P131T1, P131T2, P131P1, and P131P2, as shown in Fig. 2A). P131T1 and P131P1 were derived from tumor tissue and corresponding adjacent non-tumor tissue of P131-1, while P131T2 and P131P2 similarly corresponded to P131-2. A total of 29,767 high-quality cells were included in the subsequent analysis, leading to the identification of 23 distinct clusters after dimensionality reduction and clustering (Figure S1A). These cell populations encompassed T cells, NK cells, B cells, mast cells, dendritic cells, macrophages, malignant cells, hepatocytes, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and cycling cells (Fig. 2B), as determined by the expression of specific marker genes (Figure S1B) and differential gene expression observed in each cluster (Table S1). Specifically, epithelial cells were distinguished based on the expression of KRT8 (Figure S1B) and KRT18 (not shown), delineating subpopulations 3, 16, 20, and 22, as depicted in Figure S1. Subsequently, among the highly expressed genes within these subsets (Table S1), the elevated expression of ALB in subpopulations 16 and 20 led to their identification as hepatocytes, while subpopulations 3 and 22 were deemed potential malignant cells.

Spatial sequencing

A total of 4,252 spots from P131-1 and 4,596 spots from P131-2 were obtained through spatial transcriptomics. Each spot contained transcriptional information from 1 to 10 cells. After dimensionality reduction, all spots were divided into 15 clusters (Fig. 3A). By leveraging predictions from CopyKAT for spots containing malignant tumor cells (Fig. 3B) and integrating evaluations of frozen sections by pathologists (Fig. 3C), we classified the samples into four types: mesenchymal, tumor, liver, and juncture (Fig. 3D). Notably, the distribution of subpopulations identified through spatial transcriptomics closely matched the results obtained from stained frozen sections.

The transcriptomic signatures of inherent drug resistance cells

Based on the changes in MRI characteristics after drug application (Fig. 1A), we believe that P131-2 possesses inherent resistance traits compared to P131-1 (P131-1 exhibited pronounced necrosis post-treatment, whereas P131-2 demonstrated treatment resistance). Therefore, in both single-cell sequencing and spatial transcriptomics data, the specific tumor cells or regions in P131-2 represent the portion of tumor cells with inherent resistance. By identifying the specific characteristics of the tumor in P131-2, we aimed to identify biomarkers for inherently resistant cells.

Through differential analysis between P131T2-specific sc-clusters and other cells in the single-cell dataset (Fig. 4A), we identified a total of 137 significantly upregulated genes and 69 downregulated genes (Table S2). The spatial transcriptomics data revealed 153 upregulated genes and 104 downregulated genes specific to the tumor regions in P131-2 (Fig. 4B and Table S3). By intersecting these two gene lists, we identified 17 common upregulated genes, including PCK1, IGFBP1, CYP3A5, ARG1, SEPHS2, SULT2A1, ALDH1A1, FMO5, PAH, CYB5A, EIF1, MPC2, PSMB4, SERPINC1, TXN, PPDPF, and ATP5F1D. Considering that the tumor regions in the spatial transcriptomics data comprise a mixture of tumor cells, immune cells, and fibroblasts, we localized the expression of these genes. Among them, PCK1, ALDH1A1, MPC2, and PSMB4 were not only specifically expressed in epithelial cells in the single-cell sequencing data (Fig. 4C) but also significantly enriched in the tumor regions of the spatial transcriptomics data (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, all four genes were found to be enriched in pathways related to monocarboxylic acid metabolism (GO:0032787).

Both single-cell sequencing and spatial transcriptomics revealed the homogeneity of the two tumors, suggesting a shared tumor origin. Therefore, we performed pseudotime analysis using Monocle 3.0 (Fig. 5A), which indicated three differentiation directions for tumor cells. Specifically, the differentiation from sc-cluster 1 toward sc-clusters 2 and 5 represented the direction of differentiation toward the intrinsically resistant tumor cells in P131T2. PCK1 and ALDH1A1 exhibited significant changes in expression accompanying cell differentiation (Fig. 5B), while MPC2 and PSMB4 were not significantly associated with cell differentiation. Thus, PCK1 and ALDH1A1 may serve as potential biomarkers of inherent drug resistance in HCC.

High expression of PCK1 and ALDH1A1 exhibit drug resistance

HCC displays unique radiological characteristics, enabling a definitive diagnosis through imaging without the need for pathological confirmation. As a result, biopsies are not recommended for HCC patients. This limitation prevents us from obtaining tumor tissue from patients before they undergo targeted therapy. As an alternative approach, we assessed differences in ALDH1A1 and PCK1 expression by comparing drug-resistant (cohort 1) and untreated HCC (cohort 2) samples.

The mRECIST criteria are commonly used to evaluate the efficacy of systemic therapy in HCC patients. We collected data from 52 patients who underwent tumor resection after receiving systemic therapy. Among them, 8 patients achieved a complete response (CR), 19 patients had stable disease (SD), and 25 patients showed a partial response (PR). Four out of the 8 patients with CR achieved a pathological complete response, with histopathological examination revealing no residual tumor cells. All patients exhibited an increase in AFP levels prior to surgery, indicating that the residual malignant cells were considered drug-resistant. Among the remaining patients, 77.1% (37/48) tested positive for ALDH1A1 expression (Fig. 6A), and 79.2% (38/48) showed positive expression of PCK1 (Fig. 6B). The positivity rates of PCK1 and ALDH1A1 in SD patients were 83.3% (15/19) and 83.3% (16/19), respectively. In PR patients, the positivity rates of PCK1 and ALDH1A1 were 84.0% (21/25) and 76.0% (19/25), respectively. In CR patients, the positivity rates of PCK1 and ALDH1A1 were 75.0% (3/4) and 75.0% (3/4), respectively. The expression profiles of ALDH1A1 and PCK1 in the aforementioned patients are detailed in Table S4. Additionally, we examined the expression of ALDH1A1 and PCK1 in tumor cells from 173 untreated HCC patients using tissue microarray analysis. The positive rate of ALDH1A1 expression was 23.7% (41/173), while the positive rate of PCK1 expression was 22.0% (38/173). In subsequent analyses, the expression of ALDH1A1 and PCK1 showed no significant correlation with tumor size, CNLC staging, microvascular invasion, or patient prognosis (Table S5). The positive rates of ALDH1A1 and PCK1 in drug-resistant HCC were significantly higher than those in untreated HCC patients (both p_val_adj < 0.05).

Discussion

Tumor-intrinsic drug resistance remains a major obstacle to improving long-term outcomes in HCC. Because HCC lesions are both inter-patient and intra-patient heterogeneous, the same treatment can yield dramatically different responses, even among multiple tumors in the same liver. To date, few studies have systematically sought biomarkers that predict inherent resistance prior to therapy. Here, we combine single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics with clinical imaging and histopathology to map both the homogeneous and heterogeneous cell states within HCC lesions. By integrating these multimodal data, we identified tumor-specific candidate markers of primary lenvatinib resistance.

With the development of high-throughput sequencing technologies, spatial transcriptomics and single-cell RNA sequencing have gained widespread application in recent years. High-throughput sequencing provides transcriptomic information at the single-cell level. By utilizing higher-resolution sequencing technologies, we can more accurately analyze differences between tumor cells and identify subpopulations of tumor cells within drug-resistant tumors that exhibit true inherent resistance characteristics. In this study, two tumor nodules from a patient showed significantly different responses to treatment, with extensive necrosis observed in most regions of the P131-1 tumor, while the P131-2 tumor showed no response. We conducted single-cell and spatial transcriptomic sequencing on each sample and identified both unique and common characteristics of the two tumors. The commonalities between the two nodules suggest a possible shared origin, while the unique characteristics represent different differentiation trajectories. Four characteristic genes were identified in malignant cells with inherent resistance, all of which are related to monocarboxylic acid metabolism (GO:0032787), indicating that differences in tumor cell energy metabolism may determine drug resistance capacity. Based on the commonalities between the two nodules, we speculate that there are potential differentiation relationships among malignant cells. In the pseudotime analysis, the expression of PCK1 and ALDH1A1 varied with tumor differentiation, suggesting that PCK1 and ALDH1A1 play a role in differentiation toward inherent resistance. It has been reported that the occurrence and development of HCC are driven by intricate metabolic reprogramming [20], and the aforementioned differentially expressed genes are closely related to cellular glucose metabolism.

ALDH1A1, a member of the aldehyde dehydrogenase family, plays a pivotal role in the metabolism of retinaldehyde [21]. Additionally, ALDH1A1 is involved in metabolic regulation [22], maintenance of tumor stem cells [23–25], and drug metabolism [26]. Elevated expression of ALDH1A1 has been reported to decrease retinoic acid levels, which in turn stimulates the expression of PCK1 and G6PC, thereby augmenting gluconeogenesis [27]. The reduction in retinoic acid levels can lead to significant abnormalities in lipogenesis and lipid metabolism [28]. The roles of ALDH1A1 in drug metabolism and maintenance of tumor stem cells are directly associated with the formation of tumor drug resistance [23, 29]. Therefore, ALDH1A1 is also considered to be associated with tumor drug resistance in various malignancies.

PCK1 is one of the rate-limiting enzymes in gluconeogenesis [30], which is indirectly regulated by ALDH1A1. While glycolysis and its associated pathways promote cell proliferation [31], the gluconeogenesis pathway is suppressed in cancer due to its antagonistic relationship with glycolysis [32]. However, it has been reported that cancer cells can synthesize vital metabolites through abbreviated forms of gluconeogenesis, utilizing enzymes such as PCK1 and PEPCK1 [33]. Under conditions of energy deprivation, the function of PCK1 is enhanced, catalyzing the conversion of oxaloacetate to phosphoenolpyruvate [34]. This enzyme protects cells from nutrient-deprived environments by promoting glutamine breakdown and preventing glutamine or glucose scarcity [35].

These findings suggest that malignant cells characterized by high expression of ALDH1A1 and PCK1 exhibit increased resistance under conditions of low energy induced by angiogenesis inhibitors. Drug-resistant HCC cells display a unique metabolic pattern. In normal cells, glucose metabolism maintains a state of equilibrium. Under hypoxic conditions, glucose is converted to pyruvate and subsequently to lactate. However, in the presence of oxygen, pyruvate enters the tricarboxylic acid cycle. Tumor cells, on the other hand, rely primarily on aerobic glycolysis for energy production, even in the presence of oxygen, a phenomenon known as the Warburg effect [36]. In this study, significant upregulation of MPC2, which mediates mitochondrial pyruvate transport, was observed in inherently resistant malignant cells. This upregulation indicates an enhancement in cellular oxidative phosphorylation. These observations suggest that the metabolic reprogramming of malignant cells underlies differences in drug resistance. The interplay among MPC2, ALDH1A1, and PCK1 in gluconeogenesis raises intriguing questions about the direct involvement of gluconeogenesis in inherent drug resistance.

HCC remains a formidable challenge in oncology, and lenvatinib is widely used as a frontline therapy for advanced disease. However, many patients derive only transient benefit or rapidly develop resistance, and the drug’s frequent gastrointestinal and other toxicities can severely compromise quality of life. Identifying predictive biomarkers to spare non-responders from ineffective and potentially harmful treatment is, therefore, a critical unmet need.

Our study leverages single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics to investigate tumor heterogeneity and identify two candidate resistance markers, ALDH1A1 and PCK1. We observed markedly higher frequencies of ALDH1A1- and PCK1-positive cells in tumors from patients previously treated with anti-angiogenic agents and PD-1 inhibitors compared to untreated controls. While suggestive of an association with drug resistance, these findings must be viewed in light of several key limitations.

First, HCC clinical practice rarely yields paired pre- and post-treatment biopsies, so we could not directly track marker dynamics during lenvatinib exposure. Instead, our comparisons rely on cross-sectional cohorts, treated versus untreated, which cannot definitively distinguish inherent from therapy-induced expression changes. Second, we did not perform functional perturbation studies to confirm that ALDH1A1 or PCK1 causally mediate resistance phenotypes. Without such validation, their roles remain correlative. Third, although intratumoral heterogeneity is well characterized in our P131 case, inter-patient variability remains largely unexplored. Our immunohistochemistry results in small cohorts (cohort 1 and cohort 2) hint at broader applicability, but larger, independent patient cohorts, ideally with single-cell or spatial profiling from multiple individuals, are required to assess reliability across diverse genetic backgrounds and etiologies. Fourth, in untreated HCC samples, ALDH1A1 and PCK1 positivity was low and did not correlate with prognosis, suggesting that these proteins may be treatment-specific biomarkers rather than general prognostic factors. Although a minority of untreated tumors expressed these markers, and not all treated tumors did, this pattern underscores the need to clarify whether expression reflects pre-existing resistant clones or is induced by therapy. Finally, no large public HCC datasets currently link ALDH1A1 or PCK1 expression to lenvatinib outcomes; analogous evidence comes only from other cancers. Thus, before clinical implementation, prospective validation in multicenter HCC cohorts with standardized immunohistochemistry or transcriptomic sequencing, functional studies, and ideally paired biopsies will be essential.

In summary, while ALDH1A1 and PCK1 emerge as promising candidates for predicting lenvatinib resistance, our findings are preliminary. Future studies must overcome biopsy constraints, rigorously validate causality, and demonstrate predictive performance across independent patient populations before these markers can guide personalized HCC therapy.
