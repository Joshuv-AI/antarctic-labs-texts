Stimulation to secrete insulin induces pancreatic β-cell dysfunction through Tfe3 activation
Authors: Yuki Aida, Nozomu Kadota, Mimi Takahashi, Yuichi Koshiishi, Koji Kasahara, Kohsuke Kataoka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryDuring progression of diabetes, pancreatic β-cells gradually lose their ability to produce and secrete insulin. Dysfunction of β-cells is tightly associated with reduced expression of a set of glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS)-related genes, particularly the gene encoding Mafa, a transcription factor critical for β-cell functionality. However, the mechanisms underlying GSIS-related gene downregulation remain elusive. Here, we show that continuous supplementation of the drinking water of mice with glucose leads to nuclear accumulation of Tfe3, a transcription factor that responds to stress of the Golgi and lysosomes, in β-cells. This change was associated with Mafa downregulation and impairment of glucose tolerance. Acute stimulation of insulin secretion in vitro also induces Tfe3 activation and downregulates Mafa and GSIS-related genes. Activated Tfe3 binds to β-cell-specific enhancer regions of Mafa and other GSIS-related genes and suppresses the enhancer activity. Thus, stimulation to secrete insulin alters transcriptional program in β-cells toward dysfunction through Tfe3 activation.

Introduction

Pancreatic β-cells of islets secrete insulin in response to a rise in blood glucose levels. Compromised secretion of insulin from β-cells or perturbation of its action on target peripheral tissues, namely, insulin resistance, causes diabetes mellitus. During progression of type 2 diabetes (T2D), β-cells gradually lose their ability to produce and secrete insulin. In a classical view, prolonged hyperglycemia and hyperlipidemia due to peripheral insulin resistance leads to production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which in turn damage β-cells.1,2,3 Chronic stress of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) due to an increased demand for insulin synthesis can also trigger β-cell dysfunction.1,2,3 These findings have been obtained from T2D models such as leptin receptor-deficient db/db or high fat diet (HFD) feeding mice in which manifestation of peripheral insulin resistance, hyperglycemia, and hyperlipidemia precedes β-cell dysfunction. However, T2D in humans is a highly heterogeneous disease,4 and these model mice most closely reflect severe insulin-resistant diabetes (SIRD) of humans. On the other hand, manifestation of β-cell dysfunction precedes insulin resistance in some subtypes of T2D such as mild obesity-related diabetes (MOD) or severe insulin deficient diabetes (SIDD). Therefore, causal relationship between insulin resistance and β-cell dysfunction is obscure and the mechanisms underlying β-cell dysfunction may vary depending on subtypes of T2D.

In T2D-associated dysfunctional β-cells, the cell-type specific transcriptional program that regulates glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS) is compromised.5,6 Genes related to GSIS include those encoding preproinsulin (Ins1 and Ins2), glucose transporter 2 (Slc2a2), glucokinase (Gck), and incretin receptors (Glp1r and Gipr), as well as their transcriptional regulators Pdx1, Neurod1, Nkx6.1, and Mafa. These transcription factors (TFs) form a core regulatory network and are necessary for maintaining β-cell functionality.5,6,7 Their expression levels are decreased during progression of β-cell dysfunction. In particular, Mafa is downregulated at the earliest stages in the mouse T2D model db/db,8 suggesting that it is one of the most vulnerable TFs to β-cell-damaging insults, and its loss is regarded as a hallmark of β-cell dysfunction. The essential role of Mafa in insulin production and secretion has been demonstrated in multiple studies using knockdown approaches in cultured β cells9 and knockout mouse models.10

Based on these observations and the fact that Mafa is a critical regulator of GSIS,10,11 previous studies have focused on regulation of Mafa under pathophysiological conditions. The expression level of Mafa is regulated at multiple steps, including transcription12,13,14 and protein turnover.15,16,17,18 For instance, Mafa protein degradation is stimulated by treatment of β-cells with ROS-generating reagents through the ubiquitin-proteasome system.16,17 Mafa mRNA levels are downregulated by fatty acid palmitate.12 Furthermore, transcription of Mafa is downregulated by prolonged ER stress and the unfolded protein response through activation of stress effector TFs, namely, Xbp1 and Atf6.19,20 Conversely, incretin hormones upregulate Mafa transcription through protein kinase A (Pka) activation and subsequent inhibition of SIK kinases, followed by dephosphorylation and nuclear translocation of the transcriptional coactivator Crtc2 and its association with Creb, which binds to the promoter and enhancer regions of Mafa.9,21 However, the mechanisms underlying Mafa downregulation during β-cell dysfunction remain elusive.

The MiT/TFE family comprises TFs of genes related to autophagy, lysosomes, and the Golgi apparatus.22,23 They are basic-helix-loop-helix zipper (bHLH-zip) TFs and include Tfeb, Tfe3, Tfec, and Mitf. They are phosphorylated by mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) and sequestered in the cytoplasm under unstressed conditions. Upon nutrient deprivation, dysfunction of lysosomes or the Golgi, or pharmacological inhibition of mTORC1, they are dephosphorylated and translocate into the nucleus. They activate transcription of genes related to autophagy (e.g., Sqstm1 and Flcn), lysosomes (e.g., Ctss and Cd68), and the Golgi (e.g., Golga2 and Golgb1) by binding to a DNA element named coorinated lysosomal expression and regulation (CLEAR). In β-cells, Tfeb and Tfe3 are activated by nutrient deprivation and negatively regulate insulin gene expression.24 Mice with β-cell-specific overexpression of Tfeb or, conversely, double knockout of Tfeb and Tfe3 exhibited altered glucose tolerance,24 indicating that these TFs are regulators of β-cell physiology; however, the underlying molecular mechanism has not been fully elucidated.

In this study, we examined the effect, if any, of insulin secretion itself on the function and transcriptional program of β-cells. We demonstrate that continuous glucose stimulation in mice induces nuclear accumulation of Tfe3 in islet β-cells, which is associated with impaired glucose tolerance and downregulation of Mafa. Acute stimulation of insulin secretion in vitro also activates Tfe3 and downregulates Mafa and other GSIS-related gene expression in cultured β-cells. Mechanistically, activated Tfe3 binds to β-cell-specific enhancer regions of Mafa and GSIS-related genes. Reporter assays demonstrated that Tfe3 suppresses the Mafa gene enhancer activity. Hence, stimulation to secrete insulin leads to an altered transcriptional program and dysfunction of β-cells through stress-responsive Tfe3. We also showed that Mafa downregulation in db/db β-cells is not dependent on Tfe3 activation, suggesting multiple pathways of β-cell dysfunction in multiple T2D subtypes.

Results

Stimulation to secrete insulin impairs glucose tolerance in mice and downregulates Mafa in β-cells

Widely used T2D models such as db/db or HFD feeding mice manifest various symptoms of diabetes including peripheral insulin resistance, hyperglycemia, hyperlipidemia, and resultant transient compensatory insulin hypersecretion. Therefore, causal relationship between each symptom and β-cell dysfunction is obscure (Figure 1A). To investigate whether overload of insulin secretion affects β-cell function and glucose homeostasis, we established a novel and simple in vivo experimental model. Mice were provided drinking water containing 20% glucose ad libitum for a prolonged period and subjected to the intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (IPGTT) and immunofluorescence staining of pancreas sections (Figure 1B). Body weights of control and glucose-supplemented mice were similar even after 15 days of treatment (Figure S1A). Although no difference was observed in non-fasting blood glucose levels on day 8 or day 15 (Figures S1B and S1C), circulating insulin C-peptide levels on day 14 tended to be slightly higher, although the difference was not statistically significant (Figure S1D). First, IPGTT was performed without prior fasting (Figure 1B). As numerous studies have reported, blood glucose levels of untreated mice peaked at 15 min after intraperitoneal glucose injection and decreased toward 90 min (Figures S1E and S1G). After 2 weeks of glucose supplementation, blood glucose levels first peaked at 15 min, and in most but not all mice, increased again and fluctuated at 45 min or later, with the level and duration of the second peak varying among individuals (Figures S1F and S1G). In addition, IPGTT was performed with prior fasting (Figure 1B). Fasting glucose levels in glucose-supplemented mice were elevated compared with those in untreated mice (Figure 1C), and IPGTT indicated impaired glucose tolerance (Figure 1D). Insulin secretion following glucose injection was also reduced in these treated mice (Figure 1E).

The pancreas sections from these mice were stained for Mafa, and signal intensities in individual β-cell nuclei were quantified. To minimize potential effects of high-dose intraperitoneal glucose injection or fasting on Mafa expression levels, the mice were euthanized 3–4 days after IPGTT without prior fasting (Figure 1B). Mafa was located in the nuclei of β-cells, and its levels were decreased in mice provided with glucose-containing water (Figures 1F and S1H). Thus, only 2 weeks of continuous high glucose exposure of mice downregulates Mafa and impairs β-cell function and glucose tolerance.

We examined whether insulin secretion affects expression levels of Mafa using cultured MIN6 murine insulinoma-derived cells. Given that glucose deprivation reduces Mafa levels,14,18 the cells were maintained in culture medium containing relatively high glucose (12.5 mM) for 24 h and subsequently stimulated with 25 mM glucose. The stimulation induced insulin secretion (Figure 1G), which was accompanied by Mafa downregulation (Figure 1H). KCl treatment, which forces insulin secretion through membrane depolarization without glucose utilization (Figure S1I), also reduced Mafa levels, whereas levels of other β-cell-enriched TFs, namely, Pdx1, Neurod1, and Nkx6.1 were not affected (Figure 1I). Stimulation of cells maintained in 5 mM glucose medium with KCl also resulted in reduction of Mafa levels (Figure S1J).

In response to insulin secretion, β-cells are forced to synthesize and process preproinsulin in order to supply mature insulin, which induces stress of organelles.25 Immunostaining for the Golgi marker protein Golga2/Gm130 (Figure 1J) revealed that the Golgi was enlarged upon KCl stimulation. Nimodipine, an antagonist of the L-type voltage-dependent Ca2+ channel (Figure S1I), inhibited both basal and KCl-stimulated insulin secretion (Figure 1K) and protected against KCl-induced expansion of the Golgi area. These observations suggest that insulin secretion affects the integrity of the Golgi due to stress of the secretory pathway. Nimodipine increased Mafa protein levels, due to probably relief from stress by basal level of insulin secretion, and its pretreatment partially attenuated the KCl-stimulated downregulation of Mafa (Figure 1L). Quantification of mRNA levels revealed that KCl stimulation reduced Mafa mRNA levels within 4 h, which was blunted by nimodipine (Figure 1M). In primary islets, Mafa mRNA levels were also decreased following KCl stimulation (Figure 1N). Inhibition of autocrine/paracrine insulin signaling using hydroxy-2-naphthalenylmethylphosphonic acid tris(acetoxymethyl) ester (HNMPA-(AM)3), an insulin receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitor, did not mitigate the KCl-induced Mafa decrease (Figure S1K). Together, these observations indicate that stimulation to secrete insulin, but not insulin signaling, downregulates Mafa at least at the mRNA level. The reduction of Mafa protein levels seemed greater than that of Mafa mRNA levels; therefore, Mafa protein levels may also be regulated.

KCl stimulation downregulates β-cell-specific genes and upregulates lysosome- and autophagy-related genes

To explore the mechanism underlying Mafa downregulation by insulin secretion, we conducted RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) experiments of KCl-treated and untreated MIN6 cells. Genes downregulated by KCl included Mafa as well as the other β-cell-specific genes Pdx1, Slc2a2, and Glp1r (Figures 2A and S2A). Gene enrichment analysis revealed that “maturity onset diabetes of the young” was the top represented Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway (Figures 2B and S2B–S2D). On the other hand, analysis of the upregulated genes identified “autophagy,” “mTOR signaling pathway,” and “mitophagy” as enriched KEGG pathways (Figures 2A, 2B, and S3A–S3C). Analysis of genes related to the GO term “lysosome” (GO:0005764) revealed that genes encoding components of lysosomes/autophagosomes, e.g., Gla (galactosidase α) and Ctns (lysosomal cystine transporter), and their regulators, e.g., Flcn (folliculin and mTORC1 regulator), Fnip1/Fnip2 (folliculin-interacting protein 1/2), and Rheb (mTORC1 regulator), were upregulated (Figure 2D). These genes are MiT/TFE targets,26,27 and the upregulated genes also included Tfe3 and Mitf themselves. Genes related to “unfolded protein response” and “TNFα signaling via NF-κB” were also enriched (Figures S3D and S3E).

To explore TFs that regulate the downregulated and upregulated genes, DNA motifs enriched in the promoter regions of these genes were analyzed. Although no specific motif was detected in the downregulated gene promoters, the upregulated gene promoters were enriched with the binding sequences of several TFs (Figure 2E). The Smad2:Smad3 motif extracted by this analysis (TGACTCA) represents co-regulated AP1-binding sites (Figure 2E). Accordingly, AP1-related TFs (Fos, Fosb, Fosl1, Fosl2, Junb, and Atf3) were upregulated (Figures 2A and S3F). Of note, the motifs for bHLH factors (Arnt, Arntl, Mixip, and Npas2) resemble the CLEAR element (CACGTG), which is the binding sequence for MiT/TFE (Figure 2E). We thus assumed that KCl stimulation activates genes related to lysosomes, the Golgi, and autophagy by activating MiT/TFE.

Tfe3 is activated by stimuli to secrete insulin

Our RNA-seq analysis revealed that Tfe3 is the most abundant member of the MiT/TFE family in MIN6 cells (Figure S4A). We thus examined whether stimulation to secrete insulin activates Tfe3. Immunoblotting of MIN6 cell extracts detected Tfe3 protein as long and short isoforms, which are produced by alternative usage of initiator codons, and both isoforms were detected as multiple bands due to phosphorylation at multiple sites (Figures 3A and 3B). Upon glucose or KCl stimulation, both isoforms were quickly (1 h) hypo-phosphorylated (Figures 3B and 3C). Accordingly, KCl stimulation induced nuclear accumulation of Tfe3, which was abolished by pretreatment with nimodipine (Figure 3D). KCl-indued nuclear accumulation of Tfe3 and its inhibition by nimodipine were also observed in primary islets (Figure 3E). We also examined the pancreas sections from the pairs of the model mice (identical to those shown in Figures 1F and S1H) supplemented with glucose for 2 weeks by immunostaining with anti-Tfe3 antibody. Tfe3 protein predominantly localized to the cytoplasm in untreated mice, and quantification of signal intensities in individual β-cell nuclei revealed that its levels in the nuclei were increased after glucose exposure (Figures 3F and S4C). Therefore, Tfe3 is dephosphorylated and translocated into the nucleus in response to stimuli to secrete insulin in vitro and in vivo.

KCl stimulation downregulates Mafa by activating Tfe3

We then examined whether Tfe3 is involved in the downregulation of Mafa. Amino-terminally truncated Tfeb lacking the Rag-binding domain (Rag-BD), which is necessary for cytoplasmic retention, is constitutively nuclear and transcriptionally active.28 We thus constructed a similar mutant of Tfe3 (Tfe3 ΔN1) and designed an expression plasmid containing two transcriptional units, one for EGFP and another for Tfe3 ΔN1 (Figure 4A). To determine changes of endogenous Mafa protein levels in MIN6 cells, which have a very poor transfection efficiency, we double-stained transfected cells with anti-EGFP and anti-Mafa antibodies, and separately quantified Mafa levels in EGFP-positive (cells that received the plasmid) and EGFP-negative (cells that did not receive the plasmid) cells (Figure 4B). Mafa levels were lower in cells that expressed Tfe3 ΔN1 than in cells that expressed EGFP alone (EGFP-empty) and cells that did not receive the plasmids. In a parallel experiment, endogenous Tfe3 and exogenous Tfe3 ΔN1 proteins were detected by immunostaining with anti-Tfe3 with anti-EGFP antibodies (Figure 4C). Tfe3 signal intensities in EGFP-positive cells (predominantly Tfe3 ΔN1) were substantially higher than in EGFP-negative cells (endogenous Tfe3), confirming Tfe3 ΔN1 protein was overexpressed in transfected cells. Conversely, endogenous Tfe3 was knocked down using an expression plasmid containing transcriptional units for short hairpin RNA (shRNA) and EGFP (Figure 4D). Quantification of endogenous Tfe3 signal intensity in EGFP-positive cells revealed reduced Tfe3 signals in cells transfected with the Tfe3-targeting shRNA expression plasmid, indicating effective knockdown (Figure 4E). These results also demonstrate that this approach allows quantitative assessment of changes in endogenous protein levels. After transfection of the plasmid, MIN6 cells were treated with or without KCl, and transduced EGFP and endogenous Mafa were stained (Figure 4F). Quantification of Mafa signal intensities in EGFP-positive and EGFP-negative cells revealed that lowered Mafa levels in KCl-treated cells were recovered in cells that received shRNA for Tfe3 (shTfe3-EGFP) but not in cells that received the control plasmid (Con-EGFP). Together, these observations indicate that KCl stimulation downregulates Mafa through activation of Tfe3.

Organelle stress and starvation activate Tfe3 and downregulate Mafa

We next tested if stress of membranous organelles activates Tfe3 and downregulates Mafa. MIN6 cells were treated with various stress-inducing chemicals, namely, chloroquine (Cq), brefeldin A (BfA), monensin (Mo), thapsigargin (Tp), and tunicamycin (Tu) (Figure 5A). Cq alkalinizes lysosomes and impairs autophagy and crinophagy.29,30 BfA inhibits protein transport from the ER to the Golgi apparatus by preventing vesicle transport.31,32 Mo is a Na+/H+ ionophore and inhibits protein transport by disrupting the pH gradient of various organelles including the Golgi and lysosomes.33 Tp and Tu have been widely used as inducers of ER stress; however, their mechanisms of action are distinct. Tp act as an inhibitor of sarco/ER Ca2+ ATPase (SERCA),34 whereas Tu interferes with N-linked glycosylation of proteins.35 Among the stressors tested, Cq, BfA, and Mo induced nuclear accumulation of Tfe3, while Tp and Tu did not (Figure 5B). Expression levels of Neurod1 were decreased by the stressors tested, and Mafa protein levels were also decreased except for Tu (Figure 5C). By contrast, Pdx1 and Nkx6.1 proteins were not affected. Nuclear translocation of MiT/TFE is also regulated by nutrient conditions, and we found that removal of glucose and serum from culture medium induced nuclear accumulation of Tfe3 in MIN6 cells (Figure 5D), which was associated with downregulation of Mafa protein (Figure 5E) and mRNA (Figure 5F). Deprivation of either glucose or serum alone induced nuclear accumulation of Tfe3 and downregulation of Mafa, with glucose deprivation exerting the predominant effect (Figures S5A and S5B). Furthermore, overnight fasting of mice led to nuclear accumulation of Tfe3 and downregulation of Mafa in islet β-cells (Figures 5G, 5H, S5C and S5D). Together, these results demonstrate that various cues that activate Tfe3 reduce Mafa levels in β-cells in vitro and in vivo.

We inspected our Tfe3 ChIP-seq and RNA-seq data around the Mafa locus, together with previously published assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq)36 and H3K27Ac ChIP-seq37 data obtained from primary mouse islets (Figure 6B). ChIP-seq data of β-cell TFs (Mafa, Neurod1, Pdx1, and Nkx6.1) from a public database38,39,40 were also visualized. Tfe3 was recruited to the promoter region (P) and gene body of Mafa, as well as its β-cell-specific enhancer region (E) located at ∼8 kb upstream of the transcription start site (TSS),21,41 which was also bound by Neurod1, Pdx1, and Nkx6.1. Tfe3 also bound to four regions far upstream (-30 to 38 kb) of the TSS, which were also positive for ATAC and H3K27Ac signals and several β-cell TFs. These regions are likely super-enhancers and were designated here as SE1−SE4.

Inspection of other downregulated gene loci, such as Slc2a2 and Glul, revealed that Tfe3 was also recruited to β-cell-specific enhancer regions42,43 and was induced upon KCl stimulation (Figures S6B and S6C). By contrast, other downregulated genes such as Glp1r were not bound by Tfe3 (Figure S6D). Thus, Tfe3 is recruited to a fraction of downregulated genes. A previous report using the human β-cell line EndoC-βH1 showed that TFEB is recruited to distal super-enhancer regions of the INS gene.24 In murine MIN6 cells, although such super-enhancers were not found at preproinsulin gene loci (Ins1 and Ins2), Tfe3 was recruited to the promoter and gene body of Ins1 and Ins2, and to the distal enhancer-like region of Ins1 (Figures S6E and S6F).

For comparison, we analyzed KCl-upregulated gene loci. Binding of Tfe3 to ATAC- and H3K27Ac-positive regulatory regions of previously identified MiT/TFE target genes such as Ctns, Sqstm1, Flcn, Fnip2, Vps18, and Rheb was induced by KCl (Figures S7A–S7F). Therefore, Tfe3 is recruited to CLEAR elements and upregulates nearby genes as in other types of cells.

CLEAR-like elements were found within SE2 and SE3 peaks of the Mafa locus, but not within P, E, and the other super-enhancers (SE1 and SE4) (Figure 6B). To investigate the mechanism, if any, by which Tfe3 is recruited to the downregulated genes, we performed motif enrichment analysis of the Tfe3 peaks near to downregulated and upregulated genes. The top five motifs near downregulated and upregulated genes included binding sites for MiT/TFE (CLEAR), Fos/Jun/ATF3, and SP1/KLF (Figure S7G). A specific DNA sequence feature that distinguishes downregulated and upregulated genes was not identified.

We next tested if Tfe3 can suppress Mafa enhancer/promoter activity using reporter assays. The promoter and/or enhancer regions of Mafa, which do not contain the CLEAR element, were cloned and linked to the firefly luciferase gene21 (Figure 6C). These reporter plasmids were transfected into MIN6 cells with or without the expression plasmid for Tfe3 (Tfe3 ΔN1) (Figure 6D). Firefly luciferase activity of Mafa-E+P-Luc was reduced by co-expression of Tfe3 ΔN1, whereas activities of Mafa-P-Luc and the unrelated thymidine kinase (TK) promoter reporter were not. Therefore, Tfe3 suppresses the Mafa enhancer activity in MIN6 cells.

To elucidate the mechanism by which Tfe3 represses the Mafa enhancer, a series of deletion mutants of Tfe3 was generated (Figure 6E) and assayed for suppression of the Mafa enhancer reporter (Figure 6F). The long form of Tfe3 suppressed the Mafa enhancer similar to the constitutively nuclear ΔN1 mutant, probably because the full-length Tfe3 predominantly localizes to the nucleus due to overexpression. Deletion of the HLH or zipper domain (ΔN1-HLH or ΔN1-zip) resulted in complete loss of suppression activity. By contrast, deletion of the transactivation domain (TAD) (ΔN2) did not affect its ability to repress the Mafa enhancer. Further deletion of the carboxyl-terminal tail (ΔN2C1 and ΔN2C3) abrogated the suppression activity, but a smaller deletion of the carboxyl-terminal extremity containing multiple phosphorylation sites (ΔN2C2) did not.

We also tested the ability of these mutants to activate CLEAR-dependent transcription using a reporter containing three copies of CLEAR elements from Mcoln1 gene and rabbit β-globin promoter (Figure 6G). This reporter was activated by the constitutively nuclear ΔN1 mutant, and the HLH and TAD were necessary for activation. Unexpectedly, the mutant with deletion of the zipper region (ΔN1-zip) partially activated this reporter, probably due to its partial retention of dimerization ability. Importantly, Tfe3 mutants that completely lack transactivation function (ΔN2 and ΔN2C2) repressed the Mafa enhancer. Thus, the transactivation function of Tfe3 was not required for the suppression, whereas the carboxyl-terminal region (amino acids 442–546) with no specific annotated function was necessary (Figure 6E).

Pancreas sections from homozygous db/db and heterozygous db/+ mice were stained with anti Mafa, Tfe3, and insulin antibodies. Discussion

Here, we demonstrated that stimulation to secrete insulin from β-cells induces Mafa downregulation by activating Tfe3 using β-cell derived MIN6 cells. The stimulation also reduces expression of a set of GSIS-related genes, namely, Slc2a2, Pdx1, and Glp1r. This previously unrecognized response of β-cells was associated with deformation of the Golgi apparatus, suggesting that excess insulin secretion overloads the secretory pathway. Thus, Tfe3 activation and subsequent induction of genes related to the Golgi or lysosomes is a physiological response of β-cells to protect the secretory pathway from organelle stress. Downregulation of Mafa should also help to relieve the stress by decreasing transcription of GSIS-related genes. We also showed that continuous oral administration of glucose to mice induced Tfe3 accumulation and Mafa downregulation in nuclei of β-cells and impaired glucose tolerance. Given that Mafa is essential for insulin production and GSIS,9,10 this indicates that prolonged stimulation of insulin secretion can reprogram β-cells toward a status with lowered GSIS ability. Activation of Tfe3 and a concomitant reduction of Mafa in response to insulin secretory stimulation were also observed in primary islet β-cells, although a causal relationship between these events has not been established. To demonstrate this causal relationship, Tfe3 overexpression or knockdown in primary islet β-cells will be required. It also remains to be clarified whether Ca2+ influx into β-cells, insulin secretion itself, or subsequent stress to the secretory pathway is responsible for activation of Tfe3. Furthermore, it is important to examine whether the continuous reprogrammed status leads to irreversible β-cell dysfunction and manifestation of diabetes.

Previous studies have demonstrated that steady-state levels of Mafa are positively regulated by glucose in primary islets and cultured β-cells and thus glucose-deprivation decreases Mafa expression.14,18,44 However, prolonged higher glucose exposure of β-cells, e.g., for 1 to several days, decreases Mafa expression at the level of its transcription and protein degradation.45,46 Therefore, to carefully avoid prolonged exposure to high glucose or glucose deprivation, we performed experiments in which cells were first equilibrated in moderate glucose concentration (12.5 mM for MIN6 and 2.5 mM for primary islets) and subsequently subjected to glucose or KCl stimulation. We show here that acute high glucose stimulation downregulates Mafa in MIN6 and primary islets within hours. These findings indicate that regulation of Mafa levels by glucose involves multiple mechanisms. Our data demonstrate that acute stimulation with glucose or KCl downregulates Mafa transcription via Tfe3 nuclear accumulation. Under glucose-deprived conditions, we observed that Mafa reduction coincides with nuclear accumulation of Tfe3, suggesting that glucose starvation decreases the activity of the nutrient-sensing kinase mTORC1, thereby promoting Tfe3 activation. By contrast, previous study has shown that prolonged high glucose exposure activates mTORC1 and subsequently inhibits the Pka-Creb pathway, leading to reduction of Mafa mRNA expression.9 Thus, Mafa mRNA levels are under control of activity of these upstream signaling cascades and TFs. Interestingly, prolonged exposure to both nutrient-rich and nutrient-poor conditions reduces Mafa expression by activating and inhibiting mTORC1, respectively, thorough distinct downstream mechanisms. It remains to be determined whether Tfe3 activation in response to acute glucose or KCl stimulation is also mediated by mTORC1 inhibition.

In conventional T2D model mice such as HFD supplementation or db/db, β-cells initially undergo transient adaptation to moderately high glucose levels during the early prediabetic phase, primarily as a consequence of peripheral insulin resistance. At later stages, sustained metabolic stress, such as glucotoxicity, lipotoxicity, or exposure to ROS, induces β-cell dysfunction. Dysfunction of β-cells in both human and mice is closely associated with Mafa downregulation. For instance, expression levels of Mafa are reduced after a long period, e.g., 20 weeks of HFD feeding or 5–8 weeks of age in the db/db genetic condition.8,9 In the present study, we demonstrate that Mafa is downregulated in db/db mice through Tfe3-independent mechanisms. Blanchet et al. reported that HFD feeding of mice downregulates Mafa by activating mTORC1 and subsequent induction of Hif1α and inhibition of the Pka-Creb pathway.9 By contrast, we show here that only 2 weeks of glucose administration in drinking water is sufficient to induce glucose intolerance. In β-cells of this experimental model, Tfe3 is increased and Mafa is decreased, likely due to repeated high glucose stimulation. We therefore propose that this novel mouse model provides a useful system for investigating mechanisms of β-cell dysfunction while avoiding the confounding effects of prolonged overnutrition and/or insulin resistance observed in other T2D models, such as HFD supplementation or db/db. Future studies should also determine whether this model recaptures aspects of human T2D subtypes, particularly of MOD or SIDD.

We demonstrated that activated Tfe3 is recruited to β-cell-specific enhancer regions of Mafa and a set of GSIS-related genes including Ins1 and Ins2, and that Tfe3 represses the Mafa gene enhancer activity. Tfe3 thus seems to downregulate Mafa and GSIS-related genes by interfering with a TF(s) that binds to and activates the enhancer. ChIP-seq data of Tfeb and Tfe3 from a previous study24 and our study, respectively, indicated that these TFs occupy super-enhancer regions of insulin and Mafa genes, as well as their gene bodies, in human and murine β-cells. Together with the finding that Tfeb forms phase-separated droplets,47 Tfe3 presumably integrates into droplets of β-cell-specific super-enhancers through an interaction with the CLEAR elements or other TFs and thereby interferes with enhancer activities. The carboxyl-terminal region of Tfe3 necessary for the repression activity may play a critical role in it.

We demonstrated that glucose and/or serum deprivation in vitro or fasting of mice in vivo induced nuclear accumulation of Tfe3 and Mafa downregulation in β-cells. Nutrient deprivation leads to decreased activity of the nutrient-sensing kinase mTORC1 through mechanisms involving its regulators, i.e., Rheb, Flcn, v-ATPase, the Ragulator complex, and Rag GTPases, on lysosomal membrane, thereby leading to dephosphorylation and nuclear translocation of MiT/TFE.22,23 Damages to lysosomes by Cq activates MiT/TFE through mTORC1 inhibition.48 Accordingly, we showed that, among the organelle stressors examined, Cq induced nuclear accumulation of Tfe3 in β-cells. We further showed that the Golgi-damaging reagents BfA and Mo activated Tfe3, which is consistent with previous studies reporting that BfA inhibits mTORC149 and that Mo induces dephosphorylation and nuclear accumulation of Tfe3,50 respectively. Therefore, in β-cells, nutrient deprivation and these organelle stressors seem to activate Tfe3 and downregulate Mafa through mechanisms involving mTORC1 inhibition. By contrast, HFD feeding of mice or chronic high glucose exposure of cultured β-cells downregulates Mafa by activating mTORC1.9 Therefore, Mafa expression in β-cells is vulnerable to changes in nutrient conditions and various cellular stresses. The molecular mechanism underlying Mafa loss in dysfunctional β-cells might differ depending on the subtypes of diabetes. Elucidation of the precise mechanism underlying Mafa loss in various diabetes models and its observation human subjects might be key to classify and understand subtypes of diabetes and pre-diabetic conditions.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, we demonstrated the causal relationship among stimulation of insulin secretion, Tfe3 activation, and Mafa downregulation using the MIN6 cell line. In primary islets, we also observed activation of Tfe3 and a reduction of Mafa in response to insulin secretory stimulation. Using a mouse model administered glucose in drinking water, we observed Tfe3 activation, Mafa downregulation in β-cells, and glucose intolerance, which are consistent with the observations in vitro. However, it remains to be evaluated whether the causal relationship we elucidated in MIN6 cells is also valid in primary islets or in vivo by genetic manipulation. Gene knockout of Tfe3 in β-cells in mice might result in reduced levels of lysosome- and Golgi-related components and subsequent dysfunction of cellular processes due to reduced transcription of CLEAR-dependent genes. Instead, deletion of the C-terminal region of Tfe3 will specifically abrogate its transcriptional repression activity without affecting CLEAR network and will be suitable to evaluate the role of Tfe3 in downregulation of β-cell-specific genes including Mafa and β-cell dysfunction in response to excess insulin secretion. Second, it is necessary to determine whether the mechanism underlying Tfe3-mediated Mafa loss also applies to human subjects. In particular, as diabetes mellitus is a complex disease that manifests through divergent genetic background and lifestyles, our mechanism might not necessarily apply to all, if any, types of diabetes.

Experimental model and study participant details

Mouse models

Male C57BL/6JJcl, BKS.Cg-+ Leprdb/+ Leprdb/Jcl, and BKS.Cg-m +/+ Leprdb/Jcl mice were purchased from CLEA Japan. All animals were maintained in a specific pathogen-free facility with 12 h light/dark cycles. At 7 weeks of age, the C57BL/6JJcl mice were randomly assigned to two groups and given water or 20% glucose-containing water. Glucose tolerance was tested by injecting 2 g glucose/kg body mass of 20% glucose solution and subsequently measuring plasma glucose levels using Carefast R (Nipro) by collecting a drop of blood from the tail. Mice were sacrificed at the indicated time points and pancreatic tissues were dissected. Only male mice were used in this study. Therefore, potential sex-related differences could not be evaluated. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee of the Yokohama City University (approval number T-A-25-001).

Cell lines

The mouse insulinoma-derived MIN6 cell line was a generous gift from Dr. Jun-ichi Miyazaki (Osaka University)56 and was cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (D-MEM) supplemented with 15% fetal calf serum under a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37°C. MIN6 cells were authenticated based on characteristic β-cell morphology, ability to secrete insulin in response to glucose, the expression of β-cell genes (Insulin 1, Insulin 2, Mafa, and Pdx1), and species-specific PCR of genomic DNA. This cell line tested negative for mycoplasma contamination using MycoStrip Mycoplasma Detection Kit (InvivoGen).

Method details

Cell treatment

MIN6 cells were maintained in D-MEM containing 25 mM glucose supplemented with 15% fetal calf serum and seeded into 48-well plates. Twenty-four hours before treatment, the medium was changed to D-MEM containing 12.5 mM glucose supplemented with 15% fetal calf serum. The following reagents were used for treatments: glucose (25 mM), KCl (20 or 40 mM), nimodipine (10 μM), HNMPA-(AM)3 (20 μM), Cq (20 mM), BfA (20 μM), Mo (50 nM), Tp (100 nM), andTu (1 μM).

Immunoblotting

Whole cell extracts were prepared by direct addition of 4× sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) sample buffer (200 mM Tris-HCl, pH 6.8, 8% SDS, 400 mM dithiothreitol, 0.2% bromophenol blue, and 40% glycerol) to cells, followed by incubation at 95°C for 5 min. Samples were separated by Tris-glycine SDS-PAGE and electro-transferred to a 0.45 μm poly(vinylidene difluoride) membrane. The membranes were blocked with 2% skim milk prepared in TBS-T (TBS containing 0.05% Triton X-100) and incubated with primary antibodies at room temperature for 3 h or at 4°C overnight. After three washes with TBS-T, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h and visualized using Chemi Lumi One L reagent (Nacalai Tesque). Chemiluminescent signals were detected by LAS4000 (Fujifilm). Band intensities were quantified using ImageQuant TL (Cytiva). The primary and secondary antibodies used are listed in the key resources table.

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from MIN6 cells or mouse primary islets using TRIzol Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Primary islets of Langerhans were isolated from male C57BL/6JJcl mice (6–7 weeks of age) by a standard collagenase digestion method employing 0.5 mg/mL collagenase P (Roche Applied Science). Hand -picked islets were incubated for 18–20 h in D-MEM containing 2.5 mM glucose, 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Nacalai Tesque), and 10% fetal calf serum, and subsequently stimulated with 40 mM KCl for 4 h. Reverse transcription was performed using ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix with gDNA remover (TOYOBO). Quantitative PCR analysis was conducted using LightCycler 480 SYBR Green I Master (Roche Applied Science), and data were generated using LightCycler 96 System (Roche Applied Science). Data were analyzed with the ΔΔCt method. The sequences of oligonucleotide primers used are listed in the key resources table.

ELISA

MIN6 cells were seeded into 48-well plates in D-MEM containing 25 mM glucose supplemented with 15% fetal calf serum. Twenty-four hours prior treatment, the medium was changed to D-MEM containing 12.5 mM glucose supplemented with 15% fetal calf serum. Cells were then exposed to either 25 mM glucose for 6 h or 20 mM KCl for 1 h. The insulin C-peptide levels in the culture supernatant were quantified using an ELISA kit (Morinaga Bioscience). Total protein concentrations in cell extracts were determined using Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kits (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and were used to normalize OD at 450 nm values. Mouse blood samples were kept at room temperature for 1h and then centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatants were collected, and insulin C-peptide levels were measured using an ELISA kit (Morinaga Bioscience) as described above.

Plasmids

All plasmids were constructed using standard molecular cloning techniques, sequenced, and listed in the key resources table. For expression in MIN6 cells, the coding sequences of human TFE3 were cloned into the CMV-driven mammalian expression plasmid pCIdS.51 Substitution and deletion mutants were generated by PCR-based mutagenesis.

Cell transfection

For transfection of the MIN6 cell line, cells were seeded into 48-well plates and reverse-transfected with a total of 0.8 μg of plasmids using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Immunofluorescence

For immunostaining, MIN6 cells were seeded onto ε-poly-L-lysine-coated glass coverslips. To label transfected cells with EGFP, the dual expression plasmid pEF3/EGFPmB-CIdS/TFE3 ΔN1 (EGFP-TFE3 ΔN1) or control plasmid pEF3/EGFPmB-CIdS (EGFP-empty) was transfected. To label knockdown cells with EGFP, the dual expression plasmid for EGFP and shRNA (pEF3/EGFPmB-U6-shTfe3) or control plasmid (pEF3/EGFPmB-U6) was transfected. The target sequences for the shRNA was 5′-GCCCTTCTGGACCTACACTTTC-3’. After treatments, cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde prepared in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 15 min at room temperature and permeabilized with 0.15% Triton X-100 for 15 min at room temperature. Cells were then incubated with the indicated primary antibodies for 3 h at room temperature or at 4 °C overnight. After three washes with PBS, cells were treated with appropriate secondary antibodies and DAPI for 1 h at room temperature, and the coverslips were mounted on slides using VECTASHIELD mounting medium (Vector Laboratories, H-1000). Images were taken using a Leica DMI3000 B fluorescence microscope using Leica Application Suite (v4.2) software. All images in the same figure panel were taken under the same setting. The Golga2-positive area of individual cells and the signal intensities of Mafa and Tfe3 in individual nuclei were quantified using ImageJ (v1.52q) software. The primary and secondary antibodies used are listed in the key resources table.

Isolated mouse primary islets were fixed with 4% formaldehyde prepared in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 15 min at room temperature and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 15 min at room temperature. After blocking with 3% bovine serum albumin in PBS for 1 h at room temperature, the islets were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. They were then incubated with secondary antibodies and DAPI for 3 h at room temperature. The stained whole islets were mounted on glass slides using VECTASHIELD mounting medium (Vector Laboratories, H-1000), and images were acquired using a confocal microscope (Leica TCS SP8) with Leica Application Suite (v2.0.1.14392) software.

Luciferase reporter assay

MIN6 cells were seeded into 48-well plates and reverse transfected with 0.4 μg of the Firefly luciferase reporter plasmid, 0.2 μg of the Renilla luciferase expression plasmid phRG-TK (Promega), and 0.2 μg of the expression plasmid for Tfe3 and its derivatives (total 0.8 μg) using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were harvested 48 h after transfection, and luciferase activities were measured using the Twinlite firefly and Renilla luciferase reporter gene assay system (PerkinElmer). Firefly luciferase activities were normalized based on Renilla luciferase activities.

RNA-seq analysis

RNA was extracted from MIN6 cells using TRIzol Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A sequencing library was constructed with a NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs), and sequencing was performed using NextSeq 1000 (Illumina). Raw sequence data were converted into fastq files by bcl2fastq2 software (v2.20.0.422). The quality of raw single-ended reads was assessed with FastQC (v0.12.1). After trimming, the reads were aligned to the GRCm39 mouse reference genome using HISAT2 (version 2.2.1), and sam files were converted into bam files with Samtools (version 1.19). The bam files were used for visualization using Integrative Genomic Viewer (IGV, 2.17.1) and transcript quantification using StringTie (2.1.4). Differentially expressed genes between control and KCl-treated cells were identified using the DEseq2 program on the iDEP.96 Website. Genes with fold change (FC) > 1.5 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed. Pathway enrichment analysis was preformed using Metascape (3.5). Enriched promoter motifs were analyzed using RNAseqChef (v1.1.4), and the obtained sequence motifs were compared with known motifs using TOMTOM (v5.5.7). Volcano plot and heatmaps were generated using EnhancedVolcano (v1.24.0) and heatmap3 (v1.1.9) of R package. Gene lists (insulin secretion and lysosomes) were downloaded from the Mouse Genome Informatics database (https://www.informatics.jax.org).

ChIP-seq analysis

MIN6 cells plated on a 10 cm dish were grown in D-MEM containing 12.5 mM glucose supplemented with 15% fetal calf serum and stimulated with KCl (20 mM) for 1 h. Cells were fixed in 10% formaldehyde for 5 min at 37 °C, and the formaldehyde was quenched by adding glycine to a final concentration of 125 mM. After extensive washing with PBS, cells were lysed with sonication buffer (800 mM NaCl, 25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 5 mM EDTA pH 8.0, 0.1% SDS, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, and a proteinase inhibitor cocktail (Nacalai Tesque)) and sonicated using Bioruptor (BM Equipment) (30 cycles, 30 s ON and 30 s OFF). After centrifugation for 30 min at 15,000 rpm, the soluble chromatin fraction was subjected to immunoprecipitation with control IgG (Sigma-Aldrich) or an anti-Tfe3 (Proteintech) antibody and Dynabeads protein G (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After reversal of crosslinks and digestion with proteinase K (Nacalai Tesque) in elution buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 1 mM EDTA pH 8.0, and 1% SDS) at 42 °C for 2 h, DNA was purified using a ChIP DNA Clean and Concentration Kit (Zymo Research). From the chromatin immunoprecipitated DNA and input DNA, a sequencing library was constructed with a NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs), and sequencing was performed using NextSeq 1000 (Illumina). Raw sequence data were converted into fastq files by bcl2fastq2 software (v2.20.0.422). The quality of raw pair-ended reads was assessed with FastQC (v0.12.1). After trimming, the reads were aligned to the GRCm39 mouse reference genome using Bowtie2 (version 2.5.2), and the sam files were converted into bam files with Samtools (version 1.19). The bam files were used for visualization using IGV (2.17.1) and obtaining binding peaks using MACS2 (v2.2.9.1). Differential peaks between control and KCl-treated cells were obtained by deepTools (v.3.5.6) and the DEseq2 program of DiffBind (v3.20). Data were visualized as volcano plot using EnhancedVolcano (v1.24.0). The peak-related genes were identified by GREAT (v4.0.4). Consensus motifs of the peaks were extracted using MEME Suite (v5.5.7).

Immunohistochemistry

Pancreatic tissues of mice were fixed overnight in PBS containing 4% paraformaldehyde and processed to generate 6–8 μm-thick frozen sections. Sections were boiled in citrate buffer (0.1 N Na-citrate pH 6.0) for 5 min for antigen retrieval. After permeabilization with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 30 min at room temperature, sections were blocked with PBS containing 4% bovine serum for 1 h. Then, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After three washes with PBS-T, the sections were incubated with secondary antibodies for 3 h at room temperature. After extensive washing, sections were embedded on slides using VECTASHIELD mounting medium (Vector Laboratories, H-1000). Images were taken using a Leica DMI3000 B fluorescence microscope using Leica Application Suite (v4.2) software. Sections from each pair of mice (water vs. glucose-administrated, control vs. fasted, or db/+ vs. db/db) were simultaneously processed for staining and images were acquired under identical settings. The signal intensities of Mafa or Tfe3 in individual nuclei were quantified using ImageJ (v1.52q) software. The primary and secondary antibodies used are listed in the key resources table.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All experiments were independently reproduced at least three times, except Tfe3 staining in MIN6 cells, Mafa staining in shTfe3-transduced cells, and Tfe3 and Mafa staining in starved mouse islets, which were performed as duplicates where signals in more than 500, 30, and 500 cells were quantified per condition, respectively. ChIP-seq experiments were also performed as duplicates. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) as specified in the figure legends. Statistical significance was determined by the Student’s t test, a one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test, the Mann-Whitney U test, or the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by the Steel-Dwass test using Rstudio software. The p values and FDRs of RNA-seq and ChIP-seq data were calculated using the DEseq2 program on iDEP.96 or DiffBind. p values less than 0.05 were considered significant.
