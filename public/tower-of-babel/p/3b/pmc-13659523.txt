The hidden culprit: mesenteric venous occlusion presenting as refractory upper gastrointestinal bleeding post‑Whipple
Authors: Mucong Li, Yingfan Li, Fengdan Wang, Tao Guo, Menghua Dai, Gechong Ruan, Hong Yang
Published: 2026

A 72-year-old woman was referred for recurrent melena and hematemesis one year after a Whipple’s procedure for pancreatic adenocarcinoma (pT2N0M0, stage IB). Approximately 16 months postoperatively, she developed intermittent melena without abdominal pain. Subsequent episodes included hematochezia with a significant drop in hemoglobin requiring multiple blood transfusions. Serial contrast-enhanced abdominal computed tomography (CT) scans demonstrated progressive stenosis of the superior mesenteric vein (SMV) at its confluence with the portal vein. The SMV had been dissected and repaired during surgery for R0 resection without venous graft, and it evolved to near-occlusion (Figure 1). Extensive perigastric and mesenteric collaterals were noted (Figure 2). Upper endoscopy (including deep enteroscopy of both the afferent and efferent jejunal limbs) and colonoscopy revealed no active bleeding, varices, or tumor recurrence at the anastomoses. Serial carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA19-9) levels were normal from through 26 months postoperatively. Positron emission tomography-CT showed no hypermetabolic lesions suggesting malignancy. Therefore, mesenteric venous hypertension was considered the likely cause of upper gastrointestinal bleeding, probably from ectopic varices at the bilio-enteric anastomosis secondary to progressive postoperative SMV stenosis. SMV recanalization was not performed because it would require anticoagulation, which was contraindicated due to active bleeding. Instead, the patient received a continuous course of a non-selective beta-blocker for portal hypertension from 19 months postoperatively until the latest follow up at 23 months postoperatively, experiencing only one episode of 2-day melena during treatment.

Regional portal hypertension is a recognized but frequently overlooked cause of major gastrointestinal bleeding after pancreaticoduodenectomy, usually resulting from stenosis or thrombosis of the SMV or portal vein (1-3). This case highlights the diagnostic value of sequential CT imaging in visualizing progressive postoperative SMV narrowing, which is central to understanding the underlying pathophysiology. It further emphasizes that the diagnostic approach to upper gastrointestinal bleeding in this postoperative setting must look beyond common causes such as anastomotic ulcer or tumor recurrence (4,5).
