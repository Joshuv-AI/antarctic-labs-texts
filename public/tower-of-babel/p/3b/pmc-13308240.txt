Reply
Authors: Jieun Lee, Thanh-Huyen T Vu, Kunihiro Matsushita
Published: 2026

We appreciate Dr Adebisi and colleagues for acknowledging our recognition of several limitations of our study:1 the limited number of e-cigarette users, particularly in some subgroups (eg, older adults), the absence of time-varying exposure assessment, limited detail on e-cigarette use (eg, frequency), and potential residual confounding from combustible cigarette use. Among a few other points pointed by Dr Adebisi and colleagues, dose-response relationships, discrepancies between prevalent and incident analyses, and subgroup analyses by age group deserve some discussion.

First, we observed a few potentially important dose-responsive relationships. After accounting for potential confounders, current e-cigarette users showed the highest HR of developing hypertension, followed by former e-cigarette users. In addition, dual ever users of e-cigarette and combustible cigarette had the highest corresponding HR, followed by exclusive ever e-cigarette users and then exclusive ever combustible cigarette users, although the latter 2 groups did not reach statistical significance. This observation is consistent with some studies showing that the impact of exclusive e-cigarette use on blood pressure may be similar to that of combustible cigarette use.2, 3, 4

Second, regarding the discrepancy between analyses of prevalent vs incident hypertension as outcome variables, in general, the incident analysis is preferred in epidemiological research, as prevalent analyses have important caveats, such as ambiguity of temporality and potentially mingling reverse causation (eg, those with higher blood pressure may be more likely to quit using e-cigarette in our case). Given the differences in baseline characteristics between ever e-cigarette users vs and never users (eg, younger age and fewer women), we should prioritize the multivariable-adjusted estimates for the associations of e-cigarette use with incident hypertension.

Third, although nicotine-related mechanisms might be expected to have equal or greater effects in older adults, the apparent age-related difference may reflect heterogeneity in exposure patterns rather than a contradiction of biological mechanisms. For example, e-cigarette use in younger adults may more often represent primary exposure, whereas in older adults it may more frequently reflect smoking reduction or cessation. In addition, because our analytic sample was restricted to individuals free of hypertension at baseline, older participants may represent a relatively healthier subset, which could attenuate observed associations.

Overall, our data seem to have important signals cautioning against the notion of e-cigarettes as a “healthy” alternative to combustible cigarettes.5 Nevertheless, we are in agreement with Dr Adebisi and colleagues that there is a need for future large-scale prospective studies with detailed exposure assessment of e-cigarette and combustible cigarette and extended follow-up of hypertension occurrence.
