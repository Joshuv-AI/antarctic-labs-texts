Cuproptosis-associated PDHA1 promotes sarcoma progression and immunotherapy responsiveness via the E2F1–PD-L1 axis: a multi-omics and clinical validation study
Authors: Haotian Qin, Tiantian Qi, Nan Yao, Weibei Sheng, Jinhao Deng, Junyu Qian, Deli Wang, Hui Zeng, Jian Weng, Jun Yang, Fei Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sarcomas are aggressive, immunologically cold tumors with limited benefit from immune-checkpoint blockade (ICB). Through integrated multi-omics, functional, and clinical analyses, we identify pyruvate dehydrogenase alpha 1 (PDHA1)—a cuproptosis-linked metabolic gene—as a driver of sarcoma progression and immune evasion. PDHA1 is consistently overexpressed across TCGA/GEO/ICGC cohorts and associates with poor prognosis, stromal activation, and reduced immune scores; single-cell RNA-seq of the immune compartment shows PDHA1 expression across multiple immune populations, with higher levels in T cells and monocytes/dendritic cells. PDHA1 knockdown diminishes proliferation, invasion, clonogenicity, and PD-L1 levels while increasing apoptosis. Mechanistically, PDHA1 elevates E2F1, which binds and transactivates the PD-L1 promoter; rescue assays confirm E2F1-dependent PD-L1 induction. Copper chelation with tetrathiomolybdate lowers lipoylated DLAT and suppresses the PDHA1–E2F1–PD-L1 axis. In 3D spheroids, xenografts, and multiplex immunofluorescence, high PDHA1 aligns with larger tumors, higher Ki-67/BCL-2, lower cleaved caspase-3, increased PD-L1, and reduced CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration. PDHA1 hypomethylation correlates with worse survival. PDHA1 status also modulates sensitivity to phenformin and the E2F1 pathway inhibitor NSC-207895. Collectively, PDHA1 orchestrates a cuproptosis-associated E2F1–PD-L1 program that promotes immune exclusion yet predicts ICB responsiveness, supporting PDHA1 as a clinically actionable biomarker and metabolic–immunologic target in sarcoma.

Introduction

Sarcomas are a rare and heterogeneous group of malignant tumors originating from mesenchymal tissues such as bone, cartilage, muscle, adipose, and connective tissue1. Despite accounting for less than 1% of all adult solid malignancies, sarcomas pose considerable therapeutic and diagnostic challenges due to their vast histological diversity, complex genomic landscape, and aggressive biological behavior2,3. While localized sarcomas may be effectively managed by surgery with or without adjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy, therapeutic options for patients with recurrent, unresectable, or metastatic disease remain largely ineffective4,5. The overall five-year survival rate for advanced-stage sarcomas remains dismal, falling below 20% in most cohorts6. This highlights the urgent need to identify reliable molecular biomarkers and therapeutic targets that can inform prognosis, enable risk stratification, and guide precision therapies tailored to the tumor’s intrinsic biology.

The advent of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), particularly agents targeting programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte antigen 4 (CTLA-4), has revolutionized cancer treatment by harnessing the immune system to eradicate tumors7. However, the benefits of ICIs have not translated well to sarcomas. Most sarcoma subtypes are characterized by an “immune-cold” tumor microenvironment (TME), featuring low tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte density, high stromal content, low mutational burden, and weak expression of antigen presentation and costimulatory molecules8–10. As a result, only a small fraction of sarcoma patients respond to ICI therapy, and durable responses are rare11. These limitations underscore the need to uncover tumor-intrinsic molecular drivers of immune exclusion and dysfunction that may serve both as therapeutic targets and as predictive biomarkers of immunotherapy response.

Pyruvate dehydrogenase alpha 1 (PDHA1), a key component of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDC), plays a central role in mitochondrial metabolism by linking glycolysis to the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle12. In recent years, PDHA1 has been implicated in a variety of cancer-related processes, including metabolic reprogramming, redox regulation, and epigenetic remodeling13,14. Furthermore, PDHA1 has been associated with a novel form of regulated cell death termed “cuproptosis”-a copper-dependent, TCA cycle-linked mechanism of proteotoxicity15,16. While cuproptosis-related genes have garnered attention as metabolic vulnerabilities in cancer, the role of PDHA1 in shaping the tumor immune landscape remains poorly understood. Preliminary studies in other malignancies suggest that PDHA1 may modulate immune escape mechanism17,18, but its relevance in sarcoma, particularly in relation to immune evasion and immunotherapy sensitivity, has not been systematically explored.

To address this gap, we performed an integrated multi-omics and functional investigation of PDHA1 in sarcoma. Through bulk RNA sequencing, single-cell transcriptomic profiling, DNA methylation and somatic mutation analyses, and multi-cohort clinical validation, we identified PDHA1 as significantly overexpressed in sarcomas and closely associated with poor prognosis, immune exclusion, and PD-L1 upregulation. Single-cell analysis of the immune compartment further characterized PDHA1 expression across infiltrating immune populations. Functional experiments demonstrated that PDHA1 knockdown impairs sarcoma cell proliferation, invasion, and clonogenicity, while downregulating E2F1 and PD-L1 expression. In vivo, PDHA1 silencing suppressed tumor growth and enhanced immune infiltration in xenograft models. Clinical validation further linked high PDHA1 expression with adverse outcomes and immunosuppressive phenotypes. Collectively, these findings establish PDHA1 as a critical driver of sarcoma progression and immune evasion via the E2F1–PD-L1 axis, highlighting its potential as both a prognostic biomarker and a therapeutic target for precision immunotherapy. Results

Identification and analysis of CRG-related clusters in SARC

Twelve cuproptosis-related genes (CRGs), including FDX1, LIAS, LIPT1, DLD, DLAT, PDHA1, PDHB, MTF1, GLS, CDKN2A, SLC31A1, and ATP7B, were identified through comprehensive literature review. Based on the expression profiles of these CRGs in TCGA-SARC samples, unsupervised consensus clustering was performed to categorize the sarcoma cohort into k clusters (k = 2–6). According to the consensus clustering results, k = 2 was selected as the optimal number of clusters, effectively stratifying SARC patients into two distinct molecular subtypes: Cluster 1 (C1, n = 209) and Cluster 2 (C2, n = 51) (Fig. 2A–D). Although the delta area showed a larger increase at k = 3, we selected k = 2 based on the overall stability of the consensus matrix and to avoid over-fragmentation of the cohort into smaller clusters with limited statistical power. We next compared the expression of the 12 CRGs between the C1 and C2 subtypes. Most genes showed significant differential expression between the two groups (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2E). As shown in Fig. 2F, protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis revealed PDHA1 as a central hub gene, interacting with multiple other CRGs and immunogenic cell death (ICD)-related genes. In addition, we examined the pairwise expression correlations among the 12 CRGs in SARC. The majority of gene pairs exhibited significant positive correlations, as demonstrated by Pearson correlation analysis (Fig. 2G). DEGs and functional enrichment analysis

Based on the expression pattern and prognostic significance of cuproptosis-related genes, PDHA1 was identified as a key gene for further investigation in the context of SARC progression. To elucidate its potential role, SARC patients were stratified into PDHA1-high and PDHA1-low expression groups. A total of 875 DEGs were identified between the two groups, including 317 upregulated and 558 downregulated genes (p < 0.05), as visualized in the volcano plot (Fig. S1A). A heatmap of the top 10 genes significantly correlated (positively or negatively) with PDHA1 is shown in Fig. S1B. Additionally, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) further demonstrated that PDHA1-associated genes were significantly enriched in pathways related to E2F signaling, regulation of PLK1 activity at G2/M transition, DNA repair, cell cycle progression, and TP53 regulation through phosphorylation (Fig. S1C). These pathways are known to contribute to tumor initiation and progression, suggesting a potential oncogenic role of PDHA1 in sarcoma.

Prognostic value and gene expression analysis

We performed univariate Cox regression analysis to evaluate the prognostic value of cuproptosis-related genes (CRGs) in sarcoma (SARC). Among them, PDHA1 was identified as a potential risk factor for both overall survival (OS) and disease-specific survival (DSS), as illustrated by the forest plot (Fig. S2). To further verify the clinical relevance of PDHA1, we examined its expression and diagnostic efficacy across the GEO, TARGET, and ICGC datasets. PDHA1 expression was significantly elevated in the high-expression group compared to the low-expression group in all three datasets (Fig. 3A). The area under the ROC curve (AUC) values for PDHA1 in these datasets were 0.873, 1.000, and 1.000, respectively (Fig. 3B), indicating strong diagnostic performance. In the TCGA-SARC cohort, patients with higher PDHA1 expression levels exhibited a higher risk score and a greater probability of death, as well as reduced survival times (Fig. 3C). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis confirmed that patients in the high-risk group had significantly worse OS (HR = 1.68, p = 0.010) than those in the low-risk group (Fig. 3D). The time-dependent ROC analysis showed AUC values of 0.663, 0.604, and 0.627 for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS, respectively (Fig. 3E). DSS analysis also indicated poorer outcomes in the high PDHA1 expression group (HR = 1.63, p = 0.030) (Fig. 3F). These results were further validated in independent datasets GSE21257 (n = 53) and GSE17674 (n = 44), which demonstrated that high PDHA1 expression was associated with significantly shorter OS (Fig. 3G, H). Clinical correlation analysis

We further investigated the association between PDHA1 expression and clinical-pathological features through subgroup survival analysis. High PDHA1 expression was significantly associated with shorter overall survival in patients over 60 years old (p = 0.024), patients aged 60 or younger (p = 0.014), females (p = 0.009), males (p = 0.026), White patients (p = 0.042), those who did not receive radiation therapy (p = 0.028), patients with positive surgical margins (p = 0.013), tumors located in deep tissue (p = 0.042), and patients with non-multifocal tumors (p = 0.018). In contrast, no statistically significant association was observed between PDHA1 expression and survival in subgroups such as Asian or Black patients, those receiving radiation therapy, patients with negative margins, R0/R1 residual tumors, multifocal tumors, varying degrees of necrosis, or different metastasis statuses (Fig. S3). These results suggest that PDHA1, in conjunction with certain clinical characteristics, may serve as a valuable marker for risk stratification in SARC.

Establishment and validation of a predictive Nomogram

To enhance clinical applicability, we constructed a prognostic nomogram by integrating PDHA1 expression with other survival-related clinical features. Univariate Cox regression analysis showed that age, residual tumor, metastasis, tumor multifocality, margin status, and PDHA1 were significantly associated with OS in SARC. Multivariate analysis further confirmed that age (p < 0.001), residual tumor (p < 0.001), metastasis (p < 0.001), margin status (p = 0.029), and PDHA1 (p = 0.034) were independent predictors of OS (Table 1). These five variables were used to construct a nomogram for predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS in SARC patients (Fig. 4A). The nomogram demonstrated robust predictive ability, with a concordance index (C-index) of 0.780 (95% CI: 0.743–0.816), and calibration curves showed excellent agreement between predicted and observed survival probabilities (Fig. 4B). ROC curve analysis further validated the model’s performance, with AUC values of 0.767, 0.794, and 0.805 for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival, respectively (Fig. 4C). The time-dependent AUC curve indicated consistent predictive capability over time (Fig. 4D), and decision curve analysis (DCA) demonstrated that the nomogram had considerable clinical utility for survival prediction in the TCGA-SARC cohort (Fig. 4E). In addition, we evaluated the generalizability of the PDHA1-based prognostic model in independent external cohorts. In the combined GSE21257 and GSE17679 datasets, the ROC curves yielded AUC values of 0.621, 0.756, and 0.725 for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS, respectively (Fig. S4A). Based on the TARGET cohort, the corresponding AUCs were 0.629, 0.647, and 0.692 (Fig. S4B). Correlation analysis of genetic alterations

Genomic alterations of cuproptosis-related genes (CRGs) in sarcoma (SARC) were analyzed using the GSCA database. Copy-number variation (CNV) and DNA methylation are known to significantly impact gene expression and clinical outcomes. We therefore assessed the correlations between CRG expression and both CNV and methylation levels. Overall, CNV showed a positive correlation with mRNA expression, whereas DNA methylation was negatively correlated with mRNA expression (Fig. S5A). Moreover, CNV and methylation levels of several CRGs were significantly associated with poor prognosis in SARC patients, including overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), disease-free interval (DFI), and disease-specific survival (DSS) (Fig. S5B). The CNV landscape of all 12 CRGs in the SARC cohort is shown in Fig. S5C, with high-frequency heterozygous deletions and amplifications observed across the panel (Fig. S5D). In particular, PDHA1 and DLD exhibited high-level homozygous amplification, whereas CDKN2A and FDX1 showed high-level homozygous deletions, indicating substantial genomic instability at these loci. The detailed distribution of CNV events in PDHA1 among SARC tumor samples is presented in Fig. S5E, and PDHA1 copy-number gain or loss was positively correlated with its mRNA expression (Spearman r = 0.45, P < 0.01; Fig. S5F). Given the central role of the TP53–MDM2 axis in cuproptosis regulation, we further examined the association between PDHA1 and TP53/MDM2 expression in TCGA-SARC. PDHA1 expression showed modest but significant negative correlations with both TP53 (r = −0.180, P < 0.001) and MDM2 (r = −0.299, P < 0.001) (Fig. S6), suggesting that disruption of the TP53–MDM2 pathway may be accompanied by compensatory metabolic rewiring involving PDHA1 and other CRGs.

DNA Methylation and RNA Modification Analysis

Using the GSCA platform, we observed a significant negative correlation between PDHA1 expression and its DNA methylation levels in SARC (Fig. S7A). DNA methylation profiling from the MethSurv database identified seven CpG sites within the PDHA1 gene region, with most sites showing hypomethylation in tumor samples compared to normal tissue (Fig. S7B). Among them, methylation at cg12175122 was significantly associated with poor prognosis in SARC patients (Fig. S7C), suggesting that epigenetic silencing of PDHA1 may be linked to sarcoma progression. To further investigate epigenetic regulation, we examined the expression of pan-RNA modification-related genes between PDHA1 high and low-expression groups. Significant upregulation of m⁶A, m⁷G, m⁵C, and m¹A-related genes was detected in the high PDHA1 group (P < 0.01) (Fig. S7D). Correlation analysis in the TCGA-SARC cohort also revealed strong positive associations between PDHA1 expression and these RNA modification regulators (Fig. S7E). Moreover, these modification-related genes were significantly associated with survival outcomes (Fig. S7F). These findings suggest that PDHA1 expression is tightly linked to both DNA and RNA epigenetic landscapes in sarcoma.

Immune infiltration analysis

Based on CIBERSORT and ssGSEA algorithms, we first assessed the association between PDHA1 expression and immune infiltration across pan-cancer cohorts. Heatmap visualization showed that PDHA1 expression was broadly correlated with the infiltration levels of multiple immune cell subsets in most tumor types (Fig. 5A). In the TCGA-SARC cohort, CIBERSORT revealed marked differences in immune cell composition between PDHA1 high- and low-expression groups. Naïve B cells and M2 macrophages were significantly enriched in the low PDHA1 group, whereas M0 macrophages were more abundant in the high PDHA1 group (Fig. 5B). Correlation analysis further showed that PDHA1 expression was positively associated with memory B cells and M0 macrophages, but negatively associated with resting mast cells, M1 macrophages, monocytes, plasma cells, naïve B cells, neutrophils, and M2 macrophages (Fig. 5C). Consistent results were obtained using ssGSEA. The high PDHA1 group exhibited increased infiltration of aDCs, B cells, CD8⁺ T cells, cytotoxic cells, DCs, eosinophils, iDCs, macrophages, mast cells, neutrophils, NK CD56^dim cells, pDCs, total NK cells, total T cells, TFH, Tem, Th1 cells, and Th17 cells, whereas Th2 cells were significantly enriched in the low PDHA1 group (Fig. 5D). Correspondingly, PDHA1 expression was positively correlated with eosinophils, DCs, mast cells, pDCs, cytotoxic cells, total B cells, and Th1 cells, and negatively correlated with Th2 cells (Fig. 5E). ESTIMATE analysis further indicated a significant negative correlation between PDHA1 expression and ImmuneScore, StromalScore, and ESTIMATEscore (all P < 0.001), and all three scores were significantly higher in the low PDHA1 group than in the high PDHA1 group (P < 0.001; Fig. 5F), suggesting that high PDHA1 expression is associated with a less inflamed and less stromal-rich tumor microenvironment. Survival analysis showed that the infiltration levels of several immune subsets, including aDCs, B cells, cytotoxic cells, DCs, eosinophils, iDCs, mast cells, NK cells, pDCs, total T cells, Th2 cells, and Th17 cells, were significantly associated with overall survival (Fig. S8). Potential value of PDHA1 in immunotherapy of SARC

To explore the immunotherapeutic relevance of PDHA1, we first examined the association between PDHA1 expression and immune checkpoint genes across 33 tumor types. Pan-cancer correlation analysis showed that PDHA1 expression was positively correlated with immune checkpoint signatures in THCA, TGCT, LUSC, LGG, COAD, SKCM, and GBM, but negatively correlated in UVM, BLCA, and ACC (Fig. S9A). In the TCGA-SARC cohort, we then compared the expression of eight immune checkpoint–related genes between PDHA1 high- and low-expression groups. Significant differences were observed for HAVCR2 (P < 0.001), TIGIT (P < 0.01), and SIGLEC15 (P < 0.01) (Fig. S9B). Correlation analysis further revealed that PDHA1 was negatively associated with HAVCR2 (Cor = −0.2333, P < 0.001), PDCD1 (Cor = −0.1371, P = 0.0271), and TIGIT (Cor = −0.1955, P = 0.0015), whereas it was positively correlated with SIGLEC15 (Cor = 0.1770, P = 0.0042) (Fig. S9C). Using the TIDE algorithm, we next predicted the response to immune checkpoint blockade. Patients in the high PDHA1 group exhibited a higher predicted response rate to immunotherapy (Fig. S9D). Consistently, the high-PDHA1 (high-risk) group showed a lower overall TIDE score, reduced dysfunction score, and higher exclusion score (Fig. S9E), suggesting that although PDHA1-high tumors are more immune-excluded, they may still be more sensitive to checkpoint inhibition. We further validated the predictive value of PDHA1 in a real-world cohort of 32 advanced sarcoma patients treated with anti–PD-1/PD-L1 therapy. Clinical benefit (CR/PR) was enriched in the PDHA1-high group, and ROC curve analysis confirmed the accuracy of PDHA1 expression in discriminating responders from non-responders (Fig. S9F). In addition, analysis of the TCGA-SARC dataset showed that PDHA1 expression was higher in leiomyosarcoma and pleomorphic sarcoma, but lower in dedifferentiated liposarcoma (Fig. S9G). Collectively, these findings indicate that patients with high PDHA1 expression are more likely to benefit from immune checkpoint blockade.

TMB, MSI, mRNAsi, and MMR analysis

To further investigate the relationship between PDHA1 and genomic features relevant to immune response, we analyzed tumor mutational burden (TMB), microsatellite instability (MSI), and mRNA stemness index (mRNAsi) in the TCGA-SARC cohort. The levels of TMB, MSI, and mRNAsi were all significantly higher in the high PDHA1 group than in the low PDHA1 group (P < 0.05; Fig. S10A).

Kaplan–Meier analysis integrating PDHA1 expression and these genomic features revealed strong prognostic interactions. Patients with low TMB and low PDHA1 had the best overall survival, whereas those with high TMB and high PDHA1 showed significantly worse outcomes (P = 0.029). Similarly, the combination of high MSI and high PDHA1 expression was associated with poor survival (P = 0.014). In terms of stemness, the low mRNAsi + low PDHA1 subgroup exhibited better prognosis than the high mRNAsi + high PDHA1 subgroup (P = 0.005) (Fig. S10B). Moreover, the expression of DNA mismatch repair (MMR) genes was significantly higher in the PDHA1-high group than in the PDHA1-low group (Fig. S10C), suggesting that PDHA1 may be involved in regulating genomic stability and mutational processes in SARC. Together, these results support a close link between PDHA1 expression, mutational/instability burden, and immunotherapy-relevant genomic features.

Drug sensitivity analysis

To explore the therapeutic vulnerability associated with PDHA1, we first analyzed the correlation between PDHA1 mRNA expression and drug sensitivity using the GDSC and CTRP datasets. Multiple agents, including 17-AAG, phenformin, Dinaciclib, NSC-207895, and 5-fluorouracil, showed significant associations with PDHA1 expression (FDR < 0.05), suggesting that PDHA1 status may influence response to both metabolic and cell-cycle–targeting drugs (Fig. S11A). When samples were stratified into PDHA1-high and PDHA1-low groups, the PDHA1-high group exhibited significantly lower predicted IC50 values for phenformin, Dinaciclib, NSC-207895, and 5-fluorouracil (Fig. S11B), indicating enhanced in silico sensitivity. Molecular docking further showed that phenformin, Dinaciclib, NSC-207895, and 5-fluorouracil could all bind to PDHA1 with binding energies < -5.0 kcal/mol through key residues such as R259, Y198, and E347 (Fig. S11C), supporting a potential direct interaction between these compounds and PDHA1. To functionally validate these predictions, we performed CCK-8 assays in MG63 cells with high (sh-NC) and low PDHA1 expression (sh-PDHA1). PDHA1 knockdown markedly increased the sensitivity of sarcoma cells to phenformin (0–10 mM) and to the E2F1 inhibitor NSC-207895 (0–10 μM), as reflected by significantly reduced cell viability at higher drug concentrations in sh-PDHA1 cells compared with sh-NC controls (Fig. S12A, B; ***P < 0.001). These results indicate that modulation of PDHA1 profoundly alters the response of sarcoma cells to metabolic and E2F1-targeted therapies and support the notion that PDHA1 expression may serve as a useful biomarker to guide personalized drug selection and combination strategies in SARC.

Clinical and in vitro validation of PDHA1 rxpression

In a clinical cohort of 60 paired sarcoma and adjacent normal tissues, PDHA1 was markedly upregulated in tumor samples (P < 0.001; Fig. 7A). Western blotting further confirmed increased protein levels of PDHA1, PD-L1, and E2F1 in tumor specimens compared with matched normal tissues (Fig. 7B, C). Immunohistochemistry in liposarcoma and synovial sarcoma samples consistently demonstrated higher expression of PDHA1, PD-L1, E2F1, and Ki-67 in tumor regions than in adjacent normal areas (Fig. 7D, E), supporting their association with tumor proliferation and immune checkpoint activation. Based on this clinical tissue cohort, we constructed a PDHA1-centered (cuproptosis-related gene, CRG-based) prognostic nomogram, which achieved a concordance index (C-index) of 0.826 (95% CI: 0.793–0.860) (Fig. 7F). Survival stratification showed that patients with high PDHA1 expression had significantly shorter overall survival than those with low PDHA1 expression (P = 0.029, HR = 2.31, 95% CI: 1.09–4.91; Fig. 7G). ROC curve analysis indicated strong prognostic accuracy, with AUCs of 0.836, 0.885, and 0.933 for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS, respectively (Fig. 7H). Time-dependent AUC and decision curve analysis further demonstrated the stable predictive performance and clinical utility of this PDHA1-based model (Fig. 7I, J). To further establish the regulatory hierarchy, we performed rescue experiments. PDHA1 knockdown markedly decreased E2F1 and PD-L1 protein levels, whereas ectopic E2F1 overexpression restored PD-L1 expression despite PDHA1 silencing (Fig. 10D, E). Flow cytometry showed that PDHA1 knockdown significantly increased apoptosis, which was partially reversed by E2F1 restoration (Fig. 10F, G), suggesting that PDHA1 promotes cell survival via the E2F1–PD-L1 axis.

Copper chelation with TTM, which inhibits cuproptosis pathway activation, provided additional support for this mechanism. TTM treatment reduced Lip-DLAT levels and suppressed PD-L1 expression, and PDHA1 knockdown exerted similar effects. Combined TTM treatment and PDHA1 silencing induced the most pronounced decrease in E2F1 and PD-L1 expression (Fig. 10H, I), indicating that PDHA1 regulates PD-L1 through a cuproptosis-associated E2F1 transcriptional program.

To validate the in vitro findings in patient samples, multiplex immunofluorescence staining of clinical sarcoma tissues revealed that tumors with high PDHA1 expression exhibited markedly elevated PD-L1 levels and reduced CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration, whereas low-PDHA1 tumors showed decreased PD-L1 expression and abundant CD8⁺ T cells (Fig. 10J, K). A schematic model summarizes this mechanism, in which PDHA1 activates E2F1, enabling E2F1 binding to the PD-L1 promoter and driving its transcription, thereby promoting immune evasion by attenuating CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration (Fig. 10L).

Discussion

In this study, we identify PDHA1 as a clinically relevant oncogenic and immunoregulatory factor in sarcoma. Through integrative analysis of bulk transcriptomics, DNA methylation, single-cell RNA sequencing, and in vitro/in vivo functional assays, we demonstrate that PDHA1 is significantly upregulated in sarcoma and contributes to tumor progression, immune evasion, and heterogeneity of immunotherapy response. Our data indicate that PDHA1 promotes sarcoma growth through both cell-intrinsic and tumor microenvironmental mechanisms, supporting its candidacy as a prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target for precision immuno-oncology.

PDHA1 is a key subunit of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDC), catalyzing the conversion of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA and thereby linking glycolysis to the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS). Its role as a central metabolic hub enables it to regulate ATP production, redox balance, and anabolic biosynthesis, as well as to integrate metabolic cues with signal transduction networks19,20. In particular, PDHA1 controls metabolic flux into the TCA cycle and can influence the epigenetic state of cancer cells via acetyl-CoA–dependent histone acetylation21,22. Recent work has further implicated PDHA1 and other lipoylated TCA cycle components in cuproptosis, a copper-triggered form of regulated cell death characterized by aggregation of lipoylated proteins and proteotoxic stress15,16. In lung and liver cancers, cuproptosis regulators such as FDX1, DLAT, and PDHA1 have been linked to tumor proliferation and immune escape23,24. Consistent with these observations, our data show that copper chelation with tetrathiomolybdate (TTM) reduces lipoylated DLAT (Lip-DLAT) levels and downregulates E2F1 and PD-L1 in PDHA1-high sarcoma cells, while PDHA1 knockdown alone produces similar suppressive effects. Combined TTM treatment and PDHA1 silencing induce the most pronounced decrease in E2F1 and PD-L1 expression, suggesting that part of the PDHA1–E2F1–PD-L1 axis is copper- and lipoylation-dependent. Although we did not systematically quantify canonical cuproptosis endpoints (e.g., iron–sulfur cluster destabilization, proteotoxic aggregates), these findings support a mechanistic intersection between cuproptosis-related metabolism and immune checkpoint regulation in sarcoma.

Mechanistically, we identify PDHA1 as an upstream regulator of the E2F1–PD-L1 axis, both of which are implicated in immune modulation. E2F1 is a well-established transcriptional driver of cell-cycle progression and tumor aggressiveness, but has also been shown to directly regulate the PD-L1 promoter in several cancer types, including hepatocellular carcinoma and triple-negative breast cancer25,26. Elevated PDHA1 levels led to increased E2F1 and PD-L1 expression in sarcoma cells, supporting the existence of a PDHA1–E2F1–PD-L1 axis. This is in line with recent reports in melanoma and liver cancer in which metabolic rewiring and altered TCA flux promote PD-L1 upregulation and immune escape27. By interrogating public E2F1 ChIP-seq datasets and performing ChIP–qPCR in sarcoma cells, we confirmed robust E2F1 binding at the CD274 (PD-L1) promoter. Luciferase reporter assays demonstrated that E2F1 markedly increased the activity of wild-type PD-L1 promoter constructs, but not mutants lacking E2F1-binding sites, indicating direct transcriptional activation. Rescue experiments further showed that ectopic E2F1 expression restored PD-L1 and partially rescued cell survival in PDHA1-knockdown cells. Together, these data establish E2F1 as a key transcriptional mediator of PDHA1-driven PD-L1 induction and immune evasion.

Functionally, our in vitro results demonstrated that silencing PDHA1 significantly suppresses sarcoma cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and clonogenic growth. These findings align with prior studies in hepatocellular carcinoma and other solid tumors, where PDHA1 knockdown or pharmacologic inhibition reduced colony formation, anchorage-independent growth, and tumorigenic potential, suggesting a conserved oncogenic function28. In our in vivo models, tumors derived from PDHA1-knockdown cells exhibited markedly reduced growth and tumor burden, alongside disorganized architecture and prominent necrosis. Histological and molecular analyses revealed a notable increase in apoptotic activity, as evidenced by elevated TUNEL-positive cells and cleaved Caspase-3 expression. Cleaved Caspase-3, an essential executor of intrinsic mitochondrial apoptosis, reflects irreversible engagement of the cell death cascade and has been widely validated as a robust biomarker of apoptotic response29. Simultaneously, key survival and proliferation regulators, including Bcl-2 and Cyclin D1, were significantly downregulated, reinforcing the role of PDHA1 in maintaining tumor cell viability and cell-cycle progression. These findings suggest that PDHA1 promotes sarcoma progression at least in part by protecting tumor cells from mitochondrial stress-induced apoptosis and sustaining proliferative signaling.

Beyond cell-intrinsic effects, our data indicate that PDHA1 contributes to the establishment of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME). Silencing PDHA1 in vivo not only attenuated tumor growth but also enhanced CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration and sensitized tumors to anti-PD-1 therapy, supporting a mechanistic link between metabolic regulation and immunotherapy responsiveness. Prior work has shown that targeting mitochondrial metabolism—such as by inhibiting pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase (PDK) with dichloroacetate or blocking glutaminase activity—can modulate T-cell function and synergize with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) in preclinical models30–33. Our results complement these observations by positioning PDHA1 as a pivotal metabolic–immunologic hub in sarcoma, in which modulation of PDC activity intersects with E2F1-driven immune checkpoint expression and T-cell exclusion.

PDHA1 also appears to influence the TME beyond malignant cells. Integrative analyses combining bulk RNA-seq deconvolution, stromal activation signatures, and single-cell transcriptomics revealed high PDHA1 expression in malignant tumor cells and CAF-like stromal populations, as well as in the immune compartment—particularly CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and myeloid populations (monocytes and dendritic cells). CAFs are key components of the stromal niche that can suppress immune responses by secreting TGF-β, CXCL12, and other immunomodulatory factors, remodeling the extracellular matrix, and physically excluding lymphocytes from tumor nests34,35. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, CAF-derived lactate has been shown to reinforce fibrosis and immune suppression, further limiting effector T-cell access36,37. In our cohort, PDHA1-high sarcomas were enriched in CAF/ECM signatures and displayed reduced CD8⁺ T-cell and B-cell/tertiary lymphoid structure (TLS) signatures, consistent with an “immune-excluded” phenotype, which is in line with prior work showing that B-cell/TLS-rich sarcomas exhibit improved survival and better response to immune checkpoint blockade and that TLS-associated B cells shape clinically relevant T-cell phenotypes in solid tumors38–40. Although the precise cell type–specific functions of PDHA1 in CAF-like and DC-like compartments remain to be determined, these findings raise the possibility that PDHA1 contributes to non–cell-autonomous immune regulation by shaping stromal metabolism, cytokine production, and antigen-presentation quality.

An important conceptual challenge raised by our data is the apparent paradox that PDHA1 overexpression in sarcoma is associated with poor clinical prognosis yet also predicts a favorable response to immunotherapy. This duality can be reconciled by considering the layered metabolic–immune effects of PDHA1. As a rate-limiting component of the PDC, PDHA1 enhances mitochondrial OXPHOS and anabolic biosynthesis, fueling rapid tumor proliferation and invasion and thereby explaining the unfavorable survival outcomes in PDHA1-high patients who do not receive immunotherapy. At the same time, PDHA1-driven metabolic reprogramming depletes glucose and glutamine in the TME, impairs T-cell metabolic fitness, and promotes accumulation of lactate and reactive oxygen species (ROS), which directly suppress cytotoxic T-cell activity and promote exhaustion41,42. This metabolic competition and oxidative stress contribute to an immune-cold baseline state characterized by low CD8⁺ T-cell and B-cell/TLS infiltration, poor spontaneous anti-tumor immunity, and adverse prognosis. This apparent discrepancy reflects bulk deconvolution artifacts in stromal-rich sarcomas versus spatial exclusion observed in multiplex immunofluorescence, consistent with the higher TIDE exclusion score in PDHA1-high tumors.

Paradoxically, PDHA1-high tumors in our study also exhibited elevated tumor mutational burden (TMB), microsatellite instability (MSI), and mismatch repair (MMR) gene expression, together with strong upregulation of immune checkpoint molecules such as PD-L1, CTLA-4, TIGIT, and SIGLEC15. These features are typically associated with high neoantigen load and increased sensitivity to ICIs. We propose that PDHA1-high sarcomas represent “immune-excluded but immunogenically primed” tumors in which oncogenic metabolism and stromal remodeling dominate at baseline, but abundant neoantigens and high checkpoint expression create latent vulnerability to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade once immunotherapy is applied. In this framework, the negative correlation between PDHA1 and B-cell/TLS signatures explains poor survival in the absence of treatment, whereas high TMB/MSI and immune checkpoint expression underlie improved benefit under ICI exposure. Similar phenomena have been described in other immune-cold malignancies, where tumor-intrinsic metabolic and epigenetic drivers shape an immune-excluded TME despite high mutational load43–45. These insights also suggest that combining metabolic interventions or TGF-β/CAF-targeted strategies with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors may help convert PDHA1-high “cold” sarcomas into immune-inflamed tumors and improve immunotherapy efficacy.

Our epigenetic analyses add another layer to PDHA1 regulation. We observed a significant negative correlation between PDHA1 expression and DNA methylation across the PDHA1 locus, and identified specific CpG sites whose hypomethylation is associated with poor prognosis. PDHA1-high tumors also exhibited elevated mRNAsi scores and upregulation of multiple RNA modification regulators (m⁶A, m⁷G, m⁵C, m¹A), which were themselves linked to adverse survival. These findings support the notion that PDHA1 may contribute to immune escape and therapy resistance via dedifferentiation and epigenetic reprogramming. Prior studies in glioma, renal cancer, and other solid tumors have shown that promoter demethylation and altered epigenetic marks on metabolic genes foster stem-like, immune-suppressive phenotypes46–49. Future work should investigate whether PDHA1 directly modulates histone acetylation, chromatin accessibility, or RNA modification landscapes to promote tumor plasticity, and whether reversing these epigenetic changes can restore immune sensitivity.

Clinically, PDHA1 holds strong promise as a biomarker for sarcoma prognosis and treatment response. Our multivariate Cox analyses confirmed PDHA1 as an independent predictor of survival, and incorporating PDHA1 into a nomogram with clinical variables substantially improved individualized risk stratification. PDHA1 expression was strongly correlated with multiple immune checkpoint markers—such as PD-L1, TIM-3, TIGIT, and SIGLEC15—which are involved in T-cell exhaustion and immune escape. These correlations are consistent with prior studies demonstrating metabolic control of checkpoint expression and immunosuppressive transcriptional programs in glioblastoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, and other malignancies50–52. Integrating PDHA1 expression or methylation status into immunotherapy selection algorithms could therefore improve the precision of patient stratification, helping to identify high-risk yet potentially ICI-responsive sarcoma subsets.

From a therapeutic standpoint, our findings suggest several avenues for targeting the PDHA1–E2F1–PD-L1 axis. Direct inhibition of PDHA1 or the PDC complex using RNA interference or small molecules (e.g., CPI-613 and related agents) is currently under clinical investigation in other cancers and may be repurposed for sarcoma. Alternatively, targeting upstream regulators (such as PDKs) or copper transporters involved in cuproptosis may indirectly modulate PDHA1 activity and rewire tumor metabolism. Beyond direct metabolic targeting, our drug-sensitivity and docking analyses highlight agents that mechanistically converge on the PDHA1–E2F1–PD-L1 axis. CDK inhibitors such as Dinaciclib and the E2F1 pathway inhibitor NSC-207895 act on the pRB–E2F1 cell-cycle machinery, and CDK4/6 blockade has been shown to attenuate E2F-driven transcription, downregulate PD-L1 and other immune checkpoints, and synergize with ICIs in preclinical and early clinical studies53–55. MEK inhibitors such as Trametinib target MAPK/ERK signaling, a central regulator of AP-1/Myc activity and PD-L1 expression, and BRAF/MEK inhibition has been reported to reshape T-cell infiltration and PD-1/PD-L1 signaling in melanoma, providing a rationale for MEK–ICI combinations56,57. Biguanides such as Phenformin inhibit mitochondrial complex I and reprogram OXPHOS and AMPK signaling, directly intersecting with the PDHA1-controlled TCA/OXPHOS axis and potentially influencing immunometabolic fitness and PD-L1 expression in the TME58,59.

In the present study, computational analysis of GDSC and CTRP datasets revealed that PDHA1-high tumors exhibit increased sensitivity to several metabolic and cell-cycle–targeting agents, including Phenformin, Dinaciclib, NSC-207895, and 5-Fluorouracil. Molecular docking suggested that these compounds can form stable interactions with PDHA1 or related targets, providing a structural basis for direct or indirect modulation of PDHA1 activity. Consistently, in vitro CCK-8 and dose–response assays in sarcoma cells with differential PDHA1 expression showed that PDHA1-high cells are more vulnerable to Phenformin and NSC-207895, and that PDHA1 knockdown alters drug sensitivity in a manner consistent with disruption of the PDHA1–E2F1 axis. Although we did not experimentally test drug + ICI combinations in vivo, these data support a model in which PDHA1-driven metabolic and transcriptional programs create exploitable vulnerabilities to metabolic and CDK/E2F1-targeted drugs. We therefore propose that PDHA1 expression could be used to select patients for rational combination regimens—such as PDHA1/PDK or mitochondrial metabolism inhibitors plus PD-1/PD-L1 blockade, or CDK/E2F1 pathway inhibitors plus ICIs—in future preclinical and clinical studies.

Our work also has limitations. First, the immunotherapy validation cohort was single-center and relatively small (n = 32), and thus the predictive value of PDHA1 for ICI response requires prospective validation in larger, multicenter sarcoma cohorts. Second, while we demonstrate a mechanistic PDHA1–E2F1–PD-L1 axis and provide evidence for metabolic–immune crosstalk, more detailed studies of metabolic flux, cytokine and metabolite profiling, and spatial transcriptomics will be essential to fully dissect PDHA1’s activity across distinct cellular compartments within the TME. Co-culture assays with T cells, B cells, CAFs, and DCs, as well as cell type–specific genetic models (e.g., conditional PDHA1 deletion in stromal or antigen-presenting cells), will be necessary to clarify non–cell-autonomous effects. Third, our drug-sensitivity analyses are largely hypothesis-generating and require systematic in vivo testing of specific combinations (e.g., Trametinib or Phenformin plus anti-PD-1 in PDHA1-high models) to establish causal relationships and optimize dosing/scheduling.

In conclusion, our study identifies PDHA1 as a central metabolic and immunoregulatory driver of sarcoma progression. Through integrative multi-omics analyses and comprehensive experimental validation, we demonstrate that PDHA1 is significantly upregulated in sarcoma and promotes tumor growth, immune evasion, and resistance to immune checkpoint blockade. Mechanistically, PDHA1 facilitates oncogenesis through a novel PDHA1–E2F1–PD-L1 axis and shapes an immune-cold yet immunogenically primed TME via metabolic competition, epigenetic remodeling, and stromal/antigen-presenting cell reprogramming. PDHA1 expression and hypomethylation are associated with poor prognosis and may serve as dual-layer biomarkers for risk stratification and therapeutic decision-making. Importantly, PDHA1 knockdown reprograms the tumor immune landscape and enhances responsiveness to anti-PD-1 therapy, highlighting its potential as a targetable vulnerability in immunologically refractory sarcomas. These findings support the inclusion of PDHA1 in precision oncology frameworks and lay the groundwork for the development of PDHA1-directed metabolic–immunotherapeutic strategies in sarcoma.

Methods

Data sources and preprocessing

This study utilized RNA sequencing data and corresponding clinical information for sarcoma (SARC) from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/)60. A total of 260 SARC tumor samples and 2 normal tissue samples were included. All expression data were normalized to Transcripts Per Million (TPM), and data distribution was approximately normal, as visualized using the R package ggplot2 (v4.0.3). Gene expression matrices were extracted for subsequent analysis. Differential expression analysis was performed using the Wilcoxon test.

Subtype identification

Based on previous literature, 12 potential cuproptosis-related genes (CRGs)16 were selected. Unsupervised consensus clustering of these genes was conducted using the R package ConsensusClusterPlus (v1.54.0)61, with the number of clusters (k) ranging from 2 to 6. Parameters included: 80% resampling rate, 100 iterations, clusterAlg = “hc”, and innerLinkage = ‘ward.D2’. The optimal number of clusters was determined by evaluating the consensus matrix and cumulative distribution function (CDF). Heatmaps were generated using the pheatmap package (v1.0.12), retaining genes with variance > 0.1. Based on CRG expression profiles, TCGA samples were stratified into two subtypes: Cluster 1 (C1) and Cluster 2 (C2). Protein-protein interaction (PPI) data were retrieved from the STRING database (https://string-db.org)62, and network visualization was performed using the ggraph package (v2.1.0). Key genes were identified through topological analysis of the PPI network. Functional association among genes was further evaluated using the GOSemSim package (v2.22.0) via Friends analysis.

Identification and enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between high and low expression groups were identified using the Limma package (v3.40.2)63 in R. Adjusted p-values were calculated to correct for false positives in the TCGA dataset. DEGs were defined using the threshold: adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |log₂(Fold Change)| > 1.5. Heatmaps were generated with the pheatmap package to visualize expression patterns. Gene Ontology (GO) functional annotation and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were conducted using the clusterProfiler package (v3.18.0)64. Additionally, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA)65 was performed to explore enriched biological pathways. DEGs were categorized into upregulated and downregulated groups. For each analysis, 10,000 permutations were performed. Pathways with adjusted p < 0.05 and FDR < 0.25 were considered significantly enriched.

Somatic mutation and multi-omics correlation analysis

Somatic mutation data for SARC were obtained via the Gene Set Cancer Analysis (GSCA) platform (http://bioinfo.life.hust.edu.cn/GSCA/#/)66 which integrates genomic, transcriptomic, and pharmacogenomic data across 33 cancer types. Mutation types-including missense and nonsense mutations—were visualized using the maftools package in R. Spearman correlation analyses were performed to explore associations between CRG expression and genomic alterations, including copy number variation (CNV) and DNA methylation. Further analyses assessed the impact of CNV and methylation status on patient outcomes, including overall survival (OS), disease-free interval (DFI), disease-specific survival (DSS), and progression-free survival (PFS).

Prognostic value and expression analysis of CRGs in Sarcoma

The prognostic relevance of CRGs in SARC was evaluated using log-rank tests and univariate Cox regression. Kaplan-Meier survival curves, hazard ratios (HRs), and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were generated. Among the CRGs, PDHA1 was identified as a key prognostic biomarker and subjected to further validation. PDHA1 expression and diagnostic performance were validated using three independent datasets: GSE33382 (84 SARC tissues, 3 normal tissues) from NCBI-GEO67, TARGET (98 osteosarcoma samples) from the TARGET database68, ICGC (57 Ewing sarcoma samples) from the ICGC database. Additionally, a cohort of 60 clinical SARC tissue samples from Peking University Shenzhen Hospital was used to validate PDHA1 expression and prognostic significance.

Construction of a prognostic Nomogram

Clinical and pathological data for 260 SARC patients were retrieved from TCGA, including variables such as age, gender, race, metastasis status, radiation therapy, margin status, residual tumor, tumor depth, multifocality, and necrosis (Table S1). Associations between the prognostic CRG (PDHA1) and clinicopathological features were analyzed. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to identify independent prognostic factors, and the results were visualized using forestplot. A prognostic nomogram for predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS was constructed using the rms package, based on multivariate Cox regression results. Model performance was evaluated using calibration plots, time-dependent ROC curves, area under the curve (AUC), and decision curve analysis (DCA). Furthermore, we integrated and analyzed data from TARGET, TCGA, GSE21257, and GSE17679 to reconstruct the nomogram and ROC analysis. Detailed information on the GEO dataset cohorts, including platform and sample specifics, is provided in Table S2.

Immune infiltration and immunotherapy response analysis

To assess immune infiltration, the immunedeconv package69 in R (v4.0.3) was employed, integrating six advanced deconvolution algorithms: TIMER70, xCell71, MCP-counter72, CIBERSORT73, EPIC74, and quanTIseq75. The infiltration levels of immune cells between Cluster 1 (C1, n = 209) and Cluster 2 (C2, n = 51) were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was performed using the GSVA package76 to quantify the infiltration of 24 immune cell types. Immune cell infiltration differences between high and low expression groups of the prognostic CRG (PDHA1) were analyzed via the Wilcoxon test. The correlation between immune cell abundance and patient prognosis was evaluated using Spearman correlation. The expression of immune checkpoint-related genes—including CD274, CTLA4, HAVCR2, LAG3, PDCD1, PDCD1LG2, TIGIT, and SIGLEC15—was compared between PDHA1 high and low expression groups. Spearman correlation was further applied to examine associations between PDHA1 expression and immune checkpoint gene expression. Tumor immune microenvironment scores, including immune score, stromal score, and ESTIMATE score (tumor purity), were calculated using the ESTIMATE algorithm. The Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) algorithm77 was used to predict potential responses to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB), with visualizations generated via ggplot2 and pheatmap. To further validate the immunotherapy response associated with PDHA1, external datasets including GSE176307, IMvigor210, and clinical tissue samples were analyzed.

Analysis of TMB, MSI, mRNAsi, MMR genes, and drug sensitivity

The association between PDHA1 expression and tumor mutation burden (TMB), microsatellite instability (MSI), mRNA stemness index (mRNAsi), and mismatch repair (MMR) genes was assessed using Spearman correlation. mRNAsi was calculated using the one-class logistic regression (OCLR) algorithm as described by Malta et al.78, based on a gene expression signature of 11,774 genes. The index was scaled to the [0,1] interval by subtracting the minimum value and dividing by the range. Drug sensitivity analysis was conducted using gene expression and drug response data from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) and Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal (CTRP) databases. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of chemotherapeutic agents was estimated using the pRRophetic package79 via ridge regression. Default parameters were used, with batch effects corrected using the ComBat algorithm. Duplicate gene values were averaged.The two-dimensional (2D) and three-dimensional (3D) structures of phenformin, dinaciclib, NSC-207895, and 5-fluorouracil were retrieved from the PubChem database. The crystal structure of PDHA1 protein was obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB). Blind docking of these four compounds with PDHA1 was performed using the online version of CB-Dock2 to identify binding cavities and predict binding modes and affinities. Refined molecular docking was subsequently conducted using AutoDock Tools, and the resulting protein–ligand interactions and docking poses were visualized and analyzed with PyMOL.

Single-cell transcriptomic analysis

This study utilized the GSE250523 dataset from the GEO database. Downstream analysis was performed using the Seurat R package, following these specific steps: First, low-quality cells were filtered out (nFeature_RNA > 50 and percent.mt < 15%). Subsequently, the expression matrix was normalized using the LogNormalize method. Next, 2000 highly variable genes were selected for principal component analysis (PCA) using the first 20 principal components. Batch effect correction was applied using the Harmony algorithm. This was followed by clustering analysis (FindNeighbors and FindClusters at a resolution of 0.5) and dimensionality reduction visualization via Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). Cell clusters were annotated based on the clustering results, the CellMarker 2.0 database, and the expression patterns of classic marker genes. Subsequently, PDHA1 expression levels across different cell types were visualized. Furthermore, cell-cell communication networks were inferred using the CellChat R package (v1.6.1). Communication numbers and strengths were calculated based on a known database of ligand-receptor pairs, with key signaling pathways highlighted using network diagrams and heatmaps.

DNA methylation analysis of CRGs in Sarcoma

DNA methylation of PDHA1 at specific CpG sites and their prognostic relevance in SARC were analyzed using the MethSurv database (https://biit.cs.ut.ee/methsurv/)80. Wilcoxon tests were applied to compare the expression of RNA modification-related genes (m⁶A, m⁵C, m¹A, and m⁷G) between PDHA1 high and low expression groups, using ggplot2 for visualization. Correlation analyses were conducted between PDHA1 and the expression of the following gene sets: m⁶A: HNRNPC, RBM15, RBMX, METTL14, YTHDC2, METTL3, ZC3H13, WTAP, YTHDF1, YTHDC1, FTO, YTHDF2, ALKBH5. m⁵C: NOP2, NSUN2, NSUN3, NSUN4, NSUN6, DNMT1, DNMT3A, DNMT3B, TET2, TET3. m¹A: TRMT6, ALKBH1, YTHDC1, YTHDF1, YTHDF2, YTHDF3. m⁷G: AGO2, CYFIP1, DCP2, EIF4A1, EIF4E, EIF4E3, EIF4G3, GEMIN5, LARP1, LSM1, NCBP1, NCBP2, NCBP3, NSUN2, NUDT10, NUDT11, NUDT3, NUDT4, SNUPN, WDR4

Human specimens

This study included 60 paired sarcoma and adjacent normal tissue samples, comprising osteosarcoma (n = 15), liposarcoma (n = 11), leiomyosarcoma (n = 8), and synovial sarcoma (n = 26). Clinical follow-up data were obtained from Peking University Shenzhen Hospital. All specimens were formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded, and histologically confirmed by experienced pathologists. All procedures involving human participants were approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University Shenzhen Hospital (Approval No. 2024-490) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to sample collection.

Cell culture and transfection

The cell lines used in this study were MG63, SW982, SW872, the human osteoblast cell line hFOB1.19 (Punosai, Wuhan, China), human fibroblast-like synoviocytes (HFLS, Jennio Biotech, Guangzhou, China), and a human preadipocyte line (HPA-v, Sciencell, USA). All cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 U/mL streptomycin (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The hFOB1.19 cell line was maintained at 34 °C in an incubator with 5% CO₂, whereas the other cell lines were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. For gene knockdown and overexpression, MG63 and SW982 cells were seeded into six-well plates and allowed to adhere for 24 h until reaching 60–70% confluence. Cells were then transfected with shRNA targeting PDHA1 (sh-PDHA1) or a non-targeting control shRNA (sh-NC) (ZhuHai GeneRulor Medical Technology Corporation, Zhuhai, China) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For rescue experiments, an E2F1 overexpression plasmid (E2F1-OE; pcDNA3.1 backbone, ZhuHai GeneRulor Medical Technology Corporation) or the corresponding empty vector was transfected into sh-PDHA1 cells using the same protocol. Forty-eight hours after transfection, cells were harvested for RNA and protein extraction, flow cytometry, and drug-sensitivity assays as indicated. Transfection efficiency and PDHA1 knockdown were confirmed by RT-qPCR and Western blotting. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Proliferation and colony formation assays

For proliferation assays, 2000 cells were seeded into 96-well plates. Cell viability was assessed daily for five days using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (Dojindo, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions; each experiment was performed in triplicate. For colony formation assays, 1000 cells were seeded into six-well plates and cultured for approximately two weeks. Visible colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 1% crystal violet, and counted.

Wound healing assay

Cells were seeded in six-well plates and grown to 90% confluence. A wound was created using a pipette tip, and detached cells were removed with PBS. Images of the wound area were captured 24 hours post-wounding, and the wound area was measured using ImageJ.

Transwell migration and invasion assays

Cell migration and invasion were analyzed using 24-well Transwell chambers with or without Matrigel coating (Corning, NY, USA, 354480, 3422). Cells suspended in serum-free medium were placed in the upper chamber, while medium containing 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber. After 24 hours of incubation, non-migrated cells in the upper chamber were removed. Migrated cells on the Transwell membrane were fixed with methanol, stained with crystal violet, and counted under a microscope (×100 magnification) in five randomly selected fields.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

First, Total RNA was isolated from cells using the Quick-RNA MiniPrep kit (Zymo research, Irvine, CA, USA, R1054). Then, reverse transcription was performed with the Takara PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara, Kusatsu, Japan, RR037A). Finally, a miScript SYBR Green PCR kit (Qiagen, Germany) was used to detect the expression of target genes on a Lightcycler 480 Real-Time PCR System (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany). The relative standard curve method (2-△△CT) was employed to determine the relative mRNA expression, with the glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) gene as the reference. Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

For IHC, formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded sarcoma tissue sections (4μm) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-mediated antigen retrieval in EDTA buffer (pH 9.0). Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked using 3% hydrogen peroxide, followed by incubation with primary antibodies against Ki-67 (ab15580, Abcam, 1:400), Cyclin D1 (ab16663, Abcam, 1:400), PDHA1 (ab168379, Abcam, 1:400), Bcl-2 (ab182858, Abcam, 1:400), PD-L1 (#13684, CST, 1:400), E2F1 (ab4070, Abcam, 1:400), cleaved Caspase-3 (#9661, CST), and MMP-9 (ab76003, Abcam, 1:400) overnight at 4°C. Sections were then incubated with biotinylated secondary antibodies, followed by streptavidin-HRP and visualized using diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate. Slides were counterstained with hematoxylin and scanned using a digital pathology scanner.

Western blotting

For Western blotting, total protein was extracted from cultured cells and xenograft tumor tissues using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Lysates were incubated on ice for 30 min and clarified by centrifugation, and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay kit. Equal amounts of protein (30 μg per lane) were separated by 10% SDS–PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against PDHA1 (ab168379, Abcam), E2F1 (#3742, CST), PD-L1 (#13684, CST), Bcl-2 (ab182858, Abcam), MMP-9 (ab76003, Abcam), cleaved Caspase-3 (#9661, CST), and lipoylated DLAT (anti-lipoic acid rabbit pAb, 437695, Millipore), as well as GAPDH (ab8245, Abcam; 1:2,000) as a loading control. After incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence reagents (ECL, Millipore) and quantified using ImageJ software.

EdU staining

Cells were seeded in 24-well plates and incubated with 10 μM EdU (Click-iT EdU Alexa Fluor 488 Imaging Kit, Invitrogen) for 30 minutes at 37 °C. Following fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde, cells were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 and stained with DAPI (1 μg/mL) to visualize nuclei. Fluorescent images were acquired using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope.

Sphere formation assay

Single-cell suspensions (5,000 cells/well) were cultured in ultralow-attachment 96-well plates (Corning) with serum-free DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 1× B-27 (Gibco), 40 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (EGF; PeproTech), and 20 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF; PeproTech). Fresh medium was replenished every 3 days. Tumor spheroid growth and morphology were monitored and imaged under phase-contrast microscopy on days 1, 4, and 7 post-seeding. Sphere diameter and invasive protrusions were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH).

Xenograft mouse model

MG63 cells (1 × 107) stably expressing sh-PDHA1 or control shRNA were subcutaneously injected into 4-week-old BALB/c nude mice (n = 6 per group). Tumor volume was measured weekly using the formula V = length × width2 / 2. For all procedures, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (2–3% for induction and 1–2% for maintenance, delivered in oxygen). After 28 days, the mice were euthanized by carbon dioxide (CO2) inhalation using a gradual-fill method, followed by cervical dislocation to ensure death, in accordance with the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA, 2020 edition) guidelines. Xenograft tumors were then excised, weighed, and processed for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL, Vazyme), and immunohistochemistry (IHC). Protein lysates extracted from xenografts were subjected to Western blotting. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Shenzhen Peking University–The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology Medical Center (Approval No. 2025-955) and conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE 2.0 reporting standards.

Apoptosis assay by flow cytometry

Apoptosis was assessed in MG63 cells using an Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit (BD Biosciences) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, sh-NC, sh-PDHA1, or sh-PDHA1 + E2F1-OE cells were harvested, washed twice with cold PBS, and resuspended in 1× binding buffer (1 × 106 cells/mL). Subsequently, 5 μL Annexin V-FITC and 5 μL propidium iodide were added to 100 μL of cell suspension and incubated for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. After adding 400 μL binding buffer, samples were analyzed on a BD flow cytometer within 1 h. The percentage of early and late apoptotic cells (Annexin V-positive) was calculated using FlowJo software.

Tetrathiomolybdate (TTM) treatment

To modulate cuproptosis, MG63 cells with stable PDHA1 knockdown or control (sh-PDHA1 and sh-NC) were treated with the copper chelator ammonium tetrathiomolybdate (TTM; MedChemExpress, HY-100276). Briefly, cells were divided into four groups: sh-NC, sh-NC + TTM, sh-PDHA1, and sh-PDHA1 + TTM. Twenty-four hours after seeding, cells in the TTM groups were incubated with 10 μM TTM for 24 h, while control cells received an equal volume of vehicle (DMSO). Cells were then harvested for Western blot analysis of PD-L1, lipoylated DLAT (detected by anti-lipoic acid antibody, 437695, Millipore), and E2F1 as described above.

Drug sensitivity assay with metabolic and E2F1 pathway inhibitors

To evaluate the effect of PDHA1 knockdown on drug sensitivity, MG63 cells with stable PDHA1 knockdown (sh-PDHA1) or control cells (sh-NC) were seeded into 96-well plates at 3 × 103 cells per well and allowed to attach overnight. Cells were then treated with increasing concentrations of the metabolic inhibitor phenformin (0, 4, 8, and 10 mM; MedChemExpress, HY-16397A) or the E2F1/MDMX pathway inhibitor NSC-207895 (0, 4, 8, and 10 μM; MedChemExpress, HY-100622) for 48 h. Cell viability was measured using a CCK-8 assay (Dojindo) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, and absorbance at 450 nm was recorded with a microplate reader. Cell viability was expressed as a percentage of the untreated control.

Public E2F1 ChIP-seq data analysis

Publicly available E2F1 ChIP-seq datasets (GSM2492420 and GSM2492421) were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). Peak signals were visualized in the UCSC Genome Browser (GRCh38/hg38 assembly) to examine E2F1 occupancy at the CD274 (PD-L1) locus.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) was performed to examine the binding of E2F1 to regulatory regions of the PD-L1 gene using the EZ-Magna ChIP™ A/G Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# 17-10086) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, MG63 and SW982 cells stably expressing either empty vector (EV) or E2F1-overexpression (E2F1-OE) constructs were cultured to ~80% confluence and cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. Cross-linking was quenched with 125 mM glycine for 5 min. Cells were washed with ice-cold PBS containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors and harvested by scraping. Chromatin was fragmented by sonication (Qsonica) to an average size of 150–500 bp. Equal amounts of sheared chromatin were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following antibodies: anti-E2F1 (Genetex, GTX70163, 10 μg/ml), anti-acetyl-histone H3 (Millipore, 17-10085, 5 μg/ml; positive control), or normal rabbit IgG (Millipore, 17-10085, 5 μg/ml; negative control). Protein A/G magnetic beads were then added to capture the immune complexes, followed by sequential washes under low- and high-salt conditions. Cross-links were reversed at 65 °C overnight, followed by proteinase K digestion. DNA was purified and subjected to qPCR using primers flanking the predicted E2F1 response elements (RE1 and RE2) within the PD-L1 promoter (Table S3). ChIP enrichment was calculated as a percentage of input DNA and normalized to IgG. Data are presented as mean ± SEM from at least three independent experiments, and statistical significance was determined using one-way ANOVA.

Promoter reporter assay (PD-L1)

To evaluate the functional relevance of E2F1 binding, human PD-L1 promoter fragments containing wild-type (WT) or mutant (M) E2F1 response elements were cloned into the pGL3-basic luciferase reporter vector (Promega). MG63 and SW982 cells were co-transfected with WT or mutant promoter constructs together with empty vector (EV) or E2F1 expression plasmid using X-tremeGENE HP DNA Transfection Reagent (Roche). After 48 h, luciferase activity was measured with the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) and normalized to Renilla luciferase. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Multiplex immunofluorescence staining of PDHA1, PD-L1 and CD8 in sarcoma tissues

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded sarcoma specimens were sectioned at 4 µm thickness. After deparaffinization and rehydration, antigen retrieval was performed in EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) using a pressure cooker. Sections were cooled, washed, and blocked with 5% BSA for 30 min at room temperature. For multiplex immunofluorescence, sections were sequentially incubated with primary antibodies against PDHA1, PD-L1, and CD8 (overnight, 4 °C), followed by appropriate fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies, with additional antigen retrieval steps between rounds when required. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and slides were mounted with antifade medium. Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (or confocal microscope) under identical exposure settings. PDHA1-high and PDHA1-low cases were defined according to PDHA1 staining intensity, and the expression of PD-L1 as well as CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration was quantified by ImageJ and expressed as integrated optical density per area or percentage of positive cells.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (v4.2.1). Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare differences between tumor and normal tissues. Spearman or Pearson correlation was applied for association analyses. Kaplan-Meier survival, univariate, and multivariate Cox regression analyses were conducted for prognostic evaluation. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (p < 0.05, p < 0.01, p < 0.001). All analyses were conducted using specific R packages and public datasets as described.
