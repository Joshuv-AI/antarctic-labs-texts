Attitude of consumers towards community pharmacy services in Oman: a qualitative approach
Authors: Hisham Amjad Abdul Razzaq, Amer Hayat Khan, Syed Azhar Syed Sulaiman, Sabin Thomas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundConsumers are the main hub of the healthcare system, and assessing their perceptions towards the healthcare services is a paramount indicator that helps policymakers shape and develop healthcare services to meet their needs.AimThis study aims to explore consumer perceptions and identify suggestions for improving community pharmacy services (CPS) in Oman. Methodology: online interviews were conducted with 19 community pharmacy consumers to assess their perceptions towards various issues related to CPS in Oman. The data were analyzed using NVivo software.Resultsthree themes were extracted: (a) consumers’ satisfaction with the CPS, (b) suggestions to improve CPS, and (c) willingness to extend the role of CPs and pay for the extended pharmacy services (EPS).Conclusioninterviewees showed good satisfaction with the CPS in Oman. However, several issues were highlighted that need to be addressed to develop the service. Raising public awareness toward the unique role of CPs in healthcare services, extending the professional role of CPs, and interconnecting them with prescribers would reflect positively on the healthcare system.

Introduction

Globally, pharmacists comprise the third largest healthcare group, preceded by physicians and nurses [1]. In the US, patients visit CPs 12 times more frequently than their primary healthcare providers [2], and that is not strange, as CPs are skilled healthcare experts and have the potential to contribute effectively to improving patients’ healthcare. Moreover, they are the first healthcare providers to be approached by the public for healthcare advice and to collect medications [3].

The last few decades have witnessed an increasing interest in extending the role of CPs in response to the escalating demands due to the prevalence of chronic diseases and the growing load on the healthcare system [4]. Furthermore, their roles are expected to continue extending to meet the growing demand of the public [5]. In this regard, Witry et al. stated that the role of pharmacists is becoming dynamic, responding to the escalating need of consumers for the services delivered by pharmacists [6]. However, numerous studies indicated various issues regarding customers’ perceptions concerning pharmacists’ roles. For instance, El Hajj et al. stated that consumers consider pharmacists as medication vendors and lack awareness regarding their potential to offer advanced pharmaceutical services [7]. Moreover, several studies showed that the public is unaware of their roles [8] and their capabilities to provide essential healthcare services, like diabetes and blood pressure screening, public health education, smoking cessation, oral contraceptives, and vaccinations [9].

In the healthcare system, consumers are the main hub, and their satisfaction is the cornerstone in social pharmacy research [10, 11] and mirrors the quality of healthcare management [12]. Therefore, identifying their perceptions is paramount to understanding their satisfaction and expectations [13] and would assist policymakers in shaping and optimizing healthcare services to match their needs [14].

Oman is a Middle Eastern country; its capital is Muscat. Oman is one of the Arabian Gulf countries, and it is located on the southeastern coast of the Arabian Peninsula [15]. Islam is its religion, and Arabic is the official language in Oman. English is widely used and understood by the majority of the population [15]. According to the Omani National Centre for Statistics and Information, the total population of Oman in 2025 is 5,299,459. Omanis made up 56.7%, and the remaining 43.3% are non-Omanis [16].

Healthcare services in Oman have expanded dramatically over the last 50 years. The Omani Ministry of Health has released the health vision for the next 30 years (Oman’s Health Vision 2050), which summarizes Oman’s vision for the healthcare system in the next years [17]. In this context, Alsaeedi et al. stated that the healthcare systems in Gulf countries are in continuous development; however, the pharmacy profession is still in its beginnings, and the role of CPs is still seen as a drug dispenser [18].

In Oman, the community pharmacy sector is a major healthcare provider to the public, and the sector has witnessed rapid expansion. According to the Omani Ministry of Health statistics, the number of community pharmacies has risen from 309 in 2002 to 975 in 2022 and continues to increase. In parallel, the number of licensed CPs increased to 2215 in 2022, and more than 70% of the total pharmacists in Oman practice in community pharmacies, compared to less than 30% in the government sector [19].

Despite the mentioned potential of the community pharmacy sector in Oman, like other countries in the world, there are scant studies assessing consumers’ perspectives concerning pharmacy care [20]. Furthermore, the provision of pharmaceutical care is still seen as a new area for practice, particularly in community pharmacies, and the focus of CPs is medication dispensing [21].

One of the utmost aims of the Omani Health Vision 2050 is to prioritize health research to develop the healthcare system [22]. Similarly, Obaid et al. stated that healthcare services can only be promoted by applying the concept of evidence-based practice, which necessitates robust research to enrich the practice. Moreover, the study raised the issue of the paucity of research in pharmaceutical care in the Arab region, particularly in Oman [23].

This study is in line with the MoH perspective, and it is a pioneer as it is the first of its type to be conducted in Oman. The findings of this study would, undoubtedly, act as a compass showing the directions to the health authorities to optimize CPS in Oman.

Aim

This study aims to explore consumer perceptions and identify suggestions for improving CPS in Oman.

Methodology

This descriptive study was conducted online through the Zoom meeting platform in the period between August and November 2021, involving community pharmacy consumers in Oman. The sampling methods followed in this study were purposive and snowball, which are commonly followed in qualitative studies [24]. An interview guide was used in this study to enhance and facilitate the interviews. The interview guide was developed based on extensive literature reviews of similar studies [25–27].

This study is a part of a larger qualitative study that was done to explore the perceptions of community pharmacists and the general public towards community pharmacy in Oman and the potential to extend the role of CPs.

The present study involved in-depth semi-structured interviews conducted by the principal investigator (HAR) in Arabic or English based on the preferences of the participants. Using the preferred language in qualitative studies reduces the complexity and challenges in the research process [28].

The average duration of the interviews was approximately 45 min, during which each interviewee was given sufficient time to express their ideas and suggestions. The time of the interviews was fixed earlier according to the participants’ preferences.

All interviewees consented and received an explanatory report about the study before commencing the interviews. Interviews were audio-recorded for easy retrieval and transcribed verbatim. Arabic transcripts were translated to English by the principal investigator with the assistance of a senior community pharmacist working in Oman. Transcripts were thematically analyzed to identify and describe codes, sub-themes, and themes following the method of Braun and Clarke [29]. Analysis was done using NVivo software (version 10). The interviews continued till the saturation point was reached, at which no new information was obtained. This study was approved by the Directorate General of Pharmaceutical Affairs and Drug Control (MH/DGPA&DC/DLPD/489) and the National Centre for Statistics and Information (NCSI/1477/2020).

Discussion

This study is unique, as it is the first of its kind to be conducted in Oman. In our study, interviewees showed good satisfaction with the current CPS. This finding is consistent with another previous quantitative study conducted in Oman, which showed that the perception of the public is good and moderate toward the role of CPs and the delivered CPS, respectively [30]. In addition, our study might address the reasons behind the moderate perception of the public concerning CPS in the previous study.

In the current study, interviewees welcomed extending the role of CPs and offering EPS, like blood pressure and blood glucose monitoring. Furthermore, they showed a willingness to pay for such services. These findings are in the same line with other Middle Eastern Arab countries, including the Gulf countries, where consumers showed similar perceptions towards the role of CPs, the services they are delivering, and the willingness toward extending their professional role [31].

For a more detailed image, issues related to pharmacies and those related to pharmacists are discussed individually below.

Pharmacist-related issues

Interviewees showed concern regarding particular issues, such as the inadequate counseling delivered by CPs and their reluctance to engage in prolonged discussions with customers. They attributed the former issue to numerous factors, such as work customer pressure, which is aggravated by staff shortages, communication/ language barriers, as most CPs are non-Arabic speakers, and the lack of trust between customers and CPs, which further discourages CPs and renders them unwilling to prolong the discussion.

Additionally, the interviewees expressed their dissatisfaction with the unfriendly behavior of some CPs. They stressed that the smile is the first service that CPs should deliver to customers, and they stressed that friendly behavior is an integral element of the role of CPs. A similar Australian study indicated that the friendly behaviour of pharmacy staff is one of the factors affecting consumers’ preference for a particular community pharmacy [32]. Furthermore, it enhances trust and positively impacts health outcomes. CPs should consider it a crucial part of their responsibilities [33, 34].

The inaccessibility of CPs to patients’ records was also indicated as a challenge that affects the quality of counseling, renders it suboptimal, and is not well-tailored to patients’ health conditions. In this context, many studies indicate that the lack of patient records is the most common challenge to extending the role of CPs [35, 36]. In parallel, Goode et al. concluded that the lack of interconnection between CPs and other health professionals underutilizes the knowledge of the former [37]. The mentioned issue can be attributed to the fact that the lack of access to patient records would discourage the CPs from being involved in deeper discussions, as they do not know the patient’s health status, i.e., allergies and diagnosis, hence they would not fully utilize their knowledge.

In our study, the interviewees stressed the importance of CPs to update their knowledge and skills to extend their role and deliver quality services. In this regard, a couple of Saudi studies have indicated that the public is willing to extend the role of CPs, considering they would develop their clinical skills. Moreover, they linked improving their clinical skills with strengthening the trust between them and enhancing their willingness to pay for the EPS [38, 39]. In parallel, Currie et al. stated that training programs significantly enhanced the ability of CPs to provide pharmaceutical care [40].

Additionally, the interviewees raised the issue of poor handwriting of some CPs in labelling medications as a challenge. In this regard, Jeetu et al. stated that labelling is one of the potential causes of medication errors. The problem becomes more challenging if the labelling is in English for non-English speakers [41]. Furthermore, chronically ill patients are at higher risk than others, as they are prone to take more prescribed medications, and their visual and cognitive impairments might affect their reading and comprehension abilities [42].

Another issue highlighted by interviewees is the importance of informing patients about the cost-effectiveness of medications, as patients tend to purchase less expensive brands. This finding is consistent with a Scottish study, which showed that the public is concerned with and apprehensive about the loyalty to pharmacies and the possible conflict with the pharmacy’s economic issues [43].

Interestingly, the interviewees showed readiness to pay for the potential EPS, provided that the cost is reasonable and the service is speedy. Similarly, Soodi et al. concluded that the public is willing to pay for the EPS, and this would incentivize CPs to provide quality counselling and a wider range of pharmacy services [44].

Finally, the interviewees encouraged CPs to develop their knowledge and competencies. Also, they added the importance of reducing their workload by recruiting more staff, introducing a reward system (staff of the month), and raising public awareness towards the unique role of CPs. In this context, Bondi et al. stated that enhancing the recognition of pharmacists and rewarding competent ones would enhance their work satisfaction [45]. Likewise, the American Society of Health-System Pharmacists (ASHP) urges health systems to enhance the recognition of pharmacists and reward them to increase their productivity [46].

Pharmacy-related issues

The interviewees indicated several (pharmacy-related) challenges to developing CPS in Oman. The shortage of some medicines was considered by interviewees as a challenge to extend the role of CPs in Oman. Similarly, evidence stated that drug shortages negatively impact patients and the healthcare system [47], reducing patients’ dissatisfaction and increasing pharmacists’ workload [48].

Interviewees showed a willingness to obtain printable material, i.e., lifestyle/ medicine advice brochures, and to get a longer counselling duration. Similar findings were highlighted by a Saudi study, which stated that patients prefer obtaining brochures and talking to pharmacists to learn more [49].

Interestingly, the interviewees mentioned that some medication packets enclose leaflets written in English only (not Arabic), which is a challenge for those who do not understand English. Moreover, they highlighted that the leaflet contains a lot of information which is difficult for some patients to read, and the CPs should deliver the most important points. Similarly, a study done in London indicated that patients do not recognize medication leaflets as a source of information [50].

The lack of patient waiting areas in the pharmacies, especially for the elderly and pregnant, was raised by interviewees as an essential requirement. This issue becomes more significant in the presence of staff shortages, which would increase waiting times for patients. In this context, Yulia et al. stated that waiting for long times in pharmacies frustrated patients [51].

The importance of the availability of drinking water facilities in pharmacies was highlighted by interviewees as a necessity, particularly for patients who need to take the medicine urgently. Similarly, a Lebanese study indicated that participants responded positively to the availability of waiting areas with seats inside community pharmacies [52]. In Sri Lanka and Estonia, the provision of drinking water for patients in community pharmacies is necessary to ensure good pharmacy practice [53, 54].

Interestingly, in another study done by the author, the mentioned challenges, i.e., inaccessibility of patient records, language barriers, shortages of time/staff, and lack of counseling areas, were also indicated by the CPs themselves as challenges in extending their role [55]. In addition, the former study indicated that the underestimation of their role by some consumers renders them less willing to engage in an elongated discussion with patients.

The location of the pharmacy was highlighted by the interviewees as an influencer on consumers’ preferences in choosing a particular pharmacy. A similar qualitative study showed that Australians stated several factors affecting their preference to visit a particular community pharmacy, among them is the convenient pharmacy location [32]. Similarly, Peter et al. stated that pharmacy location is one of the factors affecting the customers’ preferences in selecting a particular pharmacy to visit [56]. In the same context, Razzak et al. stated that CPs practice in different locations showed different levels of willingness to extend their role, due to the variation of workload in different locations, i.e. in some areas where the number of customers is greater, CPs are overloaded and show more reluctance to extend their role and tend to spend less time in counselling and vice versa [55].

The availability of parking facilities was mentioned as an influencer when selecting a particular community pharmacy. Interviewees mentioned that sometimes, when they are in a hurry, they might forget to pay for parking and would be fined. In this regard, evidence indicates that the lack of parking lots is a challenge for patients to collect their medications. Moreover, it is considered the main factor for visiting a particular pharmacy [57, 58].

Finally, interviewees called for the implementation of online pharmacy services. They listed several benefits of such services, like reducing the crowdedness in pharmacies, and offering an easy way to collect medications without visiting the pharmacy (particularly the elderly, pregnant, and mothers with children). In parallel, studies from Gulf countries showed that Emiratis and Saudis positively perceived online pharmacy services and wished to develop and expand them [59, 60].

Conclusion

The findings of this study provide valuable insights and guide the regulatory bodies to develop CPS in Oman.

In our study, interviewees welcomed extending the role of CPs. However, they highlighted several issues that need to be addressed. Broadly, three strategies were suggested to develop the CPS in Oman. First, CPs need to enhance their abilities (knowledge and skills) toward strengthening the liaison with patients through delivering more intensive counselling, as well as CPs need to take the initiative to talk to the public and not wait for them to ask. Second, it is crucial to raise the awareness of the public regarding the unique role of CPs. The mentioned strategies would boost the liaise and the trust between them. Third, the regulatory bodies need to act towards broadening the scope of practice of CPs.

Finally, we can say that once the public recognizes CPs as medication experts and trusted healthcare professionals, then they would encourage their role extension, which would in turn reflect positively on CPs’ satisfaction and performance, i.e., the jewel would not show without opening the shells. Therefore, all efforts should be directed towards manifesting the potential of CPs to be tangible to the public.

Study limitations

Like other studies, several limitations might have affected this study. For instance, this study was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, and inviting participants was challenging. Also, conducting the interviews online resulted in an under-representation of females, as they were generally unwilling to share their details for arranging online interviews. In parallel, the snowball sampling technique followed in this study might also influence the study findings in somehow. Another potential limitation is the variation in the frequency of visiting community pharmacies, i.e., citizens usually prefer government pharmacies because they are free, and the elderly are more likely to visit pharmacies compared to younger ones. Finally, the huge stress on the participants and the healthcare team involving CPs - due to the pandemic- might have influenced their responses.

Recommendations

Future studies need to be conducted on a larger scale using a quantitative study (e.g., a questionnaire) to generalize the findings and better understand consumers’ expectations concerning CPS. Moreover, it is recommended to compare the perceptions of consumers in Oman and other Gulf countries.
