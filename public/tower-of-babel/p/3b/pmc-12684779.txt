Nonsteroidal Versus Steroidal Mineralocorticoid Receptor Antagonists in Heart Failure: Real‐World Evidence From a Multicenter Cohort Study
Authors: Jheng‐Yan Wu, Kuan‐Jui Tseng, Yu‐Min Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs) reduce mortality and hospitalization in patients with heart failure (HF). Finerenone, a selective nonsteroidal MRA, has recently demonstrated clinical benefits in patients with HF. However, real‐world evidence comparing nonsteroidal and steroidal MRAs in HF is lacking.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, propensity score–matched cohort study using the TriNetX database. Adult patients diagnosed with HF from January 2021 to February 2025 who initiated nonsteroidal or a steroidal MRA were included. The primary outcome was a composite of all‐cause mortality, all‐cause hospitalization, and worsening HF at 1 year. Secondary outcomes included each component individually and safety events. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were performed, including Kaplan–Meier survival, E‐value estimation, and landmark analysis.ResultsAfter matching, 1619 patients were included in each group. The incidence of the primary composite outcome was significantly lower in the nonsteroidal MRAs group (hazard ratio [HR], 0.79 [95% CI, 0.70–0.90]; P<0.001). Nonsteroidal MRAs were also associated with lower risks of all‐cause mortality (HR, 0.49), hospitalization (HR, 0.80), and worsening HF (HR, 0.76). Subgroup analyses showed consistent benefit. No significant differences in safety outcomes were observed.ConclusionsIn this large real‐world cohort, nonsteroidal MRAs use was associated with improved 1‐year clinical outcomes compared with steroidal MRAs in patients with HF. These findings support the potential clinical utility of nonsteroidal MRAs across HF phenotypes and highlight the need for further randomized studies to validate these results.

Heart failure (HF) is defined as a clinical syndrome caused by structural or functional cardiac abnormalities and supported by elevated natriuretic peptide levels or objective evidence of pulmonary or systemic congestion. 1 In 2017, HF was estimated to affect approximately 64.3 million people globally, underscoring its status as a widespread and growing public health concern. 2 Additionally, risk‐adjusted survival analysis revealed that HF with different phenotypes had similar 5‐year mortality rates, with mortality reaching up to 75%. 3 Fortunately, novel guideline‐directed medical therapies have been developed that improve the cardiovascular outcomes of individuals with HF. 4

Spironolactone and eplerenone, 2 steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs), have been shown in pivotal clinical trials to reduce mortality and hospitalization in patients with HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF). 5 , 6 By contrast, the efficacy of steroidal MRAs in patients with HF with mildly reduced ejection fraction (HFmrEF) or preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) remains uncertain. In a randomized controlled trial, spironolactone did not lead to a significant improvement in the primary composite outcome of HF hospitalization, resuscitated cardiac arrest, or cardiovascular death. 7 However, a substantial proportion of participants in that trial may not have had true HF, as event rates were much lower than expected. In addition, many patients randomized to spironolactone had no detectable drug metabolites in their urine, suggesting poor adherence to the assigned treatment. 8 Post hoc analyses indicated potential benefits among those who were more adherent. 9 As a result, current guideline recommendations for MRAs in patients with HFmrEF or HFpEF are weak or lacking. 10

Finerenone, a nonsteroidal and selective MRA, has demonstrated more potent anti‐inflammatory and antifibrotic effects than steroidal MRAs in preclinical models. 11 Furthermore, a recent randomized controlled trial demonstrated that finerenone significantly reduced the risk of the primary composite outcome of total worsening HF events and cardiovascular death in patients with HFmrEF or HFpEF compared with placebo. 12 However, the comparative cardiovascular benefits of nonsteroidal versus steroidal MRAs in patients with HF remain uncertain. To address this knowledge gap, we conducted a real‐world data analysis using the TriNetX database to evaluate the cardiovascular outcomes associated with nonsteroidal MRAs compared with steroidal MRAs in patients with HF.

METHODS

Data Availability and Reproducibility

The data that support the findings of this study were obtained from the TriNetX research network under a data use agreement. The data are deidentified and proprietary; therefore, they are not publicly available. Researchers interested in accessing the data may contact TriNetX (https://www.trinetx.com) for more information. The analytic methods and statistical code used in this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

The retrospective cohort study leveraged data sourced from the TriNetX platform, a comprehensive repository of deidentified electronic health records. This platform compiles data predominantly from academic medical centers, encompassing their primary hospitals, affiliated institutions, and outpatient clinics spanning multiple health care organizations. The data set includes patient demographic details, diagnostic codes based on the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD‐10‐CM) system, procedural data captured through the ICD‐10 Procedure Coding System or Current Procedural Terminology, medication information categorized via the Veterans Affairs Drug Classification System and RxNorm, laboratory results encoded using Logical Observation Identifiers Names and Codes, and metrics reflecting health care use. The analysis drew upon the TriNetX Global Collaborative Network, which aggregates records from approximately 150 million patients across 146 health care organizations. Given that TriNetX provides solely aggregated counts and deidentified data summaries—without accessing protected health information or conducting study‐specific interventions—it operates under an exemption authorized by the Western Institutional Review Board.

Ethical Approval

This study was conducted using deidentified, aggregated data from the TriNetX research network, which does not contain protected health information or permit the identification of individual patients. As such, it was granted exemption by the Western Institutional Review Board, and no additional institutional review board approval or informed consent was required.

Cohort

The study cohort comprised adults aged 18 years and older diagnosed with HF between January 2021 and February 2025, a period selected to mitigate potential time‐related biases. HF diagnoses were determined using the ICD‐10‐CM code I50. 13 Participants were divided into 2 groups: the nonsteroidal MRAs group included patients who initiated finerenone therapy after their HF diagnosis, and the steroidal MRAs group comprised those treated with either spironolactone or eplerenone. In both cohorts, the index date was defined as the first documented prescription of the study medication. Baseline covariates and comorbidities were assessed during the 12‐month period preceding the index date (Table S1).

Covariates

Statistical models were matched to account for a range of covariates, including age, sex, race, comorbidities, pharmacological treatments, and laboratory measurements. The comorbidities considered encompassed nicotine dependence, diabetes, overweight and obesity, dyslipidemia, atrial fibrillation, ischemic heart disease, and chronic kidney disease. Pharmacological covariates included HMG‐CoA reductase inhibitors, ezetimibe, metformin, glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists, sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 inhibitors, insulin, angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin II receptor blockers, beta blockers, and calcium channel blockers. Laboratory parameters incorporated into the analysis comprised hemoglobin A1c, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, and left ventricular ejection fraction (Table S2). A directed acyclic graph was constructed to visualize hypothesized causal relationships between exposure and outcome, and to guide variable selection for adjustment (Figure S1).

Outcomes and Follow‐Up

Considering that medications generally require a period to achieve therapeutic efficacy, the observation phase commenced 30 days following the index date and continued until 1 of the following events transpired: the occurrence of an outcome event, the patient's last recorded clinical visit, death, or the conclusion of 1 year after the index date. The primary end point of the study was a composite measure encompassing all‐cause mortality, all‐cause hospitalization, and worsening heart failure (WHF). 14 WHF was defined with either (1): a diagnosis of pulmonary edema (ICD‐10‐CM: J81), or (2) administration of injectable thiazide‐ or loop‐diuretics, identified using Veterans Affairs classification codes CV701 and CV702, respectively 14 (Table S3). In addition to the primary composite outcome, each of its components was analyzed as a secondary outcome to assess consistency of treatment effect across individual end points and to facilitate clinical interpretation. 14 Additionally, we evaluated safety outcomes, which included hyperkalemia, acute kidney injury, and gynecomastia.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical data were presented using mean±SD for continuous variables and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. To reduce confounding, propensity score matching was used to balance baseline characteristics between the nonsteroidal and steroidal MRA groups. Propensity score matching was performed independently for the primary analysis, each subgroup analysis, and each sensitivity analysis. Specifically, we conducted 1:1 nearest‐neighbor matching without replacement using a greedy algorithm and a fixed caliper of 0.1 pooled SDs. This approach ensured that each analytic cohort consisted of newly matched pairs. Therefore, Cox proportional hazards models were fitted directly to each matched cohort without the use of stratified Cox models. 15 Given that we used 1:1 nearest‐neighbor matching with the nonsteroidal MRA group as the treated cohort, the estimated treatment effect corresponds to the average treatment effect on the treated. 16 Hazard ratios (HRs) with their corresponding 95% CIs were calculated using Cox proportional hazards models, with the proportional assumption assessed via Schoenfeld residuals. 17 For survival analysis, Kaplan–Meier curves with log‐rank tests were employed. To confirm the reliability of our results, we analyzed a negative control outcome with skin cancer and acute cholecystitis. We further calculated E‐values to evaluate the potential influence of unmeasured confounding factors. 18 Finally, landmark analysis was conducted to assess time‐dependent effects. 19 All statistical analyses were executed within the TriNetX platform.

To ensure methodological rigor and transparency in our use of propensity score methods, we adhered to the checklist for design, analysis, and reporting proposed by Austin et al. 20 We prespecified relevant covariates based on clinical relevance and literature review, applied 1:1 nearest‐neighbor matching with a caliper of 0.2 SDs of the logit of the propensity score, and evaluated covariate balance using standardized mean differences, with all postmatching values <0.1. We also reported key parameters including event rates, HRs with 95% CIs, Kaplan–Meier estimates, and conducted sensitivity analyses using E‐values and negative control outcomes. These steps were taken to minimize bias and enhance the credibility of our comparative effectiveness estimates.

To further assess the impact of treatment adherence, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using an as‐treatment approach. Specifically, we restricted the cohort to patients who received a second prescription of the same MRA within 6 to 12 months after the index date, using this as a proxy for sustained treatment. This method allowed us to partially address limitations related to medication persistence. To address potential survivorship bias, we conducted sensitivity analyses by restricting the cohort to patients who had a documented HF diagnosis within 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, or 1 year before the index date.

Subgroup Analysis

We conducted an additional subgroup analysis to investigate distinct patient cohorts, stratified according to the type of MRAs, HF classification (including HFrEF and HFpEF), and the presence of atrial fibrillation, coronary artery disease, or obesity. For both HF phenotypes, we calculated HRs for each clinical outcome comparing nonsteroidal versus steroidal MRAs. The results were presented in additional supplemental tables to enhance interpretability (Tables S4 and S5). In addition, Kaplan–Meier time‐to‐event curves were generated for HFrEF and HFpEF separately.

RESULTS

Patient Selection

Using the TriNetX network, which includes data from 146 health care organizations, we identified a total of 157 698 357 patients as of March 21, 2025. Among them, 54 065 008 patients had at least 1 health care organization visit between January 1, 2021, and February 28, 2025. Patients were excluded if they were <18 years old, had a prior history of receiving the study drug, or lacked a diagnosis of HF, resulting in the exclusion of 53 689 006 individuals. After applying these criteria, a total of 376 002 patients with HF who received either nonsteroidal or steroidal MRAs were included in the initial cohort. Of these, 1632 were identified as new users of finerenone following an HF diagnosis, and 374 370 were new users of an MRA. After matching, 1619 patients in the nonsteroidal MRAs group and 1619 patients in the steroidal MRAs group (Figure 1).

Demographic Characteristics

Before propensity score matching, patients in the nonsteroidal MRAs group (n=1632) were generally older than those in the steroidal MRAs group (n=374 370), with a mean age of 69.8±11.5 years compared with 66.5±14.7 years. The proportion of female patients was slightly lower in the nonsteroidal MRAs group (38.1%) than in the steroidal MRAs group (42.2%). Racial distribution varied between the groups, with a higher proportion of White patients in the steroidal MRAs group (55.7% versus 45.0%) and a greater representation of Asian (22.7% versus 4.3%) categories in the nonsteroidal MRAs group. The nonsteroidal MRAs group also exhibited a higher prevalence of diabetes (68.3% versus 31.4%), chronic kidney disease (64.1% versus 22.3%), and overweight or obesity (31.4% versus 24.2%). Baseline metabolic parameters showed significant differences, with a higher mean hemoglobin A1c level in the nonsteroidal MRAs group (7.2±1.6% versus 6.7±1.7%) and a lower mean low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol level (73.1±34.3 mg/dL versus 84.4±37.2 mg/dL). After matching, the nonsteroidal and steroidal MRAs groups were well balanced across all baseline characteristics, with standardized differences <0.1 for most variables. The mean age was comparable between groups (69.8±11.5 years versus 70.1±12.5 years), and the proportion of female patients remained similar (38.3% versus 40.3%). Racial composition was also well balanced post matching. The prevalence of key comorbidities, including diabetes (68.0% versus 69.0%), chronic kidney disease (63.8% versus 63.5%), and ischemic heart disease (46.3% versus 45.8%), showed minimal differences between groups. Similarly, metabolic parameters such as hemoglobin A1c (7.2±1.6% versus 7.2±1.8%) and low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol level (73.4±34.3 mg/dL versus 77.8±37.6 mg/dL) were well matched. Left ventricular ejection fraction remained slightly higher in the finerenone group (57.1±12.2% versus 52.4±15.2%). The use of cardiovascular and glucose‐lowering medications was also balanced post matching, including beta blockers (57.7% versus 55.7%), sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (41.0% versus 40.5%), and insulin therapy (46.5% versus 46.6%; Table 1).

Primary Outcome

The incidence of the primary composite outcome was lower in the nonsteroidal MRAs group compared with the steroidal MRAs group (Table 2). Over the follow‐up period, 430 events occurred in the nonsteroidal MRAs group, corresponding to an incidence rate of 26.6 per 100 person‐years, whereas 550 events were observed in the steroidal MRAs group, with an incidence rate of 34.0 per 100 person‐years (Table 2). The use of nonsteroidal MRAs was associated with a significantly lower risk of the primary composite outcome, with an HR of 0.79 (95% CI, 0.70–0.90, P<0.001). The E‐value for this association was 1.85 (lower confidence limit [LCL]: 1.46; Table 2). Kaplan–Meier curves consistently demonstrated a higher event‐free probability in the nonsteroidal MRAs group than the steroidal MRAs group (log‐rank P<0.001, Figure 2A). The test for proportionality showed no violation of the proportional hazards assumption for all outcomes (Schoenfeld test >0.05).

Subgroup Analysis

Subgroup analyses demonstrated that the reduction in the primary composite outcome associated with nonsteroidal MRAs use was consistent across most patient subgroups (Figure 3). When stratified by the type of MRAs, finerenone was associated with a significantly lower risk of the primary outcome compared with spironolactone and to a lesser extent compared with eplerenone. The benefit of nonsteroidal MRAs was consistent across HF classifications, with significant associations observed in both HFrEF and HFpEF. Expanded subgroup analyses by HF phenotype are shown in Tables S4 and S5, Figure 2B, and Figure 2C. These analyses demonstrated that the use of nonsteroidal MRAs was associated with consistently lower risks of the primary and secondary outcomes compared with steroidal MRAs in both patients with HFrEF and HFpEF. A pronounced risk reduction was observed in patients with atrial fibrillation, and a similarly significant effect was also seen in those without atrial fibrillation. Similarly, patients with diabetes exhibited a substantial benefit with nonsteroidal MRAs, whereas the association was not statistically significant among those without diabetes. A significant reduction in the primary outcome was also observed in patients with coronary artery disease, whereas no significant difference was found in those without coronary artery disease. When stratified by obesity status, the risk reduction was not statistically significant among individuals with and without obesity.

In the as‐treatment sensitivity analysis, we restricted the cohort to patients who received a second prescription of the same MRA within 6 to 12 months after treatment initiation, as a proxy for continued use. The results remained consistent with the primary analysis (Table S6).

To further assess potential survivorship bias, we performed sensitivity analyses by restricting the cohort to patients who had a documented HF diagnosis within 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, or 1 year before the index date. The results of these restricted analyses were consistent with the primary findings, supporting the robustness of our conclusions (Table S7).

Secondary Outcomes

The analysis of secondary outcomes revealed that nonsteroidal MRA use was associated with significant reductions in all‐cause mortality, all‐cause hospitalization, and WHF compared with steroidal MRAs use (Table 2). 58 deaths occurred in the nonsteroidal MRAs group, corresponding to an incidence rate of 3.6 per 100 person‐years, whereas 130 deaths were recorded in the steroidal MRAs group, with an incidence rate of 8.0 per 100 person‐years. The risk of all‐cause mortality was significantly lower in the nonsteroidal MRAs group, with an HR of 0.49 (95% CI, 0.36–0.66, P<0.001). The E‐value for this association was 3.50 (LCL: 2.40; Table 2).

Similarly, the incidence of all‐cause hospitalization was lower in the nonsteroidal MRAs group (21.8 per 100 person‐years) compared with the steroidal MRAs group (27.9 per 100 person‐years), with nonsteroidal MRAs use associated with a significantly reduced risk (HR, 0.80 [95% CI, 0.69–0.91], P=0.001) and E‐value 1.81 (LCL: 1.43; Table 2). Additionally, WHF occurred in 172 patients in the nonsteroidal MRAs group (incidence rate: 10.6 per 100 person‐years) and 239 patients in the steroidal MRAs group (incidence rate: 14.8 per 100 person‐years), corresponding to an HR of 0.76 (95% CI, 0.62–0.92, P=0.006). The E‐value for this association was 1.96 (LCL: 1.39; Table 2).

Landmark Analysis

The landmark analysis evaluated outcomes across 4 distinct periods (Table S8). Regarding the primary outcome, the nonsteroidal MRAs showed lower risks in the 2‐month to 1‐year analysis, 3‐month to 1‐year analysis, 1‐month to 2‐years analysis and 1‐month to 3‐years analysis (Table S8).

Safety Outcomes

No statistically significant differences were observed between the nonsteroidal and steroidal MRAs groups in terms of hyperkalemia, acute kidney injury, or gynecomastia (Table S9).

Negative Outcome

The associations between treatment groups and the 2 negative control outcomes, acute cholecystitis and skin cancer, were not statistically significant (Table S10).

DISCUSSION

Our study, which included 3238 participants, found that nonsteroidal MRAs use was associated with a lower 1‐year incidence of the composite outcome of all‐cause mortality, all‐cause hospitalization, and WHF compared with steroidal MRAs use. Consistent results were observed across subgroup analyses, including different types of steroidal MRAs and patients with atrial fibrillation, diabetes mellitus, HFrEF, HFpEF, or coronary artery disease. Notably, although the benefit of nonsteroidal MRAs was significant in patients with diabetes, no statistically significant association was observed in the subgroup without diabetes. This may reflect a lower baseline risk or limited sample size in this subgroup and warrants further investigation in future studies. In addition, the secondary outcomes were consistent for each component of the primary composite outcome. To ensure the robustness of our findings, a negative control analysis using acute cholecystitis and skin cancer showed no significant differences between groups. Additionally, landmark analyses with different time periods were conducted to assess potential variations in prognosis, and the results remained consistent for the composite outcomes. Furthermore, the safety outcomes—including hyperkalemia, acute kidney injury, and gynecomastia—did not differ significantly between groups. Although not statistically significant, the safety outcomes numerically favored finerenone across all categories, which may reflect its greater receptor selectivity and lower off‐target hormonal effects. These trends, although encouraging, should be interpreted with caution given the limitations of electronic health record‐based adverse event detection.

A systematic review and meta‐analysis including 4 trials and 13 846 patients showed that MRAs reduced the risk of cardiovascular death or HF hospitalization, with an HR of 0.77 (95% CI, 0.72–0.83) in patients with HF. 21 Another network meta‐analysis involving 15 685 patients demonstrated that steroidal MRAs are safe and effective in patients with HFrEF, but their efficacy appears to be less pronounced in those with HFmrEF. 22 Although a previous randomized controlled trial showed that spironolactone did not significantly reduce the incidence of the primary composite outcome of cardiovascular death, cardiac arrest, or HF hospitalization in patients with HFpEF (HR, 0.90 [95% CI, 0.78–1.05]), 7 other trials have suggested that steroidal MRAs treatment significantly improves cardiac structure and function, indicating potential benefits in patients with HFpEF. 23 , 24

Finerenone, a novel nonsteroidal MRA, exhibits more favorable pharmacological properties compared with traditional steroidal MRAs. Although direct clinical comparisons of anti‐inflammatory and antifibrotic effects between steroidal and nonsteroidal MRAs are lacking, finerenone has demonstrated superior pharmacologic properties, including more favorable tissue distribution, a shorter half‐life, and greater selectivity for the mineralocorticoid receptor compared with spironolactone and eplerenone. 25 , 26 These properties may contribute to its improved efficacy and safety profile observed in clinical trials. Additionally, a post hoc analysis showed that finerenone was associated with better blood pressure control and a lower risk of hyperkalemia compared with spironolactone in chronic kidney disease. 27 Recently, a randomized controlled trial involving 6001 patients with HFmrEF or HFpEF demonstrated that finerenone significantly reduced the incidence of a composite outcome of total worsening HF events and cardiovascular death compared with placebo (risk ratio, 0.84 [95% CI, 0.74–0.95]; P=0.007). 12

These studies have demonstrated the benefits of both nonsteroidal and steroidal MRAs in patients with HF. However, no large‐scale clinical trial has yet directly compared the efficacy and safety of these 2 classes of MRAs in participants with HF. To address this knowledge gap, we leveraged a large multicenter database and propensity score matching to enhance statistical power and reduce the impact of measured confounders. Our findings indicate that in patients with HF, nonsteroidal MRAs use is significantly associated with a lower 1‐year incidence of the composite outcome, including all‐cause mortality, all‐cause hospitalization, and WHF, with no increased rate of complications compared with steroidal MRAs use. These findings highlight a novel therapeutic option for patients with HF that may provide improved efficacy and safety compared with traditional steroidal MRAs. Although our real‐world analysis provides valuable preliminary insights into the comparative effectiveness and safety of nonsteroidal versus steroidal MRAs across a broad spectrum of patients with HF, these findings should be interpreted within the context of observational data. Further large‐scale randomized controlled trials with extended follow‐up are warranted to validate these associations and clarify the clinical role of nonsteroidal MRAs in different HF phenotypes.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, as the TriNetX database is registry based, there is a risk of misidentification and underrepresentation, particularly for milder cases or individuals with limited health care engagement, which may affect the results. Additionally, the lack of detailed demographic data limits our ability to adjust for geographic and socioeconomic disparities, lifestyle factors, and access to care, potentially reducing the generalizability of our findings. Moreover, the use of diagnostic codes to define variables and outcomes may lead to misclassification, introducing potential bias. To mitigate this, we conducted a negative control analysis, which showed no significant differences between groups, suggesting minimal registration bias. Second, unmeasured confounders may have influenced the results. Given the observational nature of the study and baseline differences between groups, residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded. To address this concern, we calculated E‐values for the primary and secondary outcomes. The E‐value for the primary composite outcome was 1.85 (lower confidence limit: 1.46), suggesting that an unmeasured confounder would need to be associated with both the exposure and the outcome by a risk ratio of at least 1.85, beyond the measured covariates, to fully explain away the observed association. For all‐cause mortality, the E‐value was even higher at 3.50 (LCL: 2.40), indicating a high degree of robustness. These findings suggest that it is unlikely that residual confounding alone accounts for the observed treatment effects. Third, due to limitations of the database, we were unable to determine the specific causes of mortality and hospitalization, which may have introduced additional bias. To address this limitation and further support our conclusions, we included a secondary outcome of WHF, which showed consistent results with the primary composite outcome. Fourth, the duration of medication use could not be ascertained, limiting our ability to evaluate treatment adherence. However, to partially address this limitation, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using an as‐treatment approach by restricting the cohort to patients who received a second prescription of the same MRA within 6 to 12 months after treatment initiation. The consistent results support the robustness of our findings. Future studies incorporating more granular clinical data and prospective designs are warranted to further validate these findings.

Conclusions

In patients with HF, the use of nonsteroidal MRAs was significantly associated with a lower 1‐year incidence of the composite outcome, including all‐cause mortality, all‐cause hospitalization, and WHF, compared with steroidal MRAs. These findings provide valuable insights into the optimal management of this high‐risk population and enhance our understanding of the potential role of nonsteroidal MRAs in HF treatment. However, given the observational nature of this study, further randomized clinical trials are warranted to validate these findings and establish definitive treatment recommendations.

Translational outlook

Future research should focus on large‐scale randomized controlled trials directly comparing nonsteroidal and steroidal MRAs across diverse HF phenotypes. In addition, real‐world studies incorporating medication adherence, dose–response relationships, and long‐term outcomes are needed to further clarify the comparative effectiveness of these agents. Integration of biomarker‐guided therapy and personalized medicine approaches may also enhance the clinical application of nonsteroidal MRAs in HF care.
