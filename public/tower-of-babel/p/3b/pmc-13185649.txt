New and Emerging Nonimmunosuppressive Drug Therapies for Primary Adult Glomerular Diseases
Authors: Vladimir Mushailov, Jai Radhakrishnan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Primary adult glomerular diseases (GDs), including immunoglobulin A nephropathy, membranous nephropathy, and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, are important causes of chronic kidney disease and may progress to kidney failure. Nonimmunosuppressive foundation drug therapy for primary adult GD focuses on renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibition to reduce proteinuria, alleviate hypertension, and reduce the risk of kidney disease progression. Additional agents with a distinct mechanism of action may be added or substituted in patients with a high progression risk or who are intolerant to renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibitors. This review highlights the role of lifestyle optimization as well as summarizing the currently nonimmunosuppressive pharmacologic treatment options, for the management of primary adult GDs. We also provide an overview of the key pathophysiologic processes contributing to disease progression. Given the safety concerns associated with immunosuppressive therapies, we discuss the future role of newer nonimmunosuppressive agents including finerenone (a nonsteroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist), sparsentan (a dual endothelin-angiotensin II receptor antagonist), atrasentan (an endothelin receptor antagonist), and iptacopan (complement inhibitor), which are currently Food and Drug Administration approved or are under investigation in clinical trials for primary GDs. These and other agents may expand the currently limited treatment landscape for patients with primary adult GD.Plain-Language SummaryEach kidney contains about 1 million tiny functional units called nephrons, and each nephron has a filter called a glomerulus. The glomeruli filter waste products and remove excess fluids from the blood, which exit the body through urine. When the glomeruli are damaged, there is loss of protein through the urine. This can result in a condition called glomerular disease (GD). Treatments for GD include eating a diet that is low in sodium, potassium, and protein because eating these in excess can make GD worse. People with GD may also be advised to exercise, stop smoking, and aim for an ideal target weight. Drug medicines are also an important part of treatment for GD. A type of drug called a renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) inhibitor helps to reduce the amount of damage to the glomeruli and also helps to reduce blood pressure in people who also have high blood pressure (called hypertension). If a person is taking a RAAS inhibitor drug (or an appropriate alternative) but their GD does not improve, then they are at a high risk of kidney disease progression. In this scenario, the person will be offered an additional drug (to take alongside their RAAS inhibitor drug) or an alternative drug that targets their GD in a different way. Some of these work by inhibiting parts of the immune system (called immunosuppressive drugs), whereas others do not do this (called nonimmunosuppressive drugs). Our review focuses on nonimmunosuppressive drugs for GD. We consider nonimmunosuppressive drugs already available for treating GD, ones that have only recently been made available, and drugs that are being tested in large clinical trials involving people with GD.

Introduction

Glomerular disease (GD) may lead to acute kidney injury and/or chronic kidney disease (CKD) and progress to kidney failure requiring long-term dialysis or kidney transplantation.1 GD is labeled primary when the injury is not due to an underlying condition; alternatively, GD may be secondary to an existing illness.1 Most GDs are classified as rare diseases (affecting <200,000 people in the United States)2; consequently, there is a paucity of randomized controlled trials on which to base treatment recommendations.3

Primary GDs in adults include immunoglobulin A (IgA) nephropathy (IgAN), membranous nephropathy (MN), and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS).3,4 Nonpharmacologic management of primary GD forms part of the foundation (or supportive) treatment for GD and includes dietary sodium and potassium management and management of cardiovascular risk factors.3 Most drug treatments for primary GD have some immunosuppressive effects, which may have general or specific effects to certain parts of the immune system. Nonimmunosuppressive drugs do not directly modify lymphocyte activation, function, or proliferation.5,6 Examples of nonimmunosuppressive drugs that have shown efficacy in primary adult GD include renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) inhibitors,3 sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors,7, 8, 9 sparsentan (an endothelin receptor 1 and angiotensin II receptor dual antagonist),10, 11, 12 atrasentan (endothelin type A receptor antagonist),13,14 and iptacopan (complement inhibitor).15,16 The main objectives of nonimmunosuppressive drug therapy in primary GD are to optimize blood pressure, reduce proteinuria, and prevent kidney disease progression.1,3 Systemically acting immunosuppressive drugs (such as glucocorticoids, budesonide [a nonsystemically acting glucocorticoid], cytotoxic drugs, calcineurin inhibitors, and B-cell and other targeted therapies) are reserved for high-risk patients owing to the risk of significant side effects.3,17 Immunosuppressive therapies are typically reserved for patients with GDs characterized by immune-mediated pathogenesis who are at a high risk of progression—such as those with biopsy-confirmed primary FSGS and nephrotic syndrome, crescentic IgAN, or severe MN with high phospholipase A2 receptor titers.3 In such cases, immunosuppressives may be initiated early alongside foundation therapy to control disease activity, whereas nonimmunosuppressive agents remain essential for managing blood pressure and proteinuria.3

This study gives a detailed review of the nonimmunosuppressive foundation therapies for primary adult GD in which the treatment focus is optimizing blood pressure, reducing proteinuria, and preventing kidney disease progression; we also consider the pathophysiologic processes involved in the development of progressive GD and the importance of lifestyle optimization as part of the GD management process. Finally, given the side effect concerns associated with immunosuppressive drug treatments, we also discuss the potential application of newer nonimmunosuppressive drug treatments currently being investigated for primary adult GD.

Pathophysiology of Primary Adult Glomerular Syndromes

An Overview of Immune and Nonimmune-Related Pathophysiology

Primary IgAN and MN result from deposition of immune complexes (in situ or circulating) in kidney glomeruli.18,19 In contrast, primary FSGS is caused by putative circulating permeability factors and cytokines from extrarenal sources, which cause diffuse podocyte injury.20,21 People with IgAN have abnormal (galactose-deficient) IgA1 levels in their circulation. Autoantibodies generated against these abnormal antibodies result in immune complex deposition at the glomerular mesangium. These immune complexes activate mesangial cells (causing them to proliferate and secrete extracellular matrix) as well as activate the complement system and cause proinflammatory cytokine release, leading to glomerular inflammation and fibrosis.18,22, 23, 24 In primary MN, autoantibodies to a protein naturally expressed by podocytes (mostly phospholipase A2 receptors19,25) deposit in the subepithelial layer, leading to complement activation and consequential proteinuria.24,26,27 In primary FSGS, podocyte injury is associated with loss of podocytes28 that causes release of growth factors and upregulation of proinflammatory cytokine release and proinflammatory immune cell phenotypes, collagen matrix deposition, fibrosis, and significant proteinuria.20,28

Kidney disease progression in primary adult GD is caused by upregulation of RAAS (including the mineralocorticoid receptor [MR] pathway), the complement system, and endothelin system pathways. Upregulation of RAAS has many pathophysiologic effects cumulating in acute and chronic kidney injury. These effects include sustained glomerular hyperfiltration, systemic hypertension, inflammation, and increased glomerular permeability. They are predominately mediated by angiotensin II signaling via the Ang II type 1 receptor.29, 30, 31, 32 Aldosterone (part of RAAS) levels may also increase, and overactivation of the aldosterone receptor (MR) in the kidneys contributes to sodium dysregulation, inflammation, glomerular fibrosis, and systemic hypertension.33 Abnormal complement activation contributes to acute podocyte loss/damage, inflammation, and fibrosis (particularly in MN and IgAN as noted in the above section).24 In IgAN, this is predominantly via the alternative complement pathway,22 and in MN, this is predominantly via the classical complement pathway.27 Dysregulation of the endothelin system in primary adult GD involves an increase in endothelin peptide 1 levels plus an imbalance between its type A (ETAR) and type B (ETBR) receptors in which ETAR dominates.34 Endothelin peptide 1 (via ETAR) causes kidney injury by contributing to sustained vasoconstriction, inflammation, and damage to and loss of podocytes.35 Endothelin peptide 1 (via ETAR) and Ang II signaling via the Ang II type 1 receptor are important contributors to hypertension and proteinuria in primary GD.36 Damage to the glomerular filtration barrier is an important reason for proteinuria, and clinically significantly increased levels of proteinuria are associated with kidney disease progression and tubulointerstitial damage.37

Role of Hypertension and Proteinuria in the Progression of Primary Adult GD

Hypertension is a common feature of primary adult GD, especially in nephrotic syndromes such as primary MN and FSGS. It is driven via both RAAS-dependent and RAAS-independent mechanisms that cause sodium retention.38 In addition, sodium retention in nephrotic syndrome is a cause of edema; use of diuretics may help ease symptoms for patients (Fig 1). For hypertension, lifestyle optimization and appropriate pharmacologic treatments (Fig 1) are key to managing primary GD because hypertension is a significant risk factor for disease progression. Proteinuria is a predictor of worsening of kidney function and cardiovascular disease, and is associated with ongoing significant and sustained proteinuria, global kidney sclerosis, and decreased kidney function.39, 40, 41, 42 The mechanisms by which proteinuria leads to progression of kidney disease and tubulointerstitial damage have not been completely elucidated, but it is thought that filtered proteins are reabsorbed by proximal tubular cells, leading to production of reactive oxygen species, autophagy dysfunction, lysosomal membrane permeabilization, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and complement activation.37Figure 1First-, second-, and third-line pharmacologic foundation treatment recommendations for proteinuria, hypertension, and edema in patients with primary GD according to the KDIGO 2021 glomerular disease clinical practice guideline.3 This figure is based on recommendations, not requirements, so the drug or drugs used will depend on patient and physician choice as well as patient risk factors and comorbid conditions. Management of Primary Adult GD: Lifestyle Optimization

Dietary management in primary GD is an essential component in slowing down worsening of the condition and in reducing the risk of associated complications.3 It is important that individuals with primary GD reduce their sodium consumption. According to the KDIGO (Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes) clinical practice guideline for management of GDs, individuals should restrict their sodium intake to <2.0 g/d (90 mmol/d) to reduce their risk of edema and hypertension, and to control proteinuria.3 A low-potassium diet may also be recommended for individuals with significant kidney dysfunction (CKD stages 3-5) and/or a RAAS inhibitor in which there is actual hyperkalemia or hyperkalemia risk.43 Other dietary recommendations include restricting dietary protein intake to a level based on a person’s kidney function and also to reduce the consumption of cholesterol-heavy foods to decrease the risk of cardiovascular complications.3 In addition to changes in diet, other lifestyle modification recommendations include the maintenance of a target body weight, smoking cessation, and exercise.3,43 Each patient with primary GD should have a personalized diet and weight goal plan alongside regular check-ins with their primary care provider to monitor progress and make changes where appropriate.43

Management of Primary Adult GD: Goals of Nonimmunosuppressive Pharmacologic Therapy

Optimization of both nonpharmacologic (via lifestyle modifications) and pharmacologic therapy is needed to reduce GD progression risk. In addition to achieving blood pressure targets (<120 mm Hg), clinically significant reduction in proteinuria is an important treatment goal because it reflects control of the primary disease, reduction in glomerular hypertension, and reduction of podocyte damage.3 Thus, proteinuria can be used as a marker for pharmacologic intervention and efficacious evaluation of treatment effect, but it is important to note that individuals who are traditionally considered to be at a low risk may still go on to develop kidney failure.44 Treatment should also aim to improve patient-reported quality of life. The KDIGO 2021 clinical practice guideline for management of GD recommends a trial of optimized supportive foundation therapy for 3 months (especially for IgAN and MN patients) for those at a low or moderate risk of kidney disease progression.3 In contrast, patients with high-risk features, such as rapidly progressive disease, crescentic lesions, or high phospholipase A2 receptor titers, may require the addition of immunosuppressive therapy from the outset.3

RAAS Inhibitors (Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme Inhibitors and Angiotensin Receptor Blockers)

The KDIGO 2021 clinical practice guideline for management of GD recommends using a RAAS inhibitor (either an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor or an angiotensin receptor blocker [ARB]), first line, at the maximum tolerated dose for treatment of primary adult GD in individuals presenting with both hypertension and proteinuria (Fig 1).3 However, a RAAS inhibitor should be used as a first-line therapy owing to their antiproteinuric effects, even in the absence of hypertension. Indeed, RAAS inhibitors may reduce proteinuria by up to 40%-50% in a dose-dependent manner, especially if the patient also follows dietary salt restriction.3 A RAAS inhibitor should not be stopped if there is a modest and stable increase in serum creatinine levels (up to 30%).3 If hyperkalemia occurs as a side effect of RAAS inhibitor use, the KDIGO guideline recommends using potassium-wasting diuretics, treatments to correct metabolic acidosis, or oral potassium-binding agents rather than reducing the dose or stopping RAAS inhibitor treatment. For patients with primary adult IgAN, RAAS inhibitor treatment should continue at the maximum tolerated dose unless the patient has a high risk of progression (proteinuria > 0.75-1 g/d despite ≥90 days of optimized foundation pharmacologic care).

Nonimmunosuppressive Alternatives/Add-ons to RAAS Inhibition

Patients who have resistant hypertension or who are intolerant to angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor or ARB therapy may be prescribed an available mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (MRA) (second line) owing to the antihypertensive and antiproteinuric effects of this drug class (Fig 1), but patients should be monitored closely for hyperkalemia risk.3 Alternatively, patients may take a direct renin inhibitor as an alternative to a RAAS inhibitor (Fig 1). Patients who cannot take an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor or ARB, a direct renin inhibitor, or an MRA may be prescribed a nondihydropyridine calcium channel blocker (third line) (Fig 1) such as diltiazem or verapamil to reduce proteinuria, although the proteinuria-reducing potential of these drugs is much less vs that of RAAS inhibitors.3

Patients at Risk of Disease Progression Despite Guideline-Recommended Foundation Therapy

If foundation therapy does not improve proteinuria to target levels, then add-on nonimmunosuppressive drugs should be considered where appropriate. These add-on therapies have different mechanisms of action and include SGLT2 inhibitors,45,46 finerenone47 (a nonsteroidal MRA), sparsentan11 (an endothelin and angiotensin II receptor antagonist), atrasentan14 (an endothelin receptor antagonist), iptacopan16 (a complement inhibitor), and semaglutide48 (a glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist [GLP-1 RA]). These add-on nonimmunosuppressive drugs are described in the upcoming section.

SGLT2 Inhibitors

SGLT2 inhibitors have shown significantly greater efficacy in CKD (with or without diabetes present) relative to standard of care (RAAS inhibitor). SGLT2 inhibitor effects include reducing glucose reabsorption in the proximal tubule, preventing the downstream reabsorption of sodium, and causing vasoconstriction of the afferent arteriole, which decrease intraglomerular pressure and reduce proteinuria (such as albuminuria).50 The indications for these drugs include CKD, as an antiglycemic agent in type 2 diabetes, and as an add-on treatment in heart failure,45,46,51, 52, 53, 54 although they are not indicated for specific types of primary GD. A prespecified analysis from the placebo-controlled DAPA-DKD phase 3 trial involving the SGLT2 inhibitor dapagliflozin showed a statistically significant reduced risk of multiple adverse outcomes vs placebo in patients with primary IgAN. These included the primary outcome of a composite of sustained ≥50% decline in the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), onset of kidney failure, or death from a kidney disease–related or cardiovascular cause in patients with primary IgAN (n = 270) vs placebo.7 In another prespecified analysis from the DAPA-DKD trial, dapagliflozin reduced the risk of eGFR decline in patients with biopsy-confirmed FSGS (n = 104), although this was not significant vs placebo.8 The SGLT2 inhibitor empagliflozin has also shown greater efficacy (reduced risk of kidney disease progression and eGFR decline) relative to placebo across different primary GD types (at a high progression risk).9

Nonsteroidal MRA: Finerenone

Finerenone is a nonsteroidal MRA that is indicated in the United States for use in adult patients with CKD associated with type 2 diabetes and in adult patients with heart failure with left ventricular ejection fraction ≥ 40%.47 Finerenone is being investigated in clinical trials for broader use in nondiabetic CKD and GD.

Endothelin Receptor Antagonists

Sparsentan is a dual endothelin-angiotensin II receptor antagonist indicated in the United States for reduction of proteinuria in adults with primary IgAN who are at risk of kidney disease progression (urine protein-creatinine ratio ≥ 1.5 g/g).11 Results from the phase 3 active controlled PROTECT trial showed that proteinuria was reduced to a significantly greater extent with sparsentan vs the ARB irbesartan (at the maximum tolerated dose) in adult patients with IgAN.55 However, sparsentan has a black box warning for hepatotoxicity and embryofetal toxicity, so close monitoring during treatment for at-risk patients is advisable.11 Sparsentan has also been investigated as a potential treatment option for patients with primary FSGS, but results from a phase 3 trial showed a numerically but not statistically greater reduction in proteinuria with sparsentan vs irbesartan.56

Atrasentan is a selective endothelin type A receptor antagonist recently approved in the United States for primary IgAN.13,14 An interim analysis from the phase 3 placebo-controlled ALIGN trial in patients with primary IgAN showed that treatment with artrasentan resulted in a significant and clinically meaningful reduction in proteinuria compared with patients taking placebo (mean % change in urine albumin-creatinine ratio: −38.1% vs −3.1%, respectively [95% confidence interval, −44.6 to −26.4; P < 0.001]).13

Complement Inhibitor

Iptacopan inhibits the action of factor B in the alternative complement pathway and, similar to sparsentan, is indicated in the United States for reduction of proteinuria in adults with primary IgAN who are at risk of rapid disease progression.16 This approval is based on the results from a prespecified interim analysis from the ongoing APPLAUSE-IgAN phase 3 trial (which also includes a roll-over extension safety study [Table 1]) in which a 38.3% reduction in proteinuria was observed with iptacopan vs placebo (P < 0.0001).15

GLP-1 RAs

GLP-1 RAs are indicated for the treatment of type 2 diabetes, as cardioprotective agents, and more recently as a treatment option for adults with CKD associated with type 2 diabetes. The kidney protective effects of GLP-1 RAs could also potentially extend to included treatment of primary adult GD, although this has not been tested. GLP-1 RAs, such as semaglutide, reduce albuminuria and are associated with kidney and cardiovascular protective effects in patients with CKD or obesity with or without diabetes.57, 58, 59, 60, 61 It is important to consider that some patients with primary GD, particularly those with FSGS and metabolic comorbid conditions, may already be receiving GLP-1 RA therapy for weight or glycemic control. As GLP-1 RAs are associated with reduction in albuminuria, their concurrent use could potentially confound assessments of proteinuria-lowering efficacy in clinical trials or real-world studies of other investigational therapies.

According to the KDIGO 2021 guideline, patients who are at a high risk of progression or who have already progressed despite foundation therapy may receive an immunosuppressive drug. A detailed discussion of the therapeutically available immunosuppressive drugs is beyond the scope of this review.

Conclusions

An important pathophysiologic component of primary GD is the upregulation of pathways that cause chronic kidney damage and hemodynamic dysfunction, and this upregulation is an important target for pharmacologic treatments. Foundation therapy for primary adult GD centers on taking a RAAS inhibitor drug to the maximum tolerated dose, plus patient-centered lifestyle optimization, such as eating a low-salt diet, reducing protein consumption, and maintaining a healthy weight. The focus of foundation therapy (low- or moderate-risk patients) is to reduce proteinuria and to stabilize blood pressure, ultimately to reduce a person’s risk of kidney disease progression. Patients may be offered immunosuppressive drug therapy (eg, glucocorticoids) if they are still at a high risk of kidney disease progression despite foundation therapy.3 The effectiveness of immunosuppressive therapy varies depending on the type of primary GD being treated; in addition, immunosuppressive drugs are associated with significant side effects if used in the longer term, and some patients cannot take these types of drugs because of specific comorbid conditions (such as diabetes, obesity [body mass index > 30 kg/m2], and/or active peptide ulcer) or contraindications.3 Thus, there is a clear need for nonimmunosuppressive drug treatments for adult patients with primary GD who are at risk of kidney disease progression despite foundation therapy.

SGLT2 inhibitors, as well as sparsentan and atrasentan, have shown efficacy with acceptable tolerability in adults with IgAN or primary FSGS. It is important to note that the results with SGLT2 inhibitors are from prespecified analyses from phase 3 clinical trials that had mixed-etiology CKD patient populations, and that the indications for SGLT2 inhibitors are not for specific types of primary adult GD (although they are for CKD). More recently, results from an interim analysis of a phase 3 trial showed efficacy of iptacopan in primary IgAN.15 Sparsentan, iptacopan, and atrasentan were recently approved for use in adult primary high-risk IgAN in the United States, so they represent newer treatment options. Phase 3/4 clinical trials of all 3 drugs/classes are ongoing (Table 1). Finerenone is an emerging drug treatment option for primary GD with phase 3/4 clinical trials in nondiabetic CKD, IgAN, and MN ongoing (Table 1). The GLP-1 RA semaglutide was recently approved by the Food and Drug Administration for use in patients with CKD associated with type 2 diabetes,48 but data are needed to establish the potential benefit of this drug for use in primary adult GD. Notably, GLP-1 RAs may reduce albuminuria independently of glycemic control, raising the possibility that background use of these agents, particularly in patients with FSGS and metabolic comorbid conditions, could contribute to observed reductions in proteinuria and confound interpretation of treatment response in both clinical practice and trials.

The effect of finerenone treatment is also being explored (phase 3) in other therapy areas, including in patients with CKD associated with type 1 diabetes (FINE-ONE; NCT05901831), across different symptomatic heart failure subtypes (results from the FINEARTS-HF trial [NCT04435626] in patients with heart failure with preserved or mildly reduced ejection fraction were recently published62), and in children and young adults (aged 1-18 years) with CKD and proteinuria (FIONA OLE; NCT05457283). Other therapy areas of investigation with iptacopan (phase 3) include atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome and C3 glomerulopathy (source: clinicaltrials.gov). Sparsentan is not being investigated in phase 3 clinical trials outside of IgAN and FSGS.

In summary, there are several new and emerging nonimmunosuppressive drug treatments being investigated for use in adult patients with primary GD who are at risk of kidney disease progression despite foundation therapy. Although these therapies show promise, there are shared challenges across all agents that may limit their clinical uptake. These include a high drug cost,63 restricted access or reimbursement in some regions, and uncertainty about long-term safety and efficacy, especially given the relatively short duration of most current trials. In addition, side effect profiles may differ across agents but remain incompletely characterized in real-world populations. Participant recruitment in randomized controlled trials for primary adult GD remains suboptimal, limiting generalizability. Collaborative efforts, such as global patient registries and anonymized data sharing, are essential to address these gaps.
