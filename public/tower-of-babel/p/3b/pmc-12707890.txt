Efficacy and Safety of Protamine Use following Transcatheter Aortic Valve Implantation for Minimizing Post-Procedural Bleeding Risk: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Arwi Omar Kara, Mohammed S. Beshr, Ali M. Abdelaziz, Alhasan S. Alzubi, Arowa Hassan Abdulrahman Alansari, Esraa Arhaym, Dania Mukhtar Aburawi, Mostafa El Awami, Abdelrahman Farag, Heyam Almezghwi, Anas Atiyah Abdulsalam, Hatim Nasruldin Shahin, Muhammed Elhadi, Mamas Mamas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTranscatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) may be complicated by postoperative bleeding due to heparin use. Heparin antagonists, such as protamine, have shown potential in reducing bleeding risks. This meta-analysis assesses the efficacy and safety of protamine in reducing bleeding complications after TAVI.MethodsA systematic search was conducted up to March 2025. Inclusion criteria encompassed studies reporting the use of protamine post-TAVI with a control group. Primary outcomes included major bleeding events as the efficacy endpoint, 30-day mortality, and ischemic events (stroke and transient ischemic attack, or TIA) as safety endpoints. Secondary outcomes included life-threatening bleeding events and the need for blood transfusion. A random-effects model was used to calculate odds ratios (ORs).ResultsFour studies involving 1,569 patients were included. As compared to the control group, protamine was not associated with a statistically significant difference in major bleeding events (OR = 0.59, 95% CI = 0.26–1.34, p = 0.21), life-threatening bleeding events (OR = 0.3, 95% CI = 0.06–1.62, p = 0.16), need for blood transfusions (OR = 0.75, 95% CI = 0.46–1.24, p = 0.27), 30-day mortality (OR = 1.07, 95% CI = 0.54–2.11, p = 0.85), or rate of stroke and TIA (OR = 0.86, 95% CI = 0.05–13.58, p = 0.91).ConclusionProtamine use after TAVI appeared to be safe with no increase in 30-day mortality or stroke and TIA rates, but there was no observed statistically significant benefit in the reduction of major or life-threatening bleeding events and the need for blood transfusions. These findings are based on a limited number of studies, and larger randomized controlled trials are warranted to confirm them.

Introduction

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is a rapidly evolving standard of therapy for patients with severe, symptomatic aortic stenosis. It has become the preferred method for aortic valve replacement in such cases. This procedure is particularly beneficial for individuals over 75 years old who are deemed inoperable or considered at high risk for surgery [1].

A crucial part of the procedure involves administering heparin to achieve an active clotting time of 250–300 s. This helps reduce the risk of thromboembolism and catheter thrombosis during the procedure [2]. One of the complications of heparin administration is postoperative bleeding in patients who have undergone transfemoral TAVI, and it can be life-threatening [3].

In recent studies, Heparin antagonists, such as protamine, have shown potential in mitigating this complication [4, 5]. However, their underpowered nature makes it difficult to determine whether their administration may increase the risk of ischemic events.

In this meta-analysis, we aimed to assess the potential role of protamine in reducing the risk of bleeding in patients undergoing TAVI. To evaluate its safety, we also assessed the 30-day mortality rate and the thrombotic risk associated with ischemic events, such as stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA).

Methods

The study was conducted in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [6] and adhered to the PRISMA 2020 standard reporting guidelines [7]. The PRISMA checklist is provided in online supplementary Table S4 (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000549115). The protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the registration number CRD42024525031.

Search Strategy

We systematically searched the literature in the following databases: PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, Virtual Health Library, and Google Scholar using relevant search terms including “protamine” and “Transcatheter aortic valve implantation.” A manual citation-based search was also conducted to identify any further studies that may have been missed in the literature search. The detailed search terms for each database are included in online supplementary Table S1.

Inclusion Criteria

We included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and observational studies (both prospective and retrospective) that reported the use of protamine in patients undergoing TAVI, aiming to reduce the bleeding risk compared to administering a placebo or no treatment.

Exclusion Criteria

We excluded studies that did not report our outcomes of interest, studies with overlapping data or without full-text availability, review articles, case reports, and editorials.

Study Selection and Data Extraction

Following the established inclusion and exclusion criteria, eight reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts using the Covidence online platform, with any conflicts resolved by the first authors. The first authors subsequently assessed full texts of potentially relevant studies and discussed resolving any remaining discrepancies to confirm eligibility for final selection.

The primary outcomes were major bleeding events (efficacy endpoint), a 30-day mortality rate, and any events of stroke or TIAs as safety endpoints. The secondary outcomes were life-threatening bleeding events and the need for blood transfusions.

All endpoints are defined according to the Valve Academic Research Consortium 2 standardized definitions [8]. Data extraction was systematically performed using an Excel sheet and organized into three sections: an overview of the included studies, baseline characteristics of study subjects, and our review outcomes. Two reviewers initially performed the extraction and subsequently reviewed it twice by two independent reviewers to ensure accuracy and consistency.

Critical Appraisal Tool and Risk of Bias Assessment

Quality assessment of RCTs was conducted using the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials (ROB-2) [9] and the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale for observational studies [10]. Two reviewers independently conducted the quality assessment and addressed any conflicts through discussion.

Statistical Analysis

Meta-analysis was performed using Review Manager (The Cochrane Collaboration, London, UK). A random-effects model was applied, and I2 statistics were used to assess heterogeneity. We calculated the effect size using the odds ratio (OR) with a 95% confidence interval, and a p value of <0.05 indicated statistical significance. A sensitivity analysis was conducted to evaluate the impact of each study on the overall effect size.

Publication Bias

We could not assess publication bias using Egger’s test since we did not have a minimum of 10 studies for any of the outcomes, which is necessary to achieve reliable results.

Risk of Bias Assessment

RCTs were appraised using the Cochrane risk of bias version 2 (ROB2) tool, with the ACE-PROTAVI trial [13] showing a low risk of bias and the trial by Zbroński et al. [12] (2021) showing a high risk of potential bias since the outcomes were not independently adjudicated, the investigators were unblinded, and there was no reported information on the randomization process. The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale was used to evaluate both observational studies. Studies scoring 7–9 were considered high quality, while those scoring ≤6 were considered low quality. Accordingly, both observational studies were considered high quality. Online supplementary Table S2 and online supplementary Figure S1 present a detailed illustration of the risk of bias assessment.

Meta-Analysis of the Primary Outcomes

Efficacy Endpoint (Major Bleeding Events)

Out of 955 patients who received protamine, 25 experienced major bleeding events, compared to 58 patients out of 599 in the control group, with no statistically significant difference observed between the two groups (OR = 0.59, 95% CI = 0.26–1.34, p = 0.21; Fig. 2a).

Meta-analysis of primary outcomes. a Major bleeding events. b 30-day mortality. c Stroke/TIA Events.

Safety Endpoints

As compared to the control group, 30-day mortality rate was less frequent in the protamine group (24/955 vs. 17/599 in protamine and control groups, respectively), while the rate of stroke and TIA was more frequent in the protamine cohort (7/86 vs. 15/200 in protamine and control groups, respectively), with no statistically significant difference between both groups in either outcome (OR = 1.07, 95% CI = 0.54–2.11, p = 0.85 for 30-day mortality and OR = 0.86, 95% CI = 0.05–13.58, p = 0.91 for stroke and TIA; Fig. 2b, c).

Meta-Analysis of the Secondary Outcomes

As compared to the control group, both life-threatening bleeding events and the need for blood transfusion were less frequent in patients who received protamine (0.6% vs. 3.5% with respect to life-threatening bleeding events in protamine and control groups, respectively) and (9.4% vs. 20.2% as regards blood transfusion events in protamine and control groups, respectively, with no statistical difference between both groups in either outcome (OR = 0.3, 95% CI = 0.06–1.62, p = 0.16 for life-threatening bleeding and OR = 0.75, 95% CI = 0.46–1.24, p = 0.27 for blood transfusions; Fig. 3a, b).

Meta-analysis of secondary outcomes. a Life-threatening bleeding events. b Blood transfusion events.

Sensitivity Analysis

A sensitivity analysis excluding Zbroński et al. [12] (2021), due to the high risk of bias, did not alter the results of the primary analysis. The detailed findings are presented in online supplementary Table S3.

Discussion

Bleeding complications post-TAVI associated with intra-procedural heparin use remain a concern in clinical practice [14]. Heparin reversal using protamine may play a role in mitigating this risk. In our analysis, we evaluated the role of protamine in decreasing major and life-threatening bleeding events in four studies comprising 1,569 patients. We also evaluated some of its potential adverse events, including 30-day mortality and ischemic events.

The analysis revealed no significant benefit of using protamine post-TAVI to reduce major and life-threatening bleeding events or the need for blood transfusions. Similarly, assessment of potential adverse events of protamine, such as ischemic events (e.g., stroke or TIA) and 30-day mortality, showed no difference between the protamine group and the control group, indicating a potentially safe profile for protamine when used to reverse heparin in post-TAVI patients.

In contrast to our findings, a meta-analysis of 6,762 patients evaluating the safety of protamine after percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) showed a significant reduction in major bleeding complications in the protamine group, although there were no significant differences in short-term mortality or the incidence of myocardial infarction [15]. In a prospective registry of 4,587 patients who underwent carotid endarterectomy and received protamine to reverse heparin, the analysis showed a decrease in bleeding risk without any increase in adverse outcomes such as stroke, myocardial infarction, or death [16], further confirming the safety of protamine use.

The differential findings between TAVI and other cardiovascular procedures may reflect several factors. First, the bleeding mechanisms in TAVI are multifactorial, involving not only systemic anticoagulation but also access site trauma, device-related vascular injury, and patient-specific factors such as advanced age and comorbidities that are prevalent in the TAVI population [3]. Second, the anticoagulation protocols and heparin doses used in TAVI may differ from those in PCI or surgical procedures, potentially affecting the efficacy of protamine reversal.

The interpretation of our findings must consider the quality and heterogeneity of the included studies. Our meta-analysis included two randomized controlled trials and two observational studies. The ACE-PROTAVI trial [13] demonstrated low risk of bias using the Cochrane ROB-2 tool, representing the highest quality evidence in our analysis. However, the trial by Zbroński et al. [12] (2021) showed a high risk of bias due to a lack of independent outcome adjudication, unblinded investigators, and insufficient information about the randomization process.

Both observational studies [4, 11] were considered high quality based on the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, scoring 7–9 points. However, observational studies are inherently subject to selection bias and confounding factors that may influence treatment decisions and outcomes. The heterogeneity in study designs, patient populations, and protamine dosing protocols across the included studies may have contributed to the lack of statistical significance in our pooled analysis.

Moreover, moderate heterogeneity in bleeding outcomes may reflect differences in study design, protamine dosing and timing, and variations in patient risk profiles or procedural characteristics. These factors underscore the need for standardized protocols and additional randomized trials to more clearly define the role of protamine after TAVI.

The mechanism of protamine action involves rapid neutralization of heparin through electrostatic binding, forming inactive complexes that restore normal coagulation function [17–19]. However, protamine itself possesses anticoagulant properties, particularly when administered in excess of the heparin present in the circulation. This dual nature of protamine underscores the importance of appropriate dosing strategies to maximize efficacy while minimizing potential adverse effects.

The studies included in our meta-analysis employed varying protamine dosing protocols, which may have influenced the observed outcomes. Optimal protamine dosing remains a subject of ongoing research, with evidence suggesting that protamine-to-heparin ratios should be carefully calibrated to avoid both under-reversal and over-reversal of anticoagulation. Future studies should standardize dosing protocols and consider patient-specific factors that may influence protamine requirements.

Despite the lack of statistical significance in our primary endpoints, the consistent numerical trends favoring protamine use across multiple outcomes suggest potential clinical benefits. The safety profile observed in our analysis, with no increased risk of mortality or ischemic events, supports the consideration of protamine use in selected TAVI patients, particularly those at high risk for bleeding complications.

The decision to use protamine should be individualized based on patient-specific factors, including baseline bleeding risk, procedural complexity, and institutional protocols. Patients with preexisting coagulopathy, advanced age, low body weight, or renal dysfunction may be at higher risk for bleeding complications and could potentially benefit from protamine administration.

Limitations

Our review included only a small number of studies. The mixed design of the included studies in our review was due to the limited availability of randomized controlled trials in the literature; only two randomized clinical trials were identified, while the other two were observational studies. This may introduce potential variability in the data due to differences in study protocols and patient selection criteria. Patient selection bias might be present, particularly in the cohorts, where treatment decisions were not randomized and could be influenced by underlying patient conditions or physician preferences. Due to these limitations, it is difficult to draw any recommendations for clinical practice. Randomized controlled trials with large sample sizes would provide valuable insights into whether routine protamine use should be implemented post-TAVI or if it should be limited to selected cases.

Recommendations for Future Research

To explore the potential benefits of protamine administration post-TAVI, large, sufficiently powered RCTs are necessary to evaluate protamine efficacy and safety. Future studies should incorporate standardized bleeding definitions, preferably using the updated Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 (VARC-3) criteria for bleeding outcomes [20], and employ consistent protamine dosing protocols based on current best evidence.

It would also be beneficial to study its application in patients with a higher preoperative bleeding risk, as these patients would benefit the most from such an intervention. Risk stratification tools could help identify patients most likely to benefit from protamine administration, enabling a more personalized approach to bleeding prevention in TAVI.

Additionally, future research should investigate optimal protamine dosing strategies, taking into account factors such as patient weight, renal function, baseline coagulation status, and the heparin dose. Point-of-care coagulation testing may help guide individualized protamine dosing and assess the adequacy of heparin reversal.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our analysis suggests that protamine administration following TAVI is potentially safe but not associated with clinical benefits in reducing major or life-threatening bleeding events. However, the analysis was based on a small number of studies. Large randomized controlled trials are needed to establish the safety and efficacy of protamine use after TAVI, as well as to determine whether routine protamine use should be implemented after TAVI or if it should be limited to selected cases.
