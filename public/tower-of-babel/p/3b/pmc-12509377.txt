Dental outcomes following treatment of pediatric odontogenic cysts: an 18-year retrospective analysis
Authors: Selen Onat Kayıkcı, Ezgi Ergezen Arık, Gaye Mısırlıoğlu, Caner İncekaş, Seçil Çubuk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis 18-year retrospective analysis of pediatric odontogenic cysts reviewed characteristics, treatment approaches, and associated risks, including root canal treatment (RCT) and tooth extraction.MethodsThe clinical records of patients treated for odontogenic cysts between 2005 and 2023 included demographics, symptoms, radiographic findings, cyst size and location, histopathological diagnoses, treatment methods, and follow-up results were analyzed. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS, with significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsAmong the 97 patient records analyzed, there were 43 females (44%), and 54 males (56%) with a mean age of 13.32 ± 3.55. The histopathological diagnosis were dentigerous cysts (n = 58, 60%), followed by radicular cysts (n = 28, 29%) and odontogenic keratocysts (n = 9, 9%), mainly in the posterior mandible. Root canal treatment (RCT) was performed in 18% of the patients, and 72% underwent extraction of at least one permanent tooth. The need for RCT and extraction was higher in larger cysts significantly (p = 0.013). The recurrence rate was low, occurring in 3.1% of with two cases of dentigerous cysts and one odontogenic keratocyst recurring, with an average follow-up of 46 months.ConclusionThe findings suggest that mandibular posterior cysts in the pediatric population often needed tooth extraction, underscoring the importance of early diagnosis by radiographic screening to prevent complications. There is a need for multi-center collaboration and national registries for development of protocols and uniform treatment guidelines in pediatric population.Keywords: Pediatric lesions, Odontogenic cysts, Root canal treatment, Tooth extraction

Introduction

Pediatric jaw cysts represent clinically important lesions with the potential to cause significant functional and developmental impairments. They may adversely affect facial growth and compromise the integrity of the dental arch by damaging unerupted permanent teeth or precipitating the premature loss of primary teeth. Such disturbances can result in space loss, malocclusion, delayed tooth eruption, and complex long-term rehabilitative needs [1]. Within the pediatric population, odontogenic cysts constitute a considerable proportion of jaw lesions, although available data remain limited. Reported prevalence rates vary from 60% (n = 50) to 80% (n = 596) [2, 3]. Similarly, one recent study reported that 73% (n = 73) of benign pediatric jaw lesions were odontogenic cysts [4].

Odontogenic cysts can typically be managed successfully with conservative surgical techniques. However, since these cysts are usually localized within the bone, they have a negative impact on dental health by causing periodontal tissue loss in adjacent teeth, potentially leading to the loss of multiple teeth. This can be especially concerning in growing children as tooth loss during this stage interferes with proper dental development and alignment. Furthermore, the rehabilitation of partial edentulism is often achieved with removable prostheses, in such cases until skeletal development is completed [5].

Since most pediatric bone lesions are asymptomatic, they are usually noticed accidentally by radiological imaging [6]. When symptoms are present, they tend to be non-specific, such as swelling, facial asymmetry, pain, and mobility of teeth [6]. Any disturbance in the development of the maxillary or mandibular bone or uneven tooth eruption in children should prompt suspicion of a potential jaw lesion [7].

The 2022 World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Odontogenic and Maxillofacial Bone Tumours [8], published only five years after the previous edition (2017), reflects the impact of rapidly evolving molecular technologies and their potential clinical relevance [9]. In the 2022 classification of Head and Neck Tumors (5th edition) by the WHO, odontogenic cysts continued with mostly the same entities, but the subheadings were grouped as “jaw cysts” eliminating the previous distinction between inflammatory or developmental origin [8, 10, 11].

Among the notable changes, the post-surgical ciliated cyst has been incorporated into the classification for the first time [8]. This uncommon lesion arises from the traumatic implantation of maxillary sinus or nasal mucosa, is most frequently diagnosed in the fifth to sixth decades, and is typically associated with previous maxillofacial surgical interventions [12, 13]. The definition of calcifying odontogenic cyst has been revised to emphasize the presence of ghost cells, with its pathogenesis linked to CTNNB1/Wnt pathway mutations [14]. For glandular odontogenic cyst, the presence of hobnail cells is now considered the most characteristic histological feature, and rare cases have demonstrated MAML2 rearrangements previously regarded as exclusive to central mucoepidermoid carcinoma [15]. The odontogenic keratocyst remains classified as a cyst, with PTCH1 mutations representing the most common genetic alteration [16], and ongoing molecular research may support the development of future non-surgical therapeutic approaches [17, 18].

Although the literature suggests that dentigerous and radicular cysts remain the most prevalent lesions in children [19], there remains a paucity of studies that reveal the distribution, clinical and radiographic features of pediatric jaw cysts based on the 5th WHO classification of Head and Neck Tumors [3].

Previous research on oral and maxillofacial lesions has often been limited in scope concerning pediatric and adolescent populations, primarily focusing on adults or specific lesion types. Consequently, there is a lack of comprehensive data on lesion distribution, age-related prevalence, anatomical localization and treatment outcomes in children. This study aims to address this gap by evaluating the characteristics of pediatric odontogenic cysts diagnosed over an 18-year period at a single center. Additionally, it seeks to identify risk factors associated with root canal treatment (RCT) and tooth extraction in the management of these lesions.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

This study is a retrospective cohort study and has been prepared in accordance with the STROBE checklist criteria [20]. It was approved by Başkent University Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee (Project no: D-KA23/17). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The records of patients treated in the Başkent University Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery between January 2005 and August 2023 were retrospectively evaluated. An electronic search was performed in the NUCLEUS v9.40.267 Patient Information System of Başkent University using the following search terms: “oral biopsy,” “odontogenic cyst enucleation,” “odontogenic lesions,” and “oral pathology.” Subsequently, the retrieved records were screened by the authors, and patients meeting the inclusion criteria were enrolled in the study. Demographic data, symptoms, radiographic findings, location of the cyst, histopathological diagnosis (according to the 2022 WHO classification of jaw lesions), treatment modalities and type of anesthesia (local or general anesthesia) were recorded and analyzed. Data extraction was conducted by a single reviewer (SC) who followed a standardized protocol. To ensure intra-rater reliability, the data were re-evaluated by the same reviewer after a one-month interval. All patients underwent enucleation, and definitive diagnoses were established through microscopic examination of the entire excised lesions by pathologists, who also determined the lesion size by measuring the maximum diameter of the macroscopic surgical specimen with a ruler. The incidence of recurrence, time to recurrence, and follow-up duration were also recorded.

Treatment protocol

The treatment planning and management of all patients were conducted in a multidisciplinary manner within the Departments of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery, Orthodontics, Pediatric Dentistry (for patients aged < 12 years), and Endodontics (for patients aged ≥ 12 years) at Başkent University. The treatment decision-making was based on clinical and radiographic assessments, and the overall therapeutic approach is summarized in a flow diagram (Fig. 2).

Flow diagram illustrating the clinical decision‑making protocol for the management of odontogenic cysts in pediatric patients

Surgical procedures were performed under local or general anesthesia, depending on the patient’s age, cooperation level, and lesion characteristics. A mucoperiosteal flap was elevated and the overlying bone was removed to access the cystic lesion. Complete enucleation of the cyst was performed, and if indicated, the associated tooth was extracted. In cases where the cyst was related to the root apex of teeth that underwent endodontic treatment, apical resection was performed concurrently with the enucleation. The surgical site was irrigated and sutured for primary closure. Postoperative instructions and follow-up appointments were provided in accordance with institutional guidelines.

For cases in which preservation of the associated tooth was clinically indicated, endodontic treatment was completed at least 7 days prior to surgical enucleation. Root canal therapy was performed following standard endodontic protocols, including mechanical instrumentation, chemical irrigation with sodium hypochlorite and EDTA, and obturation using gutta-percha with lateral condensation technique.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of quantitative variables was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Non-normally distributed data were reported as median (min–max), while qualitative variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between cyst types (dentigerous, radicular, other) were conducted using the Kruskal–Wallis test, with Dunn–Bonferroni post-hoc tests when significant. Associations between cyst type and qualitative variables were evaluated using the Chi-square test or Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test. Effect sizes were calculated using Cramer’s V. For the relationship between age group and cyst type, Cohen’s w was 0.312, yielding a post-hoc power of 79.1% (df = 2, α = 0.05; G\*Power 3.1), indicating moderate statistical power. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Odontogenic cysts constitute the majority of benign jaw lesions. The response of odontogenic cysts to surgical treatment is generally good, with a low recurrence rate. On the other hand, they may reach to large size within the bone resulting to loss of multiple teeth loss and severe alveolar bone deformity [8, 10]. In addition, only 43% of the residual cavity can undergo spontaneous bone healing 12 months after odontogenic cyst enucleation, which may complicate the rehabilitation of the edentulous alveolar bone with an implant-supported prosthesis [21].

In general population, marsupialization is recommended for cysts larger than 4 cm size [22]. Öztürk et al. [23] demonstrated that decompression significantly improved bone healing in pediatric patients with non-syndromic odontogenic cysts, as indicated by the progressive increase in fractal dimension values from baseline to the end of treatment. Their findings support the use of conservative approaches in children, given that decompression achieved comparable bone quality to control areas while preserving growth and development. However, the long-term prognosis of teeth associated with the cysts following this protocol have not been reported [23]. Most of the reports in scientific literature on this topic are case reports, and as a result, there is a lack of adequate scientific data and consensus regarding the optimal surgical approach for treating odontogenic cysts in the pediatric population [24].

While the average cyst size in our cohort was relatively small, about 72% of patients required at least one permanent tooth extraction during surgery. Furthermore, 18% of the patients underwent RCT. Based on the findings of our study, which showed a positive correlation between cyst size and the mean number of teeth that underwent at least one procedure, including extraction or RCT, it is crucial to diagnose and treat odontogenic cysts as early as possible to minimize the risk of tooth loss and the need for RCT.

Consistent with our findings, most studies identify the dentigerous cyst as the most common odontogenic cyst in the pediatric population. The variation in the distribution of developmental cysts between adult and pediatric patients are likely because these lesions begin to form during infancy [25]. This is related to the ongoing growth and development of the teeth that persist throughout puberty [26].

Typically, a dentigerous cyst is associated with an unerupted tooth, most frequently mandibular third molars, but it can also occur with maxillary third molars, maxillary canines, and mandibular second premolars [27]. However, this distribution may vary in pediatric patients [2]. In our study, we found a similar frequency of associations with mandibular third molars, maxillary canines, and mandibular canines, while notably, no dentigerous cysts were found to be developed from maxillary third molars. In the retrospective cohort by Aly et al., the dentigerous cyst was also found to be the most common developmental cyst in the pediatric population, most frequently detected in the mandible, consistent with our results [28].

In our study, dentigerous cysts predominated over radicular cysts in the < 12 group (81% vs. 16%), likely due to the longer time required for radicular cyst development and the accelerated formation of developmental cysts during growth [29]. Overall, odontogenic cysts were more frequent in adolescents than in younger children (67% vs. 33%), which may be explained by less frequent radiographic examination, making diagnosis more challenging. Dentigerous cysts are commonly associated with maxillary canines and third molars, whose eruption typically occurs after age 12, further contributing to their higher prevalence in adolescents. Consistent with our findings, Aly et al. also reported a higher frequency of odontogenic cysts in patients over 11 years of age [28].

The treatment of dentigerous cysts typically emphasizes the preservation of the affected tooth if there is a possibility for the permanent tooth to spontaneously reposition within the dental arch or with orthodontic intervention [24]. A recent systematic review reported that 6 out of 17 permanent teeth (35%) underwent extraction during the enucleation of dentigerous cysts [24]. However, in our cohort, which involved a larger sample size, 67.5% of the permanent teeth—excluding third molars and supernumerary teeth—required extraction during enucleation of dentigerous cysts. This notable difference may be attributed to several factors, such as the varying size and location of the cysts, or the treatment approach. Additionally, the larger sample size in the present study could provide a more representative view of the general trends in dentigerous cyst management in pediatric population. Further research with larger, multi-center cohorts may help clarify the factors.

In the present study, we observed a significant distribution of periapical cysts within odontogenic cysts, accounting for approximately 30% of the cases. This finding highlights the importance of understanding the diverse nature of odontogenic cysts and their varied clinical presentations [3, 30, 31]. The treatment of periapical cysts is highly dependent on the size of the cyst, with options ranging from conservative RCT for smaller lesions to surgical intervention and tooth extraction for larger cysts. Our findings indicate that approximately 44% of the teeth treated for periapical cysts were upper incisors, followed by lower incisors and lower molars. This suggests a higher predisposition for periapical cysts to affect the upper incisor region, possibly due to their anatomical position and increased vulnerability to trauma, which is often associated with pulp necrosis. Additionally, early eruption and the greater exposure to caries risk may contribute to the increased incidence of periapical cysts in this region. This highlights the importance of early diagnosis and preventive care, as timely intervention can help mitigate the progression of decay and avoid the need for more invasive treatments.

The treatment of periapical cysts in our cohort was focused on cases requiring surgical intervention, as only those with larger cysts were included in the study. Consequently, our findings primarily apply to large periapical cysts requiring surgical management, and may not be representative of smaller cysts, which can often be treated conservatively.

Odontogenic keratocysts was reported one of the most frequent jaw cysts in pediatric population. It was originally reclassified as a “keratocystic odontogenic tumor” in 2005 [32]. However, the term odontogenic keratocyst was restored by the WHO in 2017 and confirmed again in the 2022 review. In our study, the incidence of odontogenic keratocysts was lower compared to previous studies in the pediatric population [7, 30]. This difference may also explain the discrepancy in recurrence rates of odontogenic cysts, particularly considering the lower recurrence rate observed in our study compared to previous reports [7, 30].

Calcifying odontogenic cysts (COCs) were first described by Gorlin et al. in 1962 and are typically observed as cystic lesions, although they can occasionally present as solid, tumor-like growths. The 2022 WHO classification recognizes COCs with ghost cell features as being classified into cystic, solid, or malignant types. The mean age of diagnosis is around 30 years, with the majority of cases occurring in patients aged between 20 and 40. COCs associated with odontomas are more commonly seen in younger patients, with a mean age of 17 years. In our study, two pediatric patients aged 14 and 15 were diagnosed with COCs, but no odontomas were found in these cases.

The challenge of detecting asymptomatic lesions in pediatric patients is well recognized, particularly given the limitations of conventional imaging techniques during the mixed dentition phase. Current American Dental Association guidelines (2012) recommend against the routine use of OPG in children and adolescents, except when clinically indicated [33]. As a result, asymptomatic odontogenic lesions, which often do not present with noticeable clinical symptoms, can be difficult to identify at an early stage.

In our study, OPG was the primary imaging technique utilized for all patients, aligning with standard clinical practices for pediatric dental care. While OPG provides valuable information, its limitations—particularly in terms of resolution and three-dimensional detail—become evident when assessing larger lesions or those located near critical anatomical structures, such as the inferior alveolar nerve or maxillary sinus. For these more complex cases, CBCT was employed as a secondary imaging tool, despite its higher radiation dose compared to conventional methods. In our study, around 32% of the cases required 3D imaging via CBCT, underscoring its utility in the comprehensive evaluation of odontogenic cysts and tumors.

This study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. Its retrospective design limits control over potential confounding factors and may introduce selection bias. Although the single-center setting ensured a standardized treatment protocol, procedures were performed by different senior surgeons, which might have introduced operator-related variability in outcomes. The unequal distribution of cyst subtypes within the sample may have restricted the accurate evaluation of certain variables, such as recurrence rates. Additionally, follow-up duration varied among patients, with some having only 6-month postoperative evaluations, potentially limiting the detection of late changes or recurrences.

Conclusion

The analysis of 97 patients reveals a predominance of dentigerous and radicular cysts, and a frequent occurrence in the mandibular posterior region. The findings of this study highlight the critical importance of early detection through radiographic screenings, particularly in asymptomatic cases. Additionally, the study underscores the challenges posed by cysts to adjacent teeth and the complexities involved in managing these issues in pediatric patients. Although the study is limited by its single-institution design, the findings contribute to a better understanding of pediatric jaw cysts and emphasize the need for continued research and the development of standardized treatment protocols to improve outcomes for affected children. Therefore, early identification and appropriate treatment of jaw cysts should be prioritized as a key strategy for optimizing patient care.
