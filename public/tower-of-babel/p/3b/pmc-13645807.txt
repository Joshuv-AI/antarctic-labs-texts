Home-based deep sleep modulation in Parkinson’s disease: extension study on long-term feasibility and sleep-related outcomes
Authors: Jana Horlacher, Nora-Hjördis Moser, Simon J Schreiner, Renske Sassenburg, Lydia Kämpf, Sophia D Schibler, Valentino Mutschler, Esther Werth, Philipp Valko, Jelena Skorucak, Laura Ferster, Walter Karlen, Reto Huber, Christian R Baumann, Angelina Maric
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study assessed feasibility and effects of a randomised two-week, at-home intervention with phase-targeted auditory stimulation (PTAS) of slow waves in Parkinson’s disease (PD). We reproduced previously reported enhancement of slow wave activity and confirmed indications of improved patient-reported outcomes. Together with the high adherence and data quality, this suggests that home-based PTAS protocols are a feasible method for investigating the long-term therapeutic potential of deep sleep enhancement in PD.Subject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Sleep-wake disturbances are among the most prevalent non-motor symptoms in Parkinson’s disease (PD)1,2. In this context, disruption of deep sleep, also known as slow wave sleep (SWS), is frequently observed in PD3,4 and has been associated with excessive daytime sleepiness (EDS), impaired cognitive function, and decreased quality of life1,5–8. While pharmacological methods for SWS enhancement have shown some success in improving subjective and objective symptoms of EDS in PD patients, they are accompanied by mild to moderate side-effects and de novo obstructive sleep apnea and parasomnia9,10. These limitations together with the importance of SWS for physical restoration, overall health and daily functioning11,12 emphasise the need for alternative methods to enhance SWS that are both safe and effective.

In recent years, phase-targeted auditory stimulation (PTAS) has emerged as a promising non-pharmacological method to modulate SWS by presenting auditory stimuli at a specific phase of slow waves13–17, the predominant electroencephalographic (EEG) oscillation during SWS. A recent proof-of-concept study in PD demonstrated that at-home PTAS applied over three nights can increase slow wave activity (SWA), namely the spectral power in the slow wave frequency range, in addition to first indications that this can lead to a reduction of subjective daytime sleepiness over time17. However, it remains unclear whether PTAS can be feasibly and effectively self-administered over extended time periods in these patients. Additionally, it needs to be clarified if subjective improvements go along with tangible, objective symptom improvements of sleep-wake disturbances.

This extension study aimed to (1) assess the feasibility, adherence, and data quality of a two-week home-based PTAS intervention, (2) reproduce initial proof-of-concept findings over a substantially longer period, and (3) evaluate the potential of PTAS to alleviate objective symptoms of daytime sleepiness. To this end, we conducted a 2 × 2-week randomised, sham-controlled, counterbalanced, double-blind cross-over study with 10 PD patients (see Fig. 1a for study design, and Table 1 for patient characteristics). A portable, self-applicable device (Tosoo Sleep Band AXO, Tosoo AG, Switzerland) was used for at-home sleep recording and PTAS administration (see Fig. 1b for PTAS administration).

The intervention was well tolerated, with all adverse events being non-serious, mild in severity and fully resolved without lasting effects. The ease of use, comfort, and instructional support were evaluated positively overall, even though the range of responses was large for some items (Table 2). We successfully recorded 271 out of 280 nights (97%), of which 267 (98%) yielded data of sufficient quality for further analyses. Non-recorded nights were primarily attributable to user-related factors, including forgetting to wear the device (n = 2), occasional technical issues (n = 2) and incorrectly connected electrodes (n = 5). Insufficient data quality was either caused by device malfunction (n = 2) or a lack of sufficient sleep (n = 2). In addition to the high proportion of successfully recorded nights, personalisation of algorithm settings for online sleep detection by the device resulted in a satisfactory high number of PTAS stimuli being applied (average of 2297 ± 990 stimuli per night with a maximum volume of 64.4 ± 6.4 dB).

Throughout the duration of the intervention, verum auditory stimulation resulted in increased SWA (spectral power between 0.75 and 4.5 Hz) during time windows in which stimulation was enabled (ON windows) relative to windows in which stimulation was disabled (OFF windows) (Fig. 2a). The effect of PTAS on SWA differed between ON and OFF windows, with ON window SWA being higher in the verum intervention than in sham, whereas OFF window SWA was significantly lower in verum than in sham (Fig. 2b). The increase in ON windows was accentuated in low frequencies (0.75–1.25 Hz), whereas the decrease was spread across a wider frequency range (Fig. 2c). There were no signs of changes in sleep macrostructure as determined by an automatic scoring algorithm (Supplementary Table 1).

Taken together, the present study demonstrates that self-administered, at-home PTAS in PD patients over two weeks is feasible, with excellent adherence and data quality. Application of PTAS reliably increased SWA, particularly in lower frequencies. Participants reported subjective improvements in their morning sleepiness, well-being, and unchanged sleep quality. However, no significant effects on daytime sleep propensity were observed.

The increase of SWA in low frequencies during stimulation windows, together with a subsequent broadband reduction during non-stimulation windows, without evidence for changes in sleep macrostructure is in line with other auditory deep sleep stimulation studies (e.g., refs. 14,15,17). The smaller SWA increase compared to the proof-of-concept study17 may reflect lower volume settings, calibrated to each participant’s minimal perceptual threshold in the current study. Notably, we did not see a reduction of the stimulation effect across two weeks, suggesting the absence of habituation. This is again in line with results from a study with healthy elderly16 and supports the potential of PTAS for sustained therapeutic use.

Unlike in some pharmacological studies9,10,18,19, our findings show no effect on objective (MSLT) or subjective daytime sleep propensity (ESS), as commonly used to define EDS. Reasons may be that pharmacological studies targeting EDS typically span longer periods, i.e., six to eight weeks in the above mentioned examples. Also, EDS was not an inclusion criterion. Consequently, only half of the participants exhibited clinically relevant ESS scores at baseline.

In contrast, we observed positive findings in other patient-reported outcomes (PROs), particularly on morning subjective sleepiness (KSS). As a state-sensitive measure, the KSS reveals the intensity with which sleepiness is subjectively perceived, rather than the mere tendency to fall asleep in specific situations. This effect may precede changes detectable in more stable measures of daytime sleep propensity (ESS, MSLT). Additionally, subjective sleep quality remained stable in the verum intervention but declined in the sham intervention. This suggests that active PTAS may offset potential discomfort from device use, supporting its overall tolerability.

PROs are essential for an intervention’s effectiveness and acceptability, since subjective improvements not only matter clinically but also foster treatment adherence - patients in routine practice are more likely to stick with a regimen when they experience tangible, immediate benefits20,21. In our study, several further factors likely contributed to the high feasibility and adherence. This included clear instructions and training, continuous support via phone with 24/7 availability, patient motivation, device usability, and daily data monitoring through remote access. Usability issues observed in this study have been addressed in later generations of the sleep device.

Limitations of this study include the selective inclusion of patients that would show a response to PTAS during the screening procedure. This strategy helps to reveal its potential but prevents conclusions about efficacy. The exclusion of patients with more severe disease (Hoehn and Yahr stage > III), deep brain stimulation implants and the small sample size limit generalisability, with the latter also restricting statistical power. Despite the longer intervention period compared to the proof-of-concept study17, the two-week duration remains relatively short. These factors may have contributed to the lack of observable effects on objective daytime sleepiness. Longer studies with extended intervention periods and targeted inclusion of patients with marked EDS are needed to evaluate clinical efficacy and durability of PTAS effects.

Growing evidence supports a bidirectional relationship between neurodegeneration and sleep: while the disease often produces sleep-wake disturbances22, SWS is associated with a slower progression of motor symptoms in PD23, and preclinical data suggests a role in disease pathophysiology24. This makes deepening sleep a promising strategy to interfere with disease progression. Viewed in this light, the results of our study position the home-based PTAS application as a suitable candidate to test the impact of deep sleep modulation on disease progression in long-term studies.

Altogether, this study demonstrates the feasibility and strong patient adherence to home-based, self-applied PTAS in PD. The portable sleep stimulation device proved technically reliable for both recording and stimulation without habituation. Despite limited objective effects, participants reported subjective improvements, alongside persistent modulation of SWA. These findings support the feasibility of PTAS as a non-pharmacological intervention and lay the groundwork for longer-term studies and real-life clinical applications aimed at reducing subjective sleepiness in patients with PD. Prolonged intervention periods may be necessary to determine effects on objective daytime sleepiness and may even be applied in the future to assess its disease modifying potential in PD.

Methods

Ethics

The study was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki principles and approved by the Cantonal Ethics Committee of Zurich (reference: BASEC 2020-02298) and Swissmedic, the Swiss agency for drugs and therapeutic products (reference 10000791), and initially registered on 29.01.2021 at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04736017, https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04736017?id=NCT04736017) and KOFAM (SNCTP000004218,https://www.humanforschung-schweiz.ch/de/studiensuche/studien-detail/54276#query=SNCTP000004218). Study protocols and statistical analysis plans can be found on ClinicalTrials.gov. Participants provided written informed consent prior to study inclusion and were reimbursed for their inconvenience and time taken in the study participation with CHF 1000.- upon completion of the study. Harms were defined and assessed according to standard clinical trial procedures, including adverse events (AEs) and adverse device effects (ADEs).

Study design

This extension study to Schreiner et al.17 was a single-centre, randomised, sham-controlled, counter-balanced, double-blind, cross-over trial with a 1:1 allocation ratio conducted at the Department of Neurology, University Hospital Zurich, Switzerland. Data collection took place between April 2021 and July 2022 and included six participants from the aforementioned proof-of-concept study17 and four newly enrolled participants. There was no direct patient or public involvement in the design, conduct, or reporting of this trial.

After completion of the first patient, some adaptations to the study protocol were made with regards to outcomes assessed and specifics in the stimulation protocol (see description of stimulation protocol below). We still included the patient in the analysis where possible but marked the corresponding data point in the figures.

While the proof-of-concept study limited the intervention period to three days, the current study design extended the intervention period to 14 consecutive days, separated by a wash-out period of at least two weeks (range 15 to 29 days). Participants applied self-administered, at-home PTAS, supported by tablet-based or computer-based questionnaires. Participant compliance was monitored daily by checking for successful data transmission and through an online platform that tracked questionnaire completion. Additionally, two on-site visits were performed after both interventions for the objective outcome assessment. Participants in both interventions continued to receive their standard PD treatment throughout the trial. No additional experimental co-interventions were provided.

Participants

Participants were recruited through multiple channels, including printed advertisements in the magazine of the Parkinson Schweiz organisation, local patient support groups, the movement disorders outpatient clinic at the Department of Neurology, University Hospital Zurich, as well as selected neurological practices or physiotherapists across Switzerland. Initial screening was conducted during a visit in the outpatient clinic at the Department of Neurology, University Hospital Zurich and in the participants’ homes, where experimenters introduced the study protocol, delivered the sleep device and a tablet, and provided comprehensive instructions along with hands-on training to ensure participants were comfortable using the equipment. Ongoing support was provided throughout the study via regular phone calls. Additionally, participants had 24/7 access to assistance and could contact the study team at any time if issues arose.

Screening nights were conducted in participants’ homes to configure individual parameters of the sleep detection and PTAS algorithm and to ensure suitability for at-home application. Data collected during these nights were used to assess the presence of a stimulus-induced response in the EEG upon PTAS and to titrate stimulation volume if necessary. Participants who showed a persistent lack of response (despite more than 500 stimuli being delivered and increasing volume)—defined as the absence of a visible event-related potential (ERP) averaged across all available stimuli in response to auditory stimulation—were excluded from further participation. Additional inclusion and exclusion criteria were comparable to the proof-of-concept study17, with the inclusion criteria encompassing a diagnosis of moderate PD (Hoehn & Yahr stages II-III), stable Parkinson medication, plus the ability to understand and follow the study procedures. In contrast to Schreiner et al.17, self-reported sleep disturbances were no inclusion criterion. The main exclusion criteria were the presence of other neurologic, psychiatric, or sleep disorders (such as sleep apnea or restless leg syndrome, Parkinsonism without response to levodopa, and cognitive impairment (defined as a Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) score below 24). Sleep apnea was screened at home using the Alice NightOne diagnostic system.

Randomisation & blinding

An experimenter without direct participant contact and not involved in data acquisition, processing, or analysis performed the randomisation. Simple, unrestricted randomisation was used without any stratification – only counterbalancing was ensured. Allocation was implemented in a decentralised manner: the randomiser programmed a TSB Axo stimulation device according to the assigned code, setting it to either verum (active stimulation) or sham (no stimulation). The device settings and randomisation table were not accessible to other study staff (i.e., experimenters in contact with participants) throughout study conductance. Also, participants were blinded to the intervention condition. Experimenters followed the same procedures in both intervention and identical devices with the same instructions and support were used across both intervention phases. Unblinding was performed for the statistical data analysis after data pre-processing (e.g., correction of automatic sleep staging).

Phase-targeted auditory stimulation (PTAS)

Similar to the proof-of-concept study17, acoustic stimuli (50 ms pink noise) were presented via headphones, targeting up-phases of slow waves in an ON-OFF windowed fashion (meaning alternating 6-second windows with stimulation enabled/disabled throughout detected NREM sleep, see Fig. 1b). The wearable system used for sleep recording and modulation was the TSB AXO (Tosoo Sleep Band AXO, Tosoo AG, Switzerland), a further development of the previously used Mobile Health Systems Lab (MHSL) SleepBand (MHSL-SB)16,17,25. The system used a sampling rate of 250 Hz and recorded one single EEG signal (FpZ-A2) for real-time processing and one EOG signal for post-processing purposes.

In the study’s initial protocol (subjected to n = 1), we used a phase-vocoder algorithm for EEG phase estimation26 and applied whole-night PTAS, as in the first subgroup of participants assessed in the proof-of-concept study17. Due to emerging evidence at that time that this paradigm might negatively affect mood through its impact on REM sleep16, we subsequently adapted the protocol (subjected to n = 9) to part-night PTAS (duration individualised to each patient), being limited to the first part of the night with its predominance of deep sleep. Additionally, the EEG phase estimation algorithm of slow waves was changed to a phase-locked loop to align with the proof-of-concept study17, except for the minimum inter-stimulus-interval which was now set to 1 second. Additionally, we changed the personalised volume setting procedure to start at quieter volumes just above hearing threshold to counteract potential arousals14,15. In both protocols, the stimulation volume increased within predefined limits during deep sleep in the absence of arousals and paused stimulation if an arousal was detected. EEG analyses of the data with the initial protocol were limited to the first three hours after the delivery of the first stimulus, to align with the part-night PTAS data of the adapted protocol. While the verum intervention delivered audible (adjusted to individual hearing threshold) stimuli, the sham intervention consisted of muted stimuli.

Sleep macrostructure

Sleep macrostructure of intervention nights was assessed using a proprietary, automated sleep-staging algorithm based on single-channel EEG (FpZ-A2), specifically tailored for the PD population. Developed using PD patient data recorded with previous versions of the same EEG system, engineered features, and expert-labelled scores, the algorithm employed a recurrent neural network for sleep stage classification. The algorithm provided staging outputs in three consolidated categories: WAKE/N1 (combining Wake and N1), NREM2/3 (combining N2 and N3), and REM sleep. The dataset comprised 142 nights from 49 PD patients: 39 patients (116 nights) were used for training and 10 patients (26 nights) for testing. On the test set, the algorithm achieved a Cohen’s kappa of 0.67 and an overall accuracy of 88%.

Finally, spectrograms of each night were compared to their scoring and manually corrected if notable irregularities were apparent in the beginning or end of the night. This was done to ensure proper identification of the sleep period. Sleep period was defined as the duration from the first NREM2/3 sleep epoch to the first epoch of final WAKE/N1, whereas WASO is defined as the time of WAKE/N1 during the sleep period. Awakenings are defined as the number of transitions from either NREM2/3 or REM epochs to WAKE/N1 during the sleep period. Sleep efficiency was calculated as the sum of NREM2/3 and REM epochs during the sleep period. REM sleep latency is reported from the start of the sleep period until the first epoch of REM sleep.

Sleep latency of daytime naps in the MSLT was assessed using standard clinical EEG montage, with scoring performed by certified sleep experts in accordance with the criteria of the AASM Manual for the Scoring of Sleep and Associated Events.

EEG spectral analysis

Semi-automated artifact rejection was applied to NREM sleep epochs using a single-channel implementation of the High-Density-SleepCleaner artefact removal routine27. EEG analysis was conducted in line with Lustenberger et al.16 and Schreiner et al.17. EEG data were bandpass filtered between 0.5 and 40 Hz using a two-step finite impulse response (FIR) filter implemented via the eegfilt function from the EEGLAB toolbox28 in Matlab (The MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA). Subsequently, power spectral density (PSD) estimates were computed using Welch’s method, implemented via the MATLAB’s pwelch function. A Hanning window of 4 seconds was applied with 50% overlap, resulting in a mean frequency response for each 6-second window. For latter analysis steps, only consecutive ON-OFF windows with at least one stimulation in the ON window classified as artefact-free NREM epochs were used. For consistency with previous publications mentioned above16,17, EEG power spectra were normalised to the average power between 0.5–20 Hz in each assessed frequency bin for the corresponding analysis (Fig. 2c).

Subjective & objective sleep-related outcomes

Subjective momentary sleepiness was assessed twice daily (morning and evening) at home using tablet-based questionnaires featuring the Karolinska Sleepiness Scale (KSS)29. The morning measures of the fourteenth morning were analysed as the final outcome, given that the fifteenth morning measurement was tainted by slightly earlier wake-up times due to the participants having to travel to the study site. In one case the first KSS measurement (sham) was missing and was replaced by the first measurement of the other intervention (verum).

Subjective daytime sleepiness across 2 weeks was assessed using the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS)30, while subjective well-being was evaluated with a Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) based on the question: ‘Overall, how satisfied have you been during the past two weeks?’. Additionally, sleep quality was evaluated through selected items from the Parkinson’s Disease Sleep Scale Version 2 (PDSS-2 subscale)31. The PDSS-2 is a validated self-administered questionnaire comprising three domains: nocturnal motor symptoms, PD-specific symptoms during the night, and disturbed sleep. For the present analysis, we focused on the sum of items 1–3 and 14—assessing poor sleep quality, difficulty falling asleep, difficulty maintaining sleep, and feeling tired or sleepy upon awakening—to focus on the most common PD-related sleep disturbances32,33. The ESS, VAS and PDSS-2 were assessed at the patient’s home pre-intervention and at the sleep laboratory of the University Hospital Zurich post-intervention. The MSLT, a clinical measure of objective daytime sleepiness, was administered to all patients at four standardised times (10:00, 12:00, 14:00, and 16:00) after the last intervention night. The sleep latency to N1 in minutes was taken as the outcome variable and was scored by certified sleep medicine specialists.

Statistical analysis

For statistical analyses of SWA and subjective outcomes, linear mixed-effects models were applied (lmer function, lme4 package in R), including participant ID as a random intercept to account for repeated measures within individuals.

For SWA across intervention nights (Fig. 2a; n = 267 nights from 10 participants), fixed effects comprised sequence (sham–verum vs. verum–sham), period (1 or 2), self-reported nocturnal sleep duration, intervention (verum vs. sham), night (1–14), and their interaction. SWA values were analysed as log10-transformed ON/OFF ratios to improve residual normality. For clarity, data are visualised as ON-to-OFF percentages.

For SWA during ON versus OFF windows (Fig. 2b; n = 534 windows, 267 nights × 2), the model additionally included a window × intervention interaction. EEG power spectra were log10-transformed prior to analysis. Model assumptions regarding residual distribution and homoscedasticity were verified visually (check_model, performance package). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using estimated marginal means (emmeans package) without adjustment for multiple comparisons, given the limited number of planned contrasts.

For spectral analyses across frequency bins (Fig. 2c; n = 1580 power values from 10 participants, 2 windows, 79 frequency bins), normalised EEG power spectra were compared between interventions (verum vs. sham) separately for ON windows (containing ≥1 stimulation) and their subsequent OFF windows. Relative differences were expressed as percentage change from sham. Linear mixed effects models with Kenward-Roger (degrees of freedom) and Benjamini-Hochberg correction (false discovery rate) were fitted per frequency bin. p ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant; values between 0.05 and 0.10 were interpreted as trends.

Subjective outcomes (KSS, ESS, PDSS-2 subscales, and well-being; n = 36–40 observations from 9–10 participants × 2 interventions × 2 time points) were analysed using separate mixed-effects models including sequence, period, sleep duration, intervention, time point (pre/post), and their interaction as fixed effects.

To evaluate factors influencing wake probability during the MSLT, a Cox proportional hazards mixed-effects model was used (coxme package in R; n = 80 observations, events = 70) with participant ID as a random intercept. Fixed effects included self-reported nocturnal sleep duration, daytime sleepiness (ESS), intervention, nap number (1–4), and all interactions between ESS, intervention, and nap number. A survival approach was chosen because sleep onset could be right-censored if participants remained awake throughout the nap. Mean sleep latency per nap was analysed using a linear mixed-effects model with fixed effects for intervention, nap number, and their interaction, and a random intercept for participant ID. 95% confidence intervals were reported alongside point estimates. The main effect of intervention (verum vs. sham) constituted the primary comparison of interest.

Across all analyses, significance was set at α = 0.05 (two-tailed). Sample size was based on power analyses from previous studies9,10. No interim analyses were performed.
