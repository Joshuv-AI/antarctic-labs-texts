Frequency of suspected anemia in Japanese children aged 1–5 years using noninvasive hemoglobin measurement and influence of iron-fortified growing-up formula intake
Authors: Shinji Jinno, Megumu Igawa, Yoshitaka Nakamura, Fusako Mitsuhashi, Chiharu Tsutsumi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly childhood anemia can impair growth and neurodevelopment. Although noninvasive hemoglobin (SpHb) measurement is a promising alternative to blood sampling, its large-scale use in pediatric populations remains limited. This observational study estimated the frequency of suspected anemia in Japanese children aged 1–5 years and examined the association between Growing-up Formula (GuF) intake and SpHb in the highest-use age band (12–23 months).MethodsOverall, 13,642 children were recruited from November 2022 to August 2024. Hb levels were noninvasively measured using the Masimo Rad-67 device. Suspected anemia was defined as an SpHb level < 11.0 g/dL for children aged 12–59 months and < 11.5 g/dL for those aged 60–71 months. Multiple regression analysis in the 12–23-month subgroup was performed to evaluate the association between SpHb levels and GuF intake, after adjusting for age, sex, birth weight, and school lunch consumption.ResultsOf the 12,714 eligible children aged 1–5 years, 4.1% were identified as having suspected anemia (range: 2.3%–6.6% across subgroups). In a subgroup of 2,500 children aged 12–23 months, the age group with the highest GuF use in the study population, GuF intake volume was positively associated with SpHb levels (p = 0.02, R² = 0.012).ConclusionsThis study describes the frequency of suspected anemia among Japanese children aged 1–5 years (4.1%) and shows that higher daily GuF intake was associated with higher SpHb levels in children aged 12–23 months, although GuF intake explained only a small proportion of the variance in SpHb levels.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12887-026-07289-1.Keywords: Anemia, Children, Formula, Hemoglobin, Nutrition, Public Health

Category of study

Population study.

Impact

This study provides the first large-scale estimate of the frequency of suspected anemia among Japanese children aged 1–5 years by using noninvasive hemoglobin measurements.

The results demonstrated clear age- and sex-specific trends in hemoglobin levels across a population of over 12,000 participants.

The analysis identified a significant association between iron-fortified formula intake and higher hemoglobin levels in children aged 12–23 months.

These findings support the use of noninvasive screening and targeted dietary strategies as practical tools to inform nutritional policies and prevent early childhood iron deficiency.

Background

Iron deficiency anemia in infants and young children is a common and serious nutritional problem worldwide and is associated with impaired neurodevelopment and behavior [1]. Infants have high iron requirements owing to rapid growth, and by around 6 months of age, iron stores present at birth are depleted [2]. Therefore, young children are particularly vulnerable to iron deficiency [3]. Iron deficiency in young children is a significant concern, particularly regarding its impact on neurodevelopment and psychomotor development [4, 5], emphasizing the need for early diagnosis and intervention [6]. Estimates of anemia in young children vary widely, ranging from 2.0 to 3.2% in Australia and the US to 53.5% in Sudan [6–9], reflecting the varying levels of nutritional deficiencies and healthcare access across different regions. In Japan, 8% of infants and children aged 6–18 months were found to be anemic [10]. We previously estimated that 5.1% of Japanese children aged 1–5 years were suspected to be anemic and found that hemoglobin (Hb) concentrations in young children correlated with both sex and age in months [11]. However, owing to limited research focusing on young children in Japan, the prevalence of anemia in this population remains unclear.

Traditional methods for assessing Hb levels in the blood often involve invasive blood sampling, which can be distressing for young children and poses logistical challenges for large-scale screening. Noninvasive Hb measurement techniques, such as the use of Pulse CO-Oximeter Rad-67™ Spot Check (Rad-67) (Masimo Corp., Irvine, CA, USA), offer a convenient and child-friendly alternative for estimating Hb levels. These devices use spectrophotometry to analyze light absorption through the skin, providing a rapid and painless noninvasive assessment of Hb (SpHb) levels. This technology could facilitate widespread anemia screening in pediatric clinics and health checkups. Although the accuracy of Rad-67 is not guaranteed across all populations [12–16], a study conducted in Japanese young children showed a good correlation between noninvasively measured and laboratory-measured blood Hb levels, with minimal bias between the two measurement methods (correlation coefficient r = 0.548, bias: 0.188 ± 0.919 g/dL) [17].

Iron fortification in foods, including formulas for children, can be a critical public health strategy aimed at improving the health and nutritional status of young children. Observational research indicated that current or past “young-child formula” use was independently associated with better iron status at 24 months of age than non-use [18]. In clinical trials, formulas fortified with iron and other essential micronutrients have been shown to significantly enhance the iron and vitamin D statuses of young children [19, 20]. In Japan, multiple brands of iron-fortified growing-up formula (GuF) designed for children aged 12–36 months are commercially available, with iron contents ranging from approximately 1.1 to 1.4 mg per 100 mL across products. However, there is no clear evidence of their effectiveness.

This observational study aimed to assess the frequency of suspected anemia, defined as SpHb levels below the World Health Organization (WHO) age-specific threshold, in young children, as an update of a previous report [11]. The present study also examined the relationship between daily GuF intake and Hb levels in the age subgroup in which GuF use is most common in Japan. These finding would contribute valuable data for our understanding of the effectiveness of iron fortification in child nutrition and prevention of anemia in Japan.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional, observational study was conducted in Japan between November 2022 and August 2024. Participants were recruited from retail stores in collaboration with Meiji Co., Ltd.; the details are provided in the interim report [11]. Nutritionists or registered dietitians from Meiji Co., Ltd. measured SpHb levels by using the Rad-67 device, as detailed in the Data Collection Procedure section. After measuring SpHb levels, the willingness of the children’s parents or legal guardians (surrogates) to participate in the study was confirmed. Verbal informed consent was obtained from all parents or guardians who agreed to their children’s participation. Children whose surrogates consented to the use of their data for research were included as study participants. SpHb levels were measured using a 1-point reading only. No follow-ups were performed. The inclusion criteria were as follows: children aged between 1 and 5 years who participated in in-store events, had their SpHb levels measured, and whose parents or legal guardians understood the research purpose and content and agreed to participate. The only exclusion criterion was previous participation in the study.

The study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee for Research Involving Human Subjects at Sagami Women’s University (approval number: 22134; approval date: November 1, 2022). The study protocol adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was conducted only after verbal consent was obtained from the children’s legal guardians. This study was registered with UMIN-CTR (registration number: UMIN000049469).

Data collection procedures

Data collection involved noninvasive measurements and a questionnaire survey administered to parents or guardians. Trained nutritionists and registered dietitians affiliated with Meiji Co., Ltd. performed noninvasive Hb measurements using the Rad-67 device. This device uses a multiwavelength sensor and LEDs (500–1400 nm) to measure light absorption in capillary beds (e.g., fingertips, hands, and feet) during the pulse cycle. The detector converts the received light into electronic signals, which are analyzed using proprietary algorithms to calculate functional oxygen saturation (SpO₂ [%]), total Hb concentration (SpHb [g/dL]), and pulse rate. According to the manufacturer’s specifications, the validated measurement range for SpHb is 8–17 g/dL, and the overall 95% limits of agreement versus a reference method were + 2.07 and − 1.82 g/dL (bias 0.1 g/dL; precision 1.0 g/dL) under controlled conditions. Additionally, background information was collected through face-to-face interviews using a questionnaire administered to the participants’ surrogates. The questionnaire collected data on the child’s age (in months), sex, birth weight (less than 2,500 g or not), school lunch consumption (applicable or not applicable), and consumption of iron-fortified GuF formulated for children aged 12–36 months that is commercially available in Japan (applicable or not applicable). Collection of detailed information on GuF consumption, including intake frequency and serving size, was started in January 2024. Before December 2023, the questionnaire collected only qualitative data (i.e., the presence or absence of consumption) regarding GuF intake. Therefore, the analysis involving the intake amount or frequency was limited to participants enrolled from January 2024 onward.

Frequency of suspected anemia

In children aged 1–5 years, SpHb measurements were tabulated according to sex and age and presented as mean (standard deviation) values. This is because sex and age have been shown to be associated with SpHb measurements [11]. The percentage of children whose SpHb levels fell below the WHO threshold for anemia was calculated as an indicator of suspected anemia [21]. These thresholds are < 11.0 g/dL for children aged between 12 and 59 months and < 11.5 g/dL for children aged between 60 and 71 months.

Association between GuF intake and SpHb

Multiple regression analysis was performed to evaluate the relationship between SpHb levels and various factors. Because GuF use rates in children may vary by age, the analysis was restricted to the most common age group for GuF use in the study population (12–23 months) to reduce potential bias. The model included age, sex, low birth weight, school lunch consumption, and GuF intake volume as covariates, as described in the subsequent section. In this subgroup, the distribution of the SpHb measurements was confirmed using histograms.

Potential covariates and confounding factors in multiple regression analysis

GuF intake volume was incorporated as a primary variable because iron-fortified GuF with essential micronutrients improves iron and vitamin D levels in young children [19, 20]. The following factors were considered in the analysis, based on a previous report [11]: age, sex, birth weight, school lunch consumption, and amount of iron-fortified GuF intake per day. Age and birth weight were selected as confounding factors because previous studies have revealed their relationship with blood Hb level in young children [22, 23]. Sex was included as a covariate because sex differences in blood Hb concentration are observed after puberty [24]. Additionally, school lunch was included as these lunches are designed to meet children’s nutritional needs and may provide nutrients such as iron, which are necessary for maintaining Hb levels. We also collected information on additional iron sources, including consumption of iron-fortified foods other than GuF, formula use from 6 to 12 months of age, and prescription iron medication use. A dedicated item for iron-containing multivitamins was not included; depending on caregiver interpretation, some supplements may have been reported under ‘iron-fortified foods other than GuF.’

Statistical analysis

Missing data were handled according to the purpose of the analysis. For descriptive analyses, participants with missing responses for specific variables were included where applicable, and the denominators were adjusted accordingly. In contrast, multiple regression analysis was conducted using complete case analysis, and participants with missing data for any covariates were excluded. Consequently, the exclusion criteria were not uniformly applied across analyses. All analyses were performed using R Software version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria, https://www.R-project.org) and SPSS software (version 25.0; IBM Corporation, New York, USA).

Results

Participants

Between November 2022 and August 2024, informed consent was obtained for 13,642 children (Fig. 1). Of these, 928 were excluded from the suspected anemia analysis because they did not meet the age criterion (< 12 or ≥ 72 months; n = 680) or had missing information on sex (n = 57), age (n = 223), or birth weight (n = 19) (missing-variable counts were not mutually exclusive). The remaining 12,714 children aged 12–71 months (1–5 years) were included in the descriptive analysis of suspected anemia. Daily GuF intake was categorized into four groups: none, 1–100 mL/day, 101–200 mL/day, and ≥ 201 mL/day and the percentage of GuF use was higher among children aged 12–23 months than among children in other age groups, with lower use observed in older age groups (Table 1). For the analysis of the association between GuF intake and SpHb, regression analyses were restricted to children aged 12–23 months. Specifically, 10,214 children were excluded because they were enrolled before December 2023 and were not asked additional GuF-related questions (n = 7,390), were outside the 12–23-month age group (n = 2,748), or had missing data (n = 76), leaving 2,500 children for the subgroup regression analysis. Measurements were conducted at baby-specialty retail stores primarily in major urban areas, and the recruitment sites were not geographically uniform across Japan. Missing-data exclusions were limited, accounting for 248 of 12,962 children (1.9%) in the descriptive analysis and 76 of 2,576 children (3.0%) in the 12–23-month regression analysis. SpHb level and frequency of suspected anemia by sex and age

The mean SpHb level in the study population (aged 12–71 months) was 13.2 g/dL. SpHb levels according to sex and age are shown in Table 2. The mean SpHb levels in boys aged 12–23, 24–35, 36–47, and 48–59 months were higher than the corresponding levels in girls (p < 0.05) (Table 2). The mean SpHb levels increased with age. The overall frequency of suspected anemia (SpHb levels below the WHO thresholds) in children aged 1–5 years was 4.1%. The frequency according to sex and age ranged between 2.3% and 6.6% across different subgroups, as shown in Table 2.

Association between GuF intake and SpHb levels

Multiple regression analysis was used to examine the factors associated with SpHb levels in the 12–23-month subgroup, where GuF use was most common. Multiple regression analysis incorporated age (months), sex, birth weight, school lunch consumption, and daily GuF intake as variables. The analytical sample consisted of 2,500 children aged 12–23 months, the most common age group for GuF use in the study population (Fig. 1; Table 1). The distribution of SpHb levels in this subgroup is shown in Fig. 2. Regression analysis showed that age and sex were independently correlated with SpHb levels (p < 0.01 and p = 0.02, respectively). Daily GuF intake also showed a significant relationship with SpHb levels (p = 0.02) (Table 3). The coefficient of determination of the model remained at 0.012, indicating a poor fit. Sensitivity analyses that additionally adjusted for other iron-fortified foods, formula use in 6–12 months of age, and current iron prescription use produced materially similar estimates (Supplementary Table S1). Unadjusted comparisons of SpHb across daily GuF intake categories are shown in Supplementary Table S2. Additional cross-tabulation analysis among 2,500 children revealed that the proportion of children consuming school lunches was significantly lower among GuF users (17.0%) than among non-users (24.2%) (p < 0.05, chi-square test).

Discussion

This study aimed to estimate the frequency of suspected anemia among Japanese children aged 1–5 years and evaluate its association with the intake of GuF in the 12–23-month subgroup, where GuF use was found to be most common in the study population. The study identified the frequency of suspected anemia (4.1%) among Japanese children aged 1–5 years. A key finding of the study was a significant positive association between GuF intake and SpHb levels in the 12–23-month subgroup. Although the observational nature of this study limits definitive causal inferences, GuF may play a role in improving Hb levels, potentially mitigating the risk of anemia at this crucial stage of development. As limited data are available on the prevalence of anemia among young children in Japan, this large-scale investigation provides a valuable foundation for understanding the nutritional needs of this population and offers insights that could inform targeted nutritional recommendations.

The observed frequency of suspected anemia (4.1%) in Japanese children aged 1–5 years in this study was higher than the rates reported in other developed countries such as Australia and the US (2.0%–3.2%). However, cross-study comparisons should be interpreted cautiously because hemoglobin values are assessed using different methods (e.g., non-invasive SpHb vs. laboratory hemoglobin) and because the magnitude and direction of measurement bias may vary by population and setting. For SpHb, a study in Thai infants reported an estimated bias of 1.007 g/dL16; however, as noted in the Introduction, prior Japanese research suggests that such overestimation is minimal in young children [17]. Even with this caution, the frequency of suspected anemia reached 6.6% in some sex- and age-specific subgroups, suggesting that young Japanese children may be more susceptible to anemia than those in other developed countries. The median iron intake for children aged 1–2 years in Japan is 3.7 mg for boys and 3.3 mg for girls [25]. This intake is below the latest recommended dietary allowance (RDA) of 4.0 mg in Japan [26] and markedly below the RDA of 7.0 mg in the US. One possible reason for this international difference is the relatively low consumption of iron-fortified flour and cereals by children in Japan, coupled with the difficulty in obtaining the adequate amount of iron solely from common foods. Fortifying commonly consumed foods with iron could help address this nutritional gap among Japanese children.

Intake of iron-fortified formulas may contribute to increased SpHb levels in young Japanese children. Since a non-significant and slight relationship between the presence or absence of GuF intake and SpHb level was observed in the interim analysis [11], we evaluated the relationship between the “amount” of GuF intake and SpHb level in a separate population not included in the interim analysis. Notably, this analysis revealed a significant association. This discrepancy may be largely explained by changes in data collection methodology over time. In an earlier phase of the study, GuF intake was assessed only qualitatively, which may have reduced the sensitivity for detecting associations. In contrast, the latter dataset captured intake frequency and quantity, enabling a more precise evaluation. Accordingly, the analysis in this study relies exclusively on this more detailed dataset. This correlation aligns with findings from intervention studies in which GuF intake improved the iron status in young children [19, 20]. Based on the iron content of the GuFs commercially available in Japan (1.1–1.4 mg per 100 mL), a child consuming 200 mL/day would receive approximately 2.2–2.8 mg of additional iron, corresponding to 55%–70% of the Japanese RDA for children aged 1–2 years (4.0 mg/day).26 However, in this cohort, daily GuF intake volumes were generally low, and higher daily volumes were uncommon; therefore, these findings may not be directly generalizable to settings where substantially higher daily GuF intake is typical. Although a significant association was observed in the 12–23-month subgroup, the coefficient of determination (R²) for both regression models remained notably low (0.012). This limited explanatory power is consistent with the multifactorial nature of suspected anemia in early childhood and may reflect residual confounding by unmeasured determinants (e.g., detailed dietary and supplement intake, anthropometry and socioeconomic factors). Future studies with longitudinal follow-up and repeated assessments would help clarify temporal relationships.

In addition to GuF intake, an association was observed between age (months) and sex. This was confirmed in the interim analysis [11]. These findings are supported by the fact that an age-related increase in blood Hb levels has been reported in healthy children aged 6–59 months [22]. The observation of similar associations across multiple distinct study populations suggests that these findings may provide more robust insights.

To examine whether GuF intake might serve as a marker of overall favorable feeding habits, we additionally assessed the relationship between GuF use and school lunch consumption. School lunch consumption was significantly less frequent among GuF users than among non-users, indicating that GuF intake and solid food consumption represent independent dietary patterns in this age group, rather than co-occurring components of a generally healthier diet. This finding supports the interpretation that the observed positive association between GuF intake and SpHb levels reflects the specific contribution of iron fortification in GuF. However, as this cross-tabulation analysis was unadjusted for age and other covariates, residual confounding by developmental stage or unmeasured dietary and lifestyle factors cannot be excluded.

This study has several limitations. First, SpHb is a noninvasive screening measure and cannot provide hematologic indices; thus, it can identify overt suspected anemia but cannot distinguish latent iron deficiency, confirm iron-related anemia, or rule out other etiologies without laboratory testing. Second, we did not obtain laboratory Hb measurements from participants’ blood samples; however, prior studies have reported correlation with minimal bias [17], supporting the validity of our screening approach. Nevertheless, field-based measurements in young children may still be influenced by child-related physical factors (e.g., body size/weight, small finger size and sensor fit, peripheral perfusion, and skin/tissue characteristics) and measurement conditions (e.g., movement during measurement), which could contribute to variability in SpHb values. Third, because this was a large-scale, in-store screening program, we minimized participant burden and measurement time; therefore, we did not collect anthropometry or comprehensive dietary/supplement intake data, and residual confounding cannot be excluded. Fourth, recruitment at baby-specialty stores in major urban areas may limit representativeness and introduce selection bias; a nationwide screening program would be needed to estimate the frequency of suspected anemia more accurately. Missing-data exclusions were limited (248/12,962 [1.9%] and 76/2,576 [3.0%]), but procedures were occasionally discontinued to avoid distress, and complete-case analyses may still introduce selection bias. Fifth, the cross-sectional design precludes assessment of temporal relationships, and longitudinal studies with repeated measurements are warranted. Despite these limitations, the large sample provides baseline data on the frequency of suspected anemia among young Japanese children.

Conclusion

This observational study estimated that suspected anemia occurred in 2.3%–6.6% of Japanese children aged 1–5 years in various age- and sex-based subgroups. Furthermore, the study findings demonstrate that higher daily GuF intake was associated with higher SpHb levels in children aged 12–23 months, suggesting a potential, albeit modest (R² = 0.012), role in preventing iron deficiency anemia. The limited explanatory power of the model underscores that GuF intake is one of many factors influencing hemoglobin levels in young children. However, further investigations are required to determine the clinical relevance and long-term impact of this association.
