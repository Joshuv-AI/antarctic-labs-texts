Impact and resiliency of the early phase of the COVID-19 pandemic on healthcare providers and their household members
Authors: Hannah Jang Kim, Minh Nguyen, Meghan Duck, Yin Lo, Vivian Huang, Lori Kennedy, Carol Dawson-Rose, OiSaeng Hong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The COVID-19 pandemic has a significant impact on the health of healthcare providers and their families. The purpose of this study is to assess the impact of COVID-19 on the resiliency of frontline healthcare providers and their household members. A cross-sectional survey was conducted in 2 different medical centers. The Brief Resilience Scale (BRS) assessed resiliency, and the Impact of Event Scale-Revised (IES-R) assessed subjective self-reported distress caused by traumatic events. Fisher tests was used hypothesis testing of differences in BRS and IES-R scores, between healthcare versus non-healthcare providers. Random forest models were used to identify the top contributors to BRS and IES-R scores. Participants also provided free text responses to describe main losses in social and support networks. A total of 1271 participants completed the surveys. The majority (95%) of the participants were self-identified healthcare providers. The mean (SD) BRS and IES-R score for non-healthcare providers (n = 63) were 20.1 (SD 5.2) and 24.0 (SD 16.4), respectively. The average score of IES-R indicated a concern for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). The average score of the BRS was under the range of normal resilience (18–25.9). There were no statistically significant differences in resiliency (p = 0.90) and impact of event (p = 0.59) between healthcare and non-healthcare provider household members. Having someone to confide in is one of the most important factors contributing to resiliency and impact of event. The average IES-R score suggested that all participants were impacted by the COVID-19 pandemic and demonstrated the same level of resiliency.

Introduction

In the last two years, the COVID-19 pandemic has made a significant impact on the health of healthcare providers and their families around the world, both physically with illness and hospitalization, as well as emotionally with burnout and distress1,2. As the pandemic has continued, its consequences on the healthcare industry and the resiliency of healthcare providers and their family members globally have been profound, but not fully exposed using validated tools.

Background

As research studies have delved to explore the impact of COVID-19 on social behavior, resiliency, and mental status, and what predictors of positive changes in behavior could be, consistently the only predictor of adherence to guidelines such as social distancing and hand hygiene was actual fear of COVID-19 that increased people’s compliance3. Additionally, analyzing what resilience means to individuals, and using validated scales, demonstrates how the surges of COVID-19 and the ongoing pandemic has impacted resiliency among these study participants. Healthcare providers faced unexpected, uncertain, and novel events leading to various stress reactions. Perceived risk and fear of COVID-19 positively predicts depression, stress, and anxiety, while resilience mitigates these conditions4.

Resilience is defined as the ability to bounce back or recover from stress and may provide unique and important information about people coping with health-related stressors according to the Brief Resilience Scale5. When healthcare providers are faced with a long-term pandemic, the ability to bounce back, or resiliency is regularly tested. It is incredibly important to understand the resiliency of our healthcare providers and their families/households to help guide what follow up and resources are needed to be prioritized or developed for them. An integrative review found that healthcare providers worldwide had moderate resiliency scores, with data from the United States showing a decrease in nurse resilience, whereas other countries and increased resilience when compared to pre-pandemic levels6. Machine learning techniques on mental health during COVID-19 also found that the most important factor predicting mental health decline was the role of the individual, specifically a nurse, emergency room staff, and surgeon7. Another systematic review using random forest models predicted mental health conditions8. There are limited studies assessing family members or those living with healthcare providers’ resiliency and the impact of COVID on their mental health.

Healthcare providers can utilize emotion-centered coping to manage stress associated with the pandemic and substantial evidence supports effective coping behaviors, resilience, and social support are associated with positive mental health outcomes9. Additionally, the COVID-19 virus has affected our Black and non-white populations more adversely than other racial groups10,11. Furthermore, with negative public sentiment due to COVID-19 being of Asian origin, there has been an increase in Asian hate crimes, which could lead to a disparate psychological impact on this population12.

Many studies explored healthcare providers’ resiliency and impact of COVID-19 to themselves, but few studies have explored family or roommates of healthcare providers who lived with them, or household members, during the pandemic. One qualitative study (da Silva Barreto 2022) explored coranophobia, fear of COVID-19 especially for those who lived with healthcare providers at the peak of the pandemic. A systematic review assessed the impact of occupational stress on healthcare providers’ family members and found mental health outcomes like worry and anxiety, family relationships and functioning, coping skills and resilience, quality of life and social life, and practical outcomes like domestic responsibilities and impact on living location were common themes in healthcare providers’ family members13. The significance of this research study is to assess the impact of COVID-19 on healthcare providers and their household members, people who live with them during a time of a contagious pandemic, which differ from family members, who may or may not live with the healthcare provider.

The purpose of this study is to assess the resiliency and impact of COVID-19 on frontline healthcare providers compared to their household members in two different medical centers in northern California. The study hypothesized that healthcare providers would report stronger resiliency and stronger impact of the pandemic than a non-healthcare provider household member.

Methods

Study design, participants, and procedure

This is a cross-sectional survey study that utilized self-administered online questionnaires by volunteer participants. The study was conducted at two university-based health systems in a western state in the United States and their household members. The online Qualtrics survey could be accessed via two options: a web link or a Quick Response (QR) code that was the same across sites. The weblink and QR code for the survey were distributed through internal organizational list-serves at all UC locations and the QR code was also printed on paper fliers and posted in various physical locations at each site’s hospitals. Participants could visit the link or scan the QR code to access and complete the online survey via their smartphones. The survey was completed between September 15, 2020 and January 8, 2021. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. All analysis was conducted using R version 4.1.2, with caret package for random forest modeling.

Ethics

The University of California, San Francisco and the University of California, Davis approved all study procedures and data collection. All participants provided informed consent electronically to study involvement prior to completing the questionnaires online. Participants who were an employee of the medical systems as well as having a partner or living with someone in the medical systems were included in the study. The data that support the findings of this study can be available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Data collection

Demographics and work-related variables: The survey questionnaire asked participants about their age group, gender at birth, self-identified gender, race, income, education, employment status, marital status, work type (healthcare provider vs not a healthcare provider), work area, profession, patient population served, whether they have children under 18-years-old, live alone or with others, any deaths in the family and if they had anyone to confide in and all questions from the Brief Resilience Scale (BRS)5 and Impact of Event Scale-Revised (IES-R)14. Healthcare providers included the following professions: nurses (Registered Nurses, Clinical Nurse Specialists, Nurse Practitioners), other healthcare provider not listed, physician, healthcare administrator, physical therapist or occupational therapist, healthcare analyst, respiratory therapist, nursing assistants, lab technicians/phlebotomists, nutritionists/registered dieticians, unit clerk, and physician assistant/physician associate. The survey also included a free-text response for the participants to share additional stories or comments about the impact of the pandemic on their lives that were not addressed by our survey questions. Texts were analyzed by parsing words, and words were aggregated into a word cloud.

The Brief Resilience Scale (BRS) is a 6-item self-report measure assessing the responder’s ability to bounce back or recover from stress5. Each item uses a 5-point scale ranging from 1 to 5 (1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = neutral, 4 = agree, 5 = strongly agree), amounting to a total score ranging from 6 to 30. A per-item average score of (1.00–2.99) or a total score of (6–17.9) is interpreted as low resilience. An average score of (3.00–4.30) or a total score of (18–25.9) is interpreted as normal resilience. An average of (4.31–5.00) or a total of (26–30) is interpreted as high resilience15. BRS is among the most used resilience scales known for its satisfactory psychometric properties16,17. The Cronbach’s alpha of the BRS in this study was 0.85, indicating good reliability and internal consistency18.

The Impact of Event Scale-Revised (IES-R) is a 22-item instrument that was used to measure subjective self-reported distress caused by traumatic events14. Survey respondents rated each item based on how distressing each potential difficulty was for them over the past 7 days during the survey period at the start of the COVID-19 pandemic. Each item was rated on a 5-point scale from 0 (‘Not at all’) to 4 (‘Extremely’)19. Three subscales evaluate the perceived intrusion, avoidance, and hyperarousal, as well as a total score, are calculated by summing their respective items. The intrusion subscale reflects intrusive thoughts, nightmares, intrusive feelings and imagery, dissociative-like re-experiencing. The avoidance subscale reflects numbing of responsiveness, avoidance of feelings, situations, and ideas. The hyperarousal subscale reflects anger, irritability, hypervigilance, difficulty concentrating, and heightened startle. Subscale scores for both intrusion and avoidance range from 0 to 32 and 0–24 for hyperarousal. The IES-R yields a total score ranging from 0 to 88. Although the IES-R is not utilized to screen or diagnose post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), a score between 24 and 32 is interpreted as a concern for PTSD, a score between 33 and 38 indicates a possible presence of PTSD, and a score of 39 or greater is concerned for immune system suppression. These cutoff scores are cited in the literature for a preliminary diagnosis of PTSD14. The IES-R has well-established validity and reliability20. The Cronbach’s alpha of the IES-R in this study was 0.94, indicating good reliability and internal consistency18.

Data analysis

Hypothesis testing of differences in BRS and IES-R scores between healthcare providers and non-healthcare providers

We performed hypothesis testing of differences in resiliency and impact of events, measured by the BRS and IES-R scores, between healthcare versus non-healthcare providers. Given our small dataset and the between group size differences, we used both non-matched and matched analysis prior to hypothesis testing. Due to the substantial imbalance in group sizes between healthcare and non-healthcare providers, matching is a strategy aimed at increasing similarity between the two groups, thus improving the effectiveness of isolating outcome differences. Prior to hypothesis testing, we used Mahalanobis distance method to match healthcare providers and non-healthcare providers21. Mahalanobis distance method has been redefined for matching to reduce covariate imbalance and to improve the efficacy of treatment effect estimates22.

The following variables were used for matching: age group, education level, employment status, income range, race/ethnicity group, gender, partner status, co-occupancy status, having recent deaths in the family, having children under 18-years-old, support systems for confidence, having someone else in the household working in healthcare, and total number of other people living in the same household. For hypothesis testing after matching, we used Fisher randomization test. Fisher randomization test is robust because it does not assume that the participants’ scores are independent and are normally distributed23,24.

In comparison, we also conducted a non-matched analysis, where all collected data points were utilized without exclusion, unlike in the matched analysis. For hypothesis testing without matching, we used the permutation test. Permutation test, which is a type of randomization test, is robust and does not assume independence or normal distribution. In addition to the primary analysis utilizing randomization tests for our hypothesis, the appendix includes alternative tests for both matched and non-matched analyses using the Wilcoxon signed rank test and Wilcoxon rank sum test. Furthermore, we explored the contributing factors to resilience and impact of event scores using generalized linear regression and random forest models (see Appendices). As part of our exploratory analysis, we included density plots of BRS and IES-R scores to visually compare their distributions across different racial groups (see Appendices). Exploratory and additional analyses (including a word cloud representing participant free-text short descriptions, where larger words indicate higher frequency, offering a visual depiction of the thematic analysis of their experiences) are available in the Appendices.

Results

Descriptive analysis

The total original recorded number of responses was 1565. There was a total of 28 demographic questions of participants’ characteristics. Responses that were fully completed were included in the analysis. If responses were missing 25 or more answers, they were excluded from the study analysis. We excluded responses from people who were younger than 18-years-old and those missing 25 answers or more. There were three entries with 2, 3, and 8 missing numerical values of BRS and IES. For the 13 missing answers (0.0365%), we imputed them with neutral/median scores for both scales: a score of 3 for the BRS (1–5) and a score of 2 for the IES-R (0–4). Responses were mostly from Hospital 1 and Hospital 2, with only 1 from Hospital 3 which was removed. The total number of the participants included in the final analysis was 1271, where 1071 (84.3%) were from Hospital 1 and 200 (15.7%) were from Hospital 2. Quantitative analysis: differences in total BRS and IES-R scores between healthcare providers and non-healthcare providers

Our dataset was imbalanced with a small number (n = 63) of non-healthcare providers compared to healthcare providers (n = 1208). Using the Mahalanobis distance matching method with various ratios of healthcare providers and non-healthcare providers, 1:1 paired match yielded the best match in terms of covariate balance prior to hypothesis testing. Our hypothesis testing with Fisher randomization test did not show any statistically significant difference in BRS and IES-R scores between the two groups of healthcare vs. non-healthcare providers. The Fisher-exact p-values for tests comparing the averages of total BRS and total IES-R scores between the two groups are 0.90 and 0.59 respectively. Raw average scores and results from our Fisher hypothesis tests, including the three sub-categories of the IES-R scores, were included in Table 2. Our appendix includes results from other analysis: (1) match and non-matched analysis of the hypothesis with other common tests Wilcoxon signed rank test and Wilcoxon rank sum test, and (2) contributing factors to resiliency and impact of the event.

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to assess the impact of COVID-19 on frontline healthcare providers and non-healthcare provider household members. There was no statistically significant difference in resiliency between healthcare providers and their non-healthcare provide household members. However, whether a participant worked in a healthcare setting or identified as a nurse were important factors that affected resiliency. This study also found that having a support system with someone else to confide in had a positive impact on one’s resiliency. The average IES-R of study participants suggests that they are highly impacted by the COVID-19 pandemic, which is concerning, particularly with higher numbers of participants having intrusive thoughts and experiences while in the range of normal resilience. However, the participants’ BRS remained in the normal resilience range. It is important to note that the word cloud visualized our respondent’s main loss experiences as “family”, “friend”, “member”, all of which encompass participants’ losses in social and support networks, the very factor our study finds is protective for our study group’s resilience.

In addition to geographical disparities, healthcare inequities were found among different racial groups. In looking at the groups’ BRS, the Black/African American participants appear to generally have resiliency scores that equally span across low to high, whose distribution is like that of the Asian participants. The Latinx/Hispanic participants showed a similar resiliency distribution with White/Caucasian participants. But there were no statistically significant differences between the BRS or IES-R scores for various racial groups. However, only 8.5% of our participants identified as Latinx/Hispanic, 2.9% as Black/African American, and 21.9% as Asian, so care should be taken in generalizing these results.

Healthcare providers globally have reported higher than normal levels of burnout due to COVID-19, particularly in the U.S25. Nurses report higher levels of stress and burnout compared to physicians and other healthcare professionals26,27. Since most of the survey participants in this study were nurses, healthcare institutions must strategize provision of appropriate resources and support to improve the mental health of the healthcare providers, particularly nurses. Furthermore, the low response rate from 2 out of 4 study sites is concerning as it can be indicative of low engagement and mental reserve to participate during a highly stressful time. Nursing leaders in these institutions must strategize and create policies to strengthen the workforces that have been impacted by COVID-19.

Profession was the top two most important variables that contributed to both BRS and IES-R, while having someone to confide in was among the top four most important variables to both BRS and IES-R scores. Nursing, including registered nurses and advanced practice nurses, consisted of most healthcare providers in our sample, and was one of the top contributing factors for BRS and IES-R. A survey in Children’s Mercy hospital system found that nurses at risk for secondary trauma utilized “confiding in coworkers” as the most common self-care strategy utilized28. In another study, socializing with colleagues was a common strategy used by nurses that was significantly associated with resiliency29. A participant’s support system, whether they had someone else to confide in, regardless of being a healthcare provider or not, was an important contributing factor in both resiliency and an impact of event on a participant. Further studies should explore the extent in which having a confidant or someone to confide in effects resiliency and impact of an event on healthcare providers.

Given the high burnout and turnover of nursing and other healthcare professions, there is a need to address the mental health needs of healthcare providers1,2. Even with moderate resilience, healthcare providers are not immune to the negative effects of the pandemic and it is critical to assess resilience on patient outcomes6. There is limited literature on effective strategies employed during the pandemic to protect our healthcare providers, but a few suggest the benefits of a formal resiliency program, use of expressive writing, and availability of formal psychiatric counseling and decompression rooms30,31. Potentially, an improvement in the well-being of healthcare providers would have a downstream effect to improve the outcomes of the household members as well, and thereby the community at large. Healthcare providers burnout and turnover are at extremely high levels due to the stress and impact of COVID-1932 and the impact this has on their families as it relates to relationships/communication and taking on more domestic responsibilities impact their experiences and mental health13.

Limitations, strengths, and future research

This is a survey research study where recruitment was done in hospitals with potential for selection bias, resulting in an imbalance of healthcare and non-healthcare providers (95% vs. 5%). Only a small number of participants were non-healthcare providers, and most were related to the targeted healthcare providers. The imbalance in sample sizes between healthcare providers and non-healthcare providers posed a challenge in analysis. To address this issue, we employed matching as a strategy to establish a more balanced dataset with comparable characteristics between the two groups. The main healthcare provider group represented in this study were nurses (68.9%). Our results are most applicable to nurses at various skill levels and areas of practice.

There were many strengths associated with this study. A large sample size of health care providers was included across 2 different sites. There was strong collaboration among nurse leaders from various clinical and operational settings. The lead nurse research scientist partnered with professional development specialists, clinical nurses, and a nurse manager to include perceptions from the various roles nursing offers.

More research is needed to understand the mental health and psychological effects of COVID-19 on healthcare providers, particularly with the changing landscape of the pandemic. Longitudinal mental health effects should be further examined as this study only provides a small window of insight to a pandemic that continues to affect global healthcare communities. Additionally, racial and geographical considerations could highlight whether disparate psychological effects exist for healthcare communities in different racial groups or geographical areas. Further, with the significant finding on the value of support systems, more research can be done targeting various personal and institutional support strategies to better understand how to provide protective resources for our frontline healthcare providers.

Relevance for clinical practice

Results from this study provides what demographic variables unique to a clinical practice setting impacts healthcare providers, mainly nurses’, resiliency, and impact of the pandemic to their mental health. Assessing support systems for healthcare providers and their household members is exploring to encourage resiliency. Our study aligns with current literature on the reported healthcare provider burnout and recommends leaders at healthcare institutions to implement policies and examine protective support to build the resiliency and mental health of our frontline staff.

Conclusion

The COVID-19 pandemic and resiliency had a large effect on the mental health of our healthcare providers. This study highlights the significance of supporting psychological well-being for healthcare providers. Due to our study survey method, further research is needed to assess relationships among healthcare providers that identify as different race/ethnicity as well as among different professions as health providers within the healthcare industry. However, our study found that resiliency and impact of COVID-19 were not different in healthcare providers compared to their non-health provider household members. Further research is needed to identify on the psychological effects of the COVID-19 pandemic, particularly in examining protective factors for both frontline healthcare providers and their household members.
