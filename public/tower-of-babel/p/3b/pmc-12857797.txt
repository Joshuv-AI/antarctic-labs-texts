One Size Does Not Fit All: Echocardiography as a Decision-making Tool in Atrial Fibrillation Management – A Case Series
Authors: Fulvio Cacciapuoti, Rossella Gottilla, Ilaria Caso, Salvatore Crispo, Ciro Pirozzi, Fabio Minicucci, Mario Volpicelli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Atrial fibrillation can arise from a range of underlying mechanisms, including electrical triggers, structural remodeling, metabolic influences, and inflammatory substrates, each of which requires a tailored therapeutic strategy. Understanding the specific atrial substrate responsible for the arrhythmia is therefore essential to guide appropriate management. In this context, echocardiography serves not only as a diagnostic tool but also as a key decision-making instrument, enabling clinicians to noninvasively evaluate the structural, mechanical, and electrical properties of the left atrium. Parameters such as left atrial strain, conduction time (PA-tissue Doppler imaging), and left atrial volume index provide valuable insights into the extent of atrial remodeling and fibrosis, which directly influence therapeutic choices and expected outcomes. While the diagnostic role of echocardiography in atrial fibrillation is well recognized, its integration into therapeutic decision-making, particularly in guiding rhythm control strategies, remains underutilized. This case series aims to address that gap by illustrating how echocardiographic findings can inform the selection, timing, and modality of interventional versus conservative treatment. We present three patients with distinct clinical backgrounds: one with structurally normal atria, one with atrial remodeling due to epicardial adipose tissue infiltration, and one elderly patient with moderate substrate alteration, where comorbidities and minimal symptom burden led to a conservative management decision. Each case highlights how advanced echocardiographic assessment can support individualized treatment planning, from catheter ablation to optimal medical therapy, in alignment with precision medicine principles.

INTRODUCTION

In atrial fibrillation (AF), management is guided by risk scores such as CHA₂DS₂-VA to assess thromboembolic risk and HAS-BLED to identify patients at increased bleeding risk. According to the 2024 European Society of Cardiology guidelines, a holistic approach to reducing bleeding risk is essential, particularly in patients treated with oral anticoagulants. This includes the systematic identification and management of modifiable bleeding risk factors, such as uncontrolled hypertension, labile INR, alcohol use, and concomitant use of NSAIDs.[1] For rhythm control considerations, clinical tools like the HATCH score can help estimate the likelihood of progression from paroxysmal to persistent AF.[2] These scores offer an important clinical framework, but they do not fully capture the structural and functional integrity of the atria.

To bridge this gap, advanced echocardiographic techniques such as speckle-tracking echocardiography and tissue Doppler imaging (TDI) have emerged as valuable tools.[3] Specifically, PA-TDI and atrial strain provide insight into electrical and mechanical remodeling, respectively.[456]

Paroxysmal atrial fibrillation (PAF) is characterized by episodes of arrhythmia that begin suddenly and terminate spontaneously or with intervention within 7 days of onset, typically within 48 h. It is the most common initial presentation of AF and may recur unpredictably over time. Its incidence rises significantly with age, affecting approximately 1%–2% of the general population and up to 10% of individuals over 80 years old and comprising between 25% and 62% of cases of AF.[7] The etiology of PAF is multifactorial, involving both structural and electrical remodeling of the atria, often secondary to underlying conditions such as hypertension, ischemic or valvular heart disease, obesity, diabetes, sleep apnea, and hyperthyroidism.[89] An additional and increasingly recognized contributor is the accumulation of epicardial adipose tissue (EAT), which exerts local inflammatory and profibrotic effects on the atrial myocardium, promoting arrhythmogenesis.[10] These factors collectively contribute to atrial dilatation, fibrosis, and conduction abnormalities, creating a substrate favorable to the initiation and maintenance of AF.

Management of PAF is aimed at symptom control, rhythm or rate management, and prevention of thromboembolic events.[11] Antiarrhythmic drugs may be used to maintain sinus rhythm, while anticoagulation is prescribed based on individual stroke risk assessment. In patients who remain symptomatic despite medical therapy or who need a nonpharmacological approach, catheter ablation has become an increasingly common and effective treatment option.[12]

Echocardiography plays a fundamental role in the assessment and risk stratification of patients with PAF. Left atrial volume, particularly when indexed to body surface area, is a key marker of structural remodeling and is associated with a higher risk of AF recurrence.[13] Atrial strain analysis using speckle-tracking echocardiography allows for detailed evaluation of atrial function, including reservoir, conduit, and contractile phases.[14] Reduced atrial strain, particularly in the reservoir phase, is indicative of atrial fibrosis and correlates with poorer outcomes postablation.[15] TDI provides additional insight into atrial conduction by measuring the PA-TDI interval, defined as the time from the onset of the P wave on ECG to the peak of the A’ wave on TDI. Prolongation of this interval reflects electrical remodeling and may serve as a noninvasive marker of atrial fibrosis.[16]

Catheter ablation techniques for PAF have evolved significantly in recent years. The cornerstone of interventional treatment remains pulmonary vein isolation (PVI), as ectopic triggers originating from the pulmonary veins are implicated in the initiation of AF episodes.[17] Radiofrequency ablation (RFA), which uses point-by-point thermal energy delivery to create contiguous lesions and achieve electrical isolation,[18] has increasingly given way to cryoablation in recent years. The latter, most commonly performed with a balloon-based system, applies freezing energy to achieve circumferential PVI and is associated with shorter procedure times and more homogeneous lesion formation.[19] More recently, electroporation or pulsed field ablation (PFA) has emerged as a novel nonthermal technique that uses high-voltage electric pulses to selectively ablate myocardial tissue while sparing adjacent structures, offering promising results in terms of safety and efficacy.[20] In selected cases, especially in patients with persistent or recurrent AF, ablation strategies may extend beyond the pulmonary veins to include the posterior left atrial wall, the left atrial appendage, or nonpulmonary vein triggers, depending on individual electrophysiological findings.[21]

PFA delivers high-voltage electrical pulses that selectively ablate myocardial cells while sparing adjacent noncardiac structures like the esophagus or phrenic nerve, and has shown promising early results in terms of safety and efficacy in randomized studies.[22]

In clinical practice, advanced echocardiographic measures provide a noninvasive means to assess atrial health and guide individualized treatment strategies. These parameters correlate strongly with electrophysiological abnormalities and have predictive value for arrhythmia recurrence after ablation.[23] Pulmonary vein isolation, though fundamental, shows variable success depending on the degree of atrial remodeling. A thorough understanding of the atrial mechanical and electrical state is essential for selecting appropriate candidates for intervention and for tailoring procedural strategies accordingly.

CASE REPORTS

Case 1

A 54-year-old male presented with symptomatic PAF, characterized by intermittent episodes of palpitations lasting several hours, spontaneously resolving within 24 h. His medical history was notable only for well-controlled hypertension. Physical examination was unremarkable, and laboratory tests, including thyroid function, were within normal ranges. Comprehensive echocardiographic assessment revealed normal left ventricular function and dimensions (ejection fraction 62%). Left atrial size was normal, with an indexed volume of 23.4 mL/m² (normal values <34 mL/m²). Speckle-tracking echocardiography demonstrated preserved left atrial reservoir strain at 39.3% (normal values >35%), indicative of intact atrial mechanical function and minimal structural remodeling. Additionally, the atrial strain curves showed synchronous atrial contraction with no signs of dyssynchrony [Figure 1]. TDI evaluation revealed normal atrial conduction with a PA-TDI interval of 106 ms (normal <130 ms), indicating the absence of significant electrical remodeling or fibrosis [Figure 2].

Longitudinal strain analysis of the left atrium in apical four-chamber view showing normal regional strain values and preserved mechanical synchrony

Tissue Doppler imaging (TDI)-derived PA-TDI interval measured on the left atrial lateral wall indicates normal atrial conduction time and preserved interatrial conduction

Despite optimal medical therapy, including beta-blockers and class IC antiarrhythmic treatment with flecainide, the patient continued to experience frequent symptomatic AF episodes. Given his favorable echocardiographic profile and persistence of symptoms under antiarrhythmic therapy, catheter ablation was recommended. The patient underwent PVI using a second-generation cryoballoon ablation system. Each pulmonary vein was individually occluded using the balloon catheter, and cryoenergy was applied to achieve circumferential electrical isolation [Figure 3]. Electrical disconnection was confirmed by entrance and exit block verification with pacing maneuvers. The procedure was successfully completed without complications.

Fluoroscopic image during pulmonary vein isolation using cryoablation. A circular mapping catheter and a cryoballoon are positioned at the ostium of a pulmonary vein via a transseptal approach

Following ablation, the patient remained asymptomatic and free from AF episodes during a 12-month follow-up. Repeat echocardiography at 1-year confirmed unchanged left atrial dimensions and preserved atrial strain parameters, consistent with successful rhythm control and absence of progressive atrial remodeling.

Case 2

A 62-year-old obese male (Body Mass Index 32 kg/m²) presented with recurrent symptomatic PAF, manifesting as episodes of palpitations, fatigue, and shortness of breath lasting from several hours to days. The patient’s medical history included obesity, obstructive sleep apnea, and hypertension. Echocardiographic assessment revealed normal left ventricular function (ejection fraction 55%) but demonstrated significant EAT accumulation around the atria [Figure 4], indicative of a potential pro-arrhythmic substrate. EAT thickness was measured in the parasternal long-axis view during end-diastole, between the free wall of the right ventricle and the outer pericardium. The thickness was approximately 7 mm, exceeding the general risk threshold of 5 mm. Left atrial volume was mildly increased (indexed volume: 35 mL/m²), while speckle-tracking analysis showed reduced atrial reservoir strain at 10.6% [Figure 5]. Atrial conduction time assessed by TDI was prolonged, with a PA-TDI interval of 135 ms [Figure 6], suggestive of underlying atrial electrical remodeling.

Parasternal long-axis echocardiographic view showing epicardial adipose tissue, visible as an echo-lucent (dark) space between the outer wall of the right ventricle and the pericardium

Longitudinal strain analysis of the left atrium in apical four-chamber view showing reduced regional strain values and segmental mechanical dyssynchrony

Tissue Doppler imaging (TDI)-derived PA-TDI interval measured on the left atrial lateral wall indicates prolonged atrial conduction time and impaired interatrial conduction

The patient had previously undergone PVI via cryoablation about 2 years earlier; however, this had proven ineffective, as episodes of AF persisted. Considering the suboptimal response and echocardiographic findings consistent with significant atrial remodeling due to epicardial fat, a second ablation procedure using electroporation of the posterior wall was proposed. The patient underwent successful electroporation ablation, targeting both pulmonary veins and the posterior left atrial wall, guided by electroanatomic mapping to ensure complete electrical isolation [Figure 7]. No complications occurred during the procedure.

Electroanatomical voltage map of the left atrium during atrial fibrillation ablation. The image shows extensive areas of low voltage, suggesting atrial fibrosis

Following electroporation ablation, the patient maintained a stable sinus rhythm during a follow-up period of 12 months without recurrence of AF symptoms. Echocardiographic reassessment showed stable atrial dimensions and strain parameters, indicating effective interruption of arrhythmogenic triggers and stabilization of the arrhythmic substrate.

Case 3

A 78-year-old male with paroxysmal AF was incidentally diagnosed during ambulatory electrocardiogram (ECG) monitoring, with episodes characterized by minimal symptoms such as transient fatigue. His medical history included long-standing hypertension, previous myocardial infarction, and stage III chronic kidney disease (estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] 42 mL/min). Transthoracic echocardiography showed preserved left ventricular function (EF 55%), moderately enlarged left atrium (Left Atrial Volume Index 42 mL/m²), reduced atrial strain (14.3%) [Figure 8], and prolonged PA-TDI (142 ms) [Figure 9], consistent with moderate structural and electrical remodeling.

Longitudinal strain analysis of the left atrium in apical four-chamber view showing reduced regional strain values and mostly preserved segmental mechanical synchrony

Tissue Doppler imaging (TDI)-derived PA-TDI interval measured on the left atrial lateral wall indicates prolonged atrial conduction time

Despite evidence of atrial remodeling, the patient reported no significant functional limitations. A multidisciplinary evaluation concluded that catheter ablation was not indicated due to minimal symptom burden and elevated procedural risk related to comorbidities. A conservative strategy focused on rate control and anticoagulation was adopted. A direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC) was prescribed at an adjusted dose, considering renal function, with regular follow-up to monitor renal stability and treatment adherence.

At 12 months, the patient remained clinically stable without arrhythmic symptoms or adverse events. This case underscores how patient-centered decisions, guided by both imaging and clinical context, may appropriately favor noninvasive management, especially when atrial remodeling is evident.

DISCUSSION

The presented cases underscore the critical role of echocardiography in the evaluation and management of PAF, highlighting its utility not only in confirming the diagnosis and pathogenesis but also in guiding therapeutic strategies. Comprehensive echocardiographic assessment, including volumetric analysis, speckle-tracking-derived atrial strain, and TDI of atrial conduction, allows for detailed characterization of the atrial substrate. This enables clinicians to identify subtle markers of electrical and structural remodeling that may influence arrhythmia genesis and response to treatment.

All echocardiographic assessments were performed using a Philips CVx 3D ultrasound system equipped with nSIGHT Plus technology. Atrial strain was analyzed using the aCMQ software module (Philips Healthcare, Andover, MA, USA), and TDI was used for conduction time assessment. Measurements were performed and reviewed by two cardiologists with over 8 years of experience.

In the first case, the echocardiographic profile demonstrated normal atrial dimensions, preserved strain, and normal conduction time, suggesting a structurally and electrically healthy atrium. The pathophysiology in such cases often involves focal ectopic discharges originating from the pulmonary veins, without a significant underlying substrate. As a result, PVI alone is typically sufficient to interrupt arrhythmogenic triggers and maintain sinus rhythm. The absence of atrial remodeling markers served as a favorable predictor of ablation success, supporting the decision to proceed directly with catheter ablation.

The second case involved a patient with obesity and significant EAT, contributing to an arrhythmogenic milieu through local inflammatory and profibrotic effects on the atrial myocardium. The assessment of EAT by transthoracic echocardiography is limited by both the quality of the acoustic window and the intrinsic constraints of the technique, which permits visualization of anterior fat layers but not posterior deposits, due to the interposition of lung tissue and the anatomical location of the esophagus and descending aorta. Furthermore, the presence of mildly enlarged atrial volume, reduced atrial strain, and prolonged conduction time pointed to early but meaningful structural and electrical remodeling. These features support a more complex pathophysiology involving both trigger and substrate mechanisms, where ectopic activity is maintained by areas of atrial fibrosis or altered conduction. The failure of initial cryoablation, which may have inadequately addressed nonpulmonary vein or posterior wall triggers, and the subsequent success of posterior wall electroporation highlight the necessity for a substrate-modifying ablation strategy in selected patients. This case further supports the role of electroporation ablation as an effective strategy in patients with substantial EAT and failed prior cryoablation, offering promising outcomes in complex AF scenarios.

In the third case, the patient was an elderly individual with paroxysmal AF, minimal symptom burden, and multiple comorbidities, including chronic kidney disease and prior myocardial infarction. Echocardiographic evaluation revealed moderate atrial remodeling, with enlarged atrial volume, reduced reservoir strain, and prolonged conduction time, suggesting structural and electrical alterations. However, in contrast to advanced fibrotic disease, the decision to avoid ablation was not based solely on substrate severity, but rather on the patient’s low symptomatic profile and elevated procedural risk. The therapeutic approach focused instead on rate control and individualized anticoagulation management with renal-adjusted DOAC therapy. This case exemplifies how even in the presence of intermediate-grade remodeling, comprehensive imaging integrated with clinical context can guide a conservative, patient-centered treatment plan and avoid unnecessary invasive procedures.

These cases collectively support the principle that the management of PAF must be personalized, integrating imaging data, clinical history, arrhythmia burden, and comorbidities. Echocardiography serves as a central tool in this process, enabling stratification of patients not only by stroke risk but also by likelihood of ablation success or progression to persistent AF. A structurally normal atrium with preserved strain and conduction may indicate suitability for early rhythm control, particularly with catheter ablation. Conversely, evidence of remodeling or underlying pathology may prompt consideration of more extensive or alternative ablation approaches or a hybrid medical-device-based therapy.

Looking forward, the role of advanced echocardiographic techniques, including three-dimensional imaging, tissue characterization, and artificial intelligence-assisted strain analysis, is expected to grow. These innovations may further refine our ability to detect early atrial remodeling and tailor therapies accordingly. The integration of imaging biomarkers into predictive models could enhance patient selection for ablation, guide timing of intervention, and monitor disease progression, ultimately improving outcomes in AF care.

While echocardiography provides valuable noninvasive insights into atrial structure and function, its role as a standalone modality has inherent limitations. Specifically, its sensitivity for detecting atrial fibrosis – a key determinant of arrhythmia persistence and ablation outcomes – is limited compared to cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Late gadolinium enhancement MRI remains the gold standard for directly visualizing and quantifying myocardial fibrosis due to its superior spatial resolution and tissue characterization capabilities.

Echocardiographic markers such as reduced atrial strain and prolonged PA-TDI intervals have shown correlation with fibrotic remodeling; however, these are indirect indicators and may be confounded by loading conditions, rhythm irregularities, and suboptimal image quality. Furthermore, advanced echocardiographic techniques such as speckle-tracking and TDI are highly operator-dependent. Their accuracy and reproducibility can be significantly affected by inter- and intraobserver variability, particularly in technically challenging cases or in the absence of standardized acquisition protocols.

These limitations highlight the need for a multimodal imaging approach. Integrating echocardiographic data with cardiac MRI and other clinical parameters allows for a more comprehensive assessment, particularly in complex cases where nuanced characterization of the atrial substrate is essential for informed decision-making.

Despite these limitations, echocardiography remains an indispensable tool in clinical practice due to its wide availability, cost-effectiveness, and real-time imaging capabilities. Its portability and safety profile make it particularly suitable for serial assessments and longitudinal follow-up, enabling dynamic monitoring of atrial remodeling over time. These practical advantages ensure that echocardiography continues to play a central role in the initial evaluation and ongoing management of patients with atrial arrhythmias.

CONCLUSION

Advanced echocardiography enables precise characterization of atrial substrate, informing individualized strategies for managing PAF. This case series highlights the pivotal role of advanced echocardiography in the personalized management of PAF. It also emphasizes that in selected cases, such as those with advanced atrial fibrosis and poor electrical substrate, the decision to avoid catheter ablation in favor of a rate control strategy can represent the most appropriate and effective clinical choice. Integrating imaging with clinical and electrophysiologic data supports optimized, patient-specific decision-making, reducing unnecessary procedures and improving outcomes in AF management.

The heterogeneity of PAF requires a nuanced and individualized approach to diagnosis and treatment. Echocardiography provides invaluable insights into atrial structure and function, informing therapeutic choices and predicting outcomes. By combining advanced imaging with a detailed understanding of each patient’s pathophysiology and clinical context, clinicians can optimize arrhythmia management and potentially modify the natural history of the disease. As technology and our understanding of AF evolve, a precision medicine approach grounded in imaging and patient-specific data will become increasingly central to achieving durable rhythm control and improving quality of life for patients with AF.
