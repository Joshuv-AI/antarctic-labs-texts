Introduction

The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) in health care is rapidly transforming clinical practice, with large language models (LLMs) at the forefront of this change. LLMs are advanced neural networks trained on vast datasets to process and generate human-like text.1 Their applications already span administrative tasks,2 patient communication,3,4 medical documentation, and education,5,6 offering tools that streamline processes and enhance care delivery.7,8 Importantly, LLMs possess the ability to analyze and synthesize complex medical information, positioning them as valuable assets in improving health care delivery and patient outcomes.

In the field of perioperative medicine, encompassing care from surgical planning through postoperative recovery,9 time-sensitive, multidisciplinary decision-making is vital for safe and effective patient management. LLMs have the potential to enhance these processes by summarizing complex medical histories, aiding clinical decisions,10 and providing responsive, AI-driven communication tools to improve patient engagement11 while reducing provider burden. Despite their capabilities, LLMs generate outputs based on probabilistic patterns in their training data rather than genuine understanding,1 which introduces challenges in dynamic, high-stakes clinical environments such as perioperative care. Challenges such as hallucinations (plausible sounding but incorrect or nonsensical answers), ethical concerns, and reliability further complicate their integration into clinical workflows.12

To date, the understanding of LLM applications in perioperative care remains fragmented. Preliminary literature searches conducted up to April 2025 in PubMed and the Cochrane Library identified existing scoping and systematic reviews focused on LLM applications within individual specialties such as pathology,13 emergency medicine,14 orthopedic surgery,15 and biomedical research.16,17 However, no comprehensive review has yet explored the full spectrum of LLM use across perioperative care. Addressing this gap is essential to guide the safe, ethical, and effective implementation of LLM-based tools in perioperative practice.

This scoping review will systematically map the existing literature on LLM applications in perioperative settings, identify gaps in knowledge, and highlight opportunities for future research. By aligning the findings with the Institute for Healthcare Improvement (IHI) Quintuple Aim Framework (which emphasizes improving patient experience, population health, health care costs, provider well-being, and health equity18), this review aims to inform the thoughtful, responsible, and evidence-based integration of LLMs into perioperative care.

This scoping review aims to map the landscape of LLM applications in perioperative care and explore their benefits and limitations. Findings will be contextualized within the IHI Quintuple Aim Framework, which encompasses i) enhancing patient experience, ii) improving population health, iii) reducing costs, iv) supporting provider well-being, and v) health equity.18

Specifically, the objectives of this scoping review are as follows: to identify and map the types of LLMs currently used in perioperative care and the clinical tasks and challenges these LLMs addressto describe how LLMs have been reported to contribute to perioperative care across the dimensions of the IHI Quintuple Aim Framework,18 and summarize the outcome measures used to evaluate these contributionsto identify, categorize, and summarize the limitations and challenges associated with LLM use in perioperative care, including issues such as hallucinations, ethical concerns, and reliability, as well as strategies such as prompt engineering and retrieval-augmented generation proposed to mitigate these challenges.

Eligibility criteria

Appendix I describes the inclusion and exclusion criteria in more detail.

Population

The review will consider studies addressing 2 categories of population. The first category will address patients, defined as individuals preparing for, undergoing, or recovering from anesthesia or surgical procedures. To be considered for inclusion, studies must describe the use of LLMs in supporting clinical decision-making, communication, education, or care processes specifically within the perioperative period.

Studies involving decision-making processes for surgery will be excluded, as these typically involve populations who may, ultimately, not undergo surgical intervention. Their inclusion would broaden the scope beyond the perioperative context and introduce variability unrelated to perioperative care pathways. This approach ensures that the review remains specifically focused on the perioperative period, encompassing preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative phases of care. Studies involving animals or LLM applications unrelated to perioperative care will be excluded.

The second category of population is health care professionals, defined as clinicians involved in perioperative care, including anesthesiologists, surgeons, perioperative nurses, physician assistants, case managers, advanced practice nurses, allied health professionals, and other health care providers directly engaged in the management of patients throughout the perioperative period. Eligible studies should involve LLM-based tools applied in clinical practice, patient education, or clinician education workflows within the perioperative context.

Concept

The review will focus on studies examining the use of LLMs in perioperative medicine. LLMs are defined as AI systems trained on extensive text datasets capable of generating human-like language output. Areas of interest include preoperative patient education and counseling; clinical decision support systems and workflow optimization; patient-provider communication enhancement; and ethical, operational, and security challenges related to LLM deployment in perioperative settings.

Context

The context encompasses the full perioperative continuum, from preoperative assessment and planning through intraoperative management to postoperative recovery and discharge. Studies conducted in hospitals, ambulatory surgical centers, and other relevant clinical settings will be included. No restrictions will be applied to geographic region or health care system. Studies addressing general patient monitoring or clinical scenarios unrelated to perioperative care (eg, ward alarms, sleep medicine) will be excluded.

Type of sources

This scoping review will include all primary research study designs (quantitative, qualitative, mixed methods, feasibility studies, proof-of-concept studies, and simulation-based research). Simulation studies will also be considered, provided they address practical perioperative LLM applications. Narrative or systematic reviews (without meta-analysis) will be excluded; however, references within these reviews will be screened for eligible studies.

Methods

To ensure replicability and minimize bias, this review aligns with open science principles and adheres to the guidelines outlined in the JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis.19 The methodology is based on the framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley,20 with updates by Levac et al.,21 and further informed by best practices for scoping reviews.22,23 It will be reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR).24 Any amendments to the protocol will be documented in the final manuscript. The review protocol is registered with OSF (https://osf.io/a3tkw/).

Search strategy

To ensure a comprehensive and rigorous search, the search strategy was developed in consultation with a medical librarian (RS) and refined using best practices. The following databases will be queried: MEDLINE (Ovid), Embase, Scopus. Gray literature (including theses and conference proceedings) will be searched for via Google Scholar.

An initial exploratory search was conducted in MEDLINE via Ovid (Appendix II) to identify preliminary articles. The search syntax included AI terms ([exp artificial intelligence/] AND keywords like “language models,” “ChatGPT,” and “Copilot.”) and perioperative terms ([exp surgical procedures, operative/] OR [anesthesiology/]) to focus on relevant surgical and anesthesia contexts.

This exploratory phase informed the selection of relevant keywords, indexing terms, and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH). Based on these findings, a comprehensive search strategy was developed targeting the application of LLMs in the perioperative period; for example, MeSH terms such as [Natural Language Processing]; keywords for LLMs such as “ChatGPT,” “BERT,” and “Copilot”; perioperative MeSH terms, including [Surgical Procedures, Operative] and [Perioperative Period].

The MEDLINE search strategy was validated against a curated list of relevant studies to ensure sensitivity and reliability. A second information specialist peer-reviewed the strategy using the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies (PRESS) check-list, and no significant change to the search strategy based on the peer-review assessment was required.25 The finalized strategy will be adapted for other databases, including Embase (Ovid), EBM Reviews (Ovid), and Scopus, with search execution covering each database from inception to the search date. To capture gray literature, searches will be performed in ProQuest Dissertations and Theses Global (ProQuest) and Google Scholar.

No language restrictions will be applied. To manage non-English studies, we will leverage the multilingual expertise of the research team to screen and extract data where possible. For languages not spoken by team members, preliminary screening will be conducted using DeepL (DeepL, Cologne, Germany) or Google Translate. If such studies meet the inclusion criteria, professional translation services will be employed to ensure the accuracy of data extraction. This approach is intended to minimize language bias and enhance the comprehensiveness of the review.

We will search for studies published from 1946 (marking the starting point for indexed systematic indexing in MEDLINE) to November 14, 2024 (the date of the search). This wide time frame ensures comprehensive retrieval of modern medical literature, including early AI systems and LLM precursors relevant to perioperative care, which may inform the historical context and evolution of current LLM applications.

Source of evidence selection

Retrieved studies will be uploaded into Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) where duplicates will be removed. Pairs of reviewers will independently screen evidence sources for eligibility in duplicate by following prespecified criteria in 2 stages. Stage 1 will be screening of evidence sources based on title and abstract. For this, eligibility criteria will be uploaded into Covidence to guide the screening process, with criteria displayed alongside study records during title/abstract and full-text review. Reviewers will use Covidence’s highlighting and annotation tools to support screening decisions. A standardized screening form will also be developed in Microsoft Excel (Redmond, Washington, USA) to capture study characteristics and eligibility outcomes, accompanied by clear instructions to maintain consistency across reviewers. Prior to use, the screening form will be piloted on 25 random articles and repeated consecutively until agreement between reviewers is deemed excellent (determined by a kappa statistic ≥75%). If needed, the screening form will be revised based on feedback from the piloting exercise.

Stage 2 will involve full-text review of the evidence sources identified in stage 1. Like stage 1, screening forms will be generated based on eligibility criteria, piloted using at least 10 random articles, and repeated until agreement between reviewers is deemed adequate (as per a kappa statistic ≥75%). Likewise, the screening form will be revised based on feedback from the piloting exercise.

Reasons for exclusion will be documented during stage 2. A senior research team member will be available to resolve any disagreements at either stage. As per PRISMA-ScR,24 a flow diagram will be generated outlining the results of the search and study selection. We will contact corresponding authors to obtain missing or incomplete data. If no response is received after 2 follow-up attempts, the study will be excluded from data extraction

Data extraction

In pairs, the review team will input data from included articles into pre-piloted data extraction forms (Appendix III). Piloting of the data charting forms will be considered complete once there is full agreement between all reviewer pairs for at least 3 consecutive studies. The data charting forms will be revised as needed based on the piloting exercise and reviewer feedback. The data to be charted will include the study details, description, properties of LLMs evaluated, and relevant subgroup analyses. Data charting forms will be accompanied by a detailed manual outlining key definitions (Appendix IV) of the items to be extracted. The data charting tool will be revised as necessary, and any modifications will be detailed in the final scoping review. Where required, authors of included studies will be contacted to request missing or additional data, in keeping with the iterative approach to refining and completing the data extraction process. If no response is received after 2 follow-up attempts, the study will be excluded from data extraction.

Data analysis and presentation

After data extraction from all included studies is complete, the findings will be analyzed using a combination of descriptive quantitative analysis and qualitative thematic content analysis. Quantitative data will be summarized using descriptive statistics, including absolute and relative frequencies to characterize the volume and types of LLM applications in perioperative care. For qualitative data, we will employ thematic content analysis, a systematic method for identifying, organizing, and interpreting recurring patterns or “nuclei of meaning” within the extracted data. This approach will allow us to map key themes relevant to the objectives of this scoping review, particularly those aligned with the IHI Quintuple Aim Framework.18 In addition, if the number of eligible studies permits, we will conduct a temporal analysis to explore trends in the publication and application of LLMs in perioperative medicine over time.

Results will be organized to directly address each of the 3 research questions. For research question 1 (What types of LLMs have been described in the literature for use in perioperative care, and what specific clinical tasks, decisions, or challenges do they address?), we will compile and classify all identified LLM tools used in perioperative medicine, categorizing them by model type (eg, proprietary, open source, domain specific) and specific clinical applications (eg, preoperative risk stratification, intraoperative documentation support, patient education). Visual summaries (eg, tables, bar charts) will display the distribution and frequency of LLM types and tasks.

For research question 2 (How have LLMs been reported to contribute to perioperative care within the dimensions of the IHI Quintuple Aim Framework, and what outcome measures or indicators have been used to assess their impact?), we will map the domains of perioperative care where LLMs have been applied, organizing them according to the 5 dimensions of the Quintuple Aim: patient experience, population health, provider well-being, cost-effectiveness, and health equity.18 Reported outcome measures or indicators used to assess LLM contributions in these domains will be extracted, categorized, and summarized both narratively and visually.

For research question 3 (What limitations, risks, or challenges have been identified in the application of LLMs in perioperative care, and what approaches, if any, have been employed to address these challenges, including methods such as prompt engineering or retrieval-augmented generation?), we will identify and summarize the limitations, risks, and challenges reported in the literature regarding LLM use in perioperative care. These will be grouped into thematic categories such as hallucinations, ethical concerns, privacy and security, fairness and bias, environmental impact, and governance issues. We will also record whether studies reported mitigation strategies, such as prompt engineering, fine-tuning, or retrieval-augmented generation. Visual summaries (eg, heat maps or stacked bar charts) will depict the proportion of studies addressing each limitation and strategy.

Findings will be presented using narrative summaries, detailed tables, and figures. Summary tables will include study characteristics (author, year, country, study design), sample size, surgical specialty, type of LLM used, task/application, outcome measures, limitations (Appendix V) identified, and mitigation strategies employed. The final manuscript will be prepared following the PRISMA-ScR guidelines.24 As appropriate for a scoping review, no formal quality appraisal or risk of bias assessment of included studies will be conducted. Finally, we will identify and discuss the most frequently studied areas and notable gaps in the literature, offering recommendations to guide future research and inform clinical practice and policy development in perioperative medicine.
