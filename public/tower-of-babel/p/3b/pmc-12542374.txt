Exploring the role of miR-126 in diabetes and its complications: a comprehensive review
Authors: Yousef Rasmi, Masoumeh Farahani, Elmira Roshani Asl, Shirin Barati, Nima Naseri, Kakali Ghoshal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Diabetes is a metabolic disorder primarily characterized by hyperglycemia, posing a substantial global health concern. Insulin resistance significantly contributes to the pathogenesis of type 2 diabetes and its associated complications, such as cardiovascular diseases and dyslipidemia. MicroRNAs (miRNAs), critical regulators of gene expression, have emerged as essential mediators in diabetes and its related pathological conditions. Among these, miR-126 is particularly important for vascular homeostasis and endothelial function. Dysregulated expression of miR-126 is closely associated with impaired angiogenesis, endothelial dysfunction, and the accelerated progression of diabetic complications. This review comprehensively examines the diverse roles of miR-126 in diabetes and its microvascular complications, emphasizing the mechanisms by which miR-126 modulates pivotal signaling pathways implicated in the pathophysiology of diabetes. By integrating miRNA-mRNA interactions with protein-protein interaction networks in diabetic microvascular complications, this study highlights the central role of miR-126 in gene regulation and associated pathophysiological events. The “AGE-RAGE signaling pathway in diabetic complications” emerged as the most significantly enriched pathway, with AKT1, BCL2, MAPK1, TP53, and SIRT1 identified as critical common target genes involved in these processes. Collectively, these insights underscore the therapeutic potential of miR-126 in managing and treating diabetes and associated complications.

Introduction

Diabetes is projected to affect approximately 783 million people worldwide by 2045, according to estimates by the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) [1]. This escalating prevalence poses a major public health concern, substantially increasing the burden of chronic and acute illnesses, and presenting significant challenges to quality of life, healthcare systems, and economic sustainability [2, 3].

Diabetes contributes substantially to macrovascular and microvascular complications, with reported incidence rates generally ranging between 5% and 15%, influenced by diagnostic criteria, patient demographics, and existing comorbid conditions [4–6]. Higher incidence rates, reaching up to 20–30%, have been reported among elderly populations (≥ 65 years), especially those with prolonged diabetes duration (> 10 years) or associated cardiovascular or renal comorbidities. This highlights the necessity for context-specific epidemiological studies and targeted management strategies [4–11].

Specifically, diabetic retinopathy affects approximately 12–22% of adults with type 2 diabetes, with incidence correlated to advanced age, poor glycemic control, hypertension, and dyslipidemia [12–15]. Similarly, diabetic nephropathy has a wide incidence range (10–30%), influenced significantly by factors such as uncontrolled hyperglycemia, hypertension, ethnicity, and genetic predisposition [16, 17].

Insulin resistance (IR) strongly correlates with an increased risk of developing type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), cardiovascular diseases (CVD), and dyslipidemias [2, 18]. Diabetes frequently leads to macro- and microvascular complications, markedly elevating the risk of morbidity and mortality among diabetic patients [19]. Given the high prevalence of diabetes and its associated complications, there is an urgent need to identify critical molecular targets for therapeutic intervention.

In recent years, microRNAs (miRNAs) have emerged as key regulators of gene expression, playing crucial roles in diverse pathological processes, including diabetes and its complications [20]. The biogenesis of miRNAs is complex, involving multiple stages regulated by numerous factors (Fig. 1). Among these miRNAs, miR-126 has received substantial attention due to its pivotal role in vascular health and endothelial function [21], rendering it an important factor in diabetes-related vascular complications [22]. miR-126 is expressed predominantly in endothelial cells, regulating vital processes such as angiogenesis, endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and maintaining vascular homeostasis [23].

The miR-126 gene is intragenic, residing within the seventh intron of the epidermal growth factor-like protein 7 (EGFL7) gene located on human chromosome 9 [24]. The pre-miR-126 molecule generates two distinct strands, miR-126-3p and miR-126-5p. Unlike many other miRNAs, miR-126-5p is not typically degraded and functions similarly to the guide strand, miR-126-3p. Both strands recognize complementary mRNA targets, mediating their degradation or suppressing translation [21]. Perturbations in miR-126 expression consistently associate with impaired angiogenesis, endothelial dysfunction, and the progression of diabetic complications, including nephropathy, retinopathy, and cardiovascular diseases [25].

Despite its potential, miRNA-related research faces specific challenges and limitations, particularly in the context of diabetic complications. For instance, miRNA expression levels may vary considerably among individuals and pathological conditions, necessitating personalized medical approaches tailored to individual patient profiles. Moreover, miRNAs typically have short half-lives, underscoring the need for effective delivery methods to enhance their stability and bioavailability. Given the novelty of miRNA-mediated therapies, further clinical studies are essential to confirm their safety and efficacy in treating diabetic complications [26].

This review comprehensively analyzes the role of miR-126 in diabetes and associated complications, exploring underlying molecular mechanisms through which miR-126 influences critical signaling pathways, including those mediated by VEGF, EPO, and insulin. Through a critical evaluation of current literature encompassing in vitro and in vivo studies, animal models, and clinical research, this review aims to provide deeper insights into the pathogenesis of diabetes complications and highlight potential therapeutic targets by elucidating the functional roles of miR-126.

Exploring the complex interplay of MiRNAs in diabetes and metabolic syndrome

Diabetes mellitus is classified into three main categories: Type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM), Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) [27]. Type 1 diabetes mellitus, also known as insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus (IDDM), is characterized by the involvement of immune cells, particularly T-cells, which have diverse functional roles. Understanding these intricate molecular and cellular mechanisms is vital for developing effective preventive and therapeutic strategies. Multiple miRNAs significantly influence the immune system by modulating T-cell development, differentiation, regulatory T-cell (Treg) activity, and islet autoimmunity [28]. Several studies have identified miRNAs targeting key genes within pathways implicated in T1DM pathogenesis, highlighting their potential as biomarkers [29]. Recent research has documented dysregulation of miRNAs in individuals with T1DM [19], with relevant human and animal studies summarized in Table 1. Consequently, miRNAs emerge as promising biomarkers for the early detection and treatment of T1DM, with further studies potentially revolutionizing diagnostic and therapeutic approaches. Notably, the dysregulation of circulating miRNAs, including miR-21-5p, miR-24-3p, miR-100-5p, miR-146a-5p, miR-148a-3p, miR-150-5p, miR-181a-5p, miR-210-5p, miR-342-3p, miR-375, and miR-1275, serves as promising biomarkers for T1DM [38].

Dysregulated miRNAs in diabetic patients and animal models

Type 2 diabetes mellitus, or non-insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus (NIDDM), prominently features insulin resistance (IR), closely linked to obesity and progressive deterioration of pancreatic β-cell function [39]. Several investigations have revealed altered circulating miRNA profiles in T2DM, IR, and obesity [40]. Islet-enriched miRNAs, notably miR-375, play critical roles in regulating glucose homeostasis and influencing hyperglycemia onset in T2DM [41]. Dysregulated circulating miRNAs such as miR-29a, miR-34a, miR-103, miR-107, miR-132, miR-142-3p, miR-144, and miR-375 also represent potential biomarkers for T2DM [42]. Additionally, exosomal miRNAs have been implicated in the progression of T2DM and its associated complications, particularly through mechanisms involving pancreatic cell damage [40, 43].

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) research indicates miRNAs in placental tissues can significantly influence metabolic adaptations, insulin resistance, placental function, and fetal outcomes. Differentially expressed miRNAs in GDM patients target genes involved in glucose metabolism, energy regulation, and insulin signaling pathways. Notably, circulating and placental miRNAs are associated with maternal pancreatic β-cell dysfunction and fetal complications [44]. Exosomes enriched with miRNAs modulate glucose metabolism and mediate communication between gestational and other tissues, offering regenerative therapeutic potential for postpartum outcomes in GDM-affected mothers [45]. Specific miRNAs such as miR-351 and miR-335-5p have emerged as promising candidates for personalized GDM treatment strategies [46]. Moreover, research highlights differential effects of GDM on placental miR-126-3p expression in low-birth-weight infants. Reduced expression of miR-126-3p in the placenta of these infants potentially mitigates the growth-promoting effects associated with maternal overnutrition and GDM. miR-126-3p is widely expressed across tissues, particularly abundant in endothelial cells, and plays a pivotal role in maintaining endothelial homeostasis and vascular integrity. Elevated circulating miR-126-3p levels may indicate an endothelial source. Furthermore, miR-126-3p directly targets insulin receptor substrate 1 (IRS1) and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K), key molecules in insulin signaling pathways [4].

The importance of miR-126 in Macro- and microvascular complications of diabetes

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) play crucial roles in various metabolic processes, including glucose homeostasis, gluconeogenesis, glucose transporter type 4 expression, lipid metabolism regulation, adipogenesis, insulin sensitivity, and insulin signaling. Dysregulation of miRNAs contributes notably to insulin resistance (IR), particularly in critical metabolic tissues such as the liver, muscle, and adipose tissue. Additionally, miRNAs are integral to the pathogenesis of diabetes and its macrovascular complications, influencing multiple signaling pathways related to inflammation, apoptosis, cellular survival and migration, vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation, neurogenesis, angiogenesis, autophagy, oxidative stress, cardiac remodeling, and fibrosis [47]. Moreover, miRNAs significantly impact microvascular complications by modulating processes such as angiogenesis, inflammation, apoptosis, and fibrosis in tissues like the kidney, retina, and peripheral nerves. These complex interactions underline the pivotal roles of miRNAs in the development of microvascular complications [48]. Consequently, these complications negatively affect various organs and tissues, impacting approximately 30–50% of individuals with diabetes [6]. The subsequent sections will specifically explore the diverse roles of miR-126 in diabetic microvascular and macrovascular complications.

Diabetes commonly leads to both macrovascular and microvascular complications. Macrovascular complications encompass ischemic heart disease, peripheral vascular disease, and cerebrovascular disease [4]. Conversely, microvascular complications typically involve diabetic nephropathy, retinopathy, and neuropathy [5]. These complications collectively affect approximately 30–50% of diabetic individuals, significantly compromising their quality of life [6]. Effective nutritional management, especially optimal protein intake, is critical in preventing and managing these diabetes-related complications.

Current nutritional guidelines from authoritative sources, including the American Diabetes Association (ADA) and Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO), provide specific protein intake recommendations tailored to patient conditions. For most individuals with diabetes, daily dietary protein intake should range from approximately 0.8 to 1.0 g per kilogram of body weight. Diabetic patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) not yet on dialysis are advised to limit protein intake to around 0.8 g/kg/day, with careful clinical monitoring. Conversely, patients undergoing dialysis are recommended higher protein intakes of about 1.2–1.3 g/kg/day to maintain nutritional status, prevent muscle wasting, and improve clinical outcomes [49–51].

Beyond nutritional considerations, recent studies have highlighted the significance of miRNAs in regulating diabetes-related metabolic pathways. These miRNAs influence glucose homeostasis, gluconeogenesis, lipid metabolism, adipogenesis, insulin sensitivity, and signaling pathways involved in insulin activity. Dysregulated miRNAs contribute significantly to insulin resistance (IR) in key metabolic tissues such as the liver, muscle, and adipose tissue. Furthermore, miRNAs actively participate in the mechanisms underlying macrovascular complications, such as inflammation, apoptosis, angiogenesis, oxidative stress, cardiac remodeling, and fibrosis [47]. Similarly, miRNAs exert essential roles in microvascular complications by influencing tissues such as the kidney, retina, and peripheral nerves through critical processes including inflammation, apoptosis, angiogenesis, and fibrosis. The following sections provide a detailed examination of the specific roles of miR-126 in the pathogenesis of diabetic macrovascular and microvascular complications [48].

MiR-126 in diabetic macrovascular complications

Vascular complications of diabetes predominantly include atherosclerosis, hypertension, and peripheral artery disease, all resulting from abnormalities in endothelial cells (ECs) and vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) [52]. MiR-126 has emerged as an independent prognostic biomarker for long-term all-cause mortality among type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) patients and is considered a potential epigenetic mediator or indicator of cardiovascular complications [53]. Reduced miR-126 levels in plasma, coronary venous sinus, and aortic tissues have been significantly associated with impaired left ventricular function and diminished cardiac repair capacity in heart failure patients [54]. Furthermore, logistic regression analyses have demonstrated that hyperglycemia induces a decline in miR-126 concentration in both cardiac tissues [55] and plasma [56], closely correlating with diabetic macroangiopathy and microangiopathy progression [57].

Bioinformatic tools such as Targetscan and miRanda have identified several miR-126-3p targets involved in cardiovascular functions, notably phosphoinositide-3-kinase regulatory subunit 2 (PIK3R2) [12], sprouty.

-related EVH1 domain-containing protein 1 (SPRED1) [13], vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1) [7], and tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factor 7 (TRAF7) [8]. MiR-126-5p targets include high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) [9], activated leukocyte cell adhesion molecule (ALCAM) [10], and delta-like 1 homolog (Dlk1), a Notch1 inhibitor [58]. By modulating the expression and activity of these proteins, miR-126-5p significantly influences multiple cellular functions and signaling pathways.

These miRNA targets are primarily involved in angiogenesis, vascular inflammation, and endothelial cell apoptosis, elucidating why reduced circulating miR-126 levels are considered a biomarker for cardiovascular disease (CVD) in diabetic patients. Despite miR-126-3p’s beneficial effects on endothelial function, its role in atherosclerosis is complex. It promotes VSMC proliferation by inhibiting insulin receptor substrate-1 (IRS-1) [14], a protein strongly implicated in coronary artery disease development [59]. IRS-1 activation is known to decrease myocardial glucose utilization in T2DM patients’ hearts [60].

Research by Tao et al. indicates miR-126 also targets IRS-2, subsequently inhibiting β-cell proliferation [61]. This suggests miR-126 modulates glucose homeostasis through dual targeting of IRS-1 and IRS-2 [60]. Additionally, Arevalo-Martinez et al. identified miR-126 as critical in vascular smooth muscle cell metabolic memory in T2DM via activation of the MEK/ERK pathway. This pathway enhances Kv1.3 blocker efficacy in preventing restenosis in diabetic patients [62].

Tissue factor (TF), released from vascular and circulating cells during inflammatory responses, initiates blood coagulation. Elevated TF levels correlate with increased cardiovascular mortality. Circulating miR-126 demonstrates antithrombotic properties by regulating TF expression post-transcriptionally, thus impacting vascular homeostasis in diabetes mellitus [63]. A study further indicated that diabetes-induced reductions in miR-126 elevate levels of ADAM9, which is cleaved by MERTK, disrupting downstream engulfment signaling and impairing macrophage efferocytosis of apoptotic cardiomyocytes. This defective efferocytosis under diabetic conditions can potentially be reversed by miR-126 overexpression, aiding efficient wound healing and inflammation resolution following tissue injury [64].

An early molecular alteration in diabetic cardiac tissue includes impaired expression of miR-126 and miR-132, causing dysregulation of proangiogenic and antiangiogenic gene expression. These molecular changes lead to diabetic cardiac microangiopathy development. Monitoring circulating miR-126 and miR-132 levels offers a promising approach for detecting preclinical diabetic cardiac microangiopathy, and targeted therapeutic interventions directed at these miRNAs might effectively prevent or treat diabetic cardiomyopathy [65]. Additionally, research highlights a significant relationship between small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) and the concurrence of atrial fibrillation (AF) and diabetes mellitus (DM), noting elevated total sEV levels and reduced sEV-miR-126 levels in AF patients with DM [66].

Collectively, these findings underscore the potential role of miR-126 in accelerating the progression of diabetic macrovascular complications, particularly endothelial dysfunction. Integrating these insights enhances our understanding of cardiovascular disease mechanisms, enabling the development of targeted and personalized therapeutic strategies in clinical practice.

MiR-126 in diabetic microvascular complications

Diabetes often leads to significant microvascular complications, notably diabetic retinopathy (DR), neuropathy, and nephropathy. Diabetic retinopathy remains a leading cause of blindness among working-age adults. Early and aggressive management of microalbuminuria can substantially mitigate the risk of nephropathy. However, neuropathy presents diverse clinical manifestations, posing considerable challenges in its effective management. Thus, stringent control of glucose, blood pressure, and lipid levels is essential to prevent and slow the progression of microvascular complications [67].

Several studies have explored the role of miR-126 in diabetic nephropathy (DN) and chronic kidney disease (CKD). Al-Kafaji et al. suggested that reduced miR-126 expression correlates with the onset of DN, proposing it as a potential blood-based biomarker for identifying type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) patients at elevated risk for developing DN [57]. Fourdinier et al. similarly observed decreased circulating miR-126 and miR-223 levels linked to lower estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) across various CKD stages [68]. Conversely, Liu et al. reported increased urine miR-126 levels among individuals with DN [69]. Dimuccio and colleagues provided initial evidence of elevated miR-126 and miR-145 in urine-derived extracellular vesicles from DN patients, showing a significant association between these miRNAs’ upregulation and DN progression, as indicated by elevated proteinuria [70]. Furthermore, advancements in miRNA-based therapies have highlighted the potential for chemically modifying miRNAs and improving delivery methods to better manage diabetic kidney disease (DKD) clinically [71]. Despite promising findings regarding miR-126 as a DN biomarker, additional research is necessary to validate its clinical utility.

Long-standing T2DM can lead to diabetic retinopathy (DR), a critical microvascular complication frequently causing vision loss. Nonproliferative diabetic retinopathy (NPDR) represents an initial stage of DR that may progress to proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR) [72]. Pramanik et al. reported reduced miR-126 and miR-132 levels in plasma and vitreous samples of NPDR patients compared to diabetic patients without DR [72]. Their study also demonstrated negative correlations between these miRNAs and levels of VEGF, total cholesterol (TC), LDL cholesterol (LDL-C), triglycerides (TG), and malondialdehyde (MDA). In vitro, Sanguineti et al. showed that miR-126 overexpression potentially reduces VEGF-A secretion under diabetic conditions [73]. Wang et al. further indicated that elevating retinal miR-126 and Ang-1/Tie-2 levels in DR might decrease expressions of VCAM-1 and VEGF/VEGFR, potentially slowing DR progression [74]. Additionally, using miR-126 mimics represents a novel therapeutic approach for DR treatment and prevention [75]. Therefore, serum miR-126 could serve as a valuable biomarker for diagnosing and screening retinal endothelial injury in DR.

Moreover, MALAT1, a long non-coding RNA, can inhibit miR-126-5p activity in high-glucose-induced retinal endothelial cells (RECs) by binding its 3’UTR fragments. This inhibition promotes REC proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis, thus accelerating DR progression [76]. A case-control study identified an association between the miR-126 rs4636297 single nucleotide polymorphism and a decreased DR risk within a Southern Brazilian population [77]. Despite extensive insights into miR-126’s roles in nephropathy and retinopathy, its involvement in diabetic neuropathy remains significantly underexplored. Further research is critically needed to elucidate miR-126’s potential roles in diabetic neuropathy and fully understand its implications in diabetic microvascular complications.

MiR-126 and wound healing

Wound healing is a dynamic process comprising overlapping stages: hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling. The proliferation phase involves three key events: angiogenesis, re-epithelialization, and granulation tissue formation. Extensive research has highlighted the critical involvement of microRNAs (miRNAs) in wound healing by analyzing their distinct expression patterns across different repair phases [78]. In recent studies, specific pro-angiogenic (pro-angio) and anti-angiogenic (anti-angio) miRNAs have been identified that significantly influence diabetic wound healing [79]. Pro-angio miRNAs facilitate angiogenesis by targeting and suppressing negative regulatory molecules, whereas anti-angio miRNAs inhibit angiogenesis by targeting and suppressing positive regulatory factors [79, 80]. Among these miRNAs, miR-126 stands out as a prominent pro-angio miRNA, playing a critical role in regulating angiogenesis during diabetic wound repair [78, 80].

MiR-126 expression is notably abundant in healthy skin [80]; however, it is significantly reduced in peripheral blood samples from diabetic patients with foot ulcers compared to diabetic patients without ulcers and healthy controls [81]. Maggot debridement therapy (MDT), an approach that accelerates wound healing and reduces healing time, has been shown to elevate miR-126 levels in peripheral blood from diabetic foot ulcer patients [82]. Exosomes derived from synovial mesenchymal stem cells (SMSCs) overexpressing miR-126-3p encapsulated within hydroxyapatite/chitosan composite hydrogels significantly enhance wound surface re-epithelialization and stimulate angiogenesis in diabetic wound healing [83]. Additionally, negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT) increases miR-126 expression in diabetic wounds, regulating angiogenesis and thereby facilitating wound repair [81]. Intensive glucose control further enhances serum exosomal miR-126-3p expression in diabetic ulcer patients, positively influencing wound healing outcomes [84]. Hydrogen sulfide (H2S), known for its angiogenesis-regulating potential, also upregulates miR-126 under hyperglycemic conditions, contributing to improved wound repair [85].

Chitosan combined with exosomes derived from SMSCs overexpressing miR-126-3p significantly accelerates wound healing in diabetic rat models by enhancing angiogenesis and granulation tissue formation [86]. Furthermore, downregulation of long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) XIST via the miR-126-3p/EGFR axis inhibits the migratory and proliferative capacities of high glucose-induced HaCaT cells, subsequently delaying wound healing [87]. Exosomes derived from IFN-γ-pretreated bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) delivering miRNA-126-3p markedly enhance angiogenesis and therapeutic efficacy in diabetic wound healing through the SPRED-1/Ras/Erk signaling pathway [88]. Thus, the multifaceted role of miR-126 in regulating angiogenic responses and wound repair processes in diabetic conditions represents a promising research area. A more profound understanding of miR-126-mediated mechanisms could lead to novel therapeutic strategies aimed at improving clinical outcomes.

Advancements in targeted therapeutic delivery utilizing exosomes hold significant promise for improving diabetic wound healing outcomes. Recent progress in miRNA-based therapeutic approaches highlights targeted gene therapy and innovative delivery systems. Nanohydrogels, organic nanocomplexes, and inorganic nanocomplex-based strategies have demonstrated the capability to effectively deliver miRNAs, enhancing diabetic wound repair. Future studies should explore composite nanomaterial delivery systems’ potential roles and further clarify their efficacy in diabetic wound healing [89, 90].

MiR-126 as a novel biomarker for diabetes prediction

Alterations in miRNA levels, including both upregulation and downregulation, can serve as predictive indicators for diabetes and associated micro- and macrovascular complications in high-risk individuals. Circulating miR-126 expression has emerged as an essential biomarker for predicting type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and assisting in the diagnosis of uncertain cases [56]. One study identified serum miR-126 levels as significant indicators for diabetic retinopathy (DR) screening, demonstrating that miR-126 overexpression can mitigate DR by targeting polo-like kinase 4 (PLK4) expression, thereby inhibiting endothelial cell proliferation and migration [91]. Another study observed that elevated blood glucose levels result in reduced serum miR-126, proposing that increased plasma glucose levels contribute to miR-126 downregulation, VEGF resistance, and endothelial dysfunction. MiR-126 in endothelial cells is pivotal in maintaining endothelial homeostasis and vascular integrity [92]. Moreover, miR-126 is abundant in endothelial apoptotic bodies, playing a protective role in vasculature regulation through VEGF [93]. Thus, reduced miR-126 expression correlates with impaired insulin signaling and hyperglycemia. Studies have investigated serum miR-126 expression across diabetes stages and established associations between miR-126 levels, prediabetic conditions, and T2DM [92]. Notably, decreased circulating miR-126 levels in non-diabetic individuals can predict diabetes mellitus development [93, 94]. In type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) patients without apparent cardiovascular disease (CVD) or diabetic complications, heightened expression of CXCR1/2 receptors and miR-126-5p indicates an inflammatory state. Upregulated miR-126-5p may reflect a compensatory mechanism, suggesting therapeutic potential for anti-IL-7 and pro-miR-126-5p interventions to mitigate inflammation and reduce CVD risks [95].

MiR-126 and diabetes-related signaling pathways

The miRNA expression profile in endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs) from diabetes mellitus patients reveals diminished miR-126 expression, impairing EPC functionality via targeting the SPRED-1 gene. MiR-126 modulates EPC behavior through the Ras/ERK/VEGF and PI3K/Akt/eNOS signaling pathways [96]. Hyperglycemia and advanced glycation end products (AGEs) further reduce miR-126 expression levels in EPCs by inhibiting the PI3K/Akt pathway (Fig. 2) [97]. Research also highlights the ability of miR-126 to mitigate inflammation and apoptosis in diabetic nephropathy (DN) by regulating VEGF through the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [98]. Additionally, plasma circulating miR-126 demonstrates regulatory roles in insulin or insulin-like growth factor-related pathways among diabetic patients [99].

Moderate-intensity interval training can alleviate renal oxidative stress in diabetic rats experiencing acute kidney injury due to myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury by activating the miR-126/Nrf-2 pathway, subsequently reducing susceptibility to acute kidney injury [100]. In diabetic retinopathy (DR) models, miR-126 inhibits cell viability and invasion through targeting the insulin receptor substrate-1 (IRS-1) gene, modulating the PI3K-Akt-VEGF signaling pathway [101]. Another investigation demonstrated that miR-126 overexpression in retinal vascular endothelial cells under hyperglycemic conditions suppresses cell migration and sprouting by inhibiting VEGFA and PIK3R2, key components of the VEGF/PI3K/AKT pathway [102].

Naringenin has shown promise as a preventive agent against diabetic myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury by enhancing the miR-126-PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [103]. Additionally, miR-126 overexpression in human glomerular mesangial cells delays cellular senescence induced by high glucose conditions through the telomere-p53-p21-Rb signaling axis [104]. Tea polyphenols similarly delay high glucose-induced senescence in human glomerular mesangial cells via the miR-126/Akt-p53-p21 pathway [105].

A recent study demonstrated that downregulating CircTLK1 attenuates high glucose-induced inflammation, oxidative stress, and extracellular matrix accumulation by inhibiting the AKT/NF-κB pathway through a miR-126-5p/miR-204-5p sponge mechanism (Fig. 3) [106].

miRNA-126: a potential therapeutic agent for diabetes mellitus

Extensive research underscores miR-126 as a prominently studied microRNA (miRNA) in diabetes mellitus, primarily due to its vital roles in regulating hepatic glucose and lipid metabolism [107–109]. Recent studies have highlighted the relevance of miR-126 and miR-122 in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) research. Crucially, individuals diagnosed with T2DM exhibit altered endothelial miR-126 expression, which affects insulin receptor substrates, thus modulating glucose homeostasis [61, 94].

Increased miR-126 expression in endothelial cells critically regulates growth factor expression, including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), vascular cell adhesion protein 1 (VCAM-1), and angiopoietin-1, essential for vascular health and islet endothelial cell homeostasis [74]. Disturbances in this regulatory pathway can precipitate pancreatic islet dysfunction, potentially leading to diabetes. Endothelial dysfunction within islet cells frequently accompanies early stages of diabetes, characterized by hyperglycemia and insulin resistance (IR), and closely correlates with T2DM progression [110, 111]. Elevated endothelial miR-126 expression, triggered by cytokines such as IL-6, TNF, and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), mitigates inflammation and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production [9]. Venkat et al. demonstrated that ischemic stroke in T2DM mice significantly decreases pro-angiogenic miR-126 levels in serum and brain tissue. Conversely, treatment with endothelial-derived exosomes enriched in miR-126 promoted neurorestorative effects post-stroke [112]. Additionally, animal studies showed that miR-126 knockdown mice exhibited significantly elevated postpartum fasting blood glucose (FBG) levels compared to wild-type counterparts [113].

Moreover, substantial evidence indicates an inverse relationship between miR-126 expression and elevated FBG levels [114]. Wang et al. proposed biotechnologically engineered exosomes enriched with angio-miRs such as miR-126, or angiogenic proteins like Hsp20 and Ets2, as optimal strategies to promote angiogenesis in diabetic hearts [115]. Multiple human studies have further validated the negative correlation between miR-126 levels and diabetes onset or complications. For instance, He et al. identified a significant inverse correlation between circulating miR-126 and T2DM risk, although statistical significance was contingent on excluding certain cross-sectional studies [114, 115].

Zhang et al. further demonstrated that low pre-onset plasma miR-126 levels predict T2DM onset within two years, suggesting miR-126’s potential as an early predictive biomarker [116]. This study indicated a glucose-dependent decrease in miR-126, linked to impaired VEGF signaling and endothelial dysfunction [21, 110]. Consequently, measuring circulating miR-126 levels emerges as a promising, non-invasive diagnostic tool for T2DM risk assessment [56]. Fish et al. hypothesized that miR-126 sensitizes activated endothelial cells to VEGF by suppressing SPRED1 and/or PIK3R2, thereby facilitating angiogenesis [21]. Wang et al. reported that miR-126 knockout mice display compromised vascular integrity, impaired endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis, resulting in reduced cardiac neovascularization post-myocardial infarction [102]. These vascular defects mimic decreased signaling from angiogenic factors like VEGF and FGF, reinforcing miR-126’s role in promoting angiogenesis by inhibiting SPRED-1 [102].

Enhanced retinal miR-126 expression can also mitigate diabetic retinopathy (DR) complications by increasing Ang-1/Tie-2 levels and reducing VEGF/VEGFR and VCAM-1 expression, thereby fostering vascular repair [66]. Giannella et al. identified abnormal circulating CD62E + microparticles and miR-126-3p content in prediabetic individuals, correlating with endothelial inflammation markers, including VCAM-1 and antioxidant capacity [117]. Zeinali et al. demonstrated reduced miR-126-3p expression associated with inflammatory markers and IR in T2DM patients [118]. Furthermore, miR-126 inhibition of insulin signaling through IRS-1 targeting has been linked to mitochondrial dysfunction [119].

Platelet dysfunctions, such as increased aggregation, enhanced thromboxane production, or altered mean platelet volumes, are well-documented in diabetes [120]. Heishima et al. found a correlation between circulating miR-126 levels and platelet counts, suggesting that platelet-derived miR-126 leakage might minimally affect miR-126 levels [121].

Recent studies have indicated reduced circulating miR-126 levels significantly correlate with diabetic retinopathy progression, supporting its potential as a non-invasive biomarker for early DR detection and risk stratification [91]. Translational studies and early-phase clinical investigations further revealed altered miR-126 expression in diabetic nephropathy patients, exploring its therapeutic targeting potential and mechanistic involvement in endothelial dysfunction and glomerular injury [57, 98].

Summarizing current meta-analyses, elevated miR-126 expression correlates with reduced T2DM risk and lower fasting blood glucose and HOMA-IR index in healthy populations [114]. Collectively, miR-126 demonstrates considerable potential as a biomarker for diabetes detection, monitoring, and associated complications.

Deciphering shared key miR-126-target regulatory interactions for diabetic nephropathy, retinopathy, and neuropathy

To elucidate the mechanisms by which miR-126 modulates microvascular complications in diabetes, we analyzed its targets using miRTarBase, a database containing experimentally validated interactions [122]. Concurrently, we extracted genes related to diabetic nephropathy, retinopathy, and neuropathy from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/). This analysis identified 1,361 miR-126-target gene interactions, with 47 genes shared across all three diabetic complications (Fig. 3.a).

Functional analysis of these shared genes revealed significant enrichment of SMAD4, MAPK8, VCAM1, PRKCB, AKT2, BCL2, MAPK1, AKT1, PRKCA, MAPK14, RELA, and VEGFA in the “AGE-RAGE signaling pathway in diabetic complications” (KEGG_PATHWAY: hsa04933) (Fig. 4; Table 2). The AGE-RAGE signaling pathway has previously been reported to play a critical role in the pathogenesis of diabetic complications [37] (https://www.kegg.jp/pathway/map04933). Additionally, the gene ontology (GO) biological processes “response to hypoxia” and “response to insulin” showed significant enrichment among these shared targets (Table 3). The DAVID bioinformatics resource was employed to conduct these functional analyses [123].

To pinpoint critical regulatory targets, we evaluated protein interactions among these 47 genes using the STRING database, applying a confidence score threshold of 0.7 [124, 125]. Cytoscape software analysis identified a scale-free interaction sub-network (R-squared: 0.778) comprising 36 nodes and 112 edges (Fig. 3.b) [126]. Utilizing the cytoHubba plugin, ten core genes were prioritized based on five topological parameters: Maximal Clique Centrality (MCC), Maximum Neighborhood Component (MNC), Edge Percolated Component (EPC), Degree, and Betweenness [127]. This analysis revealed AKT1, BCL2, MAPK1, TP53, and SIRT1 as key regulatory targets (Fig. 3.b).

AKT1, a crucial serine-threonine kinase and downstream effector of the insulin signaling pathway, promotes cell survival and inhibits apoptosis by modulating glucose metabolism and other cellular mechanisms [128]. Recent evidence highlights reciprocal interactions between miRNAs and the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, suggesting this axis as a potential therapeutic target for diabetic complications [129–131]. Members of the BCL2 family exhibit dual roles in diabetes, regulating mitochondrial pathways linked to cytokine-induced β-cell apoptosis while also influencing glucose metabolism and β-cell functionality [132]. Dysregulation of SIRT1, a class III histone deacetylase, is documented extensively in diabetic complications, with studies elucidating its molecular pathways and regulatory mechanisms [133, 134]. Moreover, AKT1, TP53, and MAPK1 have been identified by Yang et al. as potential therapeutic targets in diabetic retinopathy (DR) [135].

Collectively, these findings suggest that deeper investigation into miR-126-mediated regulation of these critical targets could significantly advance therapeutic strategies for diabetic microvascular complications, improving disease management and patient outcomes.

Limitations and future perspectives

Exploring the roles of miRNAs in diverse diseases has significantly advanced our understanding of their diagnostic and therapeutic potential. In this review, we specifically assessed the relevance of miR-126 and its associated signaling pathways in diabetes and related complications. Current findings clearly indicate that reduced serum miR-126 expression correlates with elevated blood glucose levels and diminished insulin production. Consequently, miR-126 holds promise as a biomarker for early identification of prediabetes and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Nonetheless, several limitations and research gaps require attention.

One significant limitation is the relatively small sample size in many current studies, restricting the generalizability of results. Additionally, some research has lacked appropriate control groups and rigorous adherence to standardized methodologies and accurate analytical procedures. The inclusion of diverse species in preclinical studies adds further complexity to data interpretation. Another critical limitation is the absence of standardized methods for miRNA quantification using quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) in serum and urine samples, along with insufficient availability of additional samples for necessary follow-up studies. Occasionally, studies also lack appropriate internal controls or utilize inconsistent serum volumes in assays [136]. Future research should prioritize larger patient cohorts, incorporation of robust control groups, standardized internal and external controls, and established qRT-PCR protocols [108]. Larger, multicenter studies would substantially enhance the statistical validity and reliability of findings.

Considering the variability of miRNA expression among individuals and disease states, personalized medicine approaches may help address these challenges and better meet patient-specific needs. Additionally, limitations such as off-target effects, variability in expression, and inconsistent clinical outcomes have previously led to the discontinuation of several clinical trials. Targeted strategies specifically designed to modulate miRNA expression could potentially mitigate these issues. Furthermore, the inherent instability of miRNAs and the challenge of delivering them effectively to target cells remain significant barriers to clinical application, driving current research efforts towards improving miRNA stability and optimizing delivery through chemical modifications and advanced delivery systems [89, 137].

When evaluating therapeutic strategies involving miR-126, clearly distinguishing between robust and preliminary evidence is essential for effective clinical implementation. High-quality evidence derived from large randomized controlled trials (RCTs) strongly supports the role of miR-126 as a biomarker and therapeutic target for diabetes-associated cardiovascular and renal complications. For example, several clinical studies have consistently linked reduced circulating miR-126 levels to poorer cardiovascular outcomes in diabetic patients [53, 57, 68]. However, many promising findings currently rely on preliminary evidence from animal models and small-scale observational research, necessitating cautious interpretation until validated by larger clinical trials. Early animal and clinical studies exploring the therapeutic role of miR-126 in diabetic wound healing through enhanced angiogenesis provide promising yet provisional outcomes, underscoring the need for confirmation in adequately powered clinical trials [83, 86]. Explicit acknowledgment of limitations—including model-specific responses, limited sample sizes, methodological inconsistencies, and variability in patient populations [69, 70, 108]—alongside transparent discussions of preliminary and conflicting findings will ultimately facilitate the advancement of miR-126-based therapeutic strategies toward effective clinical interventions.

Conclusion

This review comprehensively explored the role of miRNAs in diabetes, emphasizing the importance of miR-126. The analysis highlighted the crucial involvement of miR-126 in diabetes pathogenesis and its complications, along with its potential as a therapeutic agent. Specifically, reduced serum and urine miR-126 levels were associated with increased blood glucose concentrations and decreased insulin production, suggesting miR-126 as a valuable biomarker for diabetes detection. MiR-126 regulates various essential processes, including vascular homeostasis, angiogenesis, and the development of nephropathy, retinopathy, and cardiovascular diseases. Gene regulatory networks and protein-protein interaction analyses revealed key miR-126-mediated signaling pathways and interactions in diabetic microvascular complications. Therefore, assessing miR-126 levels offers significant predictive capabilities for diabetes onset, providing essential insights for developing targeted therapeutic strategies. A deeper understanding of the regulatory roles of miR-126 in diabetes will facilitate effective interventions, ultimately improving the management and outcomes of diabetes and its related complications.
