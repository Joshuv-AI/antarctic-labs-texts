Rapid Oil Sands Extraction Processing Stream Composition Determination by a 19Fluorine Nuclear Magnetic Resonance Spectrometer with Fluorinated Tracers
Authors: Ni Yang, Kiran Krishnamurthy, Jing Shen, Ivan Trang, Natalia Semagina, Xiaoli Tan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A benchtop 19F-NMR spectrometer, coupled with the addition of fluorine-containing tracers (hereafter referred to as the 19F-NMR method), was explored as an alternative to the conventional Dean–Stark method for determining the composition of the oil sands slurry. Trifluorotoluene and sodium trifluoroacetate were identified as oleophilic and hydrophilic tracers, respectively, enabling accurate quantification of bitumen and water in both synthetic and real oil sands samples. Bitumen (0.5–90 wt %) and water (5–95 wt %) were accurately measured, demonstrating the method’s suitability for a broad range of slurry types, including oil sands ore, bitumen froth, middlings, fluid fine tailings, and coarse sand tailings. For samples containing bitumen <0.5 wt %, significant deviations were observed due to insufficient tracer dilution from the small bitumen volume, leading to minimal change of signal integrated intensity and high fluctuations that hindered reliable detection. Solid content was calculated by mass balance, eliminating the need for thermal drying. The 19F-NMR method provides significant advantages over the Dean–Stark method in terms of analysis time, cost, safety, and environmental impact; however, unlike the Dean–Stark method, it is not suitable for slurry samples with under 0.5 wt % bitumen. Overall, this study highlights the 19F-NMR method as a promising and sustainable alternative for oil sands composition analysis suitable for both laboratory and potential on-site applications.

Introduction

The Athabasca oil sands in Canada represent the largest known oil sand deposit globally. A typical oil sands ore contains about 10 wt % bitumen, 5 wt % water, and 85 wt % mineral solids. In surface mining operations, bitumen is extracted through a multistage process involving ore mining, slurry preparation, and separation in extraction facilities, followed by downstream upgrading and refining. Monitoring the composition of slurry streams generated throughout the extraction process is critical for effective process control, optimization, and maintaining overall operational efficiency. These slurry streams, commonly including bitumen froth, middlings, fluid fine tailings (FFT), and coarse sand tailings (CST), typically contain 0.2–60 wt % bitumen, 10–80 wt % water, and 10–40 wt % solids. The Dean–Stark method is widely regarded as the gold standard for determining the compositions of oil sands ore and slurry samples both in analytical laboratories and at extraction plants. However, this technique is time-consuming and energy-intensive. A single Dean–Stark analysis typically requires 24 h or more under continuous reflux with toluene, resulting in this type of lab data not being used for real-time process control and optimization. Several faster technologies have been developed or deployed by the oil sands industry in recent years, including near-infrared spectroscopy, − X-ray fluorescence, microwave transmission analyzer + prompt gamma neutron activation analyzer, laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy, , reflectance hyperspectroscopy, and time-domain nuclear magnetic resonance pulse spectrometer, , but the effectiveness is not satisfactory, with a significant challenge of accuracy and complex correlation.

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) is a well-known quantitative analytical technique to obtain content and purity information on chemical species in complex multicomponent mixtures in a liquid medium. A typical measurement for quantitative NMR (qNMR) analysis is less than 1 min with high accuracy. The basic principle of qNMR is that the signal intensity of NMR active nuclei (e.g., 1H, 13C, or 19F) is directly proportional to the number of nuclei and thus the solute concentration in samples (either molar or w/w basis). The linearity between the absolute signal area of samples (i.e., peak integral) and sample concentration makes it a universal method independent of the matrix. The 1H NMR is the most common qNMR because of its relatively high NMR sensitivity and ubiquitous presence of hydrogen atoms in many materials. The attempted use of 1H NMR on composition determination of oil sands ores, bitumen froths, and tailings has been investigated by direct 1H signal integration or distribution of relaxation times, T 1 (longitudinal relaxation time) and T 2 (transverse relaxation time), of water and bitumen, ,− but two significant drawbacks are associated with this technique: (1) the chemical shift range for 1H is compressed into a relatively narrow range of 0–12 ppm, and the overlap of 1H signals from water, bitumen, and other dissolved impurities makes the signals indistinguishable; and (2) the presence of clay/fine solids in samples complicates the 1H signal amplitudes and relaxation times, leading to poor correlations between relaxation times of 1H of water and bitumen and their actual content.

Similar to 1H NMR, 19F-NMR is used to measure the signal of 19F, stemming from the soluble fluorine components in a liquid environment. Specifically, the benchtop 19F-NMR spectrometer has been developed in recent years to detect and quantify fluorine-containing compounds in various matrices for routine analysis , and monitor the chemical reaction kinetics of fluorine-containing compounds for different industrial applications. − The drawbacks of using 1H NMR on complex samples could be surmounted by using 19F-NMR, which offers several advantages: (1) 19F-NMR measurements are almost equally fast as 1H NMR experiments (84% relative sensitivity compared with 1H) with high accuracy and repeatability; (2) fluorine’s chemical shifts span a relatively large range of −200 to +200 ppm, so signal overlapping is less likely than in 1H NMR even at a high concentration of the solutes; and (3) 19F-NMR specifically analyzes the fluorinated molecules, and therefore, neither nonfluoride-containing compounds (e.g., water and bitumen in the slurry) nor the opacity of samples interferes with the 19F signals (common in oil sands slurry samples). However, a direct measurement of fluorine signals in oil sand slurry is impractical, as bitumen does not contain fluorine elements, and the fluorine content in process water (mainly fluoride salts, <10 ppm) is well below the detection limit of 19F-NMR.

Our approach was to add a small amount of two complementary F-containing compounds to the slurry sample as 19F-NMR tracers: an oleophilic tracer (Tracer A), which dissolves or disperses mainly into the bitumen phase, and a hydrophilic tracer (Tracer B), which dissolves mainly in the aqueous phase. Upon phase separation, dilution by bitumen and water, respectively, causes the 19F peak integrals of Tracer A and Tracer B to change inversely with the bitumen and water concentrations in the sample. Under constant NMR acquisition parameters and sample-handling procedures, simple univariate calibration curves for trace A in the bitumen phase and tracer B in the aqueous phase can be generated. In this study, we developed a benchtop 19F-NMR spectrometer with the addition of suitable F-containing tracers to determine the oil sands slurry’s composition, with the aim of replacing the Dean–Stark method currently used in the analytical laboratories and oil sand extraction plants. For simplicity and consistency, the term “19F-NMR method” is used throughout this paper to refer to this combined approach involving a benchtop 19F-NMR spectrometer and tracer addition. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first successful application of the 19F-NMR method to quantify a range of oil sands slurry compositions, including oil sand ores, bitumen froth, middlings, FFT, and coarse sand tailings (CST).

Experimental Section

Materials

Trifluorotoluene (TFT, anhydrous, >99%), sodium trifluoroacetate (NaTFA, 98%), and xylene (ACS grade, >98.5%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Toluene (ACS grade, 99.5%) was purchased from Fisher Scientific. All of the reagents were used as received. Bitumen, process water, FFT, CST, and oil sands ores were provided by oil sands operators in north Fort McMurray mining sites. Solids used in this experiment were extracted from an oil sand ore using the Dean–Stark method. Densities of bitumen and tracer solutions were measured in triplicate using a 25 mL pycnometer and are reported as mean ± standard deviation.

19F-NMR Sample Preparation

Tracer and Calibration Solution Preparation

Fluorine-containing compounds, TFT and NaTFA, are tracers used to analyze bitumen and water contents using benchtop 19F-NMR, respectively. The tracer solutions serve two purposes: (1) construction of calibration curves and (2) determination of the bitumen and water contents in oil sands slurry samples. A TFT/xylene stock solution (2.53 wt %) was prepared by dissolving 0.391 g of TFT in 15.077 g of xylene in a glass jar with 5 min mixing by a Vortex mixer (Fisher Scientific). The stock solution (2.53 wt %) was used to prepare two diluted TFT/xylene solutions (1.52 and 0.52 wt %) by further diluting it in known amounts of xylene. A NaTFA/water stock solution (2.46 wt %) was prepared by dissolving 0.379 g of NaTFA in 15.018 g of water in a glass jar with 5 min mixing by a Vortex mixer. The NaTFA/water stock solution (2.46 wt %) was used to prepare two diluted NaTFA/water solutions (1.48 and 0.25 wt %) by further diluting it in known amounts of water.

Synthetic Oil Sands Slurry Sample Preparation with Known Concentrations

To evaluate the performance of the 19F-NMR method, synthetic oil sands slurry samples were prepared using known quantities of bitumen, process water, and solids. These samples contain a wide range of compositions, bitumen (0.2–90 wt %), water (5–95 wt %), and solids (5–57 wt %) to mimic real slurry samples such as bitumen froth, middlings, and tailings produced during the oil sands extraction process. These controlled samples are referred to as “synthetic oil sands slurry” to distinguish them from real oil sands slurry samples from the plant. For a typical synthetic oil sands slurry sample preparation, predetermined amounts of bitumen, solids, and process water were mixed using a benchtop Orbital Shaker (Innova 2000, New Brunswick) at 300 rpm for 5 min. A total of 11 different slurry compositions were prepared with 3 replicates per composition. The known contents of bitumen, water, and solids in each synthetic sample enabled a quantitative assessment of the accuracy of the 19F-NMR method.

Real Oil Sands Slurry Samples with Unknown Concentrations

Real oil sands slurry samples, including FFT, CST, middlings,and oil sands ores, were analyzed as received. Prior to measurement, each sample was homogenized using a hand-drill mixer in 4-gallon plastic pails, after which aliquots were promptly collected and further mixed with a Benchtop Orbital Shaker (Innova 2000, New Brunswick) at 300 rpm for 5 min to minimize subsampling variability.

19F-NMR Analysis

19F-NMR Sample Preparation

The detailed procedures for sample preparation and separation of bitumen, water, and solid layers for the 19F NMR method are illustrated in Scheme and described below for both synthetic and real oil sands slurry samples.

Sample Preparation Process for the 19F-NMR Method and the Corresponding Process Time for Each Step

A 20 g of oil sands slurry sample was mixed with 5 g of 2.53 wt % TFT in xylene and 5 g of 2.46 wt % NaTFA in water in a preweighted 50 mL centrifuge tube. The exact weights of the slurry and tracer solutions added were recorded for subsequent back-calculation. Following tracer addition, the slurry sample was mixed by a benchtop Orbital Shaker at 300 rpm for 0.5 h to fully dissolve TFT and NaTFA in the bitumen and water phases, respectively. The sample was then centrifuged at 7,000 rpm (5,725 RCF) for 3 min, resulting in three distinct layers in the tube: a black bitumen layer (top), a transparent aqueous layer (middle), and a sedimented solid layer (bottom).

Top Layer for Bitumen Content Analysis

The bitumen layer (top) contains a solution of bitumen, TFT, and xylene. A 2 mL aliquot of this layer was transferred into a 2.5 mL centrifuge tube and further centrifuged at 14,000 rpm (18,407 RCF) for 3 min to remove any impurities (e.g., water or solid that may still be entrained in the bitumen layer after the low centrifugation process). Then, a 0.6 mL aliquot of the purified bitumen layer was transferred to an NMR tube for bitumen content analysis.

Middle Layer for Water Content Analysis

The middle aqueous layer contains process water from the slurry sample, NaTFA, and dilution water from the NaTAF solution. A procedure analogous to that used for the bitumen layer was followed. Specifically, a 2 mL aliquot of the middle water layer was transferred into a 2.5 mL centrifuge tube and then centrifuged at 14,000 rpm (18,407 RCF) for 3 min to remove any bitumen droplets or solids entrained in the water layer. A 0.6 mL aliquot of the clarified aqueous phase was then transferred to an NMR tube for water content analysis.

Bottom Layer for Solid Content Analysis

Following the removal of bitumen and water phases, the remaining bottom layer consisted mainly of solids along with a trace amount of residual bitumen and water. The solids were washed by redispersing the bottom layer material in 10 mL of water and 10 mL of toluene; this is then followed by centrifugation at 7,000 rpm (5,725 RCF) for 5 min. After decanting the rinsing solution (water and toluene), the solids were dried in a vacuum oven at 120 °C for 3 h. The final solid weight in the slurry sample was measured gravimetrically using a benchtop balance. It is important to note that the solid isolation and washing step was performed solely to validate the method and for mass balance purposes. For routine sample analysis, the solid content can be determined by subtracting the measured bitumen and water contents from the total sample mass.

19F-NMR Analysis and Operating Parameters

All 19F-NMR spectra were collected at ambient temperature (∼22 °C) with a Nanalysis 60e benchtop NMR spectrometer at a 60 MHz magnetic field (1.4 T magnet) with a 1H–19F probe without using deuterated solvents. In brief, an NMR tube (5.0 mm in diameter) containing 0.6 mL of the as-prepared 19F-NMR sample was loaded into the NMR spectrometer. Table S1 lists the T 1 values of TFT in xylene at different concentrations with and without bitumen, as well as those of NaTFA in water. The T 1 values range from 0.9 to 1.7 s for TFT in xylene and xylene/bitumen and 3.1 s for NaTFA in water. All spectra were collected using the following optimized parameters: spectral width, 200 ppm; spectral center, −100 ppm; time domain (number of points), 16,384; number of scans, 8; scan delay, 10 s for NaTFA and 15 s for TFT, respectively; pulse angle, 90°; digital resolution, 0.10 Hz. All NMR measurements were carried out with a fixed receiver gain of 45 dB to ensure consistency of quantitative results and to avoid receiver saturation. Although qNMR typically requires a scan delay >5 × T 1, a delay of 3–4 × T 1 for NaTFA still provided accurate water content quantification while saving experimental time. The MestReNova software (v14.2.2, Mestrelab Research S.L.) was used to correct spectra phase and baseline distortions and to integrate the NMR peaks. The region of integration was kept constant among the analyzed samples. Data analysis and calculations were conducted with Microsoft Excel.

Results and Discussion

19F-NMR Calibration Curves

TFT was selected as a 19F-NMR tracer to quantify the bitumen content in oil sands slurry samples. The bitumen-soluble TFT has low solubility in water (<1 g/L at 20 °C), so it stays mainly in the bitumen phase as a solute. Similarly, NaTFA is a suitable NMR tracer for water content quantification, since NaTFA is highly soluble in water (625 g/L at 20 °C). Given its high polarity, NaTFA is expected to be insoluble in nonpolar organic solvents such as xylene and bitumen. The expectation was confirmed experimentally, as no NaTFA signal was detected in either xylene or bitumen under any of the tested conditions (Figure S1).

The 19F-NMR spectra of TFT/xylene solutions with concentrations of 0.5–2.5 wt % and NaTFA/water solutions with concentrations of 0.3–2.5 wt % were collected and are shown in Figure a–c, respectively. Both TFT and NaTFA tracers exhibit a single but distinguishable peak of fluorine signals over the NMR spectrum region, without overlapping, i.e., the chemical shifts for TFT and NaTFA are −57.5 to −58 ppm and −75 to −76 ppm, respectively, measured without the use of an internal standard as the reference. It should be noted that the 19F chemical shift of the tracers can vary over a ±5 ppm range in this study due to the absence of an internal standard; however, this does not affect signal integration, as each spectrum contains only the tracer signal with no interference. For both tracers, 19F peak intensities increased proportionally with the tracer concentration in solutions. Calibration curves of TFT and NaTFA tracers (Figure b–d) were constructed by plotting the absolute integral areas of 19F signals (after baseline correction) against the tracer concentration. Both data sets show excellent linear correlation with a coefficient of determination (R 2) of 1.0000 for TFT and NaTFA. It is expected that an excellent linear correlation can be maintained with a wide range of concentrations, but the current concentration range tested is sufficient to be used to determine the slurry compositions in this study.

19F-NMR Method Validation

Density Factor

All NMR samples were prepared gravimetrically, and a fixed volume of 0.6 mL aliquot was transferred into each tube to fill the probe’s active volume consistently. When the sample and tracer solution have identical densities, eq can be used directly to calculate the bitumen content. However, since bitumen (ρ = 1.0054 ± 0.0008 g/mL) and TFT/xylene stock solution (ρ = 0.8714 ± 0.0004 g/mL) differ in density, a correction factor, k, defined as the ratio of the bitumen density to the density of the TFT/xylene mixture, was introduced to adjust the bitumen mass term, and the mass of bitumen in sample can be determined by eq . Therefore, the TFT concentration in the bitumen phase, x, is then obtained from its calibration curve (integral vs concentration), as shown in Figure b, leaving bitumen mass as the only unknown in eq , which can be solved directly. The bitumen content in oil sand slurry is then calculated using eq . 4 Mb=Ct×Mt−x×MtMt×100×k where k is the density correction factor. In this study, k = 1.1538.

The introduction of k was further validated by mixing bitumen and xylene in different ratios with a fixed TFT concentration. As shown in Figure S2, increasing the bitumen concentration does result in an increased integrated 19F signal area, consistent with changes in sample density. It is noteworthy that slight line broadening and chemical shift changes were also observed with increasing bitumen concentrations; however, these effects may result from factors such as increased viscosity or molecular interactions between bitumen molecules and tracers.

For water content determination, on the other hand, no density correction is required, as the water in the aqueous phase serves as the NaTFA solvent solely. The densities of pure water and the NaTFA solution are effectively equivalent at the tested concentrations. eqs and can be used directly to calculate water content.

Bitumen Content Determination

Synthetic slurry samples containing 0.2–90 wt % bitumen were analyzed using 19FNMR method to validate the method and identify any limitations of using a TFT/xylene tracer solution. Figure a–b compares the bitumen content calculated with and without applying a density factor (k = 1.1538) using equations and . Both data sets yielded excellent linearity (R 2 = 0.9992). However, without k, the slope is 0.8853, indicating that the bitumen content was significantly underestimated, especially at higher bitumen contents (Table S1). On the other hand, incorporating k improved accuracy dramatically, yielding a slope of 1.0215, which suggests the importance of k for accurate quantifications. The corrected 19F-NMR results (with k) are also in close agreement with values obtained via the Dean–Stark method across from 0.5 wt % to 90 wt %, demonstrating that the 19F-NMR method is reliable for quantifying bitumen content in oil sand slurry samples for bitumen content above 0.5 wt %. As shown in Figure b, which focuses on the low bitumen content region (<5 wt %), minor deviations in slope and linearity are observed for the 19F-NMR method, indicating increased measurement uncertainty. This is particularly evident at concentrations below 0.5 wt %. For instance, the lowest bitumen content tested in this work was 0.2 wt %, which approximates the lowest bitumen content typically encountered in oil sands slurries (e.g., CST). At this concentration, the 19F-NMR method reported a value of only 0.09 ± 0.02 wt %, underestimating the true content by more than 50%. This discrepancy may arise from minor solvent (xylene) loss due to evaporation during the extended mixing (up to 1 h) and subsequent centrifugation. However, a more likely explanation is the very small bitumen volume in the samples, which limits the dilution of the tracer solution. As a result, the signal intensity changes are minimal and exhibit higher fluctuations, making them difficult to distinguish reliably. This suggests that 19F-NMR, under the current experimental conditions, may have a practical detection threshold around 0.5 wt % for bitumen quantification in oil sands slurries.

Water Content Determination

Similarly, synthetic slurry samples containing 5–95 wt % process water were analyzed by 19F-NMR with a NaTFA/water tracer solution through the same sequence of sample preparation. As discussed previously, no density correction factor was required in the calculation since water served as the sole solvent to dilute NaTFA. As shown in Figure , the 19F-NMR results exhibit an almost perfect correlation with the actual water contents, yielding a parity plot slope of 1.0104 and an R 2 value of 0.9999. When compared to the Dean–Stark method, the 19F-NMR method demonstrates superior accuracy in quantifying the water content. The Dean–Stark method slightly underestimates water content, with a parity plot slope of 0.9819. Column 3 of Tables and presents a direct comparison of the water contents determined by 19F-NMR and Dean–Stark methods. Notably, the 19F-NMR method outperforms Dean–Stark methods, particularly at the lower water content (≤10 wt %). For instance, at the lowest tested water content (5.00 wt %), the 19F-NMR method reports 5.02 wt % compared to 5.24 wt % by Dean–Stark. Water contents below 5 wt % were not examined further, as typical oil sand slurries contain water well above this threshold.

A key challenge in water analysis by the 19F-NMR method for oil sands slurries is the selection of a suitable tracer for the aqueous phase. Unlike the bitumen phase, where nonpolar organic fluorinated tracers are typically used and exhibit minimal interaction with solids, the water phase requires polar, often ionic, tracers to ensure full solubility in the aqueous medium. However, this ionic nature introduces a key complication: strong interactions between the tracer and solid components in samples, particularly clay minerals, can bias the NMR signal. Clay minerals constitute a significant fraction of the solids fraction in oil sands slurries, and they are known for their amphoteric surface properties. Depending on the solution pH relative to their point of zero charge (pHpzc), clay surfaces can carry either positive or negative charge. Under common slurry conditions (pH = 7.5–9), positively charged clay surfaces readily adsorb anionic species such as fluoride ions (F–). Consequently, a common fluoride salt, e.g., sodium fluoride, cannot be a suitable tracer for this purpose, as substantial F– adsorption onto solids takes place, thereby depleting the tracer concentration in the analyzed liquid phase and causing a consistent overestimation of water content. To overcome these limitations, NaTFA was selected as the hydrophilic tracer. The trifluoroacetate anion is a larger and more weakly coordinating species than free F– due to steric hindrance from its organic backbone and its weaker electrostatic interaction, making it less likely to strongly associate with clay minerals compared to F–. The effectiveness of NaTFA as a nonadsorbing aqueous-phase tracer was confirmed through direct comparison of 19F-NMR results with gravimetric water content determined via the Dean–Stark method (Figure ). This tracer selection strategy enabled accurate and reproducible water quantification without requiring additional experimental steps to address tracer loss. Overall, the developed 19F-NMR method provides a robust and practical approach for water content determination in oil sands slurry samples containing a wide range of water concentrations. Further research is warranted to investigate the adsorption behavior of hydrophilic tracers on mineral solids, including the roles of surface chemistry, electrostatic interactions, and mineral composition. A deeper understanding of these mechanisms will help refine hydrophilic tracer selection and improve the robustness of future applications in complex slurry systems.

Solid Content Determination

Slurry samples containing 5–57 wt % solids were quantified by gravimetric analysis, in which solids were recovered from the bottom layer following centrifugation and dried at 120 °C for 3 h under vacuum. The solid contents determined via this gravimetric approach (solid–gravimetric) were compared to those calculated from the 19F-NMR method (solid–calculated) as well as the Dean–Stark method. As shown in Figure , a nearly linear correlation between gravimetric measurements and the actual solid compositions of the slurry samples is observed. In contrast, the Dean–Stark method shows a slightly lower accuracy in terms of both slope and linearity. The calculated solid contents from 19F-NMR method also exhibit good agreement with actual values, albeit with some degree of underestimation. This discrepancy is mainly attributed to the minor overestimations in bitumen and water contents by the 19F-NMR method, as discussed in previous sections. While gravimetric analysis offers direct and reliable measurement, it involves lengthy drying steps that can limit throughput. The strong correlation between gravimetric and NMR-calculated values demonstrated in this study indicates that solids quantification by subtraction is indeed a practical and accurate alternative. This indirect method effectively eliminates the need for vacuum drying, streamlining the overall 19F-NMR workflow and enhancing the efficiency in slurry sample analysis.

For the middlings sample, excellent agreements across all three components were observed between the 19F-NMR method and the Dean–Stark method with minimal NMR deviations. Similarly, strong agreement was observed for water and solid contents in the FFT sample between the two methods. However, a larger relative deviation (36%), corresponding to a small absolute difference (0.43 wt %), was observed for bitumen content in FFT, likely due to the subsampling issue. For CST samples, bitumen and water content measurements demonstrated good agreement between the two methods, while solid quantification yielded a larger relative deviation (16%). On the other hand, the relatively large standard deviation for the three components in CST was also observed for both methods, especially in water and solid quantification. These discrepancies are ascribed to the heterogeneous composition and sampling challenges of CST, a common problem encountered during sampling due to the quick settling of coarse solids in tailings.

For oil sands ore samples, the 19F-NMR method consistently yielded lower bitumen and water contents compared to the Dean–Stark method by approximately 18% and 25%, respectively. These discrepancies are mainly attributed to limitations in the current sample preparation protocol for the 19F-NMR method, such as incomplete bitumen liberation and insufficient phase separation of bitumen and water from the solids matrix, rather than to shortcomings of the 19F-NMR method itself. Despite these issues, the 19F-NMR method remains a promising approach for analyzing oil sands ore samples, and further optimization of the sample preparation procedures is needed.

Although quantification of bitumen, water, and solids using 19F-NMR for oil sands, FFT, and CST showed relatively large deviations in comparison with the Dean–Stark method, these discrepancies can be largely attributed to the heterogeneous nature of slurry samples and sampling inconsistencies, rather than limitations of the 19F-NMR method itself. This is supported by the results with good accuracy and precision in synthetic oil sand slurry systems. Nevertheless, results from synthetic oil sand slurry samples highlighted a key limitation of the method in quantifying low bitumen contents (i.e., <0.5 wt %). Reducing the amount of tracer addition to the slurry (e.g., a lower tracer concentration), which would increase the relative dilution factor, could theoretically improve resolution at low bitumen contents. While challenging to implement in practice, this approach is worth further investigation. Therefore, the use of the 19F-NMR method is not recommended for slurry samples containing bitumen below this threshold.

From an analytical standpoint, the developed 19F-NMR method demonstrates accuracy comparable to that of the Dean–Stark method for multiphase characterization of oil sands–related samples. However, 19F-NMR requires pretreatment steps including the addition of a fluorinated tracer and effective separation of bitumen, water, and solid phases prior to measurement. In contrast, the Dean–Stark method achieves phase separation via thermal distillation and solvent extraction. Each method offers unique advantages depending on the sample type and operational constraints.

19F-NMR Analysis Time, Cost, Safety, and Environment

The above sections have demonstrated the proof of concept using the 19F-NMR method for quantifying bitumen and water contents in oil sands slurry samples. This method offers accurate and rapid analyses with minimal costs and reduced material consumption, although it has limitations such as reducing accuracy at low bitumen contents (i.e., <0.5 wt %).

Based on the limitations and accuracy of the developed 19F-NMR method, the analysis time was estimated according to the number of water and bitumen NMR measurements required for the specific type of oil sand slurry samples. For example, when bitumen content is ≥0.5 wt % (e.g., middlings or bitumen froth samples), one NMR measurement for bitumen and one for water is sufficient (see Table S3 in the supporting document). Solids can be calculated by subtracting the bitumen and water contents from the total slurry mass. However, for slurry samples containing <0.5 wt % bitumen (e.g., CST), bitumen measurement is not accurate. In this case, one NMR measurement for water and one gravimetric measurement for solids are required (see Table S4 in the Supporting Information). Moreover, a sample like oil sands ore will require one measurement for bitumen and three measurements for water to achieve average compositions and standard deviation similar to those obtained with the Dean–Stark method, which is likely less attractive to replace the Dean–Stark method (see Table S5 in the Supporting Information). Therefore, the estimation is restricted only to the tailing samples from extraction streams. Figure compares the total time required for slurry composition estimation and 19F-NMR capacity for analyzing a large number of slurry samples (10–20 samples and 1–2 NMR setup). The data were collected from Tables S3 and S4 in the Supporting Information. Figure a shows that the compositions of 10 tailings samples (bitumen ≥0.5 wt %) can be estimated in 3.5 h using the developed 19F-NMR technique, with one measurement for bitumen and one measurement for water per sample, without the analysis for solid. If solids were measured gravimetrically by oven drying, the total analysis time would increase to 5.4 h. The 19F-NMR method has the capacity to analyze a large number of samples simultaneously. For instance, the total estimation time for 20 middling samples is 5.3 h, if a second NMR unit is invested. An additional 1 h is required if the solid is also analyzed. However, solid analysis may not be necessary due to the high precision of the 19F-NMR technique. For CST, FFT, middling, and bitumen froth samples, solids must be analyzed gravimetrically. Total estimation times for analyzing 10 samples with 1 NMR setup and 20 samples with 2 setups are 5.2 and 6.5 h, respectively, which include the time for solids analysis, as it cannot be calculated from the total mass balance. In contrast, each Dean–Stark analysis typically requires at least 24 h of reaction time under refluxing conditions inside a fume hood. Typically, 4 to 5 setups can be placed inside a standard lab fume hood. A large number of samples may require substantial fume hood space in a fully equipped analytical lab. For an analytical lab with 5 Dean–Stark setups, it takes at least 48 h to complete 10 middling samples analysis (vs. 3.5 h with 19 F−NMR method). It is especially useful when the analytical results need to guide the process conditions, where a rapid analysis is absolutely necessary.

Another advantage of the 19F-NMR method is its low cost, as it consumes a minimal amount of chemicals and materials (both slurry samples and solvents), resulting in a minimal amount of organic and inorganic waste production. In contrast, Dean–Stark analysis requires large amounts of flammable solvents (such as toluene, which is a Category 2 hazard) and involves constant heating for up to 3 days, depending on the water content in the sample.

In summary, the 19F-NMR method offers significant advantages over the Dean–Stark method in terms of analytical throughput, time efficiency, cost-effectiveness, and health, safety, and environmental perspectives. Conclusions

A benchtop 19F-NMR spectrometer, coupled with fluorine-containing tracers (referred to as the 19F-NMR method), was successfully developed to quantify the bitumen, water, and solid content in oil sands slurry containing more than 0.5 wt % bitumen content.
