Perioperative cognitive behavioural therapy compared with pain education and mindfulness for chronic postsurgical pain in breast cancer patients with high pain catastrophising characteristics: a randomised, controlled, parallel group clinical trial☆
Authors: Aneurin Moorthy, Damien Lowry, Carla Edgely, Margarita Blajeva, Máire-Bríd Casey, Donal J. Buggy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe incidence of chronic postsurgical pain (CPSP) is relatively high after breast cancer surgery. Psychological factors, especially high pain catastrophising, are predictive of CPSP. Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) can reduce anxiety and depression and help emotional self-regulation. We tested the hypothesis that perioperative CBT is more effective than a pain education and mindfulness (PEM) programme at reducing CPSP intensity at 3 months after breast cancer surgery in high pain catastrophising patients.MethodsWomen having primary breast cancer surgery were screened (n=208) for pain catastrophising characteristics using the pain catastrophising scale (PCS). A total of 45 patients scoring ≥24 received four 1-h sessions with the same psychologist, randomised 1:1 to receive either CBT or PEM during the perioperative period. The primary outcome was Brief Pain Inventory (BPI) average pain severity measured at 3 months. Secondary outcomes included BPI composite pain interference scores, PCS scores, and Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) score.ResultsThere were no significant between group differences at 3 months with regard to the primary outcome, BPI average pain intensity score difference (median 25–75%) PEM vs CBT, 0 (0–1) vs 0 (0–1). P=0.45. Similarly, there were no significant between group differences at 3 months in relation to the study’s secondary outcomes: quality of recovery score at 24 h after surgery, pain interference, pain catastrophising, anxiety levels, and mood. There were within group improvements over the 3 months observation period in pain interference (PEM: P=0.05, CBT: P=0.01), catastrophising (PEM: P<0.01, CBT: P<0.01), anxiety (PEM: P<0.01, CBT: P<0.01) and mood (PEM: P=0.03, CBT: P=0.01) scores in both study arms after 3 months when compared with preoperative baseline values.ConclusionsDelivery of four one-to-one, perioperative CBT or PEM sessions to patients with high pain catastrophising characteristics achieved similar reductions in pain intensity at 3 months after breast cancer surgery. Perioperative psychology might potentially help reduce the incidence of CPSP after breast cancer surgery.Clinical trial registrationNCT04924010.

Chronic postsurgical pain (CPSP) is defined as pain at or near the site of surgery persisting for ≥3 months. The incidence of CPSP is up to 50% at 3 months and varies with surgical procedure.1,2 CPSP after breast cancer surgery has been observed in 28% of patients at 3 months making this group among the highest risk of developing CPSP.3 Clinical developments that could mitigate the development of CPSP after breast cancer surgery would potentially yield individual patient and societal benefit in improving physical and mental health outcomes, reducing future healthcare utilisation and costs.4

Several predictive factors for CPSP have been identified, the most important being the presence of chronic preoperative pain, high intensity of acute postoperative pain, and several psychological factors.5,6 Among psychological factors, pain catastrophising has emerged as one of the strongest predictors of pain severity and disability among individuals with a range of pain presentations including CPSP.7, 8, 9 Pain catastrophising is a maladaptive psychological coping strategy involving an exaggerated reaction to anticipated or actual pain, which can involve mental rumination, magnification of the perceived danger or threat associated with dynamics of pain intensity, and feelings of helplessness in relation to what can be done.10 A recent systematic review on psychological factors in patients undergoing major elective abdominal surgery suggests that pain catastrophising can have a direct influence on the neuropathophysiological mechanisms underlying pain experiences and can worsen pain and psychological outcomes after surgery.11

These psychological factors known to contribute to CPSP are interesting from a clinical perspective because they are modifiable and therefore may be a promising clinical target for psychotherapeutic intervention. Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) is an evidence-based, psychosocial intervention that aims to reduce symptoms of various mental health conditions, primarily depression and anxiety disorders. It focuses heavily on challenging cognitive distortions (such as thoughts, beliefs, and attitudes) and their associated behaviours to improve emotional regulation, making it an ideal therapeutic modality to ameliorate identified cognitive risk factors associated with CPSP. It is an established component of psychological intervention for patients with chronic neuropathic pain.12, 13, 14

While CBT and other psychological interventions are being evaluated for chronic pain conditions in general,15 there are limited available randomised, controlled trials that have evaluated the effect of perioperative CBT for prevention (i.e. on the progression of acute postoperative pain to CPSP after breast cancer surgery). Therefore, the primary objective of this randomised, controlled trial was to test the hypothesis that a perioperative CBT-based psychological intervention is more effective at reducing CPSP pain intensity at 3-month follow-up, in high pain catastrophising patients undergoing breast cancer surgery, compared with a pain education and mindfulness (PEM) programme. An active control arm was designed on the basis of facilitating participant blinding. Control arm content was deliberately selected to be generic (pain education) enough in nature so as not to be overly impactful, but also a ‘proxy therapy’ (mindfulness) for patients to know they were being ‘treated’. Non-specific mindfulness exercises were selected in an attempt to minimise any theoretically targeted effect on catastrophising tendencies, the identified primary psychological modifier of CPSP.

Methods

This single-centre, randomised, controlled trial was approved by Mater Misericordiae University Hospital’s Institutional Review Board (reference: 1/378/2217) on 12 May 2021. The trial was pre-registered on clinicaltrials.gov (reference: NCT04924010) on 11 June 2021 and the full study protocol was published online.16 As there was no external grant funding, this trial was undertaken at a single site, using available, existing resources.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Neither patients nor the public were involved in the design, reporting, or dissemination plans of this clinical trial. Patient participation was through voluntary recruitment. The inclusion criteria were female patients, between 18 and 75 yr of age, undergoing elective breast cancer surgery (wide local excision [WLE] with or without sentinel lymph node biopsy [SLNB]; or full mastectomy), endorsing a pain catastrophising scale (PCS) score ≥24. Exclusion criteria included absence of informed consent, surgery for benign breast disease, requirement of major surgery <3 months after current breast cancer surgery, severe comorbid psychiatric conditions that were likely to adversely affect insight or adherence to study protocols (e.g. dementia, schizophrenia, active psychosis or mania), known or suspected non-compliance, known or suspected drug or alcohol problems within the past 3 months resulting in demonstrable psychosocial impairment, inability to follow study protocol because of non-proficiency in the English language (the sole language of the psychology interventionist), pregnancy, having an opioid intrathecal pump, having received CBT in the previous 12 months, and inability to complete at least one psychological session before proposed elective breast cancer surgery.

Randomisation

The trial arms were CBT or PEM. The randomisation process was performed by an independent person, who had no other involvement in the study, using an online computer-generated block randomisation, in blocks of eight. The patient study number and group allocation (CBT or PEM) were typed onto paper and concealed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. The sealed envelopes were opened by the treating psychologist (DL) only after each patient was recruited and before the first scheduled session with the psychologist. This was strictly adhered to for protocol fidelity reasons. Patients enrolled in this study were not informed which study group they had been allocated to. Furthermore, investigators collecting primary and secondary outcome data (AM, CE, and MB) and statistical analysis of the collected data (DJB, MBC) were masked to the patient’s group allocation, thus helping the trial achieve a double-blind design.

Standard of care and interventional groups

All patients received general anaesthesia and were monitored according to the Association of Anaesthetists of Great Britain and Ireland guidelines.17 Administration of supplemental regional anaesthesia (e.g. medial and lateral pectoralis nerve blocks, serratus anterior or erector spinae block) and intraoperative opioids were at the discretion of the treating consultant anaesthesiologist. All patients received regular paracetamol, a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (i.e. diclofenac or ibuprofen), and oral oxycodone as required for postoperative analgesia.

Participants allocated to the CBT intervention group received a total of 4 × 60 min 1:1 CBT sessions during the perioperative period, with at least one and at most two of these taking place before surgery. Participants receiving one preoperative session had three postoperative sessions and those receiving two before operation only had two after operation. These CBT sessions were delivered in-person or by video teleconference by the same dedicated senior psychologist (DL). The standardised CBT intervention was developed with reference to two sources: an evidence-based CBT manual specifically constructed to treat chronic pain18 and national implementation of CBT using a video teleconferencing format.19 The components of the CBT manual were selected in order to reflect the study’s primary objective, namely, mitigating catastrophising, cognitive characteristics. Similar studies evaluating psychological interventions on postsurgical outcomes often have between one and six sessions in their protocols. With this in mind we chose four in an attempt to balance the following considerations: resource limitations, protocol burden, and ‘dose’ of therapy likely to have an effect. A summary of the key components of CBT sessions delivered for this trial can be found in Supplementary Table 1. The CBT intervention largely followed the session plan as outlined in Supplementary Table 1, as much as was practicable.

Participants allocated to the PEM intervention received a total of 4 × 60 min 1:1 sessions during the perioperative period, with at least one and at most two of these taking place before surgery. An equal number of PEM sessions were selected in order to achieve participant blinding. These PEM sessions were delivered in-person or by video teleconferencing by the same psychologist (DL) who delivered the CBT sessions. We selected PEM as our control intervention to counter the potential bias of additional time spent with a professional psychologist during the CBT intervention. The control intervention consisted of pain education content and generic, non-specific, mindfulness-based exercises, which were facilitated during each session. Mindfulness exercises followed the same standardised order (general mindful breathing, body-scan, ‘RAIN’ (recognize, allow, investigate, and nurture) exercise, progressive muscle relaxation). Pain education information was derived from the self-management section of the Chronic Pain Ireland website (https://www.chronicpain.ie/our-services/self-management) and the pain section of the pain toolkit website (https://www.paintoolkit.org/persistent-pain).

Study outcomes

The primary outcome was average pain severity measured at 3 months after breast cancer surgery. Average pain severity was assessed by the Brief Pain Inventory-Short Form (BPI-SF), which asked participants to rate their breast surgical pain on average on a scale between 0 and 10, where ‘0’ indicated no pain and ‘10’ indicated pain as bad as you can imagine. It is not time-specific and asks the respondent to estimate their average pain experience. The interviewer used phrasing along the lines of, ‘ ….Thinking about your pain where you had your surgery in the breast area, can you tell me how it has changed since then……’

Reductions in chronic pain intensity of 10–20% have been suggested to reflect a minimally important change that is meaningful for people with chronic pain.20 Secondary outcomes included:1.Average pain interference at 1 and 3 months after surgery. This was measured by the pain interference subscale of the BPI-SF. This subscale is a 7-item questionnaire that measures the impact of pain on functions including general activity, walking, normal work, mood, relations with people, sleep, and enjoyment of life.20 It is scored between 0 and 10, where ‘0’ indicates no interference and ‘10’ indicates complete interference resulting from pain. An average pain interference composite score is obtained and was selected to evaluate any potential differentiation between groups, post-surgically, with respect to this aspect of the pain experience.2.Levels of pain catastrophising at 1 and 3 months after surgery. This was measured by the PCS. An overall score of ≥24 is significant for pain catastrophising.21 Given that participants were enrolled based on high catastrophising characteristics, the study sought to investigate any potentially significant differentiation between groups.3.Levels of depressed mood and anxiety at 1 and 3 months after surgery. This was measured by the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS). A depression or anxiety score >10 is considered clinically significant.22 This scale was included on the grounds that psychological distress is a common pre- and postsurgical feature and potentially significant differentiation between groups was worth investigating.4.Quality of recovery (QoR) at 24 h after surgery. This was measured by the QoR-15 scale and is scored between 0 and 150, where the greater the number indicates better postoperative recovery.23 This is a routinely used measure of wellbeing immediately after surgery, which itself can be a predictor of emerging clinical outcomes. On this basis it was important to control for QoR as a potential co-factor, should groups exhibit any significant differences.

Sample size calculation

Our primary outcome was the difference in the BPI-SF ‘average’ pain severity score between the CBT and PEM study groups at 3 months after breast cancer surgery. A clinically important difference on the BPI-SF is a 2-point raw score reduction on the 11-point Numerical Pain Rating Scale (NRS).24, 25, 26 The standard deviation of BPI scores after breast cancer surgery is in the order of 2.3.27 Assuming a type 1 error (α)=0.05 and type II error (β)=0.2, taking a BPI score reduction of 2 as the minimal clinically relevant difference with a standard deviation of 2.3, we prospectively calculated that a sample of n=21 participants per group was required to yield 80% power to detect a statistically significant difference in mean BPI-SF ‘average’ pain severity scores. We enrolled n=24 per study group to account for loss to follow-up, withdrawal, or significant missing data.

Statistical analysis

Outcome analyses were conducted by researchers (MBC and DB). Descriptive statistics were calculated for all outcome measures at each time point. Means and standard deviations or median (25–75% range) were calculated for normally distributed continuous variables or non-parametric data whereas frequencies and percentages were calculated for categorical variables. Most data were skewed (non-parametric) and these data were analysed using the Mann–Whitney test for between group comparisons where there were two independent groups or Kruskal–Wallis one-way analysis of variance (anova) for comparing repeated pain data. Normally distributed data were tested by plotting the data points on a normal QQ plot. Supplementary Figure 1 illustrates the distribution of CBT and PEM pain scores at baseline, 1 month, and 3 months.

The primary analysis compared the effect of the interventions on the primary outcome; average pain severity at 3 months post surgery. Outcome analyses were conducted according to an intention-to-treat principle (i.e. all randomised participants were included in the main analysis and were analysed as randomised, regardless of protocol adherence). Secondary analysis involved the analysis of the secondary outcomes: QoR-15 score at 24 h after surgery, PCS, HADS, and average pain interference on the BPI-SF at 1 and 3 months post surgery. Linear mixed models on the outcome measures over time were fitted to evaluate the effectiveness of both interventions, which intrinsically adjusts for baseline scores. Statistical significance was assessed from a P-value <0.05 from the group by time interaction term. For all tests, two-sided P-values were used and reported to four decimal places with P-values <0.001 reported as P<0.001. Analyses were conducted using SPSS version 27 and GraphPad Prism version 10.0.3.

Results

Participant flow

The flow of participants through the trial is illustrated in Figure 1. Between June 2021 and November 2022 a total of 208 individuals were assessed for eligibility to take part in this study. One hundred and twenty-five individuals (60%) were found to be ineligible and 35 (16.8%) declined to participate. In total, n=48 patients agreed to take part and provided consent before enrolment. If possible, baseline measurements were taken at the time of enrolment, though some individuals (<10%) requested to do this at the initial point of contact. Participants were then randomly allocated to receive either the CBT intervention (n=24) or PEM (n=24). Enrolment, baseline measurement, and group allocation all happened before operation. Three participants did not receive the intervention to which they were assigned. One of these was found to be ineligible to take part after randomisation, because of a suspected neurocognitive disorder, and two participants withdrew from the study after randomisation, and had not completed the patient-reported outcome measures. Overall, the participants attended a median (25–75% range) of 43, 4, 5 sessions of the intervention to which they had been allocated. A total of 43 participants (90%) completed the patient-reported outcome measures at 1-month follow-up and 42 participants (88%) completed the measures at 3-month follow-up. Three participants did not complete the follow-up measures at either time point (one from the PEM group and two from the CBT group). One PEM group member was lost to follow-up as a result of serial unresponsiveness. Two CBT group participants were withdrawn because of suspected dementia (n=1) and reversal of participation willingness after enrolment and allocation.

Analyses for the primary outcome: Brief Pain Inventory average pain severity

No statistically significant differences in average pain severity were found between the PEM group and the CBT group at 1 month post surgery or at 3-month follow-up (Table 4). A marginal improvement in average pain severity was observed within the CBT group at 3-month follow-up, relative to baseline. There were no significant differences within the PEM group at 1-month or 3-month follow-up.

Analyses for secondary outcomes: pain interference, pain catastrophising, anxiety, depression, and quality of recovery

No statistically significant differences in pain interference, pain catastrophising, anxiety, or depression were found between the CBT and PEM groups at 1 month post surgery or at 3-month follow-up (Table 4). Quality of recovery scores were similar between both groups at 24 h after surgery (P=0.49). Significant improvements were observed within both groups for pain interference at 3-month follow-up when compared with baseline values (PEM: P=0.05, CBT: P=0.005). These improvements in pain interference at 3-month follow-up within both the PEM and CBT groups (1.2 and 1.7, respectively) represent clinically meaningful improvements, exceeding 1 point on the BPI interference scale.28 Statistically significant improvements were also observed within both groups for pain catastrophising, anxiety, and depression at both 1-month and 3-month follow-up post surgery (Table 4).

Post hoc analysis

Given the variation of both primary and secondary variables across time, including the difference between our groups at baseline in relation to average pain severity, we considered the possibility that certain variables could be moderating our primary BPI pain outcome across the study period from baseline to 3 months. With group allocation as our independent variable and 3-month average pain as our dependent variable, we considered the hypothesis that the following variables could exert a moderating influence on our primary outcome variable: age, baseline average pain severity, mood, and change in pain catastrophising.

There were no issues regarding multicollinearity. However, in an effort to balance concerns regarding the under-powered nature of our analysis with those variables most likely to be exerting a moderating effect, we reduced our regression model to include only ‘age’ and ‘baseline average pain severity’. We determined that baseline measurements of age combined with self-reported average pain severity explained 36% of the variance at 3-months average pain severity (R2=0.361). Furthermore, our analysis found that these two moderators had an effect on the relationship between group allocation and our primary outcome variable that approached statistical significance (P=0.05) to the extent that those allocated to the CBT group reported lower pain than those in the PEM arm. Age was found to have a significant effect on average pain severity 3 months post surgery, b=0.74, bias-corrected CI [0.321–0.375], P<0.01. Younger age benefitted more from CBT rather than education. Similarly, higher baseline average pain severity was found to have a more profound effect on the primary outcome variable, average pain severity 3-months post surgery, b=0.331, BCa CI [0.235–0.475], P<0.01.

Discussion

This randomised, controlled trial examines the effectiveness of CBT as a perioperative intervention to reduce pain severity after breast cancer surgery, when compared with a PEM control intervention, in patients with high levels of preoperative pain catastrophising. We performed this study in response to the fact that, despite significant medical advances in individualised breast cancer treatment,29 chronic pain after breast cancer surgery remains high at 28% at 3 months.3 Recent large-scale clinical trials have failed to demonstrate that optimising perioperative acute pain by pharmacological or opioid-sparing interventional strategies (e.g. regional anaesthesia) may have the potential to mitigate the prevalence of CPSP. 30,31 However, one observational study (n=123)32 demonstrated that targeting regional anaesthesia techniques to patients with high baseline pain catastrophising scores may reduce the incidence of CPSP. Therefore, studies examining whether non-opioid-based pharmacological interventions (e.g. psychological therapies), delivered during the perioperative period, may influence the incidence of chronic pain after surgery, are warranted.

Our hypothesis that perioperative CBT would be superior to a PEM intervention at reducing pain levels 3 months post surgery was not confirmed because both interventions had similar findings. Instead we demonstrated that levels of pain interference, pain catastrophising, anxiety, and depression were all significantly reduced in both groups at the end of the 3-month follow-up period when compared with preoperative baseline values. It is well established that these variables are known risk factors for developing chronic pain.33, 34, 35 Therefore, it appeared that study participants benefitted comparatively from both interventions, perhaps highlighting different mechanisms of therapeutic action regarding the mitigation of negative psychological prognostic indicators. This suggests that psychological interventions, similar to the ones we compared, may be a useful perioperative adjuvant therapy in the context to mitigate CPSP after breast cancer surgery.

While we initially envisaged a ‘CBT cohort’ and a ‘control’ cohort (i.e. no psychological intervention whatsoever, which is current standard practice), we subsequently reasoned that, if we observed a benefit of CBT intervention, there would be criticism for not comparing directly with a ‘human interaction’ intervention. Perhaps this was an error.

Regarding the post hoc analysis of potential moderating factors on the primary outcome of BPI over the 3-month study period, which suggested age and baseline pain intensity have a significant effect on the BPI at 3 months: from these results, it can be inferred that there is a potentially significant clinical phenotype of the patient undergoing breast cancer surgery who, characterised by older age and elevated reports of average pain severity before surgery, might benefit from CBT-style interventions in the perioperative period. Limitations of this analysis notwithstanding, including the small sample size, future studies, incorporating larger sample sizes, might wish to further explore the moderating effects of these specific variables.

A recently published meta-analysis of 15 randomised, controlled trials concluded that active perioperative psychological intervention (CBT or relaxation therapy) reduces persistent surgical pain and pain interference after non-cancer surgery.36 In addition, the authors found that perioperative education did not influence chronic pain or physical impairment after non-cancer surgery. Our study is novel in that we examined the effect of a perioperative psychological intervention in patients with high levels of pain catastrophising after breast cancer surgery. In comparison to non-cancer surgery, pain after cancer surgery may be further exacerbated by high levels of anxiety and depression related with the patient’s recent diagnosis of ‘cancer’37 and consequently may increase the risk of developing chronic postoperative pain.38 Therefore, any non-pharmacological intervention that reduces perioperative anxiety and depression levels may help to mitigate this risk.

Currently, there are two ongoing trials investigating the effects of CBT on the prevalence of chronic pain after breast cancer surgery. The AMAZONE trial is multicentre randomised, controlled trial, with an additional non-randomised control arm examining the effects of online CBT vs online education on the development of chronic pain at 6 months after surgery.39 The PREVENT trial is investigating whether a preoperative hypnosis session and postoperative web-based Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) intervention is superior to a mindfulness and usual care intervention in preventing postoperative chronic pain after breast cancer surgery.40 Another study evaluated the impact of a single, preoperative psychology workshop on postsurgical pain, reporting small, positive effects.40

Strengths of our study included its double-blinded, randomised, controlled study design; inclusion only of patients with high levels of preoperative pain catastrophising (PCS ≥24); and controlling for the potential cofounding effect of additional time spent, attention given, or both by a healthcare professional.

There are limitations to this study which may explain why we did not observe any clinically significant differences in pain severity scores at 3 months after surgery between our two study groups. Firstly, despite robust statistical sample size calculation (i.e. 80% power to detect a clinically meaningful difference of 2 on the BPI scale), our numbers are modest and future studies would benefit from a larger sample size. The relatively low postoperative pain scores reported may be explained by recent advances in surgical techniques, less invasive surgical approaches reducing surgical tissue trauma and therefore reducing the risk of developing postsurgical chronic pain.

Secondly, despite randomisation, the presence of a history of chronic pain was greater in participants in the PEM (44%) vs the CBT (22%) group (Table 1). This may have resulted in the participants in the PEM group having a higher resistance to the psychological pain intervention that was delivered to them during the perioperative period. In addition, it is worthy to note that the reported baseline pain levels in both groups were mild-moderate (mean of 3/10). This preoperative BPI average baseline pain level reported was not specifically localised to the breast region but included other areas (e.g. axilla, arm, back, shoulder, etc). The presence of this degree of preoperative pain is consistent with other studies within the literature.41 42 However, future studies may wish to consider restricting captured pain measures to local sites of surgical intervention.

Thirdly, we did not include a third non-intervention control group (i.e. usual care), which may have been a lost opportunity to demonstrate whether CBT or any psychological intervention, even a non-specific one such as our PEM group, was superior to usual care in reducing chronic pain scores after breast surgery. By excluding a usual care or ‘sham’ group, we were unable to truly determine whether the observed benefit in both groups in this study; in terms of pain, pain catastrophising, anxiety, and depression scores when compared with preoperative baseline values were as a result of our active psychological interventions (PEM or CBT) as described in our study, or as a result of natural alleviation of the patient’s anxiety levels at 3 months after ‘having surgery to treat cancer’. Inclusion of such a group would have had its own inherited bias (i.e. exposing participants to human interaction vs no interaction). Nevertheless, the results from our study may be used for future sample size calculation to develop a larger-scale study which incorporates a usual care group in addition to an active psychological intervention group. In addition, future studies would also benefit from multi-site designs with multiple therapists administering the interventions, with in-built cluster analyses to control for inherited bias which may be present by including a usual care group.

In conclusion, this single-centre, randomised, controlled trial demonstrated equivalence between CBT and a PEM intervention in reducing chronic pain scores at 3 months after breast cancer surgery, among patients with high pain catastrophising scores. While there was no control group without a psychological intervention, both interventions studied achieved similar reductions in postoperative levels of pain interference, anxiety, and depressive mood at 3 months after surgery, when compared with baseline preoperative values. Future studies evaluating the role of CBT in mitigating chronic pain after breast cancer surgery should include a non-psychotherapeutic control group.
