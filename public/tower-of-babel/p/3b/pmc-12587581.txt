Supporting infants with severe perinatal diagnoses, their families and healthcare providers: development of a perinatal palliative care program
Authors: Laure Dombrecht, Ellen Roets, Filip Cools, Kristien Roelens, Eve Van den Mooter, Linde Goossens, Kenneth Chambaere, Kim Beernaert
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPerinatal palliative care (PPC) provides essential support for families and healthcare providers at the end-of-life. Despite clear global need, structured PPC programs are not widely established.AimTo develop a PPC program that supports families confronted with a severe perinatal diagnosis for their (unborn) child, with a focus on evidence-based development of such program, ensuring care is effective, consistent, and aligned with best practices.MethodsWe systematically designed a PPC program following a 7-step method, using Bleijenberg’s extended Medical Research Council framework. We conducted a qualitative interview study with 22 healthcare providers and 18 bereaved parents from six hospitals to identify care gaps alongside an integrative review study of international programs. These combined findings informed the development of a PPC prototype program, intended to be integrated in routine practice. Subsequently, we refined the program, identified barriers to implementation, and explored opportunities for contextual tailoring through six participatory workshops involving both healthcare providers and bereaved parents at three hospitals. In these sessions we reviewed the preliminary care components, identified practical challenges, and adapted the model to align with local workflows, staffing realities, and documentation systems.ResultsFive core components were developed:Dedicated fixed PPC Team with care coordinators Formal/Specialized 1,5 day training covering essential PPC aspectsStepwise PPC Approach with a structured PPC plan including individualized care pathways and centralized resources and information sharingProactive psychological support for both families and staffStructured Team DebriefingsKey barriers to implementation included fragmented documentation systems, limited staffing and time resources, and challenges in cross-departmental coordination.DiscussionWe developed a PPC program based on existing international evidence and current PPC practices. This approach ensured both relevance and practical applicability. The program design is a strength, as it is grounded in evidence and shaped through active involvement of parents and healthcare providers, with attention to contextual tailoring. The program is now ready for pilot testing to assess feasibility and acceptability in clinical practice. Future implementation will require institutional support, contextual adaptation, and ongoing evaluation to ensure sustainable integration into perinatal care. Introduction

Despite major advances in obstetric and neonatal medicine, perinatal death—commonly defined as occurring between 22 weeks’ gestation and 7 days post-birth—remains a reality worldwide. In this manuscript, we extend the definition to include deaths up to 28 days post-birth to encompass the full neonatal period, which is typically managed within neonatal intensive care units. Each year, approximately 2.4 million neonatal deaths and 2.6 million stillbirths occur globally [1]. These losses have profound impacts on families and on the healthcare professionals involved, who face intense emotional and practical challenges. When an infant is diagnosed with a life-limiting condition, either before or shortly after birth, care needs arise: the baby needs comprehensive comfort care [2], parents need emotional and practical support [3], and clinicians benefit from guidance on how to adequately provide care [4]. Perinatal palliative care (PPC) is the multidisciplinary care approach developed to meet these needs and improve the quality of life for affected families [5]. It focuses on symptom prevention and relief, tailored family-centered care, infant comfort, and addressing psychosocial, spiritual, and practical issues [6].

PPC can be implemented either as exclusive comfort care or in parallel with curative efforts [7]. In adults, palliative care integration has been shown to improve symptom management and quality of life [8]. In the perinatal period, palliative care remains a relatively new and underexplored field and its full impact has yet to be systematically evaluated on a larger scale [6]. Nevertheless, observational and case studies show that PPC protocols can improve care transitions, reduce unnecessary invasive treatments, and enhance symptom management/control at the end of life [9, 10]. Beyond the infant’s care management, PPC also offers support to families through means of bereavement care, memory-making, and clear, emphatic communication [11–13]. Additionally, PPC could address psychosocial needs experienced by healthcare providers, hereby reducing moral distress and burnout risks [14, 15], for example through structured staff support [16].

Despite international evidence supporting PPC’s benefits [9, 10, 16], in many countries no formal PPC programs exist. Existing PPC programs, primarily in the U.S., emerged without evidence-based foundation [6]. Although elements of PPC are already present in many standard perinatal care practices, these efforts often lack integration into a cohesive program. Existing PPC initiatives worldwide rarely report their effect on infant comfort, parental experiences such as psychological outcomes, or well-being of healthcare professionals [16]. Additionally, existing PPC programs are not targeted toward certain patient groups, such as families opting for pregnancy termination following fetal diagnosis or those confronted with an unexpected life-limiting neonatal condition after birth [6, 17, 18]. These gaps can leave families without much needed support. A standardized and comprehensive PPC framework, spanning both obstetrics and neonatology, could bridge these gaps by formalizing tasks, referral structures, and procedures to deliver palliative care in the perinatal setting, while simultaneously enhancing staff preparedness.

PPC is a crucial yet scientifically underdeveloped field. Despite the clear global need for such services, structured PPC programs are not widely established in many health systems. We therefore aim to develop a holistic, family-centered PPC model, integrating best practices into standard perinatal care. For testing purposes, the model will be tailored to be feasible and acceptable specifically in the Flemish (Belgian) context, but will be internationally relevant and adaptable. This model aims to be both evidence-informed and developed in close consultation with key stakeholders, ensuring its relevance, feasibility, and sustainability within clinical practice.

Methods

Study design

We used the extended 7-step Medical Research Council (MRC) framework made by Bleijenberg [19] to structure the development of our PPC program (see Table 1 for an overview of steps and methods used), in order to strengthen the internal and external validity by including end-users such as healthcare providers and bereaved parents in almost every step of the development process. We opted for this rigorous and extensive development since PPC is still in its infancy and not much is known about working components, care components and effectiveness. All steps together provided us with a guide to systematically gather information needed to develop a program with the highest likelihood of success.

Step 1: problem identification by means of semi-structured interviews

We conducted face-to-face semi-structured interviews with healthcare providers and bereaved parents, to inform the program’s conceptualization, identifying needs and gaps in care provision. Interviews were performed between May 2021 and February 2022 with healthcare providers with experience in end-of-life care in the perinatal period, and bereaved parents who experienced perinatal loss. Recruitment was done by a representative physician at every participating hospital, who informed colleagues and bereaved parents of the study and transferred contact info of people interested in participating in the study to the researchers. No information on people refusing to participate was made available to the researchers. Informed consent was asked before the start of the interview, interviews were audiotaped and transcribed verbatim. Translated topic guides can be found in appendix 1. Two researchers independently analyzed the data, using thematic content analysis to extract current care provided, and hiatuses in current palliative care provision. Data were collected until no new information emerged, and data saturation was achieved. As a result, we have an overview of current practices within which our program should be installed, and identified hiatuses in care that could be addressed by our program.

Step 2: evidence identification by means of an integrative review

To identify the working components of existing PPC programs, we conducted an integrative search in MEDLINE, Embase, CENTRAL, CINAHL, PsycInfo and Web of Science, with a protocol registered in PROSPERO (CRD42021239005). Details of the procedure and results are reported elsewhere [6]. Study designs examining the effect of PPC compared to regular care, and (empirical) articles describing the components of care included in existing PPC initiatives were included. Both quantitative (e.g., RCTs, cohort studies) and qualitative designs were included, as well as studies describing PPC programs. NVivo was used to categorize key care components, which were synthesized into summary tables and a schematic overview.

Step 3: identifying or developing a theory using grounded theory

To develop the program, we employed a grounded theory methodology [20]. Without an a priori idea of our final model, we inductively looked at data from the interviews, analyzing them using the constant comparative method, allowing for the emergence of conceptual categories grounded in the experiences and insights of parents and healthcare professionals. Concurrently, findings from the review were incorporated to provide solutions to experienced barriers and hiatuses in current care provision. This was used as the basis for how a PPC program in the Belgian healthcare setting could work. This resulted in a preliminary version of the description of the PPC program components and the desired outcomes.

Step 4: determining the needs using workshops

To refine the PPC program, three joint workshops were organized at three separate hospitals, bringing together bereaved parents and healthcare professionals from the same institution. These workshops allowed participants to discuss hospital-specific policies and care practices, ensuring the program was contextually relevant. Researchers contacted all participants of the first phase who had indicated that they wanted to participate in these workshops. Additional recruitment through snowball sampling occurred. Workshops took place in March-April of 2023. Informed consent was obtained before the start of the workshop, conversations were audiotaped and transcribed verbatim. Translated topic guides can be found in appendix 1. Group discussions on the program components took place until consensus was reached. Data-analysis consisted of documenting the group consensus, and identifying expected barriers mentioned during the group discussions. During the workshops, the preliminary program components were extensively reviewed, discussed, and adapted to better align with the needs of both healthcare providers and parents.

Step 5: examining the practice using workshops

In the following three workshops in the same three hospitals, with significant overlap in the attendees from the first focus groups, we aimed to identify possible barriers and facilitators regarding our proposed program. Translated topic guides can be found in appendix 1. Additional participants were recruited using the same methods as in step 4, and data collection and analysis were done in a similar manner. During the workshops, we finetuned the components and determined the need for tailoring to specific hospital wards. Workshops took place in May 2023. A refined version of the program tailored to the specific hospital wards was drafted.

Step 6: modelling process and outcomes

The research team prioritized, reduced, selected and refined components added to the theoretical framework in steps 1–5, seeking balance between the most optimal ingredients, components, costs and time to achieve maximum success of the program on one hand, and the best possible fit with the current perinatal healthcare practice on the other hand. This step included tailoring to the specific context (step 4), and taking into account possible barriers (step 5). This was done by all involved researchers (2 psychologists and 1 sociologist with experience in perinatal palliative care research, 1 neonatologist and 2 gynecologists with experience in perinatal palliative care).

Step 7: intervention design

In this final step, the research team made a prototype of the actual program, including detailed descriptions of the program components, their goals and implementation strategies.

Overview of the development of the care components of the perinatal palliative care program

1. A fixed PPC Team

A fixed core team is proposed, comprising prenatal and neonatal clinicians (physicians, nurses, midwives), psychosocial support staff (social workers, psychologists, grief counselors), and pediatric palliative care liaisons. A designated care coordinator ensures continuity across appointments and follow-up care, acting as a central point of contact for families. This role, ideally filled by a midwife, nurse, or social worker, is pivotal in coordinating documentation, debriefings, and family support.

2. Targeted Training in Perinatal Palliative Care

The program includes formal training (1–2 days) for PPC team members, covering communication, palliative trajectories, medication protocols, advance care planning, and cultural sensitivity training. Modular learning allows for repetition as needed. Additionally, on-the-job training and structured medical mortality review meetings enhance team learning and integration.

3. A perinatal palliative care plan, including:

- A care pathway: describing which steps are usually followed in a perinatal palliative care setting.

- A manner of centralizing information: easy access to a shared folder with important information on palliative care in the perinatal period.

- A way to share case specific information with other care providers: where involved professional carers can indicate the most important take-home messages so that they are visual for everyone.

3. A structured Perinatal Palliative Care Plan

- A formal PPC care pathway is to be developed, building on existing templates of the participating hospital wards. This includes structured multidisciplinary meetings to co-create individualized care plans, checklists with space for narrative input, and clearly defined roles and responsibilities.

- Additionally, a centralized repository will house essential resources—religious and cultural information, bereavement templates, palliative medication guidelines, and ward-specific protocols (e.g., for stillbirth or late termination).

- Case-specific information will be shared via the hospital-wide Electronic Patient Record (EPR), with a dedicated tab for advance care planning (ACP) updates visible to all relevant providers. As social work and psychology notes are typically hidden from others, the ACP tab ensures critical updates are shared across disciplines, in compliance with privacy policies and parental consent.

4. Psychological Support for Families and Providers

Parents are proactively offered psychological support, initiated by a psychologist without requiring a request or fee. Peer support resources are distributed, and follow-up contact is ensured post-bereavement. For healthcare providers, structured psychological check-ins will be provided shortly after a death, stillbirth or termination of pregnancy.

Step 4: determining the needs

We conducted three focus groups with 15 healthcare providers and 1 bereaved parent from three hospitals (one focus group per hospital). The preliminary program components were expanded and specified to better reflect the practical needs of healthcare providers and parents, including: broader team composition and clearer roles (e.g., care coordinator), more detailed training structure and content, more structured care plans with centralized resources and electronic patient record integration, proactive psychological support for both families and staff, and debriefings made more flexible and standardized (Table 2).

Step 5: examining the practice

In the follow-up focus groups in each of the three hospitals a total of 13 healthcare providers and three bereaved parents participated. These sessions not only identified key implementation barriers for each component of the PPC model but also explored practical solutions to address them, which were subsequently integrated into the final program design:

Continuity of care was challenged by staffing shortages, making it difficult to assign a consistent point of contact for families. While families often identified a trusted caregiver, this did not always align with formal roles. To address this, the final model introduced a case manager role within the PPC team, ensuring each family has a designated contact person for follow-up and questions, while allowing flexibility in who fulfills this role depending on availability.

Step 6: modelling process and outcomes

The finalized PPC program consists of five core components each linked to specific intended outcomes and grounded in stakeholder-informed priorities. The program components were reduced and refined, moving from a broad set of potential components toward a more focused and feasible set, balancing clinical relevance, stakeholder priorities, and practical constraints. Each component includes targeted strategies to address previously identified implementation barriers. The final program model, visually summarized in Fig. 2, outlines the structure of the program and maps how each component is expected to contribute to improved care for families and healthcare providers, by implementing change at the level of the involved hospital wards.

Overview of the PPC program model

Step 7: intervention design

A finalized prototype of the PPC program was drafted, consisting of five core components with corresponding materials and an implementation protocol (See Table 2). The program begins with the assembly of a fixed multidisciplinary team composed of professionals from prenatal diagnostics, maternity, delivery, and neonatal care, alongside psychosocial support staff. Each family is assigned a case manager to ensure continuity and serve as a central contact point. All team members receive a two-day training in perinatal palliative care, supplemented by biannual mortality review meetings to enhance clinical learning and preparedness. A step-by-step care plan guides the PPC process, including clear inclusion criteria, individualized care pathways, and mechanisms for phasing out care when appropriate. Information is centralized and made accessible, ensuring that all involved providers can share and access relevant case-specific updates. Psychological support is systematically offered to both parents and healthcare providers, with proactive consultations and follow-up sessions integrated into the care trajectory. Structured debriefings are held after each neonatal death, stillbirth, or termination of pregnancy, organized in a hybrid format to facilitate participation and emotional reflection. Materials, such as contact sheets of PPC team members, protocols detailing responsibilities, and care flowcharts, support the implementation and consistency of the program across hospital settings. While the materials were tailored to each pilot site, the standardized template including the full program is available as online addendum (see online appendix 2).

Discussion

This study developed a perinatal palliative care (PPC) program tailored to the Flemish (Belgian) healthcare context, using Bleijenberg’s extended Medical Research Council (MRC) framework. The resulting program comprises five core components: a dedicated PPC team with care coordinators, formal training, a structured care pathway, proactive psychological support, and routine team debriefings. PPC program components were tailored to the current perinatal healthcare context, and materials were tailored to specific hospital sites where the pilot test will take place. At the same time, the program remains grounded in international evidence, ensuring its relevance to a broader international audience.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study lies in its rigorous and systematic development process, guided by the extended MRC framework. By means of triangulation of evidence through the combination of a literature review and needs-assessment in end-users, we ensured that the program was both evidence-informed and grounded in real-world clinical experiences. The iterative co-design process involving bereaved parents and healthcare professionals throughout the development process fostered contextual relevance, stakeholder ownership, acceptability of the model and early identification of implementation barriers.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the program was developed within the specific context of the Belgian healthcare system, which may reduce its direct applicability elsewhere without adaptation. However, the program incorporates several components that align with internationally recognized principles and guidelines in perinatal palliative care, supporting its broader relevance. Secondly, although we aimed to develop an evidence-based program, our literature review revealed that none of the identified components are supported by a strong evidence base. As such, the program should be considered evidence-informed. Each component still requires further evaluation to determine its effectiveness, of which our program development and later pilot test is a first step. Additionally, while the co-participatory focus group format fostered valuable dialogue, it may have influenced which parents chose to participate and what they felt comfortable sharing. Some may have self-selected out due to emotional vulnerability or perceived power dynamics, while others may have joined precisely to voice strong or contrasting views. Lastly, while the model emphasizes individualized care, its structured nature may still pose challenges in accommodating diverse family preferences. This issue may remain unaddressed, as despite efforts, the included family members did not represent a diverse background.

General discussion

Our program shares several core components with existing PPC models identified in the mentioned integrative review [6]. These include the establishment of a dedicated PPC team, structured training for healthcare providers, a formalized care pathway, psychological support for families and staff, and routine debriefings. These elements are widely recognized as foundational to PPC programs internationally and were among the most frequently reported components across the 69 reviewed publications [6]. However, our model diverges from many existing programs in some ways. First, while most international PPC models include detailed components of child-directed care such as pain and symptom management, our program does not explicitly address these aspects as one of the main care components for improvement. This omission was intentional and grounded in our qualitative findings: both healthcare providers and bereaved parents consistently reported that the comfort care provided to infants was already well-established and did not require further improvement. As such, our development efforts focused on areas where significant gaps were identified, particularly in care coordination, communication, and psychosocial support. As adequate pain- and symptom control is a key aspect of good PPC, it is added as part of the care pathway. Second, our model places a strong emphasis on cross-departmental integration and the use of shared electronic documentation systems, which were less commonly detailed in the reviewed literature. This again was grounded in our qualitative findings, as these gaps were most frequently mentioned in current PPC care provision. Lastly, our inclusion of proactive psychological check-ins and hybrid debriefing formats for staff represents a more structured and systematic approach to provider support than is typically described in existing models [6, 7]. These differences highlight the importance of contextual tailoring in PPC program development. While the integrative review offers a comprehensive checklist of potential components, our findings underscore that not all elements are equally relevant or necessary in every setting. Future PPC initiatives may benefit from a similar needs-based approach, ensuring that programs are both evidence-informed and responsive to local practice realities.

A key feature of our perinatal palliative care model is its deliberate balance between standardization and flexibility, allowing it to be both structured and responsive to individual family needs and institutional contexts. While standardized care pathways are essential for ensuring consistency, quality, and coordination across multidisciplinary teams, rigid protocols risk overlooking the nuanced preferences of families and the clinical judgment of care providers [21]. Our model addresses this tension by embedding flexibility within the standardized framework, for example, by including phrasing such as “to be offered if the parents wish” throughout the care pathway. This approach acknowledges that not all families will require the same type or intensity of support, and that their needs may evolve over time. Similarly, it respects the professional discretion of clinicians who must adapt care to the specific circumstances of each case. By incorporating optional components and decision points based on wishes of parents, the model fosters shared decision-making and supports individualized care within a cohesive structure.

Implementation of the PPC program is expected to face several challenges, as highlighted during the co-creation workshops. A key factor for success identified by participants was role clarity, particularly that of the care coordinator, whose presence is essential for ensuring continuity and communication across departments. However, the feasibility of consistently assigning such a role is threatened by existing workload pressures and staffing shortages. These structural barriers, including limited time and fragmented documentation systems, were repeatedly cited as major obstacles. Moreover, successful implementation will depend heavily on institutional and policy-level support, including leadership engagement, interdepartmental collaboration, and adequate resourcing in terms of staff, time, and funding. While many PPC-related practices already exist in hospitals, integrating them into a unified model requires careful contextual tailoring. This includes adapting existing templates and workflows, and encouraging professionals to work across traditional departmental boundaries. Such changes demand not only logistical adjustments but also a broader cultural and behavioral shift, which may take time to establish. To address these uncertainties, the program will be pilot tested to evaluate its feasibility and acceptability in actual perinatal practice. This pilot phase will allow for necessary modifications and contribute to addressing the current lack of rigorous testing in the PPC domain [6]. The pilot trial will be conducted in two tertiary perinatal centers in Flanders and will include 20 families (10 prenatal and 10 neonatal cases) and their healthcare providers. Using a pre-post design, we will assess parental satisfaction, short-term grief responses, and provider experiences through validated questionnaires and interviews. This will help identify which components of the PPC program are most impactful and feasible in routine clinical care.

The development of our PPC program followed a rigorous and time-intensive process, guided by the extended MRC framework by Bleijenberg [19]. This approach required sustained engagement with stakeholders across multiple phases, including problem identification, evidence synthesis, theory development, and iterative refinement. While this prolonged the development timeline, it allowed for a deeply grounded program that is both responsive to the needs of families and feasible within the current healthcare context. Compared to other studies using the MRC framework, our approach placed particular emphasis on co-production with end-users throughout all phases [22]. This included not only consultation but active involvement of bereaved parents and healthcare professionals in shaping the program’s structure and content. By embedding stakeholder perspectives from the outset and maintaining them throughout, our model is more likely to be contextually appropriate, acceptable, and sustainable in real-world perinatal care settings.

Involving bereaved parents and healthcare professionals in the development of our perinatal palliative care program was both essential and ethically complex. We aimed for co-productive involvement, including joint workshops where both groups could reflect on care practices and co-create program components. However, this approach raised significant concerns during ethical review, particularly regarding the emotional vulnerability of bereaved parents and the potential for hierarchical or emotionally charged dynamics when interacting with healthcare providers who may have been involved in their care [23, 24]. Ethics committees, often unfamiliar with research involving this population, tended to overestimate the risk of distress and undervalue the potential benefits of participation. Yet, literature suggests that sharing experiences can be meaningful and even healing for vulnerable groups [25], and our findings support this. In practice, the workshops fostered mutual learning: healthcare professionals gained insight into parental needs and perspectives, while parents appreciated the opportunity to contribute to future care improvements. To mitigate risks, we implemented safeguards including prior rapport-building of the researcher leading the workshops, transparent introductions of participants before the workshop, trained psychological facilitators, and optional emotional support afterwards when needed. These measures enabled respectful dialogue and constructive collaboration, demonstrating that with appropriate preparation, simultaneous involvement of parents and professionals is not only feasible but highly valuable for developing sensitive and contextually grounded programs. There were however less parents than healthcare providers present during the organized focus groups. The imbalance in parents versus healthcare providers stems from the large variation in care provider roles such as physicians, nurses and psychosocial support which needed to be present, and is similar to daily clinical practice where families are surrounded by a larger multidisciplinary healthcare team.

Conclusion

This study presents the development of an evidence-informed stakeholder-informed perinatal palliative care program. Through iterative integration of qualitative data, international literature, and stakeholder workshops, we developed a model that is both theoretically grounded and practically relevant. It comprises five core components, namely a fixed dedicated team, with formal PPC training, following a step-by-step PPC plan, with systematic psychological support for parents and healthcare providers, and systematic debriefings after every death. While initially tailored to the Belgian healthcare context, our model incorporates internationally recognized core principles of PPC and follows a structured development process that can guide adaptation in diverse international settings. In contrast to many existing descriptive PPC programs with limited evaluation, our method establishes a replicable pathway from needs assessment to prototyping and refinement, offering transferable insights for global PPC implementation. Implementation will require institutional commitment, adequate resourcing, and ongoing evaluation to support sustainable integration into perinatal care systems. The next step is to pilot test the program to assess its feasibility, acceptability, and contextual fit in clinical practice.
