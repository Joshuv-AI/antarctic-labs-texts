Protocol for CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing of a swine cell line via electroporation
Authors: Patricia Kiesler, Stella S Lee, Alexis L Norris, Mayumi F Miller, Carlo J Mercado, Adam L Moyer, Samantha Maragh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryGenome editing technology is being used in animals for a variety of purposes, including improvement of animal and public health outcomes. Characterization of genome editing reagents and anticipated genomic alterations is an essential step toward the development of an edited animal. Here, we present a protocol for genome editing in the swine testicular (ST) cell line. We describe steps for evaluating CRISPR-Cas9 complex functionality in vitro, delivering editing molecules into cells by transfection, and assessing target editing via Sanger sequencing.Subject areas: Cell-based Assays, CRISPR, Biotechnology and bioengineering

Before you begin

The CRISPR-Cas9 system1 has been adopted to engineer many types of animals, including pigs. Genome editing has been performed in swine to generate animals that can be used to model human disease, confer resistance to infectious diseases, and reduce immune rejection of organs and tissues transplanted in humans.2,3,4,5,6,7 However, the availability of resources for the characterization of CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing methods and reagents in swine cells is still limited. The ST cell line is a fibroblast-like cell line originally isolated from normal swine testis.8,9 These cells are routinely used by the scientific community10,11,12,13,14 due to their commercial availability and ease of culture, making them a suitable tool for the characterization of genome editing systems that target the swine genome. The protocol below describes a step-by-step method to edit the genome of ST cells, from the delivery of CRISPR-Cas9 via nucleofection to the characterization of editing outcomes generated at the target region using Sanger sequencing.

CRISPR-Cas9 is a ribonucleoprotein (RNP) enzyme, consisting of a Cas9 nuclease in complex with a guide RNA (gRNA), that is designed to bind and make a double strand break at a DNA target. The Cas9 protein can be delivered into cells in a variety of formats: DNA plasmid (can also encode the gRNA sequence), mRNA, or purified protein. The gRNA comprises two parts: the CRISPR RNA (crRNA) and the trans-activating CRISPR RNA (tracrRNA). The crRNA is the customizable portion that enables specificity because it contains the sequence, typically 17-20 nucleotides in length, that is complementary to the DNA target. Numerous web-based tools (such as those reviewed in ref. 15 and 16) with various features and scoring methods have been developed for the selection of this sequence, assisting users in designing effective gRNAs for their research. Of note, not all web-based tools allow the use of the swine genome (most recent build Feb. 2017 Sscrofa11.1/susScr11).

Once designed, gRNAs can be generated via in vitro transcription using a DNA template that includes a promoter and the desired guide sequence or purchased from a commercial source. Commercially synthesized gRNAs can be ordered as a single guide RNA (sgRNA) consisting of a single continuous RNA molecule comprising the crRNA and tracrRNA or as a dual system where the two parts are synthesized separately and then hybridized to make the gRNA. Furthermore, synthetic gRNAs can be made containing chemical modifications that increase their stability and protect them from exonucleases. This protocol focuses on the delivery of CRISPR-Cas9 as pre-formed RNP, in the format of purified Cas9 protein complexed with synthetic modified gRNA.

Prior to attempting genome editing of ST cells, we recommend performing in vitro cleavage (IVC) assays to evaluate the effectiveness of a gRNA design by measuring the efficiency with which a CRISPR-Cas9 RNP formed at a chosen Cas9 to gRNA molar ratio cuts DNA substrate outside of the cellular context. In general, the efficiency observed in IVC cannot be used to directly infer the editing activity expected at the target in cells. However, poor cleavage efficiency in IVC will likely not become high efficiency editing in cells and will inform the need to optimize the guide sequence or the RNP formation conditions.

Evaluation of gRNA editing efficiency in cells involves the amplification and sequencing of the target of interest before and after editing takes place. When performing Sanger sequencing, a primer design tool such as Primer3 can be used to design primer sequences for target region amplification, bearing in mind that the primer closest to the target sequence should be at least ∼160 nucleotides away to enable identification of edits. Smaller distances will likely produce Sanger sequencing traces that exclude the target sequence.

To demonstrate the use of this protocol, we employ two previously published gRNA sequences that target swine safe harbor loci. gRNA1 described as sgRNA91 in Xie et al.17 targets the Rosa26 safe harbor locus (Target1, depicted in Figure 1) while gRNA2 described as Cas9-H11-g1 in Ruan et al.18 targets the H11 safe harbor locus (Target2). This protocol, however, can be extended for use with other gRNAs that target the swine genome by designing guide sequences specific to the gene/locus of interest as well as other swine cell lines by optimizing nucleofection conditions.

Expected outcomes

The procedures described in this protocol enable editing at a target of interest in ST cells and provide a fast and cost-effective method for assessing editing of the target via Sanger sequencing. To illustrate these procedures, we have evaluated gRNAs that target two separate swine genomic loci. Both gRNAs are found to be functional in vitro (Figure 2) and highly effective in editing their cellular targets (Figure 5). Successful editing using these example gRNAs demonstrates that nucleofection is a suitable method to deliver CRISPR-Cas9 editing reagents into ST cells. Moreover, preliminary results using swine LLC-PK1 cells (data not shown) suggest that the transfection steps described herein can be applied to other cell lines when nucleofection parameters (i.e., pulse code and solution) are optimized specifically for the cells of interest and result in a suitable transfection efficiency.

Limitations

Nucleofection technology is an electroporation method that can be used to transfect a wide selection of cell types. Nucleofection is frequently used to deliver CRISPR-Cas9 editing reagents into mammalian cells including swine cells.20,21 However, a key limitation of this technology is that it requires specialized equipment and reagents. Furthermore, the exact composition of these reagents and the electroporation parameters associated with each Nucleofector pulse code including the magnitude and length of the electrical pulse constitute information that is not available.

Sanger sequencing was originally designed to evaluate a single sequence rather than a mixture of sequences, which is the expected outcome in an edited sample. Thus, the assessment of editing efficiency and identification of edits resulting from genome editing using Sanger sequencing relies on the use of software to deconvolute sequence data. Although various sequence deconvolution algorithms have been developed and shown to yield comparable results to targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS),19,22,23,24 NGS is the recommended approach to verify genome editing outcomes when high confidence molecule-level resolution and higher sensitivity are needed.
