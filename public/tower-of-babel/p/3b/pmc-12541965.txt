The hidden driver of osteoporosis: mitochondria-dominated inflammation
Authors: Binghan Yan, Wenhan Ju, Haipeng Xue, Zhanwang Xu, Daodi Qiu, Guoqing Tan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMitochondria are powerful multifunctional intracellular organelles involved in a wide array of cellular processes. Emerging evidence has highlighted mitochondria as pivotal activators of innate immunity and key participants in inflammatory responses, including in sterile inflammatory diseases, beyond their well-established role as the “powerhouse” of cells. Osteoporosis (OP) is a metabolic disorder characterized by chronic low-grade inflammation, suggesting potential therapeutic value for OP treatment by targeting mitochondria-driven inflammatory pathways. Current research on mitochondrial involvement in OP primarily focuses on its functions in energy metabolism, calcium signaling transduction, oxidative stress, and cell death. However, it remains elusive regarding the precise relationship between mitochondria-mediated inflammatory mechanisms and OP pathogenesis. Accordingly, this review aims to synthesize recent advancement in understanding mitochondria-mediated inflammatory mechanisms and their implications in OP development, to offer insights that may inform future mechanistic investigations and therapeutic strategy development.Keywords: Osteoporosis, Mitochondria, Inflammation, mtDAMPs, mtDNA

Introduction

The skeletal system, as a dynamic biological structure, continuously adapts to mechanical stimulation, repairs micro-injuries and maintains calcium homeostasis through a continuous remodeling process [1]. This complicated biological process is primarily modulated by the basic multicellular unit, a transient functional complex comprising osteoclasts (OCs) responsible for bone resorption and osteoblasts (OBs) derived from mesenchymal stem cells. These cell populations, featured by spatiotemporal synchronization, coordinate the bone resorption-formation coupled bone remodeling process through a sophisticated intercellular communication mechanism [2]. In the presence of unbalanced coupling mechanism described above, a pathological disorder, i.e., osteoporosis (OP), will be triggered, a common deterioration of bone mass and structure characterized by decreased bone mass, microstructural destruction and decreased biomechanical properties, accompanied by significantly increased risk of fractures [3].

OP has been accepted to be one of the major diseases threatening the health of the aging population worldwide. Among individuals aged 50–85 years, its prevalence reached as high as 21.7%, as pooled by a meta-analysis encompassing 40 studies [4]. Alarmingly, approximately 8.9 million fractures globally each year are attributed to OP, equivalent to one osteoporotic fracture occurring every three seconds [5]. Populations suffering from OP-related fractures may experience compromised quality of life, as well as increased hospitalization, disability, and mortality [6]. Beyond fractures, OP may result in escalated risk of hospitalization due to other complications. According to existing epidemiological data, OP-related fractures will continue to impose a growing burden on the global medical system with the aging of the population [7]. Current clinical interventions mainly use anti-resorptive agents (e.g., bisphosphonates) and anabolic agents (e.g., teriparatide). Despite their effects on effectively increasing bone density, they also exhibit obvious limitations such as insufficient efficiency in preventing non-vertebral fractures, high treatment costs, and post-withdrawal rebound bone metabolism [8]. Noticeably, there is currently no clear application standard for the sequential treatment of drugs with different mechanisms [9]. Therefore, it highlights the priority of developing novel and highly effective anti-OP drugs, urgently requiring in-depth exploration of the pathogenesis of OP.

Beyond a well-established role as a “powerhouse” of cells, mitochondria also serve as the core regulators of inflammatory signals [10]. The synergistic effect of mitochondrial dysfunction and chronic inflammation has been reported to play a key role during aging [11]. At present, there is insufficient investigation on the dynamic connection of these two in aging-related diseases in OP, even with the clarification of their independent mechanisms. In the field of osteoimmunology, there is a so-called “inflammatory accident” when the physiological inflammatory response unexpectedly transforms into pathological bone loss [12, 13]. Mitochondrial dysfunction, as a potential driver of inflammatory activation, has attracted increasing attention in the development of OP. With respect to the above, this review intends to decipher the core mechanisms of the interaction between mitochondria and inflammation, including the release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) to trigger innate immune responses, the accumulation of mitochondrial damage-associated molecular patterns (mtDAMPs), the imbalance of mitochondrial dynamics, the dysregulation of mitophagy and the mitochondrial transfer. Based on the complex network relationship between these mechanisms, this study further discusses the “mitochondrial-inflammatory axis” as a potential driver of OP, so as to offer a theoretical reference for the development of new therapeutic strategies.

Inflammatory regulatory mechanisms in OP

Inflammation is a protective immune response of the host to infection, injury or toxic stimulation. Its mechanism of initiation begins with the specific recognition of exogenous pathogen-associated molecular patterns or endogenous damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) [14]. As a common bone disorder with complicated pathogenesis, OP is profoundly influenced by inflammatory responses. In 2000, Arron and Choi proposed for the first time the concept of “osteoimmunology”, highlighting an intimate interplay between the immune system and bone metabolism [15]. Physiologically, immune cells can facilitate the regulation of the expression of RANKL, OPG, and key osteogenic transcription factors by secreting appropriate levels of pro- and anti-inflammatory factors, thereby maintaining a dynamic balance between bone resorption and formation. In addition, through the secretion of cytokines that promote matrix deposition and OBs function, T cells are positive contributors to bone healing and mineralization [16, 17].

Pathologically, however, there is usually a disruption of this finely tuned regulatory network. For instance, estrogen deficiency, one of the major risk factors for OP, promotes the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6, IL-17) by T cells, B cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells, thereby establishing a chronic inflammatory microenvironment [18]. In addition, estrogen deficiency may also induce the pyroptosis of OBs and impair osteogenic differentiation by activating the NLRP3 inflammasome in osteocytes [19].

Critically, inflammatory mediators can exert inhibitory effects on osteogenesis. To be specific, TNF-α can suppress autophagy in OBs via inhibiting the activation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, impeding their differentiation, while also targeting the transcription factor Osterix to block the maturation of OBs [20, 21]. IL-6 exerts dual roles in bone metabolism. Firstly, it can interfere with the crosstalk between OBs and OCs, attenuating bone formation, and exacerbate bone loss in the context of lipid metabolic disturbances, possibly via activating the SHP2/MEK2/ERK and SHP2/PI3K/Akt2 pathways [22]; while IL-6 can also promote osteogenic differentiation under specific signaling conditions such as JAK/STAT3, suggesting its highly dependent on the microenvironment and signaling balance [23].

Conversely, inflammation is a strong promoter of osteoclastogenesis. Activated T cells can secrete IFN-γ and TNF-α to strengthen the differentiation of OCs in a RANKL-dependent manner [24]. Th17 cells, through IL-17 production, further facilitate osteoclastogenesis and bone erosion [25]. Conversely, anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-4 and IL-13) may suppress the formation of OCs and enhance bone protection by downregulating RANKL and RANK expression as well as upregulating OPG via a STAT6-dependent pathway [26]. Activated γδ T cells also inhibit osteoclastogenesis through the secretion of IFN-γ [27]. Moreover, IFN-γ has also been reported to suppress the formation of OCs by suppressing NFATc1 expression as well as blocking nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) and JNK activation [28]. In animal models, IL-1RI deficiency significantly weakened the count of OCs in both trabecular and cortical bone, urther suggesting an important role of inflammatory mediators in osteoclastogenesis [29]. Importantly, Treg cells are generally regarded as bone-protective, with CD8⁺ Tregs shown to suppress the actin ring formation of OCs via the IFN-γ-TRAF6 axis, thereby limiting the activity of OCs [30].

Antiresorptive and osteogenic drugs, two therapeutic agents currently used clinically with certain efficacy in improving bone mass, fail to adequately counteract inflammation-driven bone loss. Thus, in order to realize effective OP management, one feasible solution may be the application of therapeutic strategies targeting inflammation and its signaling pathways. Notably, mitochondria can release ROS and mtDNA as DAMPs under stress conditions, directly triggering inflammatory responses and amplifying inflammatory signaling in bone and immune cells. In this regard, mitochondrial dysfunction may represent a pivotal source of inflammation in OP and offer a novel therapeutic target.

Mitochondria as key drivers of inflammatory signals

mtDAMP - a “amplifier” of inflammatory signals

Among them, mitochondrial components [e.g., mtDNA, reactive oxygen species (ROS), ATP and cardiolipin] can be classified as DAMPs due to their molecular characteristics, commonly known as mtDAMPs, which is also the core mechanism of mitochondria as “amplifiers” of inflammatory signals [31]. This characteristic stems from the evolutionary origin of mitochondria. As the evolutionary remnant of As evolutionary remnants of Alphaproteobacteria, mitochondria retain molecular features that are highly similar to those of bacterial pathogens, endowing them with an inherent potential to activate PRRs [32]. Under physiological conditions, the mitochondrial inner membrane (MIM) and the outer membrane (MOM) form a double membrane barrier, realizing strict compartmentalization that can prevent mtDAMPs from interacting with homologous PRRs within the cytoplasm [33]. However, under the stimulation of oxidative stress or death signals, the pro-apoptotic protein BAX/BAK1-mediated MOM permeabilization (MOMP) and the permeability transition pore complex-caused membrane permeability transition (MPT) may lead to the collapse of the double membrane system, in turn triggering mitochondrial matrix swelling and MOM rupture, eventually allowing mtDAMPs to break through the compartment barrier and enter the cytoplasmic matrix [34]. Subsequently, these “danger signals” located abnormally may further activate corresponding PRR pathway and induce a cascade inflammatory response. For example, ATP, after being released into the extracellular environment, may play the role of DAMP, and activate the purinergic signaling pathway through the P2RY2/P2RX7 receptor on the surface of myeloid cells, forming a cascade amplification effect [35, 36]. Cytochrome C in the mitochondrial membrane space may enter the cytoplasm to cause the assembly of the apoptotic body, while components such as naked mtDNA directly activate the cytoplasmic PRR pathway [37, 38]. It has been documented previously that the imbalance of mitochondrial quality control mechanisms is essential in the occurrence and development of OP. The accumulation and release of mtDAMPs may act as an important trigger of inflammatory activation, participating in the initiation of immune responses in the bone microenvironment and the destruction of bone homeostasis [39].

Inflammatory activation of mtDNA

Release of mtDNA

Since its discovery, mtDNA, as the autonomous genetic material of mitochondria, has continuously refreshed the academic community’s understanding of the genetic system of eukaryotic organisms. The biological significance of mtDNA continues to expand from the unique circular genome structure and its maternal inheritance rules to the degenerative diseases and aging phenotypes caused by tissue-specific mutations. The new chapter of mtDNA research reveals its more complicated physiological and pathological functions. Specifically, under physiological conditions, it is usually confined to mitochondria and mainly participates in encoding proteins that maintain mitochondrial function [40]. However, mtDNA can be released extracellularly under various cellular stress conditions. The specific conditions may include gene replication errors, dysfunction of the oxidative phosphorylation system (OXPHOS) (often accompanied by excessive ROS production), metabolic disorders such as cholesterol or pyrimidine imbalance, mitophagy defects, and endoplasmic reticulum stress. In these contexts, the released mtDNA may act as a strong innate immune activator (mtDAMP) to induce innate immune and inflammatory responses [41].

Release of mtDNA from the mitochondrial matrix to the cytoplasm depends on the crossing of the dual physical barriers of MIM and MOM. Noticeably, the proposed release pathway is diverse based on current research. In the apoptosis-related pathway, there may be activation of the pro-apoptotic proteins BAX and BAK after receiving apoptosis signals, boosting the formation of a large transmembrane pore in the MIM-MOM contact area following the oligomerization of the two factors, leading to MOMP [42]. Afterward, cytochrome C may be released that may cause damage to the mitochondrial respiratory chain and impair the ability of mitochondria to maintain MIM ionic balance. Simultaneously, when the pores of BAX and BAK pores gradually widen and expand to a sufficient width, the complete nucleolar structure carrying mtDNA is allowed to be released into the cytoplasm [43]. It has been revealed that the absolute concentrations of BAX and BAK as well as the ratio of the two factors might have a direct impact on the pore expansion rate, thereby determining the kinetic characteristics of mtDNA release [44]. Critically, cytoplasmic leakage of mtDNA, despite initial observation in a caspase-independent apoptosis model, is not limited to apoptotic cells, but is ubiquitous under various stress conditions [45]. For example, Sam50 deficiency or depletion can disrupt the integrity of mitochondrial membrane, triggering membrane remodeling, mtDNA aggregation and release [46].

The coordinated regulatory mechanism of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) on the MIM and the voltage-dependent anion channel (VDAC) on the MOM also constitutes an important pathway for mtDNA release. mPTP has been recognized to be a transmembrane protein complex on the MIM, and its activation is usually triggered by a variety of pathological stimuli (e.g., matrix calcium ion overload and oxidative stress). Different from the BAX/BAK-mediated apoptotic release pathway, mPTP typically exhibit unique biological functions under non-apoptotic stress conditions, rather than participating in the outflow of mtDNA during apoptosis. For example, radiation exposure can induce a transient opening of mPTP, prompting mitochondria to release fragmented mtDNA [47]. Under oxidative stress conditions, oxidized mtDNA fragments may be released into the cytoplasm through the mPTP channel [48]. Importantly, instead of a complete “nucleoid form”, mtDNA released by this pathway generally presents a characteristic fragmentation pattern, which has an intimate association with the molecular sieving effect of the mPTP channel (pore size: <1.5 kDa) [49]. The oligomerization event of VDAC in the MOM plays a key regulatory role in this process. For instance, VDAC 1 and VDAC3 can form oligomers, and the N-terminal domain of VDAC is involved in binding to negatively charged mtDNA, usually through its amino acid residues (e.g., arginine and lysine), which are positively charged that can interact with the negative charge of mtDNA. This interaction can both stabilize the conformation of VDAC oligomers, and also promote mtDNA transport from the membrane gap to the cytoplasm by reducing pore activation [50]. Critically, the opening of mPTP may increase the concentration of mtDNA in the membrane gap, thereby enhancing its binding with VDAC, and finally completing the release of mtDNA through the formation of transmembrane pores via VDAC.

The Gasdermin protein family is the core effector molecule that mediates pyroptosis, which is activated by caspase-dependent proteolysis [51]. It currently remains controversial as to whether the Gasdermin pore can perforate across double membranes [52]. However, in a latest research, the N-terminal pore-forming Gasdermin D fragment can specifically bind to mitochondrial cardiolipin, destroy MIM and MOM, and release mtDNA [51]. In addition, mitophagy defects hinder the clearance of damaged mitochondria and the effective degradation of their DNA, leading to the accumulation of leaky mitochondria and intracellular unseparated nucleoids, ultimately leading to the abnormal release of mtDNA into the cytoplasm or systemic circulation [53].

mtDNA activates cyclic guanosine monophosphate-adenylate synthetase (cGAS)-STING

cGAS acts as a cytoplasmic DNA sensor. In the presence of mtDNA, cGAS can bind to mtDNA and trigger the conversion of ATP and GTP into c-cyclic guanosine monophosphate-adenosine (cGAMP), a second messenger enabling the initiation of an inflammatory response through STING [54]. cGAMP can bind to STING, triggering STING conformational rearrangement, dimerization, and translocation from the endoplasmic reticulum to the ER-Golgi intermediate compartment (ERGIC) and the Golgi apparatus (Golgi) [55]. Subsequently, STING dimers can recruit and activate TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1), while directly phosphorylating interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) to promote its dimerization [56]. The activation of STING1 can also recruit NF-κB-inducing kinase and heterotrimeric IκB kinase (IKK), thus promoting non-classical and classical NF-κB signaling, respectively [38–40]. IRF3 and NF-κB may further translocate to the nucleus, inducing the transcriptional expression of pro-inflammatory factors such as type I interferon (IFN-I), tumor necrosis factor (TNF) and interleukin (IL)−6, thereby initiating a systemic immune response [57].

mtDNA activates NLRP3 inflammasome

The NLRP3 inflammasome is a core regulatory element of the innate immune system, exhibiting unique signal integration characteristics in its activation mechanism. The protein forms a multimeric complex through oligomerization of the NACHT domain, recruits the ASC adaptor protein and activates caspase-1, and then cleaves pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 precursors to IL-1β and IL-18 [58]. Unlike typical inflammasomes, NLPR3 can be assembled under the drive of various mitochondrial stress signals, among which oxidatively damaged mtDNA (ox-mtDNA) is confirmed as a core molecular trigger [59]. In the presence of functional deficiency of the 8-oxoguanine (8-oxoG) DNA glycosylase 1 (OGG1), it is impossible to realize effective repair of the oxidative damage sites such as 8-oxoguanine in mtDNA, resulting in abnormally accumulated ox-mtDNA within the cytoplasm, significantly enhancing the activation efficiency of the NLRP3 inflammasome [60]. This process can be partially reversed by targeting and enhancing the expression of mitochondrial OGG1, further validating ox-mtDNA as a core trigger [61]. VDAC has been suggested to play an important role in activating NLRP3 inflammasomes, which can form transmembrane pores that enable mtDNA fragments to flow out through oligomerization, allowing mtDNA to leave the mitochondria. Besides, serving as a molecular bridge, it can directly connect VDAC oligomers and NLRP3 aggregation, or mediate VDAC-dependent activation of NLRP3 through mtDNA, ultimately initiating inflammasome assembly [62].

mtDNA activates other inflammatory pathways

In addition to triggering immune responses through the NLRP3 inflammasome, mtDNA, a DAMP, can also regulate the activity of other inflammasomes through multiple mechanisms. Similar to NLRP3, absent in melanoma 2 (AIM2) is a PRR that can specifically recognize cytoplasmic double-stranded DNA (dsDNA). It can bind to abnormally released mtDNA, recruit ASC and activate caspase-1, thereby promoting IL-1β and IL-18 maturation [63]. In addition, NLRC4, which belongs to the NOD-like receptor (NLR) family like NLRP3, can be synergistically activated by ox-mtDNA and ROS released by mitochondrial oxidative stress in the Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection model, which would further activate inflammasomes [64]. Other mtdamps and inflammatory response activation

Generally, mitochondrial dysfunction-induced release of mtDAMPs involves both the abnormal outflow of mtDNA, and mtDNA breakage in MOMP mediated by BAX and BAK. These nucleic acid fragments exert their effects through the retinoic acid-inducible gene I protein (RIG-I)/melanoma differentiation-associated protein 5 (MDA5)/mitochondrial antiviral signaling protein (MAVS) cascade reaction [65, 66]. The specific mechanism involves BAX/BAK-mediated release of Era-like 12 S mitochondrial rRNA chaperone 1 (ERAL1), a protein that may maintain the stability of MAVS on the mitochondrial surface. Its key role has been confirmed by a high mortality rate of ERAL1-deficient mice in RNA virus infection. Mechanistically, ERAL1 could be translocated to the cytoplasm through the BAX/BAK channel during viral infection, and enhance immune recognition by catalyzing the K63 ubiquitination modification of RIG-I/MDA5 and promoting the assembly of MAVS polymers [67].

Furthermore, MAVS is an adaptor protein of the outer mitochondrial pattern. The dysfunction of MAVS, acting as the core hub of the innate immune response, may weaken the host’s defense against RNA viruses [68]. The key role of MAVS begins with the interaction of its N-terminal CARD domain with the upstream PRR RIG-I/MDA5, further triggering supramolecular self-assembly on the mitochondrial membrane and amplifying antiviral signals [69]. Activated MAVS complex may result in the activation of the TBK1-IRF3 pathway and guide the transcription of IFN-β and ISGs [70]. Actually, MAVS is essential for the localization of NLRP3 to the mitochondrial membrane, suggesting that MAVS can also regulate innate immunity and inflammatory pathways, in addition to involving in antiviral defense [55, 56].

Mitochondrial ROS and mtDNA can also synergistically activate NLRP3 inflammasomes to stimulate IL-1β/IL-18 maturation and secretion [71, 72], exhibiting intimate association with the imbalance of ATP homeostasis caused by electron transport chain (ETC) dysfunction. Beyond modulating ATP availability by affecting phosphocreatine (PCr) levels, ETC, in the presence of functional defects, can also enhance inflammatory responses independently of the ROS production pathway [73]. Dynamic changes of mitochondria and activation of inflammatory response

Inflammatory regulation of mitochondrial dynamics

Rather than an accidental event, the release of mtDAMP may be attributed primarily to the collapse of the dynamic regulatory network of mitochondria [74, 75]. Organisms have evolved a multi-level monitoring system, enabling real-time monitoring and responding to mitochondrial damage signals to dynamically optimize the mitochondrial network architecture, thereby effectively preventing mitochondrial dysfunction. Mitochondria are highly dynamic organelles, which is inseparable from the role of mitochondrial dynamics. Given that mitochondria undergo continuous fusion and fission processes in a dynamic balance, mitochondrial dynamics can precisely regulate the morphology and functional integrity of the mitochondrial network. Specifically, fission can remove damaged mitochondria and initiates apoptosis when cells are stressed to achieve quality control; while fusion can mix mitochondrial contents to maintain their functional stability [76]. This dynamic balance is essential for maintaining mitochondrial function and determining cell fate, while facilitating the prevention of abnormal mtDAMP leakage [77]. In the context of aging, oxidative damage or mtDNA mutations, mitochondrial dynamics can enhance the adaptability by regulating morphology, transport proteins and separating damaged components, thereby improving the efficiency of OXPHOS [78]. This plasticity enables mitochondria to flexibly respond to changes in cellular energy needs. However and critically, its dysfunction may be related to the development of various diseases, including OP.

Mitochondria may integrate functional mitochondria with damaged components through fusion under mild to moderate stress conditions. It may facilitate the maintenance of the stability of mitochondrial membrane potential, promote the preservation of high-quality mtDNA and the transmission of genetic information, while improving the efficiency of ATP synthesis and inhibiting the excessive generation of ROS, eventually alleviating cellular stress responses effectively [79]. This process depends on the coordinated regulation of three core GTPases, where MFN1 and MFN2 mediate MOM fusion, and OPA1 dominates MIM fusion. It has been revealed that MFN1 has higher GTP hydrolysis activity, while MFN2 has a tighter GTPase binding interface than MFN1, thereby promoting more efficient fusion [80]. MOM fusion depends on the cooperation of MFN1 and MFN2, despite their different functions, and corresponding mechanism involves the trans-interaction of the HR2 domain to form a transmembrane bridge and GTP hydrolysis-driven remodeling of membrane conformation [81]. In addition, through the dynamic regulation of its long and short isoforms, OPA1 exert important roles in MIM fusion, Cristae structure maintenance and ROS regulation, and the related functions are finely regulated by proteases such as YME1L and OMA1 [82, 83]. Mitochondrial fission is a dynamic process in which a single mitochondrion splits into two smaller organelles. Under pathological stress conditions, excessive fission may suggest mitochondrial dysfunction and cell death, and fine-tuning this process is crucial for cellular homeostasis [84]. Notably, this process is tightly coupled to cell cycle, which can be explained by the phosphorylation modification of the fission GTPase DRP1 Ser616 site by the G2/M phase checkpoint to ensure equal distribution of mitochondria to daughter cells during mitosis [85, 86]. Mitochondrial fission is mainly driven by DRP1, and its recruitment to MOM relies mainly on the interaction of receptor proteins such as mitochondrial fission factor (MFF), mitochondrial dynamics proteins 49 and 51 (MID49 and MID51), and mitochondrial fission 1 (FIS1) [86, 87]. DRP1 can polymerize to form a contractile spiral structure during fission, which may break the membrane structure to promote the generation of new mitochondria [88, 89]. However, abnormally activated DRP1 can induce mitochondrial fragmentation to trigger mtDNA leakage. As a result, it can further induce the activation of nucleic acid sensing pathways such as cGAS-STING, further upregulating the expression of inflammation-related factors, and initiating or exacerbating the inflammatory cascade [90]. (Fig. 3B)

Noticeably, mitochondrial dynamics, rather than subjected to regulation in a single direction, necessitates a dynamic balance regarding its fusion and fission processes to ensure the integrity of mitochondrial function and the moderate regulation of inflammatory response [78]. Recently, mitochondrial fission process 1, a protein promoting mitochondrial fusion and inhibiting mitochondrial fission, has been discovered to mediate mitochondrial quality control, and exhibits an intimate association with the maintenance of mtDNA homeostasis [77]. Moreover, mitochondrial fission has been validated to release mtDNA, activate the NF-κB signaling pathway through TLR9, and promote the biosynthesis of chemokines such as CCL2 subsequently. In addition to driving the directional migration of macrophages, this molecular event can also induce their differentiation into a pro-inflammatory phenotype, ultimately forming a persistent inflammatory process [91]. Collectively, the aforementioned findings provide a new perspective for our understanding of the mechanisms of the function and structure of mitochondria in responding to inflammatory responses intracellularly and reveal the complex relationship of mitochondrial dynamics with cell homeostasis and immune response. The double-edged sword effect of mitophagy in regulating inflammation

As a selective autophagy, mitophagy functions primarily to dynamically regulate mitochondrial quality and maintain the homeostasis of cellular energy metabolism by removing damaged mitochondria. Classical autophagy includes five steps of initiation, nucleation, elongation, maturation, and degradation [92]. Supported by the fundamental framework of classical autophagy, mitophagy involves the coordination of various autophagy-related proteins (ATG), and forms a molecular bridge between the specific receptor PTEN-induced kinase 1 (PINK1)/Parkin on the mitochondrial membrane and the autophagosome marker protein LC3, thereby achieving accurate identification and selective recruitment of damaged mitochondria [93]. At the molecular level, the initiation of mitophagy begins with the loss of mitochondrial membrane potential. In case of damage to mitochondria, PINK1 stabilizes on the MOM and undergoes autophosphorylation, further recruiting the E3 ubiquitin ligase Parkin to the mitochondrial surface [94]. Subsequently, Parkin may catalyze the ubiquitination of MOM proteins to form a ubiquitin chain signaling platform. This platform, acting as a molecular “eat-me signal”, can be specifically recognized by selective autophagy receptors (e.g., OPTN, NDP52, and TAX1BP1). It then binds to LC3 through the LC3 interaction region (LIR), and mediates the selective encapsulation of damaged mitochondria, under the synergistic action of the ATG family, by the expanding autophagosome membrane [76–78]. Mitophagy is thought to be an important intracellular anti-inflammatory mechanism. It can inhibit the activation of inflammasomes (e.g., NLRP3 and AIM2) by removing damaged mitochondria [95, 96]. Notably, PRKN-dependent mitophagy can also prevent mtDAMPs from being packaged into mitochondria-derived vesicles (MDVs) and released as part of mitochondrial quality control, which may avoid the propagation of inflammatory signals to neighboring cells, which would otherwise drive inflammatory responses [97, 98]. Furthermore, the anti-inflammatory effect of mitophagy can be realized by suppressing excessive IL-1β production, which had been strongly supported by a research conducted by Zhong et al. They found that NF-κB could induce delayed accumulation of autophagy receptor p62/SQSTM1 to activate mitophagy and remove damaged mitochondria, thereby inhibiting the abnormal activation of NLRP3 inflammasomes and limiting IL-1β release [99]. MOMP may be triggered after the damage of mitochondria, resulting in the release of mtDNA into the cytoplasm, followed by activation of the cGAS-STING signaling pathway. Fortunately, these damaged mitochondria can be effective cleared by the PINK1 and Parkin-dependent mitophagy mechanism, thereby inhibiting the inflammatory response [100]. Notably, when encountering mtDNA leakage, mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM) may interact with LC3 through the autophagy pathway to assist in the clearance of free mtDNA, eventually benefiting the control of inflammation [101]. Similar to the double-edged sword effect of autophagy in various diseases, mitophagy also exhibit a bidirectional effect in regulating inflammation. As evidenced by prior research, the activation of autophagy can also promote the release of mtDNA, which can be further recognized and bind to Toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9). TLR9 can then interact with myeloid differentiation primary response protein 88 (MyD88) through the downstream TLR9/MyD88 signaling pathway, activate NF-κB, and induce the transcription and translation of pro-inflammatory factors. Subsequently, the released pro-inflammatory cytokines may promote the formation of a cascade amplification effect of inflammatory cytokines, drive inflammatory responses and cell damage, and ultimately aggravate cyclic stretching-induced lung epithelial cell damage and inflammatory responses [102]. Altogether, maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis is crucial for immune metabolic balance. Disordered mitochondria, as mtDAMPs, can trigger the release of a series of pro-inflammatory mediators to induce excessive inflammatory responses. In contrast, mitophagy—a tightly regulated mitochondrial quality control mechanism—plays a pivotal role in ensuring appropriate innate immune responses by balancing endogenous and exogenous inflammatory stimuli. (Fig. 4B)

Normally, mitochondrial fission is a prelude to autophagy, which can recruit Parkin after PINK1 accumulation by separating out damaged fragments with lower membrane potential [103, 104]. Parkin can further ubiquitinate MFN1/2 to directly suppress fusion and promote the removal of damaged fragments through mitophagy [105]. Larger mitochondrial structures can decrease the probability of being recognized and encapsulated by autophagy, and hence excessive mitochondrial fusion may inhibit autophagy [106]. Mitophagy and fusion/fission are both part of mitochondrial dynamic changes, which work jointly to maintain mitochondrial quality under synergistic effects. Disruption in this dynamic balance may induce progressive decline of mitochondrial function, which is closely related to the development of chronic inflammation. Obviously, the imbalance of mitochondrial dynamic changes may be a key contributor to “inflammatory accidents” in OP.

Mitochondrial transfer and inflammatory response activation

For a long time, people believed that mitochondria were confined to the cytoplasm. Actually, mitochondrial transfer is a novel and intriguing mechanism of intercellular communication. Mitochondria often undergo reprogramming and intracellular movement to participate in maintain tissue homeostasis, respond to stress injury, and regulate immune responses [107]. A majority of studies have illustrated mitochondria transfer through tunneling nanotubes (TNT), microvesicles (MV) carrier free transfer mode, or synaptic contact [108]. Among these, TNT remains the most typical channel, which is a tubular structure formed by the extension of the plasma membrane with a diameter of 50 − 1,500 nm and a length of 50–500 μm. By establishing a persistent cytoplasmic bridge, the proposed type of dynamically connected membrane topology can achieve directional transport of mitochondria between donor and recipient cells [109]. The p53 signaling axis plays a core regulatory role in the formation of TNT. For cells under stress stimulation, p53 is activated and initiates TNT biogenesis through a dual molecular mechanism. First, the EGFR-mediated Akt/PI3K/mTOR signaling cascade is involved to upregulate the expression of M-Sec (TNFαip2) [110], which can further cooperate with the Ral-extracellular complex to promote actin microfilament reorganization and drive TNT formation on the cell membrane [111]. Second, the p53-dependent oxidative stress response continuously amplifies the signal intensity through the ROS-p53 positive feedback loop to power TNT extension continuously [112, 113]. Notably, p53 can also activate caspase-3, which can specifically cleave S100A4 (a member of the S100 calcium-binding protein family), forming a transcellular S100A4 concentration gradient. This chemotactic gradient can facilitate a directed extension of TNTs from S100A4 low-expression initiating cells to high-expression target cells, thereby navigating mitochondrial transport pathways precisely [114]. Microvesicle carriers, a heterogeneous group of cell-derived vesicular structures, can encapsulate intact mitochondria to complete transmembrane transport. Besides preserving mitochondrial structural integrity, this membrane-encapsulation mechanism can also enable precise delivery of contents through membrane fusion events. Free mitochondria can also be extruded or internalized independently of carriers, providing an alternative pathway for emergency mitochondrial replenishment [115, 116]. In addition, osteocytes, with their unique cellular characteristics, can boost the formation of intrinsic dendritic synaptic connections through dendritic membrane protrusions generated during differentiation, facilitating the establishment of channels for mitochondrial transfer. Evidently, mitochondrial transfer exists within the bone microenvironment to modulate its homeostasis [117]. (Fig. 5A)

The “mitochondrial-inflammatory axis” in OP

The importance of mitochondria in bone physiology

Beyond the central hub of cellular energy metabolism and signaling integration, mitochondria are also highly dynamic organelles, exhibiting rod-like or spherical morphologies with marked heterogeneity across species and cellular states. In bone tissue, OBs, OCs, and osteocytes sustain their distinct metabolic and biological properties relying on mitochondrial function.

During differentiation and extracellular matrix synthesis, OBs have exceptionally high energy demands, with their metabolism depending primarily on aerobic glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation [120]. In OBs, mitochondria are widely distributed throughout the cytoplasm, with a characteristic double-membrane ring-like structure, and are often embedded within the rough endoplasmic reticulum [121]. During differentiation and matrix production, mitochondria also participate in calcium homeostasis and intracellular signaling, in addition to providing ATP to fuel osteogenic activities [122].

As the only bone-resorbing cells, OCs are typical energy-intensive cells. Oxidative phosphorylation is also indispensable for sustaining their resorptive activity, even though glycolysis serves as their primary energy source [123]. According to existing research, in mature OCs, mitochondria display significantly enlarged volumes, more complicated cristae structures, and extensive tubular networks, which may hence meet the local and transient high metabolic demands during bone resorption [124]. However, elevated metabolic activity of OCs is usually accompanied by increased generation of ROS, which may accelerate bone resorption and contribute to OP progression ultimately [125].

Osteocytes are long-lived cells embedded within the mineralized matrix, accounting for approximately 90–95% of all bone cells in adults [126]. Abundant mitochondria constitutes an important premise for their function as mechanosensors and mediators of intercellular communication through dendritic networks. During the transition from OBs to osteocytes, there may be a progressive increase in the content of mitochondria to facilitate their adaption to a hypoxic and nutrient-limited microenvironment. Moreover, this transition is accompanied by a metabolic shift from glycolysis to more efficient mitochondrial oxidative pathways, thereby maintaining long-term survival and function of osteocytes [127].

In the context of OP, there is a lack of systematic quantification of the “abundance-function” profile of mitochondria in bone-lineage cells, hampering a comprehensive understanding of metabolic dysregulation in this disease. Despite differences in metabolic characteristics and functional requirements, OBs, OCs, and osteocytes all critically depend on mitochondria for energy supply, signaling transduction, and redox homeostasis. Importantly, in addition to disrupting skeletal homeostasis, mitochondrial dysfunction may also exacerbate bone loss in OP by activating inflammatory pathways.

mtDAMPs and inflammation exacerbation in OP

mtDNA activates the cGAS-STING pathway to trigger sterile inflammation in OP

Under normal conditions, the skeletal system maintains homeostasis depending on the balance between bone mass and structural characteristics specifically. This physiological process is controlled primarily by the continuous metabolic activity of bone cells, and is modulated by neural regulation, vascular supply, endocrine hormones, and growth factors [128]. Bone tissue can establish a dynamic remodeling equilibrium through a coordinated regulation of these factors. However, disruption of this balance may be a culprit for the occurrence of OP as it may trigger bone remodeling imbalance. OBs and OCs are two major cells responsible for maintaining bone homeostasis [129]. Specifically, OBs are derived from bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) and participate in bone formation by secreting matrix proteins and mediating bone matrix mineralization [130]. Runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2) can drive the lineage-specific differentiation of BMSCs into OBs while inhibiting differentiation into adipocytes and chondrocytes [131]. As bone formation progresses, by upregulating OB-specific markers such as Osterix (OSX), osteocalcin (OCN), and type I collagen (COL-I) gradually, immature OBs can eventually differentiate into mature OBs on the bone surface to maintain bone metabolic homeostasis [132]. On the other hand, OCs originate from hematopoietic stem cells of the monocyte-macrophage lineage. Specifically, the differentiation of OC precursors (pre-OCs) comes from the process that macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) induces hematopoietic stem cells; while mononuclear OCs can be differentiated from CFU-M following the activation of the receptor activator of NF-κB ligand (RANKL)-receptor activator of NF-κB (RANK) signaling pathway, a classic upstream activator of NF-κB signaling [133]. After cell fusion, these mononuclear precursors form multinucleated mature OCs, which secrete acidic substances and matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP-9) to create resorption pits on the bone surface, facilitating the degradation of bone matrix [134]. Through TRAF6-mediated signal transduction, the RANKL-RANK axis can activate NF-κB and NFATc1, thereby inducing the differentiation of pre-OCs into mature OCs.

A dynamic balance between OBs and OCs can contribute to the stabilization of bone remodeling. However, due to mitochondrial dysfunction, this balance is frequently disrupted in the process of OP. Aging and estrogen deficiency may impair mitochondrial function in OBs and osteocytes during OP, manifested as reduced ATP production, decreased mitochondrial membrane potential and elevated ROS levels [135]. Consequently, all these changes inhibit OB differentiation and weaken bone-forming capacity.

Mitochondrial dysfunction can contribute to both the disturbance of skeletal homeostasis and abnormal opening of the mPTP, leading to the release of mtDNA. Extracellular mtDNA may activate the cGAS-STING pathway, in turn triggering downstream NF-κB and TBK1-IRF3 signaling, thereby driving sterile inflammation. This forms a vicious cycle that accelerates the progression of OP [136]. In addition to NF-κB activation, STING can also induce IFN-β production in OCs. It is different from that of NF-κB, IFN-β suppressing OC activation through a negative feedback mechanism [137]. Thus, highlighting dual effects of STING exerted in OP. To be specific, the activation of the STING/NF-κB axis can promote bone resorption and inhibit bone formation, thereby exacerbating disease progression; conversely, activation of the STING/IFN-β pathway may restrain the differentiation of OCs and attenuate bone resorption. Supporting this mechanism, the use of RTA-408 resulted in effective inhibition of osteoclastogenesis and alleviation of ovariectomy (OVX)-induced bone loss through selective blockage of the STING-dependent NF-κB signaling pathway, which was independent of the STING-IFN-β pathway [138].

Moreover, aging-related factors can upregulate STING expression and disrupt the interaction between hexokinase 2 and VDAC1, resulting in mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired osteogenic differentiation, and aggravated skeletal imbalance [139]. Despite the shortage of a definition for precise STING ligands in OP, mtDNA is hypothesized to be a potential ligand, providing fresh molecular insight and research direction for exploring the activation of cGAS-STING axis in disease pathogenesis. In addition, in STING-independent pathways, reducing mtDNA release can remarkably decrease the expression of IL-6, IL-8, MCP-1, TNF-α and other inflammatory mediators, thereby preserving mitochondrial homeostasis in BMSCs, mitigating inflammation, and alleviating OP [140].

Collectively, mitochondrial dysfunction and mtDNA leakage create a vicious cycle. Mitochondrial dysfunction may induce mtDNA release, leading to the activation of STING-mediated sterile inflammation, further exacerbating mitochondrial impairment. These findings support the view that mitochondrial dysfunction and mtDNA release may contribute to the pathological nature of OP as a sterile inflammatory disease.

mtDNA-driven inflammasome activation exacerbates inflammation and bone loss in OP

In patients with OP, pro-inflammatory cytokine levels are generally elevated, with confirmed abnormally activated inflammasome sensors such as NLRP3 [141, 142], NLRC4 [143], and AIM2 [144]. However, we know little about the underlying mechanisms driving the induction of these pro-inflammatory factors. Recently, great concern has been attached to the role of mtDAMPs as key mediators. Specifically, mtDNA has been shown to activate NLRP3, NLRC4, and AIM2 inflammasomes. Not just the involvement of the disturbance of bone metabolic homeostasis, the occurrence and progression of OP may be attributed primarily to mitochondrial dysfunction. It may result in insufficient energy metabolism, ROS accumulation, and mtDNA leakage. These alterations serve as both direct causes of bone cell injury and key drivers of aberrant inflammasome activation [145].

Mitochondrial dysfunction is prevalent in OP. In addition to reducing cellular energy metabolism, mitochondrial damage can also promote pro-inflammatory responses of inflammasomes through the release of mtDAMPs to exacerbate bone loss. Therefore, it highlights mitochondria as critical sensors and amplifiers of inflammatory signaling, more than the “powerhouses” of bone cells. Their dysfunction can be regarded as direct determinant of whether bone metabolism remains in homeostasis. During mitochondrial injury, the mtDNA released from damaged mitochondria acts as an endogenous danger signal that can be recognized by the NLRP3 inflammasome, thereby exacerbating the inflammatory response within osteoblasts OBs. Glucolipotoxicity has been reported to enable the activation of TLR4, subsequently enhancing the interaction between NLRP3 and MAVS, further promoting ROS accumulation, ATP depletion, and inducing OB apoptosis. These molecular alterations impede OB differentiation and accelerate bone loss. Such events not only suppress OB differentiation but also further exacerbate skeletal deterioration, suggesting that the TLR4–NLRP3–MAVS axis is closely associated with mitochondrial dysfunction and may be linked to mtDNA-mediated inflammasome activation [146]. Additionally, under high-glucose conditions, mitochondrial dysfunction may also trigger excessive production of ROS, which, as an upstream signal, can further activate the MAPK pathway, eventually modulating the activation of NF-κB and the NLRP3 inflammasome. Subsequently, NF-κB can upregulate NLRP3 expression through feedback mechanisms, creating an amplifying and sustained inflammatory cascade. From a perspective of pathological mechanism, the proposed mitochondrial dysfunction-induced inflammatory positive feedback loop can enhance the activity of OCs, beyond weakening osteogenic capacity, thereby driving the sustained progression of OP.

Moreover, deficiency of either NLRP3 or NLRC4 may attenuate OC differentiation, leading to increased baseline bone mass and preventing LPS-induced inflammatory osteolysis, linking to the reduced serum IL-1β levels. However, in the context of simultaneous deletion of both inflammasomes, bone mass in mice appears comparable to that of wild-type controls, indicating restored bone homeostasis following a dual loss of inflammasome complexes. Given that both NLRP3 and NLRC4 can be activated by mtDNA and other mtDAMPs released from damaged mitochondria, these findings confirm the critical regulatory role of NOD-like receptors in bone metabolism, and imply the existence of a finely tuned compensatory mechanism within the mitochondria-mediated inflammatory signaling network [143]. In a prior animal experiment, interventions with insulin and lipoxin A4 have been demonstrated to effectively inhibit the activation of this inflammatory axis and improve bone metabolic disorders, suggesting the significance of restoring mitochondrial homeostasis and suppressing inflammation in treating diabetic OP [147].

NLRP3 inflammasome can be activated by cytosolic ATP and ion flux changes, whereas AIM2 primarily senses cytosolic dsDNA. Although its direct link to OP remains unclear, AIM2, as a key DNA-sensing receptor, may play a role in chronic inflammation-related bone metabolic abnormalities. In rheumatoid arthritis (RA), peripheral blood CD14⁺ AIM2⁺ monocytes from RA patients are reduced, accompanied by elevated IL-1β levels and increased caspase-1 activity, suggesting that pro-inflammatory factors can still be released under AIM2-deficient conditions, possibly involving regulation by POP3 [148]. This phenomenon indicates that AIM2 expression levels are closely associated with chronic inflammatory states, indirectly implying its potential regulatory role in bone-immune-related diseases. Moreover, AIM2 is closely linked to cellular senescence and osteogenic differentiation: its deficiency can enhance the osteogenic-like potential of vascular smooth muscle cells, suggesting a possible inhibitory effect on bone homeostasis [144]. Therefore, although direct evidence linking AIM2 explicitly to the pathological processes of OP is currently lacking, its multiple roles in inflammation, mtDNA recognition, cellular senescence, and regulation of osteogenic differentiation suggest a potential involvement.

In summary, existing investigation on NLRP3, NLRC4, and AIM2 supports the role of mitochondrial dysfunction in contributing to the onset and progression of OP through multiple inflammasome signaling pathways. It inspires us that combined interventions targeting mitochondrial protection and inflammasome inhibition may represent a promising therapeutic strategy.

Mitochondrial oxidative stress drives inflammatory OP

Under stress stimulation, mitochondria are prone to excessive fission, resulting in a fragmented morphology. This morphological alteration may further disrupt mitochondrial membrane potential and increase membrane permeability, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and excessive ROS production. Beyond being mere metabolic byproducts, ROS acts as critical signaling molecules in normal bone metabolism, participating in the regulation of bone remodeling and maintaining the dynamic osteogenesis-bone resorption balance. During RANKL-induced stimulation of pre-OCs, transient ROS bursts are essential for OC differentiation, underscoring their role as intracellular mediators in bone resorption [149]. While physiological levels of ROS are indispensable for osteoclastogenesis and bone remodeling, their excessive accumulation disrupts the balance between bone formation and resorption, leading to resorption predominance and consequent bone loss [150]. However, this delicate equilibrium is highly susceptible to disruption under pathological conditions. When ROS levels under oxidative stress exceed the antioxidant capacity of OBs, accumulated ROS will induce oxidative damage, leading to aberrant OC activation, impaired OB function, ultimately reduced bone mass and disrupted bone microarchitecture, all of which are hallmarks of OP [151].

The imbalance between ROS-induced inflammation and the antioxidant defense system may constitute a key molecular basis of inflammatory OP. ROS act not only as upstream signals of NF-κB but can also be further amplified by NF-κB, thereby intensifying the inflammatory cascade and promoting bone resorption. In previous research based on the establishment of an OVX-induced OS model, estrogen deficiency markedly increased the accumulation of ROS and lipid peroxidation products. Under this oxidative microenvironment, there existed activated NF-κB signaling pathway, causing upregulated pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-8), and enhanced activity of NFATc1, a key transcription factor involved in osteoclastogenesis, thereby accelerating bone resorption [152]. Conversely, the Nrf2-mediated antioxidant defense system is critical in maintaining bone metabolic homeostasis. Nrf2 can participate in counteracting ROS over-accumulation and mitigating ROS-induced bone damage by inducing the expression of downstream antioxidant enzymes such as heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) and NAD(P)H quinone dehydrogenase 1 (NQO1) [153]. Furthermore, inhibition of NF-κB and MAPK, two pathways closely associated with OC differentiation, can significantly decrease ROS generation, and downregulate key markers such as c-Fos, NFATc1, and MMP-9, thereby suppressing OC-mediated bone resorption and inflammation. Activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 axis can further enhance cellular antioxidant capacity and attenuate oxidative stress-induced bone loss. In in vivo experiments, ROS accumulation could elevate the expression of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, contributing to disrupting RANKL/OPG system and exacerbating bone loss. Importantly, suppressing ROS production and its downstream inflammatory responses has been shown to effectively alleviate OVX-induced OP [154].

Mitochondrial dynamics imbalance drives cellular dysfunction and inflammation in OP

Mitochondria have long been recognized as the central regulator of programmed cell death in various bone cell types, with mitochondrial dynamics considered a key determinant of bone cell fate [155]. Notably, the imbalance of mitochondrial dynamics may lead to disrupted energy homeostasis and signaling equilibrium in particular, serving as the driver of cellular dysfunction and inflammatory responses, which has been recognized as one of the key pathological mechanisms of OP [155]. Mitochondria are evenly distributed throughout the cytoplasm during the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs, accompanied by downregulation of DRP1 as well as upregulation of OPA1 and MFN2, suggesting that mitochondrial fusion can facilitate osteogenic commitment [156, 157]. However, in case of disturbed mitochondrial dynamics, there are functional impairments characterized prominently by ROS accumulation, a major contributor to BMSC dysfunction. In senescent BMSCs, through in vitro expansion, it was observed with elongated mitochondrial morphology, increased MFN2 expression, and decreased phosphorylated DRP1 levels, indicating an association between enhanced mitochondrial fusion and cellular aging [158]. Owing to impaired clearance of damaged components due to blocked mitophagy, excessive fusion exacerbates mitochondrial network dysfunction. However and noticeably, increased fusion is not universally harmful, as indicated by certain studies [159]. By tethering damaged mitochondria to healthier ones, mitochondrial fusion may allow the exchange of mitochondrial contents and membrane potential, which may provide a protective mechanism to sustain cellular function [160]. This indicates that mitochondrial dynamics exert a dual role in regulating the fate of BMSCs.

In OCs, DRP1 and its adaptor proteins (e.g., FIS1, MID49, MID51) were concurrently upregulated during differentiation; while the deletion of DRP1 obviously suppressed osteoclastogenesis by inhibiting the expression of c-Fos and NFATc1 [161]. Moreover, mitochondrial fusion defects, such as OPA1 knockdown, would disrupt cristae architecture and impair OC formation [162]. In contrast, the deletion of Mfn1/2 might weaken osteoclastic activity and induce increased bone mass in mice [163]. These studies collectively indicate that both osteogenesis and osteoclastogenesis rely on a finely tuned balance of mitochondrial dynamics.

Beyond impairing the differentiation and function of bone cells, disruption of mitochondrial dynamics can also alter behaviors of OBs and OCs through enhanced ROS generation and activation of downstream inflammatory pathways, ultimately exacerbating bone loss. Inhibiting mitochondrial fission has been documented to effectively attenuate inflammation and protect osteogenic function. For instance, treatment using Mdivi-1, a DRP1 inhibitor, or DRP1-targeting siRNA can remarkably suppress mitochondrial fragmentation in OBs and effectively block inflammation mediated by the NLRP3 and cGAS-STING signaling pathways. Conversely, knockdown of MFN1/2, which impaired mitochondrial fusion in OBs, might aggravate inflammatory responses and disrupt osteogenic capacity. Further experimental evidence also confirmed that mitochondrial fission-induced ROS accumulation was a key driver of inflammatory amplification. Both the ROS scavenger N-acetylcysteine and Mdivi-1 could effectively alleviate mitochondrial dysfunction and inflammatory injury [164]. Collectively, the ROS-DRP1 axis is proposed to play an important role in mediating mitochondrial dynamic imbalance and osteogenic impairment under inflammatory conditions.

Mitophagy deficiency exacerbates inflammatory activation in OP

Recent evidence has proposed mitochondrial dysfunctionas a critical pathogenic factor in OP. In this context, great concern has been attached to mitophagy, a key mechanism for maintaining mitochondrial quality control. Proper mitophagy can facilitate the removal of damaged mitochondria, thereby preventing excessive ROS accumulation and aberrant inflammasome activatio. Moreover, it can restrict DAMP release and excessive inflammatory responses, which is critical for maintaining bone cell function and bone remodeling balance.

In OP, downregulation of the PINK1-Parkin pathway, a core regulatory axis of mitophagy, may lead to autophagy defects [165]. It has been reported with regard to the upregulation in both Pink1 expression and mineralization markers during OB-mediated bone formation, whereas Pink1 knockdown can significantly impair OB mineralization. Furthermore, in an OVX mouse model, Lee et al. observed that PINK1 deficiency resulted in markedly reduced bone mass, impaired osteoblast differentiation, disrupted mitochondrial homeostasis, and persistent ROS accumulation [166]. Among them, ROS accumulation serves as a critical signal for the activation of NLRP3 inflammasome, which can upregulate IL-1β and IL-18, promote OC differentiation and bone resorption, thereby exacerbating bone loss [167]. Conversely, under oxidative stress, enhancing PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy can remove excessive ROS and damaged mitochondria, thereby leading to significant alleviation of OB apoptosis and improvement of bone microarchitecture [168]. Overall, inactivation of the PINK1-Parkin pathway can hamper the timely clearance of damaged mitochondria and induce persistent ROS accumulation to trigger inflammatory responses. The resultant sustained inflammation amplification and heightened oxidative stress can further impair the differentiation and mineralization of OBs, while promoting OC activation, forming a vicious cycle that accelerates bone mass loss and trabecular deterioration.

It is noteworthy that a complex bidirectional regulatory relationship exists between inflammatory responses and mitophagy. On one hand, mitophagy exerts negative feedback to suppress inflammation; on the other hand, inflammatory signals can modulate mitochondrial autophagy. Inflammatory signals themselves can activate cellular autophagy mechanisms, forming a self-limiting feedback loop to restrain the sustained amplification of mitochondria-derived inflammation [99]. However, under chronic inflammatory conditions, this protective mechanism may be altered. For instance, in the presence of TNF-α, Parkin expression in pre-osteoblasts is upregulated alongside mitochondrial damage, indicating that although chronic inflammation can activate Parkin-mediated mitophagy, the outcome paradoxically inhibits osteoblast differentiation [169]. This suggests that during the development of OP, the regulatory interplay between inflammation and mitophagy may shift from initially maintaining homeostasis to forming a deleterious feedback loop that promotes disease progression.

Mitochondrial transfer: a key mechanism in bone metabolic homeostasis and immune regulation

Owing to imbalanced osteogenesis and bone resorption, OP is generally featured by reduced bone mass and increased bone fragility. Mitochondrial dysfunction has been accepted to be one of the key drivers in its pathogenesis. Recently, mitochondrial transfer has been proposed as an important compensatory mechanism in bone tissue under energy crisis and oxidative stress, exhibiting close association with the maintenance of bone metabolic homeostasis. In bone tissue, OBs embedded within the mineralized matrix differentiate into osteocytes, which extend dendritic processes to form a highly interconnected lacuno-canalicular system, thereby facilitating intercellular material exchange and signal transmission [170]. Osteocytes are long-lived and are constantly exposed to microenvironmental stressors (e.g., hypoxia and mechanical loading), resulting in their higher dependency on tight regulation of energy homeostasis and oxidative stress. Recently, researchers proposed a novel mechanism of mitochondrial transfer between osteocytes mediated by their dendritic processes, allowing them to cope with energy crises and maintain cellular function [171].

Under various stress conditions, including ATP depletion, excessive accumulation of ROS, loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, stimulation by inflammatory factors (such as TNF-α and IL-1β), and estrogen deficiency–induced oxidative damage, osteocytes can be triggered to initiate mitochondrial transfer [172]. In this process, healthy neighboring osteocytes can actively donate functional mitochondria enriched in mitofusin-2 (MFN2) through their extended dendritic networks and ER-associated pathways. Through such mitochondrial transfer, it may lead to enhanced ATP production, reduced intracellular ROS levels, and improved redox state of recipient cells, offering a compensatory strategy for sustaining the viability and function of osteocytes under adverse conditions. However, with aging, osteocytes undergo structural and functional alterations, manifesting as reduced dendritic complexity, diminished mitochondrial content within the dendrites, and decreased MFN2 expression. These changes may further compromise mitochondrial transfer, weakening the self-repair mechanisms of osteocytes under stress and impairing their ability to regulate bone homeostasis [117]. This process is closely associated with the typical decline in osteogenic activity and the loss of bone mass observed in OP.

In addition to involving osteocytes, mitochondrial transfer can also occur extensively within the bone marrow microenvironment, particularly between immune cells and bone progenitor cells. As supported by emerging, bone progenitor cells can actively transfer mitochondria to immune cells, especially myeloid lineages (e.g., monocytes/macrophages and neutrophils) and lymphocytes, thereby modulating their differentiation and function. Unlike the traditional mechanism that relies on ligand–receptor signaling to promote osteoclastogenesis, mitochondrial transfer conveys metabolic signals, thereby suppressing the differentiation of myeloid precursors into mature OCs and attenuating bone resorption [173]. Currently, several studies have confirmed the critical roles of these myeloid immune cells and lymphocytes after receiving mitochondrial transfer in bone metabolism balance, despite a lack of full elucidation of their bone metabolism regulatory functions [174, 175]. The inflammatory cascade reactions dominated by these cells are highly consistent with the pathological characteristics of “chronic low-grade inflammation” in OP. Consequently, it can be speculated that mitochondrial transfer may disrupt the OB-OC balance by activating the immune inflammatory network, thereby leading to bone homeostasis imbalance.

Moreover, inhibition of mitochondrial transfer has been shown to impair immune cell function. For instance, in a model of acute respiratory distress syndrome, the blockage of mitochondrial transfer was found to significantly compromise macrophage phagocytic capacity and antibacterial defense [176]. This suggests that mitochondrial transfer plays a universal role in immune regulation. Moreover, glucocorticoids, a potent immunosuppressive agent used extensively, have been found to markedly reduce the frequency of mitochondrial transfer from bone lineage cells to myeloid and lymphoid populations. Moreover, it also may weaken the ability of bone lineage cells to participate in immune regulation and bone metabolism balance through mitochondrial transfer. The resulting suppression of osteogenesis further induce the development of OP, particularly in glucocorticoid-induced cases [173]. Essentially, mitochondrial transfer is increasingly recognized as a pivotal nexus between immune regulation and bone metabolism, even in the context of poor understanding of the precise molecular mechanisms underlying these effects. Such emerging paradigm offers novel insight into the mechanisms of inflammation-driven bone loss and glucocorticoid-associated skeletal disorders.

In summary, mitochondrial transfer, functioning between bone cells as well as between bone progenitor cells and immune cells, is essential in maintaining both bone homeostasis and immune balance. Impaired mitochondrial transfer may weaken the self-repair capacity of bone cells, disrupt the dynamic balance between bone formation and resorption, and promote the development of chronic inflammation-driven OP. The clinical relevance of this mechanism is further underscored in OP, given the inhibitory effect of glucocorticoids on mitochondrial transfer. Therefore, defects in mitochondrial transfer, together with mitochondrial dysfunction, may represent fundamental pathological processes in OP.

Mitochondria-mediated crosstalk between hormones and inflammation: a new perspective on bone metabolic imbalance

OP is a prototypical metabolic bone disease. Estrogen deficiency and immune-inflammatory responses remain two major contributors to its onset and progression. Accumulated evidence suggests that mitochondrial dysfunction may represent an important pathological link connecting hormonal signaling with immune dysregulation, offering a new perspective for understanding the mechanisms underlying postmenopausal OP. In addition to the direct detrimental effects of estrogen deficiency on bone, the development of postmenopausal OP is also driven by profound alterations in immune status. Postmenopausal women often exhibit a phenotype of chronic low-grade inflammation, coupled with altered cytokine expression and immune cell lineage distribution [177]. Intriguingly, mitochondria have been identified as key subcellular targets of estrogen action. Inflammation-induced reductions in estradiol (E2) levels may impair mitochondrial function directly [178]. This discovery provides new insights into the underlying mechanism of mitochondria mediating the inflammatory regulation of OP.

By binding to estrogen receptors (particularly ERα), E2 can exert its effects to induce the activation of nuclear respiratory factor 1 (NRF-1) and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC-1α) [179]. Subsequent activation of PGC-1α may further promote mitochondrial biogenesis by inducing TFAM expression. TFAM specifically binds to mtDNA, regulating its transcription and replication, thereby maintaining mitochondrial function [180]. Concurrently, ERα-mediated signaling can also upregulate the expression of SIRT3, a NAD⁺-dependent deacetylase. Then, SIRT3 can decrease ROS production by deacetylating manganese superoxide dismutase, and it also can also interact with FOXO3A to facilitate its nuclear translocation, further enhancing PGC-1α expression [181]. Accordingly, under physiological conditions, E2 maintains mitochondrial integrity and energy metabolic stability while suppressing inflammatory responses, through the coordinated action of the PGC-1α/NRF-1/TFAM and SIRT3/FOXO3A signaling pathways. However, E2 levels continue to decline, which not only impairs mitochondrial biogenesis but also weakens its anti-inflammatory effects. Specifically, NF-κB, a central regulator of inflammation, is competitively inhibited by E2 via ERα signaling, which may further decrease the secretion of IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β, and other pro-inflammatory cytokines [182, 183]. E2 can also strengthen the production of anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10, thereby balancing inflammatory response [184]. Therefore, the decline in E2 not only diminishes the inhibition of inflammation but also exposes the body to a pro-inflammatory state, further exacerbating the imbalance in bone metabolism.

In OP, the “hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal” axis is considered a key regulator of bone homeostasis. Gonadotropin-releasing hormone secreted by the hypothalamus can stimulate the pituitary to release follicle-stimulating hormone and luteinizing hormone to regulate estrogen synthesis [185]. Estrogen can activate the PGC-1α/ERRα signaling axis to power mitochondrial biogenesis [186], thus enhancing energy metabolism and antioxidant defenses required for OB differentiation. Additionally, estrogen can also suppress mitochondrial ROS production in OCs to retard bone resorption [187]. However, postmenopausal sharp decline in estrogen levels may disturb the balance in mitochondrial dynamics. In OBs, increased activity of DRP1, a mitochondrial fission protein, may induce mitochondrial fragmentation and loss of membrane potential [188]. Correspondingly, in OCs, DRP1 may promote differentiation by activating NFATc1 signaling [161]. Mitochondrial dysfunction may also be a contributor to the creation of a chronic low-grade inflammatory microenvironment in BMSCs, which is essential in bone remodeling. This pro-inflammatory milieu further disrupts bone homeostasis. Pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α) within the inflammatory microenvironment may impair OB activation and bone formation while upregulating RANKL expression, thereby promoting osteoclastogenesis [160–162]. Consequently, it establishes a vicious cycle— of “estrogen deficiency-mitochondrial damage-inflammatory storm”, thereby driving the progressive deterioration of bone mass in OP. Beyond direct impairment of OB activity, this vicious cycle can also promote OC activation through amplified inflammatory response, serving as one of the central mechanisms driving OP progression.

In summary, the mitochondrial-inflammatory axis occupies a central position in the pathogenesis of OP. As an mtDAMP, mtDNA induces a sterile inflammatory response and promotes chronic inflammation of bone tissues by activating the cGAS-STING signaling pathway and its downstream inflammasomes. Concurrently, bone metabolism imbalance and bone loss may be driven by multiple factors, such as abnormal ROS accumulation caused by oxidative stress, the disorder of mitophagy and dynamics, mitochondrial transport disorders, and estrogen deficiency-induced aggravation of inflammation. This mechanistic framework provides new potential targets and research directions for preventing and treating OP, which is expected to promote the development of precision treatment strategies.

Aging serving as a bridge linking inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and OP

In general, aging is primarily driven by conservative features, among which mitochondrial dysfunction and chronic inflammation remain two hallmark features. Besides serving as an initiator of cellular senescence, mitochondrial dysfunction may also progressively deepen during aging, forming a self-reinforcing positive feedback loop. Specifically, mtDNA mutations and impaired repair accumulate with age, leading to electron transport chain dysfunction and reduced energy production efficiency [189]. Simultaneously, damaged mitochondria cannot be cleared inadequately through mitophagy, resulting in accumulated dysfunctional mitochondria, further exacerbating cellular metabolic dysregulation and oxidative stress, eventually promoting aging [190]. Moreover, aging-associated metabolic reprogramming may be induced owing to weakened adaptability of mitochondrial stress resulting from declined NAD⁺ levels and sirtuin activity [191]. Disruption of mitochondrial Ca²⁺ homeostasis and Ca²⁺ overload further induce excessive ROS generation, mPTP opening, and energetic collapse, resulting in accelerated aging as well [192]. Moreover, through ROS and mtDNA release, mitochondrial dysfunction may also activate the inflammasome and cGAS-STING pathway to trigger chronic inflammation and driving inflammatory aging. Previous studies have shown that aging in cardiomyocytes is associated with increased ROS levels and enhanced NLRP3 activation. Conversely, mice lacking NLRP3 display telomere elongation and delayed aging, indicating that suppression of the inflammasome may contribute to slowing the aging process [193]. Similarly, mitochondria-mediated inflammatory responses in immune cells also exhibit an intimate association with aging. For instance, the dysfunction of T cells, despite not within the category of classic pro-inflammatory cells, can accelerate senescence. Desdín-Micó et al. reported that T cells lacking TFAM exhibited reduced mtDNA content, resulting in mitochondrial dysfunction and premature activation of inflammatory factors (e.g., IL-6 and Stat1), thereby inducing senescence-associated inflammatory responses and inducing early cellular senescence [194].

The accumulation of senescent cells (SnCs) is another hallmark of aging, which has been confirmed to be closely related to the occurrence of various age-related diseases, including OP. SnCs can secrete a broad array of inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, oxidative stress-related proteins, growth factors, and proteases, collectively termed the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), producing adverse impact on the tissue microenvironment [195]. SASP factors drive systemic chronic inflammation, characterized by persistent, low-grade inflammatory responses in the absence of acute infection or overt clinical disease [196]. In addition to impairing the function of neighboring and distant healthy cells, the sustained inflammation may also accelerate the decline of skeletal stem cell function, promoting chronic bone disorders such as OP [197]. In bone tissue, senescent osteogenic cells and myeloid progenitors can produce multiple SASP-related factors (e.g., IL-6, IL-8, MCP-1, and TNF-α), which are significantly upregulated in aged murine bone cells [198]. Senescent bone cells have been revealed to suppress osteogenesis and promote the formation of OCs by secreting these factors, thereby disrupting the dynamic balance of bone remodeling [199, 200]. Among various SASP components, IL-6 is recognized to be one of the inflammatory factors exhibiting the closest association with age-related bone loss [201]. Meanwhile, IL-6 can accelerate the senescence of BMSCs via the IL-6/STAT3 signaling pathway, whereas IL-6 knockout may prevent BMSC senescence and alleviate obesity-induced bone loss [202]. Furthermore, via the Akt pathway, IL-6 may inhibit Setd7 expression that may further impair the osteogenic differentiation of MSCs [203]. Beyond bone cells, immune cells also undergo senescence and release SASP molecules within the bone microenvironment, which may contribute to the formation of inflammatory crosstalk with aged MSCs and exacerbate cellular senescence subsequently [204]. Collectively, aging orchestrates a vicious cycle in which SASP-driven inflammation and mitochondrial dysfunction reinforce each other. In this way, it may accelerate skeletal aging and predispose elderly individuals to OP due to cumulative cellular senescence and impaired bone remodeling.

Intervention strategies based on current research and new unresolved issues

Intervention strategies for mitochondrial quality control defects

Existing evidence suggests that defects in mitochondrial quality control may lead to a self-amplifying vicious cycle of inflammation. It highlights the potential relevance of targeting mitochondrial function to support cellular energy homeostasis and modulate inflammatory responses. In OP-related studies, current strategies to enhance mitochondrial function mainly involve the regulation of mitochondrial dynamics, promotion of mitophagy, application of antioxidant interventions, and stimulation of mitochondrial biogenesis. For instance, hydroxytyrosol, via activating the AKT-GSK3β signaling pathway, may alleviate mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress-induced apoptosis in OBs, facilitating the maintenance of the balance of mitochondrial fusion and fission by reducing OPA1 cleavage [205]. Similarly, silibinin has been shown to inhibit the conversion of long-form OPA1 to short-form OPA1, downregulate FIS1 expression, and promote mitochondrial fusion, potentially reducing advanced glycation end product-induced OB apoptosis [206]. Moreover, inhibiting SIRT3 has been observed to activate the PINK1-mediated mitophagy pathway, impair mitochondrial function in OCs, and suppress their overactivation [145]. All the above experimental attempts provide a potential therapeutic strategy for preventing age-related and estrogen deficiency-induced OP. However, these findings are primarily based on animal models, necessitating further validation of their efficacy and safety in humans.

Classic antioxidants such as melatonin have also been reported to influence mitochondrial function and inflammation. For instance, melatonin may activate the canonical Wnt pathway, enhancing β-catenin nuclear translocation and subsequently inhibiting the assembly of the NLRP3 inflammasome. Experimental evidence supports that the use of melatonin can increase serum levels of alkaline phosphatase and osteocalcin, while decreasing tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase activity, suggesting its potential role of remodeling the bone metabolic microenvironment by maintaining the mitochondrial homeostasis-inflammation axis. This effect was inversely verified in NLRP3-knockout mice, in which the osteogenesis-inhibiting effects induced by OVX were significantly alleviated following a genetic silencing of the inflammasome, further suggesting an important role of the inflammasome in the pathogenesis of OP [207]. Nonetheless, we know little about the translational relevance of these results to human OP, deserving further in-depth investigation.

In addition, various natural products have been reported to exert antioxidant and mitochondrial protective effects. For example, lutein, a natural antioxidant, may activate the Nrf2 pathway and upregulate HO-1 and NQO1 expression, potentially reducing ROS production and lipid peroxidation, as well as effectively downregulating inflammatory cytokines and NFATc1 expression, which may facilitate the mitigation of bone loss in OVX-modeled rats. This evidence suggests an important role of ROS in inflammation-driven OP and indicates the potential of antioxidant therapy as a possible intervention strategy [152]. Similarly, an extract from the peel of the Thai purple sweet potato cultivar Maejo 341 (MSP) is also found to be a potent antioxidant and anti-osteoporotic activities. The MSP extract, rich in anthocyanins, has been shown to inhibit LPS-induced inflammation and reduce the expression of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, and suppress RANKL-induced activation of the NF-κB and MAPK pathways. These effects were accompanied by decreased ROS production and increased antioxidant gene expression, suggesting potential benefits for bone structure and OB activity [208]. Moreover, ginsenoside Rb2 has also been reported to reduce the expression of RANKL and IL-6, as well as suppress ROS production, possibly through preserving mitochondrial function and attenuating oxidative stress-related inflammatory pathways [209]. In addition, overexpression of SIRT3 and PGC-1α has been demonstrated to enhance mitochondrial biogenesis and restore intracellular energy metabolic homeostasis [210, 211]. Unresolved new issues

Currently, there are limited therapeutic strategies targeting mitochondria-dominated inflammation available for the treatment of OP, which are primarily confined to basic research. At this stage, broad consensus has not yet been reached concerning the clinical translational potential of modulating inflammation via mitochondrial regulation to intervene in the progression of OP. Drug development targeting MOMP mechanism is still in its exploratory stage. Venetoclax, a BCL-2 inhibitor, is currently the most widely used representative agent in the clinical setting, mainly for hematological malignancies, while its application in solid tumors remains under clinical investigation [212].

To date, there is still a lack of specific regulatory factor developed for modulating mitochondrial inflammation in humans. Moreover, different types of bone cells rely on distinct mitochondrial functions to maintain their biological activities. For instance, enhanced mitochondrial fusion is critical for BMSC differentiation; increased mitochondrial fission supports OB bone-forming activity; and Opa1-mediated fusion is also essential for OC development [162, 213]. These functional differences pose a significant challenge, namely, an unsettled issue of selective modulation of mitochondrial inflammation in one cell type, without disrupting physiological processes in others.

At present, despite the report of various modulators targeting mitochondrial dynamics and function, no significant achievement has been made regarding the selective modulation of mitochondrial-inflammatory axis in specific bone cell types. Therefore, it remains a great challenge during clinical translation with regard to the precise modulation of mitochondrial quality in specific cell types, without interfering with physiological processes in others. Encouragingly, recent progress in gene-editing tools and nanoparticle-mediated delivery systems offers substantial promise for developing mitochondria-targeted therapeutic strategies [214, 215]. However, these approaches are still largely experimental, which should be further investigated to identify their efficacy and safety in human OP.

Conclusion and outlook

The mitochondrial-inflammatory axis is proposed to serve as an important regulatory node in osteoimmunology, linking inflammatory imbalance in the bone microenvironment to disrupted bone metabolism and thereby contributing to the pathogenesis of OP (as shown in Fig. 6). Growing evidence recently has suggested potential roles of mtDAMP release,, activation of inflammatory signaling pathways such as cGAS-STING and NLRP3, mitochondrial dynamics disruption, impaired mitophagy, and intercellular mitochondrial transfer in the progression of OP. Altogether, mitochondrial homeostasis has an intimate association with bone metabolic balance.

Mitochondrial-Inflammatory Axis in Osteoimmunology and Its Impact on Bone Homeostasis. OBs are derived from BMSCs, with Runx2 driving their differentiation toward the osteogenic lineage while suppressing alternative lineages. Aging leads to upregulation of STING, which disrupts the interaction between HK2 and VDAC1, triggering the opening of the mPTP, resulting in mitochondrial dysfunction and impaired osteogenesis, thereby compromising bone homeostasis. MtDNA activates the cGAS-STING pathway, inducing sterile inflammation. The RANKL–RANK axis activates NF-κB and NFATc1 via TRAF6, promoting osteoclast differentiation. USP7 binds to STING to prevent its degradation, enhancing IFN-β expression and providing negative feedback that inhibits osteoclastogenesis. Concurrently, USP7 suppresses NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways, reducing bone resorption. Reduced STAT3 activity promotes PINK1-dependent mitophagy to clear damaged mitochondria and suppress NLRP3 inflammasome activation. ROS accumulation activates the NF-κB pathway, enhancing the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and NFATc1, thereby accelerating bone resorption. Activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 signaling pathway strengthens antioxidant defenses and mitigates bone loss. TNF-α induces upregulation of Parkin and mitochondrial damage, while downregulation of the PINK1-Parkin pathway leads to defective mitophagy. Additionally, mtDNA activates the NLRP3 inflammasome, exacerbating inflammation and osteoblast apoptosis, a process closely linked with the TLR4-NLRP3-MAVS axis, especially in diabetic OP. Inhibition of mitochondrial fission through the DRP1 inhibitor Mdivi-1 or DRP1-targeting siRNA suppresses mitochondrial fragmentation and blocks associated inflammatory responses

Noticeably, in view of the global aging and rising prevalence of chronic skeletal disorders, the development of therapeutic solutions targeting the mitochondrial-inflammatory axis may represent a potentially valuable therapeutic direction. Nevertheless, several important challenges remain. Specifically, multiple mechanistic insights are derived from preclinical or in vitro models, requiring further validation to unveil their direct relevance to human diseases. Moreover, there is a poor explanation of the complexity of mtDAMP-mediated signaling and the context-dependent interactions across different bone cell types. In particular, a deeper understanding of the dynamic changes across different bone cell types and microenvironmental states remains an urgent research priority. Significantly, the achievement of translational opportunities for OP prevention and treatment depends largely on such efforts.
