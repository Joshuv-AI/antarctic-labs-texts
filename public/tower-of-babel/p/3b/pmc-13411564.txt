Evaluating the Effects of Pesticides Residues on Seminal Fluid Antioxidants Status and Semen Parameters among Males with Infertility at a Tertiary Hospital in Nigeria
Authors: Waliu O. Oladosu, Saheed O. Jimoh, Oseni Ismaila, Adewale M. Alayo, Ganiyu A. Gbagirimojo, Oyewo Rasaki Adewale, Adekunle B. Okesina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aims:To evaluate the impacts of seminal concentrations of selected pesticide residues on SFA parameters and seminal antioxidants in men with abnormal sperm parameters.Settings and Design:An analytical cross-sectional study of consenting infertile male patients, with at least one abnormal seminal fluid analysis (SFA) parameter, at a tertiary Health Centre.Methods and Materials:Between July 2022 and June 2023, 130 men with abnormal sperm parameters and 130 with normal sperm parameters were studied as controls. Semen samples were collected, treated, and centrifuged. Analyses included pesticide residues (GC/MS), Vitamins C and E (spectrophotometry), and enzyme levels (ELISA), with data analyzed using SPSS 20.0.Result:Subjects showed significantly higher seminal pesticide levels and lower antioxidant levels than controls. Sperm count and motility were positively linked to antioxidant status and negatively linked to pesticide levels, except for Carbamate. Seminal levels of Malathion (13.57 ± 2.68 vs 3.54 ± 1.81 μg/ml, P < 0.001), DDT (24.03 ± 1.93 vs 13.65 ± 1.75 μg/ml, P < 0.001), Permethrin (28.59 ± 3.87 vs 3.82 ± 1.78 μg/ml, P < 0.001), and Carbamate (35.75 ± 1.52 vs 17.75 ± 3.90 μg/ml, P < 0.001) were significantly higher among subjects than controls, respectively. Also, seminal levels Vitamin C (21.74 ± 2.30 vs 54.55 ± 2.78 mg/100 ml, P < 0.001), Vitamin E (5.62 ± 2.17 mg/100 ml vs 14.73 ± 1.62 mg/100 ml, P < 0.001), GPx (74.28 ± 2.63 vs 136.14 ± 2.79 IU/L, P < 0.001), and CAT (102.28 ± 1.44 vs 185.57 ± 1.24 IU/L, P < 0.001) were significantly lower among subjects than controls.Conclusion:Human exposure to various types of pesticides, including herbicides and insecticides used in various daily activities at homes, farmlands, and agricultural storage sites, negatively affects semen parameters and semen antioxidant status.

INTRODUCTION

For 50 years, civilization, especially industrialization, has reduced male fertility worldwide by diminishing spermatozoa quality and quantity.[1] Developing countries like Nigeria are industrializing in telecommunications, agriculture, automobiles, rail transportation, and petrochemical discovery and processing. Industrialization has health risks, directly and indirectly, despite its economic benefits.[2]

As sub-Saharan Africa industrializes, noncommunicable disorders like male infertility are rising. Pesticides exemplify a significant category of environmental toxins, defined as chemical or biological agents employed to prevent, control, or eradicate pests that threaten agricultural production, livestock, and human health.[3] These agents are classified into five principal categories: Organochlorides, such as Dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane (DDT); Organophosphates, including Malathion and parathion; Carbamates; Pyrethroids; and Anilides.[4] Long-term exposure can elevate blood toxin levels, leading to serious health issues. High suspicion and specific studies are needed to connect these environmental poisons to various ailments.[5] DDT, Malathion, Carbamate, and Permethrin, which cover the four primary classifications of pesticides, were analyzed in this study.

Human plasma contains antioxidants that protect against toxins and prevent lipid peroxidation and DNA damage from free radicals.[6] Excessive environmental pollutants can overwhelm these mechanisms, leading to cellular damage and gonadotoxicity.[6] While studies on environmental toxins and male fertility often rely on subjective occupational exposure questionnaires, this research will directly quantify pesticide residues and antioxidant levels in seminal fluid and relate them to participant parameters.[56]

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

The Federal Medical Centre’s Department of Chemical Pathology and Immunology in Abeokuta, Ogun State, Nigeria, conducted a study on male spouses of infertile couples undergoing seminal fluid analysis (SFA) done over 12 months from the beginning of July 2022 to June 2023. Participants had abnormal SFA results, while controls had normal sperm count and motility.

This cross-sectional study involved infertile male patients with abnormal seminal fluid analysis (SFA) parameters, selected through simple random sampling.

The minimum sample size required for the study will be estimated using the modified Fisher’s formula[7]:

Given as: n= (Z^2 pq)/d^2

n = the desired minimum sample size.

z = the standard normal deviation, usually set at 1.96, corresponding to a 95% confidence interval.

p = the prevalence of male infertility in the target population from the previous study was estimated to be 8.45%.[8]

q = the proportion of the target population with no particular characteristic.

d = tolerable margin of error, an observed difference of 5% is considered significant.

An attrition rate of 10% will be assumed (≈12).

Men referred for SFA by Gynaecology, Urology, and GOPD clinics consented to participate. Subjects had at least one abnormal semen parameter, while controls had normal values.

This study excluded patients with clinically identified cryptorchidism (undescended testes) or atrophic testes, Azoospermia (lack of spermatozoa in the semen), Aspermia, or oral antioxidant therapy/supplements.

Before taking patient samples, the study and process were explained, and written consent was obtained. The Federal Medical Centre, Abeokuta, ethics committee granted IRB/ethics clearance.

Patients collected semen via masturbation or coitus interruptus into a sterile, wide-mouthed container after 72 hours (3–4 days) of sexual abstinence.

The laboratory received the samples within an hour. Samples were centrifuged at 2–8°C for 20 minutes at 1000 × g. Assays were performed on sediments immediately or within seven days of storage at 2–8°C.

The supernatants were used for pesticide residues, thus:

Assay of Pesticide Residues Using Gas Chromatography/Mass Spectrometry[9]

The gas chromatography/mass spectrometry (GC/MS) equipment separates chemical mixtures and identifies their molecular components, making it one of the most accurate environmental sample analyzers. The GC operates by heating a mixture to separate its components. Heated gases pass through an inert gas column (like helium). The separated substances pour into the MS from the column entrance. Mass spectrometry identifies chemicals by analyzing the mass of the analyte molecule. A computer database of mass spectra from thousands of substances detects and quantifies the quantities of Malathion, DDT, Permethrin, and Carbamate residues.

Assay of Seminal Glutathione Peroxidase.[10]

The Elabscience human glutathione peroxidase (GPX1) ELISA assay kit was used.

Test principle

This kit uses sandwich-ELISA. The kit’s micro-ELISA plate is pre-coated with a Human GPX1 antibody. Standards or samples are mixed with the specific antibody in micro-ELISA plate wells. A biotinylated detection antibody for Human GPX1 and Avidin–Horseradish Peroxidase (HRP) conjugate are added to each microplate well and incubated. Free parts wash away. The substrate solution is applied to each well.

Only wells with Human GPX1, biotinylated detection antibody, and Avidin–Horseradish Peroxidase conjugate are blue. The stop solution stops the enzyme–substrate reaction, turning the color yellow.

Optical density (OD) is measured spectrophotometrically at 450 nm ± 2 nm. OD is proportional to Human GPX1 concentration. Calculate Human GPX1 concentration in samples by comparing their OD to the standard curve.

Assay of Seminal Catalase.[11]

An Elabscience colorimetric assay kit was used.

Assay principle

Catalase (CAT) decomposes hydrogen peroxide, and this reaction can be quickly stopped by ammonium molybdate. The remaining hydrogen peroxide forms a yellowish complex with ammonium molybdate, allowing CAT activity to be measured at 405 nm.

Assay of Seminal Vitamin C.[12]

An Elabscience colorimetric assay kit was used.

Detection principle.

VC reduces Fe3+ to Fe2+, enhancing intestinal iron absorption. Ascorbic acid converts Fe3+ to Fe2+, reacting with phenanthroline to produce color. Vitamin C content can be indirectly determined from OD.

Assay of Seminal Vitamin E.[13]

Vitamin E, comprising four tocopherols and four tocotrienols, with α-tocopherol being the most active, prevents ester peroxidation chain reactions to protect cell membranes.

Detection principle

VE deoxidizes Fe3+ to Fe2+, which, under specific conditions, forms a pink complex with phenanthroline. VE content is then determined using a standard curve or formula through colorimetric assays.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 20.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). Data with normal distribution are presented as mean ± SD, while non-normally distributed data are expressed as median and interquartile range, with logarithmic transformation applied as necessary. ANOVA was used to compare normally distributed data, and Pearson’s correlation analysis assessed variable relationships. Significance was set at P < 0.05.

DISCUSSION

Most of our research participants were from Abeokuta, an urban area with many civil servants and artisans, surrounded by farming villages that access the study site for secondary and tertiary healthcare.

Controls had higher mean sperm counts and motility percentages than subjects, but normal sperm morphology showed no significant difference. This aligns with the study criteria and WHO guidelines, which state that normal morphology has less impact on fertility than motility and count, with 5% normal morphology being acceptable.

As established in previous studies exploring the roles of pesticide residues in male infertility, this study found that seminal Malathion, DDT, and Permethrin levels were significantly higher in subjects than controls, negatively correlating with sperm count, motility, and morphology. Carbamate showed no correlation with these parameters.[141516]

Pesticide exposure can cause weight loss in the epididymis, seminal vesicles, and prostate gland, as well as seminiferous tubule degeneration, hormonal disturbances, and suppressed spermatogenesis.[1617]

DDT, an important organochlorine, is an example of endocrine-disrupting chemicals that affect androgenic and estrogenic receptors, potentially harming male reproductive health in both the long term and short term.[18] While its use in malaria control is being reduced in some countries, it remains high in malaria-endemic regions like Nigeria, where the Stockholm agreement exempted it from restrictions.[1920]

Chronic DDT exposure is linked to increased sperm DNA fragmentation, possibly due to faulty apoptotic processes during spermatogenesis. It has estrogenic and antiandrogenic effects that harm male reproductive health in the short and long term. Efforts to reduce its use in malaria control programs in developing countries are ongoing, with some countries planning to phase it out.[1821] In malaria-endemic countries like Nigeria, DDT pesticides are still commonly used for mosquito control to prevent malaria.[17]

High sperm DNA fragmentation is linked to chronic DDT exposure, likely due to defective apoptotic mechanisms and disruptions in cellular and nuclear remodeling during the final stages of spermatogenesis.[21]

Organophosphate pesticides like Malathion exhibit toxic effects similar to DDT. Most research on its effects on the male reproductive system has been conducted on rats and mice, but findings align with limited human studies.[2223] Malathion’s acute and chronic harmful effects cannot be solely explained by acetylcholinesterase inhibition. Due to its lipophilic nature, it binds to the phospholipid bilayer of cell membranes, disrupting their structure and promoting the production of reactive oxygen species.[24]

Permethrin, used in lice and scabies treatments, is mildly to moderately toxic like other pyrethroids.[25] This study showed negative correlations with sperm count and motility,[2627] though others did not.[282930] These discrepancies may arise from geographical differences, exposure levels, genetic responses, and analyses of urine metabolites rather than whole semen.

Seminal Carbamates were significantly higher in subjects than controls, but did not correlate with significant sperm parameters. Some studies reported a negative impact of Carbamates on these parameters,[3132] potentially due to geographic location, genetic modification, or study design differences.

The antioxidant system, comprising enzymatic (glutathione peroxidase and catalase) and nonenzymatic (Vitamins C and E) components, protects male reproductive systems from toxins such as heavy metals and pesticides. Research indicates that seminal levels of Vitamins C and E are significantly higher in controls than in subjects, correlating positively with sperm count and motility.[3334] Vitamins C and E have protective effects against cellular damage from excessive ROS by scavenging excess ROS and balancing beneficial oxidant generation with oxidative stress.[353637] In artificial reproductive technology, these vitamins improve oligospermia, asthenozoospermia, teratozoospermia, sperm DNA fragmentation, and live births. Studies indicate that oral vitamin supplementation improves sperm quality in a dose-dependent manner.

Seminal levels of glutathione peroxidase and catalase were significantly higher in controls than in subjects, positively correlating with sperm quality due to their hydrogen peroxide scavenging activities, consistent with some studies.[3839] However, another study found no correlation between glutathione peroxidase and sperm count, noting a negative correlation with sperm motility and morphology.[40]

CONCLUSION

Human exposure to various types of pesticides, including herbicides and insecticides, used in various daily activities at homes, farmlands, and agricultural storage sites negatively affect semen parameters. They are implicated in the aetiopathogenesis of male infertility, most especially in developing countries like ours. Hence, there is a need for improved regulatory measures to reduce exposure to these harmful chemicals.

Key messages

Seminal pesticide residues negatively affect sperm count and motility. They also significantly negatively impact seminal antioxidants in males with impaired sperm parameters.
