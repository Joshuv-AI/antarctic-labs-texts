Metabolomics in breast cancer: insights into treatment responses, disease progression, and prognostic assessment
Authors: Dyah L. Dewi, Elya Manik, Ema Damayanti, Muslih Anwar, Suratno, Syam Budi Iryanto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlterations in metabolic pathways are a hallmark of cancer and play a pivotal role in breast cancer development and progression. The inherent metabolic heterogeneity of breast cancer contributes to differences in therapeutic response and patients’ prognosis. Clinical metabolomics has emerged as a promising approach for identifying metabolic biomarkers that reflect tumor biology, treatment-related changes after diagnosis, and patients’ outcomes.Aims of reviewThis review summarizes the metabolomic profiles of breast cancer patients, using various biological materials and analytical methods, to assess their potential role as biomarkers for monitoring therapeutic response, adverse treatment effects, tracking disease progression, and predicting prognosis.Key scientific concept of reviewMetabolomic shifts generate unique signatures with promising potential as biomarkers for evaluating treatment response, monitoring therapeutic adverse effects, disease progression, and predicting clinical outcomes in breast cancer patients. Biological matrices, such as serum, plasma, and tumor tissue, were commonly used in both untargeted and targeted metabolomics approaches. Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry is the most commonly used analytical method in clinical metabolomics studies. Altered metabolites were identified and linked to metabolic pathways, particularly amino acids, glucose, and fatty acids metabolism. When integrated with genomic and transcriptomic data, these metabolic fingerprints offer a multidimensional perspective on disease trajectory, thereby enhancing patient stratification and informing personalized therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Breast cancer remains a major health problem, owing to its high incidence and mortality rates among women worldwide (Sung et al., 2021). While early-stage breast cancer is often successfully managed through a combination of surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, endocrine therapy, and targeted treatments, a substantial proportion of patients will eventually develop disease recurrence and progression, despite advances in therapeutic strategies (Early Breast Cancer Trialists’ Collaborative Group, 2005). One of the major contributors to these clinical challenges is molecular heterogeneity of breast cancer, which comprises several distinct subtypes, including luminal A, luminal B, human epidermal receptor (HER)2-positive, and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). Each molecular subtype exhibits distinct biological features, therapeutic responses, and recurrence risk, which critically influence treatment strategies and prognostic outcomes (Harbeck et al., 2019; Hennigs et al., 2016; Howlader et al., 2018). Consequently, although this issue has long been investigated intensively in many studies, there is still a growing need to identify reliable biomarkers that can provide clinically meaningful information after disease diagnosis, particularly for monitoring therapeutic response, assessing disease progression, predicting patient prognosis, and stratifying patients at higher risk of recurrence (Duffy et al., 2017; Freitas et al., 2021; Królewska-Daszczyńska et al., 2025; Kwa et al., 2017; Russnes et al., 2017; Zheng et al., 2022).

Among the emerging hallmarks of cancer, metabolic dysregulation has gained increasing attention in the pathogenesis of cancer that supports malignant progression (Hanahan & Weinberg, 2011). Cancer cells rewire their metabolism to sustain energy production, redox homeostasis, and biosynthesis under stressful conditions such as hypoxia and nutrient scarcity (Pavlova & Thompson, 2016). As terminal products of gene and protein activity, metabolites provide a dynamic and sensitive readout of cellular phenotypes and functions (Danzi et al., 2023). In addition to reflecting tumor cell biology, metabolites also contribute to signal transduction, immune evasion, and epigenetic modifications (Martinez-Outschoorn et al., 2017). Aberrant metabolic signaling is particularly relevant in breast cancer pathogenesis, including glucose, lipid, fatty acid (Ferraro et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2025), as well as amino acid metabolism (Liu et al. 2025b). The metabolic profiles differ across breast cancer subtypes (Budczies et al., 2013; Kanaan et al., 2014). As such, TNBC exhibits elevated levels of metabolites associated with glycolysis, the citrate cycle (TCA cycle), and β-oxidation compared with hormone receptor (HR)-positive tumors (Kanaan et al., 2014).

Metabolomics—the comprehensive analysis of small-molecule metabolites such as amino acids, lipids, organic acids, and carbohydrates—has emerged as a powerful tool for biomarker discovery. By profiling metabolites in biological specimens like plasma, serum, or tumor tissue using various analytical platforms, researchers can gain insights into tumor biology and host-tumor interactions (Holmes et al., 2008; Qiu et al., 2023). While early metabolomics study has frequently focused on breast cancer risk assessment (Füreder et al., 2026), and early detection (Jasbi et al., 2019; Song et al., 2024), increasing attention is now being directed toward the identification of post-diagnostic metabolic biomarkers that can inform patient management throughout the course of the disease. In particular, metabolomics signatures have demonstrated potential in distinguishing responders from non-responders to therapy, detecting metabolic changes associated with disease progression, and predicting recurrence or survival outcomes (Cardoso et al., 2022; Euceda et al., 2017; Giskeødegård et al., 2012a; Hart et al., 2017; Talarico et al., 2024). In this narrative review, we explore the application of metabolomics-derived signatures in clinical studies to assess therapeutic efficacy, treatment adverse effects, track disease progression, and predict prognosis or recurrence in breast cancer. By summarizing current evidence from clinical metabolomics studies and considering the influence of breast cancer molecular heterogeneity, this review aims to highlight the potential of metabolite-based biomarkers to support more precise and personalized management of breast cancer patients.

Methods

A systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed to objectively identify relevant articles for this narrative review. We included studies published between 2006 and 2025. The keywords used for the literature search were ((((“breast cancer”[Title/Abstract]) OR (“breast tumors”[Title/Abstract])) AND (“metabolites”[Title/Abstract])) AND (“metabolomics”[Title/Abstract])) NOT (“review”[Publication Type]). A flowchart depicting the literature search process is presented in Fig. 1.

Studies were included in screening if they contained the aforementioned keywords and met the following inclusion criteria: original articles on metabolomic profiling in women with various breast cancer subtypes, using human biological samples. This review encompasses clinical studies on metabolomic profiling for evaluating treatment response, therapeutic adverse effects, tracking disease progression, and prognostic assessment in breast cancer patients. The exclusion criteria were publications other than clinical studies, such as animal models, in vitro cellular studies, technical studies, in silico studies, non-peer-reviewed articles, bibliometric analysis, drug metabolism studies, and microbiome metabolism studies (Fig. 1).

Results

An initial database search yielded 445 articles. Following the application of exclusion criteria as described in Fig. 1, such as the use of in vitro or animal models, we excluded 390 studies. For this narrative review, we focused on clinical studies investigating metabolomics in therapeutic response, disease monitoring, and breast cancer prognosis (Fig. 2). A total of 53 studies met our inclusion criteria: 36 addressed metabolomics analysis for monitoring therapeutic response, 9 focused on metabolomics as prognostic markers, and 8 examined metabolomics signatures in disease progression (Fig. 1). In therapy-monitoring studies, we further refined our analysis to include only those evaluating interventions aligned with current clinical guidelines, such as systemic therapies, surgery, and radiotherapy.

The molecular classification of breast cancer plays a crucial role in guiding treatment decisions, with implications for the prognosis of patients with breast cancer. Previous studies have shown that metabolomic profiles can distinguish breast cancer subtypes based on established molecular classifications (Budczies et al., 2013; Kanaan et al., 2014). Among the studies included in this review, only 18 reported specific analyses of predefined molecular subtypes. The study population included TNBC, HER2-positive, and HR-positive breast cancer. The sample size remains crucial in metabolomics profiling, particularly in epidemiological studies (Rakusanova & Cajka, 2024; Sampson et al., 2013). Among the included studies, the sample sizes range from 8 (Zidi et al., 2021) to 699 (Hart et al., 2017) breast cancer patients. The latter came from a large international multicenter study.

Metabolomic profiling in the present clinical studies used diverse biological materials, including serum, plasma, tumor tissue, urine, and feces. Serum was the most commonly used biological sample type in metabolomic analyses of breast cancer patients, followed by plasma, tumor tissue, urine, and feces. Two studies used a combination of serum and tumor tissue for analysis. The use of plasma or serum may offer an advantage, as it profiles not only tumor metabolites but also host metabolites, potentially reflecting the global effects of treatment.

Multiple analytical platforms have been employed for metabolite detection, each offering distinct advantages in sensitivity, specificity, and metabolite coverage. Among the studies included, 41 employed untargeted approaches, 7 utilized targeted metabolomics, and 5 combined both strategies to enhance metabolite profiling. Liquid chromatography (LC)-mass spectrometry (MS) emerged as the most commonly used technique, followed by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, and gas chromatography-MS (GC-MS). Several studies employed dual-technology approaches to broaden metabolite coverage and improve data robustness, such as combining NMR and LC-MS (Wei et al., 2013), NMR and GC-MS (Asiago et al., 2010), LC-MS and GC-MS (Yang et al., 2024).

Metabolites, as the downstream products of enzymatic and protein activities, represent the final output of gene expression and are therefore more closely linked to phenotypic changes than genomic or proteomic data alone (Danzi et al., 2023). In the era of integrative multi-omics, incorporating metabolomic data with genomic, transcriptomic, epigenomic, and proteomic profiles provides a more holistic view of the biological processes driving cancer initiation and progression. Such integration enhances the understanding of complex disease mechanisms and supports the development of precision oncology strategies. Notably, two studies in our review integrated metabolomics with transcriptomics, 1 study integrated metabolomics with proteomics, and 1 study applied a multi-omics approach combining metabolomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics.

Metabolomics analysis for monitoring therapy response in breast cancer

In recent years, metabolomics has emerged as a powerful tool for monitoring therapeutic response in breast cancer, enabling real-time insight into treatment efficacy, tumor biology, and systemic metabolic adaptation. Our PubMed database search identified 38 articles reporting metabolomics profiling in the scope of breast cancer treatment. We categorized these studies into 3 purposes: metabolomics studies investigating cancer therapy-induced metabolic adaptation; metabolomics studies for monitoring treatment responses, including chemotherapy (neoadjuvant and adjuvant), endocrine therapy (ET), targeted therapy, surgery, and radiotherapy; and metabolomics studies for monitoring therapeutic adverse effects. Cancer therapy-induced metabolic adaptation

The administration of systemic cancer therapy (chemotherapy, endocrine therapy, and targeted therapy) and locoregional therapy (radiotherapy and surgery) induced metabolic perturbation that can be detected in various biological specimens (Table 1). Systemic therapy, such as chemotherapy, induced systemic metabolomic perturbations in breast cancer patients. Sun et al. reported that paclitaxel administration induced significant changes in plasma concentrations of 2-hydroxybutyrate, 3-hydroxybutyrate, pyruvate, and several amino acids within the first 24 h of treatment. Those metabolites are involved in the TCA cycle and glycolysis (Sun et al., 2018). Comparative analysis of pre-chemotherapy and post-6-8 cycles of chemotherapy serum from breast cancer patients reveals significant alterations in metabolite levels, primarily affecting pathways involved in sphingolipid and amino acid metabolism, including phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan biosynthesis (Fang et al., 2025). In line with that study, alterations in tyrosine metabolism were also observed in early breast cancer (EBC) treated with adjuvant chemotherapy, along with lysine degradation, branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) synthesis, and biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids (Lyon et al., 2023). This line of evidence indicates that chemotherapy administration induces metabolic perturbations, mainly in amino acid metabolism, as early as 24 h. In the case of targeted therapy, elevated plasma methionine levels were observed in metastatic breast cancer (MBC) patients undergoing anti-HER2 treatment (Nees et al., 2022), while McMillen et al. reported an increase in pantothenic acid, taurine, and L-histidine in early breast cancer upon treatment with trastuzumab (McMillen et al., 2023). In HR+ breast cancer treated with adjuvant endocrine therapy (AET), 17b-estradiol and cholesterol were potential biomarkers for monitoring AET response (Sheikh et al., 2025).

While most current studies focus on the application of metabolomic profiling to assess responses to systemic therapies, its use in the contexts of locoregional therapy, i.e., surgery and radiotherapy, remains comparatively underexplored. Surgical tumor resection has been shown to influence circulating metabolite levels. A GC-MS-based metabolomic analysis revealed significant postoperative reductions in plasma concentrations of saccharic acid, leucrose, gluconic acid, citramalic acid, and acetol in breast cancer patients (Beksac et al., 2023). However, as the postoperative metabolomics analysis was performed 24 h after surgery, the reduction in those metabolites might rather be due to a stress response to surgery itself than to tumor removal. An improvement in the study design, with a time-course post-surgical metabolomics analysis, will be necessary to confirm this issue. As such, a recent study performed a plasma metabolomics analysis 2 weeks post-surgery and found increases in sucrose and dodecanoic acid (Jiménez-Franco et al., 2024). The increase of sucrose levels might indicate a prolonged physiological stress response after surgery. Dodecanoic acid, also known as lauric acid, is a medium-chain saturated fatty acid that is known as an apoptosis-inducing and anti-proliferative metabolite through the induction of p21 (Lappano et al., 2017). The increased levels of dodecanoic acid post-surgery indicate systemic metabolic adaptation and recovery following tumor removal. A previous study demonstrated the potential of metabolomics for evaluating surgical margins. Elevated phosphocholine levels in tumor tissue suggest its utility as a biomarker for tumor presence, while metabolic profiling of glycine, taurine, and glucose may further aid in delineating resection boundaries (Bathen, 2013). Although limited, these initial studies might pave the way for further investigation into the application of blood-based metabolomics to determine minimal residual disease after surgery and to stratify patients who need further adjuvant treatment. In addition, there is definitely a lack of studies investigating the prognostic value of post-surgical metabolite markers for relapse.

Radiotherapy also induces metabolic reprogramming that can be detected systemically. Arenas et al. reported increased levels of pyruvate, fumarate, malate, citrate, and glutamine in breast cancer patients following surgery and radiotherapy. Significantly, radiotherapy shifted several serum metabolite levels—such as leucine, isoleucine, serine, lactate, alanine, valine, proline, malonyl-CoA, glycine, succinate, and α-ketoglutarate—toward those observed in healthy individuals, indicating a partial normalization of metabolic profiles post-treatment (Arenas et al., 2018).

All the above-mentioned studies showed that cancer treatments, including surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy, induced metabolic perturbations detectable by measuring metabolite levels in biological specimens. However, although the results are of interest from a biological perspective, such studies have limited clinical utility, as they do not address clinically relevant problems, such as therapeutic response or prognostic prediction. Further studies that delve into the roles of these metabolite markers and altered metabolic pathways that provide predictive power for prognosis and therapeutic response are necessary and will be discussed in the next section.

Metabolomics for monitoring treatment responses

Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) has become a pivotal component of breast cancer management, particularly for HR-negative subtypes, owing to its ability to reduce tumor burden and improve disease-free and overall survival among patients achieving a pathological complete response (pCR). As achieving pCR is crucial for patients’ prognosis, early prediction of NAC response is critical (Haque et al., 2018). Identification of biomarkers for NAC response is a well-studied area in breast cancer. A rigorous search for bona fide predictive markers of NAC response has been conducted, including proliferative marker Ki67, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), immune-related mRNA signatures, and immune checkpoint protein PD-L1 (Denkert et al., 2018; Derouane et al., 2022; Straver et al., 2010). Hence, metabolite markers are potentially used as the next-generation biomarkers that have predictive capacity for NAC response for each breast cancer subtype.

Several studies have explored the utility of metabolomic profiling in the pretreatment setting as a predictive marker for NAC response in patients with locally advanced breast cancer (LABC). For instance, NMR-based metabolomics has identified amino acids—leucine, formate, valine, and proline—along with HR status, as significant discriminators of NAC responsiveness (Cardoso et al., 2022). Similarly, LC-MS-based profiling of plasma samples revealed significant differences in 19 metabolites between NAC-sensitive and NAC-resistant patients. These included glycerophospholipids, fatty acyls, amino acids, and bile acids—metabolites linked to various biological pathways such as amino acid transport, MAPK/MAPK3 signaling, ERBB2 signaling, SLC transporter function, and lipoprotein metabolism, including plasma lipoprotein assembly, remodeling, and clearance (Silva et al., 2024).

Although the above-mentioned studies have identified metabolite profiles and pathways that contribute to NAC resistance, they have several limitations, including small sample sizes, a lack of regimen-specific metabolic signatures, a non-uniform indicator for NAC response, and a lack of analysis across specific breast cancer subtypes. However, a few studies have attempted to analyze the metabolomics profile for predicting NAC responses in distinct breast cancer subtypes. TNBC, HER2-positive, and luminal breast cancer studies revealed that each subtype possesses distinct metabolite perturbations that might serve as biomarkers predictive of NAC response.

In HER2-positive breast cancer, we identified 5 studies that specifically analyzed the role of systemic metabolomics analysis for NAC response, using LC-MS as an analytical method (Liu et al. 2025a; Miolo et al., 2016; Yamada et al., 2024; Zapater-Moros et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024) and plasma (Yamada et al., 2024; Zapater-Moros et al., 2023) or serum samples (Liu et al., 2025a; Miolo et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2024) as biological materials. Among those studies, 2 had a similar design, although not identical, and found increased pre-treatment serum spermidine levels in HER2-positive breast cancer patients who responded well to NAC and anti-HER2 agents (Liu et al. 2025a; Miolo et al., 2016). This indicates that the polyamine metabolism pathway plays a significant biological role in mediating treatment resistance, and spermidine might serve as a metabolite marker for predicting NAC response in HER2-positive breast cancer. This result is likely due to the role of polyamine metabolism, via spermidine, in antitumor immune response, as shown recently that intratumoral accumulation of spermidine is positively correlated with the abundance of activated CD8+ T cells (Al-Habsi et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2025). The activation of immune response, as shown by high tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), is linked with better response to NAC in HER2 + breast cancer (Ha et al., 2018). Therefore, this finding likely explains why high levels of spermidine correlate with pathologic complete response (pCR) after NAC and anti-HER2 treatment in this subtype.

In TNBC, 5 studies examined systemic metabolite signatures associated with NAC responses (He et al., 2021; Irajizad et al., 2022; Salvador-Coloma et al., 2020; Yamada et al., 2024; Zapater-Moros et al., 2023). A predictive factor for poor response was an increase in chlorokynurenine, anthranilic acid, or 3-hydroxykynurenine in pre-treatment plasma in one study (Salvador-Coloma et al., 2020), and an increase in acetylspermidine, diacetylspermidine, and diacetylspermine in pre-treatment plasma, indicating that altered tryptophan and polyamine metabolism is involved in NAC responses. These findings are particularly interesting, as both pathways are highly involved in immune responses against cancer (Bündgen et al., 2025; Fallarino et al., 2002), which correlates with NAC response. As shown in a pooled analysis of 6 randomized trials, an increase in stromal TILs, as a surrogate marker of immune response activation, predicts pCR after NAC in TNBC (Denkert et al., 2018). In addition, comparison between 2 studies that used pCR as an indicator of NAC response evaluation shows that decreased plasma level of trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), cystein S-sulfate, pipecolate, and cystine (Yamada et al., 2024) while the other study shows higher level metabolites related to long-chain fatty acid metabolism and reduced amino acid-related metabolites, mainly valine, leucine, isoleucine, and tryptophan (Zapater-Moros et al., 2023). Among these identified metabolites, a previous analysis of plasma TNBC found TMAO- a gut microbiota-produced metabolite- was enriched in immune-activated subtypes and correlates with significantly improved immunotherapy efficacy. Mechanistically, TMAO activates endoplasmic reticulum stress kinase PERK, leading to a gasdermin E-mediated pyroptosis in tumor cells and enhanced CD8+ T cell-mediated antitumor immunity (Wang et al., 2022). To this end, based on cumulative studies, the reproducibility and robustness of metabolite markers for predicting NAC response in TNBC remain elusive. There is high heterogeneity in metabolite markers identified across studies, particularly due to differences in the use of biological materials and technical methods for metabolite identification. In addition, there is a discrepancy in how the NAC response is measured and categorized, which makes comparisons between studies impossible. Nevertheless, among the metabolites significantly altered in the NAC-responsive group, i.e., polyamines and tryptophan metabolites, are those linked to immune activation, one of the established mechanisms underlying a good response to NAC.

In contrast to TNBC and HER2-positive breast cancer, the biomarkers for NAC response in HR+ breast cancer are still limited. Currently, there is no blood-based metabolomics study to predict NAC response in this subtype. However, one study explored the potential of fecal metabolites to predict NAC response, a finding that would require noninvasive sampling. An NMR-based metabolomics analysis suggested that increased levels of amino acids, including methionine, valine, alanine, and isoleucine, and reduced hypoxanthine were observed in the good responder (Zidi et al., 2021). As amino acids are highly consumed by tumor cells, a study found decreased serum levels of amino acids, including isoleucine, valine, and alanine in breast cancer patients (Eniu et al., 2019). Hence, the increased levels of excreted amino acids in the good responder group indicate a lower demand for these amino acids by the tumor due to tumor shrinkage resulting from the cytotoxic effect of chemotherapy. Although this study had a small sample size, fecal metabolite profiling shows promise as a clinical application owing to its noninvasive nature. This study further highlights the interaction between the gut microbiota and chemotherapy agents, which influences therapeutic efficacy, as shown by previous studies (Fernandes et al., 2022; Iida et al., 2013; Lehouritis et al., 2015). Taken together, across breast cancer subtypes, in general, amino acid and polyamine metabolism play an important role in predicting responses to NAC.

Metabolomics for monitoring therapeutic adverse events

Metabolomic analysis has been used to monitor adverse events associated with chemotherapy, as summarized in Table 3, such as cardiometabolic events (Lyon et al., 2023), peripheral neuropathy (Sun et al., 2018), hypersensitivity (Zhuang et al., 2022), pain and fatigue symptoms (Lyon et al., 2017), long-term neurologic and metabolic toxicity (Piffoux et al., 2024). A study revealed that levels of L-histidine, urocanic acid, myristicin, and D-aldose differed between patients who experienced hypersensitivity reactions and those who did not after doxorubicin treatment (Zhuang et al., 2022). Amino acids, including histidine, phenylalanine, and threonine, serve as predictive markers of the severity of paclitaxel-induced peripheral neuropathy (Sun et al., 2018). Both studies highlight histidine, an essential amino acid, which can be converted by histidine decarboxylase to histamine, a well-known mediator of hypersensitivity reactions upon mast cell degranulation. Increased histidine levels lead to greater histamine accumulation in mast cells, contributing to the severity of hypersensitivity reactions (Brosnan & Brosnan, 2020). In addition, histamine is also an important neuromodulator of the inflammatory process that contributes to peripheral neuropathy (Farshid et al., 2015). Another study has found that the alteration of amino acid metabolism, glyoxylate, and dicarboxylate metabolism has a predictive role in the development of long-term sensoric and motoric neuropathy, as well as metabolic toxicities in HR-positive HER2-negative breast cancer patients (Piffoux et al., 2024). Aromatase inhibitor (AI), the mainstay treatment for post-menopausal HR-positive breast cancer patients, frequently induces musculoskeletal symptoms in the long-term treatment. A plasma metabolomics study found upregulation of organic acids and downregulation of lipid derivatives and of sphingolipid signaling pathways in patients with musculoskeletal symptoms following AI treatment (Jing et al., 2025). Together, these findings indicate that pharmacometabolomics may provide valuable insights into treatment-induced adverse events, potentially enabling early intervention and informing dose-modification strategies.

In addition to systemic therapy, radiotherapy is also part of the management of breast cancer, and often causes adverse events such as skin reactions. Alterations in 13 metabolite markers, including ethanolamine, thymine, and 3-ureidopropionate, are associated with early adverse skin reactions induced by radiotherapy. Pathway enrichment analysis showed alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism as the most significantly altered for this adverse event (McMahon et al., 2024).

Metabolomics provides a dynamic reflection of phenotypic and biochemical alterations, offering a promising approach for detecting subtle metabolic reprogramming associated with progression, distant metastasis, and recurrence. Longitudinal metabolomic profiling enables early detection of biochemical changes preceding clinical manifestations, thereby offering a window of opportunity for timely therapeutic interventions.

A study using LC-MS and GC-MS revealed a dynamic shift in the plasma metabolic signatures across disease stages. In early-stage disease (stage I), carbohydrate metabolism predominates, whereas in stage II, glycerophospholipid metabolism becomes more prominent, with decreased lysophosphatidylcholine and increased phosphatidylcholine, indicating disrupted lipid remodeling. In addition, the concurrent rise in non-lipid metabolites, such as threonic acid and glucuronic acids, further underscores extensive metabolic reprogramming and highlights their potential as biomarkers for disease monitoring (León-Carreño et al., 2025).

As the disease progresses to distant metastasis, metabolic perturbation occurs in comparison to early breast cancer as an adaptive mechanism against cellular stress during the metastasis process. An NMR-based metabolomic study reported higher concentrations of acetoacetate, 3-hydroxybutyrate, glycerol, N-acetylglycoproteins, pyruvate, lipids, phenylalanine, glutamate, and mannose in the serum of MBC patients than in that of early breast cancer (EBC) patients (Jobard et al., 2014). Another study comparing serum metabolomics profiles found higher concentrations of lactate, formate, choline, phenylalanine, glutamate, and glycine in MBC patients than in EBC patients (Hart et al., 2017). Together, these studies highlight increased levels of phenylalanine and glutamate in MBC patients. The role of phenylalanine in metastasis is supported by a study showing that low phenylalanine levels suppress lung metastasis in a mouse melanoma model (Elstad et al., 1990). Glutamate, a product of glutaminolysis, plays a critical role in metastasis by serving as a precursor for the antioxidant glutathione, thereby reducing oxidative stress. High levels of glutamine consumption and activation of the system Xc-antiporter, which facilitates glutamate export, promote metastasis. Extracellular glutamate disrupts the basolateral membrane and promotes invasion via the GRM3 metabotropic glutamate receptor (Dornier et al., 2017).

Previous studies indicate that metastatic site-specific metabolic alterations occur in breast cancer (Wang et al., 2021). In cases involving brain metastasis, plasma metabolomic analysis reveals significant alterations in several metabolic pathways, including aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, arginine biosynthesis, and the metabolism of valine, leucine, isoleucine, glycine, serine, threonine, alanine, aspartate, and glutamate (Özer et al., 2022). A set of 15 metabolites serves as predictors of brain metastasis, with an accuracy of 96.9% (Özer et al., 2022).

Breast cancer patients frequently experience relapse, necessitating treatment modifications over the course of the disease. Therefore, sensitive tools for monitoring disease progression and recurrence are essential for early identification of patients at risk. Early studies established the feasibility of using serum metabolite profiling to detect recurrence. For instance, patients with recurrent breast cancer exhibited significantly lower serum levels of metabolites such as formate, histidine, proline, choline, glutamic acid, N-acetylglycine, and 3-hydroxy-2-methylbutanoic acid compared with non-recurrent patients (Asiago et al., 2010). Pathway analysis revealed significant alterations in branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) metabolism—specifically the degradation of valine, leucine, and isoleucine—in recurrent cases (Yang et al., 2024). Additionally, a study analyzing preoperative serum from HR-positive EBC patients identified a metabolite signature that served as an independent prognostic indicator of recurrence, irrespective of established clinicopathological factors. Patients who developed recurrence had elevated levels of valine, leucine, isoleucine, choline, phenylalanine, histidine, glycine, tyrosine, and lactate, as well as reduced glutamate levels (Hart et al., 2017). These 2 studies indicate the critical role of BCAA metabolism in the recurrence of breast cancer. BCAAs are essential amino acids that function as a carbon source for protein, nucleotides, and fatty acid synthesis and are necessary for cell proliferation. BCAAs are metabolized by branched-chain amino transferase (BCATs) into branched-chain ketoacids, which fuel the TCA cycle for ATP production (Wang et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2023). In addition, BCAAs activate mTOR signaling, promoting cell proliferation and tumor growth. Previous studies indicate that valine promotes cell cycle progression via translational regulation of cyclin D2 (Yamauchi et al., 2026). These mechanisms support the findings that elevated valine in pretreatment serum and plasma correlates with recurrence.

Metabolomics for prognosis evaluation in breast cancer

Prognostic assessment based on clinicopathological characteristics and molecular profiles has been extensively investigated in breast cancer patients. Several studies have explored the potential use of metabolomics profiling to predict the prognosis of breast cancer patients (Table 5). Tissue metabolomics study combined with unsupervised machine learning had successfully stratified breast cancer subtypes into three distinct prognostic groups, underscoring the role of metabolic dysregulation in disease progression (Gal et al., 2020). In addition, the 5-year survival prediction for ER-positive patients using metabolite profiles proved more precise and reliable than those based on traditional clinical factors (Giskeødegård et al., 2012a).

Tissue-based metabolomics further supports the prognostic relevance of metabolic alterations. Elevated levels of glycine and lactate in tumor tissue have been associated with lower survival rates in breast cancer patients. They are considered potential biomarkers for prognosis in ER-positive breast cancer patients, but not in ER-negative patients (Giskeødegård et al., 2012a). The correlation between lower tissue glycine levels and good prognosis was also confirmed in another study (Sitter et al., 2010). Another important biomarker for prognostic studies comes from NMR analysis of serum, which identified elevated lactate and pyruvate as associated with reduced relapse-free survival, breast cancer-specific survival, and overall survival (OS) in ER-positive patients (Lende et al., 2019). These studies highlight the correlation between increased lactate levels in the tumor tissue and in the serum with poor prognosis of ER-positive breast cancer (Giskeødegård et al., 2012a; Lende et al., 2019). This result was also reproduced in other studies regardless of breast cancer subtype (Madssen et al., 2019). High lactate levels reflect enhanced glycolysis and aggressive tumor metabolism, hallmarks of rapidly proliferating cancer cells. Accumulated lactate contributes to an acidic tumor microenvironment that promotes tumor invasion (Liu et al., 2024). It also stimulates angiogenesis by activating hypoxia-related signaling pathways, facilitating tumor growth and metastasis (Fan et al., 2024). In addition, lactate suppresses antitumor immune responses by inhibiting cytotoxic T cells and natural killer cells (Angelin et al., 2017; Husain et al., 2013). Lactate-rich, hypoxic tumor regions are also less responsive to radiotherapy and certain chemotherapies. This metabolic environment enhances tumor cell survival and contributes to treatment resistance. Moreover, lactate regulates gene expression through post-translational modification such as lactylation, promoting tumor progression (Wu et al., 2026). Collectively, these effects explain the link between elevated lactate levels in tumors with advanced disease, therapy resistance, and poor prognosis.

Another class of metabolites identified in plasma-based metabolomic analyses for prognosis prediction was glycochenodeoxycholate, a primary bile acid. Glycochenodeoxycholate was positively associated with improved survival outcomes in breast cancer patients. Notably, levels of glycochenodeoxycholate and other bile acids were inversely correlated with tumor proliferation scores and with the expression of cell-cycle and G2/M checkpoint genes (Tang et al., 2019). The polyamine metabolism pathway also has a significant role in breast cancer prognosis. In the TNBC subtype, elevated plasma diacetylspermine (DAS), a catabolic byproduct of spermine regulated by spermine synthase, was detected. Higher plasma DAS levels in TNBC were associated with an increased risk of metastasis and poorer OS (Fahrmann et al., 2020).

Challenges and future perspectives

Metabolomics studies have been explored to aid in monitoring therapeutic responses, disease progression, and prognosis of breast cancer patients. We have discussed in the above section that, in general, we observed across studies that dynamic changes in amino acid metabolism were profound in breast cancer, contributing to tumor progression, affecting therapy response, and patients’ prognosis. Although there is broad variation in the specific alterations to amino acid metabolism, a general pattern of reprogramming of amino acid metabolism is consistently observed across different subtypes, analytical platforms, and biological matrices, warranting further attention in future studies. Reprogramming of amino acid metabolism has emerged as a cornerstone of tumorigenesis and malignant progression (Lieu et al., 2020; Liu et al. 2025b). In addition to their role as proteogenic building blocks, amino acids serve as indispensable signaling molecules and precursors for energy production, nucleotide synthesis, redox homeostasis, and epigenetic regulation (Phang et al., 2013; Rabinovich et al., 2015; Takahara et al., 2020). To sustain these high-demand processes, cancer cells increase amino acid flux by upregulating specific amino acid transporters and altering enzymes involved in amino acid synthesis and degradation (van Geldermalsen et al., 2016). In addition to amino acid metabolism, polyamine metabolism, glycolysis, and bile acid metabolism warrant further investigation in future studies for their contributions to tumor progression.

Although the studies discussed in this review increasingly support the predictive value of metabolomics for breast cancer treatment and outcome monitoring, several challenges remain, particularly for clinical metabolomics studies. Clinical metabolomics studies often report heterogeneous findings. The major contributor to this variability is the lack of standardization across analytical platforms, treatment protocols, and response evaluation criteria. These methodological differences limit the comparability of results across studies and complicate the identification of consistent metabolic biomarkers.

First, differences in analytical platforms substantially influence metabolite detection and quantification. The most commonly used techniques are LC-MS, NMR, and GC-MS, which vary in sensitivity, metabolite coverage, and quantification accuracy (Zamboni et al., 2015). NMR offers simple sample preparation and provides highly reproducible, quantitative measurements, but it detects relatively few metabolites due to its lower sensitivity. In contrast, MS-based platforms offer greater sensitivity and broader metabolite coverage but are more susceptible to variability arising from sample preparation, ionization efficiency, instrument calibration, and data processing pipelines. Within MS-based studies, differences in chromatographic methods, ionization modes, and metabolite annotation databases can lead to inconsistent metabolite identification and quantification, thereby limiting cross-study comparison (Dona et al., 2016; Letertre et al., 2021; Tsedilin et al., 2015). There are also issues related to sampling time, storage, waiting time between blood drawn to actual metabolomics analysis, blood matrices, extraction methods, column type that might influence the results of identified metabolites (Emwas et al., 2025; Floegel et al., 2011; Guerra et al., 2026; Sampson et al., 2013; Wallace et al., 2010; Xiao et al., 2014). The substantial number of unannotated metabolites remains a significant challenge for the untargeted approach (Xu & Zhu, 2025).

Second, heterogeneity in treatment regimens further contributes to variability in metabolomics profiling. In breast cancer, clinical metabolomics studies often include patients receiving different therapeutic modalities such as chemotherapy, targeted therapy, radiotherapy, and surgery, and often a combination of those. Each treatment type induced distinct alterations in metabolites through different mechanisms of action, including effects on tumor metabolism, immune modulation, and systemic host responses. Additionally, variation in treatment regimens, doses, durations, the timing of sample collection relative to therapy stages, and prior treatment history can significantly influence metabolic signatures. This complexity has a significant impact on metabolomics biomarkers for prognosis prediction. As a result, metabolomics patterns associated with treatment response in one cohort may not be directly comparable to those observed in another study involving different therapeutic strategies. We observed that numerous studies have addressed metabolite markers for predicting NAC response; however, it remains difficult to identify reproducible and comparable markers, as chemotherapy regimens and cycles vary across studies. Most studies have pooled multiple chemotherapy regimens, thereby limiting metabolomics’ ability to distinguish drug-specific effects. In this direction, addressing regimen-specific metabolomic signatures of neoadjuvant chemotherapy will be essential in the future.

Third, inconsistencies in response definition and clinical endpoints represent another important source of variability. Studies frequently classify patient outcomes using different criteria, such as pathologic complete response and residual cancer burden to evaluate NAC response, and overall survival and progression-free survival as surrogates for prognosis. Even when a standardized framework, such as Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST), is used, variation may still occur in how responders and non-responders are grouped (e.g., complete/partial response vs. stable/progressive disease. These differences in outcome classification affect how metabolomic data are interpreted and can lead to inconsistent identification of metabolite biomarkers across studies.

In addition to the above-mentioned factors, several studies used small sample sizes, warranting further validation. Large, multi-institutional prospective trials are needed for this purpose. Collectively, these factors introduce substantial methodological heterogeneity in clinical metabolomics research. Without harmonization of analytical techniques, standardized treatment stratification, and consistent response criteria, it remains challenging to directly compare findings across studies or to validate metabolite biomarkers in independent cohorts. A carefully designed study with strict inclusion criteria will be necessary in the future to have a clear comparison between treatments. Future efforts towards methodological standardization, transparent reporting of experimental workflows, and multicenter validation studies are essential to improve reproducibility and facilitate the translation of metabolomics-based biomarkers in clinical practice.

Most studies have investigated the metabolomics profile to predict response to NAC; however, there is a general scarcity of studies addressing its use as a marker in ET resistance. As HR-positive is the most common breast cancer subtype, and ET resistance also occurs in a large segment of patients, the potential use of metabolomics for the prediction of ET resistance should be investigated. As metabolomics is inherently dynamic, a longitudinal study with repeated sampling during treatment seems essential for monitoring both treatment efficacy and side effects.

As molecular subtypes generally affect baseline metabolomics profiles, the specific metabolic adaptations of breast cancer subtypes must be considered in analyses, which are often not incorporated. A critical limitation of current literature is the frequent lack of subtype-specific metabolic signatures, despite the established molecular heterogeneity of breast cancer. Elucidating how metabolic profiles vary across subtypes will be key to advancing personalized diagnostics and treatment strategies.

One of the greatest strengths of metabolomics lies in its ability to detect early biochemical perturbations, often preceding changes identified by conventional biomarkers or imaging modalities. However, isolating disease-specific metabolic alterations remains a major challenge, given the influence of confounding factors such as diet, comorbidities, physical activity, exposure to exogenous chemicals, smoking, and therapy-induced systemic effects—issues that are often insufficiently addressed in many current studies. Hence, future studies need to incorporate information on such confounding factors obtained through a thorough questionnaire.

Several studies performed untargeted metabolomics, resulting in the profiling of metabolite shifts that often lack mechanistic follow-up. Integration with other omics data, such as genomics, transcriptomics, epigenomics, and proteomics, is rarely performed in the field. Omics integration strategies may provide mechanistic insights into underlying biological phenomena and improve biomarker precision. Although metabolomics holds significant promise as a non-invasive tool for monitoring treatment response, disease progression, and long-term outcomes, its successful translation into clinical oncology will depend on strategic integration with clinical workflows, robust biomarker validation, and mechanistic clarity.

Conclusion

This review highlights a spectrum of metabolite alterations and pathway disruptions with potential as biomarkers for evaluating therapeutic response, monitoring adverse treatment effects, tracking disease progression, and predicting prognosis in breast cancer. Biological matrices such as serum, plasma, and tissue each offer distinct advantages, but their relative utility in prognostic and predictive applications warrants systematic head-to-head comparison in future research. Metabolomic shifts generate unique signatures with promising potential as biomarkers for evaluating treatment response, detecting toxicity, monitoring disease progression, and predicting clinical outcomes. When integrated with genomic and transcriptomic data, these metabolic fingerprints offer a multidimensional perspective on disease trajectory, thereby enhancing patient stratification and informing personalized therapeutic strategies.
