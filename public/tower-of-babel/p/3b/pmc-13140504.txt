Perinatal outcomes of expectant management versus fetal reduction in triplet pregnancies: A single‐center retrospective cohort study in Korea
Authors: Yoo Jin Lee, Jin Hoon Chung, Jae Yi Jeong, Jihye Koh, Mi‐Young Lee, Hye‐Sung Won
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWe aimed to evaluate perinatal outcomes in triplet pregnancies and compare outcomes between pregnancies managed expectantly and those undergoing fetal reduction.Material and MethodsThis retrospective study included triplet pregnancies confirmed by ultrasound at Asan Medical Center between January 2020 and May 2025. Pregnancies were classified into an expectant management group or a fetal reduction group (reduction to twins or a singleton). Maternal characteristics, obstetric complications, and perinatal outcomes were compared. Within the fetal reduction group, outcomes were further analyzed by indication—multifetal pregnancy reduction versus selective fetal termination—and method—intracardiac injection versus radiofrequency ablation.ResultsA total of 208 triplet pregnancies were included; 133 (63.9%) were managed expectantly, and 75 (36.1%) underwent fetal reduction. In the expectant management group, the mean gestational age at delivery was 33.2 weeks, with preterm birth before 32 weeks in 21.6%, and neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission in 76.7%. Fetal loss of at least one fetus before 24 weeks occurred in 7.8%, and the incidence of nonviable pregnancy loss was 2.3%. Compared with expectant management, the fetal reduction group demonstrated more favorable perinatal outcomes, including higher Apgar scores and lower rates of oxygen therapy (18.4% vs. 50.2%, p < 0.0001) and NICU admission (34.6% vs. 76.7%, p < 0.0001). No nonviable pregnancy loss or stillbirth occurred after reduction, whereas both were observed in the expectant group. Survival rates were similarly high in both groups (100% vs. 98.4%). Within the reduction group, outcomes did not differ significantly by indication or procedure type.ConclusionsTriplet pregnancies carry significant maternal and neonatal risks. Fetal reduction appears safe and may improve perinatal outcomes by extending gestation. These findings individualized prenatal counseling and management strategies for triplet pregnancies.

Key message

Triplet gestations remain vulnerable to early loss and preterm birth. Appropriately timed fetal reduction improved perinatal outcomes in our cohort. These results strengthen evidence for individualized management strategies.

INTRODUCTION

As the rate of assisted reproductive technologies (ART), such as in vitro fertilization, increases and maternal age rises, multifetal pregnancies have become more common worldwide. In Korea, while the overall birth rate continues to decline, the proportion of multiple births has steadily increased from 3.5% in 2014 to 5.5% in 2023. 1 Multifetal pregnancies are associated with increased risks not only for infants—such as prematurity and cerebral palsy—but also for mothers, with higher rates of obstetric complications. 2 The rate of cerebral palsy per 1000 live births increases from 1.6 in singleton pregnancies to 28 in triplet pregnancies, and infant mortality rises from 5.4 to 52.5 per 1000 live births. 3

In response to these risks, fetal reduction procedures, including multifetal pregnancy reduction (MFPR) for reducing the number of fetuses and selective termination for confirmed congenital or chromosomal abnormalities, are increasingly being considered, while clinicians must navigate ethical, religious, and social concerns.

Beginning in the late 1990s, numerous publications reported perinatal outcomes before and after fetal reduction, and by the early 2000s, several series reported improvements in fetal loss and early prematurity after fetal reduction. 4 , 5 Subsequent studies examined pregnancy loss, 6 , 7 and both single‐center and large multicenter series have demonstrated that overall pregnancy loss rates after MFPR have declined with increasing operator experience, stabilizing at approximately 4%–6%. 5 , 6 , 7 Research has also highlighted the need to evaluate neonatal survival and long‐term outcomes. 8 A 2010 review noted advances in the expectant management of triplet pregnancies with acceptable perinatal outcomes but also concluded that elective reduction to twins is associated with a lower risk of preterm birth without an increased risk of miscarriage. 9

The existing literature has addressed not only the potential benefits of fetal reduction but also the procedure‐related risks, including overall pregnancy loss. However, controversy remains regarding the optimal management of triplet gestations. Accordingly, evidence‐based clinical guidance and patient counseling on the management and outcomes of triplet pregnancies have become increasingly important.

In this study, we aimed to evaluate perinatal outcomes of triplet pregnancies and compare outcomes between pregnancies managed expectantly and those undergoing fetal reduction.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Study design

We retrospectively reviewed pregnant women with triplet pregnancies confirmed by ultrasound at Asan Medical Center between January 2020 and May 2025. Cases in which fetal reduction was performed, and the pregnancy was subsequently maintained as a twin or singleton, were also included. Patients who had not delivered at the time of analysis or were lost to follow‐up before the second trimester, with no subsequent data available at our institution, were excluded.

We collected the following baseline characteristics: maternal age, height, body mass index (BMI), parity, history of preterm delivery, method of conception, presence of hypertensive diseases, pregestational diabetes, gestational diabetes mellitus, type of chorionicity, and use of invasive diagnostic tests, such as chorionic villus sampling (CVS) and amniocentesis. Perinatal outcomes included gestational age at delivery, delayed‐interval delivery, mode of delivery, procedure‐to‐delivery interval in cases undergoing intervention, and delivery before 28, 30, or 32 weeks of gestation. We defined unintended intrauterine death of at least one fetus before 24 weeks of gestation, as well as the loss of all fetuses before 24 weeks, as nonviable pregnancy loss. We also collected stillbirths occurring after 24 weeks. We recorded delivery‐related and obstetric complications, including McDonald cerclage, admission due to incompetent internal os of the cervix, preterm labor (PTL), and preterm pre‐labor rupture of membranes (PPROM), hypertensive complications, including preeclampsia and superimposed preeclampsia, and postpartum hemorrhage requiring ≥4 units of transfusion or surgical or nonsurgical intervention. Neonatal outcomes included sex, birth weight, 1‐ and 5‐min Apgar scores, need for oxygen therapy within the first 24 h after birth, admission to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU), and survival at 28 days of life.

For cases undergoing fetal reduction, we additionally collected the following data: gestational age at the time of the procedure, type of procedure, indication for the procedure, medications used, need for repeat procedures, amnioreduction, duration of hospitalization, and any adverse events within 2 weeks post‐procedure, including PPROM, chorioamnionitis, intrauterine fetal demise, and PTL.

All cases were assessed to determine whether fetal reduction had been performed. Cases in which all three fetuses were maintained without reduction were classified as the expectant management group, while those that underwent fetal reduction were classified as the fetal reduction group. Outcomes were compared between the two groups. Further sub‐analyses were conducted according to the indication and method of the procedure, comparing multifetal pregnancy reduction vs. selective fetal termination, and intracardiac injection vs. radiofrequency ablation (RFA). This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Asan Medical Center (IRB number: 2025‐0872).

Clinical practice and protocols for fetal reduction

Fetal reduction was classified into two categories based on the indication: Multifetal pregnancy reduction and Selective Fetal Termination. Multifetal pregnancy reductio was performed to reduce the number of fetuses in a multifetal pregnancy, whereas selective fetal termination was performed when a structural anomaly or chromosomal abnormality was identified in one fetus.

Although the indications differ, the procedural techniques were largely similar. In pregnancies without monochorionic (MC) twins, intracardiac injection of lidocaine or potassium chloride (KCl) was performed using a 21‐gauge needle under ultrasound guidance. In pregnancies involving MC twins, RFA was performed using a 15‐gauge LeVeen™ Needle Electrode (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA, USA) with a total length of 12 cm and a 2‐cm shunt to prevent hemodynamic complications in the co‐twin caused by placental vascular anastomoses.

Peri‐procedural management was standardized. Patients were admitted on the day of the procedure, maintained fasting prior to intervention, and underwent fetal reduction under continuous ultrasound guidance. Follow‐up ultrasound examinations were performed on the day of the procedure and on the following day to assess the remaining fetal status and procedure‐related complications, after which patients were discharged if clinically stable.

In this study, when a triplet pregnancy was diagnosed by ultrasound at the initial hospital visit, serum‐based screening tests were not routinely performed. First‐trimester screening was primarily based on nuchal translucency measurement, with invasive diagnostic testing offered when indicated. Following diagnosis of a triplet pregnancy, patients received counseling regarding the potential complications and obstetric outcomes associated with expectant management and fetal reduction. Management decisions were based on medical considerations and the patients' informed preferences. When fetal reduction was selected, genetic diagnostic testing, such as CVS, was recommended. CVS was routinely performed according to chorionicity, with one sampling in MC triplet pregnancies, two in dichorionic triplet pregnancies, and three in trichorionic triplet pregnancies. Genetic testing results and ultrasound findings were reviewed to determine the target fetus. If no structural or chromosomal abnormality was identified, reduction was performed on the fetus in the most accessible and technically safe position.

Statistical analysis

SAS software for Windows, version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA), was used for statistical analysis. Percentages were calculated based on available cases for each variable, and denominators differed because of missing data. Variables were organized to analyze case characteristics. Categorical variables were compared using the chi‐squared test and Fisher's exact test, and continuous variables were compared using the two‐sample t‐test or Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. Subgroup analyses were performed by dividing cases into the “intracardiac injection” and “RFA” groups, and comparisons were made between each subgroup and the expectant management group. An additional subgroup analysis divided cases into the Multifetal Pregnancy Reduction and Selective Fetal Termination groups. A two‐sided p‐value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Natural course and outcomes in the EM group

Of the 208 triplet pregnancies, 133 (63.9%) were managed expectantly without fetal reduction. In the expectant management group, the mean maternal age was 35.1 years, and only 5.3% of cases were spontaneous pregnancies; the remaining 94.7% conceived through ART. The most common types of triplet pregnancies were dichorionic triamniotic (DCTA) pregnancies (48.9%) and trichorionic triamniotic (TCTA) pregnancies (45.9%) (Table 1).

Early fetal loss before 24 weeks, involving the death of at least one fetus, occurred in 10 cases (7.8%). Among these, three pregnancies (2.3%) resulted in nonviable pregnancy loss due to the demise of all three fetuses. Two cases were delivered following PPROM at 22 and 17 weeks of gestation, and one case was delivered at 22 weeks due to spontaneous PTL. Among the remaining seven cases, intrauterine fetal death (IUFD) of one or two fetuses occurred between 14 and 21 weeks of gestation in five cases. Two cases involved the intrauterine demise of two MC diamniotic twin fetuses, resulting in continuation as singleton pregnancies. Excluding the five IUFD cases, two pregnancies delivered one fetus first, following PPROM. Stillbirth after 24 weeks occurred in two cases: one at 24 weeks and one at 31 weeks of gestation (Table 2). The detailed antenatal course and outcomes in these 10 cases are summarized in Table S1.

Expectant management versus fetal reduction

A comparative analysis was conducted between 133 cases in the expectant management group and 75 in the fetal reduction group. Maternal characteristics were generally comparable between the two groups, with no statistically significant differences except for BMI, the incidence of maternal diabetic disorders, and the rate of CVS performed before the procedure. CVS was conducted in 82.7% of cases in the fetal reduction group, which was significantly higher than that in the expectant management group (Table 1).

Fetal reduction was performed at a mean gestational age of 16 weeks. The mean duration of hospital stay following the procedure was 2.8 days. Procedure‐related complications within 2 weeks after fetal reduction occurred in 4 of 75 pregnancies (5.3%). These complications included PPROM in two cases and PTL requiring treatment in two cases. No cases of chorioamnionitis were observed. Neither PPROM nor PTL resulted in fetal demise (Table 4). No cases of unexpected fetal loss or stillbirth were observed in the fetal reduction group. No cases of nonviable pregnancy loss occurred in the fetal reduction group, whereas such losses occurred in 2.3% of cases in the expectant management group. The incidence of preterm birth before 32 weeks of gestation was 21.6% (24/111) in the expectant management group and 11.4% (5/44) in the fetal reduction group. However, because of the significant proportion of missing data for this outcome, these findings should be interpreted with caution (Table 3).

The mean birth weight of neonates was higher in the fetal reduction group (2157.0 ± 529.5 g) than in the expectant management group (1816.5 ± 439.4 g, p < 0.0001). The 1‐ and 5‐min Apgar scores were also significantly higher in the fetal reduction group (7.9 ± 1.4 and 9.0 ± 0.9, respectively) than in the expectant management group (7.1 ± 1.8 and 8.3 ± 1.5, respectively; p < 0.0001 for both). The proportion of neonates requiring oxygen therapy within the first 24 h after delivery was significantly lower in the fetal reduction group (18.4%) than in the expectant management group (50.2%, p < 0.0001). The rate of NICU admission was significantly lower in the fetal reduction group (34.6%) than in the expectant management group (76.7%, p < 0.0001). Survival rates were high in both groups, with no statistically significant difference (98.4% in expectant management vs. 100% in fetal reduction, p = 0.5770).

Results of subanalysis

Within the fetal reduction group, outcomes were compared between procedures performed using intracardiac injection (n = 49) and RFA (n = 26) (Figure 2). The mean gestational age at the time of the procedure was slightly earlier in the intracardiac injection group (15.9 ± 4.2 weeks) than in the RFA group (16.3 ± 1.0 weeks); however, the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.5029) (Table 4). No significant differences were observed between the two groups in the need for reintervention or duration of hospital stay. Notably, while no cases of PPROM occurred in the intracardiac injection group, two cases (7.7%) occurred in the RFA group within 2 weeks post‐procedure; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.1111).

Another subgroup analysis was performed within the fetal reduction group, comparing 53 cases of multifetal pregnancy reduction and 22 cases of selective fetal termination (Table 5). The mean gestational age at the time of the procedure was significantly earlier in the multifetal pregnancy reduction group (15.2 ± 3.0 weeks) than in the selective fetal termination group (18.0 ± 3.8 weeks, p = 0.0008). Despite this difference in timing, no statistically significant differences were observed in adverse events within 2 weeks post‐procedure between the two groups.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we analyzed the natural course of triplet pregnancies according to management strategy. Among triplet pregnancies managed expectantly, the rate of fetal loss involving at least one fetus before 24 weeks was 7.8%, and the incidence of nonviable pregnancy loss was 2.3%. The mean gestational age at delivery was approximately 33.2 weeks, and postpartum hemorrhage occurred in 18.7% of cases. In contrast, pregnancies that underwent fetal reduction demonstrated overall more favorable perinatal outcomes. The procedure‐related complication rate was approximately 5%, and no cases of nonviable pregnancy loss occurred before 24 weeks of gestation. Notably, neonatal outcomes were better in the fetal reduction group, with significantly lower rates of NICU admission and oxygen requirement. These findings support the safety and efficacy of fetal reduction when appropriately indicated.

In the expectant management cases, the rate of nonviable pregnancy loss before 24 weeks was slightly higher (2.3% vs. 1.1%) than the rate reported in a recent cohort. Compared with that report, which noted single and double IUFD rates of 18.2% and 9.1%, respectively, after 24 weeks of gestation, the stillbirth rate of 1.9% in our cohort suggests a relatively favorable outcome. 10 Several studies have reported fetal loss rates before 24 weeks ranging from 7.3% to 13.3%. 8 , 11 , 12 A 2015 systematic review also reported a pregnancy loss rate of 9.3%. 13 Another, more recent systematic review reported a higher pregnancy loss rate, further highlighting the comparatively lower rate observed in our study. 14 These findings are relatively favorable compared with previous reports; however, the risk of pregnancy loss remains a clinically significant concern in the expectant management of triplet pregnancies.

Regarding preterm birth, the rate of delivery before 32 weeks in the expectant management group was 21.6%, which is somewhat lower than previous reports showing rates of 26%–27.9%. 10 , 15 Compared with data from the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, which reported a mean gestational age at delivery of 31.7 weeks for triplet pregnancies, our cohort delivered at a slightly later mean gestational age of 33.0 weeks. 3 These findings may partly reflect advancements in perinatal care over time, contributing to improved outcomes in more recent cohorts. The rate of preterm birth before 32 weeks in the expectant management group was slightly lower than previously reported rates; however, it still represents a significant risk. This underscores the persistent challenge of reducing preterm birth in triplet pregnancies, despite progressive improvements in obstetric and neonatal care.

Regarding fetal reduction, the overall timing of the procedure was approximately 16.0 weeks of gestation, with the multifetal pregnancy reduction subgroup showing a mean gestational age of 15.2 weeks. The timing of fetal reduction in this cohort occurred relatively later than the 10+0 to 13+6 weeks generally recommended in earlier literature. 14 , 16 This delay likely resulted from the high frequency of CVS performed before the procedure, which allowed fetal selection based on comprehensive genetic and structural evaluation. Despite the advanced gestational age at the time of the procedure, no cases of fetal loss occurred before 24 weeks of gestation, and the overall rate of procedure‐related complications remained low. However, the absence of pregnancy loss following fetal reduction in this cohort should be interpreted with caution and does not necessarily indicate a protective effect of the procedure itself. Rather, this observation may reflect certain factors such as performance at an experienced fetal therapy center, careful patient selection, standardized procedural techniques, and close post‐procedural surveillance. In addition, the relatively small number of cases in the fetal reduction group raises the possibility that this finding may, at least in part, be attributable to chance. Therefore, these results should not be generalized and warrant confirmation in larger, multicenter studies.

Subgroup analyses by indication and method of fetal reduction showed broadly consistent outcomes, with a low incidence of post‐procedural complications across subgroups. Neonatal outcomes appeared favorable across different approaches. These findings are consistent with the notion that fetal reduction, when appropriately indicated and carefully selected, may be considered as a management option to optimize outcomes in triplet pregnancies.

Neonatal outcomes appeared favorable across different approaches; however, these findings should also be interpreted cautiously, given the limited sample sizes. Taken together, the subgroup results are consistent with the notion that fetal reduction, when appropriately indicated and performed in experienced centers, may be considered as one of the management options in selected triplet pregnancies.

Notably, most triplet pregnancies in this cohort resulted from ART. Although fetal reduction may mitigate some risks associated with higher‐order multiple pregnancies, prevention remains the most effective strategy. Ongoing efforts to promote single embryo transfer and to limit the number of embryos transferred during ART cycles are therefore essential to reduce the incidence of triplet pregnancies.

This study has certain limitations. First, not all women delivered at the same tertiary center where the fetal reduction was performed. Therefore, missing outcome data for some variables may limit the accuracy of the findings, and this factor should be considered when interpreting the results. Second, the relatively small sample size may reduce the statistical power of the analysis because the data were collected retrospectively, and some cases were lost to follow‐up, resulting in a limited number of cases included in each analysis.

Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable data on recent outcomes of triplet pregnancies in Korea and offers important insight into perinatal outcomes following fetal reduction. Moreover, as this study included MC triplet pregnancies in which the reduction was performed using RFA, it uniquely encompasses outcomes across different reduction techniques, adding to its strength and novelty. Outcomes in the natural course of triplet pregnancies appear to be improving with advances in perinatal care; however, the risk of fetal loss remains. Fetal reduction, when appropriately indicated and performed at the optimal time, may prolong gestation and improve neonatal outcomes. These findings support fetal reduction as a viable management option and provide a foundation for individualized counseling in the management of triplet pregnancies.

CONCLUSION

Triplet pregnancies are inherently associated with significant maternal and neonatal risks, even under expectant management. Our findings highlight that adverse outcomes, including early fetal loss, preterm birth, and peripartum complications, remain frequent in the natural course of triplet gestations. Fetal reduction, when considered in the context of triplet pregnancies, appears to be a relatively safe procedure and may be discussed as a management option in selected cases. It has the potential to prolong gestation and improve neonatal outcomes. These results underscore the importance of individualized counseling and a personalized prenatal approach when managing triplet pregnancies.
