Diversity, metabolic potential and global distribution of the anaerobic fermentative bacteria Phylum Candidatus Cloacimonadota
Authors: Yi Liu, Min Yu, Xing Chen, Lingman Ran, Xiao-Hua Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe phylum Candidatus Cloacimonadota (formerly known as Cloacimonetes, WWE 1) is a group of strictly anaerobic organisms that frequently associated with engineering and wastewater systems. At present, it cannot be cultured using traditional cultivation methods, and the taxonomic position within this phylum remains unclear, with only one class, Candidatus Cloacimonadia. Furthermore, the diversity and metabolic characteristics of Candidatus Cloacimonadota members in marine environments have yet to be explored. Therefore, the taxonomy and metabolism of the phylum Candidatus Cloacimonadota require further investigation.ResultsIn this study, six high-quality metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) of Candidatus Cloacimonadota were acquired from the anoxic zone of the Yongle Blue Hole (YBH), potentially representing new taxa. Additionally, 483 Candidatus Cloacimonadota genomes from global databases were downloaded, and all genomes were analyzed and compared. Candidatus Cloacimonadota is widely distributed across diverse environments worldwide, and its class, Candidatus Cloacimonadia, can be divided into two clades, Clade A and Clade B, the latter of which contains six YBH-derived MAGs. The Clade A and Clade B showed distinct genomic features, metabolic strategies and evolutionary histories, which are associated with their environments. For instance, they employ different anaerobic respiratory pathways: Clade B utilizes heterodisulfide reductase (HdrABC)–[NiFe]-hydrogenase (MvhADG) complex (NiFe/MvhADG-HdrABC), while Clade A utilizes Hnd/FeFe Group A3 hydrogenase complex for hydrogen utilization. Furthermore, YBH-derived MAGs have unique metabolic genes, such as those encoding chitinase and α-galactosidase, and the chitinase activity in MAG213-F140 from YBH was confirmed by heterologous expression. Divergence time analysis revealed that YBH-derived MAGs diverged around 3.36 million years ago.ConclusionThis study enhances the understanding of the diversity, metabolic potential, and global distribution of Candidatus Cloacimonadota. We found this phylum could be divided into Clades A and B, revealing significant differences in genetic traits and metabolic capabilities between the two clades, and focusing on their ecological roles in marine environments. Moreover, this research holds substantial value for the development and utilization of marine resources, as well as for advancing the understanding of biogeochemical cycles, further highlighting the crucial role of microorganisms in these key ecological processes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40793-025-00796-1.

Introduction

Microorganisms are crucial components of ecosystems and play a significant role in global biogeochemical cycles. Among them, anaerobic microorganisms distributed across various global anoxic environments are essential to ecosystem functioning. However, most anaerobic microorganisms are difficult to culture, and cannot be obtained through traditional pure culture methods. Recently, advancements in high-throughput sequencing technologies have revolutionized our understanding of anaerobic microbiology [1]. Gene-based surveys and metagenomic data from environmental samples have greatly expanded the tree of life and transformed our understanding of biogeochemical cycles in various environments [2]. For example, these methods have led to an expansion of the tree of life, as evidenced by the discovery of new phyla such as the Minisyncoccota (Patescibacteriota) and Promethearchaeota (Asgardarchaeota) [3–5].

Marine blue holes are submerged caves typically formed by carbonate karst processes, and often found near coastlines [6–9]. They are characterized by their deep blue appearance at the surface waters [8]. Marine blue holes have been discovered worldwide, and include the Yongle Blue Hole (YBH) [10, 11] (depth 300.89 m), Bahamas Blue Hole [12] (depth 202.08 m), Dahab Blue Hole in Egypt [13, 14] (depth 131.06 m), Amberjack Blue Hole and Jewfish Blue Hole in the Gulf of Mexico [15, 16], and the Great Blue Hole in Belize [17, 18] (depth 124.05 m).

Among these blue holes, YBH, which is located in the Yongle Atoll of the South China Sea, and is the deepest blue hole globally with a depth reaching up to 300 m [19]. Due to its remote location, human activities have minimal impact on the internal environment of the YBH. The water column of YBH exhibits characteristics of multi-level, large gradients and high heterogeneity, displaying distinct gradients from oxic to hypoxic to essentially anoxic and completely anoxic zones [20]. The water column in YBH is semi-enclosed, with only the uppermost few meters exchanging with the external environment. This limited exchange creates a relatively stable anoxic layer within YBH, maintaining its pristine state. Consequently, YBH serves as an ideal habitat for studying anaerobic microbial communities and associated metabolic processes. Previous studies have indicated that the diversity of anaerobic bacteria was higher in completely anoxic zones [21], with Pseudomonadales being dominant, particularly at depths of 130–170 m and 270 m [20]. At the genus level, Thiomicrospira and Arcobacter were significant contributors to the microbial community within the anoxic regions of YBH [20]. Besides, our previous investigations found that the relative abundance of Candidatus Cloacimonadota was approximately 0.02% of the total microbial community in the anoxic zones of YBH. However, the diversity and distribution of Candidatus Cloacimonadota were rarely studied, as well as their unique metabolic potentials and ecological functions in the anoxic water layers of YBH.

Candidatus Cloacimonadota is a strictly anaerobic phylum whose habitats mainly include engineered systems, such as wastewater and sludge bioreactors, as well as the gastrointestinal tracts of animals [22–28]. However, members of Candidatus Cloacimonadota have rarely been detected in marine environments. Currently, representatives of this phylum cannot be cultured using any existing culture methods, and only one class, Candidatus Cloacimonadia, has been classified. Previous studies [29, 30] have reported that certain environmental representatives of Candidatus Cloacimonadota participate in acetogenesis through flavin-based electron bifurcation, mixed fermentation and anaerobic respiration. However, the taxonomic status, metabolic characteristics and ecological function of this bacterial phylum remain to be thoroughly explored.

In this study, we acquired six MAGs of Candidatus Cloacimonadota bacteria at water depths of 120, 140, and 170 m in the completely anoxic zone of the YBH employing metagenome sequencing and analysis. By collecting genomes of Candidatus Cloacimonadota from various samples worldwide, combining the phylogenetic construction, geographic distribution and functional annotation, we aim to gain deeper insights into the phylogeny, ecological distribution and evolutionary history of this phylum, and assess the physiological potential of different clades within Candidatus Cloacimonadota. Unique physiological functions and ecological significance of bacteria in Candidatus Cloacimonadota from the YBH were also investigated. This study will provide a comprehensive understanding of the role of Candidatus Cloacimonadota in global biogeochemical cycles.

Materials and methods

Sampling

Seawater samples were collected from depths ranging from 0 to 190 m at YBH on October 24–25 in 2019 using Niskin samplers (8 L, Keruiou, Tianjin) deployed via a winch mounted on the platform aboard Qiong Qionghai 03138 at the outer reef slope of Yongle Atoll [31]. Sampling covered various oxygen concentration zones in the YBH water column, ranging from oxic, hypoxic, essentially anoxic to completely anoxic conditions. Hypoxic refers to significantly reduced oxygen levels, with small amounts of oxygen still present but generally insufficient to support the metabolic demands of most aerobic organisms (e.g., 50–85 m in YBH, 20 μM < Dissolved Oxygen (DO) < 100 μM). Essentially anoxic denotes extremely low oxygen concentrations near the detection limit but has not yet reached completely anoxic conditions (e.g., 90–115 m in YBH, DO below detection limit, NO2− > 0, NO3− > 0, H2S > 0) [32]. The sampling intervals were set at 5 or 10 m; seawater samples were then sequentially filtered through 3 μm (TSTP, 142 mm, Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) and 0.22 μm (GTTP, 142 mm, Millipore, MA, USA) polycarbonate membranes. Consequently, microbial communities retained on the 3 μm and 0.22 μm filters were classified as particle-associated (PA) and free-living fractions (FL), respectively. Metagenomic analysis was conducted on a total of 14 samples, comprising free-living and particle-associated fractions from each of the depths at 0, 30, 50, 90, 120, 140 and 170 m. Each sample was labeled according to its fraction size and depth of collection. The specific sample labeling and detailed preservation methods have been described in previous studies [32].

DNA extraction, metagenomic assembly and binning, genome collection

Total DNA was extracted from the 3 μm and 0.22 μm membranes using a DNA Isolation Kit (Qiagen, Germany), following the procedure outlined by Liu et al. [33]. Metagenomic raw reads were quality controlled through clipping off primers and adapters, filtered low-quality reads and trimmed by Trimmomatic v3.6 [34]. Clean reads from each sample were independently assembled using MegaHit v1.1.2 after initial filtering [35]. Metagenome binning was conducted using MetaBAT v2.14 [36] and MaxBin v2.2.7 [37], followed by merging and refinement using MetaWRAP v1.2 [38]. Metagenomic reads were then remapped to each metagenome-assembled genome (MAG) and reassembled using SPAdes v3.11.1 [39] in careful mode to enhance the quality of MAGs. Finally, the completeness and contamination of MAGs were assessed using CheckM v1.0.8 [40]. Ultimately, we obtained six high-quality MAGs belonging to the phylum Candidatus Cloacimonadota.

Genome collection and phylogenetic analysis

Twelve sets of metagenomic data with high-abundance of Candidatus Cloacimonadota from different environments around the world were downloaded from the sandpiper website (https://sandpiper.qut.edu.au/). Each metagenome was assembled using metaWRAP v1.2 to obtain MAGs of Candidatus Cloacimonadota. Also, we downloaded all the genomes of the phylum Candidatus Cloacimonadota from the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) [41]. Thus, 489 genomes of Candidatus Cloacimonadota from various environments were collected, including 6 MAGs obtained from YBH in this study, 19 MAGs assembled from the data downloaded from the Sandpiper website, and 464 reference genomes from NCBI. The genomic annotations were performed using Prokka v1.14.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/prokka) [42]. After manual inspection, 85 genomes with complete 16S rRNA gene sequences were identified. By aligning the complete 16S rRNA sequence with that of the reference genome Candidatus Cloacimonas acidaminovorans strain Evry (accession number: GCA_000146065.1) from the Candidatus Cloacimonadota, 48 genomes with > 80% sequence similarity were selected. These sequences were then aligned using MAFFT v7.487 [43], with Fermentibacterota (GCA_002030045.1) designated as the outgroup. The results of the alignment were trimmed with TrimAl v1.2 [44], and the maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree was constructed using IQ-TREE v1.6.12 with the following parameters: -m MFP -bb 1000 -alrt 1000 -redo -nt. Additionally, the phylogenetic tree based on 120 single copy marker genes obtained using GTDB-Tk v1.5.1 (https://gtdb.ecogenomic.org/) [45] was constructed using the same method with IQ-TREE v1.6.12. The parameters were: -m LG + C60 + F + R -bb 1000 -nt bnni -alrt 1000. Furthermore, orthologous genes were identified using OrthoFinder v2.3.10 across the 48 Candidatus Cloacimonadota genomes and the outgroup genome (Fermentibacterota, GCA_002030045.1) [46]. Then, 15 orthologous groups of ribosomal proteins were aligned and concatenated for phylogenetic analysis, including L2, L3, L4, L5, L6, L14, L16, L18, L22, L24, S3, S8, S10, S17 and S19. The ML tree was constructed by using IQ-TREE v1.6.12 with the following parameters: -bb 1000 -alrt 1000 -m MFP -madd LG + C10,LG + C20,LG + C20,LG + C30,LG + C40,LG + C50,LG + C60,C10,C20,C30,C40,C50,C60 –score-diff ALL -T AUTO –threads-max 20. The three phylogenetic trees were uploaded to the interactive tree viewer ChiPlot (https://www.chiplot.online/) [47] for visualization.

The 48 genomes mentioned above, together with an additional four Candidatus Cloacimonadota genomes (lacking complete 16S rRNA sequences) that are phylogenetically close to the YBH-derived genomes (Additional file 1: Table S1), were assessed for completeness and contamination using CheckM v1.0.5 [40]. Only MAGs with completeness > 80% and contamination < 10% were used for subsequent phylogenetic and metabolic analyses. However, two genomes, GB_GCA_000402615.1-SCGC AAA252-E13 and GB_GCA_000493925.1-JGI OTU-1, which exhibit 100% completeness, were retained for analysis despite contamination levels > 10%. Ultimately, 40 MAGs, including 19 from engineered environments, 3 from cold seep, 4 from marine sediments, 3 from brackish water, 2 from saline lake waters, 1 from Black Sea saline water, 6 from YBH, and 2 from unknown sources, were selected for downstream analyses. The genomic features and environmental origins of these MAGs were based on genome data downloaded from NCBI and sampling locations (Additional file 1: Tables S1). In addition, to ensure the accuracy of the classification within the phylum Candidatus Cloacimonadota, the aforementioned 48 MAGs containing complete 16S rRNA gene sequences were selected for analysis using average amino acid identity (AAI), average nucleotide identity (ANI), and digital DNA–DNA hybridization (dDDH). The AAI values of these MAGs was calculated using EZAAI v1.2.1 [48]. The ANI was calculated using pyANI [49]. The dDDH values were calculated using the Genome-to-Genome Distance Calculator 3.0 (https://ggdc.dsmz.de/ggdc.php). Finally, the AAI/ANI/dDDH matrix was constructed using TBtools [50].

We selected the 40 Candidatus Cloacimonadota genomes with > 80% completeness, along with Shewanella, Gloeobacter, Escherichia coli and Salmonella enterica as reference strains for divergence time calibration. A ML phylogenetic tree was initially constructed using IQ-TREE v1.6.12, followed by calibration of this tree to estimate divergence times through the penalized likelihood method implemented in r8s v1.81 [51]. The estimated origin time of cyanobacteria (2700–3500 million years ago, MYA) [52] and the divergence time between E. coli and S. enterica (140 MYA) [53] were used as calibration points. The reference ML tree was constructed based on 10 single-copy core orthologous genes shared by the 44 bacterial species mentioned above. The optimal smoothing parameters were determined through a cross-validation procedure involving the pruning of terminal branches.

Metabolic annotations

To explore the metabolic differences among bacteria of varied taxa status and origins within the Candidatus Cloacimonadota phylum, the predicted gene files of 40 genomes were uploaded to the KEGG Automatic Annotation Server (KAAS) [54] for functional annotation. The parameters utilized by KAAS included GHOSTX, a custom genome dataset, and the BBH (bi-directional best hit) assignment method. Additionally, the annotation results were validated using the BLAST tool within UniProt (https://www.uniprot.org/blast), with default parameter settings. Carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) of the 40 genomes were annotated using dbCAN3 [55] against database v9 [56] under default settings. CAZymes includes glycoside hydrolases (GHs), carbohydrate esterases (CEs), polysaccharide lyases (PLs), glycosyltransferases (GTs) and carbohydrate-binding modules (CBMs). Peptidases were annotated via BLAST searches against the MEROPS database [57] using an e-value cutoff of 1e − 5, with identification and coverage both > 30%.

Reconstruction of gene gain and loss events

Using the phylogenetic gain–loss-duplication model implemented in the COUNT [58], the ancestral gene content and the events of gene gain and loss during the evolution of Candidatus Cloacimonadota were reconstructed. This reconstruction considered the number of paralogs for each identified gene family across all genomes, with the topology of the core genome tree serving as the reference tree. The function of gained and lost genes during evolution was annotated using the eggNOG-mapper online annotation server (http://eggnog-mapper.embl.de/).

Chitinase expression

To confirm the activity of chitinase in the Candidatus Cloacimonadota, the chitinase gene predicted in the MAG213-F140 isolated from the YBH environment was chemically synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology (https://www.tsingke.com.cn). The pET-28a (+) vector containing the chitinase gene was obtained, as described by Ran et al. [59]. This was then transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) for protein expression [59]. Chitinase expression was performed following the method described by Tang et al. [60] with some modification. Briefly, recombinant cells were grown in LB medium supplemented with kanamycin (50 μg/mL) at 37 °C until they reached the logarithmic phase (OD600 nm = 0.4–0.6). Protein expression was induced at 16 °C by the addition of 0.1 mM IPTG for 12 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 8000 g for 10 min and resuspended in binding buffer (20 mM Tris, 50 mM NaCl, pH = 8.0). The suspension was sonicated on ice, and then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min to separate the bacterial lysate from the pellet. The crude enzymes was analyzed using 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) following Laemmli's method [61].

To test the chitinase activity of the recombinant protein, 1% (w/v) colloidal chitin was used as the substrate, which was prepared from shrimp chitin powder (Sigma-Aldrich) according to He et al. [62]. Chitinase activity was measured using a modified method based on Lee et al. [63]. After incubating 150 μL of substrate at 50 °C for 30 min, 50 μL of crude enzymes were added, and the mixture was further incubated at 50 °C for 1 h. The release of reducing sugars was quantified using a modified dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) method [64]. The previously reported chitinase Chi4963 was selected as the positive control [59], and the empty pET-28a ( +) vector was used as the negative control.

Global distribution

Using Sandpiper (https://sandpiper.qut.edu.au), the samples from global metagenomic datasets where the abundance of Candidatus Cloacimonadota exceeded 1% were collected. The latitude and longitude coordinates for each sample were obtained using Kingfisher (https://github.com/onevcat/Kingfisher), and the global distribution of Candidatus Cloacimonadota was compiled in Additional file 1: Table S2.

Results and discussion

Reclassification of the class Candidatus Cloacimonadia within the phylum Candidatus Cloacimonadota into two clades

The YBH is recognized as the deepest blue hole in the world [32], and the water body of YBH is characterized by multi-layered structure, significant gradients, and high heterogeneity. At a depth of 150 m, the water enters a stable and fully anoxic condition, with anoxic layers extending to a thickness of 150 m, and an age exceeding 6,000 years. This makes it the thickest and most long-term stable anoxic seawater environment in the global oceans to date. This unique ecological environment inevitably leads to the evolution of specialized microorganisms and mediates distinctive biogeochemical cycles [20]. In our previous study, a total of 391 MAGs from YBH with completeness > 50% and contamination < 10% were acquired through metagenomic assembly and binning. Among them, six high-quality genomes (completeness ranging from 97.8 to 98.9%), belonging to Candidatus Cloacimonadota, were recovered from depths of 120, 140, and 170 m in the anoxic zone of YBH. The genome sizes of these MAGs were relatively small, ranging from 2.70 to 3.00 Mbp, and their G + C contents ranged from 38.50% to 38.70% (Additional file 1: Table S1). These MAGs shared a relatively high dDDH, ANI, and AAI values, ranging from 99.9% to 100%, 99.9% to 100%, and 99.9% to 100% (Additional file 1: Table S3), respectively. Therefore, they were classified as the same species. After gene prediction and annotation, only the genome MAG213-F140 contains a complete 16S rRNA gene sequence, which showed a similarity of 82.33% to the 16S rRNA sequence of the reference genome Candidatus Cloacimonas acidaminovorans strain Evry within the class Candidatus Cloacimonadia of the phylum Candidatus Cloacimonadota (Additional file 1: Table S4). This suggests that these 6 MAGs may represent a novel taxon within the phylum Candidatus Cloacimonadota.

To further investigate the phylogenetic relationships within Candidatus Cloacimonadota, we obtained 48 MAGs from diverse global habitats that contain complete 16S rRNA gene sequences and share more than 80% similarity with Candidatus Cloacimonas acidaminovorans strain Evry. The phylogenetic tree based on 16S rRNA gene sequences of these MAGs was constructed and it may be divided into two clusters with high bootstrap support and named cluster A and B (Fig. 1A). Cluster A included genomes that has been previously identified as the class Candidatus Cloacimonadia in the phylum Candidatus Cloacimonadota, with the Candidatus Cloacimonas acidaminovorans strain Evry as the reference genome [29]. The similarities of 16S rRNA gene sequences in genomes of cluster B with the Candidatus Cloacimonas acidaminovorans strain Evry were relatively low, around 82% to 83% (Additional file 1: Table S4). Furthermore, the phylogenetic trees constructed based on 120 single copy marker genes identified by GTDB-Tk v1.5.1 (Fig. 1B) and the concatenated sequences of 15 ribosomal proteins (Additional file 2: Fig. S1) were consistent with the phylogenetic tree based on 16S rRNA gene sequences. To ensure the accuracy of the classification within the phylum Candidatus Cloacimonadota, the aforementioned 48 MAGs containing complete 16S rRNA gene sequences and exhibiting > 80% similarity to the Candidatus Cloacimonas acidaminovorans strain Evry were selected for AAI and dDDH analyses. The AAI values within the Cluster A ranged from 54.72 to 100%, whereas that within Cluster B ranged from 57.42 to 100%, respectively. The AAI values between all genomes in Cluster B and the Candidatus Cloacimonas acidaminovorans strain Evry in Cluster A ranged from 51.86 to 53.97%. Similarly, the values between all genomes in Cluster A and MAG213-F140 ranged from 51.81 to 55.15%, respectively (Additional file 1: Table S5). The results of the dDDH analysis are presented in Additional file 1: Table S6. Furthermore, we classified the aforementioned 48 genomes using GTDB-Tk, and the results indicated that all genomes belong to the phylum Candidatus Cloacimonadota within the class Candidatus Cloacimonadia (Additional file 1: Table S7). Notably, the 6 MAGs from YBH were assigned to the previously described genus Ca. Celaenobacter [30]. These 6 MAGs are closely related to Ca. Celaenobacter polaris within the genus; however, the 16S rRNA sequence similarity and AAI value between MAG213-F140 and Ca. Celaenobacter polaris are relatively low, being 98.586% and 82.413% (Additional file 1: Table S5 and S8), we propose naming these 6 MAGs from YBH as a new species within the genus Ca. Celaenobacter, tentatively named Candidatus Celaenobacter longdongensis. Finally, based on all the aforementioned analyses, we suggest dividing the genomes under the class Candidatus Cloacimonadia of Candidatus Cloacimonadota into two clades according to the distribution in the phylogenetic tree, named Clade A and Clade B. For subsequent metabolic analyses, we selected the representative genome of Clade A as Candidatus Cloacimonas acidaminovorans strain Evry and the representative genome of Clade B as MAG213-F140.

The name Candidatus Celaenobacter longdongensis is derived from the Latinized genus name Celaenobacter, which reflects its phylogenetic affiliation within the genus, combined with the Latinized term longdongensis, referring to “Longdong” (Yongle Blue Hole, Sansha, South China Sea), indicating the bacterium’s ecological and geographic origin from this anoxic marine cave.

Genomic characteristics and habitat of Candidatus Cloacimonadota

Genomic characteristics of MAGs from the two clades within the class Candidatus Cloacimonadia exhibit minimal differences. Genome sizes of the Clade B range from 0.99 Mbp to 3.17 Mbp, with G + C content ranging from 33.37% to 38.80% and the predicted gene number ranging from 2090 to 2918. Most genomes in the Clade B contain around 2200 predicted genes whereas BS750m-G26c contains only 956 predicted genes. The completeness of the genome BS750m-G26c is 82.42%, with a contamination level of 0.06%. The number of coding sequences (CDSs) may be affected by the completeness and contamination levels of the MAG. In contrast, genome sizes of the Clade A are 1.75 Mbp to 3.34 Mbp, and the G + C content and the predicted gene number ranges from 34.32% to 55.65% and 1418 to 2585, respectively. Most genomes in the Clade A contain approximately 2000 predicted genes (Additional file 1: Table S1). Despite the relatively small differences in general genomic features, the environmental sources of the two clades exhibit significant divergence. Global distribution analysis of Candidatus Cloacimonadota reveals that this phylum is broadly distributed across terrestrial and marine ecosystems (Additional file 1: Table S2). However, bacteria in the Clade B were predominantly found in natural environments, such as blue holes, cold springs and brackish waters, whereas representatives of the Clade A were primarily found in artificial environments, including anaerobic digesters, wastewater, biogas fermenters and leachate wells. These differences in source environments have likely driven the phylogenetic divergence between Clade A and B, possibly due to the diverse influences exerted by distinct environmental conditions on bacterial communities [65], leading the two clades to evolve in distinct directions and ultimately develop adaptations suited to their respective environments.

Rudimentary respiratory machinery

Most of genomes within the phylum Candidatus Cloacimonadota encode a relatively complete glycolytic pathway. In 95% (38/40) of the genomes, pyruvate orthophosphate dikinase (PPDK) functionally replaces or complements the role of pyruvate kinase (PK) (Additional file 1: Table S9). Notably, MAGs from YBH and their closely related phylogenetic counterparts (e.g., GCA_030765445.1-MAG_3300035698_1703 and GCA_030765865.1-MAG_3300025698_8) harbor genes encoding both PK and PPDK. In contrast, all members of Candidatus Cloacimonadota generally exhibit incomplete tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycles, with an average completeness of approximately 12%. MAGs from YBH, as well as MAG_3300035698_1703 and MAG_3300025698_8, display significantly higher completeness of TCA cycle, reaching up to 50%. Additionally, citrate synthase is found only in MAG_3300035698_1703 and MAG_3300025698_8, while isocitrate dehydrogenase is predominantly present in members of the Clade B, with an estimated coverage of 74% (14/19). Genes associated with the reductive TCA (rTCA) cycle are not widely distributed across either clade of Candidatus Cloacimonadota.

Members of the phylum Candidatus Cloacimonadota encode a simple respiratory electron transport chain. All the genomes contain partial electron transport chains, which are incomplete with key subunits of complexes I, II, III, and IV either lacking or missing. For the complex V, Clade B possesses a complete F-type ATP synthase (Figs. 2, 3e), whereas Clade A harbors a partial V/A-type ATPase. All Candidatus Cloacimonadota genomes possess the full complement of genes encoding the Rnf electron transfer complex (rnfABCDEG). In addition, members of Clade B encode a NiFe/MvhADG-HdrABC complex (Figs. 2, 3e). This complex is complete in 58% (11/19) of the Clade B MAGs, including all MAGs from YBH. Each member of the Clade A encodes either a complete or partial Hnd/FeFe Group A3 hydrogenase complex (Fig. 3e). Both types of complexes are localized in the cytosol [66, 67]. The NiFe/MvhADG-HdrABC complex in the Clade B MAGs includes both [NiFe] hydrogenase and heterodisulfide reductase (Hdr) as catalytic components. It belongs to the family of flavin-containing cytoplasmic multienzyme complexes, which have been reported in certain anaerobic bacteria and archaea [66]. Under conditions of ≤ 100 kPa, this complex can catalyze the reduction of low-potential iron–sulfur proteins using high-potential electron donors such as NAD(P)H or H2 [66]. Moreover, it has been shown that HdrABC functions in conjunction with the hydrogenase MvhADG to couple an endergonic H2-dependent reduction reaction (reduction of oxidized ferredoxin, Fd) with an exergonic H2-dependent reduction of CoM-S–S-CoB, a process known as flavin-based electron bifurcation (FBEB) [68–70].

The Hnd/FeFe Group A3 hydrogenase complex is a tetrameric enzyme composed of hndA, hndB, hndC, and hndD subunits. It belongs to Group A3 of the FeFe hydrogenase classification [71], which encompasses hydrogenases capable of electron bifurcation. These multimeric electron-bifurcating FeFe hydrogenases couple the oxidation of H2 with the simultaneous reduction of NAD+ and ferredoxin. Some studies have demonstrated that these enzymes can also operate in reverse, confurcating electrons from NADH and reduced ferredoxin to generate H2 [72–74]. Therefore, both the NiFe/MvhADG-HdrABC complex and the Hnd/FeFe Group A3 hydrogenase complex are involved in energy metabolism through FBEB. FBEB is considered a third mechanism of energy conservation in addition to substrate-level phosphorylation (SLP) and electron transport phosphorylation (ETP). FBEB is predominantly found in anaerobic prokaryotes and functions by coupling an endergonic redox reaction with an exergonic one [75, 76].

Previous studies [77] proposed that the Rnf complex (Fig. 2) and ATP synthase together may constitute a simplified two-step respiratory chain system. In this system, the Rnf complex catalyzes the oxidation of ferredoxin and transfers electrons to NAD+, a process coupled with the translocation of Na+ ions across the membrane, thereby generating a sodium ion gradient. The potential energy stored in this gradient is subsequently utilized by the F1F0 ATP synthase to drive ATP synthesis. The oxidized ferredoxin can be regenerated via pyruvate-ferredoxin/flavodoxin oxidoreductase or pyruvate ferredoxin oxidoreductase, or alternatively through reduction by the NiFe/MvhADG-HdrABC complex or the Hnd/FeFe group A3 hydrogenase complex. Notably, pyruvate-ferredoxin/flavodoxin oxidoreductase is widely distributed among Candidatus Cloacimonadota genomes (95%, 38/40), whereas pyruvate ferredoxin oxidoreductase is predominantly found in Clade B, particularly those derived from the YBH.

Although a complete ETC with defined terminal electron acceptors such as nitrate, sulfate, or metal ions was not identified, our findings further support the role of the Rnf complex in Candidatus Cloacimonadota, consistent with previous studies [29, 78]. H2 is widely considered a primordial electron donor that supported energy metabolism during early microbial evolution [79]. As key enzymes in microbial hydrogen metabolism [71], hydrogenases exhibit functional diversity. Although both the NiFe-type MvhADG and the Hnd/FeFe Group A3 hydrogenase complexes are capable of utilizing H2, they differ markedly in phylogenetic classification and specific function. This distinction suggests that the two clades within Candidatus Cloacimonadota may have evolved divergent metabolic strategies to adapt to distinct environmental conditions.

Substrate fermentation

Candidatus Cloacimonadota bacteria are members of a strictly anaerobic phylum that derive energy primarily through the fermentation of organic substrates [30]. In this study, the fermentation substrates of this phylum were analyzed (Fig. 3d, Additional file 1: Table S9). Clade B and Clade A both possess genes fdoG (K00123), korA (K00174), korB (K00175), por (K03737), and acyP (K01512). fdoG is the catalytic subunit of formate dehydrogenase O (EcFDH-O), which is composed of three subunits, fdoG, fdoH, and fdoI, forming a membrane-bound enzyme complex [80]. Within this complex, fdoG and fdoH assemble into a dimer oriented toward the cytoplasmic side, while fdoH is anchored to the cytoplasmic membrane via its C-terminal hydrophobic region, and fdoI associates with them [81]. The fdoG gene encodes the catalytic subunit of formate dehydrogenase, which functions as the core component responsible for catalyzing the oxidation of formate to CO2 with concomitant electron release. fdoH mediates the transfer of electrons generated by fdoG to the transmembrane subunit fdoI, which anchors the entire enzyme complex to the cytoplasmic membrane and subsequently transfers the electrons to the membrane-bound respiratory chain, thereby accomplishing energy conversion. Notably, in some anaerobic bacteria (e.g., sulfate reducing bacteria and Syntrophobacter fumaroxidans), formate dehydrogenase exist in soluble form and are thought to mediate electron transfer in the periplasm through interactions with diverse periplasmic electron carrier proteins [82]. The subunit composition is relatively simple, consisting of either a single catalytic subunit or a catalytic subunit in combination with one to two electron-transferring subunits. In the genomes of Candidatus Cloacimonadota, only fdoG has been identified, suggesting that Candidatus Cloacimonadota may also utilize formate in a manner analogous to that observed in sulfate reducing bacteria. The korA encodes the catalytic subunit of 2-oxoglutarate:ferredoxin oxidoreductase, catalyzing the decarboxylation of 2-oxoglutarate to succinyl-CoA while transferring electrons to ferredoxin. The por encodes pyruvate-ferredoxin/flavodoxin oxidoreductase, which catalyzes the decarboxylation of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA and CO2, concomitantly transferring electrons to either ferredoxin or flavodoxin. The acyP encodes acylphosphatase, which catalyzes the hydrolysis of acyl-phosphate compounds, such as acetyl phosphate, producing acetate and Pi. Candidatus Cloacimonadota may use these enzymes to ferment formate, 2-oxoglutarate, and pyruvate, thereby enhancing their growth in anaerobic environments.

Compared to Clade A, Clade B also possesses genes porA (K00169), porB (K00170), porD (K00171), porG (K00172), which encode subunits of pyruvate ferredoxin oxidoreductase. Notably, both pyruvate ferredoxin oxidoreductase and the aforementioned pyruvate-ferredoxin/flavodoxin oxidoreductase can catalyze pyruvate degradation. The presence of these two types of enzymes in Clade B indicates its enhanced capacity for pyruvate degradation. Additionally, Clade B has the gene acs (K01895) (79%, 15/19), which encodes acetyl-CoA synthetase. The enzyme is bifunctional and catalyzes the reversible conversion between acetate and acetyl-CoA. The acetyl-CoA produced by pyruvate cleavage is converted to acetate by this enzyme, coupled with ATP generation. Through this way, Clade B may also obtain energy. In contrast, Clade A possesses genes ackA (K00925) and pta (K00625), which encode acetate kinase and phosphate acetyltransferase [30]. The acetate kinase catalyzes the formation of acetyl phosphate. This intermediate may be utilized by phosphate acetyltransferase in subsequent reactions to generate acetyl-CoA or could be used in substrate-level phosphorylation to produce ATP, thereby supplying energy to the bacteria. Members of the phylum Candidatus Cloacimonadota encode multiple types of ferredoxin oxidoreductases, which are primarily involved in the fermentation of amino acids [83]. Candidatus Cloacimonadota are predicted to utilize a broad range of amino acid substrates, including threonine, aspartate, glutamate, cysteine, glutamine, and glycine (Fig. 2 and Fig. 3b). Detailed metabolic information is provided in Additional file 9.

Additionally, members of the phylum Candidatus Cloacimonadota encode several enzymes involved in propionate oxidation, including methylmalonyl-CoA mutase, succinyl-CoA synthetase (Clade B, 15/19), succinate dehydrogenase/fumarate reductase (Clade B, 3/19), and malate dehydrogenase (oxaloacetate-decarboxylating). Under low hydrogen partial pressure, the oxidation of propionate to acetate and CO2 is thermodynamically favorable [30, 83]. Previous studies have proposed that Candidatus Cloacimonadota member may engage in syntrophic associations with other syntrophic bacteria [83]. Moreover, acetate and hydrogen are known to benefit hydrogenotrophic and acetoclastic methanogens; Candidatus Cloacimonadota may be metabolically linked to both methanogens and Desulfobacterota [30]. Desulfobacterota encode H2-uptake [NiFe]-hydrogenases, enabling efficient hydrogen consumption [84]. In the completely anoxic zones of YBH, microbial community diversity markedly increases and Desulfobacterota are relatively abundant [21]. These features may suggest that Candidatus Cloacimonadota members in YBH likely establish metabolically interdependent relationships with surrounding microbial taxa.

Overall, compared to Clade A, Clade B (particularly the MAGs from YBH) possesses a greater number of genes associated with fermentation substrates, indicating a higher efficiency in utilizing these substrates. This may facilitate their adaptation to nutrient-limited marine environments such as YBH.

Degradation of macromolecular organic compounds

It has been reported that the phylum Candidatus Cloacimonadota possesses the capability to degrade carbohydrates [29]. In this study, we analyzed carbohydrate-degrading enzymes encoded by this phylum and identified 89 potential CAZymes, which are categorized into GHs, CEs, PLs, GTs, and CBMs (Additional file 2: Fig. S2A–B, Figs. 3a and 4, Additional file 1: Table S10). Among these, GH family enzymes were the most abundant, while other families were relatively less represented. Although there are few differences between the clades A and B in terms of the total number of enzymes, each clade possesses a distinct set of carbohydrate-degrading enzymes. For example, Clade A possess AA6, GH133, and GH57, which are absent in the Clade B. Enzymes in family AA6 work synergistically with lignocellulose-degrading enzymes, such as lignin peroxidases and laccases, to enhance lignocellulose breakdown. The GH57 family exhibits diverse enzymatic activities [85], including α-amylase, 4-α-glucanotransferase, α-glucan branching enzyme, amylopullulanase, dual-specificity amylopullulanase–cyclomaltodextrinase, non-specified amylase, maltogenic amylase, and α-galactosidase. The sequence annotated as GH57 from the genome of Candidatus Cloacimonas acidaminovorans strain Evry was identified as α-amylase using KEGG and UniProt. Therefore, the primary function of the GH57 family in Clade A is likely α-amylase activity. In contrast, Clade B, particularly MAGs derived from YBH, exhibit a remarkable advantage in carbohydrate degradation capabilities. These MAGs encode several unique enzymes, such as GH13_3 and GH13_4 (MAGs from YBH and MAG_3300025698_8), which encode α-amylases involved in starch degradation. Similar to the GH57, the GH65 family (68%, 13/19) also displays diverse enzymatic functions, encompassing various α-glycoside hydrolases, phosphorylases, and α-glycosyltransferases [86]. Notable examples include maltose phosphorylase and branched-dextran exo-1,2-α-glucosidase. These enzymes typically act on glucose residues and their derivatives, such as trehalose, and are capable of degrading glycosidic linkages including α-1,1, α-1,2, α-1,3, α-1,4, and α-1,6 types. Additionally, GH16_3 (MAGs from YBH and GCA_014382685.1-NIOZ-UU3) exhibits activities including MLG endo-β-1,3/1,4-glucosidase and glucan endo-β-1,3-glucosidase, whereas GH30_1 (MAGs from YBH and NIOZ-UU3) is associated with β-glucosidase and ceramide β-glucosidase activities, contributing to the degradation of xylan, chitosan, and algal polysaccharides. GH158 (MAGs from YBH) encodes glucan endo-β-1,3-glucosidase, which is involved in the degradation of various polysaccharides. CBM26, CBM48, CBM5, and CBM9 are associated with enzymes that hydrolyze cellulose, β-1,3-glucans, starch and glycogen, chitosan or chitin, and lignocellulose, respectively. Compared to Clade A, the collective action of these enzymes suggests that Clade B may possesses the capacity to degrade not only polysaccharides such as starch, but also more complex carbohydrates including trehalose and cellulose. The degradation of these macromolecules produces monosaccharides such as glucose, which can be converted to pyruvate via the glycolytic pathway. Pyruvate is then converted to acetyl-CoA by pyruvate ferredoxin oxidoreductase or pyruvate-ferredoxin/flavodoxin oxidoreductase. Subsequently, acetyl-CoA is converted to acetate, releasing energy that may be utilized by Clade B.

Based on the annotation analysis, we hypothesized that all six MAGs derived from YBH may be capable of degrading various macromolecular organic compounds, such as chitin (Figs. 2 and 3a). To validate this hypothesis, the chitinase coding genes from these six MAGs were identified and compared. Each MAG encodes a chitinase gene, and the sequences of these genes are identical, with a length of 1368 bp. The predicted protein is composed of 456 amino acids and its molecular weight is 54.72 kDa (Additional file 1: Table S11). The chitinase gene from MAG213-F140 was selected for heterologous expression, naming chitinase Chi2032. The protein sequence of Chi2032 was the most similar to ChiD from Niallia circulans (P27050.4), with a sequence similarity of 51.18% (Additional file 1: Table S11). The gene chi2032 was successfully cloned and expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3), and the molecular mass of the protein Chi2032 was estimated by SDS-PAGE, which was consistent with the predicted molecular weight (Additional file 2: Fig. S3A). The results of the DNS assay showed that the recombinant Chi2032 exhibited chitinase activity (Additional file 2: Fig. S3B). The primary hydrolysis products of chitin are chitobiose and N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc), both of which require specific transport systems for translocation into the cytoplasm for further metabolism [87]. In MAG213-F140, we identified several genes encoding components of an ABC-type multiple sugar transport system, including ABC.MS.S, ABC.MS.P, ABC.MS.P1, and msiK, which may collectively mediate the uptake of chitobiose, GlcNAc, and other carbohydrates. Moreover, several other genes associated with sugar transport were identified in MAG213-F140, including fruA/B, ganQ, and mdxG. However, no key enzymes (NagK, NagA, NagB) required for further introduction of GlcNAc into the glycolytic pathway have been identified in the YBH-derived MAGs. This suggests the potential presence of alternative, yet uncharacterized, metabolic routes for GlcNAc utilization in MAG213-F140. Alternatively, we hypothesize that the Candidatus Cloacimonadota in the YBH may engage in mutualistic or cross-feeding interactions with coexisting microbial populations. Following extracellular hydrolysis of chitobiose and GlcNAc, MAG213-F140 may release a portion of the hydrolyzed products into the surrounding environment, making them available to neighboring bacteria. In return, these microorganisms might supply alternative carbohydrate substrates to MAG213-F140, such as fructose, thereby facilitating a reciprocal exchange of metabolic intermediates and energy resources. Although this hypothesis remains speculative, it provides a potential framework for understanding metabolic interdependencies within microbial communities in nutrient-poor environments, such as the YBH.

Furthermore, 54 potential peptidases were identified in Candidatus Cloacimonadota, which could be classified into families A, C, I, M, P, S, T, and U (Additional file 2: Fig. S2C and Fig. 4, Additional file 1: Table S12). Similarly, Clade A and B encode distinct set of peptidases. For example, members of Clade A contain enzymes from the M55 family, which belong to the D-aminopeptidase dipeptide-binding protein family within the dipeptide ABC transport (dpp) operon [88]. Enzymes from the M55 family are known to be activated under bacterial starvation conditions, thereby providing peptides as nutritional resources for the bacteria [89]. In contrast, Clade B encodes unique peptidase families including A31 (MAGs from YBH, MAG_3300035698_1703, GSL_CB2_1 and GSL_GSL3510_2), C40 (MAGs from YBH and CSMAG_2405), C56, C59 (MAGs from YBH, MAG_3300025698_8, Zod_Metabat.1183 and AUK288), I51 (MAGs from YBH), I87, S08, S09 and S12 (MAGs from YBH). Notably, enzymes from the C40, C59, S12, and I51 families are almost exclusively found in MAGs derived from YBH. Enzymes in the C40 and C59 families are cysteine proteases that participate in the degradation of cysteine substrates. Furthermore, the number of genes encoding peptidases from the M50 and U62 families in Clade B is significantly higher than in Clade A (Additional file 1: Table S12). Information about other peptidases is provided in Additional file 10.

Compared to Clade A, these unique degradative enzymes in Clade B may contribute to more efficient utilization of environmental carbohydrates and peptides by Candidatus Cloacimonadota. In addition, heterologous expression confirmed the activity of the chitinase identified in MAG213-F140, suggesting that Clade B members from YBH may have diverged from other Candidatus Cloacimonadota members in their ability to degrade macromolecular organic compounds. Although this result does not directly confirm chitin utilization by Candidatus Cloacimonadota in the anoxic layer of YBH, it provides the indirect evidence for their potential metabolic capabilities.

Transport proteins and the cell envelope

Candidatus Cloacimonadota possess a variety of transport proteins (Fig. 3c), including CorA, FeoB, PstABCS, and PhnCDE, which may facilitate their adaptation to the respective environmental conditions. For example, CorA is a primary magnesium ion (Mg2+) transporter responsible for importing magnesium into the cell, and magnesium is essential for DNA and protein synthesis. Thus, CorA, being a primary magnesium transporter, is crucial for maintaining intracellular magnesium levels in Candidatus Cloacimonadota and may also be involved in the transport of other metal ions. FeoB is an Fe2+ transporter that is related to the uptake of ferrous iron from external environments. Phosphorus is essential for microbial growth and metabolism. The concentrations of PO43− in the YBH at depths of 120 m, 140 m, and 170 m are 2.1 μmol, 3.33 μmol, and 4.66 μmol [32], respectively, suggesting that the PO43− levels in the YBH are relatively low. However, Candidatus Cloacimonadota can efficiently acquire PO43− from the external environment via the PstABCS transport system. Additionally, the PhnCDE complex in Candidatus Cloacimonadota may be involved in the transport of organic phosphorus compounds, such as phosphoethanolamine. However, we did not find enzymes associated with phosphoethanolamine degradation, such as PHOSPHO1 (phosphoethanolamine/phosphocholine phosphatase), nor the phn gene cluster responsible for phosphonate degradation in Candidatus Cloacimonadota. Instead, we annotated enzymes involved in phosphoethanolamine biosynthesis, including fom1 (phosphoenolpyruvate phosphomutase/2-hydroxyethylphosphonate cytidylyltransferase).

Additionally, Clade B was found to encode phosphoglycerol geranylgeranyltransferase (K17104) (26%, 5/19), geranylgeranylglycerol-phosphate geranylgeranyltransferase (K17105) (95%, 18/19), and digeranylgeranylglycerophospholipid reductase (GGR) (K17830) (95%, 18/19), which have the potential ability to generate mixed archaeal/bacterial membranes [29, 30]. The genomes encoding these enzymes in Clade A are absent. This may suggest that the capability of Clade B to synthesize mixed archaeal/bacterial membranes facilitates its adaptation to marine environments, such as the YBH. In contrast, Clade A possesses CopA, a copper transporter that is crucial not only for protecting cells from high external copper concentrations but also for maintaining intracellular copper homeostasis. Even in environments with trace copper levels, the absence of CopA may result in slow growth due to cytoplasmic copper toxicity [90]. This characteristic of Clade A is associated with its growth in environments such as anaerobic digesters and wastewater treatment systems [29], where elevated concentrations of copper ions may be present. Clade A may utilize CopA to mitigate the toxicity of copper ions to the cells. In contrast, the absence of this protein in the Clade B group is likely due to its predominant existence in natural environments, where it is less likely to be affected by the toxic effects of copper ions during its growth.

In addition, the capB (K019320 (90%, 36/40) and capC (K22116) (92.5%, 37/40) genes were identified in Candidatus Cloacimonadota genomes. These genes encode poly-γ-glutamate synthetase (PGA synthetase), which catalyzes the synthesis of poly-γ-glutamic acid (PGA), a natural biopolymer composed of L- or D-glutamate monomers linked by γ-amide bonds. PGA is found in members of Candidatus Cloacimonadota [30], and it can function as a capsular component or be secreted into the extracellular matrix as a hydrophilic substance that retains water in biofilms [91]. Due to its strong hydrophilicity, PGA can locally reduce osmotic stress caused by high salinity, thereby supporting cell survival under saline conditions [91, 92]. This may suggest that members of Candidatus Cloacimonadota could utilize PGA synthesis as an adaptive strategy to cope with high-salinity environments, such as YBH and wastewater sludge anaerobic digester.

Gain and loss of gene family and divergence times

Previous studies [32] have reported the concentrations of various chemicals at different depths in YBH, and these environmental conditions are significantly different from the artificial environment where the Clade A strains live. The gene gain and loss analysis revealed significant differences in the dynamics of gene family numbers across various clades (Fig. 5A). Specifically, the gene family count in the Clade B showed a clear upward trend, particularly in the MAGs derived from the YBH environment. This suggests that bacteria in the Clade B have undergone significant gene family expansion during their evolutionary history. This expansion resulted in a ~ 35% increase in gene number from the most recent common ancestor (MRCA) of the two clades, rising from 1541 gene families in the MRCA to the currently predicted 2078 gene families. COG functional annotation analysis of gained and lost genes (Additional file 1: Table S13) revealed that the most prominent category of gained genes in these MAGs was related to CAZymes, such as chitinase involved in chitin degradation, α-galactosidase, and glucanase, followed by proteins associated with cellular antioxidant functions, such as glutaredoxin. The lost genes are primarily involved in cellular transport, including zinc and nickel/cobalt transporters. This suggests that gene gain has enhanced the capacity for degrading complex organic compounds and antioxidant functions, thereby facilitating adaptation to the marine hypoxic environment of the YBH. In contrast, the gene family numbers in the Clade A displayed an initial increase followed by a decrease. The majority of genomes in Clade A (except CSMAG_498, KA01, and crispr.contigs_1) showed little deviation from the gene counts observed in the Candidatus Cloacimonadota MRCA. According to the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 5B), Candidatus Cloacimonadota emerged as a distinct bacterial phylum approximately 2.585 billion years ago, and further diverged into Clades A and B around 1.755 billion years ago. As Clades A and B evolved under different environmental conditions, they diverged approximately 1.472 billion years ago and 1.049 billion years ago, with significant differences in their genomic and metabolic characteristics emerging. Around 3.36 million years ago, Candidatus Cloacimonadota bacteria evolved traits specifically adapted to the anoxic conditions of YBH and formed a distinct cluster in the phylogenetic tree. This period corresponds to the Pliocene epoch [93], a period marked by the flourishing of marine microbial communities. The proliferation of these communities lays the foundation for subsequent climate and environmental changes, ultimately driving the formation of biodiversity and further evolution of Earth’s ecosystems.

Conclusion

Based on phylogenetic and functional prediction analyses of MAGs, we classified the genomes of Candidatus Cloacimonadia within the phylum Candidatus Cloacimonadota into two clades, A and B, and designated MAG213-F140 as the type genome for Clade B. The macromolecular organic matter degradation and energy conservation in Clade A and Clade B are closely linked to their respective environmental niches. Although both clades possess the ability to construct a simple ETC using the Rnf complex and ATP synthase, they encode markedly different types of ATP synthases and hydrogenases. In addition, MAGs derived from the YBH exhibit several unique features, that may enhance the adaptation. Gene loss and gain enabled Candidatus Cloacimonadota to evolve into efficient heterotrophic microbes, with members of both Clades A and B employing distinct metabolic strategies to adapt to the vast environments. Time-divergence analysis reveals the timing of evolutionary divergence between Clade A and Clade B. Collectively, our study advances the understanding of Candidatus Cloacimonadota, and clarifies the ecological role in biogeochemical cycles.
