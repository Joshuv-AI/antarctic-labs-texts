Managing relapses during or after adjuvant CDK4/6 inhibitors in HR-positive/HER2-negative early breast cancer: an emerging challenge
Authors: R Gerosa, G Gentile, L Arecco, C Dauccia, S Nannini, S Lobo-Martins, E Agostinetto, M Lambertini, A Santoro, P Aftimos, M Piccart-Gebhart, E de Azambuja
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCyclin-dependent kinase 4 and 6 inhibitors (CDK4/6i), such as abemaciclib and ribociclib, have recently been incorporated as adjuvant strategy in combination with endocrine therapy (ET) for patients with hormone receptor-positive, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative early breast cancer at higher risk of recurrence. However, despite a significant reduction in recurrence rates, a subset of patients still experiences distant metastatic spreading, with nearly 10% recurring during or shortly after adjuvant CDK4/6i completion, as observed in pivotal trials. To date, only one small retrospective study has described this emerging population while ongoing trials are not specifically addressing this scenario, leaving both the efficacy of postrelapse treatments and the biological background largely unknown. As adjuvant CDK4/6i use expands, these patients with resistant disease pose a novel clinical challenge. Therefore, we propose a pragmatic approach for the management and treatment of relapses occurring during or after adjuvant CDK4/6i exposure, also highlighting key unanswered questions, future perspectives and the urgent need for dedicated research efforts.Key words: cyclin-dependent kinase 4/6 inhibitors, hormone receptor-positive HER2-negative breast neoplasms, early-stage breast cancer, adjuvant therapy, distant relapse

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is the most commonly diagnosed cancer in women, with hormone receptor (HR)-positive/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-negative subtype accounting for ∼75% of all cases.1 While it is often curable when diagnosed at early onset, recurrences may still occur up to 20 years after diagnosis, depending on the clinical-genomic risk. Over the past two decades, the management of high-risk HR-positive/HER2-negative early BC (eBC) has advanced with novel adjuvant therapies and, possibly in the future with promising different neoadjuvant approaches such as immunotherapy and radiotherapy, although their long-term impact remains awaited.2

Given their proven efficacy in the metastatic setting, cyclin-dependent kinase 4/6 inhibitors (CDK4/6i) were also evaluated as part of adjuvant therapy in phase III trials, encompassing considerable heterogeneity in dosing, endocrine therapy (ET) backbone, treatment duration, and eligibility criteria to define the higher risk of relapse.3,4 Specifically, abemaciclib and ribociclib in addition to standard adjuvant ET have both demonstrated a significant reduction of invasive and distant relapse risk, with a remarkable ‘carry-over’ effect beyond the treatment period, although overall survival (OS) data remain immature for both agents.5, 6, 7 Based on these results, the major regulatory agencies have recently granted their use in HR-positive/HER2-negative eBC patients meeting the eligibility criteria of the respective trials, albeit approval is not uniform across countries.

Resistance to Adjuvant CDK4/6i

Despite the above mentioned benefit, a subset of patients still experience distant relapse, with a notable group progressing early, either during or shortly after completing adjuvant CDK4/6i regimen. Specifically, in the “monarchE” trial, 9.1% of patients experienced early distant recurrence: 6% within 2 years from treatment initiation (planned treatment period) and an additional 3.1% in the third year.7 In the “NATALEE” trial, 7.1% of patients experienced distant spreading within the first 3 years of ribociclib treatment, while the exact 4-year relapse rates were not reported in the latest trial update.6

However, this population remains poorly characterized, leaving unknown whether their profiles mirror those of patients who progress on CDK4/6i in the metastatic setting, either in terms of prognosis, response to subsequent systemic treatment and underlying resistance mechanisms. To date, only one small retrospective study has described this resistant group.8 Among 163 patients treated with adjuvant abemaciclib, 15 (9.2%: closely aligned with monarchE early relapse rates) experienced recurrence (all distant), with a median disease free-survival (DFS) of 19 months after treatment start [interquartile range (IQR) 10.5-26 months]. Concerningly, a marked shift toward a triple-negative profile, seen both at immunochemistry (50%) and molecularly [absence of the typical CDK4/6i resistance markers seen in metastatic setting], correlated with a very poor median duration of subsequent first-line therapy (3.0 months, IQR 1.6-5.0 months).

Concerningly, whereas latest trials in the luminal metastatic scenario do not provide evidence about novel agents efficacy in this emerging population (less than 1-3% of enrolled patients received adjuvant CDK4/6i),9, 10, 11, 12 the use of adjuvant CDK4/6i is expected to expand. Indeed, real-world data indicate that while abemaciclib’s stricter eligibility criteria limit its use to ∼15%-20% of luminal eBC patients, ribociclib’s broader access could extend it to nearly 50%.13

As a result, uncertainties regarding the optimal management of metastatic recurrence are likely to impact a substantial number of patients in the near future, highlighting a critical clinical issue.

Proposed Management Approach for Relapses During or after Adjuvant CDK4/6i

While locoregional or selected distant ‘oligo’ relapses may be suitable for curative local treatment alongside continuation of the same adjuvant therapy, widespread distant relapses present the challenge of selecting the most appropriate subsequent systemic treatment. Clinical parameters such as the timing of relapse relative to the start or completion of adjuvant ET and CDK4/6 may remain useful for patient stratification and guiding treatment in this underexplored scenario. Indeed, although definitions of endocrine resistance were mainly prognostic, according to the latest guidelines they are incorporated for first-line treatment selection.14 Although they do not account for CDK4/6i use or duration, emerging evidence in the metastatic setting suggests that the length of prior CDK4/6i exposure may indeed predict the benefit from subsequent endocrine-based therapies.15 Therefore, in the absence of evidence-based cut-offs, we arbitrarily propose thresholds to identify three broad disease groups: (i) ‘CDK4/6i–endocrine resistant’, who relapse during or within 12 months after completing/discontinuing adjuvant CDK4/6i; (ii) ‘CDK4/6i-sensitive/endocrine resistant’, who relapse >12 months after CDK4/6i completion/discontinuation and during or within 12 months from completion/discontinuation of adjuvant ET; and (iii) ‘CDK4/6i-sensitive/endocrine sensitive’, for those who relapse >12 months after completing/discontinuing adjuvant ET. Biopsy at relapse remains crucial to detect phenotypic shifts and for next-generation-sequencing-based genomic profiling. ‘CDK4/6i–sensitive/endocrine sensitive’ disease

Althought they could be managed with the standard first-line therapy for luminal-like metastatic BC (mBC), it remains unclear, also from a regulatory perspective, whether this subgroup can be considered truly treatment naive, or if residual effects from prior CDK4/6i exposure persist.

‘CDK4/6i-sensitive/endocrine resistant’ disease

Patients who relapse after a reasonable interval following completion/discontinuation of adjuvant CDK4/6i while receiving ET may still benefit from further endocrine-based strategies. Identifying activating phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate 3-kinase catalytic subunit alpha (PIK3CA) mutations is crucial (nearly 45% after CDK4/6i first-line).16 Indeed, adding inavolisib, a selective PIK3CA inhibitor, to palbociclib and fulvestrant significantly improved OS in CDK4/6i-naive patients (only 1.2% prior exposure), harboring this mutation and early relapsing on/after adjuvant ET.11 Notably, even immediately after CDK4/6i failure, selective PIK3CA inhibition has proven effective in metastatic setting.17 An alternative strategy, regardless of PIK3CA status, could be switching both the CDK4/6i and the ET backbone.12 Moreover, for those progressing after adjuvant ribociclib, combining abemaciclib with imlunestrant, a novel oral selective estrogen receptor degrader (SERD), may be another viable option.9 In both cases, the regimens' benefit was observed, however, in cohorts progressing mainly on palbociclib and with rare adjuvant CDK4/6i exposure, thus not fully representative of the population under consideration.

Beyond these options, ‘biomarker-driven’ treatments may offer valuable alternatives or be considered after failure of initial preferred strategy. For patients with alterations in the PIK3CA/AKT1/PTEN pathway, capivasertib plus fulvestrant displayed a significant benefit after CDK4/6i failure (only 1% in adjuvant setting).14 Conversely, targeting the same pathway with everolimus [an inhibitor of mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR)] plus exemestane appears suboptimal in CDK4/6i-pretreated patients, based on real-world data,18 while the potentially more effective combination with fulvestrant remains less studied.

Lastly, in patients harboring estrogen receptor 1 (ESR1) mutation (nearly 31% after CDK4/6i first-line),16 SERDs such as elacestrant and possibly vepdegestrant, a novel proteolysis targeting chimera (PROTAC) estrogen receptor degrader, represent possible options.10 Although, also in this case, their benefit was observed mainly in patients progressing on palbociclib (only 3% received adjuvant CDK4/6i), their favorable toxicity profile, oral administration, and greater benefit in patients with longer benefit from CDK4/6i support their use.

‘CDK4/6i–endocrine resistant’ disease

Focusing on the most challenging group, especially for very early progression, cytotoxic rather than further endocrine-based therapy may be preferable, with the possible exception of patients harboring PIK3CA/AKT1/PTEN alterations. Another valid choice before other cytotoxic agents could be, depending also on prior use in the early setting, poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors in patients with germline Breast cancer gene 1/2 (gBRCA1/2) and possibly Partner and localizer of BRCA2 (gPALB2) pathogenic variant.

Additionally, patients with HER2-low and possibly ultra-low expressing cancer who relapse within 6 months after starting adjuvant CDK4/6i may benefit from trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd), a new revolutionary antibody–drug conjugate (ADC). Indeed, T-DXd showed a significant survival benefit (OS data yet immature) over physician’s choice chemotherapy (CT), mostly capecitabine, in a small patient subgroup (∼9%) early-progressing on first-line CDK4/6i + ET.14 Conversely, access to other newly approved ADCs (sacituzumab govitecan, datopotamab deruxtecan) requires exposure to at least one prior line of CT in the advanced setting.

For all other patients CT remains a key option. Depending on prior (neo)adjuvant treatments and clinical urgency, regimens may include taxanes, anthracyclines and other validated drugs in either monotherapy or combinations. Single-agent capecitabine is often preferred for its safety, oral use and lack of alopecia, and retrospective data also suggest better outcomes versus intravenous CT or ET-based treatments after CDK4/6i progression.15

Future Perspectives and Open Questions

Numerous ongoing trials are evaluating earlier use of already approved agents, innovative combinations and next-generation therapies in first/second line setting for luminal-like mBC.19 However, among them, only a limited number enroll explicitly those patients relapsing on (Table 1) or shortly after adjuvant CDK4/6i plus ET (Table 2) and do not require any prior line of treatment in the advanced setting. Moreover, since they explicitly but not exclusively include this patient subgroup, their results will nevertheless provide only indirect evidence. The only exception is represented by two ongoing phase II trials in China exclusively focused on this population: NCT05806047 (recruitment status unknown) and NCT06849947 (not yet recruiting).

Overall, the anticipation in the adjuvant setting of therapies already proven effective in metastatic patients, such as CDK4/6i, and potentially in the future novel SERDs and ADCs, raises concerns. Firstly, a key issue is treatment selection at recurrence, particularly for early relapse, where prior adjuvant exposure may induce resistance and limit the efficacy of subsequent therapies.8 Therefore, exploring the mutational and potentially broader omic landscape of metastatic recurrence is warranted, as it could uncover a different biological background and help refine treatment decisions.8 Secondly, adjuvant CDK4/6i adds toxicity and financial burden without proven OS benefit yet, while high censoring rates in registrative trials also raise doubts about the reliability of reported invasive DFS gains.20 Thus, improved patient selection, through the identification of novel prognostic and predictive signature beyond the insufficient clinical features, is essential. Lastly, in this context, advanced noninvasive tools such as circulating tumor DNA analysis and novel imaging techniques could be true ‘game changers’, both for detecting occult metastases before adjuvant therapy and for guiding personalized follow-up strategies.

Conclusion

Currently, the almost complete lack of evidence limits clear management guidance for patients relapsing during or after adjuvant CDK4/6i. Our proposed approach is mainly based on assumptions and speculations drawn from the luminal metastatic setting, acknowledging also the limitations in treatment availability due to heterogeneous regulatory approvals and reimbursement policies across countries. As CDK4/6i use in the early setting is expanding, dedicated prospective translational studies and clinical trials are urgently needed to address this emerging unmet need.
