Quality and reliability of neonatal jaundice short videos on TikTok and Bilibili
Authors: Zhendong Liu, Xiaoping Yang, Hongyan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study evaluates the content, quality, and reliability of TikTok and Bilibili videos on neonatal jaundice, and examines how accurately they inform parents and healthcare professionals.MethodsOn November 1, 2025, we searched TikTok and Bilibili for the top 100 videos using the terms “neonatal jaundice/新生儿黄疸,” yielding 200 videos in total (100 per platform). Two independent reviewers evaluated each video with the Global Quality Scale (GQS) and modified DISCERN (mDISCERN), independently rating scope, reliability, and overall quality. Disagreements were resolved by discussion with a third arbitrator.ResultsThere was no significant difference in GQS or mDISCERN between TikTok and Bilibili videos (both p > 0.05). Median scores indicated high quality and high reliability on both platforms (GQS median = 5; mDISCERN median = 5). Videos uploaded by pediatricians were significantly more reliable and of higher quality than those uploaded by family/patients (p < 0.001), and videos focused on disease knowledge or disease knowledge/treatment care outperformed experience sharing and other content types (p < 0.01). Platform engagement was higher on TikTok (likes/comments/saves/shares, all p < 0.05), but engagement did not correlate with quality (all p > 0.05).ConclusionFor neonatal jaundice videos, overall information quality was high on both TikTok and Bilibili, with no platform-level difference in GQS or mDISCERN. Higher quality and reliability were consistently associated with content created by pediatricians and with disease knowledge/treatment care topics, whereas popularity metrics did not predict quality. Platforms should strengthen medical content review and verification, and creators—especially healthcare professionals—should prioritize evidence-based, structured education to better serve parents and caregivers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-06771-0.Keywords: Neonatal jaundice, Hyperbilirubinemia, Phototherapy, Short-video platforms, TikTok, Bilibili, Information quality assessment, Health communication

Introduction

Jaundice occurs in 60% of full-term and 80% of preterm infants during their first week These high rates make it a very common issue in neonatal wards [1]. For the majority of infants, jaundice is a temporary issue that goes away without any help. However, when bilirubin levels become dangerously high, the risks are severe. If these cases are missed or poorly managed, they can lead to permanent brain damage, or kernicterus, causing lifelong disabilities like cerebral palsy and hearing loss [2]. To prevent this, neonatal care must prioritize early screening and an accurate diagnosis.

Several factors make misinformation about newborn jaundice a serious problem for public health. First, the condition requires time-sensitive intervention; delays in seeking medical attention—potentially caused by misleading online advice—can result in irreversible neurological harm [3]. Second, the visual detection of jaundice presents particular challenges in infants with darker skin tones, as clinical recognition of yellowing is significantly more difficult in these populations [4]. The NHS Race and Health Observatory has documented that current guidelines and training resources inadequately address the assessment of jaundice in Black, Asian, and minority ethnic neonates, potentially perpetuating diagnostic delays and health inequities [5]. This gap is especially concerning given that ethnic minority newborns may have elevated baseline risk for hyperbilirubinemia while simultaneously being most likely to experience missed diagnoses through visual assessment alone. Third, parental anxiety surrounding newborn health makes caregivers particularly susceptible to seeking and acting upon unverified information, including potentially harmful home remedies [6].

Social media has changed where parents look for health advice. For millions, apps like TikTok and Bilibili are now the first stop for medical info [7]. In China, parents rely heavily on these platforms for guidance on child health [8]. These apps are built to spread engaging videos fast, but this same system often pushes advice that is inaccurate or even dangerous [9, 10].

A critical question in digital health communication concerns whether efforts should focus on improving the quality of medical content on social media platforms or on redirecting parents toward professional healthcare websites and clinical consultations. Research indicates that parents increasingly use social media for child health information as part of the modern parenting experience, often before and after consultations with healthcare professionals [11]. These platforms are popular with caregivers who have little time, as they are easy to reach and offer a sense of community. However, picking out accurate info from misleading posts is a challenge. Most parents lack the specific skills needed to tell what is actually true [12]. While professional medical websites and direct clinical consultations offer more reliable information, their accessibility and user-friendliness may not meet the immediate information needs of anxious parents [13]. This suggests that a dual strategy may be warranted: improving the quality of social media health content while simultaneously facilitating parent access to authoritative professional resources.

Research shows that wrong advice about newborn care—such as suggesting home remedies that don’t work or missing red flags—can delay medical help and harm the baby [14]. In areas like breastfeeding and asthma, we already know that the quality of health info on social media varies a lot [15–17]. Usually, videos from doctors are more accurate and better organized than those from regular users. But so far, there has been very little research on the quality of jaundice videos on short-video apps.

Despite the availability of well-established clinical guidelines for hyperbilirubinemia management—including the 2022 American Academy of Pediatrics guidelines and recommendations from the Chinese Society of Pediatrics [18]—potentially misleading content continues to circulate on social media platforms. This includes advice that contradicts evidence-based practice, such as recommendations for direct sunlight exposure as a substitute for clinical phototherapy or suggestions to supplement with glucose water to reduce jaundice [19]. The risk posed by such misinformation is particularly acute given the anxiety that jaundice commonly provokes in new parents and the potential for online content to influence their healthcare decision-making and care-seeking behaviors.

Research systematically evaluating the quality and reliability of neonatal jaundice information on short-video platforms remains scarce. Cross-platform comparisons of video quality are particularly limited, and the relationships between content characteristics—such as video source, content type, and engagement metrics—and information quality have not been comprehensively analyzed. The link between a video’s popularity and its actual quality is key. It directly affects how platforms are designed and how health groups should share information.

Therefore, this study aims to systematically evaluate short videos related to neonatal jaundice on two major Chinese video-sharing platforms: TikTok (Douyin) and Bilibili. Specifically, we compare video quality (assessed using the Global Quality Scale, GQS) and reliability (assessed using the modified DISCERN tool, mDISCERN) across platforms, video sources, and content types. We also looked at how people interact with these videos to see if popularity is linked to better information. The findings are intended to inform digital health communication strategies, guide the development of more credible and evidence-based neonatal health education content on emerging short-video platforms, and provide recommendations for both platform governance and clinical practice.

Methods

Study design and ethics statement

This cross-sectional study evaluated the content, quality, and reliability of short videos related to neonatal jaundice on two major Chinese video-sharing platforms: TikTok (Douyin) and Bilibili. Data collection was conducted from November 1 to November 2, 2025.

Ethics approval

This study analyzed publicly available video content and did not involve direct interaction with human participants. To protect privacy, all user-identifiable information (e.g., usernames) was anonymized during data analysis. Although some videos may have contained images of neonates, we did not extract, store, or analyze identifiable infant images; our assessment focused solely on informational content quality. This approach aligns with established internet research ethics guidelines for analysis of publicly available social media content [20, 21]. According to the policy of the Ethics Committee of Gansu University of Chinese Medicine, this type of study does not require formal ethical approval.

Search strategy and data acquisition

We searched for the term “neonatal jaundice/新生儿黄疸” on both platforms. To simulate the information retrieval behavior of general users (e.g., parents), we logged out of all personal accounts and cleared browser cookies to minimize algorithmic personalization bias. Videos were screened sequentially based on the platforms’ default algorithmic ranking (identified as “Comprehensive Sort”). This ranking is determined by proprietary algorithms that weigh keyword relevance, user engagement, and content recency.

The analysis was limited to the top 100 videos on each platform for two primary reasons. First, this strategy aligns with established methodologies in digital health research, as empirical evidence demonstrates that users rarely browse beyond the first few pages of search results. Limiting the sample to the top-ranked content maximizes the ecological validity of the study by reflecting actual user consumption patterns. Moreover, previous studies have proven that videos exceeding this threshold will not have a significant impact on the results [22–24]. Second, a sample size calculation based on Cochran’s formula for descriptive studies indicates that a sample of 96 videos is sufficient to achieve a 95% confidence level with a 10% margin of error. Thus, our sample of 100 videos per platform provides sufficient statistical power.

Videos were assessed sequentially in the order of their default comprehensive sorting. To reach the target of 100 eligible videos per platform, a total of 236 videos were initially screened (Fig. 1). Subsequently, we carried out inclusion and exclusion. After the inclusion and exclusion process, a total of 200 videos were left and included in the final analysis.

On Bilibili

Exclusion criteria

Videos were excluded if they were: (1) duplicates; (2) unrelated to neonatal jaundice; (3) pure advertisements (excluded to minimize commercial bias); (4) posted for fewer than 10 days (excluded to allow a “washout period” for engagement metrics to stabilize); or (5) shorter than 10 s (excluded due to insufficient information density for quality assessment). In cases where a video became inaccessible (e.g., deleted) during the study period, it was replaced by the next consecutive eligible video in the search results.

Duplicate content within the same platform was identified by comparing video URLs, titles, and visual signatures.

Videos containing the same content that appeared on both TikTok and Bilibili were included as independent entries. This was done because the engagement metrics (e.g., likes, comments) and dissemination patterns are platform-dependent, and excluding them would lead to an incomplete assessment of a platform’s specific health information environment.

Data collection was completed within a short window to ensure content consistency. In the event that a video became unavailable or inaccessible (e.g., due to deletion or privacy setting changes) during the data extraction or scoring phase, it was excluded. Such videos were replaced by the next available video in the platform’s default search results to ensure a final sample of 100 eligible videos per platform.

Classification of videos

Videos were classified by source and content type by the first author (Z.L.) based on explicit and objective indicators. Video sources were identified via platform-verified credentials, account biographies, and professional context (e.g., clinical attire or institutional backgrounds). Content types were categorized based on the primary purpose stated in the video titles and content. Since the information about video content type and video source is relatively objective, no consistency assessment was performed.

The ‘Disease Knowledge/Treatment Care’ category was created to represent comprehensive educational content that integrates pathophysiology with management strategies. Videos were reviewed separately in ‘Disease Knowledge’ or ‘Treatment Care’ if they focused exclusively on one domain. This allowed for a comparative analysis of informational completeness versus specialized focus.

Definition of duration

We defined “video duration” as the total length of the video content in seconds. To ensure sufficient information density for a meaningful quality assessment, videos shorter than 10 s were excluded from the study during the data acquisition phase.

Quality and reliability assessment

The quality and reliability assessment was conducted by two independent reviewers (X.Y. and H.W.), both of whom possess extensive experience in clinical neonatology and the application of standardized health information assessment tools. To ensure scoring consistency, a pre-scoring standardization meeting was conducted to align the interpretation of the GQS and mDISCERN criteria within the context of neonatal hyperbilirubinemia. Then, they evaluated the videos using the Global Quality Scale (GQS) [25] and the modified DISCERN (mDISCERN) tool [26]. Any discrepancies in scoring were resolved through deliberative discussion among three researchers (Z.L., X.Y., and H.W.) until a consensus was reached. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC).

GQS (1–5 points)

Assesses the overall flow, quality, and usefulness of the video. Scores of 1–2 indicate low quality, 3 moderate quality and 4–5 high quality.

Quality (assessed by GQS)

Refers to the instructional presentation and educational utility of the video. It evaluates the flow, clarity, and media production standards suitable for patient education.

mDISCERN (0–5 points)

Assesses the reliability of health information. Scores of 0–2 indicate low reliability, 3 moderate reliability and 4–5 high reliability.

Reliability (assessed by mDISCERN)

Refers to the trustworthiness and evidence-based nature of the medical content. It evaluates the clarity of sources, attribution, and balance of the information.

Blinding

To minimize popularity bias, raters were blinded to all engagement metrics (likes, comments, etc.) during the scoring process. However, blinding to the platform or uploader identity was not feasible due to visual content characteristics.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1). Data normality was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Given that the data—especially engagement metrics and quality scores—exhibited a non-parametric distribution, descriptive statistics were reported as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR). The Kruskal-Wallis test was used to assess differences between groups, followed by Dunn’s multiple comparison test for pairwise comparisons. Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to evaluate the relationship between quantitative variables. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Descriptive statistics

Continuous variables were reported as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR). Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages.

Group Comparisons: The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare differences between platforms, and the Kruskal-Wallis test was used for comparisons among multiple source/content groups.

Regression analysis

Multivariate Poisson regression models were constructed to identify factors influencing GQS and mDISCERN scores. Prior to modeling, multicollinearity was assessed using the Generalized Variance Inflation Factor (GVIF). To improve model stability and the interpretability of regression coefficients, continuous independent variables with large ranges were scaled: engagement metrics (likes, comments, saves, and shares) were divided by 100 (e.g., reported as ‘per 100 likes’), and video duration was converted from seconds to minutes. No log-transformations or standard normalizations were applied to these predictors. Results are reported as Incidence Rate Ratios (IRRs), representing the relative change in GQS or mDISCERN scores associated with a 100-unit increase in engagement or a 1-minute increase in duration.

Sensitivity analysis

To verify the robustness of the findings given the ordinal nature of the scores, a sensitivity analysis was conducted using Ordinal Logistic Regression. Variable standardization is also applicable to ordinal logistic regression. Due to model convergence constraints, 95% confidence intervals were estimated using the Wald approximation.

No missing data were present in the final dataset; all 200 videos had complete information for all analyzed variables.

In terms of content recency, TikTok videos were published significantly more recently than those on Bilibili (median: 247.00 days [IQR: 165.75–525.75] vs. 1196.00 days [IQR: 644.00–1852.00]; p < 0.001, Mann-Whitney U test). Regarding user engagement, TikTok videos exhibited substantially higher metrics than Bilibili videos across all indicators. Specifically, the median number of likes on TikTok was approximately 72 times higher than on Bilibili (1080.00 [IQR: 226.75–2788.75] vs. 15.00 [IQR: 4.75–50.00]; p < 0.001). Similar patterns were observed for comments (160.50 vs. 1.00; approximately 161-fold difference), saves (454.50 vs. 13.00; approximately 35-fold difference), and shares (965.50 vs. 19.00; approximately 51-fold difference; all p < 0.001, Mann-Whitney U tests). This marked disparity in engagement likely reflects TikTok’s algorithmic promotion of narrative-style and emotionally engaging content, as well as its larger active user base compared to Bilibili. Notably, the finding that newer videos received higher engagement is counterintuitive, given that older content would typically have more time to accumulate interactions. This pattern may be attributed to platform-specific factors: (1) TikTok’s algorithm heavily prioritizes recency, actively promoting newer content in users’ feeds; (2) both platforms may archive or suppress visibility of older engagement metrics over time; and (3) the short-video ecosystem favors rapid content turnover, with user attention concentrated on recently uploaded material.

Both TikTok and Bilibili videos achieved median scores of 5.00 (range: 1–5) for both the Global Quality Scale (GQS) and the modified DISCERN (mDISCERN) tool. Statistical analysis using the Mann-Whitney U test revealed no significant differences between the two platforms regarding video quality (GQS: p = 0.137) or reliability (mDISCERN: p = 0.506).

Video quality and reliability under different classifications

Inter-rater reliability for the GQS and mDISCERN scores demonstrated excellent agreement: GQS (ICC = 0.955, 95% CI [0.940–0.966], P < 0.001) and mDISCERN (ICC = 0.962, 95% CI [0.908–0.980], P < 0.001).

The Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post hoc comparisons revealed that both video source and content type were significantly associated with video quality (all P < 0.001) (Figs. 4 and 5). Videos created by pediatricians achieved significantly higher GQS and mDISCERN scores than those produced by other physicians (P < 0.05), science communicators (P < 0.001), and family/patient contributors (P < 0.001). It is important to note that these associations do not imply causality; the observed differences may reflect confounding factors such as access to clinical resources, professional training, or content production support.

Analysis of correlation

Due to the non-normal distribution of the data, Spearman’s correlation analysis was employed in Fig. 6. There was a strong positive correlation between GQS and mDISCERN (R = 0.901, P < 0.001), indicating that the two assessment tools yielded highly consistent results.

Spearman correlation analysis between video variables

Critically, video quality was not associated with popularity metrics. GQS showed no significant correlation with likes (R = 0.06, P = 0.376), comments (R = 0.08, P = 0.281), saves (R = 0.04, P = 0.535), or shares (R = 0.04, P = 0.609). Similarly, mDISCERN showed no significant correlation with any engagement metric (all P > 0.05). This finding suggests that platform algorithms, which prioritize emotional resonance and narrative appeal over scientific accuracy, may not effectively surface high-quality medical content to users.

Video duration had virtually no association with quality metrics (GQS: R = − 0.02, P = 0.764; mDISCERN: R = 0.01, P = 0.876). Days since publication was negatively correlated with likes (R = − 0.240, P = 0.0006), comments (R = − 0.244, P = 0.0005), and saves (R = − 0.330, P < 0.001), indicating that newer videos tend to receive more engagement.

Independent influencing factors of quality score

Multicollinearity adjustment

Initial diagnostics indicated severe multicollinearity between likes and shares (GVIF > 16.0). Consequently, shares were excluded from the final models (Table S1 and Table S2).

The multivariate Poisson regression analysis evaluated independent predictors for both GQS and mDISCERN scores (Table S3). Source and Content Associations: While strict statistical significance (P < 0.05) was not reached, distinct trends were observed.

Videos produced by pediatricians demonstrated consistently higher probability of better scores compared to the family/patient group (GQS: IRR = 1.41, 95% CI: 0.96–2.11, P = 0.086; mDISCERN: IRR = 1.44, 95% CI: 0.98–2.17, P = 0.072). Notably, comprehensive videos covering both Disease Knowledge and Treatment Care showed the strongest association with high quality, with IRR estimates exceeding 2.0 (GQS: IRR = 2.19, 95% CI: 1.03–5.43, P = 0.06; mDISCERN: IRR = 2.08, 95% CI: 1.02–4.85, P = 0.062) and 95% confidence intervals excluding the null value of 1.0, although Wald test P-values were marginally above the threshold (P ≈ 0.06).

Independence from engagement

None of the engagement metrics (likes, comments, saves) were significantly associated with GQS or mDISCERN scores (all P > 0.05), reinforcing the finding that popularity metrics are poor indicators of medical information reliability.

In the multivariate Poisson regression analysis, after adjusting for platform, source, content type, and engagement metrics, video duration (measured per minute) was not a significant independent predictor of quality scores. For GQS scores, video duration was not significantly correlated with GQS (IRR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.96–1.05, P = 0.837), and for mDISCERN scores, video duration was not significantly correlated with GQS (IRR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.95–1.04, P = 0.891). This reinforces the finding that external video characteristics, such as duration, are poor indicators of medical reliability.

In the multivariable regression analysis, comprehensive videos focusing on disease knowledge and treatment or care tended to be associated with higher quality scores compared with the reference category.Although the association did not reach conventional statistical significance based on Wald tests (P = 0.06), the profile likelihood confidence intervals excluded the null value (95% CI: 1.03–5.43 for GQS; 1.02–4.85 for mDISCERN), suggesting a potential association.

Sensitivity analysis

In the multivariable regression analyses, consistent patterns were observed across the GQS and mDISCERN models (Table S4).

Regarding platform, videos published on Bilibili showed no statistically significant differences in quality compared with those on TikTok in either the GQS model (OR = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.16–1.14, P = 0.089) or the mDISCERN model (OR = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.24–2.65, P = 0.705).

With respect to video source, pediatrician-produced videos were strongly associated with higher quality scores in both analyses. Compared with family- or patient-generated videos, pediatrician videos demonstrated significantly higher odds of better quality in the GQS analysis (OR = 6.85, 95% CI: 3.45–13.59, P < 0.001) and in the mDISCERN analysis (OR = 23.18, 95% CI: 4.62–116.37, P < 0.001). In contrast, videos produced by other physicians or science communicators were not significantly associated with quality in either model.

Content type emerged as the most influential factor in both analyses. Videos focusing on disease knowledge and treatment or care were associated with substantially higher quality compared with other content types in the GQS model (OR = 24.41, 95% CI: 13.02–45.77, P < 0.001) and the mDISCERN model (OR = 49.24, 95% CI: 18.73–129.42, P < 0.001). Similarly, videos emphasizing disease knowledge alone and experience sharing were consistently associated with higher quality across both models (all P < 0.001). Extremely large effect estimates observed for disease knowledge content in the mDISCERN analysis likely reflect sparse data and should be interpreted with caution.

Regarding video characteristics, neither duration nor days since posting showed statistically significant associations with quality in either analysis, although days since posting demonstrated a borderline association in the mDISCERN model (OR = 1.00, 95% CI: 1.00–1.00, P = 0.053). Engagement metrics, including likes, comments, and saves per 100 interactions, were not significantly associated with quality in either model.

To verify the robustness of these findings, sensitivity analysis using Ordinal Logistic Regression confirmed the lack of association between video duration and quality levels. In the GQS analysis, video duration was not significantly correlated with GQS (OR = 1.12, 95% CI: 0.88–1.44, P = 0.361), while in the mDISCERN analysis, the video duration was not significantly correlated with GQS (OR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.76–1.30, P = 0.957). Even when considering the ordinal nature of the scores, duration remained statistically decoupled from information quality across all models.

Discussion

This study conducted a systematic analysis of neonatal jaundice-related videos on TikTok (Douyin) and Bilibili, representing the first cross-platform comparison of information quality and reliability for this clinically important condition. These results show the reality of how jaundice information is shared online today. Our study helps guide how platforms should be managed and how doctors should talk to parents.

Quality and reliability of neonatal jaundice videos

The primary finding of this study is that the overall quality and reliability of neonatal jaundice videos on both TikTok and Bilibili were relatively high, with median GQS and mDISCERN scores reaching the maximum value of 5 on both platforms. This finding is notably more positive than reports from studies examining other health topics on short-video platforms, where quality has often been characterized as moderate to low [27, 28]. Several factors may explain this relatively favorable result. First, the high proportion of videos produced by pediatricians (71% on TikTok and 49% on Bilibili) likely contributed to elevated quality scores. And, neonatal jaundice is a well-defined clinical entity with established guidelines, which may facilitate the creation of accurate educational content.

However, it is essential to interpret these median scores with appropriate caution. While the medians indicated high quality, the interquartile ranges revealed notable heterogeneity, particularly on Bilibili (GQS IQR: 3–5; mDISCERN IQR: 3–5), suggesting that users may encounter videos of variable quality. Furthermore, GQS and mDISCERN assess structural quality and reliability—not clinical accuracy per se. A video may score highly on these metrics while still containing subtle inaccuracies that could influence parental decision-making.

High-quality videos were typically produced by pediatricians with formal training and clinical experience, focused on disease knowledge and/or treatment care, and presented information in a structured, evidence-informed manner consistent with established clinical guidelines.

In contrast, lower-quality videos more often relied on unsystematic, experience-based narratives, offered vague or non-standardized recommendations, or promoted practices inconsistent with evidence-based guidance, such as home sunlight exposure as a substitute for phototherapy or glucose water supplementation. These patterns reflect qualitative observations rather than formal misinformation coding.

Association between video source and quality

Videos produced by pediatricians were associated with significantly higher quality and reliability scores compared with those from other sources. Like earlier studies, we found that videos made by doctors are usually better organized [29, 30]. The specialized training and clinical experience of pediatricians likely enable them to present information in a structured, evidence-informed manner. We cannot assume a direct cause-and-effect relationship here. The higher quality seen in these videos may simply reflect who has access to better equipment or technical support.

The relatively limited quantity of physician-generated content despite its superior quality highlights a persistent challenge in digital health communication: high-quality medical education resources remain underrepresented on social media platforms [31].

Risk considerations and potential misinformation

Low-quality advice on social media is a major risk for jaundice because the timing of treatment is so critical. The 2022 AAP guidelines show that care depends on three specific things: how early the baby was born, their age in hours, and their actual bilirubin levels [2]. Advice that delays appropriate medical consultation may result in progression to dangerous bilirubin levels and irreversible neurological harm.

Our qualitative observations during video review identified content promoting practices inconsistent with evidence-based guidelines, such as home sunlight exposure as a substitute for clinical phototherapy or glucose water supplementation. It is important to note that we did not systematically code for specific misinformation content; these examples represent illustrative observations rather than quantified prevalence estimates.

Another risk is that jaundice is much harder to see in babies with darker skin. This makes it easier to miss the early signs of the condition. The NHS Race and Health Observatory has documented that visual recognition of jaundice is significantly more challenging in Black, Asian, and minority ethnic neonates [32]. Whether neonatal jaundice videos on social media adequately address this clinical reality remains an important question for future research.

The quality-popularity paradox

A critical finding is the absence of significant correlations between video quality and engagement metrics. This “quality-popularity paradox” has important implications: platform algorithms optimized for engagement do not automatically surface high-quality medical content [33, 34].

Several mechanisms may explain this decoupling. First, platform algorithms are designed to optimize for engagement metrics rather than informational accuracy [35]. Research demonstrates that engagement metrics primarily promote content fitting immediate human social, affective, and cognitive preferences rather than quality content [36]. Second, evidence-based health information often requires nuance and caveats that may reduce shareability. Third, algorithmic amplification of emotionally resonant content may create feedback loops perpetuating visibility of engaging but potentially lower-quality material [37].

Understanding whether this disconnect stems primarily from algorithmic factors, content characteristics, or user preferences has important implications for intervention design. Our study does not show which of these causes is the right one. It remains unclear which process is actually at work, and figuring this out will be a key task for future research.

Understanding the negative correlation between publication age and engagement

Newer videos demonstrated higher engagement despite having less time to accumulate interactions. This counterintuitive finding likely reflects algorithmic recency bias, whereby platforms actively prioritize and promote recently uploaded content within users’ recommendation feeds. As content ages and is no longer favored by the algorithm, its exposure sharply declines, limiting further opportunities for engagement. These findings have implications for content sustainability: even high-quality professional content may have a limited effective lifespan, requiring ongoing content creation to maintain visibility.

Implications for practice and policy

Based on our findings, we outline several potential strategies for improving the quality of neonatal jaundice–related information on social media platforms. Importantly, these suggestions should be interpreted as expert opinion informed by the present results, rather than direct inferences derived from causal evidence.

For healthcare professionals, our data show that videos produced by pediatricians are generally associated with higher quality and reliability scores. This observation suggests that greater professional participation in content creation may help improve overall information quality. In addition, professional organizations could consider providing training focused on effective short-video health communication. Given the known challenges in recognizing jaundice in infants with darker skin tones, future content may benefit from explicitly addressing guidance relevant to families from diverse ethnic backgrounds.

For platform governance, although our study did not directly evaluate algorithmic mechanisms, the observed lack of association between content quality and engagement metrics indicates that current recommendation systems may not preferentially promote high-quality medical information. From an expert perspective, platforms might consider implementing verification mechanisms for healthcare professionals and exploring the incorporation of quality-related signals into recommendation algorithms. Enhanced review processes for medical content could also be explored.

For public health education, discharge counseling could incorporate guidance on both critically evaluating social media content and identifying reliable professional websites for further information. Healthcare providers may proactively recommend trustworthy online resources while acknowledging that parents frequently use social media as an initial information source.

Importantly, improving the quality of health information on social media should not be viewed as a replacement for directing parents to professional medical websites or clinical services. Rather, these approaches are complementary: social media often serves as an initial information source, while professional platforms and healthcare providers remain essential for comprehensive guidance and appropriate clinical action.

Strengths

This study has several notable strengths and novel contributions to digital health research. First, to our knowledge, it is the first study to conduct a systematic cross-platform comparison of neonatal jaundice–related short videos on both TikTok (Douyin) and Bilibili, providing new insights into how health information quality varies across distinct social media ecosystems. Second, the use of two standardized assessment tools (GQS and mDISCERN), combined with independent double rating and excellent inter-rater reliability (ICC > 0.95), demonstrates a methodologically rigorous and reproducible approach for evaluating the quality and reliability of short-form health videos. This standardized framework may serve as a reference for future studies examining digital health content across platforms and topics. Third, our analysis showed no significant association between video engagement metrics and assessed quality or reliability. This observation suggests that highly engaged videos are not necessarily those with higher informational value, and that engagement metrics alone may be insufficient indicators of content quality in digital health contexts. Importantly, this study explicitly focuses on structural quality and information reliability rather than formal clinical accuracy or detailed misinformation coding, ensuring consistency with the study design and analytic scope. Finally, multiple statistical approaches were applied to confirm that the main findings were robust to different analytical methods.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, we analyzed only the top 100 videos per platform, which may not represent all available content. Second, findings may not generalize to other platforms (e.g., WeChat Channels, YouTube). Third, despite rigorous double rating, GQS and mDISCERN assess structural quality rather than clinical accuracy. Fourth, we did not systematically code for specific misinformation content. Fifth, comment content and sentiment were not analyzed.

Additionally, despite excluding shares due to multicollinearity, residual correlation among engagement metrics may influence estimates.

Algorithmic instability represents an inherent challenge in social media research. Our search strategy relied on the platforms’ default algorithmic ranking (‘Comprehensive Sort’), which is algorithm-driven and potentially unstable across sessions; repeating the same search at a different time may yield different video rankings, affecting sample reproducibility. Even after logging out and clearing cookies, search rankings are not perfectly neutral. Search results are rarely identical for everyone; the order often shifts based on the user’s location, the device they use, and when the search is performed. Consequently, another researcher conducting the same search on the same day may have obtained a different set of 100 videos, limiting the reproducibility of our sample selection. Additionally, platform algorithms change frequently, and content visibility and engagement patterns observed during our data collection period may not reflect current or future platform behavior. In addition, we did not assess how video content accounts for patient diversity, including differences in ethnicity, skin tone, language, or health literacy. Addressing how health information is tailored to diverse populations represents an important gap in the current literature and a key priority for future research.

Conclusion

This study provides the first systematic cross-platform evaluation of the quality and reliability of neonatal jaundice–related short videos on TikTok (Douyin) and Bilibili. Overall, information quality on both platforms was relatively high, with no significant differences observed at the platform level. Importantly, associations were identified between video quality and certain characteristics: videos produced by pediatricians and those focusing on disease knowledge and treatment care were associated with higher quality and reliability scores, whereas experience-sharing content from non-professional creators tended to score lower. These findings should be interpreted as correlational rather than causal. In addition, no significant correlations were observed between engagement metrics and quality scores, suggesting a disconnect between popularity and assessed information quality. While this observation does not establish causal mechanisms, it highlights a potential “quality–popularity paradox” that may have implications for platform governance and public health communication. Based on these correlational findings, several considerations may be proposed. Greater involvement of healthcare professionals in content creation may contribute to improving the overall informational environment, and platforms could consider exploring ways to incorporate quality-related signals into recommendation systems. From a user perspective, parents may benefit from being guided to prioritize content from verified professional sources, while recognizing that popularity does not necessarily reflect information quality We still need to find out why this quality gap exists. Future work should look at other platforms and follow babies over a longer period. Testing different ways to help will be key to finding out what really makes a difference.
