Ultra-processed food intake and risk of renal cell carcinoma in the NIH-AARP Diet and health study: a prospective cohort analysis
Authors: Kaelyn F Burns, Mark P Purdue, Neha Khandpur, Euridice Martinez-Steele, Rashmi Sinha, Hyokyoung G Hong, Erikka Loftfield
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundUltra-processed foods (UPF) are widely consumed and have been linked to obesity and hypertension, risk factors of renal cell carcinoma (RCC) and the common clear cell (cc) RCC subtype. We evaluated the association between UPF and RCC.MethodsIn the NIH-AARP Diet and Health Study, a US prospective cohort, participants with no cancer history at baseline (October 1995–May 1996) were followed until the date of first cancer diagnosis, death, or end of follow-up (December 31, 2018). Diet was self-reported using a food frequency questionnaire (FFQ). FFQ items were assigned to Nova classifications to determine UPF intake (grams per day per 1000 kcal). Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed on May 12, 2026, for cohort and case–control studies evaluating associations between ultra-processed foods (UPF) and renal cell carcinoma (RCC). The following search terms used were: (“ultra-processed food” OR “ultraprocessed food” OR “food processing”) AND (“renal cell carcinoma” OR “kidney cancer”). Our search identified three prospective studies. All studies found positive associations between UPF intake and RCC risk, although the results of two studies had non-statistically significant results given limited RCC diagnoses in the cohorts. Further, none of the studies evaluated RCC histological subtypes. Conducting an analysis in a cohort with sufficient RCC diagnoses to have precise estimates and to stratify by RCC histological subtypes is important to further clarify the relationship between UPF intake and RCC risk.

Added value of this study

The findings of this study add to the limited literature on the association between UPF intake and RCC risk. Our results showed that higher UPF intake was associated with higher risk of RCC overall, and of the clear cell RCC subtype. When adjusting for body mass index, and established RCC risk factor, a positive association remained, particularly for the clear cell subtype. This study was conducted among a large cohort with a greater number of RCC diagnoses than previous work, providing greater precision for effect estimates. Moreover, our study considered RCC histological subtypes, which is critical to understanding the potential mechanism underlying a UPF-RCC association since obesity is most strongly related to the clear cell RCC subtype. Finally, this study is the only one to use a food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) that was validated for UPF intake estimation using multiple 24-h dietary recalls, demonstrating the performance of the exposure. In addition, we accounted for measurement error in the FFQ using regression calibration, which strengthened the observed associations, providing evidence that measurement error attenuated associations towards the null.

Implications of all the available evidence

UPF intake has been increasing in the US, in parallel with increasing RCC rates. Our findings strengthen the current body of evidence, which suggests that dietary patterns that minimize intake of UPF and associated weight gain may reduce risk of RCC. These results could inform RCC prevention methods through diet modification. Future research may investigate mechanisms, beyond obesity, that link UPF to RCC.

Introduction

Incidence rates of kidney and renal pelvis cancer have increased in the US over the past two decades, and an estimated 80,980 cases will be diagnosed in 2025.1 Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) accounts for over 90% of kidney cancers,1 with clear cell RCC (cc-RCC) the most common subtype that accounts for the majority of kidney cancer deaths.2

RCC rates have increased in parallel with increasing consumption of ultra-processed foods (UPF), one of several factors that may have contributed to the rise in RCC diagnoses.3, 4, 5 UPF, defined using the Nova classification system, are food products made of several ingredients and manufactured using various processing techniques.6 Diets high in UPF have been linked to weight gain in randomized, controlled cross-over trials,7,8 and higher UPF intake is associated with obesity and an increased risk of hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and certain cancers.9,10 Established risk factors of RCC include obesity and hypertension11; however, only three prospective studies have evaluated the association between UPF and RCC risk, and these studies included relatively small case numbers, restricting the statistical power to detect modest associations, and did not consider histologic supbtype.12, 13, 14

The objective of this study was to evaluate the association between Nova-defined UPF intake, measured using a food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) validated for UPF intake, with RCC and cc-RCC risk in the NIH-AARP Diet and Health Study, a large prospective cohort with over 20 years of follow-up. Given UPF is linked with obesity and hypertension, established risk factors of RCC, we hypothesized that higher UPF intake would be associated with higher RCC and cc-RCC risk.

Methods

Study design and participants

The NIH-AARP is a US population-based cohort study that has been described in detail previously.15 Briefly, participants aged 50–71 years were recruited from 3.5 million AARP members from six states (California, Florida, Pennsylvania, New Jersey, North Carolina, and Louisiana) and two metropolitan areas (Atlanta, Georgia and Detroit, Michigan) between October 1995 and May 1996. Participants were mailed a 16-page questionnaire that inquired about demographic, lifestyle, and health characteristics. Returning the baseline questionnaire by mail was considered providing informed consent to participate in the study. A second questionnaire, the Risk Factor Questionnaire (RFQ), was mailed to participants 6 months after completion of the baseline questionnaire. The NIH-AARP Study was approved by the Special Studies Institutional Review Board of the National Cancer Institute. The current study was exempted from review given it was an analysis of deidentified data.

Participants were followed from baseline to the date of first primary cancer diagnosis, death, or end of follow-up on December 31, 2018. Incident RCC cases were identified through probabilistic linkages to cancer registries of the eight states/metropolitan areas of baseline study recruitment, as well as three states that were popular for relocation during follow-up (Arizona, Texas, Nevada). In a comparison of cancer registry case ascertainment with Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) Program estimates and self-report, it was determined that approximately 90% of incident cancers were identified through cancer registries.16 RCC was defined using the International Classification of Disease for Oncology (ICD-O) 3rd edition topography code C649 and histology codes 8140–8575. Carcinoma in situ diagnoses were not included. We also evaluated the cc-RCC subtype (ICD-O C649, histology code 8310). As an exploratory analysis, we further classified the RCC cases that were not cc-RCC as either RCC Not Otherwise Specified (NOS) (ICD-O C649, histology code 8312) or non-cc-RCC (ICD-O C649, histology codes 8140-8575, excluding 8310 and 8312).

Procedures

Demographic, lifestyle, and health characteristic data were collected at baseline via a self-reported questionnaire. Options provided for indicating sex were male or female.

Dietary intake was assessed using an FFQ at baseline that inquired about habitual intake over the previous 12 months. The FFQ included 124 food and beverage frequency items, of which 116 queried about portion size, and 21 additional questions about food choices and cooking practices, resulting in 204-line items.17 FFQ line items were comprised of underlying food codes based on the 1994–1996 USDA Continuing Survey of Food Intake of Individuals (CSFII) database.18 The CSFII food codes were then translated 1:1 to Food and Nutrient Database for Dietary Study (FNDDS) codes, which were further disaggregated into component FNDDS and standard reference (SR) codes, as previously described by Steele et al.19 in their approach for classifying foods according to the Nova system. Nova aims to classify foods into four categories based on their level of processing (group 1: unprocessed or minimally processed food (MPF); group 2: processed culinary ingredients; group 3: processed foods; group 4: ultra-processed foods).6 FNDDS food codes and SR codes were assigned to a Nova group through database linkage,19 and gram weight and energy intake values for each Nova group were summed. Alcohol was not included in our UPF estimate so that it could be adjusted for in statistical models. Nutrient density adjusted20 grams of UPF (grams per day per 1000 kcal) was used as our primary exposure, as in a paper that aimed to evaluate the performance of the FFQ for estimating UPF intake compared to two non-consecutive 24-h dietary recalls, we previously demonstrated that UPF intake based on energy-adjusted gram weight has higher validity than UPF intake based on energy intake alone.18 Secondary exposures evaluated were UPF intake operationalized as percentage of calorie intake per day (%kcal/day) and percentage of gram intake per day (%g/day). Intake of UPF subgroups (grams per day per 1000 kcal) were also estimated.19

Statistical analysis

There were 566,398 participants that completed a satisfactory baseline questionnaire in the cohort. We excluded those that used a proxy reporter to complete the questionnaire (n = 15,760), had self-reported cancer (n = 51,120) or end-stage renal disease at baseline (n = 997), had extreme calorie consumption defined as >2 interquartile ranges <25th or >75th percentile of Box–Cox log-transformed intakes by sex (n = 3757), had zero person-years of follow-up (n = 52), or had a death record for cancer but no registry-confirmed diagnosis (n = 14,364), resulting in an analytic cohort of N = 480,347 participants.

In a secondary analysis, we used data on hypertension status which was only inquired about on the RFQ. The RFQ was completed by 334,905 participants, and after applying the same exclusion criteria and further excluding those who were missing data on hypertension status (n = 29,035), there were n = 269,486 participants in the secondary analysis.

The mean percent of gram and calorie intake from UPF subgroups contributing to total UPF intake in the sample was calculated. Participant characteristics were evaluated overall and by quartiles of UPF (grams per day per 1000 kcal) intake.

Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazards ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for RCC and RCC subtype risk by quartiles of UPF intake (grams per day per 1000 kcal), with age serving as the underlying time scale. The base model adjusted for energy intake (kcals/day; continuous) and sex (male, female). Covariates were selected using a directed acyclic graph (DAG) (Supplemental Figure S1). The multivariable model adjusted for energy intake, sex, race/ethnicity (Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, Asian, Pacific Islander, American Indian/Alaskan Native), smoking status (never smoked, stopped smoking ≥10 years ago, stopped 5–9 years ago, stopped 1–4 years ago, stopped within the last year, current smoker), education (<12 years, completed high school or General Educational Development (GED), post-high school training, some college, college), physical activity (never/rarely, low activity, moderate activity, high activity), alcohol intake (0, <1, 1–2, 3–4, ≥5 drink equivalents/day; equivalents standardized to 13–14 g of alcohol/drink), and self-rated health status (excellent, very good, good, fair, poor). In separate models, we further adjusted for diet quality using the Health Eating Index (HEI) 2015 score (continuous) and body mass index (BMI) as these variables may be on the causal pathway between UPF intake and incident RCC. BMI and BMI-squared (both continuous) were included in the model to account for a potential nonlinear relationship with RCC. Missing data for categorical variables were treated as an indicator variable, and missing data for continuous variables were imputed with the median value, with no covariate having >3.8% missing data. We evaluated the linear trend across UPF quartiles by assigning the median value to each quartile and treating it as a continuous variable. We also evaluated the linear association between UPF with RCC by treating UPF intake as a continuous variable (per 200-g per day per 1000 kcal increase). UPF intake (grams per day per 1000 kcal) was also scaled using a 1-standard deviation increase in UPF intake, but results did not meaningfully change. The Schoenfeld residuals were used to assess the proportional hazards assumption; no violation was observed (p = 0.37). Potential effect modification by sex, BMI, or diet quality was assessed by stratification and by using the likelihood ratio test to compare models with and without an interaction term between UPF and sex, BMI categories, and HEI categories [<51 (poor), 51–80 (needs improvement), >80 (good)].

Secondary analyses included operationalizing UPF intake in %g/day and %kcal/day. A lag analysis was conducted to evaluate if the associations differed by earlier and later follow-up periods (0–10 years, ≥10 years). We also further adjusted for hypertension status (yes, no), potentially on the causal pathway between UPF intake and incident RCC, and considered effect modification by hypertension status by stratification and by using the likelihood ratio test to compare models with and without an interaction term between UPF and hypertension status. Finally, associations between UPF subgroups with RCC and cc-RCC were evaluated.

Regression calibration was conducted to account for measurement error in UPF estimation inherent to the FFQ.21 In a sub-cohort (n = 1902)17 that completed two 24-h dietary recalls on non-consecutive days, a linear regression model was conducted where average UPF intake estimated from two 24-h dietary recalls (grams per day per 1000 kcal) was the outcome and UPF intake estimated from the FFQ (grams per day per 1000 kcal) was the predictor, adjusted for energy intake, age, sex, race, smoking, education, physical activity, and alcohol consumption. The intercept and beta coefficients from the model in the sub-cohort were used to estimate predicted UPF intake (grams per day per 1000 kcal) in the full NIH-AARP analytic cohort. The primary analysis was run to estimate the associations for quartiles of predicted UPF with RCC and cc-RCC.

Substitution analyses were conducted22 to evaluate RCC and cc-RCC risk when substituting 200 g per day per 1000 kcal of UPF for 200 g per day per 1000 kcal of non-UPF (Nova groups 1–3) by including total gram intake from Nova groups 1–4 in the model. The effect of substituting 10% g/day of UPF for 10% g/day of non-UPF was also evaluated. In mutually adjusted models, we evaluated the association of UPF (200 g per day per 1000 kcal) and non-UPF (200 g per day per 1000 kcal) intake with RCC by including both variables in the same multivariable model.

All analyses were conducted in RStudio Version 2025.05.1 + 513. Two-sided statistical tests were conducted, with p-values <0.05 used to determine statistical significance.

Role of the funding source

The funding source had no role in the study design, data collection, data analysis, interpretation, or writing of the manuscript.

Results

Among the 480,347 participants included in the analysis, the median (interquartile range [IQR]) grams per day per 1000 kcal, %g/day, and %kcals/day of UPF intake were 293 g (197.7–467.2), 19.9% g/day (12.8, 30.5), and 42.7% kcals/day (35.9–49.6), respectively (Table 1). Overall, the median (IQR) age was 62.6 (57.7–66.6) years, 59.6% (n = 286,150) of participants were male, 91.3% (n = 438,355) were non-Hispanic White, 35.3% (n = 169,403) were never smokers, 38.6% (n = 185,376) completed college, 45.9% (n = 220,696) were highly physically active, 24.3% (n = 116,555) were alcohol non-drinkers, and 33.6% (n = 161,341) had a normal BMI. Those in the highest, vs the lowest, quartile of UPF intake (grams per day per 1000 kcal) tended to be younger (60.9 vs 63.8 years), male (59.4% vs 56.3%), alcohol non-drinkers (30.3% vs 21.9%), never/rarely participate in physical activity (21.1% vs 17.9%), and have obesity (29.2% vs 15.8%) and hypertension (47.7% vs 39.7%). Participant characteristics are presented by sex in Supplemental Table S1. Carbonated soft drinks (27.5%), followed by other sweetened beverages (16.8%), and bread (12.3%) contributed the highest proportion of gram weight to UPF intake. Bread (22%) and baked goods and candy (14.8%) contributed the highest proportion of calories to UPF intake (Fig. 1). The proportion of intake contributed by MPF subgroups to total MPF intake are presented in Supplemental Figure S2.

Over a median (IQR) of 18.3 (9.0–22.5) years of follow-up, 3330 incident cases of RCC were diagnosed, of which 1216 were cc-RCC, 1486 were RCC NOS, and 628 were non-cc-RCC. In the multivariable adjusted model, higher compared to lower UPF intake (grams per day per 1000 kcal) was associated with higher RCC risk [HR (95% CI)Q4 vs Q1 = 1.16 (1.05–1.29), ptrend = 0.0006] (Table 2). The association remained when adjusting for HEI-2015 score [1.13 (1.02–1.25), ptrend = 0.0054]. When adjusting for BMI and BMI-squared, the association remained but was attenuated towards the null [1.09 (0.99–1.20), ptrend = 0.049]. Higher UPF intake was also associated with higher cc-RCC risk [1.38 (1.17–1.62), ptrend <0.0001], which remained when adjusting for HEI-2015 score [1.32 (1.12–1.57), ptrend = 0.0005], and for BMI and BMI-squared [1.22 (1.03–1.44), ptrend = 0.015]. Similar positive associations were observed for quartiles of UPF based on %g/day (RCC ptrend <0.0001; cc-RCC ptrend <0.0001) but not %kcals/day (RCC ptrend = 0.84; cc-RCC ptrend = 0.34) (Supplementary Table S2). Higher UPF intake (grams per day per 1000 kcal) was not associated with higher non-cc-RCC risk [0.95 (0.76–1.19), ptrend = 0.90] or RCC NOS [1.11 (0.96–1.26), ptrend = 0.11], although the estimate lacked precision (Supplementary Table S3).

Discussion

In a large cohort of older US adults, we found that higher UPF intake was associated with higher RCC and cc-RCC risk. These associations were attenuated but remained following adjustment for BMI, diet quality, and hypertension status, indicating that these factors may be on the causal pathway. Regression calibration demonstrated that measurement error, inherent to the FFQ, attenuated the observed associations toward the null, suggesting that the magnitude of association between UPF intake and RCC may be slightly greater than what we observed in the cohort. Finally, the substitution analysis demonstrated that the replacement of gram intake from non-UPF sources with an equal amount of gram intake from UPF sources was associated with higher RCC risk, suggesting the importance of minimizing UPF intake in the context of an overall dietary pattern.

Three prior prospective cohort studies have evaluated the association between UPF intake and RCC. Similar to our study, in the European Prospective Investigation into Cancer and Nutrition (EPIC) study, higher UPF intake was associated with higher RCC risk when adjusting for hypertension, and the association was attenuated when adjusting for BMI.13 In the Prostate, Lung, Colorectal and Ovarian Cancer Screening Trial (PLCO) cohort, a positive association was observed when adjusting for BMI and hypertension,14 and in the UK Biobank (UKB) there was a positive, albeit not statistically significant, association observed in models adjusted for BMI.12 EPIC and PLCO measured diet using an FFQ, but neither evaluated the validity of the FFQ for estimating UPF intake. In the UKB, diet was assessed using a 24-h dietary questionnaire, and approximately 40% of participants completed a single questionnaire, thus random within-person error due to day-to-day variability in diet could have attenuated observed associations. Our study provides an important contribution to this body of literature by using an FFQ validated for estimation of UPF intake and by evaluating measurement error in the FFQ through applying regression calibration.

We defined UPF quartiles based on absolute energy-adjusted gram intake, which we previously demonstrated resulted in higher validity coefficients for estimating UPF intake compared to UPF intake based on energy alone.18 Conversely, each of the previously conducted studies operationalized UPF intake as percentage of gram intake (%gram/day), which is a relative measure dependent on the total gram amount a person consumes. In the NIH-AARP calibration sub-cohort, relative and absolute grams of UPF are highly correlated (Spearman r = 0.83).18 However, we found that HR estimates for RCC and cc-RCC were stronger when categorizing participants based on %gram/day intake, perhaps reflecting not only a dietary pattern high in UPF but also one low in MPF. When operationalizing UPF intake as %kcal/day associations were null. One reason for this may be that ultra-processed beverages, like those that are artificially sweetened, contribute substantial gram weight to the diet but relatively few calories. Further research could aim to untangle the differing results for UPF intake operationalized as %gram/day vs %kcal/day on associations with cancer incidence.

Lifestyle factors are associated with RCC risk.2 Obesity is hypothesized to increase RCC risk through mechanisms including chronic inflammation and insulin resistance leading to an altered metabolic state,23 and hypertension may contribute to RCC development by increasing the risk of renal injury and leading to renal dysfunction.24 In randomized, crossover feeding trials, domiciled participants gained weight on a high-UPF diet,7,8 and in epidemiologic studies, higher UPF intake has been associated with obesity and higher hypertension risk,10 making it biologically plausible that UPF intake is associated higher RCC risk. In our analyses, we added these potential pathway variables to our multivariable adjusted models to evaluate their influence on the estimates. We found that adjustment for BMI partially attenuated HR estimates, suggesting that BMI may be explaining a portion of the observed positive association, while hypertension status had less of an attenuating effect. In stratified analyses, BMI and hypertension did not appear to be effect modifiers of the association. Future work could conduct a formal mediation analysis, with BMI assessed at multiple time points, to further investigate the mediating role of BMI in the association between UPF and RCC.

The cc-RCC subtype is the subtype that has been most strongly linked with obesity. Obesity and obesity-related chronic inflammation are associated with higher amounts of reactive oxygen species, which are genotoxic.25,26 Specifically, reactive oxygen species can contribute to mutation of the VHL gene,25,26 a tumor suppressor that is the most frequently mutated gene in cc-RCC.2,27 Although the effect estimates for the models evaluating RCC and cc-RCC were not significantly different from each other, we found modestly stronger associations between UPF intake and the cc-RCC subtype compared to models evaluating overall RCC, aligning with the idea that obesity is more closely linked with cc-RCC. Although, even with adjustment for BMI, a positive association remained. These results suggest additional mechanisms, beyond the effects of BMI, may be involved. For example, other aspects of UPF could play a role, such as food additives like sodium, which has been linked to renal dysfunction decline and chronic kidney disease progression,28,29 or emulsifiers, which have been shown to alter the gut microbiome in experimental studies, leading to inflammation and metabolic dysregulation.30,31 Compounds created during food processing such as acrylamides, or from food packaging such as phthalates and bisphenol, could also play a role.9 Additional research is needed to elucidate mechanisms, beyond BMI and hypertension, linking UPF to RCC and the cc-RCC subtype.

We did not observe effect modification by sex or diet quality. RCC is more common among males than females in the US,1 and there are sex-specific differences in metabolism.32 Lack of effect modification by sex could be partially explained by little difference in UPF intake by sex our study sample (median [IQR]: males = 298 [202–466] g per day per 1000 kcal; females = 286 [192–468] g per day per 1000 kcal), consistent with similar intake among males and females in the US population.33 We hypothesized that the association of UPF intake and RCC risk may be modified by diet quality, where those with poor diet quality would have higher risk compared to those with good diet quality. In analyses stratified by HEI score, there was some evidence that those with HEI scores classified as poor had higher risk than those with good HEI scores, although strata had small sample sizes, resulting in imprecise estimates. Further research investigating the joint effects of both diet quality and UPF intake on cancer incidence is warranted.

Our study builds on previous work evaluating the association between UPF intake and RCC by investigating associations with the cc-RCC subtype. Strengths of our study include its large size and extended follow-up time. Our method for disaggregating FFQ items to assign Nova classification enhances reliability of our UPF measure and was shown to perform well when compared to repeated 24 h dietary recalls in the NIH-AARP calibration sub-cohort.18 Leveraging this same sub-cohort, we used regression calibration to demonstrate that measurement error in FFQ-derived UPF intake attenuated associations with RCC toward the null.

Our study also has limitations. Self-reported dietary intake was collected using an FFQ, which is prone to measurement error and was not designed to capture level of food processing, potentially resulting in non-differential misclassification of UPF intake which would result in attenuated associations. In addition, dietary intake was only assessed at baseline, and UPF availability and consumption have increased over recent decades,4,5 likely resulting in our UPF intake estimate being conservative. The NIH-AARP is a cohort of older, primarily White US adults, and results may not generalize to other populations. Given the majority of participants in the sample were non-Hispanic White and there were small sample sizes among minority racial categories, analyses were not stratified by race. We cannot rule out confounding owing to unmeasured or poorly measured confounders. Finally, as with all epidemiologic research, random error may have added variability to our results; however, this was not a major concern given our study has a large sample size and our effect estimates had relatively narrow confidence intervals.

In conclusion, in a large, US cohort of older adults who were followed for more than 20 years, higher UPF intake was associated with higher RCC and cc-RCC risk. Associations were independent of BMI, hypertension status, and diet quality. These results could inform RCC prevention methods through diet modification and future mechanistic research on RCC etiology.
