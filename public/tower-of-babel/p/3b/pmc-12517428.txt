Biological roles and potential clinical value of tRNA-derived small RNAs in gastrointestinal malignancies
Authors: Yongfu Shao, Xuan Yu, Weihong Zhou, Jianing Yan, Haotian Dong, Guoliang Ye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstracttRNA-derived small RNAs (tsRNAs) are small RNA fragments generated from tRNA molecules or their precursors through cleavage by enzymes such as Dicer. As noncoding small RNAs, tsRNAs have important biological functions, including regulating mRNA stability and protein translation, serving as epigenetic regulators, and participating in intercellular communication. Recent studies have indicated that the dysregulation of tsRNAs is closely linked to the occurrence and progression of gastrointestinal malignancies. Increasing evidence suggests that abnormally expressed tsRNAs play crucial roles in various mechanisms related to the proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, invasion, metastasis, cellular metabolism, immune evasion and angiogenesis of gastrointestinal malignancies. The vast quantity, high abundance, diverse functions and tissue- and disease-specific expression of tsRNAs make them promising gastrointestinal malignancy biomarkers in terms of clinical screening, prognostic assessment and gene therapy. The biological roles and potential clinical value of tsRNAs offer novel insights into the prevention and treatment of gastrointestinal malignancies.

Introduction

Gastrointestinal malignancies, including oesophageal cancer, gastric, colon, liver and pancreatic cancers, are a major global public health challenge and continue to rank highly in terms of incidence and mortality rates among all types of malignancies [1]. Recent cancer statistics show that the number of new cases and deaths from gastrointestinal malignancies account for 25% and 33.3% of all malignancies worldwide, respectively, with significant differences in regional distribution and demographics [2]. Owing to their high incidence rate, insidious onset, high degree of malignancy, and easy metastasis, gastrointestinal malignancies pose a serious threat to human health [3–6].

Most gastrointestinal malignancies present subtle early symptoms, and approximately 60–70% of patients are diagnosed with advanced or metastatic tumours, leading to significantly reduced clinical response rates or poor patient prognosis [7]. Despite the availability of various physical examination methods for diagnosing gastrointestinal malignancies, such as computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and endoscopy, their sensitivity for detecting smaller lesions or residual tumours in the early stages is generally unsatisfactory [8]. Moreover, the sensitivity and specificity of tumour biomarkers currently used in clinical practice for the early screening of gastrointestinal malignancies, such as CEA, CA19-9 and CA72-4, fail to meet clinical needs [9]. Therefore, with breakthroughs in molecular biology technology and in-depth multiomics research, understanding the biological essence of gastrointestinal malignancies is highly practical and clinically valuable, providing new targets for early diagnosis and targeted therapy. tRNA-derived small RNAs (tsRNAs) are a class of noncoding small RNAs with important biological functions. In recent years, tsRNAs have garnered considerable attention because of their roles in various biological processes and pathophysiological conditions. Unlike transfer RNAs (tRNAs), which participate in protein synthesis via the transportation of amino acids, tsRNAs are smaller fragments of tRNA molecules or their precursors generated through enzymatic cleavage by enzymes such as Dicer [10,11]. tsRNAs significantly affect multiple physiological processes through various mechanisms, including regulating transcription, reverse transcription, protein translation and epigenetics, and participate in various processes, such as cellular stress, protein translation, apoptosis, ribosome biosynthesis, epigenetic regulation, immunity and inflammatory responses [12–15]. tsRNAs are closely associated with the occurrence and progression of chronic diseases, such as cardiovascular, neurological and immune diseases, and certain tsRNAs have become important characteristics of these diseases [13,16,17]. Abnormal expression or dysfunction of tsRNAs may lead to disease occurrence, and downregulating pathogenic genes regulated by tsRNAs may aid in disease treatment [18]. In the field of gastrointestinal malignancies, dozens of tsRNAs have been discovered to be significantly correlated with human malignancies. These tsRNA molecules participate in the regulation of gene expression through multiple mechanisms, such as epigenetic modification, transcriptional regulation and posttranscriptional processing, thereby affecting the biological processes of malignant cells [19–21]. During the progression of gastrointestinal malignancies, tsRNAs not only participate in the regulation of cancer cell invasiveness and metastasis but are also significantly correlated with the clinical prognoses of patients [22–25]. These findings provide new directions for research on gastrointestinal malignancies.

The relationship between tsRNAs and gastrointestinal malignancies is an important research area. In this review, we provide a detailed description of the sources, classification, biological characteristics and biological functions of tsRNAs. We also introduce the roles and molecular mechanisms of tsRNAs in gastrointestinal malignancies from the perspectives of cell proliferation, cell differentiation, cell cycle regulation, apoptosis, immune escape, cell metabolism, invasion, metastasis and tumour angiogenesis. Additionally, we discuss the potential clinical applications of tsRNAs in the diagnosis, prognosis and treatment of gastrointestinal malignancies, aiming to promote their clinical translation. The biological roles and potential clinical value of tsRNAs provide a new perspective for the prevention and treatment of gastrointestinal malignancies.

Biogenesis, classification and biological characteristics of tsRNAs

tsRNAs are single-stranded RNA fragments formed after the cleavage of mature tRNA or its precursor tRNA (pre-tRNA) by specific ribonucleic acid endonucleases [26]. The discovery of tsRNAs can be traced to the 1980s, when scientists first detected seven tRNA breakdown products in urine [27]. However, owing to the limitations of experimental technology and research conditions at that time, connections between tsRNAs and diseases were not discovered. Therefore, tsRNAs were long considered to be ‘waste’ produced by the degradation of tRNA. In recent years, with the rapid development of molecular biology technologies, scientists have gradually recognized the importance of tsRNAs. Studies have demonstrated that these RNA fragments are not only degradation products but also active molecules that regulate crucial biological processes in cells [28]. The expression of tsRNAs is relatively tissue-specific and dynamically regulated during biological development. For example, tRNA halves (tiRNAs) derived from tRNA-Arg-TCT-4-1 are specifically expressed in the human brain, whereas tRNA-derived RNA fragments (tRFs) derived from tRNA-Arg-CCG-2-1 are specifically expressed in the ovary, heart and skeletal muscle [29]. In addition to being regulated via cleavage by certain enzymes such as Dicer, the expression of tsRNAs is also induced by various stresses, such as nutrient deficiency, low temperature and hypoxia. For example, hypoxia significantly increases the expression levels of tDR-0009 and tDR-7336 [30], whereas various other stresses, such as nutrient deficiency, low temperature or hypoxia, significantly induce the expression of 5′tiRNA-Val-AAC [31]. Notably, while most documented tsRNA generation involves enzyme activity, certain stresses might induce tRNA fragmentation through non-enzymatic pathways, such as metal-ion-catalysed hydrolysis or oxidative cleavage. For instance, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated can cause RNA damage and fragmentation independent of classic ribonucleases [32]. Nevertheless, the exact mechanisms, especially enzyme-independent routes, remain incompletely understood. Therefore, further investigation is needed to fully elucidate the regulatory networks governing tsRNA biogenesis under diverse physiological and pathological conditions.tsRNA types are related to the types of endonucleases, cleavage sites and stress conditions [33,34]. Depending on their production methods, tsRNAs can be divided into two major categories – tiRNAs and tRFs [10] (Figure 1). tRFs range from 14 to 30 nt in length and are produced from pre-tRNA or mature tRNA [26]. These tRF fragments can be further subdivided into different types on the basis of their initiation site as follows: tRF-1, tRF-2, tRF-3, tRF-5 and i-tRF [35]. tRF-1 is produced by RNase Z/ELAC2 cleavage of the 3′ end of pre-tRNA and is composed of a fragment from the 3′ end of mature tRNA to 4 U nucleotides, with poly U characteristics; hence, tRF-1 is also referred to as 3′UtRF [36]. tRF-3 is produced by Dicer cleavage; Angiogenin (ANG) cleaves the T loop of mature tRNA and tRF-3 can be further separated into tRF-3a (length of 18 nt) and tRF-3b (length of 22 nt) [37,38]. tRF-5 includes the region from the 5′ end of mature tRNA to the D loop or anticodon loop and is formed primarily upon Dicer cleavage [39]. Depending on the different cleavage site, tRF-5 can be further subdivided into three subtypes, namely, tRF-5a (14–16 nt), tRF-5b (22–24 nt) and tRF-5c (28–30 nt) [28,39]. Interestingly, tRF-2 and i-tRF, as atypical tsRNAs, originate mostly from nuclear-encoded and mitochondrial-encoded tRNAs, the sequences of these tRFs do not start with the first nucleotide at the 5′ end of tRNA nor end with any base in the 3′ end of tRNA [40]. Studies have shown that tRF-2 is produced under hypoxic stress by the enzymatic cleavage of the anticodon loop, whereas i-tRF originates mainly from the anticodon loop, D loop and part of the T loop [41,42]. tiRNAs are produced by ANG cleavage of the anticodon loop of mature tRNA, with a length of approximately half that of mature tRNA. Depending on whether they include the 5′ or 3′ sequence of the cleavage site of the anticodon loop, they can be further subdivided into 5′-tiRNAs and 3′-tiRNAs [43]. tiRNAs are usually produced under stress conditions, such as hypoxia, heat shock, oxidative stress, amino acid deficiency, viral infection or radiation, but they can also be produced under specific nonstress conditions [44].

The biogenesis and classification of tsRNAs. Distinct tsRNA species arise through enzymatic processing of pre-tRNAs or mature tRNAs under diverse stressors including hypoxia, viral infection and radiation. Pre-tRNAs undergo site-specific cleavage by endonucleases (e.g. RNase Z/ELAC2) to generate tRF-1 fragments. tRF-3 originates from the T loop of mature tRNAs and can be divided into tRF-3a and tRF-3b. tRF-5 originates from the D loop or anticodon loop of mature RNAs and can be classified into tRF-5a, tRF-5b and tRF-5c. i-tRF and tRF-2 originate from the anticodon loops of mature tsRNAs. tiRNAs are classified into 3′-tiRNA and 5′-tiRNA based on the cleavage sites of the anticodon loop.

In the field of tsRNAs research, a widely adopted nomenclature has been established to concisely convey the biogenetic origin and classification of these fragments. Specifically, tRF-5 and tRF-3 refer to fragments derived from the 5′ end and 3′ end of mature tRNAs, respectively; i-tRF designates fragments originating entirely from the internal regions of mature tRNAs; while 5′-tiRNA and 3′-tiRNA denote the half-molecules produced by cleavage within the anticodon loop. This systematic naming convention incorporates essential information including the fragment type, the corresponding tRNA amino acid, anticodon identity and fragment length. It has been widely implemented by authoritative databases such as MINTbase and reflects a growing academic consensus [45]. Nevertheless, non-standard nomenclature may still appear in certain earlier studies or outputs from high-throughput analysis softwares. For instance, ‘tRF-19-Q1Q89PJZ’ typically represents an internal identifier assigned by bioinformatics software, and the suffix ‘-2’ in ‘tiRNA-Val-CAC-2’ often indicates an isoform derived from the same parental tRNA. When such non-canonical names are encountered, it is advisable to query public databases such as MINTbase using the sequence to obtain standardized nomenclature and comprehensive biological annotations.

Mechanisms of action of tsRNAs

Regulating mRNA stability

Owing to their structural and molecular similarity to miRNAs, tsRNAs exhibit similar biological mechanisms of action and exert biological effects by targeting mRNAs to regulate their stability [46]. Regarding the mode of action, tsRNAs primarily affect mRNA stability through three pathways (Figure 2(A)). First, tsRNAs form duplex structures with the 3′ untranslated region (UTR) of target mRNAs through sequence complementarity, regulating the binding of mRNAs to proteins such as AUF1 and HuR, thereby affecting mRNA stability [47]. For example, tRF/miR-1280 reduces the stability and expression of JAG2 mRNA by targeting its 3′ UTR, further suppressing the downstream Notch signalling pathway and thus decreasing colon cancer cell proliferation [22]. Second, tsRNAs interact with RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) and form complexes, directly regulating the stability of target mRNAs [47]. For example, under hypoxic conditions, many tsRNAs generated from tRNAGlu, tRNAAsp, tRNAGly and tRNATyr in cancer cells decrease the stability of oncogene transcripts by competitively binding to the RBP YBX1, thereby suppressing the progression of breast cancer [41]. Additionally, tsRNAs affect mRNA stability by regulating the activity of mRNA-modifying enzymes. For example, 5′-tRF-GlyGCC directly binds to FTO to increase its demethylase activity, which reduces the methylation level of eIF4G1, thereby affecting its mRNA stability and promoting cancer cell proliferation and metastasis [48]. Notably, some tsRNAs possess bidirectional regulatory capabilities. Under oxidative stress conditions, induced tiRNA-Gly-GCC binds to angiogenin with endonuclease activity, accelerating its degradation by cleaving the tail of the target mRNA [48], whereas under chronic inflammatory conditions, tiRNA-Gly-GCC binds to and increases the stability of proinflammatory cytokine mRNA [49]. These phenomena illustrate the environmentally dependent regulatory plasticity of tsRNAs.

(A) Regulation of mRNAs stability. tsRNAs modulate mRNA stability through several pathways: (1) direct binding to target mRNA, altering its interaction with stabilizing or destabilizing proteins; (2) association with RBPs under specific conditions; (3) regulation of mRNA-modifying enzyme activity. (B) Modulation of protein translation. (1) Competitively binding the eIF4E/eIF4G complex, thereby impeding translation initiation; (2) interacting with 18S rRNA, which can affect translation efficiency. (C) Influence on epigenetic processes. tsRNAs participate in epigenetic regulation through several pathways. (1) Binding PIWI proteins, impacting intracellular RNA processing and histone modifications; (2) association with snRNAs, influencing the expression of downstream histone genes. Regulation of protein translation

Protein translation is a complex biological process. tsRNAs regulate protein translation through various molecular mechanisms (Figure 2(B)). Recent studies have shown that, in addition to regulating mRNA stability, tsRNAs also regulate translation by directly interfering with the assembly of translation initiation complexes, regulating ribosomal protein-encoding gene transcription and affecting ribosome biogenesis [50]. 5′-tiRNAs block cap-dependent translation initiation by competitively binding to the eIF4E/eIF4G translation initiation factor complex through its terminal oligoguanine sequence. For example, 5′-tiRNAAla, which is formed under stress conditions by ANG cleavage of mature tRNA anticodon loops, assembles into an RNA G-quadruplex (RG4) and binds to Y-box binding protein (YB-1), which inhibits the function of eIF4E–eIF4G and leads to the inability of the 43S initiation complex to correctly anchor to the 5′UTR of target mRNA, thereby interfering with protein translation [51]. Unlike the mechanism of 5′-tiRNAAla, TOG-5′tsRNA has a pseudouridine at the 8th position, and the resulting RG4 binds to poly A-binding protein cytoplasmic 1 (PABPC1), leading to a decrease in its mRNA translation ability [52,53]. Thus, RG4, which is composed of 5′-tiRNA or 5′-tRF, is not only a key component of the cellular stress response but also essential for tsRNA regulation of mRNA translation [53]. High-resolution cryo-electron microscopy studies have shown that some tsRNAs bind to specific regions of 18S rRNA through sequence complementarity, altering the ribosome conformation and affecting its translation elongation efficiency [54]. For example, tRF-3 binds to Twi12, an AGO/PIWI family member, to regulate ribosome biogenesis [55]. In addition, La/SSB, a molecular chaperone, binds to the stem loop of the internal ribosome entry site (IRES) of viruses to promote protein translation initiation. Studies have shown that tRF-U3-1 inhibits HCV IRES-mediated translation by sequestering La/SSB from the cytoplasm [56].

Regulation of epigenetics

Epigenetic processes refer to heritable changes in gene expression without alteration of the gene DNA sequence. Abnormal epigenetic regulation of the genome is intricately linked to the occurrence and progression of tumours. Recent studies have revealed that tsRNAs constitute a crucial class of epigenetic regulators that are capable of modulating gene transcription activity by influencing DNA methylation, histone modification and chromosomal remodelling [57,58] (Figure 2(C)). PIWI proteins regulate epigenetic processes related to cell growth and development [59]. Currently, research on tsRNAs as epigenetic regulators has focused primarily on the epigenetic roles of the PIWI–tsRNA complex in intracellular RNA processing and histone modification. Couvillion et al. reported that 3′ tsRNAs activate Xrn2 exonuclease activity by binding to the Twi family protein Twi12 in the nucleus, thereby processing ribosomal RNA and promoting cell proliferation [60]. Siomi et al. reported that 5′-tsRNAGlu interacts with intracellular PIWIL4 to promote H3K9 methylation, thereby inhibiting the transcription of CD1A [61]. Furthermore, Dnmt1 mediates DNA methylation and suppresses the majority of non-long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons. In contrast, 3′tsRNA inhibits LTR retrotransposon activity through a mechanism involving complementary binding to the primer binding site (PBS), thereby influencing the activity of endogenous retroviruses [62].

In addition to their PIWI-dependent role in epigenetics, tsRNAs also alter epigenetics through other pathways. For example, Bošković and Rando reported that the expression level of tRF-Gly-GCC influences the stability and function of snRNAs. By base pairing with downstream elements of histones, snRNAs regulate histone expression, ultimately affecting chromatin accessibility [63]. Furthermore, tRFGluTTC has been identified as an epigenetic regulator of adipogenesis by inhibiting adipogenesis by downregulating several adipogenic transcription factors, such as aP2, PPARγ and C/EBPα [64].

Participation in intercellular communication

Recent studies have revealed that tsRNAs serve as novel mediators of intercellular signalling. tsRNAs undergo cross-cellular transport within the tissue microenvironment via extracellular vesicles (EVs), such as exosomes and microvesicles, establishing a complex intercellular communication network [65]. In the tumour microenvironment (TME), exosomal tsRNAs secreted by tumour cells facilitate the formation of premetastatic niches (PMNs) by modulating signalling pathways in receptor cells (Figure 2(D)). For example, tRF-1003 is significantly upregulated in serum exosomes from patients with multiple myeloma. Further investigation has revealed that tRF-1003 in exosomes is effectively delivered to endothelial cells, where it activates the downstream HIF-1α/VEGF signalling pathway by inhibiting MAPK1 expression, subsequently inducing angiogenesis [66]. Similarly, exosome-delivered tRF-41 inhibits the growth of astrocytes by regulating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and the inflammatory response [67]. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) constitute crucial components of the TME. During PD-1 immunotherapy for oesophageal cancer, MMP14-positive CAFs inhibit the tumoricidal effect of CD8+ T cells in the TME via exosomal tsRNA-10522 [68].

Relationships between tsRNAs and gastrointestinal malignancies

Cell proliferation, differentiation and cell cycle regulation

In gastrointestinal malignancies, tsRNAs regulate key oncogenic signalling pathways or factors involved in the abnormal proliferation and differentiation of cancer cells by targeting mRNAs or interacting with RBPs. Studies have shown that tRF-3019a, which is significantly overexpressed in gastric cancer cells, directly binds to the 3′UTR of the FBXO47 tumour suppressor gene and interacts with AGO2, leading to significant inhibition of FBOX47 expression at the mRNA level, thereby promoting the abnormal proliferation of gastric cancer cells [69] (Figure 3(A)). Another study has shown that 5′tRF-Gly, which is significantly overexpressed in hepatocellular carcinoma cells, directly targets carcinoembryonic antigen-related cell adhesion molecule 1 (CEACAM1), silencing its expression and promoting the abnormal proliferation and differentiation of hepatocellular carcinoma [70]. Similarly, tiRNA-Val-CAC-2, which is specifically overexpressed in pancreatic cancer tissues, specifically binds to the KH3–KH4 domain in the FUBP1 RBP, leading to increased stability of the FUBP1 protein and increased enrichment of FUBP1 in the c-MYC promoter region, thereby promoting the transcription of c-MYC and forming a positive feedback loop for proliferation [71] (Figure 3(B)). Conversely, tRF-19-3L7L73JD and tiRNA-Val-CAC-001 are significantly downregulated in gastric cancer tissues [72,73]. Mechanistic studies have shown that tRF-19-3L7L73JD inhibits the proliferation of gastric cancer cells by blocking the cell cycle in the G0/G1 phase [72], whereas tiRNA-Val-CAC-001 inhibits the proliferation and metastasis of gastric cancer cells by modulating LRP6 expression to regulate the Wnt/β-catenin signalling pathway, thereby slowing the progression of gastric cancer [73]. In hepatocellular carcinoma, 5′-tiRNA-Gln binds to eukaryotic initiation factor 4A-1 (EIF4A1), which disrupts the secondary structure of RNA during translation initiation, thereby inhibiting the translation of ARAF, MEK1/2 and STAT3 proteins, which further disrupts downstream signalling pathways that promote hepatocellular carcinoma cell proliferation and progression [74].

(E) Following binding to AGO protein, tRF-33 negatively regulates STAT3 and p-STAT3 expression, thereby promoting the apoptosis of tumour cells. (F) tRF-19-Q1Q89PJZ suppresses tumour cell proliferation and metastasis by inhibiting the glycolytic pathway and reducing energy uptake. (G) tRF-23-Q99P9P9NDD regulates fatty acid metabolism by inhibiting the expression of ACADSB, ultimately promoting tumour proliferation and metastasis.

Abnormal regulation of the cell cycle is the core driving force behind the unlimited proliferation of malignant gastrointestinal cells. tsRNAs regulate key carcinogenic signalling pathways or oncogenic factors involved in cell cycle regulation by targeting mRNAs or interacting with RBPs. Different tsRNAs have varying effects on the phases of cell cycle arrest or promotion. CyclinD1 and CyclinB are important regulators of the cell cycle. Studies have shown that tRF-Val-CAC-016, which is underexpressed in gastric cancer cells, targets the 3′UTR of CACNA1d to directly inhibit its expression, leading to reduced expression of CyclinD1, CyclinB and c-MYC through the downstream MAPK signalling pathway [75]. Thus, tRF-Val-CAC-016 indirectly regulates the levels of important cell cycle regulators by targeting CACNA1d mRNA, thereby participating in the regulation of the cell cycle in gastric cancer tumours (Figure 3(C)). The PI3K/AKT signalling pathway is also closely related to cancer cell cycle regulation. A previous study reported that tRF-5026a overexpression arrests the gastric cancer cells in the G0/G1 phase by regulating the PTEN/PI3K/AKT signalling pathway, whereas silencing tRF-5026a expression arrests the cell cycle in the G2/M phase [76]. In gallbladder cancer, tRF-3013b overexpression arrests gallbladder cancer cells in the G1/S phase [77]. Mechanistic studies have shown that tRF-3013b exerts an RNA silencing effect on TPRG1L by binding to AGO3, thereby inhibiting the downstream NF-κb signalling pathway and modulating the cell cycle [77].

Regulation of apoptosis

Apoptosis is a programmed cell death process regulated by certain genes and their encoded [78]. The process of apoptosis is usually triggered by specific cytokines produced inside the cell that activate multiple downstream signalling pathways, including the endogenous signalling pathway (mitochondrial pathway), the exogenous signalling pathway (death receptor pathway) and the cross-regulatory mechanism [79]. Recent studies have shown that tsRNAs participate in the occurrence and development of gastrointestinal malignancies by regulating key pathways of apoptosis. Activation of the p53 tumour suppressor gene is a key signal that initiates apoptosis in response to DNA damage [80]. Cui et al. discovered that tRF-Val serves as a potential oncogene to inhibit apoptosis in gastric cancer cells [81] (Figure 3(D)). Molecular mechanism studies have revealed that tRF-Val transports the EEF1A1 protein into the nucleus of gastric cancer cells, which enhances the interaction between EEF1A1 and the MDM2–p53 complex in the nucleus, thereby inhibiting p53-dependent apoptosis [81]. Similarly, tRF-Glu-TTC-027, which is underexpressed in gastric cancer cells, inhibits MAPK phosphorylation by targeting p38, leading to the inactivation of downstream genes, such as p53 and Bax, which inhibits apoptosis in gastric cancer cells [82]. tRF-33, which is overexpressed in gastric cancer cells, binds to AGO proteins to target the 3′UTR of STAT3, negatively regulating STAT3 expression; the decrease in STAT3 and p-STAT3 levels blocks downstream gene transcription, ultimately inhibiting cancer cell proliferation and promoting apoptosis [83] (Figure 3(E)). In colon cancer, the expression of tiRNA-HisGTG reduces the activity of colon cancer cells and causes cell cycle arrest; the molecular mechanism involves targeting thymidylate synthase (TS) in colon cancer cells to affect downstream p53 expression [84].

Bax, the first proapoptotic protein discovered in the Bcl-2 gene family, plays a crucial regulatory role in programmed cell death [85]. In addition to tRF-Glu-TTC-027, tRF-Tyr also participates in the apoptosis process by regulating Bax levels. Studies have shown that tRF-Tyr directly binds to the hnRNPD protein, which competitively inhibits the binding of hnRNPD to the c-Myc 3′UTR, thereby regulating the c-Myc/Bcl2/Bax pathway; this regulation ultimately inhibits gastric cancer cell proliferation, invasion and migration and it promotes gastric cancer cell apoptosis [86].

Regulation of cellular metabolism

Tumour cell metabolism is one of the core areas of cancer biology research, which lies on the foundation that cancer cells reshape energy and material metabolism pathways through metabolic reprogramming to adapt to the needs of rapidly proliferating cells and harsh microenvironments [87]. Unlike normal cells, which rely on mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation for energy, cancer cells often utilize glycolysis and consume large amounts of glucose to produce lactic acid, a process known as the Warburg effect [88]. Recent studies have shown that tsRNAs affect the activity of malignant gastrointestinal cells by influencing glycolysis, fatty acid metabolism and other pathways. For example, tRF-19-Q1Q89PJZ has been found to inhibit the proliferation and metastasis of pancreatic cancer cells, and its expression decreases during hypoxia [89] (Figure 3(F)). Molecular mechanism studies have revealed that tRF-19-Q1Q89PJZ binds to hexokinase-1 (HK-1) mRNA to inhibit its expression, mediating the glycolysis pathway to significantly reduce glucose and ATP uptake by cancer cells, which inhibits the proliferation and metastasis of pancreatic cancer cells [90]. Similarly, Lan et al. reported that the high expression of tRF-18-8R6546D2 in pancreatic cancer promotes cancer cell proliferation, migration and invasion by silencing ASCL2 expression and regulating downstream Ras signalling pathways [90]. Specific genes in the Ras pathway can help pancreatic cancer cells survive without the energy provided by glycolysis by utilizing other nutrients [90].

In addition to glycolysis, tsRNAs are involved in the regulation of cell metabolism, including fatty acid metabolism and amino acid metabolism. Zhang et al. reported that tRF-23-Q99P9P9NDD binds to the 3′UTR of ACADSB, silencing ACADSB expression to inhibit fatty acid catabolism and ferroptosis and promote lipid accumulation, which promotes cancer proliferation and metastasis [23] (Figure 3(G)). In addition to glucose, glutamine is also an important cellular energy substrate; it provides energy for cancer cell growth through its metabolism with glutamate [91]. Studies have shown that i-tRF-Glu is involved in amino acid transport during protein synthesis, which affects glutamine metabolism in colorectal cancer cells. These findings suggest that tsRNAs may serve as potential bridges between colorectal cancer and amino acid metabolism [92].

Immune escape

In the early stages of cancer development, cancer cells are effectively eliminated by the immune system. However, certain specific cells achieve immune evasion through genetic or epigenetic alterations. The immune evasion mechanisms of gastrointestinal malignancies involve multiple complex biological processes, such as hiding cancer cells to avoid recognition by the immune system, directly or indirectly interfering with the functions of immune cells, and protecting cancer cells from immune cell toxicity [93]. Recent studies have confirmed the presence of tsRNAs in immune cells, which play crucial roles in regulating immune function and provide new targets for immunotherapy of gastrointestinal [94]. At the molecular level, tsRNAs interfere with the functions of immune cells through various mechanisms, such as epigenetic modification, signalling pathway regulation and intercellular communication. The balance between CD8+ T cells and regulatory T cells (Tregs) in the tumour immune microenvironment plays a pivotal role in tumour immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy. For example, at the epigenetic level, Xu et al. reported that n6-methyladenosine modification significantly enhances the stability of 3′tRF-AlaAGC, and once it is expressed, 3′tRF-AlaAGC significantly increases the proportion of Tregs in the immune microenvironment of gastric cancer. Furthermore, 3′tRF-AlaAGC enhances glycolysis and lactate (LA) production in gastric cancer cells by binding to polyribonucleotide binding protein 1 (PTBP1) [95] (Figure 4(A)). In a high-LA environment, Tregs inhibit the expression of PD-1 on effector T cells by promoting the translocation of NFAT1 into the nucleus, suggesting that 3′tRF-AlaAGC can serve as a biomarker for predicting the efficacy of gastric cancer ICIs [95].

Exosome-derived tsRNAs are closely related to immune escape in gastrointestinal malignancies. In PD-1 immunotherapy for oesophageal cancer, the ability of exosomal tsRNA-10522 to inhibit CD8+ T cells is influenced by MMP14-positive tumour-associated fibroblasts in the immune microenvironment [68] (Figure 4(A)). Similarly, exosomal tRF-GluCTC-0005 in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma recruits a significant number of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) to the liver, creating PMNs in the immunosuppressive microenvironment, further promoting pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma liver metastasis [96]. With respect to macrophage polarization regulation, exosomal tRF-3022b derived from colorectal cancer has been shown to regulate apoptosis and induce M2 macrophage polarization by binding to galectin 1 (LGALS1) and macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) in cancer [97] (Figure 4(A)). Thus, inhibiting tumour immune escape by regulating the expression levels of tsRNAs is a potential treatment approach for gastrointestinal malignancies.

Regulation of invasion and metastasis

Tumour metastasis is a multistep process in which cancer cells acquire invasive capabilities and spread to distant organs through blood or lymphatic vessels [98]. The processes of cancer invasion and metastasis involve complex network regulation, such as TME remodelling, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) and immune evasion [99]. Recent studies have shown that tsRNAs participate in the invasion and metastasis of gastrointestinal malignancies through various molecular mechanisms. Changes in the TME caused by certain conditions, such as hypoxia, reduce the expression level of tRF-20-M0NK5Y93, promoting the migration and invasion of colon cancer cells [100] (Figure 4(B)). Mechanistic studies have shown that tRF-20-M0NK5Y93 binds to a specific sequence of the long noncoding RNA MALAT1, which upregulates MALAT1 expression and induces the SRSF2-dependent alternative splicing of SMC1A mRNA to exert carcinogenic effects [100]. In gastric cancer, tRF-3017A derived from tRNA-Val-TAC is significantly overexpressed in cancer tissues, and its expression level is positively correlated with the rate of lymph node metastasis in clinical gastric cancer patients. Mechanistic studies have shown that tRF-3017A silences the tumour suppressor gene NELL2 by forming an RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) with Argonaute (AGO) proteins, which promotes the migration and invasion of gastric cancer [101] (Figure 4(B)). Unlike tRF-3017A, high expression of tiRNA-Val-CAC-2 is involved in the metastasis of pancreatic cancer cells owing to its interaction with the RBP FUBP1, enhancing the protein stability of FUBP1 and promoting c-MYC transcription [71].

EMT is a crucial process through which cancer cells acquire migratory and invasive capabilities. tsRNAs participate in the dynamic regulation of EMT by targeting EMT-related transcription factors or signalling pathways. Hypoxia is a typical characteristic of the TME. Cancer cell acquisition of a metastatic phenotype is not only a result of dysregulation of signal transduction pathways but also a gradual adaptation process driven by hypoxia, during which cancer cells acquire invasive and metastatic potential [102]. Hypoxia significantly decreases the expression of tRF-20-M0NK5Y93 in colon cancer cells, which promotes their migration and invasion by modulating the expression of Claudin-1, an EMT-related molecule [103] (Figure 4(B)). Similarly, in colon cancer, the expression level of the specific nuclease Dicer1 increases under hypoxic conditions, leading to the increased production of tRF-20 from mature tRNA, which enhances the invasion and migration abilities of colon cancer cells [104]. Cell experiments have revealed that overexpression of Dicer1 significantly increases the expression of some EMT-related markers, such as E-cadherin, B-cadherin, vimentin and ZEB1, in colon cancer cells, promoting the ability of cancer cells to overcome stromal constraints and undergo metastasis [104]. Thus, Dicer1-induced tRF-20 expression under hypoxia promotes the metastasis and EMT of colon cancer cells. In hepatocellular carcinoma, Zhou et al. reported that Gly-tRF overexpression significantly promotes the migration and invasion abilities of liver cancer cells, reduces E-cadherin expression and increases N-cadherin expression [105] (Figure 4(B)). Mechanistic studies have shown that Gly-tRF reduces the level of NDFIP2 mRNA by binding to its 3′UTR, which activates the AKT signalling pathway and ultimately promotes the metastasis of liver cancer cells [105]. In addition, tRF/miR-1280 has been reported to target the Notch pathway ligand JAG2, which reduces the number of tumour stem-like cells, thus slowing the metastasis of colon cancer cells [22] (Figure 4(B)).

Regulation of angiogenesis

Growing cancer cells require large amounts of oxygen and nutrients, making angiogenesis crucial in cancer cell proliferation, invasion and metastasis [106]. tsRNAs play a significant role in tumour angiogenesis by regulating the vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) signalling network and the hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) pathway in multiple ways. ANG expression is closely related to tumour progression. ANG not only activates endothelial cells to induce tumour angiogenesis and promote cancer cell proliferation and metastasis but also possesses ribonuclease activity to cleave tRNA, generating small RNA fragments with diverse biological activities [107]. For example, in colorectal cancer, high expression of ANG induces 5′-tiRNA-Val and other tiRNAs to target cancer cells through the ANG-tiRNA regulatory axis, which promotes cell migration and invasion and activates endothelial cells to induce tumour angiogenesis [24] (Figure 4(C)). HIF-1 is a transcription activator that functions in a hypoxia-dependent manner. Activated HIF-1 plays a crucial role in tumour angiogenesis. For example, in colorectal cancer, the hypoxic microenvironment leads to the high expression of 5′tiRNA-His-GTG in cancer tissues through the HIF1α/angiogenin regulatory axis, which targets LATS2 to regulate the Hippo signalling pathway, thereby promoting cancer migration, invasion and angiogenesis [108] (Figure 4(C)).

Potential clinical applications of tsRNAs in gastrointestinal malignancies

Biomarkers

Given the vast number and diverse functions of tsRNAs, as well as their close association with cancer metastasis and abundance in plasma, tsRNAs could serve as cancer biomarkers [109]. Consequently, the investigation of tsRNAs as cancer biomarkers has gradually emerged as a hotspot in the biomedical field. It is worth noting that differences in sample processing, RNA extraction methods, detection platforms (e.g. RNA sequencing versus qRT-PCR) and data normalization strategies may contribute to heterogeneity in tsRNA expression profiles across studies. Special attention should be paid to pre-analytical variables affecting tsRNA stability, including the use of EDTA to inhibit RNases, rapid processing of plasma/serum samples to prevent cellular RNA contamination, and storage at −80 °C under RNase-free conditions. Standardization of these procedural variables will be essential for future clinical translation. Recent studies have indicated that certain circulating plasma tsRNAs exhibit superior diagnostic potential compared with traditional biomarkers, such as CEA, CA19-9 and AFP, and the combined use of these biomarkers significantly enhances diagnostic efficacy (Table 1). tsRNAs are potential biomarkers for gastric cancer. Studies have shown that the plasma levels of tRF-27-FDXXE6XRK45 in gastric cancer patients are significantly greater than those in healthy controls and gastritis patients. The plasma tRF-27-FDXXE6XRK45 level is significantly correlated with cancer differentiation, invasion depth, TNM staging, lymph node metastasis and nerve/vascular invasion [110]. When used as a biomarker for gastric cancer, the sensitivity and specificity of plasma tRF-27-FDXXE6XRK45 have been reported to reach 66% and 87%, respectively, achieving significantly greater values than those of traditional biomarkers, including CEA, CA19-9 and CA72-4 [110]. Similarly, the plasma level of tRF-17-WS7K092 in gastric cancer patients is significantly greater than that in healthy individuals, and its plasma concentration significantly decreases after surgical tumour removal [111]. Research has shown that plasma tRF-17-WS7K092 expression is correlated with T staging, TNM staging, lymph node metastasis and nerve/vascular invasion in gastric cancer. When used as a biomarker for gastric cancer, the sensitivity and specificity of plasma tRF-17-WS7K092 have been reported to reach 77% and 84%, respectively [111]. The combination of plasma tRF-27-FDXXE6XRK45 or tRF-17-WS7K092 with commonly used clinical biomarkers, such as CEA and CA19-9, can further increase the diagnostic value of tsRNA biomarkers for gastric cancer [110,111]. In addition, some plasma tsRNAs have shown great clinical value in the early diagnosis of gastric cancer. For example, the plasma levels of tRF-33-P4R8YP9LON4VDP and tRF-27-87R8WP9N1E5 are significantly lower in the early stage of gastric cancer. When used as biomarkers to distinguish early gastric cancer patients from healthy individuals, the AUCs of tRF-33-P4R8YP9LON4VDP and tRF-27-87R8WP9N1E5 have been reported to reach 0.757 and 0.776, respectively, indicating their potential value in early gastric cancer screening [112,113]. In the field of colorectal cancer, several types of abnormally expressed tsRNAs have been discovered. As research progresses, these tsRNAs have also demonstrated potential diagnostic value in the clinic. Compared with that in healthy controls, the plasma level of 5′-tRF-GlyGCC in patients with colorectal cancer is significantly elevated, and it increases with colorectal cancer progression and metastasis [114]. When used as a biomarker for colorectal cancer, the AUC, sensitivity and specificity of plasma 5′-tRF-GlyGCC have been reported to reach 0.882, 86% and 72%, respectively, achieving greater values than those of CEA and CA19-9 [114]. Furthermore, combining plasma 5′-tRF-GlyGCC, CEA and CA19-9 for diagnosis can further increase the AUC to 0.926, indicating that plasma 5′-tRF-GlyGCC provides excellent diagnostic capabilities for patients with colorectal cancer [114]. EVs are carriers that transmit genetic material. Studies have shown that 3′tRF-ThrCGT and 3′tRF-mtlelGAT exist in circulating plasma within EVs [115]. In patients with colorectal cancer, the level of 3′tRF-ThrCGT in plasma EVs is significantly reduced, whereas the level of 3′tRF-mtlleGAT in plasma EVs is significantly increased [115]. When used as biomarkers for colorectal cancer, the AUC values of 3′tRF-ThrCGT and 3′tRF-mtlleGAT in EVs are 0.669 and 0.656, respectively. When combined with blood biomarkers, such as CEA and CA724, the diagnostic efficiency of these tsRNAs can reach 0.938 [115]. Methodologically, detection of plasma tsRNAs typically involves RNA extraction from archived plasma or serum samples, often followed by reverse transcription and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) for validation. High-throughput sequencing is commonly employed in discovery phases, with special attention paid to RNA integrity and the use of spike-in controls for normalization.tsRNAs also have great potential for application as biomarkers in other gastrointestinal malignancies. For example, tRF-Gln-TTG-006, which is significantly elevated in the plasma of patients with hepatocellular carcinoma, has been reported to achieve an AUC of 0.919 in distinguishing early-stage liver cancer patients from healthy individuals through ROC analysis, with a sensitivity of 79.0%; because these values are superior to those obtained with plasma AFP, tRF-Gln-TTG-006 is expected to become a new screening biomarker for hepatocellular carcinoma [25]. When used as biomarkers for pancreatic cancer, tRF-Leu-CAG-002 and tRF-Pro-AGG-004, which are significantly elevated in the plasma of patients with pancreatic cancer, have been reported to have sensitivities of 72.5% and 64%, respectively, and specificities of 98.8% and 77.2%, respectively. When combined for diagnosis, the sensitivity and specificity for pancreatic cancer have been reported to reach 85% and 96.4%, respectively, which are superior to those of conventional biomarkers [118]. In addition, studies have shown that tRF-55, tRF-56 and miR-4488 have high diagnostic efficiency and great potential for oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma, and the detection of these markers through microfluidic biochips is a reliable, highly sensitive, rapid and economical method [122]. Although many recent studies have shown the application value of tsRNAs as biomarkers in gastrointestinal malignancies, their clinical translation still faces many challenges and requires verification in large clinical studies in the future.

Prognostic indicators

Dysregulated expression of tsRNAs is associated with the clinicopathological characteristics, malignant biological behaviours and clinical prognosis of gastrointestinal malignancies. Aberrant expression of tsRNAs serves not only as an important regulatory factor of cancer initiation and progression but also as a key indicator for assessing the prognosis of patients with malignant tumours (Table 1). For example, the expression level of tRF-33-P4R8YP9LON4VDP in gastric cancer tissues is closely correlated with various clinicopathological characteristics and malignant biological behaviours, including tumour invasion depth, tumour size, lymph node metastasis, TNM staging and vascular invasion. Patients with gastric cancer who have low expression of tRF-33-P4R8YP9LON4VDP tend to have a poorer overall survival (OS) rate than those with higher tRF-33-P4R8YP9LON4VDP expression. Multivariate Cox regression analysis has indicated that tRF-33-P4R8YP9LON4VDP in cancer tissues is an independent prognostic factor for gastric cancer patients [80]. Similarly, colorectal cancer patients with 5′-tiRNA-ProTGG overexpression not only have shorter disease-free survival (DFS) and OS times but also have a higher probability of cancer recurrence [116]. In hepatocellular carcinoma, Zuo et al. successfully developed a prognostic risk scoring model for liver cancer based on seven tsRNAs (ts-N20, ts-N21, ts-N22, ts-N36, ts-N37, ts-N44 and ts-N64) using LASSO regression; this model can be reliably used not only for the clinical diagnosis of liver cancer but also for the classification of patients into high-risk and low-risk [117].

Notably, certain tsRNAs in tissues associated with cancer prognosis are also found in plasma. These circulating plasma tsRNAs can serve as biomarkers for the prognostic assessment of gastrointestinal malignancies. For example, the plasma level of tRF-31-U5YKFN8DYDZDD in patients with gastric cancer is closely correlated with various clinicopathological characteristics and malignant biological behaviours, such as tumour invasion depth, lymph node metastasis, TNM staging and vascular invasion, and patients with gastric cancer exhibiting high plasma levels of tRF-31-U5YKFN8DYDZDD tend to have a poorer OS rate than those with low plasma levels of tRF-31-U5YKFN8DYDZDD [119]. As an independent prognostic factor, the serum tRF-31-U5YKFN8DYDZDD level significantly decreases after surgery in gastric cancer patients, and its plasma level is closely correlated with the tumour burden [119]. The plasma level of tRF-33-P4R8YP9LON4VDP in patients with gastric cancer is significantly negatively correlated with the diameter of the tumour. As an independent prognostic factor, patients with gastric cancer exhibiting low plasma levels of tRF-33-P4R8YP9LON4VDP tend to have a shorter OS time [83]. The serum level of tRF-29-R9J8909NF5JP in patients with gastric cancer is significantly greater than that in patients with gastritis and healthy individuals, whereas the serum level of tRF-29-R9J8909NF5JP significantly decreases after surgery in patients with gastric cancer [120]. The serum level of tRF-29-R9J8909NF5JP in patients with gastric cancer is correlated with tumour differentiation, T staging, lymph node metastasis, lymph node metastasis staging and nerve/vascular invasion [120]. High serum levels of tRF-29-R9J8909NF5JP are associated with a lower survival rate in patients, indicating its potential for monitoring the postoperative condition of gastric cancer patients [120]. In hepatocellular carcinoma, plasma tRF-33-RZYQHQ9M739P0J is not only a promising noninvasive early diagnostic marker but also a predictor of liver cancer progression. Studies have shown that plasma levels of tRF-33-RZYQHQ9M739P0J are elevated in hepatocellular carcinoma patients and are correlated with tumour metastasis, TNM staging, BCLC staging and venous invasion [121]. The plasma levels of tRF-33-RZYQHQ9M739P0J in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma decrease after surgery, and high plasma levels of tRF-33-RZYQHQ9M739P0J are associated with a lower survival rate [121]. Thus, owing to the close association between tsRNAs and gastrointestinal malignancies, tissue and plasma tsRNAs can serve as biomarkers that provide clues for the prognostic assessment of gastrointestinal malignancies.

Therapeutic targets

Research has shown that upregulating tumour-suppressing tsRNAs or silencing tumour-promoting tsRNAs can effectively inhibit the proliferation, migration and invasion of cancer cells, reduce angiogenesis and immune evasion, and exhibit strong antitumour effects (Table 1). As molecular switches regulating cancer development and progression, tsRNAs provide new potential targets for the targeted therapy of gastrointestinal malignancies. For example, carcinogenic tsRNA tRF-3017A binds to the AGO protein of RISC and targets downstream tumour-suppressing genes in a miRNA-like manner, promoting the progression of gastric cancer [101]. Similarly, tRF-24-V29K9UV3IU, a tumour-suppressing tsRNA, targets the 3′UTR of the downstream gene GPR78 in a miRNA-like manner to silence GPR78 expression, thereby inhibiting the proliferation and metastasis of gastric cancer cells [123]. Such bidirectional regulation provides a unique entry point for the development of targeted drugs. Moreover, drug resistance is a pressing issue in the field of cancer treatment. For example, the drug resistance and dose toxicity of 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), a first-line chemotherapy drug used for colorectal cancer, are the main factors limiting its clinical use. Xu et al. designed a method to encapsulate 5-FU and tsRNA-GlyGCC inhibitors using synthetic poly-β-amino ester (PAE) that targets the role of tsRNA-GlyGCC in 5-FU resistance in colorectal cancer. This modification not only improves the delivery efficiency of 5-FU to cancer cells but also significantly inhibits cancer growth and enhances the sensitivity of colorectal cancer cells to 5-FU, providing a potential new approach for targeted therapy combining 5-FU with tsRNAs [124]. Similarly, other delivery strategies such as lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) and viral vectors are being explored for tsRNA therapeutics, although stability, off-target effects and immunogenicity remain important considerations. In the context of immunotherapy, tsRNAs also show great promise. PD-1/PD-L1 blockers are currently the most effective ICIs [125]. Wang et al. reported that exosomal tsRNA-10522 inhibits the ability of CD8+ T lymphocytes in the tumour immune microenvironment to kill tumour cells, which has an adverse effect on PD-1 immunotherapy in oesophageal cancer [68]. Therefore, designing and implementing precise targeted tsRNA overexpression or silencing methods for specific tsRNAs will provide new approaches for cancer immunotherapy.

Perspectives

As a novel class of noncoding small RNAs, significant progress in recent years has been made in terms of understanding the biological functions of tsRNAs in gastrointestinal malignancies and the mechanisms underlying their carcinogenic effects on the digestive system. Currently, dozens of tsRNAs that are significantly correlated with human gastrointestinal malignancies have been discovered. These tsRNA molecules participate in the fine regulation of gene expression through multiple mechanisms, such as epigenetic modification, transcriptional regulation and posttranscriptional processing, thereby affecting cancer development and progression. During the progression of gastrointestinal malignancies, tsRNAs not only participate in the regulation of cancer cell invasion and metastasis but are also are significantly correlated with the clinical prognoses of patients. Therefore, research in this field holds great promise for identifying novel diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets related to gastrointestinal malignancies.

However, several prominent limitations and challenges remain. First, there is no unified naming convention for tsRNAs to date. Most reported tsRNAs are named in a chaotic manner, adding unnecessary confusion and interference to research. Therefore, considering the large number of tsRNAs, establishing a unified naming convention to standardize the data is important. Second, existing evidence indicates that tsRNAs participate in the occurrence, progression and metastasis of gastrointestinal malignancies through various mechanisms, such as regulating target gene expression, affecting immune cell function, regulating tumour angiogenesis and remodelling the TME. However, the spatiotemporal specificity, tissue heterogeneity and interaction mechanisms of the tsRNA molecular network with the immune microenvironment still require further investigation. Future research can combine single-cell sequencing, spatial transcriptomics and multiomics analyses to systematically elucidate the bidirectional regulatory roles and specific regulatory mechanisms of tsRNAs in gastrointestinal malignancies. Third, in terms of clinical translational, tsRNAs are expected to become novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets for gastrointestinal malignancies because of their high stability, tissue specificity and ease of acquisition via liquid biopsy. For example, the use of tsRNAs from peripheral circulating plasma and plasma exosomes may provide a noninvasive strategy for the early screening of gastrointestinal malignancies. Nanoparticle delivery strategies targeting tsRNAs can overcome the bottleneck of existing chemotherapy resistance. However, clinical translational research on tsRNAs is currently insufficient. Large-scale clinical trials are needed to evaluate the screening and clinical prognostic value of tsRNAs in different types of gastrointestinal malignancies and provide options for early precision diagnosis and targeted therapy.

In summary, tsRNAs are potential new targets for the diagnosis and treatment of gastrointestinal malignancies. Through various mechanisms, tsRNAs affect the occurrence and development of gastrointestinal malignancies, making them ideal biomarkers for clinical screening, prognostic evaluation and gene therapy. The biological roles and potential clinical value of tsRNAs provide new ideas for the prevention and treatment of gastrointestinal malignancies.
