The association between triglyceride-glucose index and all-cause/cardiovascular mortality in patients with different glucose metabolism statuses
Authors: Jiajun Liu, Jinhua Kang, Pengpeng Liang, Zhangxiao Song, Guiyun Li, Xueshan Jin, Hongyan Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThe triglyceride-glucose index (TyG) is an emerging marker of metabolic health, yet its association with mortality across different glucose metabolism statuses remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between the TyG and the risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality among individuals with normoglycemia, dysglycemia, and diabetes mellitus.MethodsParticipants from nine cycles of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) were included and categorized into three groups: normoglycemia, dysglycemia, and diabetes. Cox regression and restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses were performed to evaluate the linear and nonlinear associations between TyG and mortality. To assess the predictive power of TyG and the atherogenic index of plasma (AIP) for mortality, time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on age, sex, and blood pressure status.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 9.2 years, a total of 2199 all-cause deaths and 606 cardiovascular deaths were documented. In the normoglycemic group, a single standard unit increase in TyG was associated with a 35% higher risk of all-cause mortality and a 38% higher risk of cardiovascular mortality (HR: 1.35, 95% CI 1.17–1.56; HR: 1.38, 95% CI 1.04–1.84, respectively). Among participants with diabetes, RCS analysis revealed a U-shaped association between TyG and all-cause/cardiovascular mortality, with an inflection point at 9.1. No significant associations were observed in the dysglycemia group. TyG demonstrated superior predictive performance compared to the AIP for 3-year mortality in both normoglycemic and diabetic individuals. Subgroup analyses identified significant interaction effects of age and sex on the association between TyG and mortality.ConclusionTyG was associated with an increased risk of all-cause and cardiovascular death in the normoglycemic subgroup, but not in the dysglycemic subgroup. In the diabetes subgroup, the association between the TyG and mortality was nonlinear. Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) refers to a group of disorders affecting the heart and blood vessels, including heart failure and coronary artery disease [1]. These diseases arise from complex pathophysiological processes, such as endothelial dysfunction, atherosclerosis, thrombosis, and myocardial ischemia–reperfusion injury [2, 3]. Established risk factors for CVD, including obesity, smoking, diabetes, and hypertension, are closely linked to its development [4]. Recent studies indicate that CVD accounts for approximately 31% of global deaths, resulting in 17.3 million deaths annually [5]. Despite significant advancements in the prevention and treatment of CVD, the burden remains substantial. Thus, comprehensive prevention strategies, including early detection and effective management of risk factors, are essential to mitigating the impact of CVD.

Diabetes mellitus (DM) and CVD share many common risk factors [6]. Evidence points to a strong association between DM and adverse cardiovascular outcomes, particularly among those with insulin-resistant (IR) diabetes [7]. Recently, study have shifted from overt DM to earlier stages of IR, aiming to implement timely interventions to reduce the risk of early disability and mortality associated with CVD. The hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp technique, pioneered by DeFronzo's group, is considered the gold standard for assessing IR; however, its high cost and procedural complexity limit its widespread application in clinical practice [8]. Several surrogate markers are commonly used to assess IR, among which the homeostasis model assessment of IR (HOMA-IR) is the most widely adopted [9, 10]. HOMA-IR calculation is based on fasting plasma glucose (FBG) and insulin levels, but it has limitations in patients experiencing beta-cell failure or undergoing insulin treatment [11, 12]. In addition, HOMA-IR requires routine insulin measurement, which may present challenges for primary healthcare settings [12].

Recently, triglyceride glucose index (TyG), independent of insulin measurement, has been shown to effectively predict IR and cardiovascular risk across various populations, suggesting its utility as a practical alternative to HOMA-IR [13, 14]. Previous meta-analyses have demonstrated the role of TyG in predicting metabolic syndrome, stroke, and kidney disease [15–17]. Other observational studies have shown that TyG is associated with an increased risk of CVD in both the general and diabetic populations [18–20]. Another emerging metabolic biomarker, the atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), integrates multiple lipid components and reflects their interactions [21]. Recent studies have reported that AIP has a strong predictive value for cardiovascular outcomes in diabetic cohorts [22]. Individuals with different metabolic profiles may exhibit varying levels of CVD risk [23, 24]. Based on glycemic status, individuals can be classified into normoglycemic, dysglycemic, and diabetic groups [25]. However, few studies have investigated the predictive value of TyG and AIP in normoglycemic and dysglycemic populations.

We aimed to: (1) characterize the association of TyG with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in normoglycemic, dysglycemic, and diabetic populations; (2) evaluate the predictive value of TyG and AIP for mortality risk prediction.

Methods

Data source

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), sponsored by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, aims to assess the health and nutritional status of the U.S. population. A multistage, complex, and stratified sampling strategy is applied in the study to ensure national representativeness of the sample. The survey is conducted biennially and collects data on demographics, laboratory tests, physical examinations, and questionnaires.

Data from 9 NHANES interview cycles (2001–2018) were used, including 91,351 participants. 32,136 individuals were further studied after excluding participants under 18 years of age and those who could not judge glycemic status. Individuals lacking TyG data (n = 8762), essential covariates (n = 253), and mortality information (n = 18) were additionally removed. Finally, 23,103 individuals were enrolled, including 10,078 in the normoglycemia, 8748 in the dysglycemia, and 4277 in the diabetes (Fig. 1).

Definition of TyG

TyG was derived from the baseline levels of participants’ triglycerides (TG) and FBG. We also assessed the difference in the ability of TyG and AIP to predict mortality. AIP, derived from TG and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), indicates atherosclerosis. The indicators can be calculated using the following formulas:TyG = ln[TG (mg/dl)*FBG (mg/dl)/2] [26];AIP = log10[TG(mmol/L)/HDL(mmol/L)] [13].

Definition of glucose metabolism status

Glucose metabolism status was categorized according to the 2021 criteria from the American Diabetes Association, with consideration of the data structure of the NHANES database [27]. Normoglycemia was defined as HbA1c ≤ 5.7% and no self-reported history of DM. Dysglycemia was defined as meeting any of the following without a history of DM: 2-h oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) levels between 7.8 and 11.0 mmol/L, HbA1c between 5.7% and 6.4%, or FBG between 5.6 and 6.9 mmol/L. DM was defined as having a 2-h OGTT level ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, FBG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, or a self-reported history of diabetes.

Determination of death

The primary endpoints were all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. Causes of death were classified according to the 10th Revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10). All-cause mortality included deaths from any cause, whereas cardiovascular mortality referred to deaths attributable to ischemic heart disease, heart failure, or other cardiovascular conditions. Vital status and causes of death were determined by linking the most recent NHANES data to the national death index [28].

Assessment of covariates

Comprehensive baseline characteristics, including anthropometric measurements, demographic information, lifestyle factors, and clinical history, were obtained using standardized and validated questionnaires. Educational attainment was categorized into three levels: high school or less, some college or equivalent, and college degree or above. Smoking status was classified as never, former, or current smoker. Alcohol consumption was stratified into non-drinkers, low-to-moderate drinkers, and heavy drinkers based on frequency and quantity of intake. Hypertension was defined as meeting any of the following criteria: self-reported diagnosis, current use of antihypertensive medication, or measured systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg [29].

Statistical analysis

Participants were divided into three groups: normoglycemia, dysglycemia, and diabetes. Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages, while continuous variables were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges. Differences among groups were analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test and chi-square test. TyG was divided into quartiles (Q1–Q4). To examine the association between TyG and mortality, weighted Cox proportional hazards regression models were applied to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated to compare cumulative survival across TyG quartiles, and differences were assessed using the log-rank test. Directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) were employed to identify potential confounders and guide variable selection for model adjustment. Three models were constructed: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for gender, age, race, education level, and BMI; and Model 3 included additional adjustments for smoking, drinking, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, oral antihypertensive medications, oral lipid-lowering drugs, history of CVD, and history of cancer. The potential non-linear association between TyG and mortality across various glucose metabolism statuses was evaluated using a restrictive cubic spline (RCS) analysis. To balance curve smoothness and minimize overfitting, a four-knot RCS was constructed [30]. Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to evaluate the predictive performance of TyG and the atherogenic index of plasma (AIP) for mortality at 36, 60, and 120 months. We also performed a cause-mediation analysis. Subgroup analyses were conducted stratified by age (< 65 years vs ≥ 65 years), sex (male vs female), and blood pressure status (hypertension vs normotension). To minimize the impact of pre-existing conditions on the results, individuals who died within 6 months of enrollment were excluded from the analysis. Data was analyzed using R (version 4.4.2) and Stata (version 18.1), with a significance level of P < 0.05.

Results

Demographic characteristics

The study included 23,103 participants: 10,078 (43.62%) with normoglycemia, 8748 (37.88%) with dysglycemia, and 4277 (18.50%) with diabetes. Compared to the normoglycemic group, the dysglycemic and diabetic groups had a higher incidence of hyperlipidemia and hypertension, elevated levels of FBG, TG, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and fasting insulin, as well as decreased HDL-C levels. The normoglycemic group had a higher proportion of females, and the dysglycemic and diabetic groups had a higher proportion of males (Table 1). Survival analysis results

After a median observation period of 9.2 years, 2199 deaths from all causes and 606 cardiovascular deaths were recorded. We observed a significant interaction between TyG and different metabolic statuses in predicting mortality (P for interaction < 0.001). In normoglycemic, dysglycemic, and diabetic populations, we performed weighted COX regression analyses separately. In the normoglycemic group, an increase of one standard unit in TyG correlated with a 35% increase in all-cause mortality and a 38% increase in cardiovascular mortality (HR: 1.35, 95% CI 1.17–1.56; HR: 1.38, 95% CI 1.04–1.84, respectively). When TyG was categorized, the fourth quartile (Q4) showed a higher risk of death from any cause (HR: 2.03, 95% CI 1.42–2.61) and cardiovascular death (HR: 1.99, 95% CI 1.02–3.31) compared to the first quartile (Q1). The findings revealed no significant correlation between dysglycemia and diabetes with mortality in either categorical or continuous variables (all P > 0.05) (Table 2). Figure 3 presented the Kaplan–Meier survival curve for all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in participants with different glucose metabolism statuses. Results of RCS analysis

To construct a smooth curve, we chose the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles as RCS knots. After adjusting for all covariates, a U-shaped association was observed between TyG and both all-cause mortality (nonlinear P = 0.001) and cardiovascular mortality (nonlinear P = 0.012) among individuals with diabetes. In contrast, no significant nonlinear relationships were identified between TyG and either all-cause or cardiovascular mortality in the normoglycemic and dysglycemic groups (all nonlinear P > 0.05) (Fig. 4). The Cox regression and RCS analyses showed no straight or curved links between TyG and all-cause/cardiovascular mortality in the dysglycemia.

Results of ROC analysis

In our previous analysis, TyG showed no significant association with mortality in the dysglycemic population. Therefore, ROC curve analyses were conducted only in the normoglycemic and diabetic groups, with the area under the curve (AUC) reflecting predictive power. Previous studies have indicated that AIP is a reliable predictor of mortality [31, 32]. In the analysis of ROC, we compared TyG and AIP in terms of their mortality prediction capabilities. In comparison to AIP, TyG exhibited a stronger predictive capacity for 3-year all-cause/cardiovascular mortality among both normoglycemic and diabetic individuals (Fig. 5). Figure S1–2 depicts TyG's effectiveness in predicting mortality over a span of 5 and 10 years. Notably, TyG's ability to predict mortality decreases over extended follow-up durations.

Mediation analysis

There was no significant linear association of TyG with all-caus/cardiovascular mortality in the dysglycemic and diabetic groups (all P > 0.05), which limited us from performing mediation analyses. Therefore, we performed mediation analyses in the normoglycemic population. The results showed that LDL-C mediated the association between 7.0% and 17.3% of TyG and all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality, respectively (Fig. 6).

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses were performed with participants stratified by age, sex, and blood pressure status. Details are provided in Tables S1–3. The results showed that age and sex had significant interaction effects on the association between TyG index and mortality. TyG is associated with an increased risk of all-cause/cardiovascular mortality in individuals under the age of 65 with normoglycemia and in women with normoglycemia.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the association between TyG and all-cause/cardiovascular mortality across populations with different glucose metabolism. The results indicated that TyG was connected to a higher risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in the normoglycemic subgroup, whereas no significant association was observed in the dysglycemic subgroup. In participants with diabetes, a nonlinear relationship was identified between TyG and mortality risk.

The connection between TyG and mortality among people with diabetes is still not well understood. A Korean study of 1090,485 diabetic patients categorized diabetes duration into newly diagnosed, < 5 years, 5–9 years, and ≥ 10 years. The findings indicated that the TyG-CVD association was strongest when diabetes duration was ≥ 10 years (HR = 1.44), whereas the TyG-mortality correlation was most pronounced for durations < 5 years (HR = 1.23) [33]. Another study observed 82 CVD deaths over a follow-up of 8.2 years, revealing a U-shaped TyG-mortality relationship in CVD outcomes (threshold: 9.18) [14]. This is inconsistent with the findings of another study, which failed to detect a significant relationship between TyG and CVD mortality [34]. A recent large-scale meta-analysis involving 7,239,790 individuals across 50 cohorts demonstrated a significant association between TyG and CVD risk in both diabetic (HR = 1.72) and non-diabetic populations (HR = 1.55) [35]. However, due to the differences in follow-up time, population characteristics, and covariate adjustment, there was a large heterogeneity among the studies (I2 = 94% for diabetic group and I2 = 74% for non-diabetic group), which affected the reliability of the results to some extent. We observed a U-shaped link between TyG and mortality from CVD causes and all causes in diabetic participants (threshold: 9.1).

Previous findings have suggested that IR is a primary cardiovascular risk factor in diabetic individuals [36]. IR leads to chronic inflammation, lipid abnormalities, and damage to vascular endothelial function through various ways, finally leading to the occurrence of CVD [37, 38]. Subgroup analyses based on glycemic status revealed that elevated TyG was linked to an increased likelihood of death from any cause and cardiovascular issues in the normoglycemia but not in the dysglycemia. A study reported a similar conclusion that TyG was linked to a higher likelihood of dying in the hospital for people with DM, but not for those with prediabetes [39]. Abnormal glucose metabolism is a transitional stage from normal glucose homeostasis to DM, marked by a gradual decrease of pancreatic β-cell function [40]. Although IR is already present at this stage, the remaining β-cell secretory function may partly compensate for the impaired insulin sensitivity, which helps preserve stable blood glucose and temporarily conceals the underlying metabolic dysregulation [41, 42]. With further β-cell failure, the risk of metabolic imbalance increases significantly [43]. Given that individuals at this stage may present with comparable blood glucose levels but substantially different underlying metabolic risks, TyG may not reliably capture the true mortality risk associated with this condition. Additionally, during the baseline assessment, some participants may have experienced transient factors such as infection, stress, or steroid use, which can cause elevated blood glucose levels and lead to misclassification into the dysglycemic group [44–46]. This misclassification may affect the robustness of the findings within the dysglycemic subgroup.

Few studies have reported the association between TyG and mortality in normoglycemic participants. Our study found a positive correlation between TyG and mortality in normoglycemic individuals, with each unit rise in TyG linked to a 35% and 38% increase in all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, respectively. By contrast, TyG showed a U-shaped association with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in people with diabetes, with a threshold of 9.1. This result suggests that either a rise or a fall in TyG is associated with an increased risk for mortality and that maintaining a value of 9.1 reduces mortality. This is consistent with another study involving 10,220 older retired participants that showed a significant nonlinear association of TyG with all-cause mortality (P = 0.002), with a threshold close to 9 [47].

AIP was first proposed by Dobiasova et al. in 2001 to evaluate lipoprotein size and esterification rate [48]. Similar to TyG, AIP is a simple and effective Metabolic marker. AIP is calculated based on TG and HDL-C levels, which more reflects the level of lipid metabolism and atherosclerosis [49–51]. A study with 627 older patients who had serious heart issues and received coronary angiography found that AIP was closely related to both in-hospital and long-term death rates (P < 0.05) [52]. Another study's findings indicate a J-curve relationship pattern between AIP and mortality from all causes [21]. However, limited study has compared the effectiveness of TyG and AIP in predicting mortality from CVD. Our findings indicate that TyG is a more reliable marker for overall or cardiovascular mortality than AIP in normoglycemic or diabetic populations.

Surprisingly, subgroup assessments revealed no notable link between TyG and mortality from any cause or cardiovascular issues in those ≥ 65 years. A finding corroborated by our study, which has shown an age interaction in the association between TyG and mortality [53]. This may be due to (1) TyG quantification being susceptible to interference by multiple factors in the elderly population, including nutritional fluctuations, dyslipidemia, and comorbidity burden, which collectively reduce its validity as an isolated indicator of IR [54]. (2) Older people are more aware of their status and may be more likely to follow a healthy lifestyle and receive timely drug treatment [55]. Intensive clinical care during follow-up could help adjust the body's metabolic balance, possibly making the initial TyG measurements less important for predicting outcomes. (3) Younger people are more susceptible to IR, and it is conceivable that this group of individuals is more susceptible to severe complications as the duration of the disease increases [56, 57]. TyG predicts mortality risk in women but not men, possibly because of fundamental differences in lipid metabolism between men and women [58]. Estrogen has a protective effect on the cardiovascular system, but in women after menopause, this effect is diminished, leading to changes in lipid levels [59]. This phenomenon makes women more susceptible to dyslipidemia, as indicated by increased TG and LDL-C levels and decreased HDL-C levels [60]. These changes aggravated metabolic disorders and subsequently increased TyG. Based on the findings of age- and sex-specific associations between TyG and mortality, large cohort studies could be conducted to further validate the potential age- and sex-specific physiological mechanisms underlying TyG's impact on mortality.

TyG has a significant predictive value for mortality risk; the precise biological pathways mediating this relationship remain to be fully delineated. Available evidence suggests that TyG levels are closely and positively correlated with IR. IR induces excessive oxidative stress (OS) by up-regulating the synthesis of advanced glycation end products and the generation of free radicals, reducing the bioavailability of nitric oxide (NO), and activating the mitochondrial electron transport chain [61, 62]. This pathophysiological cascade establishes a self-perpetuating cycle in which NO/OS imbalance exacerbates the degradation of endothelial glycocalyx, thereby exacerbating the destabilization of atherosclerotic plaques [63]. Importantly, IR is epidemiologically robustly associated with risk for incident major CVDs and longitudinal all-cause death. Notably, the diagnostic performance of the ROC analysis declined over time, which may be attributed to the following reasons: (1) disease progression during the follow-up period may have led to more complex pathophysiological states or the development of comorbidities, thereby weakening the association between TyG and mortality; (2) compensatory physiological mechanisms or treatment interventions may have altered TyG levels, making them less reflective of the underlying disease severity [64].

Strengths and limitations

This study is the first to evaluate the association between TyG and all-cause/cardiovascular mortality in people with different glycemic statuses, and to explore whether this relationship may vary according to age, sex, and blood pressure status. Our findings highlight the need to implement longitudinal designs in populations based on different glucose metabolism statuses to refine the risk stratification framework in precision public health initiatives.

Our results need careful interpretation in the following ways: First, due to its observational nature, this limits our ability to infer causal relationships. Second, we excluded 8762 individuals who lacked TyG data, which may result in selection bias. Third, we used the method of DAGs (Fig. S3) to identify confounders, but confounding by unmeasured factors may affect the robustness of the results. Fourth, the reliance on TyG measurements at a single time point precludes assessment of the dynamic effect of longitudinal TyG trajectories on the stratification of the risk of death. However, cumulative TyG exposure had comparable predictive power for IR trajectories, consistent with baseline measurements performance [65, 66]. Fifth, type 1 diabetes and type 2 diabetes may have different pathophysiological mechanisms [67]. However, due to the inherent limitations of data, we cannot distinguish the relationship between these two phenotypes and the risk of death. Finally, the AUC exhibited limited performance, necessitating cautious interpretation of the ROC findings. In the future, TyG can be combined with other biomarkers or risk score tables to improve the accuracy of prediction.

Conclusion

The relationship between TyG and all-cause/cardiovascular mortality varied according to glucose metabolic status. TyG was associated with an increased risk of all-cause/cardiovascular death in the normoglycemic subgroup, but not in the dysglycemic subgroup. TyG was nonlinearly associated with increased mortality in the diabetes subgroup. Compared with AIP, TyG was a better predictor of mortality in normoglycemic or diabetic subjects. The results of this study provide actionable evidence for health interventions in populations with different glucose metabolism statuses.
