From Left Ventricular Diastolic Dysfunction to HFpEF: An Iceberg
Authors: Jesus Peteiro
Published: 2026

Like many widespread non‐communicable diseases, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) can be conceptualized as an iceberg. This analogy has long applied to coronary artery disease and is now increasingly relevant to HFpEF.

Cardiologists routinely manage patients with symptomatic heart failure, of whom approximately half have HFpEF. However, emerging evidence indicates that key structural and functional abnormalities underlying this condition are also highly prevalent among asymptomatic individuals in the general population.

In this context, the study by Lee M et al., published in this issue of Echocardiography, provides important insight [1]. Among 1,128 randomly selected Hispanic participants (mean age 52.6 years; 57.8% women), echocardiographic evidence of left ventricular diastolic dysfunction (LVDD) was present in up to 20% of individuals. Moreover, left ventricular hypertrophy or concentric remodeling was identified in 65% of the cohort. Comparable findings have been reported in other populations, reinforcing the notion that subclinical cardiac abnormalities are both common and underrecognized [2, 3].

These striking figures raise concern regarding future healthcare demands and underscore the importance of early identification of at‐risk individuals. The high prevalence of LVDD and its association with HFpEF have led to increasing emphasis on the routine assessment of diastolic function, not only during resting echocardiography performed for various indications [4], but also during exercise testing, where abnormalities may become more apparent [5, 6].

Obesity is a well‐established contributor to HFpEF. Notably, data from the current research suggest that visceral adiposity—particularly in women—may play a central role. This observation supports the “central adiposity” hypothesis, whereby HFpEF is driven less by the cumulative effect of disparate comorbidities and more by an adipose‐mediated systemic process. In this framework, excess adipose tissue acts as an active endocrine organ, promoting inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and myocardial stiffness through circulating mediators [7].

Although obesity was associated with LVDD and morphologic LV abnormalities, the association of these abnormalities with visceral adiposity was stronger. Importantly, visceral adiposity was quantified by dual‐energy X‐ray absorptiometry (DXA), rather than inferred from the waist‐to‐height ratio, providing a more direct assessment of body fat distribution.

Also important, as the authors have used the new guidelines for assessment of LVDD [4], it remains to be seen the impact of these criteria on the outcome of these asymptomatic subjects, provided longitudinal follow‐up becomes available.

All these findings suggest that clinically overt HFpEF represents only the visible portion of a much larger subclinical disease. Recognizing and addressing it may be essential for prevention strategies and for mitigating its impact.
