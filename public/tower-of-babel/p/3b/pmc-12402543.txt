Trends Analysis of Sexually Transmitted Infections Before and After Human Immunodeficiency Virus Pre-exposure Prophylaxis in the United States 2001–2022
Authors: Zailing Xing, Daniel Escudero
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe correlation between human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) usage and sexually transmitted infections (STIs) remains equivocal. Limited studies have investigated national STI trends after introducing PrEP. We aimed to examine STI incidence before and after PrEP introduction and explore correlations with PrEP use in the United States.MethodsPrEP usage data (2012–2022) were obtained from AIDSVu, and STI data (2001–2022) from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's STI Statistics Archive. We used the coefficient of determination to estimate variance in STI rates explained by PrEP usage. We used cross-correlation to identify lagged correlations and segmented linear regression to examine STI trends by demographics before and after PrEP initiation.ResultsSyphilis rates increased by 0.2/100 000 people annually before 2012 and by 1.4/100 000 persons after PrEP introduction. Gonorrhea rates dropped by 2.9/100 000 people before 2012 but rose by 14/100 000 afterward. The slope for chlamydia dropped from 20.9 to 6.6. The coefficients of determination for syphilis, gonorrhea, and chlamydia attributable to PrEP usage were 0.98, 0.88, and 0.2, respectively. Lagged correlations were significant for chlamydia, but not for syphilis and gonorrhea. Overall, STI rates were more strongly correlated with PrEP usage among males than females.ConclusionsWe found significant changes in the rate of incident STIs from 2012 to 2022 and a high correlation between PrEP usage and reported cases of syphilis and gonorrhea. Given the role of PrEP in comprehensive HIV prevention in the United States, its impact on rates of STI infection should be further studied.

Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) has emerged not only as a critical biomedical intervention but also as the standard of care for preventing human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) among people at elevated risk of HIV acquisition [1]. Since its approval in the United States in 2012, PrEP has been widely studied and shown to be highly efficacious in reducing HIV acquisition [2]. Furthermore, real-world research, such as the PROUD Study and the US PrEP Demonstration Project, has evidenced the efficacy of PrEP in both clinical and community environments, attaining risk reductions of 86% or greater with high adherence levels [3, 4]. These findings have led to broad endorsement of PrEP in public health policy and clinical guidelines as a cornerstone of HIV prevention [5, 6]. However, as PrEP use increases, there are ongoing concerns that its use may be associated with behavioral changes—such as reduced condom use and an increased number of sexual partners—that could contribute to rising rates of other sexually transmitted infections (STIs), such as syphilis, gonorrhea, and chlamydia [7, 8].

Previous studies have reported a concomitant increase in STIs and PrEP uptake [8, 9]. A California open-label clinical cohort study showed that 50% of males taking PrEP were diagnosed with STIs within 1 year [9]. The incidence of STIs increased by 41%–72% among men who have sex with men (MSM) who initiated PrEP in Canada and Australia [8]. Risk compensation, such as lower condom use, may contribute to higher STI rates despite HIV prevention benefits [10, 11]. Additionally, social networks and sexual practices among PrEP users may further elevate STI risk [12].

Nevertheless, the relationship between PrEP usage and STI rates remains inconclusive [8, 13–16]. For example, the HIV Prevention Trials Network 073 study of 226 Black MSM over 52 weeks found no significant association between STI incidence and PrEP uptake or adherence, with odds ratios (OR) (95% confidence interval [CI]) of 1.47 (.73–2.96) and 0.60 (.27–1.31) [13]. In addition, a meta-analysis of 17 studies (16 observational, 1 open-label experiment) reported a modest association between PrEP usage and chlamydia in MSM and transgender women (OR: 1.23, 95% CI: 1.00–1.51), but no significant association with overall STI diagnosis, syphilis, or gonorrhea [15].

Despite challenges in isolating the association between PrEP usage and STIs, previous studies have largely focused on specific populations, such as MSM, and often over limited timeframes [8, 13, 14, 17, 18]. Meanwhile, new STI preventive techniques like doxycycline post- or pre-exposure prophylaxis (doxyPEP/doxyPrEP) have demonstrated encouraging effectiveness in lowering bacterial STIs among high-risk populations, including MSM and transgender women [19–22]. These findings draw attention to the changing scene of STI prevention and the necessity of thorough population-level trend monitoring. Given the absence of published studies examining national-level and longitudinal trends in STI incidence over an extended period, we aimed to provide a whole picture of STIs evolving 10 years before and after PrEP introduction in the United States. Our goal was to explore potential correlations between PrEP uptake and STI patterns and to identify emerging differences across subpopulations that may inform future public health strategies.

METHODS

Data Sources

We conducted an ecological time-series study to examine national trends in STIs in relation to PrEP usage in the United States from 2001 to 2022. PrEP usage data from 2012 to 2022 were obtained from AIDSVu.org, a publicly accessible source (https://aidsvu.org/resources/#/datasets) [23]. The dataset provides annual national, state, and county-level statistics on PrEP usage and new HIV diagnoses, with national data stratified by sex, age, and ethnicity.

STI case counts and rates from 2001 to 2022 were obtained from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's (CDC) STI Statistics Archive (https://www.cdc.gov/sti-statistics/archive) [24]. The data include annual national and state-level statistics on syphilis, gonorrhea, and chlamydia by sex, age, and race.

As this study used no identifiable data, institutional review board review was not required.

PrEP Usage

PrEP usage estimates were obtained from AIDSVu, which compiles commercial pharmacy data covering 90% of retail pharmacies, its methodology has been previously documented [25]. Although these estimates may underestimate national PrEP prescriptions, consistent coverage over time can preserve correlations with STI trends [26]. Data included sex, age, and race/ethnicity, but not individual identifiers.

Sexually Transmitted Infections

STIs included in the analysis were syphilis, gonorrhea, and chlamydia. STI case data were obtained from CDC's National Notifiable Disease Surveillance System. Surveillance methods and case definitions remained relatively consistent over time, minimizing potential impacts on trend interpretation [27].

Statistical Analysis

The AIDSVu PrEP dataset reports prescriptions for individuals aged 13 years and older, with age categories defined as <25, 25–34, 35–44, 45–54, and ≥55 years. The STI Surveillance Archive provides age groups as <25, 25–29, 30–34, 35–39, 40–44, 45–54, 55–64, and ≥65 years.

To align these 2 datasets, we restricted STI data to individuals aged ≥13 and estimated the 13–14 subgroup by applying 40% of the 10–14 age group. This was combined with 15–24 to match the <25 PrEP category. Other STI age groups were similarly aggregated to match PrEP groupings. Population sizes were estimated by dividing STI cases by reported rates and used to calculate age-specific STI rates for the harmonized categories.

STI cases and rates by sex and race were also restricted to individuals aged 13 and older. As PrEP data classified race as Black, White, and Hispanic, STI cases and rates were similarly grouped for consistency.

To assess the relationship between HIV PrEP uptake and STI trends, we used coefficients of determination (R2) from linear regression models, treating annual STI rates as the dependent variable and annual PrEP usage rates as the independent variable. R2 was used to quantify the proportion of variance in STI rates explained by PrEP usage at the national level.

To explore temporal associations, we performed cross-correlation analyses between PrEP usage rates and each STI rate to identify potential lagged relationships, using cross-correlation functions [28]. This enabled us to detect whether changes in STI rates followed changes in PrEP uptake with a time delay.

To evaluate whether STI trends changed significantly after PrEP implementation, we applied segmented linear regression models consistent with an interrupted time-series framework. STI rates were modeled as a function of time (year), with a knot in 2012—the year PrEP was approved. Two separate slopes were estimated: one for the pre-PrEP period (2001–2011) and one for the post-PrEP period (2012–2022). Models were stratified by sex, age group, and race. Estimated slopes and standard errors were obtained using ordinary least squares, and we applied corrections for autocorrelation and heteroskedasticity using robust standard errors to strengthen the validity of inferences based on aggregate-level data.

We conducted sensitivity analyses using 2013 and 2014 as alternative starting points, applying the same modeling approach as with 2012. Additional analyses excluded the COVID-19 years (2020–2022) to assess robustness. We performed the analyses with SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc.) and R version 4.4.0.

RESULTS

PrEP Usage Rate

PrEP usage rose annually from 4 to 154 per 100 000 people between 2012 and 2022, increasing across sex, race, and age groups, with some variations. Male usage rates increased more dramatically, from 5 to 288 per 100 000 people, compared with 3 to 24 per 100 000 among females. Black individuals had the highest overall usage, while White individuals showed the largest relative increase, rising 55-fold (from 3 to 165 per 100 000). The 25–34 age group experienced the greatest rise (>60-fold), followed by those under 25 (Table 1).

Trends in STI Rate

The syphilis rate increased from 3 per 100 000 people in 2001 to 6 per 100 000 in 2012 and 21 per 100 000 in 2022. These national rates were based on the US population aged 13 years and older, which ranged from ∼233 million in 2001 to ∼283 million in 2022. Before PrEP was introduced in 2012, syphilis rates increased by 0.2 per 100 000 yearly but increased by 1.4 per 100 000 after PrEP rollout. Gonorrhea rates decreased by 2.9 per 100 000 annually before 2012 but increased by 14 per 100 000 annually afterward. However, chlamydia rates rose by 20.9 annually before 2012, then slowed to 6.6 after PrEP introduction (Figure 1 and Supplementary Tables 1–3).

Coefficient of Determination Between STI Rates and PrEP Usage

From 2012 to 2022, the coefficient of determination between PrEP usage and the syphilis rate was 0.98, and 0.88 for the gonorrhea rate. However, the R2 for chlamydia was much lower at 0.20 (Figure 2).

Among males, the coefficients of determination for syphilis, gonorrhea, and chlamydia were 0.98, 0.89, and 0.44, respectively. In females, the corresponding values were 0.95, 0.79, and 0.02. Across age groups, syphilis R2 values ranged from 0.94 to 0.98, and gonorrhea from 0.77 to 0.93. For chlamydia, the R2 was only 0.02 in the <25 age group but ranged from 0.66 to 0.85 in older groups. By race, syphilis R2 values exceeded 0.95, gonorrhea exceeded 0.79, while chlamydia ranged from 0.01 to 0.34 (Supplementary Figures 1–3).

Cross-Correlation Between PrEP Usage and STI Rates

The cross-correlation coefficients for syphilis and gonorrhea peaked at the same time point, with values of 0.99 and 0.94, respectively, and declined with increasing lag. Nevertheless, chlamydia showed a maximum correlation of 0.69 at a 3-year lag (Figure 2).

Syphilis showed no temporal lag, with peak correlation coefficients in both males and females. Gonorrhea coefficients were highest at lag times of zero or 1 year, regardless of sex. For chlamydia, coefficients in males remained ∼0.60 within a 4-year lag, while the highest value in females (0.66) occurred at a 3-year lag.

Across all age groups, syphilis showed the highest cross-correlation at zero lag. Gonorrhea and chlamydia also peaked at zero lag in all age groups except those under 25. By race, syphilis and gonorrhea showed the highest correlations at zero lag across all groups, while chlamydia did not (Supplementary Figures 4–6).

Subgroup Trends in STI Rates

Syphilis rates in males increased from a slope of 0.7 to 2.0 after PrEP introduction, while in females, it rose from 0 to 0.9. Gonorrhea rates dropped in both sexes before PrEP enrollment but rose post-introduction by 19.1 per 100 000 in males and 9 per 100 000 in females each year. For chlamydia, the slope decreased from 16.7 to 12.5 in males and from 24.1 to −0.5 in females after PrEP rollout.

Following PrEP introduction, syphilis rates increased more markedly across all age categories. Gonorrhea rates, previously declining, began to rise. Chlamydia rates declined among individuals under 35 but increased in other age groups. By race, syphilis and gonorrhea trends were similar across groups. However, the chlamydia rate showed divergent patterns: the rate decreased to −9.6 in Black individuals but increased to 0.6 in White individuals after PrEP rollout (Figures 3–5 and Supplementary Tables 1–3).

Proportional Composition of PrEP Usage and STI Rates

PrEP usage was predominantly male, increasing from 61.5% in 2012 to 92.2% in 2022. The percentage of male gonorrhea and chlamydia cases increased as well after PrEP introduction. PrEP usage among individuals aged 25–44 grew from 53.0% in 2012 to 64.2% in 2022, accompanied by a rise in syphilis, gonorrhea, and chlamydia cases in this age group. By race, Blacks' proportion of PrEP usage decreased from 21.8% to 14.3%, whereas Hispanics’ and Whites’ percentages increased. Similarly, the proportion of syphilis and gonorrhea cases in Blacks also declined after 2012, while it increased among Hispanics and Whites (Figure 6).

Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analyses using 2013 and 2014 as alternative start points yielded results consistent with the primary analysis, supporting the robustness of our findings. Excluding the early COVID-19 years also had no impact on the conclusions (Supplementary Figure 7).

DISCUSSION

We found that STI rate trends changed significantly across multiple demographic populations as PrEP usage increased. After PrEP introduction, syphilis rates accelerated, with a strong correlation to PrEP usage (R2 = 0.98) and no observed lag. Gonorrhea, previously declining, rose considerably post-PrEP, with an R2 of 0.88, indicating a strong association with PrEP use regardless of a 0–1 year lag. Nonetheless, chlamydia showed a weak association with PrEP use (R2 = 0.2). Associations between PrEP use and STI incidence were stronger in men than women and varied by race and age group.

The high coefficient of determination (R2 = 0.98) suggests that much of the variance in syphilis rates is explained by PrEP use, reflecting a strong correlation between PrEP uptake and rising syphilis incidence. This aligns with previous studies reporting increased syphilis rates among MSM following PrEP rollout [29–31]. For instance, an Australian sentinel surveillance study (2012–2022) of gay and bisexual men found syphilis incidence among PrEP users increased from 1.3 to 5.1 per 100 person-years, with a hazard ratio of 1.78 (95% CI: 1.61–1.97) for those prescribed PrEP in the prior 12 months [30].

Although gonorrhea rates declined before PrEP implementation, they rose sharply afterward, with the annual slope shifting from −2.9 to 14. The iCruise study involving 535 MSM similarly found that PrEP usage was associated with increased gonorrhea incidence (relative risk [RR] = 4.0; 95% CI: 1.7–9.6), but not with syphilis or chlamydia [32]. Another prospective cohort of 1326 PrEP users in Denmark reported significantly elevated rates of gonorrhea and chlamydia, even after adjusting for increased STI testing, while syphilis showed no substantial alteration (RR = 1.15; 95% CI: .76–1.72) [17]. These discrepancies may arise from variations in study design, sample size, and the relatively low incidence of syphilis, which may limit statistical power.

Annual incidence patterns of syphilis and gonorrhea before and after the PrEP introduction were similar across sex, age, and race, unlike chlamydia (Figures 3–5). Syphilis and gonorrhea exhibited faster transmission dynamics than chlamydia, especially in HIV-priority populations such as MSM [33]. Gonorrhea often presents with acute symptoms, which can lead to more frequent clinical detection. In contrast, syphilis frequently progresses through asymptomatic stages, including latent phases, and is often identified only through routine screening. Chlamydia, meanwhile, tends to persist as a more biologically stable and typically asymptomatic infection, which may result in underdiagnosis unless systematically screened [34]. Consistently, we found that the impact of PrEP on chlamydia had a longer lag period—3 years—compared with syphilis and gonorrhea. Additionally, chlamydia transmission was less concentrated in high-risk networks and more commonly associated with vaginal sex [35, 36]. Furthermore, our results showed that ∼75% of chlamydia cases occurred in females, while over 75% of PrEP users were male (Figure 6), which may explain the weaker correlation between chlamydia and PrEP usage.

Although PrEP usage explained only 20% of the variance in chlamydia incidence overall, subgroup analyses showed stronger correlations—0.44 in men and over 0.66 in individuals aged 25 or older. These findings underscored the need for targeted interventions in populations most affected by chlamydia. Overall, the association between PrEP use and STI incidence was stronger in men than in women, likely reflecting the male-dominant profile of PrEP users (Figure 6). Additional factors may contribute to these disparities. Men, particularly MSM, tend to report more sexual partners and lower condom use when on PrEP [37, 38]. High-risk sexual networks, especially among MSM, can amplify STI and HIV transmission due to overlapping infections in tightly connected groups [39]. Biological differences may also play a role: men may face higher per-contract risk for certain STIs, especially those spread through anal and vaginal mucosal contact. Rectal tissue, in particular, is highly susceptible to certain infections [40], while women may benefit from natural protective factors such as vaginal flora and mucosal defenses, potentially reducing the correlation between PrEP use and STI incidence [41].

Risk compensation may partially explain higher STI rates among PrEP users, though findings have been mixed over the past decade [8]. Individuals on PrEP may feel protected against HIV and engage in higher-risk sexual activities, including condomless sex, without thinking through the possibility of STIs [8, 17, 42]. Gaps in awareness about STI risks and the importance of frequent STI testing or condom use may further contribute [43, 44]. Additionally, PrEP users—especially MSM—might increase their number of sexual partners, and studies have reported rises in casual encounters following PrEP uptake, elevating STI risk [18, 45].

Although the relative increase in syphilis rates observed in this study may appear modest in absolute terms, it is nonetheless concerning from a public health standpoint due to the severe consequences of untreated syphilis. While chlamydia rates did not show a marked increase over time, chlamydia remains the most reported bacterial STI in the United States. Its high baseline burden underscores the continued need for sustained screening and prevention efforts.

This study has several strengths. First, the 2-decade time series enabled evaluation of STI trends before and after PrEP introduction. The use of national data enhances generalizability across diverse populations. By focusing on 3 common STIs—syphilis, gonorrhea, and chlamydia—we provide insight into how PrEP uptake might affect infection patterns. Particularly, given the low incidence of syphilis, the comprehensive national analysis could support the identification of associations. Furthermore, a complete statistical analysis included trend analysis, coefficient of determinant estimates, cross-correlation analysis, and subgroup analysis by sex, race, and age to examine trends and correlations in more detail, strengthening disparities and potential areas for focused prevention. While the link between STIs and PrEP is not new, our findings added new evidence by highlighting how the confluence of 2 epidemics highlights the importance of integrated sexual health treatments. Lastly, we also conducted sensitivity analyses to testify to the robustness of our findings.

Regarding the limitations, firstly, the national PrEP use data lacked joint stratifications (e.g., age-by-sex, race-by-sex, or age-by-race), limiting more detailed subgroup analyses. Secondly, the data may be subject to misclassification biases; for instance, race information was available for only one-third of individuals with PrEP prescriptions, and it was assumed that the racial distribution of those with missing data resembled that of those with reported data [23]. Additionally, STI surveillance data are prone to underreporting, particularly for asymptomatic infections, potentially leading to underestimation of STI diagnoses.

Importantly, the study spans 2 decades during which STI testing guidelines and diagnostic technologies evolved substantially. For example, the adoption of nucleic acid amplification tests improved detection sensitivity for gonorrhea and chlamydia, particularly among asymptomatic individuals. The application of the reverse sequence algorithm for syphilis testing enhanced the detection of latent or previously untreated infections [46–48]. These technical advancements certainly facilitated enhanced case detection, irrespective of actual incidence, and may partially explain rising STI trends. Additionally, routine STI screening—now recommended every 3 months as part of PrEP care—has increased testing frequency among PrEP users, further contributing to higher reported STI rates in recent years.

Another limitation is that no single entity or data source currently captures comprehensive information on all PrEP users nationwide. Instead, national estimates are derived from prescription data, which included imputed values to approximate PrEP prescriptions not directly observed [23]. Due to the absence of individual-level data linking PrEP use to STI outcomes, we are unable to draw direct causal inferences or assess whether the observed changes in STI trends are behaviorally driven. Additionally, the national PrEP dataset does not include information on MSM, transgender individuals, or people who inject drugs. As PrEP uptake initially concentrated among MSM and may have expanded to include more non-MSM populations over time, the absence of behavioral data limits our ability to assess shifting patterns of PrEP use and STI trends within these key groups. Nonetheless, national estimates provide valuable insight into overarching trends, support resource allocation, and offer a foundation for future research using more detailed individual-level data.

Our findings demonstrated significant changes in STI patterns before and after the introduction of PrEP, which may help inform future prevention efforts. Integrating comprehensive sexual health education with HIV prevention is essential, particularly as PrEP use continues to expand. This includes routine discussions of STI prevention strategies for PrEP users, such as the emerging use of doxyPEP, which is the first intervention in decades to demonstrate strong efficacy in preventing certain STIs [19–22]. Training for PrEP prescribers on STI risk and prevention is also critical to ensure that patients receive comprehensive sexual health counseling. While programs encouraging condom use among HIV-priority populations remain valuable, it is well documented that many individuals choose not to use condoms. Providers should give counseling that is patient-centered and nonjudgmental, helps people make educated choices, and is in line with their own preferences. PrEP care is a great chance to deliver integrated HIV and STI prevention services in a way that is both effective and responsive to patients’ real-world behaviors.

CONCLUSIONS

We examined STI trends in the United States from 2001 to 2022, before and after PrEP implementation. Marked changes in STI incidence were observed, along with strong correlations between STI rates and PrEP usage, particularly among males. The rise in syphilis and gonorrhea rates may be partially driven by PrEP uptake. While PrEP is a vital part of HIV prevention campaigns, it is also essential to give a comprehensive sexual health framework top priority, therefore addressing the related increase in STIs. As PrEP access continues to change the landscape of HIV prevention, future studies should concentrate on assessing the efficacy of integrated sexual health services and long-term trends of STIs.
