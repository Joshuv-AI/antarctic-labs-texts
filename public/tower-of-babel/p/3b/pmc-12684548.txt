Gestational Diabetes and Incident Hospitalization for Cardiovascular Disease: A Nationwide French Cohort Study
Authors: Justin B Echouffo‐Tcheugui, Solène Tapia, Sonia Bechraoui‐Quantin, Jonathan Cottenet, Emmanuel Simon, Catherine Quantin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of mortality in women. We investigated the associations between gestational diabetes (GD) and the incidence of various CVD outcomes.MethodsThis nationwide population‐based cohort study included 1 436 468 parous women in France during 2012 to 2013. We used Cox regression to (1) quantify the association of GD with incident hospitalization for chronic hypertension as well as overall and type‐specific CVD, (2) investigate the timing to GD‐related CVD onset in the postpartum period, and (3) assess the impact of the recurrence of GD on CVD incidence.ResultsWomen with a history of GD (n=101 814) had a 97% increased relative risk of chronic hypertension (hazard ratio [HR], 1.97 [95% CI, 1.89–2.06]) and a 31% higher overall CVD risk (HR, 1.31 [95% CI, 1.22–1.41]) compared with those without such history. There were increased risks of specific CVDs associated with GD (versus no GD), including increased coronary heart disease (HR, 1.71 [95% CI, 1. 50–1.94]), heart failure (HR, 1.41 [95% CI, 1.21–1.65]), and stroke (HR, 1.16 [95% CI, 1.06–1.28]) risks. The elevated risk was apparent as early as 1 year postpartum for chronic hypertension and CVD outcomes, and the elevated CVD risk was more pronounced among women with ≥2 pregnancies complicated by GD during the study period than in those with 1 GD episode.ConclusionsGD was associated with increased risks of overall and specific CVD, as well as the risk of incident chronic hypertension. The elevated CVD risk was present early in postpartum and persisted over time, and was higher with repeated GD.Keywords: cardiovascular disease, gestational diabetes, recurrence, time to disease onsetSubject Categories: Epidemiology, Cardiovascular Disease

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is common 1 and increasingly frequent among young individuals including women of childbearing age. 2 Accumulating evidence suggests that gestational diabetes (GD), a known precursor of diabetes, 3 is associated with a high risk of developing CVD in the years after pregnancy. 4 , 5 However, there is a dearth of nationwide studies on the influence of GD on the incidence of various type of cardiovascular events. 6 Moreover, the vast majority of studies on the link between GD and CVD have been cross‐sectional, case–control, or retrospective cohort studies, 4 , 5 with the few extant prospective cohort studies relying on self‐reported GD as the exposure. 4 , 5 The existing studies have also not specifically investigated the time to onset of CVD after GD, which can inform us on the most appropriate window for early detection and intervention to prevent CVD in young women. The existing studies have also seldom or not examined the impact of GD recurrence on CVD outcomes studies. 4 , 5 , 6

Using a nationwide French cohort, we investigated the association of GD with (1) incident hospitalization for postpartum or chronic hypertension as well as overall and specific types of CVD outcomes, (2) the timing to GD‐related CVD onset in the postpartum period and in the 10 years following delivery, and (3) the impact of recurrent GD on these associations. We hypothesized that the CVD risk become elevated early in the postpartum period, and that recurrent GD will be associated with a much higher postpartum risks of hypertension and CVD.

METHODS

All data sets generated or analyzed during the current study are not publicly available but are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Study Population

Data Source

Our sampling frame for participants consisted of all women in France with information in the French national Programme de Médicalisation des Systèmes d'Information (PMSI), which is a medical administrative database that contains discharge abstracts from all French hospitals, both public and private. In this database, the conditions diagnosed during a hospital stay are coded according to the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10), and the procedures performed in hospitals are coded according to the French Common Classification of Medical Procedures (CCMP). The information in the PMSI database is anonymous. The unique anonymous identification number generated for each patient allows linkage of all hospitalizations for an individual, thus allowing for longitudinal studies. The use of this database for the allocation of hospital budgets encourages improvement in data quality in terms of coherence, accuracy, and exhaustiveness, because of internal and external validations of its quality. Therefore, the PMSI hospital data have been used in medical research for many years, 7 , 8 and their high quality has been confirmed in recent perinatal studies. 9 , 10 Almost all births (out‐of‐hospital deliveries represent only 0.4% of births) are recorded in the PMSI, which enables the coverage of the entire set of deliveries within a specified timeframe. 11

Population

In the present study, we included all deliveries of women who gave birth between January 1, 2012 and December 31, 2013. We used the ICD‐10 code Z37 (except Z37.9) associated with different procedures to identify delivery (N=1 589 859). We then excluded deliveries to women whose maternal age was <12 and >55 years (to retain women of childbearing age [n=16]), and then deliveries of women who died during the stay (n=115). We then excluded deliveries to women who had diabetes (ICD‐10 codes E10‐E14) or GD (ICD‐10 code O24) in the 5 years preceding the pregnancy (n=38 425).

We then excluded deliveries to women with a history of CVD or chronic hypertension in the 5 years preceding or during the pregnancy (N=12 167). To count each woman only once, only the first pregnancy of the inclusion period and the associated delivery were considered (index pregnancy, N=1 509 844). After excluding women who did not have GD in the index pregnancy but did in a subsequent pregnancy during the follow‐up period, the remaining sample of women (N=1 436 468) was categorized into women who had GD during the inclusion period (N=101 814) and those who did not have GD during either the inclusion period or the follow‐up period (N=1 334 654). The process of selection of the study participants is shown in Figure S1.

Ethics Approval

This study was approved by the French National Committee for Data Protection (declaration of conformity to the methodology of reference 05 obtained on July 8, 2018, under the number 2204633 v0). Written consent was not needed for this study, because this was a retrospective study, and the national data used were anonymous.

Ascertainment of GD

The GD exposure was ascertained at index pregnancy and subsequent pregnancies during the study period. GD was identified using the ICD‐10 code O24 in the hospital records at index pregnancy and subsequent pregnancies during the study period.

Cardiovascular Outcomes

The outcomes of interest included incident hospitalization for chronic hypertension and CVD events. The composite CVD outcome was defined as hospitalization for coronary artery disease, heart failure, or stroke. The individual CVD outcomes included coronary heart disease (angina pectoris, myocardial infarction, and coronary revascularization), heart failure, and cerebrovascular disease (including both hemorrhagic and ischemic stroke, transient ischemic attack, and carotid revascularization). The ICD‐10 codes used for the identification of the study outcomes are presented in Table S1.

Chronic hypertension and all cardiovascular events (coronary heart disease, heart failure, and cerebrovascular disease) were searched from the discharge of the index delivery until December 31, 2023, allowing for a 10‐year follow‐up period. If a woman was hospitalized for the same condition more than once during follow‐up, only the first event was considered. Moreover, the composite CVD outcome that included any of the aforementioned cardiovascular events (excepted chronic hypertension) was defined by the first occurrence of at least 1 of the aforementioned CVD conditions.

Covariates

The covariates considered in our analyses were selected based on a priori literature‐based knowledge of their potential associations with the exposure (GD) and the CVD outcomes. The conceptual framework that guided our approach to adjustment for the covariates is shown in a direct acrylic graph included in Figure S2. The covariates included maternal age (<20, 20–29, 30–39, and ≥40 years), parity (only available for vaginal deliveries in our database), smoking (ICD‐10 code F17 [except F17.0], T65.2, and Z72.0), alcohol use (ICD‐10 code F10 [except F10.0], G62.1, G72.1, I42.6, K29.2, K85.2, K86.0, and K70), family history of CVD (ICD‐10 codes Z82.4), hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (ICD‐10 codes O10‐O16), body mass index ≥40 kg/m2 (ICD‐10 codes E6601‐02, E6606‐07, E6611‐12, E6616‐17, E6621‐22, E6626‐27, E6681‐82, E6686‐87, E6691‐92, and E6696‐97), dyslipidemia (ICD‐10 code E78), and chronic renal failure (ICD‐10 codes N18‐N19, I12.0, I13.1, N03.2‐N03.7, N05.2‐N05.7, N25.0, Z49.0‐Z49.2, Z94.0, Z99.2). These variables were extracted, for each woman, from all hospital stays during the index pregnancy (specifying whether it was a single or multiple pregnancy).

Statistical Analysis

The characteristics of the study population were examined by GD status. Continuous variables were presented as mean±SD (or median and interquartile range for variables with skewed distributions), and categorical variables as percentages. The differences in baseline characteristics tested using χ2 tests for categorical variables and appropriate parametric or nonparametric tests for continuous variables.

The rates of outcomes over the entire study period were computed by GD status as proportions. For incident hospitalization for events, we assessed the risks across the exposure categories (GD versus no‐GD [reference]), using Cox proportional hazards regression models to derive hazard ratios and 95% CIs for the GD and risks of outcomes association. The study participants who died during follow‐up were censored, if the death was not related to 1 of the cardiovascular events.

We performed Cox proportional hazard regression using the counting process formulation to consider the fact that another pregnancy with GD may have occurred during follow‐up. We also used the extended Cox regression with a step function model to estimate the effect of the exposure variable in different time intervals: during the first year, from 1 to 3 years, from 3 to 5 years, and from 5 to 10 years of follow‐up.

All models were adjusted for maternal age at index delivery, single or multiple pregnancy, vaginal delivery, family history of CVD, smoking, alcohol use, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, body mass index ≥40 kg/m2, dyslipidemia, and chronic renal failure.

We performed a series of sensitivity analyses. First, to limit the effect of the potential underrecording of smoking, we performed a sensitivity analysis in which we restricted the analyses to women with a history of smoking. Second, we conducted another sensitivity analysis including only vaginal deliveries, which allowed us to consider parity as an adjustment variable.

Two‐sided P values of <0.05 were considered statistically significant. All analyses were done using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

RESULTS

The study population consisted of 1 436 468 women (mean age, 29.80±5.40 years), among whom 101 814 (7%) had GD and 1 334 654 did not have GD. Compared with women without a history of GD, those with a history of GD were slightly older and had higher frequencies of multiple pregnancies, smoking, alcohol use, family history of CVD, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, body mass index ≥40 kg/m2, and dyslipidemia. However, they had fewer vaginal deliveries, and among these vaginal deliveries, there were more multiparous women among women with GD than among women without GD.

GD and Incident Hospitalization for CVD Events

Cumulatively over the 10‐year study period, the prevalence of chronic hypertension or CVD outcomes was generally twice as high among individuals with a history of GD as compared with those without a history GD (Table S2). Compared with individuals without a history of GD, those with a history of GD were more likely to have developed chronic hypertension (3.01% versus 0.97%, P<0.001), overall CVD (0.87% versus 0.54%, P<0.001), coronary heart disease (0.28% versus 0.12%, P<0.001), heart failure (0.18% versus 0.10%, P<0.001), and cerebrovascular disease (0.46% versus 0.34%, P<0.001).

In multivariable adjusted analyses, compared with individuals without GD, the 10‐year risks of chronic hypertension, overall CVD outcome, and each specific CVD type were significantly higher in women with GD compared with those without GD (Table 2). In particular, there was a 2‐fold higher relative risk of chronic hypertension (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 1.97 [95% CI, 1.89–2.06]), a 1.3‐fold higher risk of overall CVD events (aHR, 1.31 [95% CI, 1.22–1.41]), and a ~2‐fold higher risk of coronary heart disease (aHR, 1.71 [95% CI, 1.50–1.94]) among women with a history of GD compared with those without such history. The aHRs for heart failure and cerebrovascular disease risks associated with GD (versus no GD) were 1.41 (95% CI, 1.21–1.65) and 1.16 (95% CI, 1.06–1.28), respectively.

For chronic hypertension, the relative risk was 1.86 (95% CI, 1.78–1.95) for 1 pregnancy complicated by GD and 2.73 (95% CI, 2.50–2.98) for having at least 2 pregnancies with GD, as compared with no pregnancy with GD. The corresponding relative risks estimates for the composite CVD outcome was 1.30 (95% CI, 1.20–1.40) for 1 pregnancy with GD and 1.40 (95% CI, 1.17–1.68) for ≥2 pregnancies with GD. For heart failure, the risk was twice as high when there were at least 2 pregnancies with GD (aHR, 2.27 [95% CI, 1.63–3.16]) versus 1.3 times higher in the cases of 1 pregnancy only with GD (aHR, 1.29 [95% CI, 1.08–1.53]), when compared with no pregnancy with GD. For coronary heart disease, the risk associated with a history of at least 2 pregnancies with GD (aHR, 1.95 [95% CI, 1.43–2.66]) was higher than that associated with 1 pregnancy with GD (aHR, 1.67 [95% CI, 1.45–1.92]), when compared with no pregnancy with GD. For cerebrovascular disease, the risks were not different between 1 pregnancy with GD and having ≥1 pregnancy with GD.

Sensitivity Analyses

All sensitivity analyses (stratification on severe smoking and stratification on vaginal deliveries) yielded results that were similar to the main analysis. The risks were greater for women with severe smoking, except for those with at least 2 pregnancies with GD, probably because of the small numbers involved (Tables S3 and S4). For the sensitivity analysis focusing on women with a vaginal delivery (allowing adjustment for parity), the results are similar (Tables S5 and S6).

DISCUSSION

In a large nationwide French cohort study, extended 10‐year follow‐up after delivery revealed that a history of GD was associated with increased risks of chronic hypertension and overall CVD, as well as increased risks for specific types of CVD, in particular, coronary heart disease, heart failure, and cerebrovascular disease after accounting for pregnancy‐related factors and traditional CVD risk factors. We also observed that the recurrence of GD over the study period was overall associated with higher CVD risk compared with just 1 instance of GD. Moreover, the higher risk of CVD (overall and for specific outcomes) associated with GD was generally evident as early as 1 year after the index pregnancy and persisted over time. Of note, there was 2‐fold elevation in the risk of developing chronic hypertension as early as 6 months postpartum.

To our knowledge, our study is the first and the largest nationwide study to evaluate the effect of GD on the postpartum risk of various CVD outcomes in France, based on a combination of perinatal and postpartum data. Our study provides additional and valuable evidence on the links between GD and various specific CVD outcomes. Our results are congruent with those of prior smaller and nonnationwide investigations showing an increased risk of CVD events associated with GD. 4 , 5 However, such studies did not always examine specific CVD outcomes. 4 , 5 Our results are comparable with those of a recent nationwide Danish cohort, which showed that the risk of overall and various specific CVD outcomes were increased among women with a history of GD as compared with those without such a history. 6 However, our investigation included a much larger number of women and thus a higher frequency of GD than this latter Danish study. 6 Moreover, our observations extend the literature on GD and CVD, by providing additional evidence on the influence of the recurrence of GD on the incidence of CVD postpartum, as well as novel information on the timing to onset of CVD. Our results on a higher extent of CVD associated with ≥2 pregnancies as compared with only 1 pregnancy with GD suggest a dose–response relationship between GD and the postpartum CVD risk. This finding fulfills the Bradford Hill criterion of biological gradient, 12 strongly supporting a causal link between GD and CVD. The results on the timing of GD‐related CVD onset are critical for effective planning of postpartum CVD prevention in women.

Our results are clinically relevant because they further reinforce the importance of GD as a potential screening and therapeutic target to achieve CVD prevention among young women. Our findings on the timing to onset of CVD after a pregnancy with GD and the CVD risk associated with the recurrence of GD have potential practical implications. These suggest that an early and possibly systematic CVD detection after a pregnancy complicated by GD (especially in cases of GD recurrence) should be a priority, because it has the potential to prevent future CVD postpartum. 13 The 2‐fold elevation in the risk of developing chronic hypertension in 6 months after a pregnancy complicated by GD points to the need for regular blood pressure monitoring in the aftermath of a pregnancy complicated by GD, especially because this is infrequently performed and is currently not formally recommended in the current guidelines. 13 Such blood pressure monitoring postpregnancy complicated by GD has potential benefits, as suggested by observation in cases of pregnancies complicated by hypertension. 14 Although the rates of factors, such as maternal obesity, that influence the onset of CVD in the postpartum of pregnancies complicated by GD, are lower in our data or in France as compared with other countries such as the United States, this is unlikely to have a major effect on our estimates, because we accounted for the most severe cases of obesity (which will have the most effect) in our database. The relevance of screening for CVD risk factor or events in the postpartum period is illustrated by the rising CVD burden in the younger population 2 and the high and possibly rising prevalence of GD in France 15 and other settings such as the United States. 16 , 17 The vast majority of women (>80%) will undergo pregnancy during their lifetime 18 ; thus, screening detection of GD during pregnancy can help foster a more effective and early postpartum CVD monitoring and modification of CVD risk factors among young women. This prevention opportunity is congruent with the recommendations of the cardiovascular 13 , 19 as well as the obstetrician and gynecologist professional bodies. 20 , 21

The mechanistic pathways linking GD to CVD are incompletely understood. Insulin resistance is a possible pathway linking GD to CVD. 22 The effect of GD on incident CVD may be mediated by its effect on precursors, including cardiovascular risk factors, among which is type 2 diabetes. 3 Of note, in the years before pregnancy, evidence points to disparities in cardiovascular risk factors between women who will develop future GD and their non‐GD counterparts. Consequently, after pregnancy, these preexisting disparities in risk factors demonstrate divergently amplified trajectories. 23 Thus, pregnancy appears as a potent aggravator of the cardiovascular risk of women who had a preexisting propensity to developing GD. However, the persistence of the GD and CVD association after adjustment for well‐known cardiovascular risk factors suggests an intrinsic GD effect on the vessels and the myocardium. GD can induce endothelial dysfunction, a precursor of CVD. 24 , 25 , 26 Other possible pathways linking GD and CVD, similar to that observed with type 2 diabetes, include the effects of glucotoxicity, lipotoxicity, and hyperinsulinemia on the vessels and myocardium; increased deposition of advanced glycation end products; and systemic oxidative stress leading to myocardial or vascular injury and fibrosis. 27 , 28

There are limitations to our study. First, some cardiovascular risk factors are not captured in the administrative databases used in our investigation, thus the is the possibility of residual confounding. These include, for example, behavioral factors (eg, dietary intake and physical activity). Second, we accounted for excess adiposity at the time of index pregnancy. It is however important to indicate that extant evidence suggest that women with GD have an adverse CVD risk factor profile irrespective of body mass index before 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 and after pregnancy. 33 , 34 , 35 Women with and without GD also may not differ in the extent of changes in adiposity in the aftermath of pregnancy. 36 Third, we did not use blood glucose data to define GD, but GD diagnoses from the PMSI administrative data have been shown to be highly reliable and valid. 37 Fourth, we lacked data on the use of cardioprotective medications in the postpartum period. Last, our prevalence of GD may not be comparable with that in other countries, because this is dependent upon GD screening and diagnostic criteria, and these vary by country. Since 2010, GD screening is recommended in all French women at high risk (maternal age ≥35 years, body mass index ≥25 kg/m2, having a first‐degree relative with diabetes, or prior history of GD or delivery of large‐for‐gestational‐age baby), and is conducted with fasting blood glucose test at the initial prenatal visit and then a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test at 24 to 28 weeks of pregnancy. 38 GD diagnosis is based on fasting glucose ≥92 mg/dL in early pregnancy or postload glucose at 24 to 28 weeks of ≥180 mg/dL at 1 hour or ≥153 mg/dL at 2 hours. 39

Our study has strengths. First, our study is a prospective registry‐based cohort study comprising almost all pregnant women in France, 11 thus minimizing the potential influence of selection bias, referral, and recall bias. Our study includes assessments from a health care system in which there is systematic assessment for both GD among pregnant women and which allowed postpartum and 10‐year follow‐up for CVD, leveraging data on both cardiovascular events and relevant procedures. Our large sample size allowed for the examination of a wide range of specific CVD outcomes. We conducted a rigorous adjustment including known cardiovascular risk factors including pregnancy‐related and nonpregnancy‐related factors. Finally, substantial misclassification of CVD is unlikely because the validity of cardiovascular diagnoses and related procedures is high in the PMSI database. 40 , 41 , 42

CONCLUSIONS

We showed that a history of GD was significantly associated with increased risks of overall CVD and specific CVD types, as well as chronic hypertension, among French women. This increased risk was evident in the early postpartum period and persisted over time, and is also more pronounced in instances of recurrent GD. These results strongly suggest that systematic identification of GD in obstetrical practice, followed by immediate and continuous monitoring of women with a history of GD postpartum, especially among those with recurrent GD, is warranted and will most likely provide important opportunities to reduce their cardiovascular risk.
