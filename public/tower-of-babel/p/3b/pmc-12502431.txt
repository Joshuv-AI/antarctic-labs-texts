A survey on the trajectory of frailty in the middle-aged and elderly population in the Chengdu community: a retrospective observational study
Authors: Hang Zhao, Lujie Wei, Min Du, Xiaofeng Liu, Zhengping Tang, Jianping Li, Min Li, Pingyang Li, Xinzhu Chen, Yixiong Zheng, Cong Du, Huaicong Long
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFrailty, a prevalent clinical condition in the aging global population, increases the risk of disability, falls, and mortality. This study explores frailty trajectories and their influencing factors among middle-aged and elderly residents in Chengdu, China.Materials and methodsFrom May 2022 to April 2023, a face-to-face survey was conducted with residents of six Chengdu communities aged 50–89. Participants, all with normal cognitive function, reviewed their health conditions from age 30 to their current age. A frailty index (FI) comprising 45 parameters was calculated for each age segment. Latent class trajectory models (LCTM) were developed, and multinomial logistic regression was used to identify independent influencing factors.ResultsWe collected 1007 valid responses, with 536 from individuals over 65 years old. Women completed 51.4% of the questionnaires. Based on the FI, 48.46% of participants had no frailty (FI = 0–0.1), 36.94% had mild frailty (FI = 0.1–0.2), and 14.60% had moderate frailty (FI > 0.2). Women are more likely to experience fast-growing FI trajectories. Avoiding alcohol and ensuring adequate sleep are associated with a slower progression of frailty.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24661-5.Keywords: Middle-aged and elderly, Community-based population, Questionnaire, Frailty index, Frailty trajectory

Introduction

With rising Life expectancy, the global population aged 60 and above is expected to nearly double to 2.1 billion by 2050 [1]. Many countries are ill-prepared for this demographic shift, and healthcare systems are already strained [2, 3]. Frailty—an age-related clinical condition characterized by the decline of multiple physiological systems, including the musculoskeletal, cardiopulmonary, and immune systems—is common among older adults [4, 5]. Though prevalent, frailty is preventable and not inevitable for all aging individuals. It increases the risk of adverse outcomes such as disability, falls, and mortality [6]. The progression of frailty is dynamic, influenced by demographic factors, brain pathology, and comorbidities, and may be reversible [1, 7].

Identifying frailty trends and trajectories is a top priority in research, offering valuable insights for economic planning and policy formulation [1, 4, 7]. Despite its importance, longitudinal studies on frailty trajectories remain limited [1]. Internationally, the Fried frailty phenotype and the Frailty Index (FI) are principal tools for assessing frailty. The FI, based on a multidimensional definition, measures the accumulation of health deficits across medical, physical, psychological, and social domains, and predicts mortality as well as or better than the Fried phenotype [8, 9]. Unlike the frailty phenotype, which classifies individuals into discrete categories, the FI provides continuous measures and estimates trajectories, making it suitable for biological studies and a strong marker of biological age [6, 10]. In this study, we conducted a frailty trajectory analysis among Chengdu residents, using a frailty questionnaire to assess physiological, functional, and social conditions since age 30. The FI served as the core metric, allowing us to characterize frailty trajectories and identify influencing factors, thereby providing a foundation for developing targeted interventions.

Materials and methods

Study team and study design

The research team comprised five geriatricians and five systematically trained nurses specializing in the aging characteristics of the elderly population. The questionnaire used in this study was developed specifically for this study (The questionnaire is detailed in Supplementary File S1_Questionnaire). Questionnaire administrators were required to thoroughly understand the questionnaire content and techniques tailored for elderly participants. They were instructed to exercise patience with participants experiencing hearing and vision impairments, offer scientific guidance as needed, and conduct preliminary screenings of the collected data. This retrospective observational study utilizes longitudinal data and is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [11].

Study population

This is a retrospective longitudinal study of frailty in people aged 50 years and older in Chengdu, Sichuan, China. The research team conducted a questionnaire survey in selected communities targeting respondents aged 50–89 years who voluntarily participated and demonstrated normal cognitive function. The information about the participants was taken from the "Sichuan Geriatric Medical Center Frailty Survey" (data have not been published). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) people aged 50 years and older in Chengdu, Sichuan; (2) the ability to communicate normally; (3) cooperation with the survey and the ability to correctly recall the physical health and Lifestyle situation from the age of 30 to the present; and (4) having voluntarily signed the informed consent form and carefully completed the questionnaire. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) residing in Chengdu for less than five years; (2) having consciousness impairment, cognitive deficits, and an inability to converse regularly; and (3) being ostensibly hesitant to assist. All volunteers completed an informed consent form. To further examine the frailty trajectories in individuals over 65, we focused on this age group for the FI trajectory study. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the ethics committee of the Sichuan Academy of Medical Sciences and the Sichuan Provincial People's Hospital. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Data collection

The survey was conducted from May 2022 to April 2023, collecting data through face-to-face interviews with approximately 1,000 planned participants from 6 communities in Chengdu. Respondents, who could be accompanied by family members, recalled their past conditions, and questionnaires were completed in real-time by the research team. Respondents were asked to recall their conditions from age 30 to their current age groups (31 to 40 years as an age group, 41 to 70 years at 5-year intervals and 71 to 82 years at 4-year intervals). The questionnaire covered various aspects, including illnesses, medication use, falls, tinnitus, vision, hearing, chewing ability, self-care, mood, weight changes, fatigue, exercise, self-rated health, occupation, work stress, lunch breaks, night sleep, dietary intake, place of residence, and smoking and alcohol use at different ages. Each questionnaire took 30–60 min to complete, and quality control was conducted by two researchers. Based on previous literature [12], the FI was calculated using the self-reported information from the questionnaire.

Mitigating recall bias

To address the potential recall bias in our study design, we assessed participants' ability to accurately recall their health and Lifestyle from age 30 onwards during the initial telephone screening and baseline face-to-face interviews. Trained research coordinators evaluated recall ability by asking participants to describe at least two significant life events from early adulthood (e.g., marriage, first child's birth) and link these events to their health or lifestyle at that time. Participants were also required to undergo the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), with those scoring below 27 excluded to ensure basic cognitive capacity for recall. To further mitigate recall bias, we designed the questionnaire to focus on typical patterns of behavior and general health status within different age brackets (e.g., 30–39, 40–49) rather than precise frequencies. The recall period was segmented into intervals, and questions included explicit prompts referencing personal life events to aid recall. Data collection was conducted by trained interviewers. The questionnaire underwent pilot testing and cognitive interviews to refine ambiguous items and improve clarity. These measures ensured that participants had sufficient recall ability and that the study design and data collection process were optimized to reduce the impact of recall bias.

Calculation of FI

The FI is a continuous variable ranging from 0 (no deficiencies) to 1 (presence of all deficiencies), calculated as the number of deficiencies present divided by the total number of deficiencies in the inventory [1]. For example, an individual with 10 defects out of 50 health deficits would have an FI score of 0.2 (10/50) [13]. FI scores were calculated for each age group based on the method described in previous literature [14]. We selected 45 items for the health deficit list, including body mass index, illness, falls, tinnitus, vision and hearing, masticatory function, self-care, self-rated health, mood, and changes in body weight during the period (see Supplementary Appendix Table S1 for details).

Data analysis

Two researchers initially verified the authenticity and completeness of the questionnaires, analyzed the general information of all qualified questionnaires, and calculated the FI scores. For participants aged 65 and older, FI scores were modeled using latent class trajectory models. Independent influences on the trajectory classes were identified post-modeling. Groups were formed based on the optimal FI trajectory model, and baseline data were analyzed using medians (quartiles) for count data and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Group comparisons were conducted using the ordinary chi-squared test, the chi-squared test with continuity correction, the Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, and the Kruskal–Wallis test for count data. Univariate analyses were employed to assess potential associations between variables and FI trajectories. Multinomial logistic regression was utilized to determine independent influences on FI trajectory categories. Covariates were selected based on clinical significance, univariate analysis results, and prior literature references, with consideration given to variables altering the matched odds ratio by ≥ 10%. Multicollinearity was systematically evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIF) (Supplementary Data Table S2). To assess the robustness of our findings, we performed a sensitivity analysis by adjusting for different covariates in several logistic regression models. Model 1 was adjusted for gender, smoking, drinking, exercise and hours of sleep at night. Model 2 was further adjusted for length of lunch breaks, medical examination habits, and length of service. Model 3 was further adjusted for work stress and nutritional intake. No participants were excluded for covariate-related issues, as the dataset contained complete covariate information, and multicollinearity diagnostics were performed prior to model fitting. All analyses were performed using the R software (version 4.2.1) and free statistics software (version 2.0).

Results

A total of 1056 questionnaires were distributed in this study. After excluding 32 questionnaires of inferior quality, 16 from non-Chengdu areas, and 1 from individuals under 50 years old, 1,007 questionnaires met the criteria (for details, see flowchart Fig. 1), with 51.44% of the participants being female. Based on a previous study [15], participants were categorized into no frailty (healthy) (FI ≤ 0.1), mild frailty (0.1 < FI ≤ 0.2), and moderate frailty (FI > 0.2) according to their current FI values. The respective numbers and percentages were 488 (48.46%), 372 (36.94%), and 147 (14.60%).

The screening questionnaire flowchart. Questionnaire screening process and subsequent data analysis description

Considering the changes in trajectories and the sample size, the study included 536 questionnaires from participants aged 65 and older. Latent class trajectory modeling was conducted for each individual's FI at different ages from 30 to 70 years, following the approach of Lennon et al. [16]. Based on previous studies [17], we fitted seven categorical models, representing one to seven classifications of the FI trajectory, with corresponding Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) values of − 15,988.23, − 16,182.28, − 16,223.45, − 16,233.02, − 16,228.19, − 16,201.05, and − 16,186.91. Model 4, which classified FI trajectory into four groups (each comprising at least 2% of the total sample), was selected as the optimal model based on its lowest BIC value. Model 4 has a relative entropy of 0.65, an average maximum posterior probability of 0.804, and a high confidence individual proportion of 0.771. Detailed information on model evaluation is provided in the supplementary materials (Additional file_Fig. S1-S3). The four categories were named and grouped based on their FI trajectories: Class 1—high initial FI with fast growth (n = 15, 2.80%), Class 2—medium growth (n = 215, 40.11%), Class 3—slow growth (n = 187, 34.89%), and Class 4—fast growth (n = 119, 22.20%), as illustrated in Fig. 2.

Baseline comparisons among the four groups revealed that females exhibited faster FI growth trajectory than males, with a higher proportion of males in the slow and moderate growth groups (P < 0.001). Additionally, those with longer total years of service tended to have a slower FI growth trajectory (P = 0.004). In both the slow and moderate growth groups, there was a lower proportion of individuals aged 50–70 years with nocturnal sleep disturbances (P < 0.001), who also slept longer at night (P < 0.001) and had fewer total illnesses (P < 0.001). The percentage of individuals with systemic disorders and the number of such disorders were lower in the slow and medium growth groups compared to the fast growth groups (Supplementary Data Table S3). Notably, Class 1 (high initial FI, fast growth type) had the highest proportion of women (n = 13, 86.67%), the highest proportion of individuals aged 50–70 years with nocturnal sleep disturbances (n = 5, 33.33%), the lowest proportion of individuals aged 50–70 years with six or more hours of sleep per night (n = 7, 46.67%), and a higher number of systemic diseases (median 6, IQR (5.5, 8.5)). Univariate analyses showed that FI trajectories were associated with female sex, length of service and nighttime sleep time (Supplementary Data Table S4). We employed multinomial logistic regression to model FI trajectories, excluding Class 1 due to its small sample size (n = 15). The slow-growth group (Class 3) served as the reference for comparing FI trajectories and calculating odds ratios (ORs). Results showed that female participants were more likely to have a "fast-growing" FI trajectory (OR = 2.25, 95% CI: 1.02–4.95). Additionally, those with a history of alcohol use before age 70 had a higher risk of a fast-growing FI trajectory (OR = 2.11, 95% CI: 1.06–4.20), while those with seven or more hours of nighttime sleep before age 60 had a lower risk of a fast-growing FI trajectory (OR = 0.55, 95% CI: 0.33–0.93). Disease status was strongly associated with FI scores, as changes in the number of diseases significantly influenced FI trajectories. Logistic models adjusted for different covariates yielded consistent results. Details of each multinomial logistic regression model, including variables and outcomes, are presented in Table 2. In the latent class trajectory models of FI, the sample size of class 1 (n = 15) in the four-class trajectory model (Model 4) is comparatively diminutive. In order to conduct a further sensitivity analysis, the same multinomial logistic regression analysis was performed based on the three-class trajectory model (Model 3), and the results were consistent (Supplementary Data Table S5).

Discussion

Our study found that over half of Chengdu community residents aged 50 and older exhibited some level of frailty, which generally increased with age. Different frailty trajectories indicated varied progression among individuals. Factors such as gender, alcohol consumption before age 70, and nighttime sleep duration before age 60 independently affected frailty trajectories. The FI tended to rise with age and the trajectory of increasing frailty was linked to advancing age [18]. Despite the overall rise in frailty over time, individual variability in frailty trajectories was observed [19]. Despite a relative entropy of 0.65, Model 4 achieves an average maximum posterior probability of 0.804, with 77.1% of individuals classified into high-confidence trajectories, as sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of core findings.

Frailty is dynamic, with both deterioration and improvement commonly observed [20]. The rate of change in the FI is more predictive of short-term mortality in older adults than the current FI level [21]. Single frailty assessments fail to capture dynamic progression, as comparable baseline levels often diverge into accelerated trajectories over time [22]. Thus, examining frailty through trajectories is more meaningful. Miles et al. reviewed frailty trajectory studies, noting most used the FI, while a few used the frailty phenotype [7]. Our FI-based trajectory study aligns with existing research [23], identifying various frailty trajectories such as "no frailty," "increasing frailty," and "high frailty." Our study showed the highest percentage (40.11%) in the moderate increase group, similar to Taniguchi et al. [24]. In contrast, Yunkyung Jung et al. reported 73.6% in the slow increase group [23], likely due to differences in frailty measurement and study participants.

Chronic diseases and frailty exhibited bidirectional interdependency in middle-aged and older adults, with cumulative disease burden critically accelerating frailty trajectory progression [25]. Taniguchi et al. [24] noted that certain chronic diseases, such as hypertension, cerebrovascular disease, and diabetes mellitus, are Linked to adverse frailty phenotypes, highlighting the strong association between a high burden of chronic diseases and increased frailty. Our study corroborated these findings, particularly in Class 1 (high initial FI, high growth) and Class 4 (fast growth) groups. A substantial portion of the FI score is derived from the number of chronic diseases, and a rapid increase in disease count over a short period is closely associated with a swift rise in the FI trajectory. Despite being excluded from the regression analysis due to its small size (n = 15), Class 1 constitutes a distinct subgroup characterized by the following: (1) the highest baseline FI and the steepest progression trajectory and (2) an exceptionally high level of multimorbidity involving cardiovascular, endocrine, neurological, and musculoskeletal disorders, as well as elevated sleep disturbances. These features identify a clinically vulnerable subpopulation that warrants vigilant monitoring.

Frailty exhibits sex-specific disparities, with higher prevalence in women versus accelerated progression in men [26]. Longitudinal analyses show comparable FI progression rates between sexes [7, 10], though women retain elevated baseline FI levels [10], suggesting complex biological-social interactions underlying sex differences. In our study, gender was correlated with FI trajectories, revealing that women were more likely to exhibit fast-growing FI trajectory curves. Sex differences in frailty patterns—showing higher prevalence in women but stronger mortality associations in men—are driven by complex biological and social interactions [27]. Women's accelerated cellular aging processes, including mitochondrial dysfunction and epigenetic dysregulation [28], along with sex-specific immune-endocrine dynamics [29], correlate with greater frailty susceptibility. Socio-behavioral factors such as physiological adaptations to reproductive roles amplify these biological risks [30]. The convergence of molecular senescence pathways and lifelong social exposures creates distinct sex-based frailty trajectories [27].

Alcohol consumption exhibits contradictory associations with frailty progression: cohort studies demonstrate elevated risk with frequent use [31], while moderate intake (≤ 20 g/day) shows protective effects [32]. Meta-analyses further reveal paradoxical dose–response patterns, including potential benefits of higher consumption in some studies [33]. Mendelian randomization analyses, however, suggest no causal relationship [34]. In our study, we identified alcohol consumption as a risk factor for rapid frailty progression, potentially influenced by regional variability and differing drinking habits among populations. The mechanisms by which alcohol affects frailty are also not well understood. Unhealthy lifestyle factors, including alcohol, may increase inflammation and oxidative stress [35]. Alcohol also affects fibrinogen, adiponectin and HDL levels and influences the regulation of oxidative stress [36]. These factors likely contribute to frailty through their impact on inflammation [32].

Chronic sleep deprivation accelerates aging through conserved physiological pathways in animal models [37]. Population studies demonstrate 7–8 h of uninterrupted sleep reduces disability progression [38], while ≤ 5-h sleepers show significantly higher frailty risk over time. Sleep quality deterioration strongly correlates with debilitating outcomes, supported by Mendelian randomization confirming bidirectional insomnia-frailty causality [39]. Our study found that shorter nighttime sleep duration (less than 7 h) was linked to a rapidly increasing FI trajectory. However, another study reported that both excessive (> 8 h) and insufficient (< 6 h) sleep durations were associated with a higher frailty risk [40], which may differ from our findings due to variations in sample collection. Mechanistic evidence indicates sleep modulates endocrine-metabolic axes critical for tissue repair, while sleep fragmentation induces proinflammatory cascades through upregulated adipokine release, compromising neuromuscular integrity [40]. The age-related inflammaging process creates bidirectional deterioration cycles via impaired glymphatic clearance and redox homeostasis disruption [39].

Previous studies have explored various influences on frailty trajectories, such as educational attainment [23, 41], smoking [23, 25], exercise [1, 23, 42], and diet and nutrition [7], but findings have been inconsistent. Our study found no association between frailty trajectory and these factors. Further research is needed to clarify their role in frailty progression.

Strengths of this study include the use of face-to-face questionnaires to gather detailed past debilitating information, a broad age range of participants allowing for comprehensive FI calculations, and being the first to examine frailty trajectories among middle-aged and elderly individuals in the Chengdu community of western China. By employing a latent class trajectory model, we captured the heterogeneity in frailty progression, providing targeted information and optimizing future frailty prevention strategies. This study has several limitations. First, it did not include data on frailty in the very elderly, which may have underestimated the proportion of respondents in the increasing and very frail groups. The population studied was primarily community-dwelling and in good health, excluding those with cognitive impairment, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to a broader population. Second, mortality outcomes were not addressed, necessitating follow-up to refine FI data at later ages and document final outcomes. Third, although anchored recall periods and established frailty domains were implemented to minimize retrospective bias, such bias cannot be entirely excluded due to reliance on self-reported questionnaires. Future studies will continue to follow these populations to collect more prospective frailty data.

Conclusions

This study retrospectively analyzed frailty in the middle-aged and elderly population of the Chengdu community, finding that nearly 52% of individuals aged 50 and older exhibited varying degrees of frailty, with severity increasing with age. Frailty trajectories among those aged 65 and older were categorized into four groups: high initial FI with rapid growth, medium growth, slow growth, and rapid growth. Significant differences were observed across these groups in terms of gender, work experience, nighttime sleep quality, number of diseases, and smoking status, with the high initial FI rapid growth group showing the most pronounced differences. Rapidly increasing FI trajectories were associated with female sex, alcohol consumption, and reduced nighttime sleep. Chronic diseases also played a role in influencing FI trajectories. For the older population, maintaining non-drinking habits and ensuring adequate sleep are associated with a slower progression of frailty. Future prospective studies with larger samples are needed to further explore additional factors influencing frailty trajectories.
