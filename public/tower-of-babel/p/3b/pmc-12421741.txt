Red blood cell transfusion frequency and the risk and severity of bronchopulmonary dysplasia in preterm infants: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Zhenhai Zhang, Shengkai Lu, Xiaoyan Fang, Hao Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRed blood cell (RBC) transfusion is a common intervention for anemia in preterm infants; however, its association with bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) remains debated. While biological mechanisms suggest potential harm, the clinical impact of transfusion frequency on BPD incidence and severity remains unclear.ObjectiveTo investigate whether RBC transfusion frequency is independently associated with the risk and severity of BPD in preterm infants born before 32 weeks of gestation.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included preterm infants (< 32 weeks gestational age) admitted to the NICU at Zhangzhou Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University between January 2020 and December 2022. Only transfusions administered before 36 weeks postmenstrual age were included. Clinical data, including transfusion frequency, hemoglobin levels, and respiratory diagnoses, were collected. Logistic regression was used to identify independent risk factors for BPD, and ROC curve analysis determined the optimal transfusion threshold. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate confounding effects.ResultsAmong 228 included infants, 184 (80.7%) received at least one RBC transfusion. The BPD group received a higher number of transfusions (mean 4.24 vs. 1.89, p < 0.001). Multivariate analysis identified transfusion frequency (OR = 1.245; 95% CI: 1.023–1.514; p = 0.049) and lower gestational age (OR = 0.749; 95% CI: 0.561–0.998; p = 0.05) as independent risk factors for BPD. ROC analysis demonstrated transfusion frequency effectively predicted BPD (AUC = 0.749), with ≥ 4 transfusions (sensitivity 51%, specificity 77%) correlating with increased severity (Kendall’s τ_b = 0.453, p < 0.001). Neonatal hemoglobin levels inversely correlated with transfusion requirements (r=-0.187, p < 0.001). Subgroup analysis confirmed this association across GA strata (p < 0.05). Early transfusion (≤ 14 days of life) was not associated with increased severity.ConclusionRBC transfusion frequency is an independent, dose-dependent risk factor for BPD in preterm infants. Reducing unnecessary transfusions and optimizing anemia management may help mitigate BPD risk and severity.Keywords: Red blood cell transfusion, Bronchopulmonary dysplasia, Preterm infants, Transfusion-related lung injury, Neonatal anemia

Introduction

Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) remains a major cause of neonatal morbidity and long-term respiratory complications, particularly in preterm infants born before 32 weeks of gestation. The pathogenesis of BPD is complex, involving alveolar simplification, impaired vascular development, and chronic inflammation, which collectively contribute to persistent pulmonary dysfunction and an increased healthcare burden in survivors [1, 2]. Several well-established risk factors for BPD include mechanical ventilation, oxygen toxicity, inflammation, infection, and patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) [3, 4]. However, in recent years, red blood cell (RBC) transfusion has emerged as a potential contributor to BPD pathogenesis, although findings remain inconsistent [5–7].

Anemia of prematurity (AOP) is a common complication in preterm infants, resulting from immature erythropoiesis, frequent phlebotomy losses, and limited iron stores [8]. RBC transfusion is frequently used in neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) to improve oxygen delivery and prevent complications such as apnea, tachycardia, and metabolic acidosis [9]. However, while transfusions temporarily increase oxygen-carrying capacity, accumulating evidence suggests that they may also have adverse pulmonary consequences, particularly in infants at risk of BPD [10].

Multiple mechanisms have been proposed to explain the potential association between RBC transfusion and BPD development. One of the leading hypotheses suggests that transfusion exacerbates oxidative stress, as stored RBCs release free iron, catalyzing the Fenton reaction and generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) that damage the alveolar epithelium [11]. Additionally, transfusions may introduce inflammatory cytokines and bioactive lipids, which trigger systemic and pulmonary inflammation, further impairing lung development [12]. Another concern is the shift from fetal hemoglobin (HbF) to adult hemoglobin (HbA) following transfusion, which may reduce oxygen unloading at the tissue level, paradoxically leading to relative tissue hypoxia despite increased arterial oxygen content [13].

Despite these biological mechanisms, the clinical impact of RBC transfusion on BPD risk remains debated. Some studies have reported a dose-dependent relationship, where increased transfusion frequency is associated with higher BPD incidence and severity [6]. In contrast, other studies have failed to establish a direct causal link or suggested that transfusion only reflects greater illness severity rather than being an independent risk factor [14, 15]. Furthermore, it remains unclear whether early transfusion (≤ 14 days of life) plays a distinct role compared to cumulative transfusion burden over hospitalization.

Given these uncertainties, further research is needed to clarify the relationship between RBC transfusion frequency and BPD development. This study aims to determine whether RBC transfusion frequency is independently associated with BPD incidence and severity in preterm infants born before 32 weeks, identify potential transfusion thresholds that may predict increased BPD risk and evaluate whether early transfusion (≤ 14 days of life) has a distinct impact on BPD severity. By addressing these questions, we aim to provide evidence-based insights that can guide neonatal transfusion strategies and potentially reduce BPD risk in preterm infants.

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) of Zhangzhou Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University. The study included preterm infants born at less than 32 weeks of gestational age who were admitted to the NICU between January 2020 and December 2022. Infants were excluded if they presented with major congenital anomalies, inherited metabolic disorders, non-pulmonary death within the first 14 days of life, or if parental consent for continued care was withdrawn prior to 36 weeks postmenstrual age. These exclusion criteria ensured that only infants at genuine risk of developing bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) were included in the analysis.

Diagnostic definitions

Bronchopulmonary dysplasia was diagnosed in accordance with the 2018 National Institute of Child Health and Human Development (NICHD) criteria, which define BPD based on the need for supplemental oxygen or respiratory support at 36 weeks postmenstrual age. Other key clinical conditions were defined using standard diagnostic criteria. Patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) was diagnosed if echocardiography showed signs of.

ductus arteriosus shunt. Neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) was diagnosed based on clinical symptoms and chest radiographs displaying reticulogranular patterns and air bronchograms. Pulmonary hemorrhage was defined by the presence of blood in tracheal secretions alongside acute respiratory deterioration. Neonatal sepsis included both culture-positive cases and clinical diagnoses supported by elevated biomarkers such as C-reactive protein or procalcitonin. Necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) was diagnosed at Bell stage II or higher.

RBC transfusion criteria and institutional practice

Our institution employs the following erythrocyte transfusion thresholds: (1) For preterm infants requiring respiratory support: During the first postnatal week: hemoglobin (Hb) ≤ 115 g/L or hematocrit (Hct) ≤ 0.35;During the second postnatal week: Hb ≤ 110 g/L or Hct ≤ 0.30;From the third week onward: Hb ≤ 85 g/L or Hct ≤ 0.25;(2) For preterm infants without respiratory support: During the first postnatal week: Hb ≤ 100 g/L or Hct ≤ 0.30;During the second postnatal week: Hb ≤ 85 g/L or Hct ≤ 0.25;From the third week onward: Hb ≤ 75 g/L or Hct ≤ 0.23 Emergency transfusions were also permitted in cases of acute blood loss or hemodynamic instability [16]. Each transfusion consisted of 15 mL/kg of leukoreduced packed red blood cells, delivered over a 3–4-hour period. For the purposes of this study, only transfusions administered before 36 weeks postmenstrual age were included in the analysis, to ensure alignment with the temporal definition of BPD.

Data collection

Data were extracted from electronic medical records using a standardized abstraction protocol. Maternal information included hypertensive disorders and gestational diabetes, while neonatal outcomes. Neonatal variables encompassed gestational age, birth weight, sex, and Apgar scores. Detailed respiratory diagnoses (e.g., RDS, PDA, pulmonary conditions, including neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (NRDS), patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), and pulmonary hemorrhage) and intervention data—including duration of mechanical ventilation and oxygen therapy—were also recorded. RBC transfusion data were comprehensively collected, including frequency, volume per episode, and timing. Regarding Hb evaluation, this study collected baseline neonatal Hb and Hct levels within 24 h after birth as key indicators of initial anemia status. These parameters were specifically analyzed for their associations with both transfusion frequency and BPD development.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Before conducting statistical tests, data were assessed for normality using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, and appropriate parametric or non-parametric methods were applied accordingly.

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data and as median with interquartile range (IQR) for non-normally distributed data. Comparisons between the BPD and non-BPD groups were conducted using independent samples t-tests for normally distributed variables and Mann-Whitney U tests for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages and analyzed using the chi-square (χ²) test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

To identify independent risk factors for BPD, a multivariate logistic regression model was constructed, including variables that were statistically significant (p < 0.05) in univariate analysis. The model was adjusted for gestational age, birth weight, duration of invasive mechanical ventilation, NRDS, PDA, pulmonary hemorrhage, and the number of RBC transfusions. Since longer hospitalization increases the likelihood of receiving transfusions, length of hospital stay was included as a covariate to control for potential confounding. The results of logistic regression were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

To evaluate the predictive value of RBC transfusion frequency for BPD, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was generated, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated. The optimal transfusion threshold was determined using the Youden index, which maximizes sensitivity and specificity. The analysis identified 3.5 transfusions as the threshold with the highest predictive performance (AUC = 0.749, sensitivity = 51%, specificity = 77%). This cutoff was selected based on its alignment with prior studies suggesting that receiving ≥ 4 transfusions is associated with increased oxidative stress and inflammatory burden, which are implicated in BPD pathogenesis [17]. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess whether alternative transfusion cutoffs (e.g., ≥ 2 or ≥ 4 transfusions) altered predictive accuracy.

To further explore the association between transfusion burden and BPD severity, Kendall’s tau-b correlation analysis was performed. This test was selected due to the ordinal nature of BPD severity classification (mild, moderate, and severe). Additionally, subgroup analyses were performed to assess the association between early red blood cell transfusions (≤ 14 days of life) and BPD, guided by the characteristics and management of preterm anemia, as well as evidence from published literature [6, 18]. The relationship between early transfusion and BPD severity was assessed using the chi-square test, while Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used to evaluate transfusion frequency in relation to BPD severity.

Recognizing that infection and inflammation are key contributors to BPD, additional sensitivity analyses were conducted to adjust for neonatal sepsis, necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC). A secondary logistic regression model was constructed, incorporating these variables to assess whether the association between RBC transfusion and BPD risk persisted after controlling for these potential confounders.

Results

General characteristics

A total of 286 preterm infants born at less than 32 weeks of gestation were identified in the medical records. After applying exclusion criteria—including major congenital anomalies, metabolic disorders, parental withdrawal of care before 36 weeks corrected age, or non-pulmonary death within two weeks after birth—228 infants were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Of these, 132 (57.9%) were male and 96 (42.1%) were female. The gestational age of the cohort ranged from 25 + 2 to 31 + 6 weeks, with a mean gestational age of 29 + 5 weeks. Birth weight ranged from 500 g to 1700 g, with a mean birth weight of 1266 g.

Study flow diagram. Flowchart depicting the enrollment of preterm infants born at <32 weeks of gestation between January 2020 and December 2022. After applying exclusion criteria (major congenital anomalies, metabolic disorders, parental withdrawal of care, or early non-pulmonary death), 228 infants were included in the final analysis and stratified into BPD and non-BPD groups

Out of the 228 infants, 184 preterm infants (80.7%) received at least one RBC transfusion during hospitalization. A total of 680 transfusion episodes were recorded. Among them, 106 infants were diagnosed with BPD (n = 106), 449 transfusions were administered, averaging 4.24 transfusions per infant (range: 2–16 transfusions). In contrast, 122 infants were classified in the non-BPD group (n = 122) received 231 transfusions, with a mean of 1.89 transfusions per infant (range: 0–8 transfusions).

Infants with moderate-to-severe BPD (n = 49) accounted for 283 transfusions, corresponding to a mean of 5.8 transfusions per infant. Among extremely preterm infants (< 28 weeks, n = 33), the average transfusion count was 5.58 transfusions per infant (total = 184), compared with 2.58 transfusions per infant (total = 496) in those born at 28–32 weeks (n = 195).

Univariate analysis of risk factors clinical characteristics in the BPD and non-BPD groups

Univariate analysis was conducted to compare perinatal and clinical characteristics between the BPD and non-BPD groups. Infants in the BPD group had significantly lower gestational age (median 29.0 vs. 30.3 weeks, p < 0.001) and birth weight (median 1200 g vs. 1400 g, p < 0.001). They also required longer durations of invasive mechanical ventilation (median 1 vs. 0 days, p < 0.001), had higher rates of PDA (32.1% vs. 20.5%, p = 0.046), NRDS (92.5% vs. 73.8%, p < 0.001), and pulmonary hemorrhage (5.8% vs. 0%, p = 0.008). The number of RBC transfusions (median 4 vs. 2, p < 0.001) and length of hospital stay (median 53 vs. 39 days, p < 0.001) were also significantly greater in the BPD group. However, early transfusion (≤ 14 days) was not significantly different between groups (42.5% vs. 38.5%, p = 0.586). (Table 1)

Multivariate logistic regression analysis

To identify independent predictors of BPD, variables with significant univariate associations and known biological relevance were entered into a multivariate logistic regression model. The final model included gestational age, transfusion frequency, length of hospital stay, PDA, NRDS, pulmonary hemorrhage, birth weight, duration of invasive ventilation.

After adjusting for potential confounders, RBC transfusion frequency remained an independent risk factor for BPD (OR = 1.245; 95% CI: 1.023–1.514; p = 0.049). Gestational age also remained protective (OR = 0.749; 95% CI: 0.561–0.998; p = 0.05). Notably, length of hospital stays (OR = 1.036; 95% CI: 1.001–1.074; p = 0.049) was also independently associated with BPD risk (Table 2). Other variables—including PDA, NRDS, pulmonary hemorrhage, Birth weight and Duration of invasive ventilation—did not reach statistical significance in the adjusted model. Multicollinearity testing showed acceptable VIF values (< 2.5 for all variables), indicating no collinearity concerns(Table 3).

ROC curve analysis and clinical threshold

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to evaluate the predictive value of RBC transfusion frequency for BPD. The area under the curve (AUC) was 0.749 (95% CI: 0.687–0.810), indicating good discriminatory power (Fig. 2). The optimal transfusion threshold identified via the Youden index was 3.5 transfusions. However, to enhance clinical utility, a categorical analysis was performed using ≥ 4 transfusions as the cutoff. Infants receiving ≥ 4 transfusions had significantly higher BPD risk, with a positive predictive value of 65.9% and a negative predictive value of 64.4% (Table 4).

Hemoglobin trends and transfusion volume

An analysis of neonatal hemoglobin (Hb) levels at birth demonstrated that the non-BPD group had numerically higher values (mean 16.7 ± 2.1 g/dL) compared to the BPD group (mean 16.2 ± 2.2 g/dL), though this difference did not attain statistical significance (p = 0.051). Initial hematocrit (Hct) levels showed no statistically significant intergroup disparity (p = 0.137). Notably, correlation analyses identified significant negative associations between neonatal hemoglobin levels at birth (r = -0.187, p < 0.001) and birth Hct values (r = -0.179, p < 0.001) with the number of subsequent blood transfusions, suggesting that lower neonatal hematological parameters may predispose infants to require more frequent transfusion support.

Association between transfusion frequency and BPD severity

To assess the relationship between RBC transfusion burden and BPD severity, infants were stratified into four groups: no BPD, mild, moderate, and severe BPD. Receiving ≥ 4 transfusions was significantly associated with increased BPD severity (χ² = 19.30, p < 0.001). In contrast, early transfusion (≤ 14 days) did not show a significant association with BPD severity (p = 0.778). Kendall’s tau-b correlation analysis confirmed a positive correlation between transfusion frequency and BPD severity (τ_b = 0.453, p < 0.001), supporting a dose-response relationship.(Table 5).

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Subgroup analyses stratified by gestational age (< 28 weeks vs. ≥28 weeks) using Kendall’s tau-b correlation demonstrated a significant association between transfusion frequency and BPD severity in both groups. While the correlation coefficient was numerically lower in extremely preterm infants (< 28 weeks: τ = 0.347, p = 0.027) compared to the ≥ 28 weeks cohort (τ = 0.366, p < 0.001), the persistence of statistical significance across subgroups suggests that transfusion burden contributes to BPD progression regardless of prematurity severity. Notably, infants receiving ≥ 4 transfusions exhibited significantly higher BPD severity (p < 0.001), whereas early transfusion timing (≤ 14 days) showed no association (p = 0.778). Sensitivity analyses adjusting for neonatal sepsis and NEC confirmed the robustness of this relationship, with transfusion frequency remaining independently associated with BPD (adjusted p < 0.01). These findings collectively indicate that cumulative transfusion exposure, rather than early initiation, is a modifiable risk factor for BPD severity.

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study demonstrated that red blood cell (RBC) transfusion frequency is independently and dose-dependently associated with the development and severity of bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) in preterm infants born before 32 weeks of gestation. After adjusting for relevant confounders—including gestational age, neonatal sepsis, respiratory complications, and length of hospital stay—transfusion frequency remained a significant predictor of BPD. This association was particularly pronounced in infants receiving four or more transfusions; however, the strength of this relationship appeared attenuated in extremely preterm infants (< 28 weeks), potentially suggesting a multifactorial contribution to BPD development in this population.

Transfusion burden and BPD risk

The findings of this study align with emerging literature suggesting a dose-dependent relationship between RBC transfusion and BPD pathogenesis. Previous studies have reported similar trends, wherein each additional transfusion increased the odds of moderate-to-severe BPD by 15–20% [19, 20]. The ROC curve analysis in our study identified ≥ 4 transfusions as a clinically meaningful threshold, providing a practical reference point for transfusion decision-making in NICUs.

Notably, although early transfusion (≤ 14 days of life) has been hypothesized to increase oxidative vulnerability during critical periods of lung development [6], our analysis did not find a statistically significant association between early transfusion and BPD severity. This discrepancy with some prior reports may reflect differences in transfusion thresholds, supportive care protocols, or population characteristics across NICUs. It is also possible that the cumulative transfusion burden, rather than the timing of the first transfusion, exerts a more dominant influence on BPD progression.

Biological mechanisms linking RBC transfusion and BPD

Multiple mechanisms have been proposed to explain how RBC transfusions might contribute to BPD development. One key hypothesis involves oxidative stress: stored RBCs contain elevated levels of free iron, which can catalyze the Fenton reaction and generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) that damage the immature alveolar epithelium [21]. This oxidative injury may disrupt surfactant function and impair alveolarization, hallmarks of BPD pathology [22, 23].

Additionally, transfusion-related inflammation may play a crucial role. Stored blood products contain pro-inflammatory mediators, such as cytokines, bioactive lipids, and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which can trigger systemic and pulmonary inflammation upon transfusion [24, 25]. This inflammatory cascade may exacerbate alveolar injury, impair angiogenesis, and contribute to lung fibrosis, hallmarks of moderate to severe BPD [26].

Another possible mechanism is the oxygen delivery paradox associated with transfusion. RBC transfusions introduce adult hemoglobin (HbA) into the circulation, which has a higher oxygen affinity than fetal hemoglobin (HbF) [27, 28]. This alteration can reduce oxygen unloading at the tissue level, paradoxically leading to relative tissue hypoxia despite increased arterial oxygen saturation. Tissue hypoxia is a well-known stimulus for aberrant vascular remodeling and may further contribute to BPD severity through dysregulated vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) signaling [29].

Consideration of confounding and multicollinearity

One of the key strengths of this study is the comprehensive adjustment for clinical confounders, including infection-related variables such as sepsis, NEC. Although transfusion frequency may partially reflect underlying illness severity, our multivariate analysis and sensitivity analyses consistently demonstrated its independent effect on BPD risk. Furthermore, multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIF), confirming that transfusion frequency did not simply act as a surrogate for prolonged hospitalization or mechanical ventilation.

Nevertheless, we acknowledge the complexity of time-dependent confounding in retrospective designs. While we attempted to control for this by including hospital stay duration and conducting subgroup analyses, future studies using time-to-event analyses or propensity score methods may more rigorously disentangle causal pathways.

Comparison with previous studies

Our findings reinforce the dose-dependent relationship between RBC transfusion burden and BPD risk observed in prior studies [30–32], While this aligns with mechanistic hypotheses (e.g., cumulative oxidative stress or volume overload), the causality remains debated, as transfusion frequency may inherently reflect illness severity [33–35]. Importantly, our models adjusting for hospitalization duration, invasive ventilator duration, pulmonary hemorrhage, NRDS, PDA and sepsis preserved the independence of this association, suggesting a contribution beyond confounding. Notably, the lack of association with early transfusions (≤ 14 days) contrasts with previous reports implicating developmental window susceptibility, highlighting potential heterogeneity in transfusion-related pathophysiology.

Clinical implications

These findings have direct implications for neonatal transfusion practices. Although RBC transfusions remain an essential therapy for managing anemia of prematurity, they should be administered with caution, particularly in infants at high risk for BPD. The identification of a clinically relevant threshold (≥ 4 transfusions) may support the development of more restrictive transfusion protocols.

This study highlights that RBC transfusion frequency is an independent risk factor for bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) in preterm infants born at < 32 weeks, with a dose-response relationship between transfusion burden and BPD severity (adjusted OR = 1.245; τ_b = 0.453, p < 0.001). Multivariate analysis identified gestational age (protective factor, OR = 0.749) and prolonged hospitalization (OR = 1.036) as additional contributors to BPD risk. Notably, the observed association with extended hospital stay may potentially reflect secondary consequences of BPD development, and the causal relationship between these variables cannot be definitively determined within the current study design. ROC analysis revealed that ≥ 4 transfusions (sensitivity 51%, specificity 77%) may serve as a clinically actionable threshold for identifying high-risk infants. These findings advocate for a restrictive transfusion strategy in neonatal care, particularly for infants requiring multiple transfusion. Clinicians should prioritize individualized protocols that integrate gestational age, birth weight (median 1200 g vs. 1400 g in BPD vs. non-BPD groups), and dynamic monitoring of hemoglobin trends (lower neonatal Hb/Hct correlated with increased transfusion needs, r=-0.187~-0.179).

Although the individual infusion volume was fixed at 15 mL/kg, increased total transfusion volume might lead to cumulative volume load. Animal studies have demonstrated that fluid overload can induce pulmonary edema through elevated pulmonary capillary hydrostatic pressure [36], and pulmonary edema is a recognized risk factor forBPD. However, as this study did not directly measure clinical markers of volume overload (such as fluid balance or echocardiographic assessment of cardiac function), it remains challenging to distinguish between volume-related effects and the intrinsic effects of transfusion itself.These limitations highlight the need for multimodal monitoring in future studies to better elucidate the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying transfusion-related complications in neonatal populations.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single tertiary NICU, which may limit generalizability to other centers with different transfusion practices or patient populations. Second, the retrospective design is inherently susceptible to residual confounding and selection bias, despite our efforts to adjust for known risk factors. Thirdly, although hemoglobin levels and hematocrit were assessed, we lacked data on blood storage duration, donor characteristics, and recipient volume changes - factors that may potentially influence transfusion-related outcomes.Fourthly, although we incorporated sepsis and NEC as infection-related variables for confounding adjustment, maternal chorioamnionitis was not included in the analysis due to data limitations. This methodological consideration may have resulted in the omission of potential intrauterine infection effects on BPD pathogenesis.Lastly, the study focused on BPD development prior to NICU discharge and did not assess long-term respiratory or neurodevelopmental outcomes.

Future research should focus on prospective, multicenter trials to confirm the dose-response relationship between transfusion burden and BPD and explore long-term sequelae. Incorporating biomarkers of oxidative stress and inflammation may further elucidate pathophysiological pathways and help identify high-risk infants who would benefit from targeted interventions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, RBC transfusion frequency is an independent and dose-dependent risk factor for BPD in preterm infants born before 32 weeks of gestation. Infants receiving four or more transfusions exhibited significantly higher BPD risk and severity, suggesting the need for judicious transfusion practices. Optimizing anemia management, minimizing transfusion exposure, and tailoring respiratory support strategies may help mitigate BPD incidence and improve outcomes in this vulnerable population.
