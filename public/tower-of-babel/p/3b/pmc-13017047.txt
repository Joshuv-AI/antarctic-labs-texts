Tattoos and risk of cutaneous melanoma and nonmelanoma skin cancer in France
Authors: Tingting Mo, Marie Zins, Marcel Goldberg, Céline Ribet, Sofiane Kab, Ines Schreiver, Katherina Siewert, Khaled Ezzedine, Joachim Schüz, Milena Foerster
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith the increasing popularity of decorative tattooing, which entails the intradermal injection of inks that may contain carcinogens, investigating the related potential skin cancer risk is a public health priority.MethodsWe used data from the Cancer Risk Attributable with the Body Art of Tattooing study, nested in the French national cohort Consultants des Centres d’Examens de Santé (adults aged 18-69 years recruited in 2012-2018). Tattoo exposure was collected in 2020-2023. Skin cancers overall, cutaneous melanoma, and nonmelanoma skin cancer diagnosed during 2007-2021 were retrieved from national health insurance data. As exposure was collected after possible disease ascertainment, risks of skin cancer with prior tattoo exposure were assessed using logistic regression and Cox proportional hazards models in a retrospective cohort design.ResultsAmong 111 074 participants, 1789 (1.6%) skin cancers were recorded (693 cutaneous melanoma, 1096 nonmelanoma skin cancer). No association was found between binary tattoo exposure (yes, no) and any skin cancer type. In the highest exposure category of tattoo body surface (>2 hand palms), 2 participants with skin cancer were observed among 1633 (0.1%) participants, yielding an odds ratio of 0.21 for overall skin cancer (95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.05 to 0.83; reference no tattoos); however, the corresponding Cox model was not statistically significant (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.26, 95% CI = 0.07 to 1.05).ConclusionsNo overall association between tattoo exposure and skin cancer was observed. The inverse association in the highest exposure category is based on very few skin cancer diagnoses and should be interpreted with caution. Further studies with larger number of participants with skin cancer more detailed exposure assessment are warranted.

Introduction

During the past decades, tattooing has become increasingly popular, particularly in young adults, all over the globe. In industrialized countries, up to 40% of individuals aged 30-40 years report having at least 1 tattoo.1

Potential cancer risks from tattoos arise because of the presence of known and/or suspected carcinogens in commercially available tattoo inks.2,3 Among these substances, stemming mostly from contaminated raw materials, are polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons bound to carbon black pigments, phototoxic primary aromatic amines in bright-colored inks, and metals, found in inks of all colors.4,5 During tattooing the inks are injected into the dermis, internalized by phagocytes and partly transported to the draining lymph nodes.6-8 Although the cancer risk implications of this exposure route are poorly understood, there is no doubt that it entails high local pigment exposure to the skin and lymph nodes, and consequently oncogenesis is possible in both organs.7, 9-11

To date, 4 epidemiological studies on tattoo-associated skin tumor risk were published of which 1 included too few participants to draw conclusions.12 Using data from the early 2000s, a case-control study restricted to tattooed individuals found an increased risk of basal cell carcinoma at the precise body part that was tattooed, especially for green and yellow pigmented tattoos.13 Two more recent case-control studies on nonmelanoma skin cancer and cutaneous melanoma were published this year, using Swedish and US registry data, respectively.14,15 Tattoo exposure did not alter the risk for nonmelanoma skin cancer in the Swedish study, whereas in the US study from Utah, a decreased risk for cutaneous melanoma in the highest exposure category contrasted by an increased risk for cutaneous melanoma in situ in men with small tattoos was seen.

The Cancer Risk Associated with the Body Art of Tattooing (CRABAT) study, initiated by the International Agency for Research of Cancer, is so far the largest cohort of tattooed individuals with the future potential to investigate cancer risk associated with tattooing, including the risk of cutaneous melanoma—the deadliest skin tumor—and nonmelanoma skin cancer. Although the follow-up time of CRABAT is still too short to provide prospective results, the first results relying on retrospectively collected data on tattoo exposure and skin cancer diagnosed from 2007 to 2020-2021 are presented here.

Methods

Study design and population

CRABAT is an ongoing cohort study investigating the potential role of tattoos in the development and progression of cancer.15 The study is nested within the French national cohort Consultants des Centres d’Examens de Santé (Constances). Baseline recruitment of Constances took place from 2012 to 2019, during which 220 000 voluntary participants aged 18-69 years at inclusion were sampled to represent the country’s age, sex, and socioeconomic structure.16 Constances provides a unique research infrastructure for epidemiological research with a wealth of self-reported data on sociodemographic, lifestyle, and medical factors collected during baseline and annual follow-up questionnaires. Objective data on health outcomes are ensured through record linkage of cohort members to national health databases (Système National des Données de Santé [SNDS]). These databases log information on all medical services delivered by hospitals, private practices, and pharmacies that are reimbursed by the national public health insurance, with almost full population coverage.

CRABAT collected tattoo exposure within Constances first in the annual follow-up questionnaire 2020 via the screening question, “Are you tattooed?” then the follow-up question about tattooed surface larger than 1 hand palm or not. In the second phase, the validated Epidemiological Tattoo Assessment Tool (EpiTAT questionnaire) was sent to the tattooed subpopulation from July to December 2023 for more detailed exposure information including precise tattoo body surface, color, time period, and tattoo sun exposure, among others.17 In February 2024, the collected exposure data were merged with prior sociodemographic, lifestyle, and medical data for tattooed and nontattooed participants who responded to the 2020 follow-up.

Based on CRABAT, this study included participants who provided tattoo information on 2020. We further excluded participants with missing data on exposure or outcome, permanent makeup or facial tattoos, tattoo removal, and participants of very dark skin (phototype VI) or with albinism as they have very low or very high skin cancer risk compared with other skin types, respectively18 (see Figure 1).

Exposure data

Binary tattoo status (yes, no) was retrieved from the Constances follow-up questionnaire 2020-2021. Tattooed body surface measured in hand palms was derived as a composite variable from the same questionnaire and the detailed exposure assessment in 2023. Tattooed body surface was categorized as nontattooed, 0-1 (participants reporting tattooed body surface ≤1 in 2020-2021 and tattooed body surface ≤1 or nonresponse in 2023), at least 1-2 (tattooed body surface reported in 2023), and at least 2 (tattooed body surface reported in 2023). Participants who reported a tattooed area of at least 1 and did not respond in 2023 were excluded from dose-dependent models because of potential exposure misclassification. For participants responding tattooed body surface less than 1 in 2020-2021 but not responding in 2023, tattooed body surface was set to 0.5 hand palms (to be included in dose-dependent models on “incremental tattoo surface”). To account for potential interaction of sunlight, tattoo sun exposure was categorized as nontattooed, tattooed not sun exposed, or tattooed sun exposed.

First tattoo date was assessed through multiple choice categories and was set as the midpoint of the indicated period (last year, >1-5 years ago, >5-10 years ago, >10-15 years ago, >15 years ago), to 20 years ago if “>15 years ago” was answered and earliest at age 16 years. To minimize selection bias, we imputed the first tattoo date in the 40% of tattooed participants who did not answer the EpiTAT question in 2023, allowing to include this participant group in time-to-event models (multiple imputation described in Supplementary Methods S1 and Tables S1-S2).

Outcome data

Data on cases of cutaneous melanoma (International Classification of Diseases version10 code C43) and nonmelanoma skin cancer (C44) treated in public and/or private hospitals, and their respective hospital entry date, were retrieved from the SNDS database for the period January 1, 2007, to 2020-2021 (date of first exposure assessment). Data on tumor location, subtypes, or grade were not available. Those participants whose date of first tattoo could be determined and who developed a cutaneous melanoma or nonmelanoma skin cancer before that date were treated as nontattooed (n = 2).

Additional self-reported covariate data retrieved from Constances

Data on sex, age, highest education (no diploma, high school degree, bachelor’s degree, master’s degree and higher, according to French education system), household disposable income (≤€2100, €2100 to <€2800, €2800 to <€4200, ≥€4200), smoking status (never smoked, former smoker, current smoker), and body mass index (BMI) were retrieved from the Constances baseline questionnaires. Because barriers to health care might lead to differential underdiagnosis of skin tumors if more or less common in the exposed compared with the nonexposed population, a composite variable (no barriers, barriers reported at least once) was created from recurrently asked questions on financial, spatial, and organizational barriers to health care. Dermal skin cancer risk factors including frequency of sunburns during adolescence (never, 1-5 times, 6-10 times, every summer, I don’t know), maximum intensity of tan (absent, light, bright, dark, very dark), and skin type assessed according to the Fitzpatrick scale (very fair [white]; very fair and may be tanned; moderately fair; matt [moderate brown]; dark brown) were collected in a dedicated skin questionnaire during the 2018 Constances follow-up.19

Statistical analysis

As exposure data were obtained after potential disease ascertainment, we used 2 approaches.

Logistic regression to assess risks of historical skin cancer in relation to prior tattoo exposure (71 observations excluded because of first tattoo in the year of or after diagnosis). Exposure variables were tattooed (yes, no), tattooed body surface (not tattooed, 0-1, >1-2, and >2 hand palms), and within the tattooed population: incremental tattoo surface and tattoo sun exposure (not sun exposed and sun exposed). Two levels of adjustment were performed adjusting variables at Constances’ baseline: model 1 adjusted for sex, age, education level, smoking status, and BMI; model 2 additionally adjusted for household disposable income, skin type, maximum intensity of tan, frequency of sunburn during adolescence, and barriers to health care (covariate selection described in Supplementary Methods S2, Table S3, and Figure S1). Missing values in covariates were coded as a separate category. Moreover, we fitted model 2 with and without tattooed body surface exposure to explore independency of the tattoo exposure from other covariates.

Exposure variables and covariates remained unchanged from logistic regression (except for age, because models were fitted on the age-time scale). Two alternative entry dates were considered: January 1, 2007 (ie, date from which SNDS data was available) or the individual Constance baseline date. To account for potential birth cohort effect linked to the marked changes in sunscreen use during childhood and adult tattoo prevalence that occurred from people born before 1980 to thereafter (as those changes occurred gradually, the cutoff year can only be an approximation), analyses were stratified by age in 2020 (ages younger than 40 years, 40 years and older). Considering being tattooed as a time-varying exposure, the dataset was split (STATA: stsplit) on the date of first tattoo, thus tattooed participants will contribute person-years to the exposed group only from that date onward, being before counted as nonexposed.

Results

Of 111 074 eligible participants, 12 669 (11.5%) reported being tattooed in the 2020 questionnaire (Table 1, Figure 1). Full exposure information was available for 60% (7553 of 12 669) of participants, and tattooed body surface could be determined for more than 80% of participants, approximately half of whom reported a tattooed body surface of 0-1 hand palm. Women were tattooed more often compared with men though men more often had larger tattoos (>2 hand palms). Tattooed participants were younger, particularly among those with a large tattooed body surface. Tattooed participants had a lower education level and household disposable incomes and more often reported barriers to seeking health care. Also, tattooed participants were more likely to have darker skin and more often had sunburns before adulthood, particularly among those with a large tattooed body surface (Table 2). Besides the skin characteristics selected for analyses, tattooed participants tended to have more moles, freckles, and sunburns during adulthood (Table S4).

Exploring selection bias, sociodemographic and skin characteristics were compared between tattooed participants who answered to the EpiTAT questionnaire in 2023 and those who did not. Compared with EpiTAT nonresponders, responders were more likely to be female, have a lower BMI, not currently smoking, and have higher education and household disposable income (Table S5). Additionally, EpiTAT responders were more likely to have more moles, lighter skin, and more sunburns (Table S6).

Overall, 1789 skin cancer diagnoses (693 cutaneous melanoma, 1096 nonmelanoma skin cancer) were recorded. Fifteen-year incidence percentages were 2-3 times higher in nontattooed compared with tattooed participants (Table 3). In fully adjusted logistic regression models, compared with participants without a tattoo, being tattooed was not associated with overall skin cancer risk. In the highest tattooed body surface category (>2 hand palms) a decreased risk (odds ratio [OR] = 0.21, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.05 to 0.83) was observed based on 2 skin cancer cases among 1633 tattooed participants in this exposure category. A similar statistically nonsignificant pattern was observed for nonmelanoma skin cancer (OR = 0.39, 95% CI = 0.10 to 1.58) based on the same 2 cases. Consequently, analyses were not conducted for cutaneous melanoma, as no cases occurred in this exposure category.

Among tattooed participants, odds ratios for overall skin cancer, cutaneous melanoma, and nonmelanoma skin cancer were consistently elevated but not statistically significant (OR = 1.75, 95% CI = 0.71 to 4.32; OR = 2.81, 95% CI = 0.70 to 11.25; OR = 1.17, 95% CI = 0.31 to 4.41, respectively) if tattoos were not exposed to sunlight (compared with exposed; 4 cutaneous melanoma and 5 nonmelanoma skin cancer diagnoses among 1034 participants). Comparison of multivariate models with and without tattooed body surface supported the independency of the tattoo exposure from other covariates (Table S7).

In the main Cox regression model (with imputed date of first tattoo for approximately 40% and study entry January 1, 2007), binary tattoo exposure was not related to any type of skin cancer, and the association of tattooed body surface of more than 2 hand palms with overall skin cancer was not statistically significant (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.26, 95% CI = 0.07 to 1.05) (Table 4).

Sensitivity analyses

When time 0 was set to the baseline of Constances (excluding 854 participants that were diagnosed beforehand and thus substantially reducing power), the hazard ratios were slightly attenuated with wider confidence intervals (Table S8). Similarly, restricting analyses to participants aged older than 40 years at first exposure assessment to avoid exposure misclassification led to minimal attenuation of cross-sectional (OR = 0.24, 95% CI = 0.06 to 0.96) and longitudinal (HR = 0.30, 95% CI = 0.07 to 1.19; Tables S9-S10) associations. In survival analyses restricted to participants with known first tattoo date (ie, excluding participants with imputed dates), results were comparable (Table S11). However, these analyses were hampered by even fewer outcomes, reducing statistical power, particularly in higher exposure categories.

Discussion

Using the data of the CRABAT study on tattoo-associated long-term health effects, our results present the first estimates on the tattoo-related risk for cutaneous melanoma and nonmelanoma skin cancer on a large scale. As a strength, the analyses was based on detailed data on tattoo exposure assessed via a validated questionnaire17 and potential confounders.

In our analyses, binary tattoo exposure was not associated with either skin cancer type. When analyzing risk in different exposure categories, we observed a reduced risk of overall skin cancer in the higher exposure categories. In the logistic regression a statistically significant risk decrease was observed in individuals with a tattooed body surface of more than 2 hand palms, however, it was based on only 2 individuals diagnosed with any skin cancer among 1633 participants in this exposure category, strongly hampering that finding, which furthermore failed to reach statistical significance when analyzing the data using a retrospective cohort approach. Also, in general, the results were strongly limited by small case numbers that led to imprecise effect estimates in higher exposure categories. As that was partly due to the fact that full exposure information was only available for approximately 60% of tattooed participants, response bias minimizing case numbers in these categories cannot be ruled out.

Although our findings are consistent with a recent case-control study of McCarty et al.,20 which also observed a risk decrease for cutaneous melanoma in the highest tattoo exposure category, this potential protective effect remains speculative. Confirmation in future studies with larger numbers of participants that developed skin cancer and more robust exposure data are urgently warranted. However, the hypothesis of tattoos increasing skin cancer risk is frequently challenged by publications claiming that the number of published case studies of dermal neoplasia on tattooed skin seems rather small compared with today’s tattoo popularity.21

One plausible explanation for decreased skin cancer risk is that tattoos could protect against ultraviolet radiation from sunlight, which is the most important preventable risk factor for cutaneous melanoma and nonmelanoma skin cancer.21,22 Particularly if dark, and on sun-exposed body parts, tattoos could absorb and prevent backscattering of incoming ultraviolet radiation at the collagen layer, resulting in ultraviolet radiation exposure reduction. When exploring this hypothesis in our data, the direction of the effect pointed in that direction, but the analysis was strongly hampered by small participant numbers. Still, onset of ultraviolet radiation–induced squamous cell carcinoma was delayed in black-tattooed compared with sham-tattooed (tattooed without ink) mice irradiated with standard erythema doses of ultraviolet radiation.23 It seems unlikely that the sun-exposed skin proportion covered by tattoos is substantial even in individuals with large tattooed body surfaces; therefore, the protective effect would be, if anything, very small. Moreover, cutaneous melanoma is often attributable to sun exposure during childhood, and any ultraviolet radiation–protective effect of tattooing would occur in adulthood, further limiting the likelihood of tattoos as a major protective factor against ultraviolet radiation.22 However, the observed declining cutaneous melanoma rates in young individuals are at least not suggestive of the opposite (ie, a large increase in cutaneous melanoma risk among tattooed individuals).24

If these findings replicate prospective studies, additional immunological effects of the tattooing process may be hypothesized as the skin is an exceptionally immunogenic exposure route (which, eg, intradermal smallpox vaccination takes advantage of).25-27 One possible hypothesis could be T-cell priming against melanoma antigens that could be locally released from tumor cells (precursor) during the tattooing process, triggering profound tissue injury and skin cell death.28,29 These free antigens could be taken up by dendritic cells that mature and migrate to the draining lymph nodes, where subsequent (tumor) antigen-specific naïve T-cell priming could take place.8,30,31 Similar to what happens during vaccination, the activated T cells could increase systemic and local immunosurveillance against cutaneous melanoma cells, and because T-cell memory responses would increase and refresh with each new tattoo, protective effects against skin cancer would likely be more pronounced in individuals with larger tattooed surfaces.

Although our results are interesting and merit further research, several circumstances could have substantially biased the results. First, full exposure data were only available for 60% of the tattooed subgroup, which revealed potential selection bias as skin cancer rates were higher in tattooed nonresponders compared with responders. To account for this circumstance in Cox regression, we imputed date of first tattoo in 40% of tattooed participants without full exposure information. Indeed, when fitting Cox regression models including participants with imputed tattoo date compared with excluding these participants, the hazard ratio for binary tattoo exposure and overall skin cancer jumped from 1.04 to 0.81. This bias could be partly due to the retrospective study design; in the present analysis, tattoo exposure was self-reported in 2020 or 2023, while cancer outcomes occurred between 2007 and 2021. If response behavior to our detailed questionnaire on tattoo exposure differed in participants with and without skin cancer, recall bias could have occurred and needs to be considered when interpreting these results. However, at least exposure misclassification of tattoo status is unlikely, because minimal exposure information was available for more than 98% of participants who responded to the Constances follow-up in 2020.

Moreover, some uncertainty remains regarding the temporality of exposure and outcome, as first tattoo date was estimated based on being tattooed in a certain period (instead of an actual date). However, in participants with reported timing since tattoo, we detected only 5 participants where temporality between exposure and outcome was uncertain, reflecting that first tattoos are generally acquired in early adulthood, limiting outcome misclassifications in the remaining 40% of tattooed participants without full exposure information, which would moreover lead, if anything, to underestimation of the effect.

In our study, outcome data were obtained from patient records of hospitals. This might have led to outcome misclassification through undetected in situ or early stage skin tumors that can also be treated in private dermatological practices. In contrast, misclassification for more severe outcomes is less likely as most invasive cutaneous melanoma, as well as the more aggressive nonmelanoma skin cancer subtypes, will be treated in hospitals. Therefore, we assume, despite the lack of data on nonmelanoma skin cancer subtype, that most included diagnoses will be squamous cell carcinoma.

Additionally, we could not retrieve data on tumor locations, diagnostic stage, or cutaneous melanoma or nonmelanoma skin cancer subtypes. Tumor locations would be useful, because we would expect tattoo-induced skin tumors to be locally confined to tattooed skin, thus matching both in the analysis design would have been useful. Moreover, without this information, the potential protective effects of tattoos against ultraviolet radiation, which would similarly only decrease risk on tattooed and sun-exposed body parts, is difficult to assess.

Selective outcome misclassification might also have occurred because of lack of diagnostic stage. As tattooed participants more often reported barriers to health care, it seems plausible that skin tumors could be diagnosed at a later stage on average in that group; additionally, tumors might be more difficult to detect on tattooed skin, which could also lead to late-stage diagnosis. Although this would only slightly affect the logistic regression estimates, hazard ratios could be stronger as follow-up times would be artificially prolonged in tattooed participants.

Vast data on confounders were obtained through the Constances data pool, however, data on sunscreen and tanning bed use were not available. In the above-mentioned case-control study from Utah, sunscreen use seemed to be inversely, and frequency of tanning bed use was strongly, positively correlated with number of tattoo sessions.32 Although comparability of the French and US population might not be perfect, we would expect that lack of these factors should not lead to an overestimation of the protective effect. If anything, the opposite could be the case.

Finally, compared with Western cultures, tattoo culture is less popular in many other world regions, which also differ in skin cancer incidence rates. However, we did not adjust for region of birth, because in Constances, only 5% of participants were born outside Europe (data not shown) and because global differences in skin cancer rates are mainly driven by natural skin pigmentation, which we accounted for by adjusting for the Fitzpatrick skin type.

Given current debates and concerns about the safety of tattooing, we found no overall association between tattoo exposure and skin cancer risk. The inverse association observed in subgroups with large, tattooed body areas was based on very few skin cancer diagnoses and should be interpreted with caution. Our results do not imply any protective effect of tattoos against skin cancer. Longitudinal studies with larger numbers of participants that developed skin cancer are required to study this relationship. Moreover, potential protective mechanisms (eg, through ultraviolet absorption) are specific to skin cancer and do not imply that tattoos are safe for other health outcomes or could be recommended as protective behavior. The possibility of exposure-outcome misclassification needs to be considered as also indicated by selection bias within our tattooed population. Such biases can only be properly addressed in prospective epidemiological cohort analyses, such as of the CRABAT cohort, which delivered the underlying data for this analysis, linking current tattoo exposure to future cancer risks.
