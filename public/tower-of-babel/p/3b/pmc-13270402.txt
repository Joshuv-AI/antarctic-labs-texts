Association Between Cannabis Use and Tinnitus: A Multi‐Center Propensity‐Score‐Matched Cohort Study
Authors: Oluwatobiloba Ayo‐Ajibola, Tyler Jung, Diego Gonzalves, Asher C Park, Matthew E Lin, Ian Kim, Ziva D Cooper, Janet S Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThere are mixed findings regarding the impact of cannabis use on tinnitus, with different patterns of cannabis use potentially influencing risk in varying ways. This study investigates the association between cannabis‐related disorders (CRD) and the risk of developing tinnitus.Study DesignPropensity‐score‐matched cohort study.SettingTriNetX, a multi‐institutional research database.MethodsAdult patients (>18 years) with any CRD and no prior ear‐related disorders were matched to a control group of patients without any history of CRD. Patients with CRD were stratified by increasing levels of cannabis use intensity based on the ICD‐10 codes (use, abuse, or dependence). Propensity score matching controlled for demographic and related clinical variables, including migraine, nicotine and substance use, and mood disorders. The primary outcome was diagnosed tinnitus at 1, 5, and 10 years, analyzed using Cox regression hazard models (hazard ratios (HR), 95% confidence intervals).ResultsAfter matching (n = 92,077 in each group), tinnitus incidence after 1, 5, and 10 years of CRD diagnosis was 0.7%, 1.8%, and 2.1%, respectively. One‐year risk was significantly higher among those with any CRD (HR = 1.30, [95% CI: 1.16‐1.45]). Compared to control at 1‐year of follow‐up, effect size was relatively higher among patients diagnosed with cannabis dependence (use: 1.30 [1.11‐1.51]; abuse: 1.42 [1.05‐1.90]; dependence: 1.55 [1.02‐2.37]). There were no significant trends in tinnitus development at 5 and 10 years of follow‐up with any CRD.ConclusionCannabis use may elevate the short‐term risk of tinnitus, especially among those with more severe use. Further investigation should explore a potential dose‐dependent relationship and underlying mechanisms.Keywords: cannabis, otology, THC, tinnitus

Tinnitus is the perception of noise in the absence of auditory stimuli, which affects up to 25% of adults in the United States at any given time in their lifetime. 1 , 2 Despite a range of treatment approaches, including hearing aid use and psychosocial intervention, tinnitus is associated with decreased quality of life.3, 4, 5 Healthcare resource utilization is also increased, with costs as high as $2100 in additional annual healthcare expenses per patient. 6 The development of tinnitus has been associated with various risk factors, including hearing loss, otologic diseases, ototoxic medications, noise exposure, and cardiovascular disease.7, 8, 9, 10, 11 Lifestyle‐related exposures such as nicotine, alcohol, and inhalant tobacco use have also been associated with tinnitus risk.12, 13, 14 However, the effect of cannabis use on tinnitus remains unclear, with mixed findings reported.

Cannabis is one of the most used substances in the United States, with 19% of adults endorsing use at least once since 2021. The increasing prevalence of cannabis consumption, attributed to legalization, decriminalization, and greater social acceptance, has also raised concerns about disordered use, with some estimates suggesting rates of as high as 30% among users. 15 The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition (DSM‐IV) categorizes cannabis‐related disorders (CRD) into 3 distinct diagnoses: use, abuse, and dependence. Under this classification, individuals with dependence often exhibit tolerance, withdrawal, and a persistent urge to use the substance despite adverse social or occupational consequences, distinguishing them from cannabis abuse (or misuse), which displays similar use patterns without tolerance or withdrawal. 16 Clarifying the relationship between cannabis use intensity and tinnitus risk is important to guide clinical counseling and public health policies, especially as cannabis access continues to expand.

Emerging preclinical research suggests that endocannabinoid signaling may play a role in tinnitus‐related symptoms. 17 Observational clinical studies examining the impact of cannabis use on tinnitus have produced mixed findings, in part due to inconsistent definitions of cannabis use. 18 , 19 Previous studies have examined the association between self‐reported cannabis use and tinnitus, but have not investigated diagnosed CRDs, which are characterized by increased use frequency or tracked longitudinally to assess whether cannabis use may precede tinnitus onset. Here, we conducted a large‐scale, propensity‐matched cohort analysis using the TriNetX Research Network longitudinal data to evaluate both short‐ and long‐term risks of tinnitus development associated with CRD.

Methods

This study was deemed exempt from review by the University of Southern California Institutional Review Board as no identifiable patient data was used.

Study Design

This retrospective cohort study was conducted on the TriNetX US Collaborative Network. This globally federated research network provides access to deidentified healthcare encounter data from the electronic medical records of over 118 million patients from 67 healthcare organizations in the United States. On this platform, patient data is categorized by insurance claim codes related to diagnosis (International Classification of Disease 10th Revision Procedural Coding System Codes: ICD‐10), procedures (Central Procedural Terminology codes: CPT), and healthcare service coding (Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System: HCPCS).

Cohort Creation

To examine the association between CRD and tinnitus risk in adults without hearing issues, 2 main cohorts were created for analysis. Based on a similar work exploring the risk of HNC associated with cannabis use disorders with TriNetX, 20 a “Cannabis” cohort was created which included patients who had at least 3 outpatient visits prior to first diagnosis (HCPCS G0463) with a DSM‐IV diagnosis of cannabis abuse or dependence or unspecified use (ICD F12.1, F12.2, F12.9) at baseline. Following the ICD‐10‐CM and DSM‐IV criteria upon which this database is based, dependence is classified as the most severe, followed by abuse and use. Thus, we also further subdivided the “Cannabis” cohort to specifically investigate each of these diagnoses independently to elucidate a possible dose‐dependent relationship. The “No‐Cannabis” cohort consisted of patients who had at least 3 outpatient visits and no cannabis‐related diagnoses. Both cohorts were designed in a manner that ensured that those with an ear or hearing pathology (ICD H60‐H95) on or before their first outpatient visit were excluded from the cohort, based on criteria outlined in previous works using TriNetX to examine differential risk of otologic pathologies. 21 Patients were included for analysis at the first instance of satisfaction with the inclusion criteria. Data was drawn from de‐identified data from January 2010 to October 2024. All data was queried on January 28, 2025.

Statistical Analysis

A literature review was conducted to control for confounding characteristics predisposing the development of tinnitus.9, 19, 22, 23, 24, 25 The diagnoses and factors linked to tinnitus development were identified for propensity matching using a 1:1 nearest‐neighbor method (Supplemental Table A). Sociodemographic factors such as age at first outpatient visit, current age, sex, race, and ethnicity were selected for matching. Additionally, the cohort was matched based on the occurrence of migraines, diabetes, circulatory system disorders, thyroid issues, and psychiatric conditions, including anxiety and depression. Nicotine (ICD F17), tobacco (Z72), alcohol (F10), stimulant (F14), opioid (F11) use and dependence diagnoses were also characterized and controlled via propensity score matching. Hearing loss was also accounted for based on inclusion of a diagnosis code for “conductive and sensorineural hearing loss” (ICD H91), or unspecified or other hearing loss (H91). Comorbidities and characteristics were analyzed via descriptive statistics. To minimize order bias, TriNetX generates randomized covariate datasets of patient characteristics when calculating propensity scores with the 1:1 greedy nearest neighbor algorithm and a caliper width of 0.1 times the post‐standard deviation. Once the propensity scores were calculated, both cohorts were considered balanced as all characteristics depicted standardized differences of less than 0.100.

The primary outcome was diagnosis of tinnitus (ICD H93.1) in 1, 5, and 10 years following initial inclusion in the cohort. Cox regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals, illustrating the instantaneous risk (ie, the ratio of instantaneous event rates) at any point within the designated time periods. Significance was set at P < .05. After propensity score matching, risks and comparative frequency of the desired outcome were presented. Cohorts were defined as “All Cannabis‐Related Disorders,” referring to a cohort containing any CRDs (use, abuse, and dependence). Subgroup analyses were performed, stratified by intensity of cannabis use categorized as “cannabis use,” “cannabis abuse,” and “cannabis dependence,” representing increasing levels of use severity. While prior ideological frameworks present CRDs as a continuous spectrum, in this study, we present these diagnoses as mutually exclusive, as is acceptable practice in this field of research. 20

Results

Among the 2,965,031 patients identified in this cohort, 98,307 were diagnosed with CRD (Table 1). Prior to propensity score matching, the mean age of individuals with CRD was 43.6 years (SD = 17.0), which is significantly younger than the mean age of 51.6 years (SD = 24.7) for those without CRD (P < .001). Patients with CRD were more likely to be male (n = 51,838, 52.70%). Those with CRD exhibited a higher prevalence of comorbid medical conditions, including diabetes (17.40% vs 13.00%), obesity (25.10% vs 12.00%), hyperlipidemia (26.40% vs 20.80%, all P < .001), and migraine headache (11.8% vs 3.62%, all P < .001). Mood (54.60% vs 12.40%) and anxiety disorders (54.40% vs 13.80%) were more common among those with CRD (both P < .001). Substance use disorders related to opioids (12.90% vs 0.80%), alcohol (23.00% vs 2.40%), cocaine (8.30% vs 0.20%), and nicotine (54.10% vs 8.50%) were more prevalent among those with a diagnosis of CRD (P < .001). After propensity score matching, all aforementioned characteristics were matched to a standard difference of less than 0.100, suggesting appropriate sample matching was performed (Table 1).

Cox proportional hazards regression analyses investigating the relationship between CRDs and the risk of tinnitus over follow‐up periods of 1, 5, and 10 years are presented in Table 3. At 1 year of follow‐up, any CRD was associated with a significantly increased risk of tinnitus (HR = 1.39, 95% CI: [1.16, 1.45]). However, there was no significant association between any CRD and risk of tinnitus development at 5 and 10 years of follow‐up (HR = 1.07, [95% CI: 1.00, 1.15]; HR = 1.04, [95% CI: 0.98, 1.10], respectively). In subgroup analyses stratified by the intensity of the cannabis use, all 3 groups‐ cannabis use, abuse, and dependence—demonstrated a significantly increased risk of developing tinnitus at 1 year of follow‐up. There was a stepwise increase in effect size based on the severity of CRD, where individuals with cannabis dependence exhibited a 1.5 times higher likelihood of developing tinnitus compared to individuals without CRD (HR = 1.72, [1.02, 2.37]), followed by 1.4 times in those with cannabis abuse (HR = 1.42, [1.05, 1.90]) and 1.3 times in those with cannabis use alone (HR = 1.30, [1.11, 1.51]).

Discussion

Using a large, multi‐institutional research database network, this study found that CRDs were significantly associated with an increased risk of developing tinnitus in short‐term within 1 year of diagnosis. However, this association was no longer significant at longer‐term follow‐ups of 5 and 10 years. Additionally, those with the severe cannabis diagnosis, specifically cannabis dependence, demonstrated relatively higher effect size at the first year following diagnosis compared to cannabis abuse and use groups. These results suggest that cannabis use, particularly heavy use, may increase short‐term tinnitus risk, although this impact may diminish over time and become comparable to that of non‐users.

While prior studies have reported mixed findings regarding the association between cannabis use and tinnitus, other population‐based studies have demonstrated a significant link between heavy cannabis use and tinnitus, consistent with our findings. Qian et al and Reyes Orozco et al found that high‐volume and frequency of cannabis use was significantly associated with tinnitus in cross‐sectional analyses of large US adult samples from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey. 18 , 26 While quantity and frequency was not significant in the Qian study, Reyes Orozco et al specifically reported that high‐volume cannabis use, defined as 3 or more pipes/joints per day, was associated with tinnitus compared to non‐users, whereas low‐volume cannabis use was not. In another study, Royes et al examined the analgesic effects of acute oral delta‐9‐tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) administration (10 and 20 mg) in 34 cancer patients compared to codeine (60 and 120 mg) and placebo (0 mg). Tinnitus was reported in a greater number of participants after receiving 20 mg THC relative to the other drug conditions. 27 Our study employed a longitudinal design with survival analysis to examine the temporal relationship between cannabis use and the development of tinnitus, capturing both short‐ and long‐term outcomes.

Several preclinical studies have suggested a potential mechanism associating cannabinoid receptor modulation with tinnitus development. Zheng et al used a salicylate model to induce tinnitus in laboratory rats and subsequently employed operant conditioning to use water consumption to endorse the perception of noise (tinnitus). 28 When exposed to the CB1‐receptor agonist delta 9‐THC, rodents exhibited an increase in tinnitus‐related behavior. Follow‐up studies also found that other CB1 receptor agonists, CP55.940 and WIN55‐212‐2, also increased tinnitus‐related behavior in cases of acoustic trauma‐induced tinnitus. 17

Our findings largely examine the short‐term effects of cannabis use on the development of tinnitus after the diagnosis of CRDs, rather than the long‐term implications. This is evidenced by significant effects observed within the first year, which were no longer present as both cannabis users and nonusers were monitored over longer periods at 5 and 10 years. In the short term, cannabis use has been associated with impairments in neural activations related to memory, attention, executive function, emotion, reward processing, and social processing. 29 Our results may corroborate that cannabis use may increase the short‐term risk of tinnitus among individuals without prior ear‐related conditions via both central and peripheral pathways. However, as patients are followed long‐term, they are increasingly exposed to other various risk factors associated with tinnitus, such as hearing loss, noise exposure, cardiovascular disease, neurodegenerative disease, polypharmacy, depression and anxiety.9, 19, 22, 23, 24, 25, 30 Consequently, these additional exposures may contribute to the lack of significant differences between cannabis users and nonusers in the long term. Although we performed propensity score matching to adjust for baseline risk factors associated with tinnitus, it is reasonable to infer that the relative influence of cannabis use diminishes over time as other risk factors accumulate. As patients age through the observed periods, the increasing prevalence of these conditions may have contributed to the attenuation of the observed effect.31, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38

This study found a low incidence rate of tinnitus, with the highest rate at 2.01% among individuals with cannabis dependence after 10 years. Estimates indicate that the global incidence rate of tinnitus is approximately 1% annually, 36 with overall prevalence ranging from 10% to 25%. 2 , 36 Our lower incidence rates are largely due to the study design excluding individuals with any previous history of tinnitus, hearing loss, or other otologic pathology. This approach allowed for a clearer examination of the association between cannabis use and risk of tinnitus in individuals without prior pathology. Current management guidelines, as outlined by the American Academy of Otolaryngology‐Head and Neck Surgery, focus on mitigating symptom burden via sound therapy, cognitive behavioral therapy, hearing aid use, and neuromodulation techniques. 2 Given the complex pathophysiology of tinnitus and the lack of a definitive cure, identifying and avoiding modifiable risk factors, such as heavy cannabis use as suggested in this study, is important. Further research is still needed to examine the impact of cannabis use on existing tinnitus, as a few studies suggest a potential therapeutic role of cannabinoids. In a small cohort study involving 45 patients at a tertiary neurology clinic in Canada, 22% reported using cannabis, and 80% of these individuals noted an improvement in tinnitus symptoms. 22 Increased emotional distress in this cohort and potential benefits of cannabis for other indications may have also played a significant role in perceived symptom burden. Some experts propose a similarity in the pathophysiology of epilepsy and tinnitus, suggesting that the non‐THC cannabinoid such as cannabidiol (CBD) may diminish hyperactivity, as shown in multiple studies among those with epilepsy. 39 , 40 Thus, while THC remains the most common focus of research as the primary psychoactive cannabinoid, the study of CBD and other secondary cannabinoids is essential in the effort to characterize the effect of cannabis on tinnitus attenuation.

This study has limitations. First, health claims data is subject to coding errors and misclassification of exposure or outcome measures. This data limits precise characterization of baseline exposure, as diagnosis of CRD may be preceded by use at varied frequencies prior to formal diagnosis. Similarly, the timing of tinnitus diagnosis may not reflect onset as it is possible some patients experienced tinnitus prior to formal diagnosis. Due to this uncertainty regarding temporal initiation, it is possible that cannabis was initiated due to undiagnosed tinnitus. The sensitive nature of substance use, along with the associated social stigma, contributes to a prevalent deficiency in formal diagnosis, as a considerable incongruence exists between the documented use in electronic health records and verbal attestation of use. 41 Additionally, cannabis use is associated with engagement in risky health behaviors such as exposure to recreational noise which may further increase the incidence and severity of tinnitus, and may not be accounted for with medical coding data. 42 Although an extensive literature review was conducted to guide proximity matching, including mental health issues and substance use disorders, residual confounding may still be present, and unmeasured variables could have influenced the observed associations. Given the association between poor sleep quality and tinnitus, the lack of detailed information on sleep quality, due to reliance on ICD and CPT medical coding, further limits the conclusions of this study. Additionally, the lack of detailed information on the cannabis use—such as dose, frequency, THC/CBD ratio, and method of administration (eg, inhalation [smoking, vaporizing] versus oral use)—precludes a more nuanced evaluation of dose‐ or route‐dependent effects on tinnitus risk. Moreover, cannabis use was modeled as a time‐fixed exposure due to the nature of claims data, which precludes accurate depiction of variation in use patterns over time. While this may introduce bias, particularly if behaviors change after baseline, our work still reflects real‐world risks associated with clinically significant cannabis exposure in regard to the development of tinnitus. Audiometric data, including hearing thresholds and speech discrimination scores, were not available in the dataset and therefore could not be adjusted for. Instead, the study excluded individuals with pre‐existing hearing impairments at baseline based on ICD‐10 codes. This approach limits the generalizability of the findings to populations without prior auditory conditions, who may have different risk profiles. Finally, the absence of longitudinal symptom data limits our ability to assess the progression, persistence, or resolution of tinnitus over time.

Conclusion

In this study, CRDs—particularly cannabis dependence—were significantly associated to a higher short‐term risk of tinnitus within one year of diagnosis; however, no significant increased long‐term risk was observed. These findings underscore the complexity of the relationship between cannabis use and tinnitus development, highlighting the need for further investigation into dose‐dependent effects and underlying mechanisms.
