The effect of a structured orientation program on teamwork attitudes and nurse outcomes among novice nurses
Authors: Emre Yıldız, Seyda Seren Intepeler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOrientation programs support the transition of novice nurses into their professional roles and facilitate their integration into the team.AimThis research was conducted to examine the effect of a structured orientation program on teamwork attitudes and nurse outcomes among newly graduated nurses.MethodsThe study employed a quasi-experimental pre-test–post-test design. The study was conducted with novice nurses at a university hospital (intervention group: n = 112, control group: n = 188). The control group received the standard orientation program of the institution, while the intervention group participated in a structured orientation program. The study examined teamwork attitude, nurses’ job satisfaction, and their intention to leave the profession. An introductory information and work characteristics form and the Teamwork Attitudes Questionnaire were used as data collection tools, while job satisfaction and intention to leave the profession were evaluated with a single-item measure. For data analysis, descriptive statistics and the independent samples t-test were applied. In addition, the Transparent Reporting of Evaluations with Non-randomized Designs reporting guideline checklist was used.ResultsThe study found that while there were no differences in variables between the control and intervention groups at baseline, by the sixth month, job satisfaction among nurses in the control group had decreased, while those in the intervention group had higher job satisfaction. However, no significant changes were found in teamwork attitudes or intention to leave the profession.ConclusionsThese results suggest that multi-intervention orientation programs designed to address the competencies can be effective in increasing job satisfaction for novice nurses transitioning to their professional roles. Further research is recommended on novice nurses’ teamwork attitudes and other variables affecting their intention to leave the profession.Keywords: Intention to leave, Job satisfaction, Novice nurse, Orientation, Quasi-experimental study, Teamwork attitude

Introduction

The global shortage of nurses poses a threat to patients’ access to quality and safe care. To address this shortage, 4.7 million new nurses joined the workforce between 2013 and 2018. The World Health Organization (2020) [1] emphasizes the importance of education to ensure that new graduate nurses attain adequate levels of competency in transitioning to their professional nursing roles [1]. Therefore, managers should support the learning of newly graduated, inexperienced nurses to ensure their rapid transformation into a competent workforce [2].

According to Benner (1984), who examined the developmental process of novice nurses, a novice nurse is one who lacks prior clinical experience in patient care and responsibility and is unfamiliar with the procedures, policies, and protocols of the institution [3, 4]. Benner (1982) described the process of how novice nurses transition to expert nursing through learning workplace skills and practices in her “Novice to Expert Theory” [5]. This theory identifies five stages: novice, advanced beginner, competent, proficient, and expert. At the final stage (expertise), generally after three years, nurses are able to approach complex situations with competence acquired through experience, develop a deep understanding. Exercise intuition in decision-making, thereby attaining the proficiency of their professional role [3, 5]. Particularly during the transition of new graduates into the professional role, managers should create opportunities for them to become better equipped through education. If this transition is not a successful experience for the novice nurse, the quality of patient care declines, patient safety is jeopardized, and both the institution and financial resources incur losses [6–8]. Among the foremost institutional losses, decreased job satisfaction and turnover are significant nurse outcomes.

It is well known that new graduate nurses have the highest intention to leave and turnover rates during their first year. Ulupınar et al. [9] in 2021 reported that 47.7% of novice nurses experienced difficulties adapting to the nursing profession, 42.5% planned to leave the profession, and 50.9% intended to leave their institutions. Another study determined that 54.37% of nurses had a high or very high turnover intention [10]. To prevent such negative nursing outcomes and facilitate the transition, well-designed, comprehensive orientation or training programs are vital. Improving nurses’ knowledge and skills through orientation is one of the managerial responsibilities of nursing leaders [11–13]. Orientation training aims to foster positive attitudes toward the institution, unit, and department; provide the knowledge and skills necessary for success in the position; and instill a sense of acceptance and belonging [12]. Studies emphasize the importance of comprehensive orientation programs, particularly for new graduate and novice nurses, in ensuring their retention within the profession and institution during the first six months and in reducing their turnover intention [14, 15].

Another significant factor influencing the orientation process of novice nurses is teamwork. The literature recommends that orientation programs be designed through a holistic approach that encompasses individual, organizational, and team interaction factors [16]. In order to improve the quality of healthcare services, reduce delays and errors, and strengthen interprofessional relationships, it is particularly crucial to ensure the competency of nurses in teamwork [17]. Nurses who work in a positive teamwork environment tend to be more satisfied with their profession due to increased collaboration, communication, and interaction, which fosters their organizational identification. They also feel more confident about their competencies and qualifications. Positive teamwork similarly has favorable effects on nurse outcomes [18, 19]. In the orientation process, when novice nurses receive team support, feel like members of the team, and gain competence in teamwork, their participation in effective teamwork positively influences job satisfaction outcomes [20]. This is also an important factor in ensuring retention and preventing long-term turnover among novice nurses [21]. Accordingly, Narbona-Gálvez et al. [22] in 2024, recommended proactive interventions such as structured training and preceptorship programs, which facilitate the adaptation of novice nurses and promote enhanced teamwork, thereby positively influencing their intention to remain in the profession. Preceptorship programs also increase job satisfaction among new graduate nurses, provide opportunities for individual learning, and allow them to become familiar with the organization, colleagues, and team [23, 24]. They are therefore effective in ensuring organizational socialization for novice nurses. It is also known that orientation practices incorporating multiple strategies are more likely to be successful in preventing nurse turnover [25–27]. Based on these considerations, this study was conducted to evaluate the effect of an orientation program, consisting of structured orientation training, structured preceptorship practices, and professional skills laboratory training, on teamwork attitude and nurse outcomes in terms of job satisfaction and intention to leave the profession among novice nurses. The results obtained from this study are expected to serve as guidance for managers in planning orientation processes.

Research question

What are the teamwork attitudes, job satisfaction levels, and intentions to leave the profession among novice nurses?

Research hypotheses

H1

At the sixth month following the implementation of the structured orientation program, teamwork attitudes will be higher in the intervention group compared to the control group.

H2

At the sixth month following the implementation of the structured orientation program, job satisfaction will be higher in the intervention group compared to the control group.

H3

At the sixth month following the implementation of the structured orientation program, intention to leave the profession will be lower in the intervention group compared to the control group.

Materials and methods

Study design

The study employed a quasi-experimental design with pre-test and post-test measurements.

Setting and participants

The research was conducted between 2020 and 2021 at a university hospital in İzmir, Turkey, with a bed capacity of 1100. The study sample consisted of 335 nurses who started their jobs in 2020 and 2021 and had no prior employment at another institution or had worked there for a maximum of one year. However, 35 nurses who did not fully complete the data collection instruments were excluded from the sample. The 300 nurses in the sample group were divided into control and intervention groups according to their assignment and start dates. At baseline, the control group consisted of 188 nurses and the intervention group comprised 112 nurses; at the six-month follow-up, data were collected from 179 to 109 nurses, respectively. At the hospital, novice nurses routinely undergo a standard five-day orientation training program along with a standard preceptorship process. In this study, the control group received the standard orientation program of the hospital, whereas the intervention group participated in a structured orientation program.

Interventions

Prior to the orientation training, both groups completed an introductory information and work characteristics form and the Teamwork Attitudes Questionnaire (T-TAQ). Job satisfaction and intention to leave the profession were assessed using single-item questions included in the introductory information and work characteristics form. The same measurements were repeated at the sixth month of employment for all nurses. The intervention and control procedures are schematized in Fig. 1.

Procedures followed for the intervention and control groups in the study

Standard orientation program of the institution

The control group received the hospital’s standard orientation program, which consisted of orientation training and preceptorship practice.

Orientation training

All novice nurses were required to complete a five-day training program. The nurses in the control group participated in this orientation training, which began with an introduction to the hospital and included sessions on documentation, information security, human resources processes, personnel affairs, basic fire safety, hand hygiene, infection control measures, and the use of personal protective equipment. In addition, the program addressed patient care for various disease and patient groups. The training concluded with practical adult and pediatric basic life support sessions.

At the end of the training, all educational activities and the nurses’ feedback were reviewed by the researchers, and the orientation program of the intervention group was developed based on these recommendations. For example, one suggestion from the control group was that orientation training should include unit-specific applications. Accordingly, in the intervention group, the nurses assigned to specialized units such as operating rooms or neonatal intensive care received additional unit-specific practical training following the general orientation. Moreover, in response to the feedback, a one-week professional skills laboratory training program was added after the five-day orientation training for the intervention group.

Preceptorship practices

Each novice nurse was assigned a nurse preceptor, selected jointly by the Nursing Services Directorate and the unit’s charge nurse, in accordance with institutional procedures. On the first day of employment, the charge nurse introduced the new nurse to the unit, colleagues, and assigned preceptor. Information was provided on the work environment, workflow, physical setting, documentation, and patient and staff areas. The nurse preceptor also demonstrated patient care and education procedures in accordance with relevant protocols and guidelines. Preceptor nurses were selected from among senior nurses by managerial staff to facilitate the integration of novice nurses into the institution and workplace. Preceptorship assignments were generally determined on a daily basis, according to the number of shifts the novice nurse was expected to complete under supervision. During the preceptorship period, novice nurses could work with multiple preceptors. However, there was no standardized method for information handover between successive preceptors.

Structured orientation program

The intervention group received a structured orientation program, which included structured orientation training, professional skills laboratory training, and structured preceptorship practices.

Structured orientation training

The nurses in the intervention group participated in a structured orientation program that was developed by the researchers based on a review of the current literature [15, 26, 27] and feedback from control group participants. The program was designed to be comprehensive and employed expert instructors, incorporating methods such as demonstration, case presentations, and group work to promote teamwork.

Structured preceptorship practices

Structured preceptorship practices included structured interactive training for nurse preceptors who would be paired with novice nurses, along with practices conducted differently from the standard preceptorship model.

The structured preceptorship training was delivered to nurses who had not previously received preceptor education, were willing to serve as preceptors, were deemed suitable by managers, and met the nurse preceptor eligibility criteria. The training objectives and curriculum were developed in collaboration with the hospital and the nursing faculty and implemented through interactive online sessions.

The first difference from standard preceptorship practices was a revision in the institution’s quality standards, whereby only nurses who had completed nurse preceptorship training could be designated as preceptors. Another change ensured that during the adaptation process, each novice nurse was paired with a single nurse preceptor, and each nurse preceptor was assigned to only one novice nurse. Work schedules were arranged so that the novice nurse’s working days and shifts coincided with those of the nurse preceptor. The duration of joint work between the preceptor and the novice nurse was determined based on an assessment using a novice nurse unit adaptation evaluation form, which included specific criteria. Nurses achieving an average score of three or higher on the form were deemed competent to work independently. In cases where a preceptor change was mandatory, the continuation of the preceptorship process was transferred to a new nurse preceptor, along with recommendations, considering the developmental evaluation criteria in the same evaluation form. Furthermore, unit-specific adaptation guidelines were updated by charge nurses and education nurses for each clinical area and shared with novice nurses.

Professional skills laboratory training

Another component of the intervention group’s program was professional skills laboratory training. Through this intervention, novice nurses were provided with training in fundamental nursing practice skills via a professional skills laboratory program before fully assuming their roles as professional practitioners. The training was conducted using the facilities of the nursing school’s professional skills laboratory, in collaboration with the hospital where the study was carried out. The nursing skills to be taught were determined by a planning committee that included educational nurses, researchers, and the Nursing Services Directorate, in accordance with the literature [28]. The professional skills practices were first demonstrated on mannequins by nurse preceptors and senior nurses who were experts in the respective procedures and performed them most frequently. The participants were then given the opportunity to perform the practices themselves following observation. Since nursing practices in neonatal intensive care (n = 6) and operating rooms (n = 25) differ from those in other units, unit-specific professional skills training programs were additionally delivered by the senior nurses of those respective units.

Data collection

Introductory information and work characteristics form

This form consisted of eight questions regarding nurses’ age, gender, education level, length of service in the institution, previous work experience in another institution, current work unit, job satisfaction, and intention to leave the profession. In the study, job satisfaction was assessed using a single item, similar to large-scale nursing workforce studies [29, 30]. Current job satisfaction was determined by a four-point rating scale to the question “How satisfied are you with your profession?” (1) not satisfied at all (2), not satisfied (3), moderately satisfied, and (4) very satisfied [31]. To assess intention to leave the profession, a single-question measurement tool used in the NEXT (Nurses’ Early Departure Study) project conducted in European countries was applied [32]. The answer options to the question “How often did you consider leaving the nursing profession in the past year?” were (1) ‘never’ (2), ‘a few times a year’ (3), ‘a few times a month’ (4), ‘a few times a week’, and (5) ‘every day’, and were scored between 1 and 5 [33]. Considering the heavy workloads, time constraints, stress, and related mental health problems of nurses during the COVID-19 pandemic [34], job satisfaction and intention to leave the job of nurses were measured with a single item. Indeed, in another study conducted with newly graduated nurses in the same institution for similar reasons, the intention to leave the job after a mentoring program involving multiple strategies was assessed with a single question [35].

Teamwork attitude scale (T-TAQ)

T-TAQ was developed by Baker et al. [36] in 2010, within the framework of “TeamSTEPPS,” a project of the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality and the United States Department of Defense. It consists of five subscales: team structure, team leadership, situation monitoring, mutual support, and communication, each with six items. The adaptation study in Turkey was undertaken by Yardımcı et al. [37] in 2012. In the Turkish adaptation, the number of items in the mutual support subscale was reduced to three, while the communication subscale contained five items. The scale is of the five-point Likert type, and each item is scored based on the respondent’s agreement. The scoring ranges from “1” for strongly disagree to “5” for strongly agree. Both total scores for each subscale and mean item scores can be calculated. In interpreting the scale, a total score approaching 30 for team structure, team leadership, and situation monitoring; 25 for communication; 15 for mutual support; and a mean item score approaching “5” indicate a positive attitude toward the corresponding subscale. Reliability coefficients (Cronbach’s α values) for the subscales in the original scale were 0.70 for team structure, 0.81 for team leadership, 0.83 for situation monitoring, 0.70 for mutual support, and 0.74 for communication. These values were reported to be 0.78, 0.89, 0.82, 0.70, and 0.79, respectively, in the Turkish adaptation study, while they were found to be 0.76, 0.89, 0.85, 0.71, and 0.81, respectively, in the current study.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 25.0 (SPSS Statistics 25 Software). The normality of the data distribution was assessed using skewness and kurtosis values. In the statistical analysis, categorical variables were presented as percentages and frequencies, while continuous variables were expressed as means and standard deviations. All hypotheses were tested using the Independent Sample t-Test. Since our study was conducted in consecutive processes and the control and intervention groups consisted of different individuals, the independent samples t-test was preferred as the statistical analysis method. Since the data were collected on a group basis rather than an individual basis, the specific participants lost to follow-up could not be identified, consequently, a formal attrition bias analysis could not be performed. The significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for hypothesis testing. In addition, the Transparent Reporting of Evaluations with Non-randomized Designs reporting guideline checklist was used.

Ethical considerations

Permission to use the T-TAQ employed in the study was obtained from the researchers who developed the scale. For conducting the study in the designated institution, institutional permission was obtained (date: October 23, 2020, number: 72292585-663.03-E.99227). Ethical approval for the research was obtained from the Non-interventional Research Ethics Committee of a university (date: November 9, 2020, decision number: 2020/27–29). This study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The purpose of the study was explained to the participants, and their written consent was obtained. The article adhered to research and publication ethics.

Discussion

In this study, the effect of a structured orientation program on teamwork attitudes and nurse outcomes among novice nurses was evaluated.

Teamwork attitudes, job satisfaction, and intention to leave the profession among novice nurses

It was determined that novice nurses’ scores regarding attitudes toward teamwork were at a good level. This finding is consistent with the results of the pilot study in which the scale used was originally developed [36]. While there are many studies in the literature examining nurses’ teamwork attitudes [38, 39], no other study was identified that specifically evaluated teamwork attitudes of novice nurses during the transition process [36]. According to the findings, although there was no change between baseline and six-month results, the subscale with the lowest scores for both the intervention and control groups was team structure. The highest-scoring subscale was situation monitoring in the intervention group and team leadership in the control group. Studies conducted with the same scale and with nurses revealed that the lowest and highest subscale mean scores differed by country, institution, and unit. However, in most cases, the subscale determined to have the highest or one of the highest scores has been reported to be team leadership [39–42]. This situation illustrates the importance that the control group attached to leadership, particularly in terms of enabling team cohesion in pandemic clinics where team experience was not yet fully established. This result may also be related to the fact that in newly established clinics during the COVID-19 pandemic, many healthcare workers with limited or no experience working together had to collectively manage a challenging care process.

In both the intervention and control groups, the lowest-scoring subscale at baseline and at the sixth month was team structure. Multi-team work environments are a crucial factor for workplace socialization. The finding that both groups rated this subscale lowest may result from the fact that all participating nurses had less than six months of experience and thus had not yet become accustomed to multi-team structures in clinical practice. Similarly, according to the results of a previous study, nurses believed that working as a member of a team improved their individual, social, and scientific competencies [43].

In this study, novice nurses reported a near-high level of job satisfaction, which is consistent with the literature [44, 45]. When comparing job satisfaction scores at baseline and the sixth month, it was found that the control group started employment with higher levels of job satisfaction than the intervention group but experienced a decline by the end of the sixth month. In contrast, nurses in the intervention group, despite beginning with lower levels of job satisfaction, demonstrated an increase by the sixth month, which may have been influenced by strategies designed to enhance job satisfaction. In line with these findings, Auffermann et al. [46] in 2021 also reported that nurses who underwent a structured orientation program experienced improved job satisfaction.

The study results also showed that novice nurses’ intentions to leave the profession were at moderate to low levels. However, previous studies generally reported higher levels of turnover intention among novice nurses [47, 48]. The lower levels of turnover intention observed in this study, conducted during the pandemic, may be explained by the effective management of the COVID-19 situation within the institution [49]. In COVID-19 units, many healthcare professionals who had never previously worked together, and in some cases had never even met, were required to provide care to a patient population marked by uncertainty and complexity. Therefore, in institutions where strategies to address these challenges were implemented, the turnover intentions of novice nurses appear to have been positively influenced [50].

Another notable finding is the increase in the mean intention to leave the profession in both groups by the sixth month; however, this increase was more pronounced in the control group, whose baseline mean was lower than that of the intervention group. A report on the nursing workforce by Nursing Solutions Inc. in 2021, indicated that 24% of novice nurses left the profession within their first year, with an additional 19% leaving before completing their second year [51]. According to another study conducted with novice nurses [52], 17.2% of newly graduated nurses considered leaving the profession shortly after beginning their careers, and this rate increased further to 71.8% by the end of the first year. In Turkey, this rate was reported as 42.5% [9]. In this regard, the finding of increased intention to leave by the sixth month is consistent with the literature. Similarly, in their systematic review, Brook et al. [53] in 2019 concluded that orientation programs incorporating classroom education, structured preceptorship, practical skills training, and clinical practice collectively reduced nurse turnover rates by approximately 15–20%.

Effects of the structured orientation program on teamwork attitudes and nurse outcomes among novice nurses

The teamwork attitudes of the intervention and control groups were similar at baseline and at the sixth month. However, despite this similarity in the results, the initial values ​​of nurses’ teamwork attitudes were determined to be quite close to the upper limit that could be obtained. Therefore, the high initial measurement scores created a ceiling effect for revealing the difference in other measurements. Similarly, Ahsan et al. [38] in 2021 reported that interventions aimed at improving teamwork attitudes did not have a significant effect. In the study, although there was a difference of one to six months between the start dates of the control and intervention groups, the interventions coincided with the second wave of the COVID-19 Pandemic. Second measurements of the variables were taken at the end of the 6th month after the baseline scores. This period may not have been sufficient to see the effect of the interventions. Indeed, in a study conducted with the same measurement tool, while the 6-month results were not significant, significant changes occurred in all three dimensions of the scale at the 12th month [54]. A 2021 study indicated that nurses used their adaptability skills to overcome the challenges they faced during the pandemic, and their willingness to solve problems led them to use different ways to foster collaboration within the team. The same study stated that nurses were more sensitive and supportive towards each other during the pandemic, and thus better understood the importance of teamwork in the work environment [55]. It is possible that significant changes were not identified in subsequent teamwork impact assessments due to this high-quality teamwork [56]. Similarly, the intentions of the intervention and control groups to leave the profession were found to be similar after the structured orientation program. In contrast, the literature generally indicates that well-designed and comprehensive orientation programs reduce turnover intention [9, 57]. Given that this study was conducted during the peak of the COVID-19 pandemic, novice nurses’ intentions to leave the profession may have been influenced by a number of independent factors. The literature includes studies that explain factors contributing to intention to leave among novice nurses through themes and variables, which can generally be grouped into organizational and personal factors [58–61]. In the current study, although improvements were made to educational factors under institutional support and were reinforced through strategies based on literature and recommendations, the results were not statistically significant. Nevertheless, this finding should be interpreted with caution. Primarily, as the follow-up data could not be matched with the baseline measurements at an individual level, these findings reflect group-level differences over time rather than intervention-dependent intra-individual changes. Since the study groups were formed based on employment start dates during the COVID-19 pandemic, which could not be controlled, it is considered that temporal and contextual factors may have influenced the results; and that these may be triggered by negative psychological responses to organizational, internal, and environmental circumstances associated with individual tendencies [10, 62, 63]. Given that turnover for the first year range from 12% to 25% [64], this finding should be carefully considered by nursing managers.

While job satisfaction levels were similar between the intervention and control groups at baseline, differences emerged at the sixth month. The decline in job satisfaction observed in the control group, which received the standard orientation program of the institution, was associated with the transition shock experienced during the challenging adaptation process, as noted by Asber (2019) [65]. In contrast, the significantly higher job satisfaction observed in the intervention group compared to the control group may be attributed to the implementation of a comprehensive orientation program designed to facilitate adaptation, which included structured education, one-on-one mentoring support provided by trained preceptors, and strategies fostering teamwork through diverse approaches [15, 22, 44, 66]. Similarly, Melissant et al. [16] in 2024 emphasized the importance of designing orientation programs through a holistic approach that integrates individual, team, and organizational factors.

Limitations and strengths

This study is one of the few large-sample studies conducted at a single institution focusing on novice nurses. During the planning phase of this study, the recruitment of a large number of nurses at the research institution due to the COVID-19 pandemic ensured a sufficient sample size. Another strength is the high participation rate (89.55%) and the potential for the study’s results to be used by administrators at the institution where the study was conducted. These features are invaluable for considering the results. Despite the importance of this study, there are some limitations. First, because it occurred at a single institution and the data were based on participant self-reports, the findings not be generalize. There is a variation of one to six months between the employment start dates of nurses in the control and intervention groups. The staggered start dates and the study being conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, may have allowed time-related extraneous variables to influence the results. Although the intervention phases for both groups coincided with the second wave of the pandemic in the country of study, the potential confounding effect arising from the difference in baseline timings should be acknowledged as a limitation. Another limitation is that the sixth-month data could not directly matched with the baseline data on an individual nurse basis. Since the data were collected on a group basis rather than an individual basis, the specific participants lost to follow-up could not be identified; consequently, a formal attrition bias analysis could not be performed and six-month results were derived solely from the participants who completed the final assessment. Furthermore, this study could not control for potential confounding variables, such as the structure of the unit, team size, and personal factors, which could potentially influence the sixth-month data on nursing outcomes such as job satisfaction, intention to leave the profession, and teamwork attitudes of novice nurses. These factors could also be considered a limitation. Furthermore, in our study, novice nurses’ intention to leave the profession was measured using a single-item question. Although this data collection method has been used in large-scale nursing workforce studies, it may be considered a limitation as it may be less sensitive in detecting changes resulting from complex and multifaceted interventions, as was the case in our study.

Conclusions

This study, a quasi-experimental design, is one of the few studies in the literature conducted with a large sample of novice nurses. The control group participated in the institution’s standard orientation program, while the intervention group participated in a structured orientation program. Novice nurses generally exhibited positive attitudes toward teamwork. Job satisfaction levels were near high, and intentions to leave the profession were moderate to low. While there were no differences in teamwork attitudes and intentions to leave between the intervention and control groups at baseline, our findings revealed significantly higher job satisfaction in the intervention group at the end of the sixth month, but no significant change in teamwork attitudes or intentions to leave the profession. These results contribute to the literature by demonstrating that structured orientation programs for new nurses increase job satisfaction. Nurse managers and policymakers should prioritize structured orientation training specifically for new nurses to increase job satisfaction and, consequently, improve nursing outcomes. Research interventions designed to address the competencies needed by nurses as they transition into their professional roles (such as professional skills laboratory training, structured orientation training, and internship programs) could also be considered by educational planners. The lack of change in teamwork attitudes and intention to leave following the interventions suggests the need for comprehensive, multivariate studies to examine other contributing factors to these variables.
