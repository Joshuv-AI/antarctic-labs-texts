Apixaban-Induced Cutaneous Small Vessel Vasculitis
Authors: Vineet Relhan, Ishan Agrawal, Shweta Grover
Published: 2025

To the Editor,

Leucocytoclastic vasculitis (LCV) is small vessel vasculitis, histopathologically characterized by immune complex mediated vasculitis of dermal vasculature. It may be limited to cutaneous involvement or may have extracutaneous involvement (<30% of cases).[1]

We present a case of an 81-year-old female who developed palpable purpura following the administration of apixaban. Histopathology, history to rule out other causes of LCV and temporal correlation, helped in diagnosis. The purpura gradually subsided on withdrawal of the drug.

An 81-year-old female presented to the outpatient department of the Department of Medicine with chief complaints of breathlessness on mild exertion for the past seven days which was sudden in onset and gradually progressive. The patient had a history of hypertension for the past 10 years which was well controlled with Telmisartan 40 mg once daily. The patient denied any other significant medical history. Blood investigations including complete blood count and liver function did not reveal any abnormality. Renal function test suggested chronic renal failure. Chest X-ray revealed no abnormality. Ultrasound of the whole abdomen and pelvis revealed shrunken kidneys. Echocardiography revealed atrial fibrillation. The patient was advised lifestyle modification, and apixaban 5 mg once daily was started. After five days of starting apixaban, the patient developed erythematous rash over the bilateral knees which was associated with joint pain and no fever. The rash gradually over the course of next two days extended to involve the bilateral legs. The patient visited the dermatology outpatient department with the rash. On examination, there was palpable purpura over bilateral legs extending from knees proximally to ankles distally [Figure 1]. With a clinical suspicion of cutaneous small vessel vasculitis, blood investigations and cutaneous biopsy were conducted. The patient denied any preceding insect bite, exposure to chemicals or any vaccination. Complete blood count, anti-streptolysin O, anti-nuclear antibodies, P-ANCA, serology for HIV, HbsAg and HCV did not reveal any abnormality. The erythrocyte sedimentation rate was 44 mm/hr. 24-hour urine protein was 374 mg/day. A cutaneous biopsy with H and E stain revealed fibrinoid necrosis in the vessel walls and LCV. Few foci of RBC extravasation were seen [Figure 2]. Direct immunofluorescence was not done for the patient. Scoring on Naranjo scale helped in identifying apixaban as the causative agent. The patient was advised to stop apixaban, strict bed rest and leg elevation.[2] The purpura subsided over the course of next seven days leaving behind no residual pigmentation or scarring.

LCV presents classically with palpable purpura, small vessel involvement and bilateral lower limb involvement. Various etiological associations have been found to trigger LCV.[1] These include various oral anti-coagulants including rivaroxaban, apixaban and dabigatran.[3] Various hypotheses have been proposed for understanding the pathogenesis of apixaban-induced LCV. The most accepted one is molecular mimicry between the drug and vascular endothelium, endothelial injury by activation of the immune system, the release of inflammatory markers and subsequent hypersensitivity to immune complexes.[4]

Our case report underscores the significance of a rare vasculitic eruption linked to apixaban, amidst limited literature, emphasizing the need for heightened awareness among the clinicians.
