Non IgE-mediated food allergy: Current knowledge and unmet needs
Authors: Aikaterini Anagnostou
Published: 2025

This special issue of the Journal of Food Allergy is devoted to non–immunoglobulin E (IgE) mediated food allergies, a diverse group of conditions that continue to challenge clinicians and researchers alike. These disorders, including food protein–induced allergic proctocolitis (FPIAP), eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE), and food protein–induced enterocolitis syndrome (FPIES), represent a complex intersection of allergy/immunology, nutrition, and clinical care. Together, the articles in this issue offer insights into disease mechanisms, management strategies, and the lived experiences of affected patients and families.

Anstey et al.1 discuss key management considerations in FPIAP, a benign condition that typically presents in healthy infants within the first month of life and resolves by ∼12 months of age. FPIAP is generally managed through elimination diets, however, recent studies have noted an increased risk of developing IgE-mediated food allergies, potentially as a consequence of prolonged avoidance of the offending food. This has prompted ongoing debate with regard to the risks and benefits of early food reintroduction in patients with FPIAP. The authors provide a comprehensive summary of the current understanding of FPIAP, highlight areas that require further mechanistic and outcome-based investigation, and explore the long-term implications of elimination diets.

Thomas et al.2 present a multidisciplinary approach to EoE, a chronic inflammatory condition of the esophagus frequently associated with allergic, gastrointestinal, and psychiatric comorbidities. To optimize patient outcomes, the authors propose a unified, coordinated model of care that integrates expertise from allergists, gastroenterologists, registered dietitians, and mental health professionals. This collaborative approach is shown to enhance the likelihood of achieving both clinical and histologic remission in patients with EoE.

The multifaceted burden of EoE is further detailed by Offerle et al.,3 who describe the substantial impact of this condition on quality of life, physical well-being, psychosocial functioning, and health-care utilization. Whereas recent therapeutic advances hold promise for improved outcomes, the authors emphasize the need for integrated, holistic strategies to mitigate the comprehensive burden of disease. They underscore the importance of recognizing the complex interplay among the emotional, social, mental, and financial consequences of EoE, encouraging clinicians to address many of these factors within shared decision-making conversations.

Patel et al.4 review the epidemiology and complications associated with FPIES, including nutritional deficiencies, food aversions, financial impact, and overall effects on quality of life. The authors highlight that caregivers of children with FPIES report a poorer quality of life compared with those managing IgE-mediated food allergies, citing greater feeding difficulties, failure to thrive, poor weight gain, and nutritional deficiencies. Individuals with multiple trigger foods are particularly susceptible to food aversion. The authors advocate for a multidisciplinary approach, which encompasses nutrition, speech therapy, occupational therapy, and mental health support, to address the unmet needs of these patients and their families.

Nicolaides et al.5 examine the current literature on FPIES diagnosis by focusing on the indications and timing of oral food challenges (OFC), with special attention to adult and atypical presentations. They note that, in the United States, OFCs are typically performed 12 to 18 months after the last reaction to a suspected trigger, although practices with regard to coreactive and cross-reactive foods vary. Factors that influence OFC timing and setting include the nutritional and cultural relevance of the food, severity of previous reactions, and parental comfort. The authors recommend an individualized approach tailored to patient-specific circumstances while emphasizing the need for further research to standardize OFC protocols. Enhanced guidelines and increased awareness among health-care professionals are essential to improve diagnostic accuracy and management of FPIES, which ultimately lead to better patient outcomes.

Finally, Ortiz et al.6 present a rare case of FPIES triggered by mushroom ingestion, an occurrence reported only once previously in the pediatric population. Their discussion of fungi as a novel trigger contributes valuable insight to the expanding literature on emerging and uncommon causes of FPIES.
