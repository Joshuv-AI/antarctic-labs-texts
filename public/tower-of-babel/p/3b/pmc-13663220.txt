Determining factors of birthplace choice among women in Ghana: evidence from the 2022 nationally representative data
Authors: Issah Haruna, Abdul-Rauf Sulemana, Osei Kwame Wiredu, Baaba Amoah-Saah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe availability and accessibility of high-quality maternal healthcare remain a significant challenge for health systems worldwide. Evidence demonstrates that health insurance positively influences the utilization of maternal healthcare services in Sub-Saharan Africa. But some subscribers of health insurance poorly utilize health facilities due to some persistent sociodemographic factors in Ghana. Low Facility-based delivery increases the risk of both maternal and child mortalities. Most of the recent studies conducted in Ghana have not determined the regional prevalence of Facility-based delivery and the health insurance subscription rates. This study estimated the prevalence of Facility-based delivery and assessed the associated factors that determine mothers’ birthplace choice in Ghana.MethodsThe 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey data were used in this cross-sectional study. Descriptive statistics for the variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. The prevalence of facility-based delivery was determined by dividing all facility-based deliveries by total deliveries. Determinants of Facility-based delivery by women in Ghana were assessed in a multiple logistic regression model.ResultsWomen with National Health Insurance were 93% more likely to have Facility-based delivery than those without National Health Insurance [AOR = 1.93, 95% CI = 1.38–2.70]. Women in rural areas were 55% less likely to have facility-based delivery [AOR = 0.45, 95% CI = 0.32–0.62]. Expectant mothers with secondary education were 91% more likely to give birth to their babies through a facility-based delivery compared to mothers with no formal education [AOR = 1.91, 95% CI = 1.45–2.52]. Women with a tertiary education had an increased odds of giving birth at health facilities compared to uneducated women [AOR = 3.44, 95% CI = 1.13–10.76] Mothers who belong to other religions (traditionalists, etc.) were 72% [AOR = 0.28, 95% CI = 0.17–0.46] less likely to be delivered in health facilities. Mothers from richer and richest families had an elevated odds of giving birth at health facilities compared to women from the poorest family [AOR = 2.95, 95% CI = 1.61–5.42] and [AOR = 2.96, 95% CI = 1.27–6.94] respectively. Mothers from the Southern zone of Ghana were less likely to give birth at health facilities [AOR = 0.50, 95% CI = 0.31–0.79] compared to those from the Northern zone. Women who go for four or more ANC visits during pregnancy were 4.63 times more likely to give birth at a health facility compared to women who do not go for ANC visits during pregnancy [AOR = 4.63, 95% CI = 2.45–8.76].ConclusionThe study revealed that facility-based delivery was influenced by health insurance, residence, education, religion, zone, wealth status and antenatal care visits. Health insurance should be further subsidized for rural and women from poorest families. ANC services should be extended to the doorsteps of rural women who are in communities far away from health facilities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12884-026-09683-0.

Introduction

The availability and accessibility of high-quality maternal healthcare through health insurance schemes remain a significant challenge for health systems globally [1]. Universal healthcare coverage remains a major challenge in many struggling healthcare systems, particularly in Africa [1]. As a result of these challenges, the World Health Organization (WHO) advised many countries to establish robust health systems to improve access to quality healthcare [1]. In response to the World Health Organization’s (WHO) call, many countries implemented health financing strategies to improve universal access to quality healthcare [2]. Ghana initially implemented a tax-funded healthcare system that provided free public healthcare services to all citizens [3]. But governments’ expenditure on health declined in the 1970s and 1980s, leading to shortages of medicines and supplies and decreasing quality of care [3, 4]. The introduction of the National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS) in Ghana in 2003 was the sole aim of the government’s commitment to providing quality and accessible health care for all citizens [5]. Considerable evidence demonstrates that health insurance has improved the utilization of maternal healthcare services in Ghana [6–10]. The introduction of NHIS led to increased healthcare utilization among women in Accra [3]. But some subscribers of health insurance underutilize health facilities due to some persistent socio-demographic and socio-economic factors [11, 12]. Despite the free maternal healthcare policy, over 25% of deliveries in northern Ghana still occurred outside health facilities [13]. These high home deliveries were influenced by negative perceptions among half of these women’s husbands about hospital delivery [13]. Low facility-based delivery increases the risk of both maternal and child mortalities. It is evident that women who give birth at home face 3.2 times higher odds of maternal death and 2.8 times higher odds of early neonatal death compared to those who deliver in facilities [14]. Research has also explored the perspectives of healthcare providers on the free maternal healthcare policy [15]. A qualitative study found that healthcare providers at Kwadaso Municipality in Ghana perceived the policy as important for increasing access but also noted challenges related to quality healthcare delivery [15]. These challenges include inadequate resources, drug unavailability, limited medical personnel, and weak accountability, which can impact patient satisfaction and utilization [16]. Maternal health services utilization is crucial for reducing maternal mortality, which stood at 263 deaths per 100,000 live births in Ghana, highlighting a critical public health challenge [17, 18]. Quality healthcare must be readily available to Ghanaian women through the NHIS [19]. And to reduce the many factors that influence the underutilization of healthcare in some populations [9]. As it stands now, the national rate of facility-based delivery hovers around 70–80%, with significant regional disparities, particularly in rural northern regions where rates can be far lower [20]. Globally, empirical evidence indicates that health insurance enhances access and utilisation of health services [21, 22]. These findings are very similar in the Ghanaian context [23, 24]. Increased antenatal service utilization among women is significantly associated with high health insurance enrollment [25]. Other studies have also shown a positive association between health insurance coverage and the use of maternal healthcare services, though often with less rigorous methodology [26, 27]. According to Wang et al., (2016), some studies have reported mixed results concerning the impact of health insurance on maternal healthcare utilization [9]. Most of the recent studies conducted in Ghana have not determined the regional prevalence of facility-based deliveries and the health insurance subscription rates [15, 18, 28]. This would have helped the government to possibly review the policy to optimize facility-based delivery and NHIS subscriptions, particularly in regions with lower rates. This study highlights the current prevalence of facility-based delivery and subscription rates to the national health insurance scheme and factors that are associated with the rates for quality policy decisions. Notably, this study focused on the influence of NHIS and sociodemographic factors because previous studies in Ghana identified them as the common predictors of facility-based delivery among pregnant women [2, 23, 24, 29].

Materials and methods

Study design and data source

We employed a cross-sectional study design, using the 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey (GDHS) to estimate prevalence and determine the factors that are associated with facility-based deliveries among women of reproductive age in Ghana. We analysed the individual women’s recode file (IR file). The details of GDHS data collection, the stratification of clusters, and the procedures are discussed extensively elsewhere [30]. We included all women aged 15–49 years in this study who were residents in the sampled households [30]. Women with Antenatal care records and delivery places were included for analysis. The dataset was examined for missing values before analysis, and these were excluded. After excluding the missing data, the final sample for analysis was 5,189 women. The selection process of participation is presented in Fig. 1. This study was conducted according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines to guarantee clear communication, transparency, and rigorous methodology [31].

Study variables

Dependent variables

The primary dependent variable for this study was delivery place. The outcome was coded as follows: 1 = health facility delivery, 0 = home delivery. Mothers who were delivered at their home or other homes were grouped into home delivery, and all other deliveries at both public and private health facilities were grouped under facility-based delivery (coded as 1 = health facility delivery, 0 = home delivery). The secondary dependent variable was facility-based delivery (1 = yes or 0 = no).

Independent variable

Ten independent variables were examined in the study to identify the factors associated with the facility-based delivery among women in Ghana. These factors were possession of NHIS (yes or no), age group (15–19, 20–34, 35–49), residence (rural or urban), education (no education, primary, secondary, tertiary), distance (no big problem or big problem), wealth index (poorest, poorer, middle, richer or richest), religion (chritian, muslim, traditional), occupation (unemployed or employed), ANC visits (no visits, 1–3 visits or 4 + visits) The 16 regions of Ghana were grouped into Northern zone (Northern, Upper West, Upper East, North East and Savannah regions), Middle zone (Ashanti, Western North, Ahafo, Bono, Oti and Bono East regions), and Southern zone (Western, Central, Accra, Volta and Eastern regions).

Data management and statistical analysis

After obtaining permission from the Demographic and Health Survey, self-written commands in Stata were used to manage the data after downloading the dataset. All data cleaning, management, and analysis were conducted using Stata/MP version 17.0. The original variables were recoded into groups for analysis as presented in the supplementary document Data Recode. The survey design incorporated the primary sampling unit (v021), strata (v022), and sampling weights (v005/1000000). The dataset was declared as survey data by using the “svyset” command. Descriptive statistics for categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. The study employed descriptive statistical techniques to examine the association between women’s socio-demographic characteristics and delivery place decisions, using the Rao-Scott adjusted Chi-square test. The prevalence of facility-based delivery was determined by dividing all facility-based deliveries by total deliveries using the “svy: mean” command in STATA. The variables selected were based on an intensive literature review [2, 23, 24, 29]. Before the variables were included in the logistic regression model, multicollinearity was assessed among the independent variables through the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) for predictors in the regression model [32]. Variables with VIF ≤ 10 were declared not to have collinearity issues and were included in the univariate model. The Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values for the independent variables in the model ranged from 1.08 to 2.09, with a mean VIF of 1.36. These values suggest that there were no significant multicollinearity, indicating that the independent variables were not correlated. Details of the VIF results are shown in the supplementary document Sup.doc. Univariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify the unadjusted factors associated with facility-based delivery (Coded: Yes or No), considering a significance level of p ≤ 0.20. Other important variables with p > 0.20 were also included in the analysis. A multiple logistic regression model was also fitted for all significant socio-demographic factors, including NHIS status from the univariate logistic regression model, to determine the adjusted predictors of facility-based delivery whilst controlling for confounders. Variables that were significantly associated with the facility-based delivery were selected from the multiple logistic regression based on odds ratio (OR) with a 95% confidence interval (CI) and p ≤ 0.05. Choropleth maps in Quantum Geographic Information System (QGIS) version 3.28.9-Firenze, with regional prevalence values, were classified using an equal-count (quantile) scheme. All statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (two-sided).

Ethical consideration

The required data for this study were obtained from the official DHS website: https://dhsprogram.com/data/dataset_admin/index.cfm?CFID. The protocol for DHS surveys was approved by the Ethical Review Committee (ERC) of the Ghana Health Service (GHS) and the ICF Institutional Review Board (IRB). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study utilized anonymized secondary data available in the public domain of DHS. The DHS program explicitly sought consent from survey respondents. However, the authors obtained approval from DHS for the reuse of the data. Since the data did not contain identifiable personal data, the DHS approved the reuse of this data and waived further ethical clearance for this study. The data was accessed on 25th August, 2025. This study adheres to the STROBE guidelines for reporting observational studies [31].

Result

Background characteristics of participants

Most of the participants (68.24%) were in the 20–34 years age group. Less than half of the women (46.71%) had secondary education. About 58.91% of the women were from rural settings. Six in ten women (62.25%) were Christians. About 32.11% of the women were from the poorest families. Most women (85.41%) were delivered in the health facilities. The majority of the women (70.38%) did not have a big challenge in accessing health facilities. The Southern zone of Ghana had the most (39.13%) participants in the survey. (Table 1).

Association of the participants’ characteristics with the delivery place

From the 5,189 respondents, the majority of the women (96.22%) who had their babies delivered at the health facilities were active subscribers of NHIS. More than half (55.8%) of the women who had secondary education had their babies delivered at health facilities. About 81.50% of mothers who were employed gave birth at the health facilities. Christian mothers were the majority (71.26%) who were delivered at the health facilities. Facility-based delivery was higher (51.97%) among mothers from urban areas. Health facility deliveries were higher (39.89%) among women in the Southern zone of Ghana. The poorest wealth class were the most (48.59%) people who were delivered at home. Mothers whose distance to the health facilities was “no big problem” (closer) were the majority (77.03%) who were delivered at the health facility. Women who went for four or more ANC visits were the majority (92.01%) who were delivered in the health facility. (Table 2).

Prevalence of facility-based delivery and national health insurance coverage among women in Ghana

The cumulative prevalence of facility-based delivery in Ghana was 86.49% [95% CI: 84.53–88.46] in the whole of Ghana. The highest facility-based delivery in Ghana were in Ahafo [94.36%; 95%CI: 91.29–97.44], Ashanti [93.01%; 95% CI: 89.69–96.34] and Upper East [97.96%; 95% CI: 96.46–99.45] regions, while Oti [68.28%; 95%CI: 59.59–76.97], Northern [71.50%; 95%CI: 60.00–83.94], Savannah [73.49%; 95%CI: 66.05–80.93] and Central [83.19%; 95%CI: 76.46–89.91] regions had lower deliveries. The cumulative rate of National health insurance coverage in Ghana was 90.13% [95%CI: 89.07–91.19]. All the regions in Ghana recorded high subscription of NHIS among women of reproductive age, with Bono [98.52; 95%CI: 97.47–99.58], Ahafo [95.34%; 95% CI: 93.68–96.99] and Upper East [96.75; 95%CI: 95.62–97.89] having the highest subscribers, whiles Savannah [85.76% 95%CI: 81.55–90.02], Oti [84.08%; 95%CI: 80.78–87.39], Central [83.52%; 95%CI: 79.13–92.01] and Greater Accra [86.56%; 95%CI: 82.77–90.35] having the lower rates of subscribers. Factors associated with facility-based delivery

All variables that were significantly associated with facility-based delivery in the univariate analysis were included in the multiple logistic regression model. After including all significant variables in multiple logistic regression and controlling for confounders, the factors that influenced facility-based delivery were health insurance status, residence, educational level, religion, wealth status, zone, and ANC visits. Women with NHIS were 93% more likely to give birth in a health facility than those without NHIS [AOR = 1.93, 95% CI = 1.38–2.70, p < 0.001]. Women in rural areas were 55% less likely to be delivered in health facilities [AOR = 0.45, 95% CI = 0.32–0.62, p < 0.001] compared to those from urban communities. Women with secondary education were 1.91 times more likely to give birth to their babies at health facilities compared to mothers with no formal education [AOR = 1.91, 95% CI = 1.45–2.52, p < 0.001]. Women with tertiary level education had an increased chance of giving birth at health facilities with reference to their uneducated counterparts [AOR = 3.44, 95% CI = 1.13–10.76, p < 0.030]. Mothers who practiced other religions (traditionalists, etc.) were 72% [AOR = 0.28, 95% CI = 0.17–0.46, p = 0.001] less likely to be delivered in the health facilities compared to Christians. Mothers from richer families had an elevated odds of being delivered at health facilities compared to those from the poorest families [AOR = 2.95, 95% CI = 1.61–5.42, p < 0.001]. Women from the richest families had an increased odds of delivery at the health facility [AOR = 2.96, 95% CI = 1.27–6.94, p = 0.012] than the poorest women. Mothers from the Southern zone of Ghana were less likely to give birth at health facilities than those from the Northern zone of Ghana [AOR = 0.50, 95% CI = 0.31–0.79, p = 0.003]. Women who go for four or more ANC visits during pregnancy were 4.63 times more likely to give birth at a health facility compared to women who do not go for ANC visits during pregnancy [AOR = 4.63, 95% CI = 2.45–8.76, p = 0.001]. Discussion

The cumulative facility-based delivery rate in Ghana was encouragingly of about 93%, which has shown an improvement from the previous rate of an average of 73% recorded in the 2014 GDHS [28, 33]. This has demonstrated that some of the policies put in place to increase facility-based deliveries are gradually yielding favourable results. Higher facility-based delivery was observed in the Ahafo, Ashanti, and Upper East regions. Conversely, the Upper East, as part of the northern regions of Ghana, had previously recorded a lower facility-based delivery rate of about 61.9% [34]. The Oti, Northern, and Savannah were the regions that had facility-based deliveries lower than 80%, which is consistent with [13]. The overall subscription rate to the NHIS in Ghana is significantly higher, of about 90.13%, with all the regions recording more than 80% coverage. These findings are consistent with [35]. The findings of this study indicate that facility-based delivery among reproductive-age women in Ghana was associated with health insurance, residence, education, religion, wealth quintiles, occupation, and antenatal care visits. These factors have been reported to be associated with facility-based delivery in other studies [23, 24, 33] conducted in Ghana, except that [33] did not estimate the influence of NHIS and occupation on facility-based delivery. Women from the richer and richest families were more likely to give birth in the health facilities compared to their counterparts from the poorest families. Noting the vital role NHIS plays in improving access to healthcare services, major discrepancies in access still exist, especially among low-income households and rural communities [36]. Low facility-based delivery rates among mothers without health insurance in rural areas could be associated with a complex interplay of socioeconomic, infrastructural, and cultural factors, as its' evidence by recent studies across various regions [36–39]. Financial constraints represent a primary barrier, as the absence of health insurance directly affects the affordability of hospital services in Ghana [2]. This financial burden can lead uninsured mothers to forego institutional delivery in favor of home births, even when such choices may carry higher risks for maternal and neonatal health [40, 41]. Studies from regions like the Democratic Republic of Congo and rural Ghana demonstrate that low health insurance coverage is largely attributable to financial constraints and a lack of especially among rural womenawareness of the benefits of health insurance [2, 42]. Individuals without health insurance are more likely to struggle with the costs of treatment, sometimes opting for low-value treatments or no treatment at all due to financial constraints [43]. Even with the “free maternal healthcare” policy, many women incur substantial out-of-pocket (OOP) payments, rendering healthcare not entirely free in practice [44]. These OOP payments, often stemming from informal fees or costs for unlisted services, act as a significant financial barrier, particularly for those without robust insurance or financial resources [45]. A study in two Ghanaian municipalities found significant OOP payments for maternal healthcare services, with a high prevalence of catastrophic OOP payments [44]. Furthermore, beyond direct costs, rural areas often suffer from inadequate healthcare infrastructure, including a scarcity of hospitals, clinics, and trained healthcare personnel [46, 47]. In this study, women from rural areas of Ghana were 55% less likely to have facility-based delivery. This claim is consistently reported in other studies conducted in Ghana [24, 48, 49]. And this is not limited to finance, but the scarcity of midwives and obstetric violence against rural women [50]. The fear of mistreatment can outweigh the perceived benefits of medical assistance, driving women back to the familiar and culturally sanctioned environment of home births attended by Traditional Birth Attendants (TBAs) [29]. Despite these concerns, a few nurses from rural areas agreed to work in the rural communities in Ghana [51]. Moreover, Poor road networks, long distances to health facilities, and inadequate transportation options make it challenging for pregnant women in rural areas to reach hospitals for delivery [52–54]. These infrastructural deficiencies are further exacerbated by unreliable electricity, making the delivery of medical supplies and services difficult [54]. This is more pronounced among the Fulani population in Ghana, who face significant healthcare and medicine access issues due to their nomadic lifestyle and limited integration into the formal healthcare system [52]. Again, this study has highlighted that women with secondary school and tertiary education were 1.91 and 3.44 times more likely to give birth in a health facility, respectively. Empirical evidence supports this claim, indicating that educated women prefer facility-based delivery due to the quality healthcare services they get from skilled health staff [55]. Educated women tend to have more trust in the efficiency and accuracy of care given to them in the hospital during and after delivery of their babies [56]. Furthermore, women who practiced African traditional religion apart from the dominant Christianity and Islam were less likely to give birth in the health facility. Research indicates that Christian and Muslim women in Ghana exhibit higher rates of health facility utilization for childbirth compared to their counterparts who adhere to African Traditional Religion [57]. The women from traditional religious backgrounds prefer home deliveries to facility-based delivery because their family members are not allowed into the labour wards during birth to give psychological support, and their perceived uncultured disposal of placentas after giving birth in the hospital [58].

Strengths and limitations

This study can be generalized to the entirety of Ghana, since the GDHS is a nationwide survey that covered all parts of the country. This study has also estimated the cumulative rates of facility delivery and NHIS coverage for Ghana; this will help policymakers and implementers to understand the performance of the NHIS policy and to make appropriate amendments. The study has also elucidated various factors that could hinder facility-based deliveries in Ghana, especially among rural women. Furthermore, the facility-based delivery rates and NHIS coverage were presented on choropleth maps for easy understanding by policy-makers. The study also has several limitations. Firstly, since this study is cross-sectional, it cannot establish causality. Secondly, the information was obtained through interviews, so recall bias might have occurred. Again, misclassification of participant characteristics may have occurred during data collection. Finally, the data did not contain socio-cultural variables for analysis.

Conclusion and recommendations

This study estimated significant facility-based deliveries in most regions of Ghana, as well as high rates of enrollment of women on the national health insurance. This study again elucidated that facility-based delivery among Ghanaian women was associated with NHIS, residence, education, religion, wealth quintiles, zone, and ANC visits. Women from rural communities in Ghana should be educated by health promotion officers on the importance of NHIS as well as on giving birth at the health facilities. All maternal care services should be incorporated in the NHIS, so poorer women can also get quality and free care at the health facilities. ANC services should be extended to the doorsteps of rural women who are in communities far away from health facilities by providing mobile clinics that render services at their various communities on some days within the week. Targeted health education programmes should be organized by the health promotion officers in communities in the Northern zone of Ghana to increase the awareness and importance of NHIS and health utilization among women of reproductive age. Further studies should focus on the maternal services that are still not free, why some women with active NHIS still underutilize health facilities for delivery, and the assessment of the current attitude of women who practice African traditional religions towards the acceptance of health facility delivery.
