KAT6B/A::KANSL1 uterine sarcoma – A case with recurrences following uterine morcellation and the development of primary cell line model
Authors: Lauren Chen, Jiahui Liu, Isabelle Nechala, Pooja Praveen Kumar, Carol Ewanowich, Melissa Levecchia, Christa Aubrey, Yangxin Fu, Cheng-Han Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKAT6B/A::KANSL1 uterine sarcoma has been recently recognized as a distinct type of uterine sarcoma displaying mixed endometrial stromal and smooth muscle features.Case presentationThis report includes two cases of KAT6B::KANSL1 uterine sarcoma with the first case presenting as a prolapsing uterine mass through the cervix. Histologic and molecular analysis performed on the biopsy confirmed the diagnosis of a KAT6B/A::KANSL1 uterine sarcoma. The second is a KAT6B::KANSL1 uterine sarcoma with multiple abdominal recurrences following uterine morcellation at hysterectomy for a presumed benign leiomyoma. Primary cell line models harboring KAT6B::KANSL1 were successfully derived from both cases using fresh tumor tissue, with the aim to determine on whether their growth is estrogen-independent given their consistent strong estrogen receptor (ER) expression. However, in contrast to the respective parental tumors that were strongly ER-positive, the primary cell lines lacked ER expression; hence these cell line models cannot be used to evaluate the effects of estradiol on tumor growth.ConclusionMorcellation may increase the risk of recurrence for KAT6B::KANSL1 uterine sarcoma. While it is feasible to establish primary cell lines from KAT6B::KANSL1 uterine sarcoma, the in vitro cell line models do not retain ER expression.

Introduction

Uterine sarcomas are malignant mesenchymal tumors of the uterus which represent about 5% of uterine malignancy (Hosh et al., 2016; Nordal and Thoresen, 1997). These tumors frequently present with symptoms that are clinically indistinguishable from the much more common uterine leiomyomas. Consequently, some are inappropriately managed with surgical procedure that result in iatrogenic dissemination of the sarcoma (Obstet. Gynecol., 2021; Halaska et al., 2020). as pre-operative distinction between benign and malignant uterine mesenchymal tumors remains a challenge (Lepoivre et al., 2025).

Though uncommon, uterine sarcomas are biologically heterogeneous and the major tumor types include smooth muscle tumor (leiomyosarcoma), endometrial stromal tumor (endometrial stromal sarcoma) and myomelanocytic tumor (malignant PEComa) (Giannini et al., 2023). The increased implementation of molecular diagnostic techniques, particularly next generation sequencing-based clinical panel testing have reshaped the classification of uterine sarcomas where disease-defining genetic alterations including genetic fusions have been identified. Among these, KAT6B/A::KANSL1 uterine sarcomas is a recently recognized tumor type characterized by histologic features intermediate between endometrial stromal tumor and smooth muscle tumor (Dundr et al., 2025a; Trecourt et al., 2023; Agaimy et al., 2022). Molecular studies further demonstrated that these tumors form distinct epigenetic (methylation) and gene expression cluster apart from other types of uterine mesenchymal tumors, supporting their classification as a distinct type of uterine mesenchymal neoplasm (Trecourt et al., 2023; Kommoss et al., 2024). Given its recent recognition, there is little known about how to manage recurrent/metastatic disease clinically. In this paper, we present two clinical cases of KAT6B/A::KANSL1 uterine sarcoma with the establishment and characterization of corresponding patient tumor derived cell line models.

Case reports

Case 1(GYNE90).

(GYNE90).

A 37-year-old previously healthy premenopausal woman presented with intermittent dizziness, menorrhagia and anemia (hemoglobin of 7.4 g/dL). She was found to have a 12.7 cm enlarged uterus containing multiple presumed fibroids. Subsequent physical examination showed a large prolapsing pedunculated mass protruding through the cervix that appeared to be fibroid-like clinically. The patient underwent a hysteroscopic myomectomy and a dilation and curettage, with a pedunculated 5.8 cm mass removed. Histopathologic analysis showed a tumor displaying mixed endometrial stromal and smooth muscle differentiation with diffuse and strong estrogen receptor (ER) expression (Fig. 1). Panel-based RNA-sequencing analysis demonstrated the presence of KAT6B::KANSL1 fusion, hence confirming the diagnosis of a KAT6B/A::KANSL1 uterine sarcoma. Staging computed tomography (CT) scan showed no evidence of disseminated disease. The patient underwent total abdominal hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy (TAH-BSO) which showed residual intramural tumor that was confined to the uterus. A portion of the tumor was submitted for tumor banking (Alberta Cancer Research Biobank; HREBA.CC-18-0112) and preclinical model development (HREBA.CC-21-0445) with the consent of the patient. Given the low stage nature of the disease and clear margins, no adjuvant therapy was administered. A 48-year-old woman with a prior history of cholecystectomy presented with progressive abdominal pain and bloating. Symptoms were insidious in onset but had gradually worsened, prompting radiological evaluation. CT scan showed a markedly enlarged uterine mass arising from the superior and anterior aspect of the uterine fundus measuring 24.5 cm in size, with a substantial centralized region of necrosis and evidence of hemorrhage (Fig. 2A). The radiographic appearance was interpreted as being consistent with a large uterine leiomyoma. The patient subsequently underwent total laparoscopic hysterectomy with bilateral salpingectomy. Uterine morcellation was initially performed with the intent to remove the uterus through the vagina but due the technical difficulty of removing the large uterine mass, a mini-laparotomy via a Pfannenstiel incision was performed to facilitate the removal of the morcellated uterus and fallopian tubes; hence the morcellation was not contained within a bag. Pathology of the extensively sampled fragmented/morcellated uterus/mass showed a histologically low-grade circumscribed mesenchymal neoplasm exhibiting endometrial stromal differentiation and a diagnosis of endometrial stromal nodule was favored (Fig. 2D). The patient presented three years later to the emergency department with progressively worsening right-sided abdominal pain over the past one year. CT imaging identified a 9 cm right lower quadrant intra-abdominal/mesenteric mass and a 4.8 cm mass within the abdominal wall at the infraumbilical midline incision site (Fig. 2B). These masses were resected and two distinct tumors, the first measuring 9 cm in size located within the mesentery adjacent to the resected small bowel and the second measuring 4.5 cm within the right rectus abdominis muscle. These tumors displayed histologic features that were identical to her 2022 uterine tumor and panel-based RNA-sequencing analysis demonstrated the same KAT6B::KANSL1 gene fusion from both the recurrent tumor and the uterine primary tumor that was resected three years ago. The diagnosis was revised to recurrent KAT6B::KANSL1 uterine sarcoma. The recurrent tumors were resected with negative surgical margins. Given that her recurrent tumor showed diffuse and strong estrogen receptor (ER) expression (Fig. 2E), the patient was started on an aromatase inhibitor - letrozole. However, subsequent imaging 3 months after the start of letrozole showed tumor recurrence with a 7.6 cm solid cystic enhancing mass within the lower midline abdomen right lower quadrant extending to the right adnexa (Fig. 2C). Surgical debulking 6 months after the start of letrozole revealed 10 foci of recurrent tumors ranging in size from 0.8 to 7 cm in the abdominal wall, omentum, pelvic side wall, anterior peritoneal and the small bowel mesentery. A portion of the recurrent tumor was submitted for tumor banking and preclinical model development with the consent of the patient. The patient remained on letrozole after the second recurrence surgery and imaging at 6 months post-op showed no new metastatic deposits.

The development and characterization of primary cell line models.

Primary cell lines (2D adherent model) were developed from GYNE90 and GYNE109, which were successfully carried to passages 16 and 12 respectively (Supplementary Material). The passaged cell lines (at passage 5) were molecularly confirmed to harbor KAT6B::KANSL1 fusion by reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) using the following forward and reverse primers - 5′-GCT TAG ATG GCA AAG GGG CAC CTC-3′ (forward) and 5′-GTG GCA CAC TCG TGG TCC ACA GC-3′ (reverse) (Fig. 3A) (Panagopoulos et al., 2015). GYNE90 showed an initial doubling time of 3.5 days, which slowed to 4.5 days beyond passage 10 and GYNE109 showed an initial doubling time of 4.5 days, which slowed to 6 days beyond passage 7. Both primary GYNE90 and GYNE109 cell lines displayed spindled cyto-morphology as adherent cells (Fig. 3B). Given that KAT6B/A::KANSL1 uterine sarcomas including GYNE90 and GYNE109 consistently display diffuse and strong ER expression (Fig. 1C and 2E), an important aim here was to determine whether its growth in vitro is enhanced by estradiol to provide biologic rationale for the use of letrozole clinically. However, both primary cell lines (passage 5) lacked appreciable ERα expression in vitro by western blot analysis (Fig. 3 C), despite attempts to develop the cell lines from patient tumor samples in phenol-free media with or without estradiol supplementation. In keeping with the lack of ER expression, the addition of increasing concentrations of estradiol did not result in significant changes in cell viability at day 3 compared to vehicle control (Fig. 3D). Discussion

KAT6B/A::KANSL1 uterine sarcoma is a recently recognized type of uterine sarcoma that can display mixed endometrial stromal and smooth muscle histology but is distinct from endometrial stromal tumor and leiomyoma respectively (Trecourt et al., 2023; Kommoss et al., 2024). It is worth noting that uterine tumors harboring this genetic rearrangement has been recognized since the early 2000 with the first molecularly confirmed report by Moore et al in 2004 (Moore et al., 2004). They described 3 cases of leiomyoma (2 cellular) showing t(10;17) with the breakpoint at 10q22 mapped to MORF (now known as KAT6B). In 2015, Panagopoulos et al used RNA-sequencing to delineate the exact nature of the genetic fusion in a retroperitoneal leiomyoma with t(10,17)(q22;q21) and identified KAT6B::KANSL1 fusion (Panagopoulos et al., 2015). This was followed by a subsequent case report in 2019 by Ainsworth et al that described a rapidly enlarging leiomyoma with KAT6B::KANSL1 in a postmenopausal woman (Ainsworth et al., 2019). Following that, a series of papers (2022–2025) reported 42 additional uterine sarcomas harboring either KAT6B::KANSL1 fusion (92%) or KAT6A::KANSL1 fusion (8%). Most cases were initially classified as endometrial stromal sarcomas with some classified as leiomyomas and some diagnosed descriptively as tumors with mixed endometrial stromal and smooth muscle histology (Dundr et al., 2025a; Trecourt et al., 2023; Agaimy et al., 2022; Dundr et al., 2025b).

It is evident from the historical context and the retrospective series that KAT6B/A::KANSL1 uterine sarcomas can display mixed endometrial stromal and smooth muscle histology. This brings up an important diagnostic consideration here. As exemplified in case 2, KAT6B/A::KANSL1 uterine sarcoma can resemble benign entities such as endometrial stromal nodule and uterine leiomyoma, particularly cellular or high cellular variants. Molecular analysis (i.e. RNA-sequencing) therefore should be considered in any patients experiencing recurrence after a diagnosis of endometrial stromal nodule and cellular/highly cellular uterine leiomyoma. Moreover, as the pathology community gain increasing appreciation for the histologic features of KAT6B/A::KANSL1 uterine sarcomas, molecular analysis should be judiciously performed at the time of biopsy or hysterectomy on cases displaying these histologic features given the malignant potential of KAT6B/A::KANSL1 uterine sarcoma. Based on the reported cases with available clinical follow-up (28 patients), 5 patients (18%) died of disease at 2 months, 10 months, 47 months, 72 months and 99 months after the initial diagnosis, and 7 patients (25%) were alive with disease with 16 patients (57%) showing no evidence of disease (Dundr et al., 2025a; Trecourt et al., 2023; Agaimy et al., 2022). The sites of metastasis and/or recurrence include intra-abdominal sites (small bowel, mesentery) and lungs. However, given the retrospective nature and the potential for tertiary center bias on these initial series, it is plausible that the real-world clinical behavior of KAT6B/A::KANSL1 uterine sarcomas may not be as aggressive and further population-based study with follow-up data is needed for provide more definitive insights.

In terms of its surgical management, myomectomy-type procedure is definitely inadequate as Agaimy et al. reported two patients with locally recurrent tumor who were initially treated with myomectomy-type procedure (Agaimy et al., 2022). Furthermore, given that this is a uterine sarcoma, morcellation-type procedure should be contraindicated as illustrated in case 2 where the patient experienced multiple intra-abdominal recurrences 2–3 years after morcellated hysterectomy. KAT6B/A::KANSL1 uterine sarcoma however can present with pre-operative radiologic findings that are inseparable from the much more common uterine fibroids. It is thus not possible at the present to know without pre-operative lesional tissue sampling in whom morcellation should be avoided. This remains an area where more reliable predictive algorithms and/or biologic markers including cell-free plasma DNA/RNA analysis are needed clinically (Lepoivre et al., 2025).

Given the rare nature of KAT6B/A::KANSL1 uterine sarcoma and the need for therapeutic insights for this recently recognized distinct entity, patient tumor-derived experimental models may be utilized to gain biologic and therapeutic insights. Even though it was technically feasible to develop primary cell lines that harbored the same KAT6B/A::KANSL1 fusion from the respective patient tumor samples in both cases, there were a number of limitations identified. Firstly, it appears that the process of cell culture either silenced the strong ER expression seen in the clinical tumor or preferentially selected ER-weak/negative subclones, despite attempts at culturing cells from patient tumor samples directly with phenol-free and estradiol-enriched cell culture protocol. Not surprisingly, the addition of estradiol did not affect the growth of tumor cells in GYNE90, as the tumor cells lacked ERα expression. However, the fact that KAT6B/A::KANSL1 uterine sarcoma cells can grow in the absence of ERα expression suggest that it is capable of continued proliferation in the absence of ERα-mediated cellular signaling. Secondly, given the typically low-grade histology of KAT6B/A::KANSL1 uterine sarcoma, the in vitro growth of the developed primary cell lines are relatively slow in the initial passages with a doubling time of 3.5–4.5 days and it slows down even more significantly to a doubling time of 4.5 to 6 days in later passages. This limits the utility of the 2D adherent in vitro models, particularly with regards to the application of high throughput drug screen and molecular screen (i.e. CRISPR) to gain therapeutic and biologic insights.

In summary, molecular diagnostics (RNA-sequencing) are important to aid in the recognition of KAT6B/A::KANSL1 uterine sarcomas. Morcellation may increase its risk of recurrence, hence it should be avoided if a KAT6B/A::KANSL1 uterine sarcoma is in the diagnostic differential. Moreover, in vitro primary cell line models can be successfully established from patient tumor tissue, albeit with a number of features identified that can limit the utility of these cell line models.
