Dual Lewis Acid–Base Molecular Cages Facilitate Cooperative N2 Activation: Insights from Theory
Authors: Holliness Nose, Fernando Ruipérez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractActivating molecular nitrogen (N2) under ambient conditions remains a major challenge. Dual Lewis acid–base molecular cages are investigated to cooperatively activate N2 using computational chemistry. Cages composed of Lewis acids (LA) and Lewis bases (LB) connected by a linker chain ([LA,LB]C) are analyzed via binding free energies, interaction and deformation energies, bond lengths, angles, and Wiberg bond indices and charge distributions. Two interaction arrangements emerge: 1) rigid systems with strong LA‐LB dative bonds that deform substantially upon N2 binding and do not act as frustrated Lewis pairs (FLPs), and 2) flexible cages with extended LA‐LB separations displaying typical FLP behavior. Borylene donors enable strong, stable N2 interactions, particularly in systems 6a and 7c, whereas Verkade's base is less effective. Structural flexibility and electronic tuning of LA/LB centers are fundamental for efficient activation. Natural bond orbital and quantum theory of atoms in molecules analyses reveal partially covalent electrostatic interactions at the acidic site and covalent interactions at the basic site. These results provide design principles for molecular cages for N2 activation.Keywords: computational chemistry, molecular cages, nitrogen activation

Introduction

According to the Paris Agreement, the production of green molecules is fundamental to the decrease of global warming to ≈1.5 °C.[ 1 ] At the heart of green molecules is green ammonia, which plays a vital role in fertilizer production, enhancing the agricultural sector and ensuring food security. Since its inception in the early twentieth century, the energy intensive Haber–Bosch process remains the predominant way of producing ammonia.[ 2 ] The process involves harsh temperatures of about 400–600 °C, high pressures of 150–350 atmospheres, and is accompanied by toxic metal‐based catalysts that require sophisticated synthetic processes.[ 3 , 4 ] The harsh conditions are due to the fact that the triple bond found in the nitrogen molecule is very stable and inert with bond dissociation energy of about 941 kJ mol−1, known to limit the rate of ammonia production.[ 5 ] In addition, nitrogen has a very high ionization energy and electron affinity of 15.5 and −1.9 eV, respectively, which increase its reaction energy gap.[ 6 , 7 , 8 ] Thus, exploring options for producing ammonia using less toxic catalysts is a logical next step in building a green economy and reducing global warming.[ 9 ]

Less toxic catalysts can be designed using the frustrated Lewis pair (FLP) concept introduced by Stephan and coworkers.[ 10 ] The combination of free Lewis acids (LA) and Lewis bases (LB), which are prevented from forming adducts due to steric hindrance from bulky substituents, displays fascinating catalytic behavior similar to that observed in metallic catalysts. In this system, the LA simulates the role of a metal center by accepting electron density from nitrogen lone pairs through σ‐bonding, while the LB facilitates π back‐donation into the nitrogen π* antibonding orbitals.[ 11 , 12 , 13 ] Recent efforts to achieve N2 activation using metal‐free strategies have increasingly focused on tricoordinate boranes as LA, which may enable a broader array of acid–base combinations in FLP chemistry. Although still in its infancy, this approach has shown early promise. A recent study demonstrated that diphenyldiazomethane reacts with B(C6F5)3 to form a fleeting intermediate resembling an N2‐stabilized complex, effectively positioning the nitrogen moiety between a borane LA and a carbene‐type base.[ 14 ] However, this adduct was thermodynamically unstable and prone to releasing dinitrogen. Other investigations have attempted to generate LA‐N2 complexes directly, as in the case of (N2)BF3, which was observed only under cryogenic (170 K) and high‐pressure (600 Torr) conditions.[ 15 ] Other examples include experimental investigations of borylene‐based FLPs, particularly those supported by cyclic alkyl(amino)carbenes (CAACs), such as the [(Dur)B(CAAC)] complexes.[ 16 , 17 , 18 ] Complementary computational studies on N‐heterocyclic carbenes (NHCs) have highlighted the importance of aromaticity in facilitating the activation process.[ 19 , 20 , 21 ] Given the inherently weak basicity of N2, successful binding requires highly electron‐deficient LA. Computational studies have explored such possibilities, identifying species like carboranes and perfluoroalkyl substituted boranes (e.g., B(SiMe3)3, B(CF3)3) as viable candidates capable of forming energetically favorable adducts, with binding energies of around 15 kcal mol−1.[ 22 , 23 , 24 ] These studies have also underscored the importance of molecular geometry, specifically, pyramidalization at the boron center, which can significantly increase Lewis acidity by lowering the reorganization energy and minimizing structural deformation upon adduct formation.[ 25 ] This structural feature has been linked to higher dissociation energies and greater complex stability.[ 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 ] Pyramidal boranes such as 9‐boratriptycene exemplify this concept, showing markedly stronger Lewis acidity compared to planar analogs.[ 31 ] Other cage‐like structures, such as boraadamantane and alaadamantane, have even been predicted to bind inert species like noble gases.[ 32 ] Further enhancement of acidity can be achieved via fluorination, due to the inductive electron‐withdrawing effects of fluorine,[ 31 , 33 ] or by introducing positive charges.[ 27 , 28 , 29 ] In a recent work, we have found that the LA captures the N2 molecule and determines the thermodynamics of the process, while LB promotes the activation by reducing the bond order.[ 34 ] From that work, the most promising LAs were found to be fluorinated 9‐boratriptycene, which shows a nonplanar geometry in the acidic site (boron), reducing the energy penalty brought by the geometric rearrangement in the coordination with N2, as it was previously observed in the work of Frenking and Timoshkin,[ 31 ] and the S‐substituted derivative of this acid, which combines the pyramidal geometry with a cationic sulfur atom that strongly enhances acidity by its electron‐withdrawing character.

In this work, the triptycene molecule was used as a LA unit. The boron (B), aluminum (Al), and gallium (Ga) that make up its acceptor atom increases its acidity due to the nature of the group 13 elements. About the base, increasing the nucleophilicity significantly enhances the activation, and Verkade's base and tris‐tert‐butylphosphine were highlighted as promising bases.[ 34 , 35 , 36 ] The Verkade's base, through its phosphorous donor atom, is a strong, nonionic superbase that is known to activate bonds, such as CN and SiC, especially when combined with LA, and has a great capacity to participate in chemical reactions under mild conditions.[ 37 , 38 ] For instance, the combination of this superbase with moderate to weak LA demonstrated the ability to heterolytically cleave H2, highlighting the potential of Verkade's base in FLP‐mediated small molecule activation.[ 39 ] Borylene is also an electron rich base and acts as a LB through its boron donor atom, and is known to activate small molecules such as dihydrogen.[ 40 ] Its reactivity stems from the ambiphilic nature of the boron center, which, due to its sp hybridization, features both an empty 2p orbital and a filled orbital enabling it to act simultaneously as a LA and LB, similar to the behavior of transition metals. Due to its high reactivity, borylene typically requires stabilization through coordination with LB, most commonly NHCs or CAACs. However, the electronic structure of borylenes constrains the choice of compatible LA and bases for N2 activation, posing a challenge to the development of alternative systems that match its activation potential while offering improved stability and design flexibility.

Molecular cages are adaptable supramolecular systems able to build complex systems and have been employed in different areas of research such as sensing or catalysis.[ 41 ] Embedding a borylene moiety, functioning as a LB, alongside a LA within a confined molecular cage while maintaining sufficient spatial separation to prevent mutual quenching, embodies the core principle of FLPs. This spatial preorganization offers a key advantage: the proximity of both reactive centers enables cooperative activation of small molecules such as N2, while preserving their individual reactivity by preventing direct adduct formation.

Our study proposes a novel class of frustrated Lewis pair cages composed of Lewis donor units (Verkade's and borylene) and Lewis acceptor moieties (triptycene and sulfur‐substituted triptycene), bridged by a variety of linker groups including acetylene, pyrrole, and cyclopentadiene. The linkers serve to spatially separate the Lewis acidic and basic sites, thereby preventing adduct formation and enabling the formation of effective FLP systems. The resulting molecular architectures are hypothesized to capture and activate molecular dinitrogen through synergistic electron donation and back‐donation mechanisms.[ 42 , 43 ] Prior studies have demonstrated the potential of cage‐like structures to encapsulate atoms and activate small molecules.[ 32 , 44 ] To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to explore FLP cages featuring pyrrole and cyclopentadiene linkers for the activation of dinitrogen. Building on these precedents and addressing existing challenges, the primary objective of this study is to identify the key factors governing and enhancing dinitrogen capture and activation by tailored LA and bases. To this end, a detailed computational analysis of the interactions between the FLP components and dinitrogen was carried out to elucidate the nature of the binding and predict favorable binding modes. These insights are expected to guide the rational design of improved LA‐LB combinations and FLP architectures with enhanced potential for subsequent dinitrogen reduction to ammonia.

Computational Details

Electronic structure calculations were performed using Gaussian 16 program.[ 45 ] Full geometry optimization was carried out for all the investigated species with a long‐range corrected hybrid functional containing an empirical Grimme D2 dispersion correction, ωB97XD,[ 46 ] together with the 6‐31G(d) basis set.[ 47 ] The nature of the stationary point was confirmed by frequency calculations. All calculations were carried out in gas phase. Single point energies and natural bond orbital calculations were calculated using the 6‐311++G(2df,2p) basis set.[ 48 ] The correction of the basis set superposition error is performed using the counterpoise method.[ 49 ] The Gibbs free energy values were corrected by using Equation (1), as proposed by Finkelstein et al, in order to allow for comparisons between vacuum and solution phase experiments.[ 50 ]

Molecular Models

To investigate N2 capture by molecular cages, we employed a series of 21 molecular models (see Figure 1 ) denoted as [LA,LB]C, where LA, LB, and C represent the Lewis acid, Lewis base, and connector units, respectively. These cages are organized into seven distinct groups (1–7), each combining two types of LB (Verkade's base and borylene), four different connector motifs derived from pyrrole, cyclopentadiene, and alkyl‐alkynyl chains, and a single acid triptycene‐based scaffold featuring three acidic centers (B, Al, and Ga). This design yields three variants per group, labeled as a (B), b (Al), and c (Ga).

Analysis of Bonding Interactions

To characterize the interactions between N2 and the molecular cage, natural bond orbital (NBO) calculations were carried out.[ 56 , 57 , 58 ] Key parameters analyzed include NBO atomic charges (Q), Wiberg bond indices, bond ionicity descriptors, donor–acceptor interactions, and second‐order interaction energies. The NBO‐based bond ionicity descriptor (i AB) quantifies the polarity of the bond between atoms A and B, as defined by

where c A and cB are the polarization coefficients corresponding to atoms A and B, respectively. The ionicity parameter varies continuously from i AB = −1 (purely ionic bond localized on atom B, cA=0) through i AB = 0 (purely covalent bond) to iAB=+1 (purely ionic bond localized on atom A, c B = 0). Values of |iAB|>0.95 are typically indicative of ionic Lewis structures.[ 59 ]

The quantum theory of atoms in molecules (QTAIM)[ 60 , 61 , 62 , 63 ] methodology was also used to further characterize the nature of the interactions, using the the AIMAll program.[ 64 ] According to this method, the bond critical point (BCP) defines the bond path connecting two atoms within a molecule, and its properties serve to characterize the nature of their interaction.[ 60 ] In this work, the interaction of N2 with the cage gives rise to three BCPs between the nitrogen atoms and the LA and LB, namely, LA—N (BCP1), N—N (BCP2), and LB—N (BCP3). The BCP parameters analyzed include the electron density (ρ), the Laplacian of the electron density, (∇2ρ), the kinetic (G), the potential (V), and the total (H) electron energy densities. These parameters provide information on the density of electrons in a given bond path. The negative value of ∇2 ρ denotes electron density concentration in the interatomic region of a bond, corresponding to covalent bonds, whereas a positive value indicates depletion of electron density usually attributed to noncovalent interactions. If the ∇2ρ is positive but H is negative, the interaction is classified as partially covalent. If ρ > 0.03 a.u. and H is negative, the interaction is electrostatic with partially covalent characteristics.[ 65 ]

Results and Discussion

In this study, we explore the ability of a set of 21 molecular cages, denoted as [LA,LB]C (see Figure 1), to effectively capture and activate molecular nitrogen (N2). First, we examine the structural properties of the cages, focusing on their geometric parameters alongside an assessment of their acidity and basicity. These fundamental characteristics provide insights into how the individual LA and LB components, as well as the nature of the connector unit (C), influence the overall cage architecture and reactivity potential.

Subsequently, we analyze the formation of the [LA‐N2‐LB]C complexes, where the interplay between N2 and the cage is studied by the evaluation of both geometric distortions and electronic structure modifications. This approach allows us to elucidate the factors governing N2 binding strength and the interaction nature within these systems. Through systematic comparison across the cage series, we aim to identify key design principles that optimize N2 capture.

The LA‐LB distance ranges between 1.856 and 2.281 Å for all the compounds containing the alkyl‐alkynyl connector (series 1, 2, and 6). In most of the cages, a weak interaction between the LA and LB is observed, as indicated by Wiberg bond indexes below 0.800. In contrast, in cages 1a, 2a, and 6a, the Wiberg indexes approach 1.000, indicative of a single covalent bond between the two fragments.

Cages containing pyrrole and cyclopentadiene connectors (series 3, 4, 5, and 7) feature LA‐LB distances of between 4.188 and 5.712 Å. The rigidity of the connectors prevent the interactions between the LA and the LB, which is reflected in lower Wiberg bond indexes, with values close to zero. In the case of 7b, probably the large acidity and basicity overcome the rigidity of cyclopentadiene, showing a short LA‐LB distance (2.280 Å) and WBI of 0.651, indicating the presence of a weak interaction. Thus, cages with alkyl‐alkynyl chains as connector are very flexible and cannot mantain LA and LB separated, while pyrrole and cyclopentadiene are very rigid and can keep LA and LB apart. This will be very relevant for the capture of N2, since the LA‐LB interaction must be overcome and this will be a penalty in the binding energy. The relationship between the LA‐LB interaction and the binding energy was observed in a recent research by Dai et al.[ 22 ] These authors investigated a FLP combining carbene and borenium (or borinium) cations to screen potential candidates for N2 activation via density functional theory calculations. The closed form (borenium) could be transformed to the open form (borinium), after breaking the donor–acceptor interaction between the boron and the carbene carbon, implying the importance of the B—C bond strength. In their study, they found out that compounds with smaller Laplacian bond order (LPO) exhibited a lower isomerization energy whereas those containing larger LBOs exhibited a higher isomerization energy. This work further highlights the direct correlation between the LPO and the donor–acceptor bond distances with the bond dissociation energy.[ 66 ] A similar trend was also observed by You et al., suggesting that strong bond formation results in a thermodynamically exothermic reaction.[ 67 , 68 ]

Another relevant geometrical feature is the degree of pyramidalization at the acidic or basic center in the LA or base, since it leads to increasing acidity and basicity due to a lowering in the reorganization and deformation energy.[ 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 ] This pyramidalization is traditionally assessed using angular deformation. A commonly employed measure is the deviation of the sum of the three internal C—X—C bond angles (where X = B, Al, and Ga, in the case of the LAs, and X = B and P in the case of the LBs) from the planar value of 270°, as described by Radhakrishnan[ 69 ] and later expanded by Gavrish.[ 70 ] In the present work, we use a direct approach by using the raw sum of these three bond angles (Θ=∑θi) as a measure of pyramidalization. This total angle sum provides an intuitive and easily interpretable metric: values close to 270° correspond to a planar geometry, while larger values indicate increasing pyramidal character (see Figure 3 ).

Definition of the pyramidalization of the acidic (X = B, Al, Ga) and basic (X = P, B) centers as the sum (Θ) of the three θi angles.

The sum of the pyramidalization angles are found to range between 315° and 368° for the LAs, and 264° and 369° for the LBs. These values indicate a pyramidalized environment except for 7a and 7c cages, which show a planar geometry around the borylene base. In general, Al‐ and Ga‐containing LAs show larger pyramidalization degree than B‐containing LAs.

Electrophilicity and Nucleophilicity of the Cages

The ability of the cages to capture a nitrogen molecule is governed by the acidity of the LA center and the basicity of the LB center. The acidity of LA was evaluated using FIA, the global electrophilicity index (ω), the variation in atomic charge upon complexation (ΔQ), and the occupation of its vacant np orbital (η np). In contrast, the basicity of LB was assessed through both the relative nucleophilicity ratio (ωk−/ωk+) and the PA. The impact of the structural linker connecting LA and LB was also considered.

As summarized in Table 1, FIA trends follow the order b > a > c in compound series 1–5 and a > b > c in series 6 and 7, where a, b, and c correspond to B, Al, and Ga centers, respectively. For example, in series 1, compound 1b has the highest FIA (−112.6 kcal mol−1), followed by 1a (−108.7 kcal mol−1) and 1c (−95.2 kcal mol−1). In series 6, 6a is the strongest acid (−119.3 kcal mol−1), ahead of 6b (−106.2 kcal mol−1) and 6c (−81.8 kcal mol−1). These trends are generally consistent across all series.

The geometry around the LA atom influences acidity. Typically, compounds with pyramidalization angle ΘLA values near 360° exhibit stronger acidity, as reflected in higher FIA values. For instance, 1b (ΘLA = 363.3°, FIA = −112.6 kcal mol−1) is more acidic than 1a (ΘLA = 346.7°, FIA = −108.7 kcal mol−1). This correlation holds in series 1–5. In contrast, this trend is not maintained in series 6 and 7. Orbital occupation analysis supports this assessment: lower np orbital populations at LA correlate with higher acidity. For example, compounds 3a–3c show ηnp≈0.11, indicating strong acidity. However, this trend does not always match FIA values; Al‐containing cages often display stronger acidity despite slightly higher orbital populations. This discrepancy underscores that FIA reflects a combination of electronic and geometric effects. In highly compact cages where LA and LB are in close proximity, η np is not reported due to significant bonding interaction between the centers. The global electrophilicity index ω further characterizes the electron‐accepting ability. B‐ and Ga‐based acceptors generally yield higher ω values. The corresponding ΔQ values also support this trend: larger charge shifts indicate stronger electron withdrawal at LA.

The basicity of the LB was initially assessed via the nucleophilicity ratio (ωk−/ωk+). Compounds featuring Verkade or borylene donors exhibit a broad range of nucleophilic strengths. For example, 5a (P donor) shows an exceptionally high ratio of 68.51, marking it as the strongest donor in the dataset. This pronounced nucleophilicity can attenuate the apparent acidity of the LA by donating electron density intramolecularly. Similarly, 2a and 6b display high nucleophilicity ratios (5.58 and 4.91, respectively), reinforcing the importance of LB identity and geometry in modulating reactivity. To complement this descriptor, PA values were analyzed to provide a thermodynamic measure of basicity. High PA values indicate stronger gas‐phase basicity. For instance, 7a (277.39 kcal mol−1), 6a (263.37 kcal mol−1), and 5a (253.17 kcal mol−1) show the highest PA values in the set, confirming their strong nucleophilic nature. Notably, 5a, which also has the highest nucleophilicity ratio, emerges as a particularly powerful donor. Interestingly, some compounds present mismatches between PA and ωk−/ωk+ values. For example, 2a has a relatively low PA (158.90 kcal mol−1) but a high nucleophilicity ratio (5.58), likely due to favorable orbital alignment or proximity effects. Conversely, 3a–3c exhibit high PA values (>248 kcal mol−1) despite near‐zero or negative nucleophilicity ratios. This suggests that while thermodynamically basic, these systems may be electronically or geometrically hindered from effective nucleophilic engagement in frontier orbital‐controlled reactions. Taken together, PA and ωk−/ωk+ offer complementary perspectives on nucleophilicity. While the former captures intrinsic basicity, the latter incorporates orbital contributions. Strong nucleophiles such as 5a, 6a, and 7a rank highly by both metrics and are likely to be the most reactive donor sites.

In summary, the analysis reveals that the strongest LA are those incorporating aluminum or boron as acceptor atoms, with 4b emerging as the most acidic species overall, showing the highest FIA (−134.2 kcal mol−1) and significant charge transfer (ΔQ = 0.527). Similarly, 2b also demonstrates high acidity (FIA = −116.3 kcal mol−1; ω = 2.629). Among borylene‐based cages, 3a and 3c show low η np occupations, consistent with strong acidity even if their FIA values are slightly lower. Regarding basicity, the most nucleophilic donors are found in 5a, 6a, and 7a, each combining high PA values with strong orbital‐based nucleophilicity. Overall, optimal acid–base pairing for applications such as small molecule activation would involve combining strongly acidic centers like those in 4b or 2b with geometrically compatible, moderately strong donors to balance reactivity and stability.

N2 Capture by Molecular Cages

The capture of small molecules, such as N2, can be facilitated by interactions with both Lewis acidic or Lewis basic sites. Interaction with a LA generally involves a σ‐type donation, where the nitrogen lone pair coordinates to the vacant orbital of the acid center. Conversely, the interaction with a LB typically occurs through π‐back‐donation, where electron density is donated from the base into the π* antibonding orbitals of N2.

The interaction of N2 with the cages is evaluated using several geometrical parameters of the [LA‐N2,LB]C complexes, as well as the binding, interaction and deformation energies.

The short LA—N and LB—N bond distances, ranging from ≈1.537 to 2.023 and 1.526 to 1.808 Å, respectively, confirm that the N2 molecule is effectively captured by the LA and LB within the molecular cage. These distances are consistent with the formation of LA‐N2‐LB complexes adopting azo‐like geometries, characterized by bent coordination and angles reminiscent of a double bond, as illustrated in Figure S3, Supporting Information. The optimized geometries can be found in Figure S2, Supporting Information.

Among all systems, compounds containing boron‐based LA centers (series a) consistently exhibit the shortest LA—N distances (R 1), such as 1.537 Å in 4a and 1.539 Å in 6a, along with the highest Wiberg bond indices (WBI1), reaching up to 0.846 in 4a. These metrics indicate stronger donor–acceptor interactions at the LA—N site and suggest that boron compounds have a superior capacity to capture N2. The N—N bond length (R3) varies notably across the series, from 1.210 Å in 5b to 1.366 Å in 2b, showing significant elongation relative to free N2 (1.0977 Å).[ 71 ] This elongation is indicative of bond weakening and is accompanied by a decrease in the WBI for the N—N bond (WBI3), which spans from 1.251 (4a) to 2.052 (5b). For instance, in complex 2b, the N—N bond is among the most extended at 1.366 Å, with a notably reduced WBI3 of 1.283, reflecting a high degree of activation through electron back‐donation from the LB.

The angular parameters LA—N—N (α 1) and LB—N—N (α2) also reflect differences in steric and electronic influences of the donor–acceptor pairs. For example, in complex 4b, the LA—N—N angle is relatively large at 129.9°, suggesting reduced steric hindrance around the LA site, whereas 2b exhibits a much smaller angle of 108.6°, indicative of more constrained coordination. Systems containing borylene LBs (series 6 and 7) show a broader range of angles; in 6b, for example, α 1 reaches 152.9°, reflecting the linearizing influence of the borylene donor. These angular trends underscore the geometric flexibility of FLP systems in accommodating N2 coordination.

Bond ionicity analysis provides additional insights into the electronic structure of these complexes. The LA—N bond ionicity (i 1) ranges from 0.459 in 6a to 0.626 in 2c, indicating significant variation depending on the electropositivity and π‐accepting ability of the LA. The highest ionicity in 2c corresponds to a gallium‐based LA paired with a strongly interacting LB, reflecting enhanced charge separation. In contrast, LB—N bond ionicity (i 2) values are generally lower (0.289–0.426), with the highest value found in 7c, suggesting moderate polarization at the basic site. The ionicity values for the N=N bonds further illustrate the bond polarization upon complexation. While σ‐bond ionicity (i 3σ ) remains relatively low (0.010 in 2a, 0.011 in 6a), the π‐bond ionicity (i3π) is significantly higher in certain complexes (0.170 in 5a, 0.157 in 5c), indicating preferential electron redistribution into the antibonding π* orbitals of N2 and reflecting partial activation.

Summarizing, boron‐based LAs and strongly donating LBs such as borylenes or Verkade‐type bases favor stronger binding, greater N—N activation, and more pronounced electronic polarization, which are essential parameters in the rational design of FLP systems aimed at nitrogen activation.

The binding energy of the N2 molecule with the cage reflects the stability of the complex and is defined as the Gibbs free energy difference (ΔGbin) in the following reaction

The interaction energy is the energy difference between the [LA‐N2‐LB]C complex (E comp) and the separated constituents in their frozen geometries as they appear in the complex (Ecagefroz and EN2froz), reflecting the pure energetic attraction (or repulsion) between constituents

Finally, the deformation energy (ΔE def) is the energy cost required to distort each component from its relaxed (optimized) geometry to the geometry it adopts within the complex

The total deformation energy is the sum of the deformation energy of each component (i = cage, N2)

The interplay between the LA‐LB distance (R) in the empty cages and the deformation energy (ΔEdef) upon N2 binding reveals structure–reactivity relationships that directly influence complex stability and binding energetics. Systems with short LA‐LB distances, typically under 2.5 Å, exhibit stronger intramolecular interactions prior to N2 complexation, as evidenced by elevated Wiberg bond indexes (WBI > 0.65), and generally require substantial structural reorganization to accommodate N2, resulting in higher deformation energies. For instance, in the flexible alkyl‐alkynyl cages (series 1 and 2), LA‐LB distances range from 2.017 to 2.484 Å, and WBI values are high (0.706–0.917), consistent with the presence of LA‐LB bonding in the unbound cages (see Table 1). After N2 complexation, these systems display significant deformation energies: 1a and 2a have ΔE def values of 105.6 and 107.6 kcal mol−1, respectively, despite favorable interaction energies (ΔEint < −90 kcal mol−1). Notably, 2b, with the highest FIA of −116.3 kcal mol−1 and a short R = 2.474 Å, shows the highest deformation energy among all cages (139.3 kcal mol−1), highlighting the penalty associated with breaking the preexisting LA‐LB interaction.

By contrast, rigid systems such as those in series 3, 4, 5, and 7, with pyrrole or cyclopentadiene connectors, exhibit LA‐LB distances in the 4.1–5.7 Å range, with WBI values near zero, indicating minimal LA‐LB interaction. This structural separation reduces the need for large geometric rearrangements upon N2 binding. For example, 3a–3c show deformation energies between 64.1 and 74.8 kcal mol−1, significantly lower than those of flexible systems like 1b or 2b, despite similarly unfavorable (or even weaker) interaction energies. An exception within the rigid series is 7b, where the LA‐LB distance is reduced to 2.280 Å and WBI increases to 0.65, suggesting partial LA‐LB bonding, possibly due to the high intrinsic acidity and basicity of the LA‐LB units overcoming the rigidity of the cyclopentadiene linker. Consequently, 7b exhibits a relatively high deformation energy of 72.1 kcal mol−1.

In summary, short LA‐LB distances in the empty cages correlate with higher deformation energies upon complexation due to the energetic cost of disrupting preexisting LA‐LB interactions. This is particularly problematic for flexible systems with alkyl‐alkynyl connectors, whereas rigid linkers better preserve cage geometry during binding, minimizing deformation penalties.

The computed ΔG bin values reveal that N2 binding is generally thermodynamically disfavored for most cages, with only 7c (−4.7 kcal mol−1) and 6a (−0.6 kcal mol−1) exhibiting favorable binding. This outcome reflects a balance between the stabilizing direct interactions with N2 (ΔEint) and the energetic cost of cage deformation required to accommodate the molecule (ΔE def). The former reflects the chemical affinity for N2, while the latter relates to structural strain and geometric preorganization. Thus, 7c exhibits the most favorable binding thermodynamics arising from a favorable interaction energy (−61.8 kcal mol−1) together with the lowest deformation energy in the series (49.8 kcal mol−1). This suggests an ideal combination of strong N2 affinity and preorganized geometry. In contrast, 6c has the highest ΔGbin (46.4 kcal mol−1), even though it has a moderately favorable ΔE int (−32.5 kcal mol−1), due to a relatively high deformation cost (73.0 kcal mol−1). This thermodynamic penalty underscores the importance of preorganization in stabilizing N2 adducts. Although a general trend can be observed in which lower deformation energies (ΔE def) are associated with more favorable binding free energies (ΔGbin), particularly within groups 3, 4, 5, and 7, this correlation is not strictly linear across all cages. In some cases, such as groups 1, 2, and 6, the binding free energy is also strongly influenced by the intrinsic interaction energy (ΔE int) between the N2 molecule and the LA/LB sites. Consequently, both ΔE def and ΔEint contribute to the overall thermodynamics of N2 binding, and variations in ΔEint can offset the effect of cage preorganization.

Interaction energies span a broad range (from −15.6 to −122.5 kcal mol−1), with more negative values correlating with higher cage electrophilicity (ω), higher Lewis acidity (more negative FIA), and appropriate bonding geometry. In this way, 2b, which has the most negative ΔE int (−122.5 kcal mol−1), also exhibits high electrophilicity (ω = 2.629) and a large FIA (−116.3 kcal mol−1), indicating a highly electron‐deficient LA center. However, its ΔG bin is unfavorable (22.6 kcal mol−1) due to a very high deformation energy (ΔEdef = 139.3 kcal mol−1), showing that strong interactions alone are insufficient for efficient N2 capture. Similarly, 6a combines a favorable ΔEint (−82.9 kcal mol−1) with a moderately high deformation energy (77.6 kcal mol−1), leading to a nearly thermoneutral binding energy (−0.6 kcal mol−1). Its short LA‐LB separation (1.856 Å) and the correspondent WBI (0.947) suggest a rather strong LA‐LB interaction that must be overcome to form the complex with N2. The influence of the connector may be also highlighted. Pyrrole‐based connectors (series 3) can also interact with N2 via hydrogen bonding, increasing the interaction strength.

These comparisons highlight that ΔE int trends track well with electrophilicity (ω) and FIA, but that thermodynamic favorability also requires control over structural distortion. Deformation energy, the cost of adapting the empty cage to its N2‐bound geometry, ranges from 49.7 kcal mol−1 (5c) to 139.3 kcal mol−1 (2b). Therefore binding free energy is determined by the balance between interaction and deformation energies. As such, some systems with strong interaction energies fail to bind N2 favorably due to high deformation penalties, such as 2a, 2b, or 2c, while others with more moderate interaction energies achieve favorable or near‐thermoneutral binding energy due to low deformation requirements and better geometry (6a and 7c). This highlights the utility of deformation energy as a primary design metric for optimizing cage‐based N2 capture systems. Given that the interaction energy is largely driven by electronic descriptors (ω, FIA), and deformation energy by geometric preorganization (LA‐LB distance, WBI, pyramidalization), simultaneous optimization of both structural and electronic features is essential.

The results reveal clear distinctions in bonding character between the LAs and LBs. At BCP1, corresponding to the LA—N interaction, the electron densities range from 0.061 to 0.166 au, with positive Laplacians (0.162–0.528 au), indicative of mainly electrostatic interactions. Despite the relatively low ρ values, the negative total energy densities (H < 0 in all cases) confirm that these interactions have a partial covalent nature, consistent with the role of LA as an electron‐deficient species accepting electron density from N2.

In contrast, the LB—N bonds (BCP2) exhibit generally higher electron densities (0.163–0.215 au) and, in many cases, negative Laplacians (series 1, 3, 5, and 7), and negative H values, suggesting a covalent character of the interaction. Notably, in systems such as 2b and 2c, the BCPs exhibit both high ρ (≈0.21 au) and slightly positive Laplacian values, reflecting highly polarized bonding, where electron donation from LB into N2 is substantial but does not fully reach covalency.

The N—N bond (BCP3) serves as a sensitive probe of N2 activation. For the majority of systems, ρ at BCP2 remains relatively high (0.467–0.511 a.u.) with highly negative Laplacians, consistent with a covalent bond. However, in systems like 2b, 2c, and 4a–4c, ρ drops significantly to 0.34–0.37 au, indicating substantial weakening of the N—N bond. This trend coincides with higher ρ values and enhanced donor interactions at BCP3, suggesting that LB donation into the π* orbitals of N2 plays a key role in activation. The corresponding H values also become less negative, reflecting increased N—N bond weakening.

In summary, the QTAIM descriptors confirm that the LA—N bonds are predominantly electrostatic in nature, while the LB—N bonds exhibit stronger donor–acceptor or partially covalent character. The degree of N2 activation correlates with enhanced electron donation from LB and is most pronounced in systems featuring high ρ at LB‐N (BCP3) and reduced electron density at the N—N BCP (BCP2).

NBO analysis further supports the presence of donor–acceptor interactions identified via QTAIM. The data reveal electron donation from the N2 unit not only into orbitals on the Lewis acidic centers (B, Al, Ga) but also into acceptor orbitals associated with the basic fragments and the molecular cage itself. These findings corroborate the delocalized nature of N2 activation across the framework. While B‐based systems favor more localized interactions, Al‐based cages exhibit stronger charge‐transfer character, and Ga complexes show a combination of both, reflecting their softer and more polarizable nature. Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive thermodynamic and structural analysis of molecular cages composed of LA and LB fragments linked by connector chains (C) ([LA,LB]C) interacting with dinitrogen (N2), focusing on binding free energies (ΔG bin), interaction (ΔEint), and deformation (ΔE def) energies, as well as structural and bonding descriptors such as LA—LB, LA—N, LB—N, and N—N distances, bond angles, WBI, and charge distribution.

Two primary interaction motifs were identified depending on the flexibility of the connector chain: 1) systems with strong dative LA‐LB bonds (1a, 2a, and 6a) showing short LA‐LB distances (≈2.0–2.5 AA) and high WBIs (≥0.7), which undergo substantial structural distortion (ΔE def > 100 kcal mol−1) and are not considered FLPs, and 2) cages with larger LA‐LB distances (>4.5 Å) and negligible bonding (3a, 5a, and 7a), where FLP behavior dominates. The strength and nature of N2 activation are modulated by the acidity of the acceptor (B > Al, Ga), the nature of the donor (LB) and the flexibility of the connector (C). Borylene donors (series 6 and 7) consistently support stronger and more stable N2 interaction, as seen in the favorable geometries and bonding of systems like 6a and 7c. In contrast, Verkade's base donors (series 1–5) generally yield weaker binding and less favorable thermodynamics.

Structurally, boron‐centered cages induce greater pyramidalization and deformation, which, despite the energetic cost, facilitates stronger N2 activation. Borylene donors (series 6 and 7) consistently offer more stable and effective N2 interaction, evidenced by favorable bond distances, Wiberg indices, and complexation energies. Alkyl‐alkynyl connectors emerge as promising motifs due to their flexibility and electronic tunability. From a thermodynamic perspective, borylene donors provide the most promising N2 capture, especially in less strained variants (6a, 7c), while Verkade's base donors generally show weaker thermodynamic profiles. Variants with less steric bulk and lower deformation energies outperform bulkier or more strained analogs.

Frontier orbital descriptors, such us the electrophilicity index (ω) and the nucleophilicity ratio (ωk−/ωk+), confirm that highly electrophilic cages, particularly boron‐based systems, correlate with enhanced fluoride and PA, underscoring their reactivity potential. NBO and QTAIM analyses further reveal that N2 binding involves partially covalent electrostatic interactions at the acceptor and covalent stabilization by the donor, with significant electron density redistribution facilitating bonding activation.

Design implications emerging from these results include prioritizing borylene‐based donors with minimal cage deformation, avoiding overly rigid or bulky linkers that increase strain, and tuning electronic properties of LA and LB centers to mimic the optimal configurations observed in systems like 7c. Overall, this work highlights how strategic variation of donor/acceptor fragments and linker design enables fine control over electronic structure, deformation, and interaction strength, providing a rational foundation for engineering molecular cages capable of effective dinitrogen activation.
