Self‐Interfering Magnetic Dipole Transition Emission in Rare‐Earth Ions Doped Nanoparticles Modulated by Plasmonic Mirror Nanocavity
Authors: Chengxiang Gou, Bowen Kang, Wangze Li, Xiangtai Xi, Yuting Luo, Jiaqi Wang, Zhengkun Fu, Huan Chen, Zhenglong Zhang, Hairong Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn conventional photonics, light–matter interactions are predominantly governed by electric dipole (ED) transition. Although magnetic dipole (MD) transition emission is challenging to detect due to its intrinsic properties, it exhibited significant application potential in biological probes and quantum technologies. Eu3+ ions are considered an ideal system for investigating MD–ED transition emissions owing to their unique energy‐level structure as sensitive nanoscale probes for electromagnetic fields. In this paper, a mirror plasmonic nanocavity combining Au nanospheres (NSs) with an Au flake is applied to modulate both ED and MD emissions from Eu3+‐doped NaYF4 nanoparticles (NPs). The structure of the mirror nanocavity is systematically adjusted and precisely controlled through the nanoscale manipulation capability of atomic force microscopy (AFM). The emission of MD transition is enhanced as 3‐fold on Au flake through the construction of a monolayer Eu3+ ions doped NPs only by self‐interference effect. By coupled Au NSs and flake, the intensity of ED transition emissions was restored to 481 times its original magnitude through the utilization of localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) effects. A significant enhancement of Eu3+ ions luminescence and artificial modulation of emission ratios are achieved. This tunable platform benefits for detection and manipulation of light–matter interactions at nanoscale.

Introduction

Substantial research efforts in traditional photonics were devoted to controlling and enhancing the spontaneous emission process of electric dipole (ED) transitions [1, 2]. Across optical frequency ranges, the interactions between light and matter were predominantly dictated by the material’s response to an applied electric field [3]. It was widely accepted that electric fields were the primary mediators of light–matter interactions, whereas the Lorentz force generated by optical‐frequency magnetic fields was considered to exert a negligible influence [4, 5]. Due to the fine‐structure constant (α ≈ 1/137), the magnetic dipole (MD) transition emissions in optical materials were significantly weaker than ED transition emissions [6, 7]. Experimental efforts to detect magnetic fields at optical frequencies were severely hindered by an interaction strength with matter. Nevertheless, the ability to engineer a substantial enhancement in the emission rates of such weak sources was understood to hold considerable value for numerous applications, encompassing advanced displays, fluorescent biomarkers, and sources of quantum light. Furthermore, MD emitters were successfully implemented as effective substitutes for conventional near‐field probes in the spatial mapping of optical magnetic fields, applicable to laser beams, plasmonic nanostructures, optical antennas, and metamaterials. Consequently, the precise engineering of light–matter interactions mediated by MD transitions was confirmed to be of dual significance, encompassing both fundamental scientific inquiry and practical technological applications [8, 9, 10, 11, 12].

The significance of magnetic interactions was largely confined to materials with symmetry‐forbidden ED channels as exemplified by intraconfigurational transitions in certain transition‐metal systems (characterized by md n electronic configurations) and lanthanides (with 4f n configurations) [13, 14, 15]. Eu3+ ions were regarded as exemplary systems for the study of MD emission. A strong correlation was observed between the emission behavior and the point symmetry of the host lattice site. Incorporation of the ions into low‐symmetry noncentrosymmetric lattice sites resulted in the ED transition predominating the spectrum, which was noted for its acute sensitivity to the local chemical milieu of the luminescent center. The occupancy of centrosymmetric lattice sites by Eu3+ ions resulted in the MD transition becoming the dominant radiative pathway, which was characterized by its enhanced robustness against perturbations in the local crystal field [16, 17, 18, 19]. Therefore, the ratio of ED to MD emission intensity could be employed as a sensitive probe for tracking microenvironmental dynamics in the host material [14, 20, 21]. It was reported that the intensity of electromagnetic fields near metallic films or nanostructures could be quantified through luminescence measurements using Eu3+ ions. This approach exploited the distinct responses of ED and MD transitions to the local electromagnetic environment, enabling their relative intensities to serve as an intrinsic nanoscale probe [22, 23, 24].

The photonic environment emerged as a principal factor dictating the characteristics of light–matter interactions in lanthanide ions because of Purcell effect [25, 26]. A critical finding was that MD emitters shared a critical dependence on the photonic environment with ED emitters, extending beyond their intrinsic properties. This external influence was governed by distinct variants of the Purcell effect: the magnetic Purcell effect for MD transitions and its electric counterpart for ED transitions [10, 27, 28, 29]. A substantial research focus during the past decade was placed on the manipulation of spontaneous emission from both ED and MD transitions, achieved through the strategic implementation of resonant cavities [30, 31, 32, 33] and optical antennas [34, 35, 36, 37]. In the last few years, Montagnac et al. [38] achieved modulation of the spatial distribution of ED and MD emissions of Eu3+ ions using Si nanoantennas, through switching the laser polarization state. Bashiri et al. [39] achieved directional control of the fluorescence emission from Eu3+ complexes at MD (590 nm) and ED (610 nm) by employing a symmetry‐broken metasurface constructed from titanium dioxide nano‐resonators. Reynier et al. [40] demonstrated the selective excitation of Y2O3:Eu3+ nanoparticles (NPs) through the magnetic field of a localized plasmon. Achieved by employing a plasmonic nanoantenna., Gangrskaia et al. [41] reported a 3.0–4.5‐fold selective enhancement of the MD transition in Y2O3:Eu3+ thin file integrating in the cylindrical metallic antenna and exciting with an azimuthally polarized beam. The confinement of optical fields to deeply subwavelength volumes by plasmonic nanostructures was achieved, yielding a massive enhancement of the electromagnetic field intensity and the local density of optical states (LDOS). Subsequent studies established that metal‐based cavities and nanospheres (NSs) dimers provided a viable method for tuning the emission spectra of ED emitters through controlled alterations of the spectral profile of the LDOS [42, 43, 44]. In this paper, the study presents a tunable plasmonic nanostructure composed of Au nanospheres (NSs) and an Au flake, designed to effectively modulate multiple ED and MD transition emission from Eu3+ ions doped NaYF4 NPs. The research demonstrates how distinct self‐interference effects between ED and MD transitions can be exploited for spectral modulation of NaYF4:Eu3+ emission. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) was employed as a nanomanipulation tool for precise positioning and assembly of nanoscale particles across diverse substrates [45, 46]. The emitters were positioned at controlled distances from steerable Au NSs to systematically modulate both electric and magnetic LDOS, enabling investigation of spontaneous emission rate variations in NaYF4:Eu3+ NPs. This competition between radiative decay channels (ED vs. MD) governed the relative emission intensities of transitions originating from identical excited states. Furthermore, the developed versatile platform permits scalable expansion to more complex nanostructures, where selective enhancement of either MD or ED transitions can be achieved through Purcell effect engineering. Through quantitative analysis of the relative intensities between spectrally adjacent transitions, the spatial distribution of optical modes within the system could be mapped. This approach offers a powerful means to nanoscale display, visualize nanoscale field distributions and their influence on quantum emitter behavior.

Results and Discussion

Figure 1c shows the energy‐level diagram of Eu3+ ions and the excited‐state decay pathways relevant to the experiments. A 532 nm excitation wavelength was selected, corresponding to the ED resonance transition from the 7F1 ground state to the 5D1 excited state (green arrow). The overall optical path was designed in an inverted excitation configuration (see Supporting Information S1, Figure S2). Following excitation, Eu3+ ions can decay down the ladder of energy levels by nonradiative relaxation pathways which was indicated with a black curve in Figure 1c. An exponential dependence on the phonon energy of the host material was observed for the rate of nonradiative multiphonon relaxation [1]. All these transitions were interconnected through the common 5D0 excited state. As a result, the suppression of one specific decay pathway was found to enhance the probability of radiative decay via the alternative channels [17].

A fundamental understanding of the physical principles regulating ED and MD transitions in Eu3+ ions has long been established as critically important. The initial observation of MD transition emission in optical systems was recorded approximately in 1940, through wide‐angle interference experiments employing Eu3+ ions [47]. A distinct environmental sensitivity was observed between transition types: the ED transition (5D0→7F2) demonstrated hypersensitivity to temperature and local coordination, whereas the MD transition (5D0→7F1) remained environmentally robust. ED and MD transitions represent distinct light–matter interaction mechanisms. These processes are mediated through oscillations of the ED moment for ED transitions and the MD moment for MD transitions, respectively [48].

The light–matter interaction between a charge‐neutral quantum system and an electromagnetic field was able to be described by a multipole expansion of the underlying interaction Hamiltonian [49]. (1) H=−p·E(t)−m·B(t)−[Q∇]·E(t)−⋯, where p is the ED moment, m is the MD moment and Q is the electric quadrupole moment as a tensor. From a quantum electrodynamics perspective, ED and MD transitions represent distinct coupling pathways between electromagnetic fields and atomic systems—the former arising from charge displacement ( p·E coupling) and the latter from current loops ( m·B coupling). The different terms of the interaction Hamiltonian lead to different selection rules, and their magnitudes depend on the size of the quantum system. In an atomic system with the Bohr radius a 0, the ED magnitude is given by p = ea 0 , where e is the elementary charge. The MD magnitude is given by the Bohr magneton μB = ℏe/2me , where me is the electron mass. In free space, the magnetic field of a plane wave satisfies B = E/c, where c is the speed of light. As a result, the MD interaction is weaker than its electric counterpart by a factor of ℏ/(mca 0 ) = 1/137 = α [16]. From Figure 1c, it was observed that the common ED and MD transitions of Eu3+ ions were mutually coupled through the excited state 5D0. It followed that the inhibition of one transition resulted in an elevated probability of radiative decay through competing pathways. An ideal platform for studying this coupling is an emitter in proximity to a planar metal surface, a configuration in which the Purcell effect modifies the radiative decay rate of emitter. Physically, this modification is fundamentally a classical phenomenon arising from the self‐interference of the emitter with its own field reflected back to its origin.

A monolayer of NaYF4:Eu3+ NPs was uniformly deposited onto an Au flake to enable a systematic investigation of the self‐interference mediated modulation of the ED (5D0→7F2) and MD (5D0→7F1) transition intensities in Eu3+ as shown in Figure 2a. The supporting Au flake, synthesized through a wet‐chemical approach, was measured to have a thickness of 80 nm and demonstrated a surface roughness of approximately 0.5 nm, indicative of near‐atomic flatness as shown in Supporting Information S1: Figure S3. The monolayer of NaYF4:Eu3+ NPs deposited on the Au flake was synthesized through high‐temperature thermal decomposition, with an average particle size of 12 nm and a doping concentration of 20%. The dashed frame in Figure 2a indicates the region magnified in Figure 2d, where the uniformity of the monolayer is clearly delineated. A smooth monolayer film of NaYF4:Eu3+ NPs was achieved by spin‐coating, with the precise thickness being subsequently verified by AFM characterization as presented in Supporting Information S1: Figure S4. Furthermore, the crystalline structure of the NPs was confirmed by X‐ray diffraction (XRD) as shown in Supporting Information S1: Figure S5, with results consistent with the standard NaYF4:Eu3+ crystal structure.

The distinct symmetry and polarization characteristics of ED and MD emissions were proved. Although both emissions were found to follow the sin2θ radiation pattern characteristic of point dipoles, their electric and magnetic field vectors were demonstrated to be interchanged [14]. The distinct field symmetries of ED and MD gave rise to complementary modifications, where the enhancement of one transition type typically occurred concurrently with the suppression of the other. Figure 2b shows that the electric field of an ED transition was symmetric under mirror reflection, in contrast to its magnetic field, which was antisymmetric, a fundamental difference that originated from their respective source characteristics [17]. A reciprocal symmetry relationship was observed for MD radiation, wherein the magnetic field maintained symmetry and the electric field exhibited antisymmetry under mirror transformation. Under normal incidence, a π‐phase shift, characteristic of a half‐wave loss, was introduced in the electric field component upon its reflection at the air–metal interface [17]. Given that the emission wavelength λ of the NaYF4:Eu3+ NP monolayer substantially exceeded its separation distance D from the Au flake ( D << λ), only single‐reflection events in the near‐interface region required consideration. Accordingly, as anticipated by the initial theoretical framework, the MD transition emission from Eu3+ ions was enhanced via constructive interference at the Au flake interface, whereas the ED transition emission was suppressed due to destructive interference. This interference behavior was further elucidated using the conceptual model of image dipoles, as schematically represented in Figure 2c [13]. According to the image dipole model, ED exhibited contrasting behaviors depending on their orientation relative to a mirror interface: parallel dipoles experienced suppression, while perpendicular dipoles showed enhancement in their transition probabilities. This trend was determined to be reversed for the case of a MD near the same boundary. Therefore, when spectroscopic measurements were conducted along the surface normal, an enhancement of MD transition intensity was observed alongside attenuation of ED transition intensity. This effect originated from the phase‐dependent interference between the direct emission and the field from its image dipole.

To verify the effect on the ED and MD transition emissions of Eu3+ ions, a 532 nm continuous‐wave laser was employed to excite a monolayer of NaYF4:Eu3+ NPs deposited on both Au flakes and Si substrates and the experimental results are presented in Figure 2e. A comparative analysis revealed that when NaYF4:Eu3+ NPs were placed on Au flake; significant modifications were observed in both the fluorescence peak intensities and the integrated intensity ratio between the ED (5D0→7F2) and MD (5D0→7F1) transitions compared to those on Si substrate. Specifically, the integrated emission intensity of the MD transition was enhanced by a factor of 2.8, whereas that of the ED transition was reduced to 0.9 times its original value. Since the excitation wavelength was 532 nm, the emission of Eu3+ ions was predominantly governed by the ED transition from the 7F2 state. Due to the self‐interference effect, not only was the ED emission suppressed near the Au flake but far‐field ED excitation was also inhibited. Additionally, the Au flake itself exhibited stronger background fluorescence. Consequently, as shown in Figure 2e, the emission spectrum of Eu3+ near the Au flake demonstrated significantly higher noise levels compared to that on the Si substrate. Although Eu3+ ions exhibit significant sensitivity to the local coordination environment, this behavior is exclusively observed in the hypersensitive electric dipole transition of 5D0→7F2. The intensities of other electric dipole transitions, such as 5D0→7F4, remain largely unchanged. This consistency was demonstrated by the minimal variations observed in the corresponding spectral emission profiles. Regarding self‐interference effects, both the nature of the dipole and the wavelength are influential factors. In Supporting Information S1: S6, self‐interference effects will be discussed in detail to explain the emission phenomena across different transitions. The spectral comparison confirmed two theoretical predictions: (1) Owing to the distinct field symmetries of ED and MD transitions, their decay rates exhibited a negative correlation. (2) Due to self‐interference effects, when compared to the reference case of a Si substrate without reflective metallic surfaces, the MD emission of Eu3+ ions on Au flake was significantly enhanced, whereas the ED emission was attenuated. As shown in Figure 2f, the integrated intensity of the ED transition at 610 nm (I610) from Eu3+ ions was normalized to the corresponding MD transition intensity (I590) for different substrates.

Previous studies have demonstrated the manipulation of ED and MD emissions from Eu3+ ions through self‐interference effects at Au‐flake‐based metallic mirror surfaces. In these systems, Eu3+ ions doped emitters exhibited strong intrinsic MD transitions that could dominate over ED transitions. The currently established model involves emitters near planar metal surfaces, where both ED and MD radiative decay rates are modified by the Purcell effect according to the emitter‐surface distance [1, 10, 37]. The LSPR effect of noble metals has been extensively utilized to modulate luminescence in Eu3+ ions doped NPs. Through modification of the local electromagnetic environment of emitters via LSPR, spontaneous emission rates can be effectively tuned, enabling precise luminescence control. To systematically investigate the combined effects of plasmonic interactions and mirror symmetry on ED and MD emissions, varying quantities of Au NSs were strategically deposited on Au flake surfaces. These Au NSs serve dual functions: not only forming nanocavities with the underlying flakes but also disrupting the intrinsic mirror symmetry of the planar Au surfaces. Moreover, through precise control of the interparticle spacing between Au NS dimers, dynamic tuning of the LSPR effects was achieved, enabling in situ modulation of the dipole emissions from Eu3+ ions.

The fluorescence spectrum of monolayer NaYF4:Eu3+ NPs on Au flake was established as the reference spectrum (Figure 3d‐i), exhibiting characteristic spectral features consistent with previous results in Figure 2 where the MD transition emission was weaker than the ED transition emission, yielding an integrated intensity ratio I610/I590 of 1.0. Upon excitation at the Au NS location marked by the dashed frame in Figure 3a, significant spectral modifications were observed in the corresponding spectrum (Figure 3d‐ii), including not only substantial emission enhancement but also a remarkable increase in the ED/MD transition intensity ratio to approximately 1.5. Detailed analysis revealed comprehensive emission enhancement across all energy levels, with the ED transition exhibiting significantly stronger enhancement compared to the MD transition. Since the fluorescence enhancement area of the nanocavity was small relative to the area of laser excitation, the average enhancement factor (EF) of the fluorescence emission intensity from the nanocavity coupled ED and MD transition emissions relative to those on the Si substrate was quantified according (2) EF=INS−INPsINPs×NspotNNS, where INS is the specific emission (ED or MD) intensity from monolayer NaYF4:Eu3+ NPs with a single Au NS under an excitation spot with a diameter of 1 μm and INP is the emission intensity from monolayer NaYF4:Eu3+ NPs without a single Au NS under the same laser excitation. The substrate in each condition is Au flakes. Nspot is the number of the NPs within excitation laser spot about 3846, and NNS is the number of the NPs between the Au NS and Au flake about 18. It was found that the fluorescence emission intensity of MD (590 nm), ED (610 nm) and ED (590 nm) were enhanced by factors of 203.7, 399.0 and 381.6 relative to Au flake (without Au NS). These spectral modifications, including both the I610/I590 ratio variation and corresponding energy‐level specific changes, were systematically compared and presented in Figure 3e, demonstrating the pronounced effects of localized surface plasmonic enhancement on the dipole emission characteristics of Eu3+ ions.

The observed spectral differences between Figure 3d‐ii and 3d‐i were attributed to the introduction of a single Au NS, suggesting that the local spectrum should match the reference spectrum upon Au NS removal. This hypothesis was verified through precise nanomanipulation using AFM where the Au NS in the dashed square of Figure 3a was relocated along the red dashed arrow as shown in Figure 3b. Subsequent in situ spectral measurements at the original position after NP removal revealed nearly identical spectra to the reference as demonstrated in Figure 3d‐iii. The minor variation in 610 nm (ED) fluorescence emission was attributed to the disruption of symmetry in the monolayer Eu3+ ions doped NP array caused by the removal of Au NS, which consequently weakened the suppression of ED transition emissions through self‐interference effects. Controlling experiments conducted on Si substrates under identical conditions showed no spectral variations regardless of Au NS presence (Supporting Information S1: Figure S8), confirming that the plasmonic effects originated specifically from the Au NS‐Au flake hybrid system. Furthermore, finite element method (FEM) simulations comparing the electromagnetic field distribution of a single Au NS on a Si substrate with that of an isolated Au NS reveal that, unlike the Au flake, the Si substrate does not effectively modify the local electromagnetic environment as demonstrated in Supporting Information S1: Figure S9. These results demonstrate that a single Au NS on Au flake can generate nanocavity‐induced LSPR effects along its periphery, leading to overall fluorescence enhancement of Eu3+ ions. The FEM simulations presented in Figure 3f revealed the electric field enhancement factors beneath individual Au NS. Additional electromagnetic field distribution simulations for a single Au NS on an Au flake at the excitation wavelength (532 nm) and emission wavelengths (590, 610, and 690 nm) were performed. The results clarify that the fluorescence enhancement of Eu3+ ions by a single Au NS primarily originates from an excitation enhancement mechanism. Detailed results are presented in Supporting Information S1: Figure S10. The collective results were found to conclusively validate the initial theoretical analysis.

In addition to the LSPR‐mediated fluorescence enhancement of Eu3+ ions, the modified emission characteristics should also be attributed to the symmetry‐breaking‐induced attenuation of self‐interference effects near the Au flake surface. The introduction of Au NSs disrupts the mirror symmetry of the Au flake, leading to significant modifications in both MD and ED transition emissions of Eu3+ ions. This symmetry‐breaking effect results in weakened self‐interference at the metal‐dielectric interface, which consequently alters the relative emission intensities between MD and ED transitions. The weakening of self‐interference effects can also be verified by depositing non‐uniform multilayers of NaYF4:Eu3+ NPs on the Au flake surface, as shown in Supporting Information S1: Figure S11.

The nanomanipulation capability of AFM was utilized to precisely control the interparticle spacing between Au NS dimers, enabling investigation of the localized surface plasmonic effects on both electric dipole and magnetic dipole transitions of Eu3+ ions. This is attributed to the misalignment between the scattering resonance of the small Au NS and the emission peaks of the ED and MD transitions. In contrast, the nanocavity composed of a tunable Au NS dimer and an Au flake allowed precise control over the plasmonic interactions.

To further elucidate the photon dipole transition radiation process of Eu3+ ions under localized surface plasmon regulation, the transition branching ratio was introduced to investigate the redistribution of emission channels influenced by the photonic LDOS, which was expressed by the following formula [11, 35]: (3) βi=IiItot=ΓiradΓtotrad, where Ii is the integrated intensity of the transition labeled i (MD or ED), Itot is the sum of the three contributions, Γirad is the radiative emission rate of the transition labeled i and Γtotrad is the sum of the radiative emission rates of each transition. Thus, the relative LDOS experienced by the ED and MD transitions could be quantitatively determined as follows [11, 35]: (4) ρED,MD=βED,MD·fMD,EDβED,MD·fMD,ED+βMD,ED·fED,MD, where ρED,MD are the electric or magnetic relative radiative LDOS and fED,MD are the oscillator strengths for the electric or magnetic transitions, respectively. The value of the branching ratio βED,MD measured at large distance to the Au flake was used as an estimate of fED,MD. This method enabled the correlation between spectrally resolved emission intensities in steady‐state photoluminescence experiments and the electric and magnetic LDOS. In Supporting Information S1: Figure S14a, the energy levels of Eu3+ ions were simplified to a four‐level system for theoretical analysis. Supporting Information S1: Figure S14b presents the variation in branching ratios between the 5D0→7F2 ED transition and the 5D0→7F1 MD transition as a function of decreasing interparticle spacing in Au NS dimers, with experimental data derived from Figure 4. A linear correlation was observed between the interparticle distance and the transition branching ratios—the ED transition emission was enhanced whereas the MD transition emission was suppressed with reduced spacing. This inverse relationship demonstrates parallel evolution in the radiative decay rates of both transition types under plasmonic modulation. Consequently, by monitoring the fluorescence emission, particularly the ratio between ED and MD transition emissions at varying interparticle distances of Au NS dimers, the distribution of electric and magnetic LDOS in plasmonic nanostructures could be inferred with nanoscale precision.

As the gap distance between the Au NS dimers decreased, the variation in ED and MD transition emissions of Eu3+ ions could be attributed to two factors. First, the reduced gap provided stronger localized field enhancement, which increased the spontaneous emission rate of the ED transition, thereby enhancing its emission. Second, at a 2 nm gap distance, the resonance enhancement of the entire nanostructure precisely matched the ED transition emission peak, further amplifying its emission. In Supporting Information S1: Figure S15, the magnetic field distributions for a single Au NS, a 20 nm, and a 2 nm gap Au NS dimer are provided, demonstrating the complementary variations of the electric and magnetic fields and their impact on the ED and MD transition emissions of Eu3+ ions. As previously discussed, the emission characteristics of Eu3+ ions’ ED and MD transitions were interdependent—enhancement of ED emission inevitably led to suppression of MD emission. As illustrated in Figure 4i, strongly coupled Au NS dimers at this separation induced significantly intensified LSPR effects. At this gap distance, electron tunneling has not yet initiated, allowing the LSPR effect to provide stable electric field enhancement [50]. Consequently, the influence of LSPR on ED transition emissions of Eu3+ ions reached its maximum magnitude, thereby suppressing MD transition emissions. When the Au flake was replaced with a Si substrate while keeping other conditions unchanged, the fluorescence spectrum of Eu3+ ions exhibited negligible changes as shown in Supporting Information S1: Figure S16. The minimal alterations observed in Eu3+ ions emission characteristics—both for single Au NS and dimers with separations exceeding 20 nm—further confirmed that only strongly enhanced and spectrally matched LSPR effects could effectively modulate and enhance these transitions.

The preceding analysis demonstrated that the emission capabilities of Eu3+ ions were directly correlated with the electric and magnetic photonic quantum environment of the nanocavity. Consequently, the number of Au NSs on the Au flake could be also precisely controlled to manipulate the near‐field electric or magnetic LDOS, thereby influencing and regulating the emission properties of Eu3+ ions. Since the construction of multiple dimers relied on sequential NS manipulation, the fluorescence spectra of the monolayer NaYF4: Eu3+ NPs were acquired after each dimers relocated. The LSPR effect between the Au NSs and the flake altered the surrounding electromagnetic environment, modifying the spontaneous emission rate of the Eu3+ ions doped NPs and manifesting as spectral intensity variations. Strengthening the plasmonic nanocavity structure was predicted to amplify this effect. Meanwhile, the observed variation in Eu3+ ions luminescence emission further indicated that weak interactions existed between each pair of Au NSs, resulting in gradual enhancement of the corresponding emission ratio with increasing numbers of dimers. To validate these, numerical simulations were performed to analyze the electric field distribution and enhancement around nanostructures with different NS counts as shown in Figure 5d. As anticipated, the field enhancement intensified with increasing NS density within the laser‐excited region. These results further corroborated the regulatory role of LSPR in the spontaneous emission of Eu3+ dipole transitions and highlighted that even localized electromagnetic field enhancement could exert a pronounced influence on the overall emission profile. Statistical analysis of multiple modulation experiments performed on both individual Au NS and Au NS dimers was presented in Figure 5e. The Au NS dimers were demonstrated to exhibit superior capability in modulating the branching ratio between the MD and ED transitions of Eu3+ ions compared to single NS. Upon interaction with Au NS dimers, a distinct reduction in the β MD parameter was observed for monolayer NaYF4: Eu3+ NPs, whereas the β ED parameter was found to be significantly enhanced. Furthermore, a remarkable increase in the integrated intensity ratio of ED to MD transitions (I610/I590) for Eu3+ ions was consistently recorded. By modulating the electromagnetic properties of the excitation environment through variations in nanostructure density, the spatial distributions of both electric LDOS and magnetic LDOS were successfully mapped based on the relative intensity variations between ED and MD emissions of Eu3+ ions. This methodology was demonstrated to hold significant implications for nanoscale electromagnetic field manipulation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrated a plasmonic nanostructure platform for precisely controlling ED and MD transitions in NaYF4:Eu3+ NPs through precisely engineered local photonic environments. By employing AFM nanomanipulation to construct tunable nanostructures composed of Au NSs and an Au flake, selective modulation of electric and magnetic LDOS was achieved, enabling independent control over ED and MD transition emissions from Eu3+ ions. This platform permitted emitter positioning with nanometer precision, facilitating systematic investigation of self‐interference effects on competing radiative pathways and their influence on ED/MD transition characteristics of Eu3+ ions. The spectral analysis of ED/MD transitions served as a sensitive probe for mapping nanoscale electromagnetic field distributions. Secondly, by leveraging the broken mirror symmetry and overlapping coupling with LSPR at the Au flake‐NP interface, the ratio between ED and MD transition emissions from Eu3+ ions was dynamically tuned from 1.0 to 4.3. Meanwhile, a remarkable enhancement of ED transition emission by a factor of 481 was simultaneously achieved with an accurate nanoscale gap regulation by this platform. The introduction of transition branching ratios enabled detailed examination of electromagnetic LDOS distribution in the near‐field regime and the Purcell effect’s role in enhancing the spontaneous emission rate of Eu3+ ions. Experimental results reproduced previously demonstrated emission modification outcomes achieved through conventional spacer‐layer strategies. Combined with theoretical simulations, these findings further verified that localized electric field enhancement could quantitatively regulate LDOS and radiative decay rate via branching ratios. The method offers directness, rapid implementation, and compatibility with diverse nanostructure geometries. This approach provides a versatile tool for fundamental studies of light–matter interactions and the development of advanced nanophotonic devices, with potential applications in quantum light sources, nano‐sensors, and other systems requiring controlled electric and magnetic transition components. Furthermore, its in situ spectral monitoring capability during operation establishes a robust framework for designing functional materials with tailored emission properties through controlled plasmonic electromagnetic environments.
