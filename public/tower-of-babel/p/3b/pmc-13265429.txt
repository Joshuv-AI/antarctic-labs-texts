State educational contexts in childhood and systemic inflammation in later life: Evidence from the health and retirement study
Authors: Mateo P. Farina, Heide Jackson, Jennifer A. Ailshire, Katrina M. Walsemann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic, low-grade inflammation is a key biological mechanism linking early-life adversity to morbidity and disability in later adulthood. Although educational attainment has been consistently associated with lower inflammation, the role of early educational environments remains understudied. We examined whether exposure to better-resourced state education systems during childhood predicts lower systemic inflammation in later life. We linked historical state-level measures of educational resources (1919–1974) to biomarker data from the 2016 Venous Blood Study of the Health and Retirement Study, a nationally representative cohort of U.S. adults aged 56 and older (n = 7524). A latent construct of inflammation was derived from six biomarkers (IL-10, IL-1RA, IL-6, TNFR1, IGF-1, and C-reactive protein). Linear regression models estimated associations between state educational resources and inflammation, sequentially adjusting for demographic characteristics, early-life and state-level covariates, individual educational attainment, and adult health behaviors. Higher state-level educational resources were associated with significantly lower inflammation (b = −0.033, 95% CI: −0.051, −0.015). This association persisted after adjusting for demographic and early-life factors and was attenuated but remained statistically significant after controlling for educational attainment (b = −0.025, 95% CI: −0.049, −0.009). Associations were strongest among individuals with higher educational attainment, although this interaction became non-significant after accounting for health behaviors. These findings suggest that early educational environments may have lasting physiological consequences independent of schooling completed, partly operating through socioeconomic and behavioral pathways. Investments in public education may represent an important strategy to promote healthy aging and reduce inflammation-related health disparities across the life course.

Introduction

Conceptual background

Chronic, low-grade inflammation is closely linked to morbidity, disability, cognitive health, and mortality in later life (Cribb et al., 2022; Farina et al., 2022; Li et al., 2024) and is considered a mechanism of physiological decline across organ systems, including heart conditions, metabolic diseases, and cognitive impairment (Franceschi and Campisi, 2014). A growing body of research shows that physical and social exposures across the life course shape inflammatory processes. For example, older adults from disadvantaged backgrounds often have higher inflammation, linked to the accumulation of adversity across the life course (Mitchell and Aneshensel, 2017). Accordingly, markers of inflammation are increasingly used to study the biological embodiment of social and physical environments and their contribution to health and health inequalities in later life.

Among social determinants, educational attainment is one of the most widely examined predictors of chronic inflammation (Loucks et al., 2006; Muscatell et al., 2020) with higher attainment consistently associated with lower inflammation levels (Davillas et al., 2017; Mitchell and Aneshensel, 2017; Pollitt et al., 2008). However, educational attainment alone may not capture the full impact of early educational environments. Emerging research highlights that childhood educational environments, including school quality and resources, also predict later-life health outcomes, including cognitive functioning, cardiovascular disease, and mortality (Hamad et al., 2019; Walsemann et al., 2024; Walsemann and Ailshire, 2020). These associations likely reflect both direct effects of educational environments during a sensitive period of development and indirect pathways through behavior, socioeconomic conditions, and stress over the life course.

If school environments are upstream determinants of later-life inflammation and cognition, then secular improvements in U.S. public education during the 20th century may represent an important—and underleveraged—source of population-level variation in these outcomes. For example, between the 1920s and 1970s, per-pupil funding increased more than 6-fold (Chakrabarti et al., 2013), math and science curricula was strengthened and expanded, and extra supports were provided to improve the learning environments for children with special needs (Massey and Stephens, 2025). State investment also rose during this period, though this growth was uneven and driven by different motivations and fiscal capacities. In several states, increased spending aimed to reduce disparities between local school systems—often under court mandate—through “equalization reforms” to counterbalance unequal local property tax bases. In other states, especially in the U.S. South, the state assumed a greater share of public school financing because local communities had weak property tax bases and limited capacity to fund schools, a constraint exacerbated by fiscal pressures during the Great Depression and underfunding.This variation in resources to fund schools likely shaped early-life exposures with long-term consequences for immune function and inflammation, being an important contextual determinant in which biological risk unfolded. From a life course perspective, childhood is a sensitive developmental period when social and environmental conditions can become biologically embedded (Ben-Shlomo and Kuh, 2002; Kuh et al., 2003), influencing physiological functioning well into adulthood (Milaniak and Jaffee, 2019). Prior research has shown that nutrition, exposure to infectious diseases, and stress from socioeconomic adversity or trauma in childhood are tied to inflammation levels in young adulthood and later life (Buckland et al., 2022; Furman et al., 2019; Rasmussen et al., 2019). As state investments in public schools increased, schools increasingly served as key sites for delivering resources that shaped these early-life exposures. Greater investment increased the likelihood that schools offered school meal programs (Ruis, 2017), provided routine medical care through school nurses and vaccination programs (Schupmann, 2018; Zaiger, 2000), and promoted cognitive stimulation, structured routines, health literacy, and social, emotional, and cognitive skill development (Borghans et al., 2015; Greenberg, 2025; Heiss et al., 2025; Mondi et al., 2021; Selman and Dilworth-Bart, 2024). These material and psychosocial exposures can establish early patterns of inflammatory regulation that may shape levels of inflammation decades later.

In addition to these direct benefits, early educational environments may shape inflammation indirectly through adult socioeconomic and behavioral pathways. One central mechanism potentially linking early educational environments to adult health is educational attainment itself. Individuals who attend higher-quality schools are more likely to complete more years of education (Deming et al., 2014), which, in turn, improves socioeconomic conditions in adulthood. Higher socioeconomic status is consistently associated with lower levels of chronic inflammation (Loucks et al., 2006; Mitchell and Aneshensel, 2017; Muscatell et al., 2020). Beyond formal attainment, well-resourced schools can also promote the adoption and maintenance of health-promoting behaviors across the life course (i.e., the uptake or cessation of smoking; food consumption; and physical activity) (Pulimeno et al., 2020). Smoking and obesity, in particular, represent well-established and biologically proximal pathways to chronic low-grade inflammation (Elisia et al., 2020; O'Rourke, 2009; Rodríguez-Hernández et al., 2013) and are consistently implicated in education–inflammation associations in prior research (Friedman and Herd, 2010; Kershaw et al., 2010; Maurel et al., 2020). Taken together, these pathways illustrate how state investments in education can have enduring biological consequences that shape health trajectories across the life span.

Although well-resourced schools can promote skills and capacities that support healthier behaviors, these advantages may be constrained among individuals who experience socioeconomic disadvantage throughout adulthood. Persistent socioeconomic disadvantage and limited access to stable employment, healthcare, and safe living conditions can attenuate protective effects and elevate inflammation in adulthood (Koster et al., 2006; Muscatell et al., 2020; Pollitt et al., 2008; Sumner et al., 2020). This interactive dynamic suggests that the benefits of better-resourced schools may be most pronounced among individuals who achieve higher levels of education, given that highly educated individuals often experience greater socioeconomic status and stability during adulthood. In contrast, those who complete fewer years of schooling may experience persistent socioeconomic disadvantage and elevated risk of chronic inflammation regardless of early educational environments.

Prior evidence on education contexts and inflammation

Despite strong theoretical connections between early-life educational contexts and biological functioning, empirical evidence linking childhood educational environments to chronic inflammation remains limited and mixed. Prior work has more often examined educational attainment or related educational factors, such per pupil spending, school term length, or per pupil spending in relation to inflammation—commonly focusing on markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP), and in some studies fibrinogen and Il-6—in midlife and older adulthood (e.g., Davillas et al., 2017; Loucks et al., 2006; Maurel et al., 2020; Pollitt et al., 2008). For example, Hamad et al. (2019) estimated an association between state-level education quality and later-life cardiovascular disease risk, including inflammation defined as CRP, and found no association, using NHANES data. Related research has also examined CRP in connection with cardiovascular risk factors and school-related indicators using the HRS, though results have not always shown statistically significant direct effects (Vable et al., 2019). However, CRP is highly sensitive to acute illness and recent infections and is only modestly correlated with other inflammatory markers that may be responsive to life course exposures (Kritchevsky et al., 2005; Velissaris et al., 2017). This underscores the need to examine broader inflammatory profiles—beyond single markers—and to consider whether early educational exposures during a period of rapid change in funding and quality can shape biological risk in older adulthood. These limitations underscore the need to examine broader inflammatory profiles—using multiple biomarkers—in nationally representative samples of older adults, where chronic low-grade inflammation provides an indication of biological risk to several age-related health outcomes potentially before the onset of clinical symptoms.

Study aims/hypotheses

Our study examines whether the quality of early educational environments is associated with low-grade systemic inflammation in later life using a nationally representative sample of older adults. Using biomarker data from the 2016 Venous Blood Study of the Health and Retirement Study (HRS) and historical, state-level measures of public education systems from 1919/20–1973/74 (Walsemann et al., 2025), we test whether individuals who attended schools in states with greater educational resources exhibit lower chronic inflammation in older adulthood. We further hypothesize that educational attainment will amplify these benefits, whereas smoking and obesity may partially account for observed associations.

Material and methods

Data and study sample

Individual-level data come from the 2016 Venous Blood Study (VBS) in the Health and Retirement Study (HRS). The VBS is a subsample of HRS respondents who completed the 2016 core interview and were not residing in nursing homes at the time of data collection (Crimmins et al., 2017). Venous blood was obtained from consenting participants by trained phlebotomists during home visits, typically within two months of the core interview. Samples were centrifuged in the field and shipped to the Advanced Research & Diagnostics Laboratory at the University of Minnesota. Of the collected samples, 92% arrived at the laboratory within 24 h and 99% within 48 h. When survey weights are applied, the VBS provides a nationally representative sample of U.S. adults aged 56 and older.

Data on state-level education quality come from the State Education Contextual Data Resource (S-ECDR) (Walsemann et al., 2025), a publicly available dataset constructed from two historical sources of state-level public education data: the Biennial Surveys of Education (1919/20–1957/58) and the Statistics of State School Systems (1959/60–1973/74). These sources report biennial data from 1919/20 to 1973/74, capturing key indicators of school financing, resource allocation, and structural features of state education systems. We linked the S-ECDR to the Health and Retirement Study (HRS) using respondents’ self-reported state of residence during most of their primary or secondary schooling, or around age 10. Data were available for 48 states and the District of Columbia; we excluded Alaska and Hawaii because they lacked comparable data coverage during the study period and did not attain statehood until 1959.

Our analytical sample includes respondents born between 1927 and 1959 who participated in the 2016 VBS data collection (N = 9933), had non-missing values on six indicators of inflammation (IL10, IL1RA, IL6, TNFR1, IGF1, and CRP) (N = 9646), resided in one of the 48 continental U.S. states at age 10 (N = 7843), had non-zero values on the VBS person weights (N = 7524).

Measures

Inflammation

To measure chronic low-grade inflammation, we constructed a latent variable using confirmatory factor analysis (similar to one constructed in (Meier et al., 2023). This latent construct is estimated from six indicators of inflammation: IL10, IL1RA, IL6, TNFR1, IGF1, and CRP. These biomarkers were assayed as part of the HRS Venous Blood Study protocol and were selected by the HRS biomarker investigators for their utility in understanding aging and health, including cytokines and related immune markers that capture multiple facets of inflammatory signaling (Crimmins et al., 2017). These inflammation markers in particular are thought to represent different facets of inflammation (Justice et al., 2018; Meier et al., 2023), are associated with aging-related health outcomes (Farina et al., 2022; Franceschi and Campisi, 2014; Sanada et al., 2018), and are not highly correlated with each other (correlations ranged from 0.16 to 0.47). CRP is set as the reference indicator. Prior to loading these indicators, we top-coded them at the 99th percentile, applied a log transformation, and then standardized them. We report the standardized factor loadings in Supplemental Figure 1. Model fit was assessed with Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), and Root Mean Square Error Approximation (RMSEA). Values indicated a strong fit (RMSEA = 0.087, CFI = 0.940, TLI = 0.899). The inflammation construct is normally distributed.

State education quality

Following the methodology used by Walsemann et al.(2024), we performed confirmatory factor analysis that identified two factors (state-level education resources and state-education funding source) based on the following 7 state-level indicators: per-pupil expenditures, pupil-teacher ratio, teacher salary, per-pupil revenue from state sources, per-pupil revenue from local sources, percent funding from state sources, and percent funding from local sources. We used this data reduction strategy because several indicators were highly correlated (≥0.70). The two-factor solution explained 83.7% of the variance, with all subsequent factors having eigenvalues below 1. One factor—state-level educational resources—had high loadings for per-pupil expenditures, per-pupil revenue from local sources, and teacher salary, which we interpret as representing overall investment in public education. The other factor—state education funding source—was not used in our analyses, as ancillary models showed no statistically significant association and only small effect sizes in relation to inflammation levels in older adulthood.

Educational attainment, smoking, and obesity

Our models also consider several potential pathways through which state education quality may be associated with inflammation based on prior research (Elisia et al., 2020; Loucks et al., 2006; Muscatell et al., 2020; O'Rourke, 2009): educational attainment, smoking status, and obesity. Educational attainment, measured as self-reported highest grade completed (0-17), was centered at 12 years, corresponding to high school completion in United States. Smoking status was categorized as 1) never smoker, 2) former smoker, and 3) current smoker created from self-reported information if the respondent currently smoked and/or had ever smoked. Lastly, BMI scores were calculated using weight and height measurements from first round interview data and classified as obese (BMI score ≥30) or non-obese (BMI score <30) following CDC guidelines. We used measurements from respondent's baseline wave because prior studies have shown that BMI in older adulthood may decline due to health problems likely tied to higher inflammation levels, such as cancer diagnosis, that can lead to weight loss (Ler et al., 2025).

Covariates

Individual-level covariates included age, self-reported race/ethnicity (Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic or Other Race/Ethnicity), gender (male/female), Census division where the respondent resided while in school or approximately age 10 (East North Central, East South Central, Middle Atlantic, Mountain/Pacific, New England, South Atlantic, West North Central, West, South Central), highest years of schooling completed by either parent, health in childhood (good or better/fair or poor), and highest years of schooling completed by respondent. State-level covariates included state income inequality when the respondent was 7 years old and the percent of white residents within a state when the respondent was 10 years old.

Analytical plan

To examine the association between state-level educational resources and chronic inflammation in later life, we estimated a series of linear regression models. Model 1 estimated the association between state-level education resources and inflammation with adjustments for age, race/ethnicity, and gender. Model 2 added geographic factors including Census division of residence at age 10, state income inequality at age 7, and state percent white at age 10. Model 3 added childhood characteristics that may be associated with later levels of inflammation, including retrospectively reported health in childhood and highest years of schooling completed by either parent. Model 4 added educational attainment (years of schooling). In Model 5, we tested the interaction between state-level educational resources and educational attainment. Lastly, in Model 6, we added smoking status and obesity. All models clustered standard errors by state of residence at age 10 and applied the 2016 VBS weights to account for sampling design and non-response (see Fig. 1).

Additionally, we used coefficients from Model 5 and Model 6 to estimate predicted change in level of inflammation associated with a change in state education resources. Specifically, we set state-level educational resources to the 10th and 90th percentile and years of schooling at 8, 10, 12, 14, and 16. All other covariates were held at their sample mean. Main association

Table 2 reports regression coefficients for the association between state educational resources and inflammation. In the demographic-adjusted model (Model 1), a one-standard-deviation increase in state educational resources was associated with lower inflammation (b = −0.033, 95% CI: −0.051, −0.015). This magnitude is comparable to the increase in inflammation associated with approximately 2.5 additional years of age. The association remained similar after adjusting for childhood state context and geographic factors (Model 2, b = −0.029, 95% CI: −0.049, −0.009) and childhood conditions (Model 3, b = −0.025, 95% CI: −0.045, −0.006).

Attenuation after educational attainment

Including respondents’ years of schooling (Model 4) attenuated the association between state educational resources and inflammation (b = −0.022, 95% CI: −0.041, −0.003), although the coefficient remained statistically significant. Post-estimation tests indicated that the education-resources coefficient was significantly smaller in Model 4 than in Model 3 (p < 0.01), consistent with partial pathway through educational attainment itself.

Moderation by education level and potential role of smoking and obesity

Models 5-6 evaluate whether educational attainment moderates the association between state educational resources and inflammation (through the interaction term) and if smoking and obesity status at baseline attenuate this moderation. Because years of schooling was centered at 12, the main effect of state educational resources in Model 5 corresponds to respondents with a high school education. At 12 years of schooling, the association between early life educational resources and inflammation was negative but not statistically significant (b = −0.015, 95% CI: −0.036, 0.006). However, the interaction between educational resources and years of schooling was negative and statistically significant (b = −0.004, 95% CI: −0.008, −0.001), indicating that the inverse association between educational resources and inflammation became stronger with higher educational attainment. When smoking status and obesity were added (Model 6), the main effect of educational resources at 12 years of schooling remained negative and marginally significant (b = −0.017, 95% CI: −0.035, 0.002; p < 0.10), while the interaction term was attenuated and no longer statistically significant (b = −0.003, 95% CI: −0.008, 0.001).

Predicted differences across educational attainment levels

Fig. 2 translates Models 5-6 into predicted differences in inflammation when comparing individuals educated in states at the 90th versus 10th percentile of educational resources, across levels of educational attainment (8, 10, 12, 14, and 16 years). Consistent with Model 5, predicted reductions in inflammation were smallest among respondents with fewer than a high school education and largest among those with postsecondary attainment. For example, among respondents with 16 years of schooling, moving from the 10th to the 90th percentile of educational resources was associated with a 0.10 standard deviation lower inflammation score (Model 5: −0.100, 95% CI: −0.163, −0.038). Although the interaction was not statistically significant in Model 6, predicted differences remained most apparent among respondents with some college or higher education.

Sensitivity analyses

We conducted sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our findings (Table 3). First, to evaluate potential survival selection among the oldest HRS subcohorts, we re-estimated models after excluding respondents in the AHEAD and CODA cohorts (born before 1931).; N = 318). Second, to ensure meaningful exposure to formal schooling, we re-estimated models excluding 54 respondents with fewer than four years of schooling. Third, to account for cohort-specific differences in schooling contexts and inflammation levels, we added categorical indicators for birth cohorts to the models (1931-1941; 1942-1947; 1948-1953; 1954-1959). Finally, we tested whether associations differed by gender by including interaction terms for gender with state educational resource); these interactions were small and not statistically significant (results not shown). Across specifications, results were substantively similar to the main analyses.

Discussion

Principal findings

This study provides new evidence that exposure to well-resourced state education systems in early life is associated with lower systemic inflammation in later life, based on a comprehensive, multimarker measure of chronic inflammation in a nationally representative U.S. cohort. Using newly available historical data on state educational systems linked to the HRS (Walsemann et al., 2025), we found that this association persisted even after adjusting for individual educational attainment and state-level characteristics. However, the effects appeared to be contingent on years of educational attainment, with stronger associations among those with more years of completed schooling. The association among those with greater levels of schooling seem to be explained, in part, by differences in adult health behaviors. These findings highlight the potential importance of early educational environments on inflammation-related aging and underscore the socioeconomic and health behavior pathways that contribute to these long-term associations.

Comparison with prior studies

Our findings contribute to a growing literature demonstrating that early-life environments shape long-term patterns of systemic inflammation. In models adjusting for individual demographics, we found that older adults who grew up in states with fewer educational resources had higher inflammation levels. The difference between those who grew up in states at the 10th versus 90th percentile of educational resources—a two standard deviation contrast—was comparable to the increase associated with 2.5 additional years of aging. A recent meta-analysis by Chiang et al. (2022) similarly found that childhood socioeconomic disadvantage and psychosocial stressors were linked to elevated inflammation, with stronger associations observed at older ages. Our study extends this work by focusing on public education systems—a critical but understudied developmental context. Under-resourced schools may create environments characterized by overcrowded classrooms, limited staff, fewer health-focused courses, and reduced access to services such as school meals and preventive health screenings. These conditions can contribute to chronic stress and limited health supports in childhood, which may raise the likelihood of higher inflammation in adulthood.

Our findings differ from those of Hamad et al. (2019), who found no association between state-level education quality and CRP. However, while CRP is a widely used marker of inflammation in population studies, it can be highly sensitive to acute illness and recent infections and may therefore provide a less specific indicator of chronic, low-grade inflammation in older adulthood. By contrast, we use a global indicator of chronic inflammation derived from six inflammatory markers (IL10, IL1RA, IL6, TNFR1, IGF1, and CRP), which is a more stable and comprehensive measure of systemic inflammation that is less prone to short-term fluctuations. Differences in how state education quality was operationalized may also help explain the divergent findings. Hamad et al. (2019) averaged three standardized indicators—school-term length, per-pupil spending, and pupil-teacher ratio. With newly linked historical data, we were able to create state educational quality measures that may capture more detailed information such as per-pupil revenue from local sources and teacher salary.

We also found that educational attainment attenuated—but did not eliminate—the association between state-level educational resources and inflammation in later life. This suggests schooling shapes biological outcomes partly through its influence on attainment, consistent with prior evidence linking higher-quality schools to greater educational achievement (Dearden et al., 2002; Deming et al., 2014; Jennings et al., 2015; Klugman, 2012) and educational attainment to lower inflammation in older adulthood (Loucks et al., 2006; Maurel et al., 2020; Pollitt et al., 2008). Yet the coefficient was only attenuated by about 20%, indicating that other features of childhood educational environments likely influence inflammation through additional pathways. These results point to a broader role of education policy in shaping long-term health by structuring access social, economic, and health resources beyond attainment itself.

We also found a stronger association between state education resources and inflammation among individuals who completed at least a high school education (see Fig. 2). This pattern is consistent with life course frameworks that emphasize both biological embedding during sensitive periods of development and the accumulation of advantage across educational, economic, and behavioral pathways that shape inflammation over time (Carroll et al., 2011; Hertzman, 2012; Maurel et al., 2020). Growing up in states with well-resourced educational systems may have supported healthier immune and stress-regulatory development during a sensitive period, while also increasing the likelihood of post-secondary attainment and access to economic, social, and environmental conditions that reinforce this early biological advantage. Educational attainment is strongly linked to stable employment, higher wages, safer housing, access to preventive health care, and healthier behaviors—all of which help reduce chronic stress and support lower levels of chronic low-grade inflammation (Crystal et al., 1992; Jefferson, 2008). We find some evidence for this. When we adjusted for midlife smoking and obesity—two behavioral pathways through which attainment may influence inflammation (Elisia et al., 2020; Rodríguez-Hernández et al., 2013)—the moderating effect of educational attainment was attenuated and no longer statistically significant, and neither was the main effect of state educational resources.

In contrast, individuals who completed fewer than 12 years of schooling showed little evidence that growing up in states with better-resourced education systems was associated with lower inflammation in older adulthood. This may reflect the overwhelming influence of structural disadvantages in adulthood tied to their low level of educational attainment, including limited access to stable employment, housing, and supportive networks (Zajacova and Lawrence, 2018). These stressors can lead to chronic activation of neuroendocrine and immune pathways—particularly the HPA axis and sympathetic nervous system (Chu et al., 2025; McEwen, 1998)—resulting in persistent low-grade inflammation over time. Although educational environments likely shape inflammatory regulation through biological embedding, these effects may be attenuated or obscured by the accumulation of disadvantage over the life course. In this way, our findings underscore how early educational conditions and later social pathways interact to shape long-term physiological outcomes.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, our sample included cohorts who attended school between the 1920s and 1960s, which was a period of rapid change in the U.S. public education system, but does not fully capture subsequent reforms, including school desegregation and expanded college access. Future research should consider how these changes influenced physiological functioning in later-born cohorts. Second, our measure of educational resources was based on state-level data, which masks within-state variation. However, given that state governments exercised substantial authority over school funding during this period, state-level indicators provide a meaningful approximation of the structural educational environment. Third, the cross-sectional nature of our biomarker data limits our ability to assess temporal dynamics of inflammation. Fourth, we do not account for these chronic conditions or medication use as our focus was tied to potentially more proximal determinants of educational context in early life. Future work should use longitudinal data to better examine how these mid and later life health conditions and management may be tied to early life educational environments. Finally, selective survival may bias estimates toward the null if individuals who grew up in low-resource states were more likely to die before study enrollment (Sansani, 2011).

Conclusions

Overall, our findings underscore the importance of childhood educational environments for later-life inflammation – an important biological pathway linked to aging-related disease and disability. Because nearly all children attend K-12 schools, and educational resources are modifiable through state policy, schools represent an important lever for improving population aging. As educational attainment continues to rise (US Census Bureau, n.d.), educational environments may play an even greater role in shaping adult health in the coming decades. Thus, ensuring access to well-resourced schools should be considered a health equity imperative with the potential to improve healthy aging and reduce chronic disease burden in the United States.
