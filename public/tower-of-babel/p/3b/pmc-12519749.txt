Understanding relationships among climate change, conflict, migration/displacement and health in humanitarian settings: a scoping review
Authors: Molly E. Lasater, Gabrielle Prager, Yuni A. Choi, Tim Groteclaes, Divya Rao, Shona P. Kamps, Chiara Altare, Paul B. Spiegel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClimate change is increasing the frequency and intensity of various extreme weather events across regions, globally. Climate change has also been associated with conflict, migration and displacement, and health in complex and multi-causal relationships, particularly in humanitarian settings. This study aimed to better understand linkages and relationships at the nexus of climate change, conflict, migration/displacement, and health in humanitarian settings.MethodsWe searched the academic literature in four databases (PubMed, Embase, Scopus, GreenFILE) and the grey literature in 18 organizational websites. Titles and abstracts, and full texts from the academic literature were independently screened by two reviewers following PRISMA scoping review guidelines. For the grey literature, a single reviewer screened titles and abstracts and full texts, and extracted data. All extracted data were synthesized by domain.ResultsWe included 178 articles in this scoping review, describing four domains: climate change and migration/displacement and health; climate change and conflict and migration/displacement; climate change and conflict and migration/displacement and health; and climate change and conflict and health. Articles most frequently focused on climate change, migration/displacement and health. We identified complex, non-linear, multicausal and interrelated relationships at the nexus of these domains in diverse contexts that informed the development of a conceptual framework of direct and indirect relationships. We found that climate change, conflict, migration/displacement, and health intersect with pre-existing vulnerabilities to further exacerbate risks and vulnerabilities or cause new risks and vulnerabilities at this nexus.ConclusionWhile associations among climate change, conflict, migration/displacement and health are of significant concern, there has been limited examination at this nexus. Findings of this review indicate that research and practice should critically consider and embrace the complex and interrelated ways in which climate change has interrelated and cascading impacts on conflict, migration/displacement and health. Future research can build upon the findings of this review to identify the strength of associations at this nexus and holistic and effective responses. There is a need to adopt integrated and multi-disciplinary approaches to research, programming and policy in humanitarian settings while strengthening climate-resilient health systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13031-025-00707-8.

Background

Globally, climate change is increasing the intensity and frequency of extreme weather events and is associated with migration, displacement, and conflict [1, 2]. Current estimates suggest that 3.3–3.6 billion people currently live in highly climate vulnerable contexts [1]. Amidst growing climate change and displacement, in 2023, the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees reported that nearly 75% of forcibly displaced people were residing in countries with high exposure to climatic hazards, and nearly 50% of forcibly displaced people were living in contexts exposed to conflict in addition to climatic hazards [3]. As the magnitude and frequency of climatic shocks and hazards are projected to substantially increase, so too is the need in humanitarian settings, as natural resources for survival become increasingly scarce and conflict and migration and displacement increase in frequency [1, 4, 5]. Humanitarian settings refer to environments where a significant event or series of events (e.g., disasters, armed conflicts, epidemics) has critically threatened the health, safety, security, or well-being of a population(s), requiring external assistance from national or international actors [6]. Given that the terms migration and displacement are often used interchangeably in the literature, we refer to population mobility as migration/displacement for the purpose of this paper. Moreover, we use the term conflict to refer a broad range of conflict, including armed conflict, inter-and intra-communal violence, and domestic- and gender-based violence (see Supplemental File 3, Table 5, for a glossary of terminology).

Previous systematic and scoping reviews have established linkages between climate change and adverse health outcomes [7], conflict [8, 9], and migration and displacement [10]. For example, climate change has been associated with increases in morbidity and mortality and exacerbation of existing respiratory, cardiovascular, neurological, and mental health morbidity [7, 11]. Climatic hazards and the ensuing resource scarcity have also been found to further increase the risk of food insecurity and malnutrition, as well as exposure to infectious diseases such as dengue and malaria [1, 2, 4, 12]. Specific to humanitarian settings, numerous health risk factors converge, such as reduced access to safe drinking water and food, and protective factors erode, leading to higher levels of adverse health outcomes [2, 13]. At the same time, studies have found that health-related infrastructure may not be able to withstand climate shocks and hazards, which may lead to service interruptions that further threaten population health [14, 15].

Growing evidence suggests that climatic changes, including both acute and slow-onset events, influence various migration/displacement patterns (e.g. voluntary, forced, seasonal, immobility, planned relocation) [10, 16–19]. To date, findings indicate that local climatic conditions interact with socio-economic, cultural, and political contexts to influence migration/displacement patterns, and health outcomes [20–22]. Systematic reviews have found that climate hazards such as extreme heat, cyclones, flooding, and drought increase resource scarcity and food and water insecurity, damage infrastructure and supply chains, and decrease agricultural yields and drive migration (e.g., voluntary, forced, seasonal) and displacement [1, 10, 13, 16–18, 23]. Scholarship has also found that migration and displacement in the context of climate change is associated with numerous and diverse health impacts, including changing patterns of infectious disease, non-communicable disease, mental and psychosocial health, and access to health care [20, 24]. For example, one review found that forced displacement and relocation was associated with mental health problems, food insecurity, and access to healthcare issues, and seasonal migration was associated with food insecurity, access to health care, and infectious disease [20].

Conflicts are projected to globally increase both in response to and alongside increasing climatic hazards, resource scarcity, and governance challenges [25]. Against this backdrop, growing research has examined the impacts of climate change on conflict and health. A meta-analysis of 60 studies [26] revealed strong causal evidence linking climatic variability and events to conflict, including interpersonal, intergroup, and state conflict across all global regions. More recently, an expert consensus study concluded that climate change has impacted armed conflict within countries alongside other drivers, including low socioeconomic development and governance capabilities [27]. Furthermore, increases in climatic hazards and conflicts can drive significant levels of migration and displacement [10]. This confluence is likely to result in devastating consequences for health [8, 9].

While relationships and dynamics have been explored among the various combinations of climate change, migration/displacement and health; climate change, conflict, and migration and displacement; and climate change, conflict, and health—there is a significant evidence gap related to the complex interactions and relationships at the nexus of climate change, conflict, migration/displacement, and health, particularly in low-and middle-income countries (LMICs) and humanitarian settings, despite being among the most affected. Increased research is needed to address this gap and strengthen our understanding of complex and interrelated relationships at this nexus to inform humanitarian policy programming, strengthen health systems, and ultimately to mitigate the impacts of climate change on conflict, migration/displacement and health.

The objective of this scoping review is to gain an understanding of this nexus in low-resource and humanitarian settings by identifying relationships through which climate change is increasing and exacerbating humanitarian health needs. Specifically, our primary research question seeks to answer: “How are climate change, conflict, migration/displacement and health related to one another in humanitarian settings?”. While the primary focus of this paper is on the climate change, conflict, migration/displacement, and health nexus in humanitarian settings, we included LMICs as climate change has and will continue to disproportionately impact this group of countries, and likely lead to increased conflict, displacement, migration, and ultimately increased humanitarian need [1]. We also included LMICs given their frequent overlap with humanitarian settings. LMIC contexts are frequently characterized by unmet needs that are complex and extensive, and could benefit from significant external humanitarian assistance and resources, despite not receiving them. Therefore, we included LMICs in light of the similarities in patterns and outcomes in both settings and the need and potential for increased knowledge at this nexus.

This scoping review is the first of its kind to examine relationships among climate change, conflict, migration/displacement and health in humanitarian settings. The findings and conceptual framework or unexplored relationships at the nexus will be of significance given that climate change, conflict, and migration and displacement are projected to continue to increase, in addition to the nearly 300 million people in need of humanitarian assistance due to climate change, conflict, and migration/displacement, globally [2, 28–30]. In line with the approach described by Peters et al. [31], we sought to map the range of quantitative and qualitative evidence on this topic and to present it visually. Our use of the scoping review method was intended to capture and synthesize a broad body of literature that has not previously been systematically examined. This approach was particularly relevant to our aim of developing a conceptual framework of dynamic relationships at the nexus of climate change, conflict, migration and health.

Methods

This scoping review was conducted following the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology for systematic scoping reviews, and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) for scoping review [32]. While a protocol was developed for this scoping review, it was not pre-registered due to the broad nature of our research question and ensuing challenges with a risk of bias assessment. However, no other protocols on this topic have been registered on Open Science Framework, underscoring the uniqueness of this work.

Search strategy and eligibility criteria

We searched four databases (PubMed, Embase, Scopus, GreenFILE) and grey literature sources including: United Nations Development Programme, International Organization for Migration, United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR), United Nations Children’s Fund, World Health Organization, United Nations Assistance Mission for Iraq, United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs, International Federation of Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies, International Committee of the Red Cross, International Medical Corps, International Rescue Committee, Internal Displacement Monitoring Centre, ReliefWeb, United States Agency for International Development Bureau for Humanitarian Assistance, European Commission's Joint Research Centre, World Bank, the ADAPT Initiative, and Climate Action Accelerator. The four databases and grey literature sources were selected to ensure broad and interdisciplinary coverage (e.g., public health, climatology, demography, conflict studies, geography, policy, humanitarianism and international relations) of literature relevant to the relationships between climate change, conflict, migration/displacement, and health.

The searches were adapted to each database and grey literature source with terms describing the seven topics at the center of this review: climate change (e.g., "climate change", "drought”), conflict (e.g. "armed conflicts", "war"), migration (e.g., “human migration”, “migrant”), displacement (e.g. "refugees", “internally displaced”), health (e.g. “mortality”, “mental health”), humanitarian settings (e.g., “public health emergency”, “humanitarian”, “disaster”), and LMICs (e.g., “low-income countries”, “Kenya”) (See Supplemental File 1. PubMed search strategy).

The academic literature searches focused on articles published in the last decade, from 2016 to January 22, 2024, which is the date the searches were executed, and when scholarship on this topic greatly expanded. We included the three most recent systematic reviews to represent current state of the evidence for the following binary associations: 1) a 2017 review of climate change and conflict [33]; 2) a 2021 review of climate change and health [7]; and 3) a 2019 review of the impacts of climate change on migration [10], to capture literature published before 2016. Therefore, time limits were built into the search strategies, reflecting the date of the searches of the previous systematic reviews. Specifically, the time limits for each topic were as follows: climate change and conflict—December 1, 2016, to January 22, 2024; climate change and health—June 1, 2019, to January 22, 2024; and climate change and migration—January 1, 2019, to January 22, 2024. Grey literature searches were conducted over the month of November 2023 and limited to articles published after January 1, 2020.

Abstract and full text screening

Article screening for the academic literature was conducted using the Covidence online systematic review platform, and two reviewers (among MEL, GP, SK, DR, YC, TG) independently assessed all article titles and abstracts. If one reviewer determined an abstract to be relevant, it was then included for full-text review. Two reviewers (among MEL, GP, SK, DR, YC, TG) independently assessed all full text articles for inclusion in the scoping review. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (MEL or CA). Given the volume and heterogeneity of grey literature, a single reviewer assessed the titles and abstracts and full texts for inclusion in the review. This approach is consistent with guidance for scoping and systematic reviews where single screening of grey literature is considered appropriate, especially for scoping reviews where feasibility is a common concern, provided that the process is transparent and documented [31]. Moreover, a subset of searches and abstracts were reviewed by MEL to ensure the reliability of searches and screening.

Data extraction

The data were extracted in an excel sheet with the following code fields: region, country, study objectives, study design, population, data type, domain of focus (defined as the intersection of at least three factors: (1) climate change and migration/displacement and health; (2) climate change and conflict and health; (3) climate change and conflict and migration/displacement; and (4) climate change and conflict and migration/displacement and health), key findings, recommendations, and a summary of pathways among domains. Six reviewers (ML, GP, SK, DR, YC, TG) pretested the data extraction template, and assessed reliability among extractions using the same two articles. Following pre-testing, the six reviewers extracted data from all included articles using the data extraction template.

Data analysis

Data analysis included two simultaneous methods: (1) analysis and synthesis of each domain; and (2) conceptual framework development. For analysis of domains, key findings of included articles from the grey and peer-reviewed literature were thematically analyzed to develop a summary of each domain. The domain summaries were then inductively coded and synthesized by themes, such as, causes, health impacts and vulnerabilities, which informed the development of the conceptual framework.

The conceptual framework was developed based on the review data and was iteratively adapted throughout. Through a series of workshops, the authors collaboratively constructed a framework using Miro [34], that first mapped primary relationships among climate change, conflict, migration/displacement, and health to begin to visualize the complexity of and overlapping nature of relationship across the nexus. All causal pathways from the extracted data were then overlayed onto this framework, building and adding new relationships and pathways into the framework. For example, relationship arrows were added noting climate change can lead to supply chain destruction, which can lead to vulnerabilities (e.g. food insecurity), which can lead to migration/displacement. This complex, overwhelming framework was then iteratively edited. Similar concepts between the core components in the framework (e.g., climate change, displacement/migration, conflict and health) were consolidated, intermediary relationships, pathways and direction of influence were reviewed. Throughout the workshop meetings, the team discussed and reached consensus on each pathway depicted in the framework. Figure 4 depicts the final version of the framework.

Results

Search findings and study selection

Our search identified a total of 23,996 papers from grey and peer-reviewed literature (Fig. 1). Grey literature was identified by hand searching humanitarian organization’s websites and databases; 1,034 articles were identified, of which 48 met the inclusion criteria. A total of 22,959 peer-reviewed papers were identified across the four academic databases. After removing duplicates, 11,403 articles underwent title and abstract screening for eligibility. Of these, 10,988 were excluded, leaving 415 articles assessed for eligibility based on full texts. In the following sections, we present our findings by domain.

Domain 1: Climate change and migration/displacement and health

Articles described various linkages among climate change migration/displacement and health [19, 21, 35–108]. Climate hazards and shocks, such as extreme weather events, disasters, floods, cyclones, and droughts were reported as causes of environmental degradation, scarcity of natural resources, and the loss of livelihoods [41, 42, 49, 51, 56, 89, 91–93, 95, 103, 106]. A study in Kazakhstan found that droughts and extreme temperature fluctuations severely impacted nomadic pastoralists’ access to grazing pastures and water, thereby threatening their livestock and food security [51]. Loss of or threatened livelihoods were also identified as drivers of migration/displacement [38, 39, 50, 52, 54, 55, 74, 87, 90]. For example, a study in the Punjab region of Pakistan, prone to riverbank erosion, described how local farmers migrated to neighboring urban cities when confronted with food shortages, unemployment, insufficient crop production, and scarcity of arable land [92]. Articles also described how climate change negatively affects health including: poor mental health (e.g., PTSD, depression, and mental distress), respiratory problems, cholera, malaria, diarrhea, malnutrition, wasting, stunting, physical injuries, drowning, and death [40, 42, 44, 46, 53, 60, 61, 63–65, 69, 70, 76, 79, 98, 99, 104, 105]. For instance, in Bududa, Uganda, a cholera outbreak took place due to poor access to clean water and sanitation following fatal landslides and floods [79]. Climate change was also reported to lead to changing vector patterns and associated increases in malaria, dengue, west Nile virus, onchocerciasis, leishmaniasis, and other arboviral diseases [53, 87, 91, 93, 95, 108]. In east Java, Indonesia, substantial and significant increases in dengue hemorrhagic fever were observed among households displaced from floods [91].

Dynamic pathways were identified at the nexus of climate change, migration/displacement, and health, whereby climate change triggers migration/displacement, which then contributes to negative health impacts [40, 41, 46, 53, 61, 63–65, 69, 70, 76, 98, 99, 104, 105]. For example, a study among Ethiopian refugees living in refugee camps in eastern Sudan described how storms, heavy rains, and flooding destroyed their shelters, clean water sources, and latrines, among other health and humanitarian infrastructure, which contributed to outbreaks of vector-borne diseases, Hepatitis E, and other infectious diseases [93]. Numerous articles also discussed changing physical and mental health status following climate change induced migration/displacement due to challenges accessing health services including, financial, geographical, literacy challenges, and poor quality of care [40, 41, 46, 50, 53, 61, 63–65, 69, 70, 76, 98, 99, 104, 105]. Schwerdtle et al. described this changing health status as a “risk-exchange” whereby migrants experience consistent and differing climate, health, and socioeconomic risks at both their origin and destination [50]. Relatedly, articles reported lower rates of antenatal care, facility-based deliveries, postnatal care, and immunizations among migrant and displaced populations, which were all described to have compounding health consequences, particularly for maternal and child health [40, 41, 46, 53, 61, 63–65, 69, 70, 76, 98, 99, 104, 105]. Articles also described interactions between urbanization and social and environmental determinants of health (e.g., overcrowding, exposure to poor air quality, poor housing and sanitary conditions, and unsafe working conditions), resulting in adverse physical and mental health outcomes such as infectious disease, depression, anxiety, vector-borne diseases [19, 21, 43, 50, 55, 59–61, 73, 74, 77, 78, 83, 90, 91, 93, 94, 103]. For example, a study in Bangladesh, described how climate-induced displacement into informal settlements in Dhaka led to climate-induced immobility [94]. Following displacement, study participants described worsening socio-economic conditions, declining physical and mental health, and climate change as factors leading to their forced immobilization or inability to return home [94].

Domain 2: Climate change and conflict and health

Various relationships and associations with cascading impacts were identified at the nexus of climate change, conflict, and health [189–206]. Articles described how climatic changes, shocks, and hazards including, drought, cyclone, hurricanes, flooding, salinity intrusion, changes in vegetation, disasters, and resource scarcity were drivers of conflict [192–195, 197–199, 201–204, 206]. A study in western and southern Sudan and along its borders with Ethiopia and Somalia found that armed conflicts were more likely in regions with more vegetation [195]. Various forms of conflict were discussed in the articles including, armed conflict, political violence, community-level violence, pastoralist-farmer conflict, intimate partner violence, gender-based violence, conflicts over natural resources, and land dispute conflicts [192–195, 197–199, 202, 204, 206]. A study on the drivers of violence in post-hurricane Haiti described how increases in daily stressors related to household finances and food insecurity led to increases in intimate partner violence, child abuse, and conflicts between neighbors over land [206].

Both climate change and conflict were also observed to have a negative impact on health including, diarrhea and gastrointestinal illness, psychological distress, depression, substance misuse, injuries, food insecurity, malnutrition, wasting, and stunting [194, 195, 198, 200, 204, 206]. A study in Ethiopia and Kenya found that armed conflict, climate variability (temperature and rainfall) are all positively associated with the prevalence of underweight children [204]. The authors describe how numerous unresolved intra-clan conflicts and attacks by Al-Shabaab militias had negative consequences on the food supply chain, and ultimately child malnutrition. Furthermore, a study in Andhra Pradesh, India, compared drought-prone, conflict-affected communities to drought-prone communities not experiencing violence and observed both negative short- and long- term nutritional impacts among children only in communities affected by conflict [206]. The authors argued that conflict reduces the capacities of households, communities, and health systems to cope with climate stressors, which ultimately presents further consequences for health [206].

Domain 3: Climate change and conflict and migration/displacement

Building on the previous domains, dynamic, interrelated relationships were uncovered at the nexus of climate change, conflict, and migration/displacement [29, 109–151]. Two articles found that climate change does not have an effect on conflict [118, 119]. In their study in East Africa, Owain, and Maslin found population growth, economic growth, and political stability to be the main drivers of conflict and displacement, while climate change (drought and temperature) was not significant [118]. Bidirectional associations between conflict and migration/displacement were discussed in the included articles, whereby climate-related conflict led to migration/displacement, and climate-related migration/displacement led to conflict [109–112, 118–120, 124, 125, 127, 128, 135–139, 141, 142, 146, 149, 150]. A study in Ethiopia and Kenya identified drought, violent conflict, flooding, crop failure, limited pasture, and chronic water scarcity as the primary climatic and socio-environmental drivers of migration [126]. Moreover, a study in water-scarce Lamu, Kenya, described how the migration of pastoralists from water-scarce neighboring Garissa into Lamu exacerbated pre-existing conflicts over water access, management, and distribution among religious communities, and ultimately led to increased water- related conflicts [127]. Lastly, in the Lake Chad region, competition over scarce natural resources including, water, and tensions over land ownership were seen to instigate conflict, particularly among pastoralists and agriculturalists [128].

Domain 4: Climate change and conflict and migration/displacement and health

Lastly, several articles described a range of complex associations at the intersection of climate change, conflict, migration/displacement, and health that echo and further build upon dynamic pathways described in the three previous sections [152–188]. In Somalia, study participants described how climate change and conflict intersect with displacement, resource scarcity, and continued conflict, which have adverse impacts on health and environmental degradation. Participants in this study were displaced due to violent conflicts and droughts and residing in camps for internally displaced persons (IDPs), where natural resources, particularly firewood, were scarce, forcing women and children to travel long distances (a round trip could take an entire day in some cases) in search of firewood. The search for firewood not only contributed to overexploitation of natural resources and environmental degradation, but also exposed women and children to gender-based violence, harassment, rape, and assault while on their journeys. Additionally, firewood collection led to violent clashes between IDPs and both pastoral and farming host communities. In response to such challenges, participants noted that they were forced to consume undercooked food and skip meals due to insufficient firewood [167].

A study in Uganda described two pathways through which migration takes place in response to climate change leading to conflicts over land and resources between migrant and host communities: (1) socio-economic hardships (e.g., natural resource scarcity, food insecurity, and conflict) that drive migration are linked to slow-onset climate processes or events, which threaten livelihoods and trigger both forced and voluntary migration; and (2) migration occurs as a coping strategy to environmental and climate hazards, shocks, and stressors [162]. In famine-stricken Ethiopia, armed conflicts have led to mass forced displacement in addition to damaged public health infrastructure and constrained delivery of health services, thereby further compounding health consequences of climate change and conflict [180]. Furthermore, a study in the Philippines describes how displacement and economic strain following typhoon Haiyan led to increases in violence against women including, rape and verbal abuse [169]. The authors found that women and girls were being forced into early marriages and sex work as a means to cope with economic strain, ultimately increasing risks for sexual and reproductive health problems [169].

Furthermore, in northeast Nigeria, climate change and violent conflicts collided, resulting in high levels of displacement, food insecurity, and threatened livelihoods [186]. Climate variability including, low rainfall, flooding, and drought adversely impacted livelihoods related to farming, fishing, and livestock breeding, which contributed to fluctuations between ‘crisis’ and ‘stressed’ severity phases of food insecurity in the region [186]. At the same time, the Boko Haram crisis triggered the displacement of over 2.5 million people, further impairing livelihoods and food security through attacks on civilians, food trucks, livestock rustling, armed banditry, and disrupted trade and communication infrastructure, resulting in exacerbated health risks including, adverse maternal and child health outcomes, cholera, diarrhea, malaria, poor sanitation, and poor breastfeeding practices. Ultimately, these interacting dynamics of climate change, conflict, and displacement led to a critical dependency on humanitarian aid in northeast Nigeria [186].

Discussion

The aim of this scoping review was to summarize existing knowledge regarding relationships among climate change, conflict, migration/displacement, and health in humanitarian settings to strengthen our understanding of how climate change is affecting and exacerbating humanitarian needs. Articles in this review most frequently examined the domain climate change, migration/displacement and health. Our findings revealed complex, non-linear, multicausal and intersecting relationships at the nexus of climate change, conflict, migration/displacement, and health in diverse contexts, globally. Specifically, we found that climate change, conflict, migration/displacement, and health intersect with pre-existing vulnerabilities (e.g., political, social, economic, health status), to further exacerbate risks and vulnerabilities or cause new risks and vulnerabilities at this nexus, and ultimately exacerbate humanitarian needs. Our work extends previous studies that have examined binary associations and causality among climate change, migration/displacement, or conflict, or health [7, 9, 10, 16, 17], emphasizing the complex, non-linear and interrelated nature of factors and dynamics at the nexus of climate change, conflict, migration/displacement, and health.

A conceptual framework (Fig. 4) was developed to summarize the findings of the included articles from this scoping review. The framework illustrates how climate change, conflict, migration/displacement, and health (yellow) are related and interact with one another, both directly and indirectly, through intermediary variables (blue), as described above. The framework includes additive effects of migration/displacement and conflict on pre-existing vulnerabilities and depicts how migrant/displaced individuals' vulnerabilities may change following displacement or climate change due to changing exposures and health literacy. The impacts of good governance, early action and anticipatory action, preparedness and conflict sensitivity, as well as efforts to reduce pre-existing vulnerabilities potentially influence and reduce the downstream impacts of climate change on health, conflict, and displacement/migration. This scoping review is the first of its kind to our knowledge to examine relationships and begin to map pathways related to the nexus of climate change, conflict, migration/displacement and health in humanitarian settings, and provide a conceptual framework. Our findings and this new conceptual framework support and contribute to the literature that has minimally explored this nexus, particularly in humanitarian settings [7, 10, 25, 33, 207–210]. Similar to other studies, we uncovered complex non-linear relationships and pathways among climate change, conflict, migration/displacement, and health that intersect with pre-existing vulnerabilities to further exacerbate risks and vulnerabilities or cause new risks and vulnerabilities at this nexus [10, 24, 25, 211–213]. For example, climate change-induced resource scarcity (e.g., arable land) may lead to violent conflicts over access and use, which in turn, may cause populations to be forcibly displaced to new areas and cause tensions or conflicts with host communities, perpetuating a cycle of conflict [214, 215]. These complex pathways also align with the notion of climate change being a ‘threat multiplier”, compounding and cascading impacts on migration/displacement, conflict, and ultimately health [2]. In an era of “polycrisis” where multiple global crises, such as climate change, international wars and Covid-19, are increasingly intertwined and multiplicative, their impacts and effects can be felt far beyond one nation’s borders to have global impacts [216]. The individual pathways depicted and vulnerabilities described in our conceptual framework are likewise worsened through the confluence of crises across the climate change, displacement/migration, conflict, and health nexus.

Our findings also support emerging evidence describing the concept of risk exchange, particularly as it relates to migration and displacement in the broader context of climate change, conflict, and health [16, 50]. Numerous studies described complex risk exchanges, whereby individuals are confronted with one set of stressors, challenges, and opportunities in their places of origin in exchange for a different set in their eventual destinations [50, 59, 61, 74, 77, 78, 94]. These stressors, challenges, and opportunities are related to ecological, social, economic, and political determinants of health that may act as complex and intersecting push or pull factors related to migration and displacement [16, 17, 217]. Similar to other studies, we found instances of immobility following migration/displacement in the context of climate change [218, 219]. Articles described migrants and displaced persons being trapped or immobile in places of destination due to worsening health and socio-economic outcomes. However, in contrast to the immobility literature, we did not find examples of populations being immobile in their places of origin or unable to migrate due to social determinants of health [220–222]. This is likely a reflection of our search strategy and inclusion criteria’s focus on migration and displacement.

Our findings also differ from the literature with respect to causality and attribution. Given our global focus, it was challenging to determine the primary drivers of conflict, migration/displacement, and health. Rather, we uncovered a system of relationships that interact with one another in multiplicative and deleterious ways, ultimately producing a system of challenges and consequences at the nexus of climate change, migration/displacement, conflict, and health. This finding highlights the need to consider the specific and evolving contexts when developing responses at this nexus [223].

The findings of this scoping review have several implications for research and practice at the nexus of climate change, conflict, migration/displacement, and health. Findings of this review could inform a systematic review that further disentangles the interrelated relationships and drivers, with a focus on examining the strength and direction of associations, effect sizes, and prevalence of health-related outcomes. Such an approach will support causal inference in addition to describing general patterns by study design and related features including sample size, study site(s), and risk of bias. Moreover, as climate change vulnerability assessments are a key research component in responding to climate change, considerations related to conflict and migration/displacement are largely absent from these assessments [14]. Future research may employ predictive forecasting methods related to conflict and migration/displacement as a part of vulnerability assessment to address this gap in research.

Inherent in the complexities underlying this nexus are the numerous typologies related to climate change (e.g., direct and indirect effects), conflict (e.g., domestic violence, gender-based violence, political violence, communal violence, armed conflicts), migration/displacement (e.g., im(mobilities), forced, voluntary, temporary, seasonal, economic, rural–urban, rural-rural, urban-urban, international, internal, optimization), and health (e.g., physical, communicable, non-communicable, mental, chronic, acute, wellbeing, maternal and child health, nutrition, health systems and access). Findings of this review could inform future work that seeks to explore the potential for a framework of, but not limited to, these typologies to support standard definitions and systems for data collection, and eventually, monitoring and evaluation of interventions at this nexus. Such work may also allow for the conduct of meta-analyses at this nexus.

Future research should also recognize and embrace the complex and non-linear nature amongst climate change, conflict, migration/displacement, and health, while utilizing systems thinking approaches. Systems thinking can help foster our understanding of complex dynamics by focusing on an entire system (e.g., climate change, conflict, migration/displacement, and health) and the relationships among its components over time, as opposed to analyzing individual or dyadic components separately [224, 225]. The ‘climate change, conflict, migration/displacement and health’ system represents a complex adaptive systems (CAS), that is, a system that involves multiple components that interact and react to other components in the system, as well as the system as a whole, leading to emergent outcomes [226]. The principles and tools of CAS can be used to model the interactions and impacts of climate change, as well as adaptations to climate change, in order to better understand climate related interactions and implications related to conflict, migration/displacement and health [223]. Feedback loops are a key component of CAS, in which one component of the system impacts another component, which then feeds back to impact the original component in either positive (reinforcing) or negative (balancing) manners [227]. Practically, these mechanisms allow for non-linear dynamics, emergent behaviors and decision-making processes to be modeled within a system [223, 227–229].

As an example, riverine erosion due to climate change induced flooding, coupled with displacement may lead to overcrowding, reduced access to safe water sources and increased spread of infectious diseases [230, 231]. At the same time, flooding may also damage health facility infrastructure, which may lead to interruptions in service delivery and further exacerbate adverse health outcomes [232, 233]. These dynamics are further shaped by the adaptive capacities of individuals, communities, institutions, and systems to anticipate, prevent, prepare, and respond to climate threats that may also be shaped by factors such as weak governance and health systems, inadequate health facility infrastructure, and a lack of emergency shelter [231, 234–239]. Interactions among these factors may lead to delayed decision-making and responses and ultimately consequences related to interruptions in service delivery and exacerbated adverse health outcomes [233, 234]. At the same time, these dynamics are likely to be further compounded among individuals, households and communities through risk multiplicative interactions related to socio-economic status, access to resources, conflict sensitivity, and social capital, which will shape and reshape the adaptive capacities at the individual, household and community level [57, 235, 238, 240, 241]. The principles and tools of CAS represent a noteworthy means to model these dynamic and evolving interactions, as well as those depicted in our conceptual framework, to better inform our understanding at this nexus and ultimately inform adaptation strategies.

Community-based systems dynamics is another method of systems thinking and CAS, that relies on participatory engagement of diverse stakeholders to map out non-linear causal mechanisms including feedback loops, within a given system to prioritize potential programming [242]. Future research using community-based systems dynamics could refine our conceptual framework with both qualitative and quantitative methods to capture the climate change, conflict, migration/displacement, and health ‘system’ in a specific humanitarian setting. The conceptual framework, also referred to as a causal loop diagram within community-based systems dynamics, could then be transformed into a quantitative model for the examination of interrelationships among factors and their evolving relationships to the whole system at this nexus [243–246]. A strength of community-based systems dynamics is that it allows researchers and practitioners to quantitatively simulate and evaluate potential scenarios including, potential mitigation efforts, adaptations, responses, programs or policies, without real-world and time implementation and evaluation of each scenario [247]. This is particularly relevant for low-resource and humanitarian settings where contexts are continually evolving, presenting challenges for research and programming, and time, financial resources, and human resources are limited [248]. Lastly, a systems thinking approach reinforces global calls to move away from siloed disciplines and sectoral programming, particularly in humanitarian settings where intersectoral is critical.

The findings of this study also have important implications for humanitarian programming and policy. Given the complex and non-linear system at this nexus, as depicted in our conceptual framework, humanitarian practitioners and policymakers should recognize and critically consider this complexity and evolving contexts when developing programs, scaling up climate-resilient health services, delivering health and social services, and developing policies [237]. Our conceptual framework also serves as a complement to existing operational frameworks related to climate resilient health systems, policies and programs for humanitarian organizations [237, 249]. These frameworks provide important guidance for decision makers on how health systems can systematically address challenges and better anticipate, prevent, prepare for climate-related health risks in both low-resource and humanitarian settings. Our conceptual framework builds on these frameworks by providing a nuanced depiction of intersecting relationships among conflict and migration/displacement in humanitarian settings, in the context of climate change. For example, forecasting of health outcomes patterns in the context of climate change should consider the occurrence and impacts of migration/displacement and vice versa as depicted in our framework to strengthen the precision of forecasting models, and ultimately inform and redefine innovative adaptive responses related to programming and policy [250, 251].

Moreover, our findings and conceptual framework indicate that multisectoral collaborations are clearly necessary when responding to challenges and differing contexts of humanitarian crises at this nexus. These could include collaborations among civil engineers, urban planners, architects, Meteorologists, climate scientists, various Ministries (e.g. health, environment and finance), and humanitarian actors to develop sustainable sanitation and water resource management technologies, for example. Such collaborations could support the development of adequate infrastructure in urban, informal settlements, including safe water and sanitation facilities to reduce climate vulnerabilities and improve necessary conditions for improved health [252]. Moreover, community engagement is vital for climate-informed humanitarian programming by identifying nature-based solutions that can incorporate indigenous and local knowledge into humanitarian programming, readiness, and responses [237].

Several limitations are present in this scoping review. We only included articles published in the English language, which may have resulted in the exclusion of relevant articles, particularly those conducted by smaller community-based organizations or published in regional journals. We used time limits reflecting the search date of three previous reviews and then relied on the syntheses of those three reviews to capture the literature prior to 2016. Therefore, we may have excluded potentially relevant articles that were not included in the three reviews. Moreover, our focus on humanitarian settings limits the generalizability of our findings to non-humanitarian or high-resource settings where similar dynamics at the nexus of climate change, conflict, migration/displacement, and health may be occurring.

We also faced challenges executing the searches for the grey literature sources. The search engines, search options varied widely across sources. Some sources allowed for advanced search options and others had limits on number of search terms. To address this challenge, we conducted binary searches of all terms, (e.g., climate change and conflict) to ensure the comprehensiveness of the searches, which added substantial time to the grey literature searches, as well as reliability checks of the search strategies for each source. Relatedly, only one reviewer screened the grey literature due to time constraints. To address this limitation, we conducted reliability checks of the article screenings to ensure strict adherence to the inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Most of the included studies utilized a cross-sectional study design and methodology. Given the evolving, nonlinear, and dynamic relationships among climate change, conflict, migration/displacement and health, particularly the cascading and multiplicative impacts on health, we were unable to assess longer-term trajectories and impacts at this nexus. Moreover, articles were only included in this review if they focused on climate change in the context of at least two other factors (e.g., conflict, migration/displacement, health). However, some articles had a greater focus a dyad or binary relationship among factors in their reporting of result, rather than the intersection of all factors in a domain. For example, an article may have explore conflict and health in the context of climate change; however, linkages to health may have been underreported and explored. This presented challenges during data extraction, particularly related to extracting pathways. As a result, at times interpretation was necessary. Unfortunately, this limitation was unavoidable given that programming and related research at the nexus of climate change, conflict, migration/displacement and health remains siloed.

Our findings raise concerns over the barriers migrants and displaced populations face when accessing health services. These reinforce the need for the strengthening of health systems, focusing on health services that are migrant-inclusive, culturally appropriate, attentive to the health impacts of climate change, and climate-resilient. For example, a migrant-inclusive and climate-resilient health system should examine epidemiological profiles of the migrant population, as well as cultural and language factors, and have various health and climate-related surveillance systems in place [20, 234, 253]. This would allow for a health system that better anticipates, prevents, prepares for, and responds to shifting epidemiological profiles, climate-related events, and climate-related health risks.

Conclusion

In the context of accelerating climate change and increasing humanitarian emergencies, there is a critical need to better understand the ways in which climate change, conflict, migration/displacement, and health relate to one another in order to protect and promote health. Towards this need, this scoping review identified and mapped complex, interrelated and non-linear relationships and pathways underlying this nexus. Our findings revealed that the convergence of climate change, conflict, migration/displacement and health compounds entrenched risks and vulnerabilities or catalyzes new ones, thereby exacerbating humanitarian demands. Our conceptual framework illustrates this complexity in an effort to inform future research and practice, but also to encourage critical considerations of such complexity for research, humanitarian programming, and policy. The conceptual framework underscores the need for systems thinking approaches by academics and humanitarian practitioners alike, to help interrogate and disentangle these evolving relationships and provide actionable adaptive responses for humanitarian settings. Our findings reinforce global calls to abandon siloed approaches and programming and rather adopt integrated, multidisciplinary approaches and collaborations to strengthen health systems. At a minimum, climate change must be incorporated into adaptation frameworks and humanitarian program response plans. The uptake of these changes will ultimately enable health systems to better support the experiences and health needs of migrant and displaced populations, and improve the global preparedness to respond to climate hazards and climate-related health risks.
