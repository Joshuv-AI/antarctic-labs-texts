Trends in smoking prevalence before and after the emergence of vaping in Aotearoa/New Zealand among 14–15-year-olds identifying as Māori, Pacific, European, or Asian: an interrupted time series analysis of repeated cross-sectional data, 2003–2024
Authors: Sam Egger, Andrew Waa, Michael David, Judith McCool, Lucy Hardie, Marianne F. Weber, Qingwei Luo, Jamie Egger, Becky Freeman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundEthnic inequities in health and mortality in Aotearoa/New Zealand have been driven in part by historic and ongoing inequities in smoking prevalence, with Māori and Pacific peoples most affected. Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed up to July 2025 using the following search terms ("e-cigarettes" OR "vaping") AND ("smoking" OR "tobacco use") AND ("adolescents" OR " youth") AND ("cross-sectional" OR "population-level" OR "population-based" OR "ecological" OR "longitudinal" OR "cohort") AND (“Aotearoa” or "New Zealand"). Four studies were identified, of which only one was a cross-sectional, population-level, longitudinal or cohort study comparing adolescent smoking trends before and after the emergence and/or rise of vaping in Aotearoa/New Zealand. The study was conducted by members of our author group and concluded that vaping may have slowed the rates of decline in ever- and regular-smoking, while having little or no impact on daily-smoking. However, the study did not assess whether changes in smoking trends varied across four ethnic groups in Aotearoa, including Māori and Pacific youth as priority populations for health equity.

Added value of this study

Using the same data source as the previous study, we conducted similar analyses but stratified by ethnicity. We found no evidence that vaping is displacing adolescent smoking in any of the four ethnic groups analysed. On the contrary, our findings suggest that vaping may have slowed Aotearoa's progress in reducing regular-smoking among Māori, Pacific, and European adolescents, with more consequential implications for Māori and Pacific youth.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our findings raise concerns about the continued advocacy by some public health stakeholders for relatively easy access to vaping products, and government policies that have enabled such access. These concerns are relevant not only to public health, but also to social justice and Indigenous rights.

Introduction

In Aotearoa/New Zealand, ethnic inequities in health and mortality have been a persistent feature of the public health environment,1 and several studies have quantified the extent to which tobacco smoking has contributed to these disparities.2, 3, 4, 5 According to a 2018 analysis of 2006–2011 mortality data linked to smoking status information from the 2006 census, the absolute mortality gap between Māori and European adults would have been reduced by 12.2% for men and 21.2% for women under a counterfactual scenario where nobody in Aotearoa smoked.3 A 2020 analysis found that between 2013 and 2015, 22.6% of all Māori deaths were attributable to smoking, compared with 13.8% of deaths among Pacific peoples and 12.3% among non-Māori/non-Pacific people.4 More recently, a 2023 modelling analysis of smoking prevalence data up to 2019 suggested that under a ‘business-as-usual scenario’, smoking would continue to drive substantial inequities in health and mortality between Māori and non-Māori.5

Long-standing tobacco-related health inequities such as these have been driven, in part, by differences in smoking prevalence across ethnic groups—a pattern reflected in recent data from the 2023/24 New Zealand Health Survey. Across this 2-year period, current smoking prevalence among those aged 15 and over were 17.2% for Māori, 14.7% for Pacific peoples, 7.4% for European/Other, and 5.0% for Asian populations.6 These long-standing inequities in smoking prevalence across ethnic groups reflect, in large part, fundamental and structural determinants, including the interrelated and ongoing impacts of colonisation for Māori, racism and socioeconomic disadvantage affecting both Māori and Pacific peoples, and inequitable exposure to commercial determinants of health across groups. While the above prevalence figures include both adolescents and adults, most of the health consequences of smoking emerge in adulthood. However, for most individuals who smoke, the roots of tobacco-related health problems often trace back to smoking initiation during adolescence, by increasing the likelihood of nicotine dependence and continued smoking into adulthood.7,8 Therefore, addressing ethnic differences in adolescent smoking prevalence is an important element in reducing health and mortality inequities attributable to smoking.

Adolescent smoking prevalence in Aotearoa has declined considerably over the past two and a half decades. From 1999 to 2024, the proportion of 14–15-year-olds engaging in regular-smoking (i.e., at least monthly) decreased by 86% among Māori (from 42.8% to 5.9%), 89% among Pacific youth (from 28.4% to 3.2%), 92% among European youth (from 27.1% to 2.3%) and 94% among Asian youth (from 12.0% to 0.7%).9 Despite these remarkable declines, recent evidence suggests that the emergence of e-cigarettes in Aotearoa—beginning in around 2010—may have slowed progress in reducing adolescent smoking.10 Specifically, in a recent interrupted time series analysis of nearly 700,000 year 10 students aged 14–15 years, we found that the rates of decline in ever-smoking and regular-smoking among students slowed significantly from 2010 onwards, coinciding with the emergence and rise of vaping in Aotearoa.10 However, while our previous findings shed light on the overall impacts of adolescent vaping on smoking uptake, they do not provide insights into how these effects might vary across the four largest ethnic groups in Aotearoa (Māori, Pacific, European, and Asian). In the absence of ethnicity-specific analyses, the extent to which vaping might be reinforcing or mitigating ethnic differences in adolescent smoking remains unclear. For instance, it may be the case that vaping is displacing smoking in some ethnic groups—potentially accelerating reductions in smoking—while in others, it may be slowing or even reversing prior progress, possibly due to a ‘gateway effect’, whereby young people who initiate nicotine use with e-cigarettes are more likely to progress to smoking.11, 12, 13 To date, no study in Aotearoa has examined this issue empirically, either at the population or individual level.

To address this gap, we expanded our previous interrupted time series analysis of adolescent smoking by stratifying year 10 students (aged 14–15 years) by ethnicity. Specifically, using population-level repeat cross-sectional data from 2003 to 2024, we assessed whether the rates of decline in ever-smoking, regular-smoking, and daily-smoking changed following the emergence of vaping for Māori, Pacific, European, and Asian adolescents. By examining ethnic group–specific changes in smoking trajectories, we aimed to clarify how the emergence and rise of vaping may have affected smoking patterns across groups with historically different levels of smoking and unequal burdens of tobacco-related harm in adulthood. Understanding these dynamics is important for informing efforts to reduce health inequities, thereby supporting compliance with Te Tiriti o Waitangi (the foundational agreement between Māori and the Crown) to advance health equity for Māori, in line with Aotearoa's commitments under the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP), and with the WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (FCTC) obligations to address inequities affecting Indigenous peoples.14,15 Similar attention is needed for other priority populations, such as Pacific peoples, who also face systemic health inequities in Aotearoa.

Methods

Research team

We recognise how our lived experiences, cultural identities and positions of privilege may influence the way we frame, analyse and interpret our research, particularly in relation to Indigenous peoples and other ethnic groups. The lead author (SE) and three co-authors (MD, MFW, JE) are of Australian/European descent, JM and LH are of Aotearoa/European descent, BF is of Canadian/European descent, AW identifies as (Aotearoa) Māori and QL as Chinese Australian. We recognise the importance of Te Tiriti o Waitangi in Aotearoa as well as the need for equity-focused research that supports the aspirations of Māori, Pacific, Asian, and other communities.

Study design and participants

The Action for Smokefree 2025 (ASH, formerly Action on Smoking and Health) Year 10 Snapshot Survey is a repeat cross-sectional survey of year 10 students in Aotearoa, conducted annually since 1999, except in 2020 due to COVID-19-related school closures. It is one of the largest ongoing surveys tracking adolescent smoking behaviours in the world, with annual participation ranging between 20,000 and 33,000 students, representing approximately 35–50% of the national year 10 population. In 2014, the survey was expanded to include a question on whether respondents had ever tried an e-cigarette, and by 2015, additional questions assessing e-cigarette use frequency were introduced. Each year, all schools with year 10 students are invited to take part, except distance education institutions. Further methodological details on the Snapshot Survey are available elsewhere.16

For this study, we used publicly available population-level data from the Snapshot Survey spanning 2003 to 2024 for students aged 14–15 years by ethnicity (and also included data from 1999 to 2002 in sensitivity analyses).9 Of the 252 smoking prevalence estimates used in the primary analyses, 212 were extracted directly from reported numerical values, while the remaining 40 were extracted from graphs within ASH reports using WebPlotDigitizer Version 5.2,17 as these values were not explicitly reported. Two authors (SE and JE) independently performed the data extraction including the graphical extractions. Only a small number of discrepancies between extractors occurred, all of which were minor (no more than 0.1 percentage points) and were resolved through joint review and agreement on the final value. We also obtained Consumer Price Index (CPI) data for 'cigarettes and tobacco' and weekly income per person by ethnicity data (the ‘all people’ series, which includes those with zero income but does not capture ‘pocket money’) from ‘Stats NZ’18,19 for use in sensitivity analyses controlling for tobacco affordability. Smoking and vaping prevalence estimates reported by ASH exclude missing data, but the proportion of missing data is reported to be very low (less than 2%).20 Ethical approval for data collection in the Year 10 Snapshot Survey was granted by the Multi-region Ethics Committee (MEC/07/10/141). Our analysis used publicly available population-level data from ASH reports, requiring no direct ethics approval.

Measures

Students were categorised as having 'ever-smoked' if they responded 'yes' to the question, 'Have you ever smoked a cigarette, even just a few puffs?' Similarly, those who responded 'yes' to 'Have you ever tried electronic cigarettes (even just a single puff or vape)?' were classified as having 'ever-vaped'. Students reporting smoking and/or vaping 'At least once a day' were categorised as 'daily-smoking' and/or 'daily-vaping', respectively. 'Regular-smoking' and/or 'regular-vaping' included students who reported smoking and/or vaping at least daily, weekly, or monthly. Ethnicity classifications were coded by ASH using prioritised ethnicity classification based on Ministry of Health protocols.21 This approach assigns each individual to a single ethnic category for analysis purposes, which necessarily simplifies self-identified ethnicities into a standardised label. The terms ‘ethnic’ and ‘ethnicity’ are used in this manuscript as a pragmatic shorthand for broad population categories, noting that these include Indigenous people (Māori) as well as groups based on regions of origin (Pacific, European, Asian).

Statistical methods

Methods were largely adapted from our previous work.10 'Crude prevalence' estimates for smoking and vaping correspond to simple unadjusted percentages. Segmented logistic regression (interrupted time-series) analysis was used to assess whether, for Māori, Pacific, European, and Asian adolescents, the rates of decline in the prevalence of three smoking outcomes—ever-smoking, regular-smoking, and daily-smoking—changed since the year e-cigarettes started being used at meaningful levels across Aotearoa (i.e., beyond isolated use). This year is termed the 'change-year' in the regression models. However, as mentioned previously,10 selecting the most appropriate change-year is not straightforward because 7% of 14–15-year-olds had already tried e-cigarettes by 2012, the first year adolescents in Aotearoa were surveyed about their vaping.22 Consequently, we selected 2010 as our primary change-year, drawing on global trends from countries such as the US and regions within the UK, where youth vaping reached noticeable levels around 2010/201123,24 (although we also varied the change-year from 2008 to 2018 in sensitivity analyses).

For each of the three smoking prevalence outcomes (ever-smoking, regular-smoking, and daily-smoking), ethnicity-specific segmented logistic regression models were fitted separately for Māori, Pacific, European, and Asian adolescents as follows:Logit(crude−smoking−prevalenceyear)=α+β1background−trendyear+β2vaping−trendyearwhere crude-smoking-prevalenceyear is incorporated in the model using the full sample size of observed counts nyear/Nyear (nyear is the number of 14- to 15-year-olds experiencing the smoking outcome in that year, and Nyear is the total number of 14- to 15-year-olds surveyed); background-trendyear = 1, …, 22 corresponding to survey years 2003, …, 2024 (excluding 2020 when there was no survey due to COVID-19); and vaping-trendyear takes the value 0 before 2010 followed by single year increases (excluding the COVID-19 year). Heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent (HAC) standard errors were used to account for potential autocorrelation.

In our previous study, 1999 was chosen as the model start-year because, in the ethnicity-combined data, adolescent smoking prevalence in Aotearoa began declining reasonably consistently from that year. However, in the current study, 2003 was selected as the model start-year because the rate of decline in smoking prevalence from 1999 to 2003 was significantly slower than the decline from 2003 to 2009 for several ethnic groups and smoking behaviours (a pattern that might have been due to early adoption of smoke-free policies following the 2003 passage of the Smoke-free Environments Amendment Act, prior to its full implementation in 2004). As a result, the declines from 2003 to 2009 provide a more reliable indication of smoking trends leading up to the emergence of vaping in 2010 (nonetheless, we also conducted sensitivity analyses also incorporating data from 1999 to 2002, while introducing an additional change-year from 2004 in the segmented models).

‘Smoothed' smoking prevalence estimates (%) from 2003 to 2024 were calculated using model-estimated values. The ‘background trend’ represents estimates with the vaping-trend parameter (β2) set to 0, projecting the pre-2010 smoking trend forward as if vaping did not influence smoking rates. The ‘observed trend’ represents estimates with all model parameters, reflecting the actual smoking trend observed from 2010 onwards including the impacts of vaping. Smoothed background and observed smoking prevalence estimates (%) for 2024 are denoted B24 and O24, respectively. Excess numbers of students in 2024 who experienced each smoking outcome per 1000 students due to changes in the observed trend at the change-year were calculated as E24 = 10 × (O24–B24), with negative values corresponding to fewer experiencing the smoking outcome.

Three sets of sensitivity analyses were also conducted. First, to account for possible disagreements with our choice of change-year = 2010, we re-fitted the segmented regression models with change-year values ranging from 2008 to 2018. This range reflects the inherent uncertainty in identifying the year when vaping began influencing smoking trends. Second, to account for the potential confounding effect of tobacco affordability on smoking prevalence over time, we re-fitted the segmented regression models, adjusting for the log of the number of cigarettes that could be purchased per week by Māori, Pacific, European, and Asian 15–19-year-olds in Aotearoa. These values were calculated by dividing the average weekly income by the nominal cigarette price between 2003 and 2024 for each ethnic group, log-transforming the result. Because Stats NZ does not report ethnicity-specific income for 14–15-year-olds, we used income estimates for 15–19-year-olds as the closest available proxy. From 2003 to 2007, ethnicity-specific income estimates were extrapolated using linear regression, by modelling ethnicity-specific income as a function of average income for all ethnicities based on data from 2008 to 2024, the period during which ethnicity-specific income data are available from Stats NZ. Third, to demonstrate that the rates of decline in smoking prevalence from 1999 to 2003 were slower than from 2003 to 2009 for several ethnic groups and smoking behaviours, we re-fitted the segmented regression models using an extended dataset that included smoking prevalence estimates from 1999 to 2002. This involved introducing an additional segment into the models, with 1999 as the revised start-year and an additional change-year at 2004, to separately capture trends from 1999 to 2003 and 2003 to 2009. We also conducted supplementary analyses by re-fitting the segmented regression models for all study participants combined from 2003 to 2024.

Results

Vaping prevalence increased markedly from 2017 to 2021 within each ethnic group and then stabilised or declined slightly through to 2024. From 2003 to 2024, all four groups experienced substantial reductions in the prevalence of ever-smoking, regular-smoking, and daily-smoking. For instance, between 2003 and 2024 the (crude) prevalence of regular-smoking declined by 84% for Māori students (from 37.4% to 6.0%), 86% for Pacific students (from 23.4% to 3.2%), 87% for European students (from 17.9% to 2.4%), and 91% for Asian students (from 8.7% to 0.8%) (Fig. 3).

The segmented logistic regression analysis indicated significant changes in ever-smoking trends for Māori, European and Asians student from the change-year (2010) onwards (Fig. 2). For these groups, the slope of the smoothed observed ever-smoking prevalence trend deviated significantly upwards from the projected background trend in 2010 (p = 0.025, p < 0.001 and p < 0.018 for Māori, European and Asian students respectively). In 2024, there were 64, 72, and 14 more Māori, European, and Asian students per 1000 who had tried smoking than expected if ever-smoking rates had continued along the pre-vaping era trajectory, respectively (i.e., the E24 values). No significant change in trend was detected for Pacific students (p = 0.263), although the estimated prevalence in 2024 was lower than expected under the background trend (O24 = 12.7% vs. B24 = 15.7%), corresponding to 30 fewer students per 1000 than expected who had ever-smoked.

For regular-smoking, the slope of the observed trend (solid line) showed a significant upward deviation from the projected background trend (dashed line) in 2010 for Māori, Pacific, and European students (p = 0.012, p = 0.003, and p < 0.001 respectively; Fig. 3). In 2024, there were an estimated 20, 15, and 13 more Māori, Pacific and European students per 1000 who smoked regularly than expected if regular-smoking rates had continued along the pre-vaping era trajectory. No significant change in trend was detected for Asian students (p = 0.141).

For daily-smoking, a significant change in trend from 2010 was detected only for European students (p = 0.004; Fig. 4). In 2024, there were two more European students per 1000 who smoked daily than expected if daily-smoking rates had continued along the pre-vaping era trajectory. Although the excess was slightly larger for Pacific students (E24 = 4 per 1000) compared to European (E24 = 2 per 1000), the change from 2010 was not statistically significant (p = 0.271), likely reflecting the smaller Pacific sample size. No significant changes in trend were detected for Māori (p = 0.681) or Asian (p = 0.243) students, with small differences between observed and background prevalence in 2024 (E24 = −2 and 1 per 1000, respectively).

In sensitivity analyses varying the change-year from 2008 to 2018, excess ever-smoking in 2024 ranged from 32 to 85 per 1000 for Māori, −37 to −13 for Pacific, 68 to 75 for European, and 13 to 16 for Asian students (Supplementary Table S1). For regular-smoking, the excess ranged from 11 to 25 for Māori, 7 to 16 for Pacific, 13 to 15 for European, and 3 to 4 for Asian students (Supplementary Table S2). For daily-smoking, the range was −4 to 4 for Māori, 2 to 5 for Pacific, 2 to 4 for European, and 1 to 3 for Asian students (Supplementary Table S3). Overall, the choice of change-year did not substantively alter our findings. Sensitivity analyses adjusting for tobacco affordability for each ethnic group produced estimates similar to those from the main models (Supplementary Figures S1-3), indicating that affordability was not a confounder of estimates from our primary models. Likewise, sensitivity analyses incorporating data from 1999 to 2002 and specifying an additional change-year at 2004 showed very similar overall results, but also demonstrated that declines from 1999 to 2003 were significantly slower than those from 2003 to 2009 across several ethnic group and smoking behaviour combinations, justifying our choice of 2003 as the start-year in the primary models (Supplementary Figures S4-6). In supplementary analyses including all study participants combined, the estimated excess numbers of students per 1000 in 2024 were 65 for ever-smoking (p < 0.001), 13 for regular-smoking (p < 0.001), and 2 for daily-smoking (p = 0.329) (Supplementary Figure S7). These values were similar to the 2023 excess numbers reported in our previous publication10: 60 for ever-smoking (p < 0.001), 12 for regular-smoking (p < 0.001), and −2 for daily-smoking (p = 0.066).

Discussion

This is the first study to assess changes in adolescent smoking trends before and after the emergence of vaping among ethnic groups in Aotearoa. The current study builds on our previous work, which was not stratified by ethnicity.10 In our earlier work, we found no support for the conclusion of a widely cited 2020 study that vaping might be displacing smoking among 14–15-year-olds in Aotearoa.20 In fact, not only did we find no evidence to suggest that vaping was displacing smoking, but our results pointed in the opposite direction: declines in both ever-smoking and regular-smoking slowed from 2010, coinciding with the emergence of vaping.

Our current findings extend on our earlier work by showing that there is similarly no evidence that vaping is displacing smoking among any of the four ethnic groups analysed, including Māori and Pacific youth as priority populations for health equity. On the contrary, rather than accelerating pre-existing declines in smoking, the emergence of vaping coincided with slowing declines in smoking—particularly for ever-smoking and regular-smoking—with pronounced excesses of regular-smoking among Māori, Pacific, and European adolescents. Although Māori and Pacific adolescents do not appear to have been disproportionately affected compared to European adolescents in terms of excess numbers, this should not be interpreted as a neutral outcome. Instead, it reflects a hollow form of equity—one in which similar levels of increased tobacco burden were distributed across Māori, Pacific, and European adolescents, but with more serious implications for Māori and Pacific youth, who already face entrenched tobacco-related inequities. Furthermore, vaping itself is not a risk-free activity,25,26 with systematic review evidence suggesting that, for some diseases, e-cigarette–only risks are comparable to those of cigarettes, and that dual use is riskier than smoking alone.26 Given these risks, our results suggests that the comparatively high rates of vaping among Māori and Pacific adolescents are, at the very least, compounding existing health inequities without delivering meaningful tobacco control benefits for this age group. At worst, they may also be contributing to increased smoking uptake, as suggested by our results.

Our findings have important implications for how e-cigarettes are framed within public policy in Aotearoa. Proponents typically advocate for e-cigarettes as ‘harm reduction’ alternatives that might displace tobacco smoking, either by supporting cessation or reducing initiation. This ‘harm reduction’ framing, however, is not only highly contested by many public health stakeholders but is also contradicted by our results, at least in the context of adolescents in Aotearoa. Moreover, it is important to recognise that this framing is also constrained to biomedical paradigms that focus narrowly on physical health outcomes, unlike the more holistic harm-reduction approaches in other fields, which often include social and legal harms.27 For the Indigenous Māori population, the harms associated with nicotine—whether from e-cigarettes or tobacco—extend beyond physical health, affecting social, cultural, and psychological wellbeing as well. Prior to European colonisation, Māori were a culture free from addiction. With colonisation, tobacco was introduced as a trade commodity, and since then, nicotine addiction has continued to undermine Māori self-determination (tino rangatiratanga), while also serving as a persistent reminder of colonisation within Māori communities.27 In this context, our findings suggest that so-called ‘harm reduction’ vaping products have not only failed to reduce harm among Māori adolescents, they have introduced an additional source of nicotine dependence and cultural harm, thereby perpetuating colonial barriers on Māori autonomy. When Māori perspectives such as these are considered, serious concerns arise about government policies that have enabled widespread retail availability of e-cigarettes in Aotearoa and the ongoing advocacy by some public health stakeholders for such policies—not only from a public health standpoint, but also from a social justice and Indigenous rights perspective.27,28

Over the period the Snapshot Survey has tracked vaping behaviours, a clear pattern of widening inequities in vaping prevalence has emerged (Fig. 1). Māori adolescents consistently recorded the highest rates across all three vaping behaviours, followed by Pacific adolescents, then European adolescents, with Asian adolescents having the lowest rates. Given these inequities, one might reasonably anticipate a corresponding pattern of slowing in smoking declines—that is, where vaping prevalence is highest, one might expect the greatest impact on smoking trends. However, this assumption overlooks a key point: vaping prevalence alone is only one part of the equation. The impact of vaping on smoking depends not only on the prevalence of vaping, but also on the strength and direction of its association with future smoking. Unfortunately, this relationship has not been measured in Aotearoa, as no individual-level cohort studies have yet been conducted—let alone cohort studies stratified by ethnicity—to assess whether the strength of the vaping–smoking link differs across ethnic groups of adolescents. Nevertheless, our recent Australian retrospective cohort study of 12–17-year-olds offers useful context.29 In that study, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander adolescents—a population with high vaping and smoking prevalence that has also experienced marginalisation as a result of colonisation—had a lower adjusted incidence rate ratio (IRR) for smoking initiation associated with prior vaping compared to non-Indigenous youth (2.6 vs 5.0 respectively; p = 0.07 for difference in IRRs). This finding demonstrates that groups with high vaping prevalence can exhibit lower relative risks of smoking initiation associated with vaping than those with lower prevalence of vaping. This is not contradictory, because in epidemiology, the prevalence of exposure is typically unrelated to the relative risk of an outcome between exposed and unexposed groups. In this case, the higher IRR observed among Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander adolescents may reflect contextual factors such as economic constraints (e.g., affordability and price sensitivity), social norms, or the ways in which specific vaping and smoking behaviours are linked. Moreover, this finding underscores the importance of considering not only vaping prevalence when interpreting ethnic disparities in smoking trends, but also the strength and direction of vaping–smoking associations. It also highlights the urgent need for an Aotearoa-based cohort study to examine these relationships directly.

Our population-level findings broadly align with evidence from individual-level prospective cohort studies conducted outside of Aotearoa, which have consistently found that adolescents who have vaped are more likely to subsequently initiate smoking.11, 12, 13 While a ‘common liability’ hypothesis has been proposed to suggest that the associations observed in cohort studies may be artefactual rather than causal,30 the hypothesis remains unsubstantiated. That is, all previous cohort studies—including those adjusting for various proxies of common liabilities for smoking and vaping—have found positive associations between adolescent vaping and subsequent smoking (although some were not statistically significant). Likewise, no previous cohort study has demonstrated that adjusting for common liabilities can reverse the association from positive to negative. In contrast, ecological studies have reported mixed conclusions. For instance, a widely cited 2019 U.S. interrupted time-series analysis linked accelerated declines in smoking to rising vaping prevalence,31 whereas another U.S. interrupted time-series analysis reached the opposite conclusion.32 Divergences in findings—both between ecological studies, and between ecological and cohort studies—are consistent with the well-recognised limitations of ecological analyses.33 Such limitations include their limited capacity to control for confounding, and their inability to link individual exposures that precede individual outcomes.33 In the specific context of ecological analysis of vaping and smoking rates, contemporaneous jurisdiction-level influences—for example, policy settings, cigarette prices, and shifting social norms—often vary across regions and over time and can easily confound associations, while available region-level summaries often lack the joint detail required for adequate control.34 Furthermore, a recent methodological critique of a systematic review described in detail the flawed arguments some researchers have used to dismiss cohort study evidence on this topic, while giving undue weight to ecological analyses.35 Taking all of this together, while ecological studies have a role to play in the triangulation of evidence,30 they should always be interpreted cautiously and in the context of the stronger, more consistent evidence from cohort studies. Our current ecological analysis is no exception—although it contributes to the evidence base, it also underscores the need for prospective cohort studies in Aotearoa to establish whether the ecological patterns that we observed align with individual-level evidence from local populations.

Daily smoking is rare among adolescents in many countries. In 2024 in Aotearoa, the overall prevalence was just 1.2% among students aged 14–15 years,9 with no ethnic group exceeding 2.9%. This rarity likely explains why few cohort studies and no meta-analyses have examined daily smoking as a distinct outcome, in contrast to the more substantial evidence regarding vaping and smoking initiation or past-30-day smoking. Consistent with our previous analysis without stratification by ethnic group, we again observed clearer and more consistent slowing trends for ever- and regular-smoking than for daily-smoking. As noted in our earlier paper, this divergence is difficult to interpret given the limited cohort study evidence on daily smoking. One possibility that we previously raised was that vaping may encourage experimentation and non-daily regular smoking, but the time, opportunity, and financial costs required to engage in both vaping and daily smoking might mean that vaping has less influence on progression to daily smoking (at least among adolescents in Aotearoa). This possibility may also help explain our current findings, given the absence of large vaping-related excesses in daily smoking in contrast to the more apparent slowing observed for ever- and regular-smoking.

Our study has several limitations. First, the possibility of confounding from changes in tobacco-related policies is an inherent limitation of all interrupted time series analyses. While sensitivity analyses in our study suggested that cigarette affordability (and price) was unlikely to have confounded our estimates, our results remain vulnerable to confounding from changes in other tobacco-related policies over time. In this regard, however, it is important to recognise that the tobacco control environment in Aotearoa remained strong and largely cumulative over the study period; key policies introduced before 2010—including smoke-free bars and restaurants (2004) and pictorial health warnings (2008)—were retained after 2010 and likely continued to exert downward pressure on smoking prevalence. These pre-2010 policies were complemented by additional measures introduced after 2010, including the point-of-sale tobacco display ban (2012), plain packaging with enhanced pictorial warnings (2018), and the smoke-free cars law (2020). Importantly, none of these policies were rolled back during the study period, thereby contributing to a pattern of strengthening tobacco control over time (although we note that major provisions of the 2022 amendments to the Smokefree Environments and Regulated Products Act—including de-nicotinisation, reductions in retail outlets, and the tobacco-free generation policy—were repealed in early 2024 before they came into effect,36 and were therefore unlikely to have influenced the trends observed in our analyses). Taken together, the cumulative nature of tobacco control policies over the study period suggests that, if anything, the slowing in the decline of smoking prevalence that we observed may have been even more pronounced in the absence of these policies. Second, while changes in demographic composition or non-response patterns could have influenced our findings, any resulting bias would require these factors to have changed systematically over time in ways correlated with smoking behaviour. Given the consistency of the Snapshot Survey methods across years and the magnitude of the observed slowing in smoking declines, it seems unlikely that such factors meaningfully affected our results. Third, because our study focuses on adolescents, it remains uncertain whether similar changes in smoking prevalence following the emergence of vaping have occurred among adults, particularly given evidence that e-cigarettes can help some adults transition away from smoking.37 In this regard, the magnitude and direction of changes in smoking trends before and after the emergence of vaping may differ not only between adolescents and adults, but also across ethnic groups within the adult population. Future analyses of adult datasets are needed to investigate these potential differences. Fourth, our ethnicity measure reflects the broad prioritised categorisations in the ASH reports (Māori, Pacific, Asian, European), which apply the Ministry of Health's prioritised ethnicity protocol. Sub-ethnic identifiers—for example, within Pacific (e.g., Samoan, Tongan, Cook Islands Māori, Niuean) and within Asian (e.g., Chinese, Indian), as well as specific European national-origin groups—were not available across the series. Consequently, we report results at this aggregate level across all macro-ethnic groups, which may mask within-group heterogeneity, particularly in Pacific populations where multi-ethnic identification is common. Fifth, we were unable to stratify our analyses by gender overall or by gender within ethnicity. This is because the ASH reports did not consistently report gender-specific prevalence for the overall population, and although gender-specific prevalence estimates were reported within each ethnic group, the corresponding gender-by-ethnicity frequencies required for modelling were not available across the study period. Lastly, although not a limitation per se, some may mistakenly interpret the observed slowing in smoking declines as an inevitable result of ‘natural slowing’ as smoking prevalence approaches zero. However, it is important to clarify that segmented logistic regression inherently accounts for natural slowing due to its asymptotic, sigmoid-shaped curve, and that the slowing that we observed is above and beyond what would be expected from natural slowing. This is one of the many reasons that segmented logistic regression is widely recognised as a robust method for analysing binary outcomes over time38,39 and has been used in several previous interrupted time series studies of smoking trends.10,40,41

Our findings, at the very least, provide no evidence that the emergence of e-cigarettes in Aotearoa has displaced adolescent smoking in any four ethnic groups analysed. Instead, the emergence of e-cigarettes appears to have contributed to slowing pre-existing declines in smoking, particularly for non-daily regular-smoking among Māori, Pacific, and European adolescents. These patterns suggest that policies enabling easy access to vaping products are not only deepening structural and cultural harms by embedding new forms of nicotine dependence, but they appear to be undermining tobacco control progress within communities already disproportionately burdened by tobacco harm. Confronting these realities requires recognising e-cigarettes not only as a public health issue, but as a matter of social justice and Indigenous rights.
