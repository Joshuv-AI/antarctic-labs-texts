Congenital nephrotic syndrome with fulminant clinical course: A lesson for primary care physician
Authors: Neeraj Singh, Priyanka, Ritika Singh, Simran Dhamija, Kapil Bhalla, Shuchi Mehra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGalloway–Mowat syndrome is a rare autosomal recessive genetic disorder that is highly underrecognized. The phenotype is heterogeneous, but it is now widely accepted that early-onset nephrotic syndrome and microcephaly with brain malformation are characteristic features of Galloway–Mowat syndrome. It has been phenotypically defined as the presence of primary microcephaly, developmental delay, seizure tendency, and early-onset nephrotic syndrome. Rarely, congenital syphilis or other intrauterine infections can cause congenital nephrotic syndrome, a relatively uncommon illness in children with genetic reasons linked to podocin gene abnormalities. This syndrome is characterized by gyral and myelin anomalies in MRI, early onset of nephrotic syndrome, microcephaly, developmental delay, fascio-skeletal dysmorphism, along with end-stage renal disease. Here, the authors report a case of a 10-month-old male infant who was admitted for clinical symptomatology suggestive of nephrotic syndrome and delay in achieving key developmental milestones and, on examination, had failure to thrive with microcephaly. Following a thorough assessment of the child, a homozygous missense variant in exon 9 of the OSGEP gene was discovered on genetic analysis by whole-exome sequencing. This variant is suggestive of Galloway–Mowat syndrome type 3 and results in an amino acid substitution of proline for alanine at codon 286. Our index case emphasizes the importance of detailed evaluation so as to apprise and prognosticate the parents of a child having a rare and nearly fatal condition by the utilization of genetic analysis.

Introduction

Galloway–Mowat syndrome is a rare autosomal recessive/X-linked recessive disorder characterized by microcephaly, nephrotic syndrome that presents at an early age, brain abnormalities, with an incidence of less than one in a million persons has Galloway–Mowat syndrome, making it a rare disease.[1] The majority of those impacted die in their early years and present with severe illnesses. Here, the authors report a case of a patient diagnosed with Galloway–Mowat syndrome type 3.

Case Report

A 10-month-old male infant was brought to a tertiary care hospital with complaints of loose stools, failure to thrive, and anasarca since birth. The child had been previously admitted with similar complaints; hence, a possible diagnosis of congenital nephrotic syndrome was thought of, and the child was started on steroids (prednisolone at 2 mg/kg/day), which showed no response and hence was labeled as steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome (SRNS). The symptoms, however, persisted, and subsequently, the child was admitted and was worked up. The child was born full term via non non-consanguineous marriage, had a history of resuscitation at birth. He had a global developmental delay. On examination, vitals showed tachypnea; dysmorphism with microcephaly, microphthalmia, low-set ears, and a flat nasal bridge. He had signs of respiratory distress. Biochemical investigations revealed that hypoproteinemia, severe hypoalbuminemia, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, hypertriglyceridemia, hypercholesterolemia, hyponatremia, hypoglycemia, and renal function were deranged. A working diagnosis of complicated pneumonia with global developmental delay, with steroid-resistant congenital nephrotic syndrome with secondary hypertension, with acute kidney injury (AKI) was made. Patient was managed conservatively with intravenous fluids along with potassium chloride and calcium gluconate, intravenous antibiotics (ceftriaxone, linezolid, metronidazole), nebulization, oral prednisolone, enalapril, and spironolactone. Due to the involvement of various systems, gene analysis was planned, which confirmed the presence of a homozygous missense variant in the OSGEP gene. The child later succumbed to the disease process.

Discussion

Congenital nephrotic syndrome refers to nephrotic syndrome that manifests at birth or within the first three months of life. The Finnish type is the most common variant. Congenital nephrotic syndrome may also present as diffuse mesangial sclerosis associated with pseudohermaphroditism, as seen in Denys–Drash syndrome, or in association with microcephaly, developmental delay, and dysmorphic features, as in Galloway–Mowat syndrome.[2] While most cases are idiopathic, rare secondary causes include congenital infections such as cytomegalovirus, syphilis, rubella, toxoplasmosis, and hepatitis B. Congenital nephrotic syndrome may be inherited in either an autosomal recessive or X-linked pattern.[3] Infants with Galloway–Mowat syndrome are often born prematurely with a large placenta and typically exhibit failure to thrive, recurrent infections, ascites, developmental delay, and spontaneous vascular thrombosis. A renal biopsy is recommended in all cases to establish an accurate diagnosis, which is essential for determining prognosis, guiding management, and providing genetic counseling. Management of congenital nephrotic syndrome is challenging, as there is no specific therapy, and the disease is typically resistant to corticosteroids and cytotoxic agents.[4] The syndrome was first described in 1968 and is characterized by early-onset nephrotic syndrome, microcephaly, and structural brain abnormalities. Galloway–Mowat syndrome is a rare disorder with an incidence of fewer than one per million live births. Most affected children present with severe manifestations and die in early childhood.[5] This study presents cases of Galloway–Mowat syndrome and reviews all published patients with pathogenic variants in the OSGEP gene, focusing on their genetic, biochemical, radiological, and clinical profiles to broaden the understanding of this rare condition.

Conclusion

Pathogenic variants in the OSGEP gene define a distinct and uncommon subtype of Galloway–Mowat syndrome, designated as Galloway–Mowat syndrome. A review of literature from the past five years highlights the correlation between genotype and phenotype in affected individuals. In the Asian population, the c. 740G > A (p.Arg247Gln) variant in OSGEP appears to represent a founder mutation associated with a severe clinical phenotype and a particularly poor prognosis. Clinicians should consider the possibility of Galloway–Mowat syndrome in patients presenting with microcephaly and characteristic facial dysmorphism.[6] Additional diagnostic indicators may include the presence of proteinuria and neuroimaging evidence of brain abnormalities. Notably, patients with congenital nephrotic syndrome have been reported to have a markedly reduced median survival time of approximately eight months compared to those without congenital onset. For accurate clinical management and genetic counseling, early molecular diagnosis through gene sequencing is imperative. Early recognition, precise diagnosis, and comprehensive multidisciplinary care are crucial for optimizing outcomes in children affected by this rare and fatal disorder.
