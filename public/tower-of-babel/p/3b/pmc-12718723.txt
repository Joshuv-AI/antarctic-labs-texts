The Trendelenburg position decreases the threshold of dynamic variables in predicting fluid responsiveness: A prospective observational study
Authors: Chih-Chung Wu, Eric Yi-Hsiu Huang, Hui-Hsuan Ke, Yu-Hsien Wang, Wen-Kuei Chang, Chien-Kun Ting
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Goal-directed fluid therapy (GDFT) individualizes fluid administration to optimize oxygen delivery and tissue perfusion. Traditional static parameters, such as central venous and pulmonary artery wedge pressures, are inaccurate. Instead, GDFT employs dynamic variables like stroke volume variation (SVV) and pulse pressure variation (PPV) to assess preload responsiveness and guide fluid administration. However, the Trendelenburg position alters cardiovascular physiology, and the effects of different tilt angles on SVV, PPV, and other hemodynamic variables remain unclear.Methods:This single-center, observational study enrolled patients undergoing elective surgery. Following induction of general anesthesia, patients were positioned supine (0°) and then in the Trendelenburg position, with the angle adjusted to 5°, 10°, 15°, and 25°. Dynamic variables, including SVV, PPV, contractility (dP/dt), dynamic arterial elastance (Eadyn), and cardiac index (CI), at each angle were recorded and analyzed.Results:A total of 27 patients (mean age 66.7 ± 11.7 years, 79% male) were included. Increasing the Trendelenburg tilt angle from 0° to 25° was associated with a significant reduction in SVV (12.21 ± 4.26% at 0° to 6.94 ± 2.37% at 25°, p < 0.001) and PPV (13.47 ± 4.27% at 0° to 6.81 ± 3.20% at 25°, p < 0.001). SVV and PPV measured in the supine position correlated significantly with values obtained at 5°, 10°, 15°, and 25° (p < 0.001). dP/dt progressively declined from 711.85 ± 235.58 mmHg/s at 0° to 583.85 ± 166.19 mmHg/s at 25° (p < 0.001), and CI decreased from 2.51 ± 0.59 L/min/m² to 2.27 ± 0.60 L/min/m² (p = 0.001). Eadyn trended downward but did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.088).Conclusion:The thresholds of SVV and PPV for predicting fluid responsiveness decrease with progressive Trendelenburg tilt. These variables should therefore be interpreted cautiously in relation to tilt angle when guiding fluid management.

1. INTRODUCTION

Goal-directed fluid therapy (GDFT) relies on objective dynamic variables to guide decisions regarding the administration of intravenous fluids. These treatment decisions are informed by reproducible parameters that estimate intravascular volume.1–3 Current evidence suggests that perioperative GDFT based on dynamic variables derived from arterial waveform analysis is associated with better outcomes, including reduced mortality, shorter hospital stays, and fewer complications.4–6

During general anesthesia, mechanical ventilation induces cyclical changes in intrathoracic pressure, leading to corresponding fluctuations in left ventricular preload and stroke volume. Dynamic variables, such as stroke volume variation (SVV) and pulse pressure variation (PPV), are derived from analyzing these cyclic changes in arterial pressure waveforms. SVV >10% and PPV >13% are commonly used as criteria within decision-making algorithms to guide the administration of intravenous fluids.7,8

The hemodynamic variables derived from arterial waveforms have only been validated in mechanically ventilated patients in the supine position. Studies have shown that the relationship between these variables and intravascular volume estimates can be disrupted when patients are placed in other common surgical positions.9–11 Such disruptions may lead to inaccuracies in assessing fluid responsiveness, potentially resulting in complications, as both hypovolemia and hypervolemia can have adverse effects.4,12,13

Tilting the patient’s head below the level of the heart in the Trendelenburg position is a common practice during gynecological, colorectal, and genitourinary surgeries. This head-down position causes cranial displacement of the diaphragm and abdominal contents, further decreasing lung compliance and increasing intrathoracic pressure.14–17 The resulting pulmonary and cardiovascular changes intensify as the tilt increases and impact both SVV and PPV. Previous research has shown that SVV decreases when patients are positioned in the Trendelenburg position with constant fluid status.9,18,19 To accurately predict fluid responsiveness, it is crucial to understand the effects of varying degrees of the Trendelenburg position on SVV and PPV. To our knowledge, no previous research has quantitatively examined changes in both SVV and PPV across varying degrees of Trendelenburg tilt. Furthermore, no studies have investigated the threshold changes of SVV and PPV in predicting fluid responsiveness at different tilt angles in the Trendelenburg position. This study was designed to evaluate the quantitative changes in SVV and PPV, along with other dynamic variables, including dynamic arterial elastance (Eadyn), dP/dt, and cardiac index (CI), across incremental degrees of the Trendelenburg position. Second, we explored the threshold values of SVV and PPV for predicting fluid responsiveness in patients positioned at various degrees of Trendelenburg tilt.

2. METHODS

2.1. Study design

2.1.1. Study sample

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Taipei Veterans General Hospital, Taipei, Taiwan (IRB-TPEVGH No.: 2022-04-022AC). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and all collected protected health information was anonymized. The study was registered before patient enrollment at clinicaltrials.gov (NCT5558826, Date of registration: September 28, 2022). This single-center, prospective study was conducted at Taipei Veterans General Hospital in Taiwan. A within-subjects design was employed, wherein each patient served as their own control for a series of measurements taken during incremental Trendelenburg positioning. The inclusion criteria included patients aged 20 to 80 years, with an American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I or II, scheduled for elective surgery requiring general anesthesia. Exclusion criteria included patients younger than 18 years, ASA physical status III or IV, contraindications to the Trendelenburg position, pregnancy, a history of arrhythmia, heart failure, pulmonary disease, and a body mass index (BMI) >30 or <18 kg/m2. All patients were permitted to consume up to 400 mL of carbohydrate-rich clear fluids until 2 hours before surgery, in accordance with ASA guidelines.

2.2. Anesthetic management

Patients were brought to the operating room awake, where a team timeout was conducted, including confirmation of study participation. While the patient was awake, monitors were placed, including a five-lead electrocardiogram, arterial saturation pulse oximetry, noninvasive blood pressure cuff, skin temperature probe, and bispectral index (BIS).

General anesthesia was induced using intravenous propofol (1-2 mg/kg), fentanyl (0.5-1 µg/kg), and rocuronium (0.6 mg/kg). After intubation, patients were ventilated using volume control mode with a tidal volume of 8 mL/kg of ideal body weight, an inspiration-to-expiration ratio of 1:2, positive end-expiratory pressure of 5 mmHg, and a respiratory rate of 10 to 15 breaths/min to maintain end-tidal carbon dioxide (EtCO2) between 35 and 40 mmHg. Ideal body weight was calculated using the following formula: for males, 50 + 0.91 (height [cm]: 152.4), and for females, 45.5 + 0.91 (height [cm]: 152.4).20 General anesthesia was maintained with inhaled sevoflurane, adjusted during surgery to keep BIS readings between 40 and 60. Continuous temperature monitoring was used for all patients. All patients were managed using forced-air warming devices (3M™ Bair Hugger™ System, St. Paul, Minnesota), and warm saline was administered during surgery to maintain normothermia. Immediately following the induction of anesthesia, a 20-gauge arterial catheter was inserted into the radial artery and attached to an Acumen IQ smart sensor (Lifesciences, Irvine, CA) to transmit dynamic variables. A HemoSphere advanced monitoring platform was connected to display all hemodynamic measurements. This system enables the continuous monitoring of arterial pressure, CI, SV, SVV, PPV, contractility (dP/dt), and Eadyn by pulse contour analysis.

2.3. Dynamic variables and study algorithm

After the induction of general anesthesia, a 10-minute waiting period was observed before the first hemodynamic measurements were taken to allow the acute effects of anesthesia to subside. Hemodynamic measurements were then recorded at five incremental Trendelenburg tilts: 0°, 5°, 10°, 15°, and 25°. After each adjustment, we waited for 2 minutes before commencing the recording of hemodynamic data.

SVV and PPV were calculated and recorded using the Acumen Hypotension Prediction Index software (Edwards Lifesciences, Irvine, CA). The system automatically calculates uncalibrated arterial pulse contour-derived SVV and PPV every 20 seconds. The formulas used for these calculations were SVV = [(SVmax − SVmin)/SVmean] and PPV = [(PPmax − PPmin)/PPmean]. Eadyn was calculated as PPV/SVV. Contractility (dP/dt) and CI were also calculated and recorded.

The pressure transducer was placed at the patient’s midaxillary line, and the arterial pressure reference point was re-zeroed with each adjustment of the Trendelenburg tilt. Throughout the study period, no surgical stimuli were applied, and no intravenous fluids were administered. Patients who developed arrhythmias or required vasopressors during this time were excluded from the study.

2.4. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Dynamic variables, including SVV, PPV, dP/dt, and Eadyn, measured at each of the five Trendelenburg tilt angles, were analyzed using repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Friedman test and presented in violin plots. Linear regression models were used to examine the relationships between SVV measured in the supine position (0°) and SVV measured at 5°, 10°, 15°, and 25°. Similarly, linear regression models were used to analyze the relationships between PPV measured in the supine position (0°) and PPV measured at 5°, 10°, 15°, and 25°. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated for each regression model. All descriptive data are presented as mean ± SD. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

3. RESULTS

A total of 27 patients were enrolled. Two patients required vasopressors, and one developed frequent ventricular premature complex following the induction of general anesthesia; these three patients were excluded from the analysis. The mean age of the remaining 24 subjects was 66.71 ± 11.71 years, with an average BMI of 23.67 ± 3.02. The majority were male (79%). Most participants (n = 17) had an ASA class of II, while the remaining seven were classified as ASA class I. Eight of the 24 patients (33%) had a history of hypertension, four (17%) had diabetes mellitus, and three (13%) had hyperlipidemia (Table 1). All 24 patients underwent laparoscopic abdominal or urologic surgery.

3.1. Effects of the Trendelenburg position on SVV and PPV

In the supine position (0°), the mean SVV was 12.21 ± 4.26%, and the mean PPV was 13.47 ± 4.27%. Increasing the Trendelenburg tilt angle from 0° to 25° resulted in a significant reduction in both SVV and PPV (p < 0.001). SVV decreased progressively from 12.21 ± 4.26% at 0° to 9.43 ± 3.60% at 5°, 8.58 ± 3.05% at 10°, 7.58 ± 2.76% at 15°, and 6.94 ± 2.37% at 25° (Fig. 1). Similarly, PPV declined from 13.47 ± 4.27% at 0° to 9.62 ± 3.85% at 5°, 8.53 ± 3.04% at 10°, 7.50 ± 3.35% at 15°, and 6.81 ± 3.20% at 25° (Fig. 2).

Linear regression analysis revealed a significant correlation between SVV measured in the supine position (0°) and SVV measured at 5°, 10°, 15°, and 25° of Trendelenburg tilt (p < 0.001) (Fig. 3). The correlation coefficients were 0.662 between 0° and 5°, 0.543 between 0° and 10°, 0.353 between 0° and 15°, and 0.475 between 0° and 25°. Significant correlations were also found between PPV measured in the supine position (0°) and PPV measured at increasing Trendelenburg angles (p < 0.001). The correlation coefficients were 0.40 between 0° and 5°, 0.27 between 0° and 10°, 0.13 between 0° and 15°, and 0.28 between 0° and 25° (Fig. 4).

3.2. Effects of the Trendelenburg position on dP/dt, Eadyn, and CI

The baseline dP/dt measured in the supine position (0°) was 711.85 ± 235.58 mmHg/s. Increasing the Trendelenburg tilt resulted in a significant reduction in dP/dt from 0° to 25° (p < 0.001). A noticeable decrease in mean dP/dt was observed as early as 5° of downward tilt (644.16 ± 204.01 mmHg/s), with values continuing to decline (604.86 ± 170.05 at 10°; 577.58 ± 184.33 at 15°), reaching the lowest point at 25° of tilt (583.85 ± 166.19). In contrast, the Trendelenburg position had a negligible effect on Eadyn as the head-down tilt increased from 0° to 25°. The mean Eadyn value in the supine (0°) position was 1.18 ± 0.31, which remained relatively stable even at 25° (0.99 ± 0.30) (p = 0.088).

There was a significant decrease in CI with increasing Trendelenburg tilt. CI measurements were as follows: 2.51 ± 0.59 L/min/m2 at 0°, 2.47 ± 0.58 at 5°, 2.40 ± 0.56 at 10°, 2.34 ± 0.58 at 15°, and 2.27 ± 0.60 at 25° (p = 0.001). 4. DISCUSSION

Our study identified a consistent relationship between SVV and PPV derived from arterial waveform analysis and the degree of Trendelenburg head-down tilt. This is the first study to quantitatively demonstrate how both SVV and PPV change with varying tilting degrees of the Trendelenburg position, suggesting that the threshold for fluid responsiveness decreases as the head-down tilt increases. Furthermore, we identified significant correlations between SVV measured in the supine position and at 5°, 10°, 15°, and 25° in the Trendelenburg position. A similar pattern was also observed for PPV. These findings not only confirm that the Trendelenburg position lowers the thresholds for predicting fluid responsiveness using SVV and PPV, but they also pave the way for future research aimed at determining specific SVV and PPV thresholds at different slopes of the Trendelenburg position.

In our study, the baseline SVV and PPV values in the supine position (0°) were 12.21 ± 4.26% and 13.47 ± 4.27%, respectively, which are close to the established thresholds for fluid responsiveness. After each slope adjustment, a 2-minute stabilization period was observed before recording SVV and PPV over the subsequent 2 minutes. During this 4-minute period, both SVV and PPV consistently decreased. The Trendelenburg position is believed to redistribute blood from the lower extremities, thereby increasing central blood volume21; however, this effect is transient, and fluid status may return to baseline after a period. Terai et al22 observed that the autotransfusion effect induced by the Trendelenburg position returned to control levels within 10 minutes of tilting. Similarly, Reuter et al23 found that when patients were returned to the supine position after 15 minutes in the head-down tilt, significant decreases in mean arterial pressure (MAP), CI, central venous pressure, and pulmonary artery occlusion pressure were observed.

These findings suggest that SVV and PPV may fall below the threshold for fluid responsiveness when patients are positioned in the Trendelenburg position, but this does not necessarily indicate a euvolemic state, particularly over short periods. Therefore, it is crucial to determine the specific threshold for each slope of the Trendelenburg position to accurately assess patients’ fluid status during surgery.

The Trendelenburg position has been shown to reduce cardiac contractility.24,25 The transient increase in venous return during the Trendelenburg position activates baroreceptors in the atria, carotid sinuses, and aortic arch, thereby stimulating the baroreflex. This reflex leads to a decrease in contractility, heart rate, and systemic vascular resistance (SVR).26 dP/dt is a critical indicator of cardiac contractility.27 Previous studies have demonstrated a reduction in dP/dt when patients are placed in a deep Trendelenburg position (25°–45°).24,25 Our study expands on these findings by revealing a significant decrease in dP/dt as the Trendelenburg tilt progressively increases from the supine position (0°) to 25°. These results are consistent with earlier research, further confirming the impact of Trendelenburg positioning on dP/dt measurements.

Eadyn, calculated as the ratio of PPV to SVV, has been proposed as a functional measurement of arterial tone and is used to predict the responsiveness of blood pressure to fluid infusion in hypovolemic patients. Fluid therapy should target not only the augmentation of cardiac output (CO) but also the maintenance of adequate MAP to ensure optimal perfusion pressure. Therefore, it is crucial to assess whether MAP will increase following fluid administration. This study is the first to quantitatively assess changes in Eadyn during the Trendelenburg position. We observed a trend toward decreasing Eadyn as the slope of the Trendelenburg position steepened, suggesting a potential influence of the baroreflex in reducing arterial tone. However, this decrease did not reach statistical significance.

In our study, we observed a significant decrease in CI as the slope of the Trendelenburg position increased. While the Trendelenburg position initially enhances venous return, the subsequent activation of baroreceptors reduces cardiac contractility. Additionally, the cephalad displacement of abdominal organs against the diaphragm raises intrathoracic pressure, further diminishing venous return and decreasing CO. Moreover, previous studies have suggested that the workload of both the left and right ventricles increases when patients are in the Trendelenburg position.21,25 This effect may be more pronounced in patients with compromised preoperative cardiac function. Although earlier studies have reported that CI typically remains unchanged or slightly elevated in the Trendelenburg position,9,28,29 it is important to note that the patients in our study were in a euvolemic state while in the supine position. According to the Frank-Starling law, the transient rise in venous return observed in the Trendelenburg position is unlikely to result in a significant increase in CO under these conditions. Additionally, each patient in our study was subjected to a positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) of 5 cmH2O, which further elevated intrathoracic pressure. This discrepancy may account for the differences in our findings compared to previous research.

The sample for this study consisted of 24 patients. However, the strength of the analysis was enhanced by using a within-subjects design, where each patient served as their own control across five graded head-down tilt positions, ranging from 0° to 25°. The study’s design, coupled with strict inclusion and exclusion criteria, minimized both intra- and inter-individual variability. Only patients without conditions that could affect cardiac filling, such as cardiopulmonary disease, obesity, or pregnancy, were included. The study sample had a narrow BMI range of 23.67 ± 3.02, and although enrollment was open to individuals aged 18 to 80 years, the final sample had a relatively small age range of 66.71 ± 11.71 years. Females were underrepresented in our sample (n = 5; 21%), indicating the need for further studies to assess whether measurements differ significantly between sexes.

A strict protocol was followed to ensure a physiological steady state before collecting dynamic measurements, thereby supporting the reliability of our findings. No surgical stimulation occurred during the study period, and a 2-minute interval was observed between each head-down tilt adjustment and the recording of dynamic measurements. The consistency in data across all study subjects, who were evaluated under identical conditions, further supports the robustness of our results. Additionally, our findings align with the established physiological effects of Trendelenburg positioning.

However, the study had several limitations. All patients were studied under general anesthesia, which affects cardiac contractility and loading. Consequently, the findings may be specific to the depth and dose of anesthesia. Although we controlled the depth of anesthesia by monitoring the BIS, it is important to note that while some studies suggest the BIS correlates well with plasma anesthesia concentrations, no evidence guarantees that a specific BIS or plasma concentration produces identical cardiovascular effects. Moreover, the trends observed under our study conditions may differ from those of critically ill patients in the intensive care unit who are not under the influence of anesthetics. Further research is needed to determine whether the relationships between dynamic variables observed in our study are consistent with those in critically ill patients.

Our study population was predominantly male (79%), with 71% having diabetes mellitus and 33% having hypertension. Although no studies have conclusively shown that SVV and PPV are unaffected by gender, diabetes, or hypertension, we believe further investigation into these variables is warranted.

In conclusion, the Trendelenburg position lowers the threshold of both SVV and PPV for predicting fluid responsiveness. This finding suggests that when patients are in the Trendelenburg position, physicians should consider the degree of tilt and adjust their interpretation of SVV and PPV accordingly before initiating fluid management. Further research is warranted to evaluate the reliability and predictive value of SVV and PPV across different Trendelenburg slopes in larger and more diverse patient populations.
