Impact of Body Mass Index on Clinical Outcomes After Thrombectomy for Acute Ischemic Stroke in a Japanese Population
Authors: Natsuki Akaike, Hiroyuki Ikeda, Hidenobu Hata, Takaaki Kitano, Yasunori Yokochi, Takuya Osuki, Ryosuke Kaneko, Minami Uezato, Masanori Kinosada, Yoshitaka Kurosaki, Masaki Chin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe aimed to determine the impact of body mass index (BMI) on outcomes in Japanese patients undergoing mechanical thrombectomy for acute ischemic stroke using a Japanese obesity cutoff.MethodsThe study sample consisted of consecutive patients undergoing thrombectomy for acute ischemic stroke at our institution from January 2020 to December 2023. Patients were divided into 3 groups by BMI: low (<18.5 kg/m2), normal (18.5–24.9 kg/m2), and high (≥25 kg/m2). Baseline patient characteristics and procedural, safety, and clinical outcomes were compared between the 3 groups.ResultsA total of 309 patients were analyzed, of whom 39 (12.6%) had a low BMI, 201 (65.0%) had a normal BMI, and 69 (22.3%) had a high BMI. There were significant differences in the nutritional status index scores between the 3 groups, namely the Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index, the Controlling Nutritional Status, and the Prognostic Nutritional Index. However, there were no significant differences in procedural and safety outcomes. There was no significant difference in the proportion of patients with a modified Rankin Scale score of 0–2 at 90 days between the 3 groups. However, there were significant differences in the proportion of patients with a modified Rankin Scale score of 0–3 (overall 51.8%; low 35.9%, normal 51.7%, high 60.9%; P = 0.045) and mortality (overall 14.6%; low 30.8%, normal 13.4%, high 8.7%; P = 0.006). A multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that the odds of mortality at 90 days were significantly higher in the low BMI groups than in the normal BMI group (adjusted odds ratio, 2.69; 95% CI, 1.16–6.26; P = 0.02).ConclusionsIn a Japanese population, low BMI may be associated with poor clinical outcomes after thrombectomy for acute ischemic stroke, including increased mortality.Keywords: body mass index, ischemic stroke, obesity, outcome, thrombectomy

Clinical Perspective

This study is novel in that it is the first to examine the association between body mass index (BMI) and clinical outcomes after mechanical thrombectomy in a Japanese population, using the Japan‐specific BMI categories: low (<18.5 kg/m2), normal (18.5–24.9 kg/m2), and high (≥25 kg/m2).

This study suggests that low BMI is associated with poor outcomes after mechanical thrombectomy, highlighting the importance of comprehensive assessment of patients’ overall condition—including body composition, nutritional status, and physical resilience—in acute stroke management, which has traditionally focused on procedural factors and reperfusion success.

Mechanical thrombectomy for acute ischemic stroke caused by large vessel occlusion has been reported to significantly improve functional outcomes and survival rates. 1 , 2 Although obesity is a known risk factor for diabetes, hypertension, coronary artery disease, and cerebrovascular disease, a phenomenon known as the “obesity paradox” has been described, in which obese patients have better outcomes than normal weight or low weight patients. 3 , 4 There are conflicting reports on the impact of obesity on outcomes in patients with large vessel occlusion undergoing mechanical thrombectomy, including better outcomes, 5 worse outcomes, 6 and no significant difference in outcomes. 7

The World Health Organization defines obesity as a body mass index (BMI) of ≥30 kg/m2, but there is insufficient evidence that this criterion applies to non‐White populations. 8 In fact, the proportion of individuals with a BMI of ≥30 kg/m2 is 4.5% for adult Japanese men and 2.4% for adult Japanese women, which is significantly lower than >30% for Western populations. 9 The Japan Society for the Study of Obesity defines obesity as a BMI of ≥25 kg/m2. In the field of neurosurgery, research on how BMI affects the outcome of surgical treatment has mainly focused on Western populations, with only a little research on Japanese patients. The purpose of this study was to determine the impact of BMI on outcomes in Japanese patients undergoing mechanical thrombectomy for acute ischemic stroke using a Japanese obesity cutoff.

Methods

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Design and Patient Selection

Consecutive Japanese patients undergoing mechanical thrombectomy for acute ischemic stroke at our institution from January 2020 to December 2023 were retrospectively enrolled. Patients with a prestroke modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score of 4 or 5 or acute ischemic stroke occurring during other neurovascular procedures were excluded.

BMI categories were defined according to the criteria of the Japan Society for the Study of Obesity and the Japan Geriatrics Society as follows: obesity as a BMI of ≥25.0 kg/m2 and underweight as a BMI of <18.5 kg/m2. BMI was calculated from height and weight measured at hospital admission. Patients were divided into 3 groups by BMI: low (<18.5 kg/m2), normal (18.5–24.9 kg/m2), and high (≥25.0 kg/m2).

Baseline patient characteristics and procedural, safety, and clinical outcomes were compared between the 3 groups. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee (approval number 4382). As this was a noninterventional, retrospective study with anonymized data, patient consent was not required, and an opt‐out notification was posted on the institution's website.

Evaluation

Baseline patient characteristics collected included age, sex, smoking history, preprocedural mRS score, medical history, stroke etiology (TOAST [Trial of Org 10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment] classification), 10 preprocedural National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score, Alberta Stroke Program Early CT [Computed Tomography] Score (ASPECTS) or posterior circulation‐ASPECTS assessed by CT or magnetic resonance imaging, occlusion site, side of occlusion, tandem lesions, use of intravenous recombinant tissue plasminogen activator, type III aortic arch, time from last known well to hospital arrival, clinical laboratory findings, and nutritional status.

Nutritional status was assessed using 3 indices: the Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI), the Controlling Nutritional Status (CONUT), and the Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI). A GNRI score was calculated using the following formula: [14.89×serum albumin level (g/dL)]+[41.7×(current weight (kg)/ideal weight (kg)]. The ideal weight was calculated from the height using a BMI of 22 kg/m2. GNRI scores were categorized into 4 grades: normal nutritional status (>98), mild malnutrition (92–98), moderate malnutrition (82–<92), and severe malnutrition (<82). 11 A CONUT score was calculated from serum albumin levels, cholesterol levels, and lymphocyte counts. CONUT scores were categorized into 4 grades: normal nutritional status (0–1), mild malnutrition (2–4), moderate malnutrition (5–8), and severe malnutrition (9–12). 12 A PNI score was calculated using the formula: [10×serum albumin (g/dL)+0.005×total lymphocyte count (mm3)]. PNI scores were categorized into 3 grades: normal nutritional status (>38), moderate malnutrition (35–38), and severe malnutrition (<35). The PNI does not include a grade for mild malnutrition. 13

Procedural outcomes collected included procedure time, recanalization status, the number of passes, and first‐pass effect. 14 Recanalization status after mechanical thrombectomy was classified according to the modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction scale, with grades 2b‐3 indicating successful recanalization. 15 Procedure time was defined as the time from puncture to either successful recanalization or completion of the procedure. 16

Safety outcomes collected included vascular injury with extravasation of contrast medium, pseudoaneurysm at the femoral artery puncture site, iatrogenic dissection, radiologically confirmed intracranial hemorrhage, and symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage. Symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage was defined as hemorrhage confirmed by CT within 24 hours of the procedure associated with a deterioration of 4 or more points in the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score based on the SITS‐MOST (Safe Implementation of Thrombolysis in Stroke‐Monitoring Study) criteria. 17

Clinical outcomes collected included the proportion of patients with mRS scores of 0–2 and 0–3 at 7 and 90 days after thrombectomy and mortality rates at 7 and 90 days after thrombectomy. A favorable clinical outcome was defined as an mRS score of 0–2, indicating functional independence.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using JMP version 17 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). Quantitative data were expressed as medians [interquartile ranges]. For categorical variables, Pearson's chi‐square test was used. For continuous variables, the Mann–Whitney U test was used for comparisons between 2 groups, and the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for comparisons between 3 groups. The Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust the P values in the comparison of clinical outcomes between the 3 BMI groups to prevent false positives due to multiple comparisons. Logistic regression analysis was performed for clinical outcome variables that showed significant differences between the 3 groups. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was adjusted for age, sex, and significant factors identified in the univariate analysis. Statistical significance was defined as a P value of <0.05.

Results

Patients

During the study period, 333 patients underwent mechanical thrombectomy for acute ischemic stroke, of whom 24 met the exclusion criteria: 21 with a prestroke mRS score of 4 or 5 and 3 with acute ischemic stroke during other neurovascular procedures. The remaining 309 patients were analyzed, and their mean BMI was 22.18±0.195 kg/m2. Of the 309 patients, 39 (12.6%) had a low BMI, 201 (65.0%) had a normal BMI, and 69 (22.3%) had a high BMI (Figure 1).

Clinical Outcomes

Clinical outcomes for patients and each BMI group are shown in Table 3, and the distribution of mRS scores at 90 days is shown in Figure 2. Between the 3 groups, the proportion of patients with an mRS score of 0–2 was not significantly different at 7 and 90 days (P = 0.62 and P = 0.21, respectively) and the proportion of patients with an mRS score of 0–3 was not significantly different at 7 days (P = 0.48), but significantly different at 90 days (low, 35.9%; normal, 51.7%; and high, 60.9%; P = 0.045). While mortality at 7 days was not significantly different (P = 0.20), mortality at 90 days was significantly different (low, 30.8%; normal, 13.4%; and high, 8.7%; P = 0.006).

The results of the logistic regression analysis for an mRS score of 0–2, mRS score of 0–3, and mortality at 90 days are shown in Table 4. Multivariate logistic regression analysis, adjusted for significant factors in univariate analysis, namely age, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and PNI, showed that the odds of an mRS score of 0–2 at 90 days did not differ significantly between the 3 BMI groups. Discussion

Obesity is a well‐established risk factor for various diseases, including diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and cancer, 18 , 19 , 20 although an “obesity paradox” exists. 3 , 4 It is also a recognized risk factor for stroke, and the risk of ischemic stroke within the range of normal BMI increases almost linearly by 5% for each unit increase in BMI. 21 Although obesity is associated with favorable outcomes after stroke, a phenomenon known as the “obesity stroke paradox.” 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 some studies have found no association between obesity and favorable outcomes after stroke. 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31

In this study, there was no difference in the proportion of patients achieving functional independence (mRS score of 0–2) at 90 days between the high and normal BMI groups, suggesting that the obesity paradox does not apply to Japanese patients undergoing mechanical thrombectomy for acute ischemic stroke. However, the low BMI group tended to have a lower proportion of mRS scores of 0–3 and had significantly higher 90‐day mortality than the normal BMI group, suggesting that low BMI is associated with poor clinical outcomes after thrombectomy. Several studies from Japan have reported that low BMI is a significant risk factor for stroke‐related mortality, 32 , 33 , 34 but they did not focus on populations undergoing mechanical thrombectomy. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report to determine the impact of BMI on clinical outcomes after mechanical thrombectomy for acute ischemic stroke in a Japanese population.

There have been only a limited number of reports examining the impact of BMI on outcomes in patients with large vessel occlusion. In a post‐hoc analysis of the MR CLEAN trial, which included 366 patients, Pirson et al reported that patients with a high BMI had better functional outcomes, lower mortality rates, and lower rates of stroke progression. 5 Chen et al analyzed 335 patients and found that a high BMI was associated with a lower risk of postoperative hemorrhage, that both low and high BMI tended to be associated with worse functional outcomes, and that a high BMI was associated with an increased risk of nonhemorrhagic in‐hospital mortality. 6 In contrast, Bouslama et al reported that there was no difference in favorable outcomes or mortality at 90 days based on BMI. 7 Hoffman et al conducted a systematic review and concluded that BMI was not significantly associated with functional independence or mortality at 90 days and had no protective effect against in‐hospital complications, suggesting that the obesity paradox does not apply to patients with large vessel occlusion. 35 On the other hand, some studies have reported that patients with low BMI have poorer functional outcomes. Fecker et al reported that patients with a BMI of ≤23.62 kg/m2 had a significantly worse mRS score and mortality at 90 days than those with a higher BMI. 36

Most studies examining the impact of BMI on outcomes in patients with large vessel occlusion have been conducted in Western populations. In a study investigating the relationship between obesity and outcomes of mechanical thrombectomy, only 27 of the 11 486 patients enrolled were Asian, 37 highlighting the lack of detailed analyses focusing on Asian populations. The relationship between BMI and body fat accumulation varies among ethnic groups, suggesting that the World Health Organization's BMI‐based obesity criteria are not appropriate for Asian populations, 38 who have higher levels of abdominal fat despite lower BMIs than White populations. 39 , 40 , 41 Characteristics of obesity also vary significantly among Asian subgroups, for which a single BMI cutoff is not appropriate. 42 One reason for the inconsistent results of research on the relationship between BMI and the outcomes in patients with large vessel occlusion may be that ethnic differences have not been considered. This study enrolled only Japanese patients and used a Japanese obesity cutoff.

Studies of the outcomes of mechanical thrombectomy often focus on institutional capabilities, anatomical features such as vascular tortuosity, procedural techniques, and devices, among which the time from hospital arrival to arterial puncture is a highly influential factor. In obese patients, arterial access may be difficult to obtain, and physical mobility may be limited, potentially prolonging the time to arterial puncture. 6 It has been reported that each unit increase in BMI increases the risk of extracranial internal carotid artery tortuosity by 1.59 times due to increased intra‐abdominal pressure and visceral fat accumulation. 43 It has been reported that achieving a first‐pass effect is associated with shorter procedure time and better clinical outcomes and that a low BMI is an independent predictor of a first‐pass effect. 44 In our study, these factors were not significantly different between the 3 groups, suggesting that the differences in thrombectomy outcomes across BMI categories are due to baseline patient characteristics rather than technical aspects of thrombectomy procedures.

Acute ischemic stroke induces a catabolic state due to inflammation and generation of oxygen‐free radicals, but obesity is thought to confer greater resistance to this catabolic state. 45 Low body weight in older age is significantly associated with increased frailty. 46 Frail patients are more likely to die or become dependent within 3 months of mechanical thrombectomy, although thrombolysis rates, recanalization rates, and treatment times are comparable to those of nonfrail patients. 47 In our study, the proportion of patients with an mRS score of 0–3 and mortality was not significantly different between the 3 groups at 7 days, but the high BMI group had a significantly higher proportion of patients with an mRS score of 0–3 and a significantly lower mortality at 90 days than the low BMI group. BMI is a simple indicator that reflects both fat mass and skeletal muscle mass. Fat mass is beneficial for recovery from severe illness, and skeletal muscle mass plays a critical role in improving functional outcomes after stroke. 48 , 49 A low BMI indicates weak resistance to catabolic stress, which may have led to increased frailty, whereas a high BMI indicates strong resistance to catabolic stress, which may have led to improved functional recovery.

Other factors associated with poor outcomes after stroke include anemia, initial blood glucose levels, and nutritional status. Oxygen delivery to the brain depends on hemoglobin levels and blood viscosity. In patients with anemia, impaired oxygen and energy supply worsen outcomes and increase mortality. 50 Malnutrition at the time of hospital admission is associated with poor outcomes after stroke, 51 and serum albumin levels are associated with increased stroke‐related mortality. 52 Malnutrition can lead to sarcopenia, which negatively affects functional recovery after stroke. 53 In our study, the low BMI group had significantly lower hemoglobin and albumin levels and a higher prevalence of malnutrition, with a higher proportion of moderate to severe malnutrition, suggesting that hemoglobin and albumin levels are associated with clinical outcomes after mechanical thrombectomy for acute ischemic stroke. Careful follow‐up, including nutritional support, may prevent adverse clinical outcomes in underweight patients undergoing mechanical thrombectomy. This study has 3 major limitations. First, it is based on the experience of a single institution, and the sample size was relatively small. However, the strength of this study is the inclusion of consecutive cases, which minimizes selection bias. Further research with a larger cohort is warranted. Second, the accuracy of assessing obesity using BMI decreases with age, and alternative measures such as waist‐to‐hip ratio or waist circumference are more accurate in assessing obesity. 54 , 55 Nevertheless, BMI remains the most practical option in clinical settings due to ease of measurement. Finally, this study did not examine changes in body composition during hospitalization; types of obesity, such as visceral and subcutaneous fat; or visceral fat area, which remain issues for future research.

Conclusions

In a Japanese population, low BMI may be associated with poor outcomes after mechanical thrombectomy for acute ischemic stroke, including increased mortality.
