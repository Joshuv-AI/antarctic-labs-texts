The efficiency of secondary-level hospitals in hard-to-reach and non-hard-to-reach areas in Bangladesh: a data envelopment analysis approach
Authors: Mohammad Wahid Ahmed, Abu Noman, Md Zahid Hasan, Sayem Ahmed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEfficiency in healthcare delivery is crucial for maximizing resource utilization and achieving broader health system objectives, particularly in resource-constrained settings like hard-to-reach (HTR) areas in Bangladesh. Despite people facing barriers in these areas in accessing health services, health facilities have not undergone comparative efficiency evaluations with facilities in non-hard-to-reach (non-HTR) areas.ObjectivesThis study aims to comparatively assess the technical efficiency of secondary-level hospitals, as well as identify the factors contributing to inefficiencies in both HTR and non-HTR regions.MethodsData for 62 secondary level hospitals (30 located in HTR and 32 located in non-HTR areas) for the year 2022 were collected from the Local Health Bulletin of the Directorate General of Health Services in Bangladesh. Geographic locations with specific characteristics, such as wetland, coastal, river island, and hilly areas, were categorized as HTR areas, using the Water and Sanitation Program report published by the World Bank in 2012. Initially, data envelopment analysis was employed with output orientation to calculate the efficiency scores using inputs (e.g., number of physicians, nurses and hospital beds) and health service outputs (e.g., maternal care, outpatient visits and inpatient admission) under both constant and variable returns to scale assumptions. Then multiple Tobit regression models were conducted to determine the associations between hospital inefficiency and HTR area characteristics.ResultsThe findings revealed that, on average, secondary level hospitals were 73% and 80% efficient in terms of constant returns to scale (CRS) and variable returns to scale (VRS) assumptions, respectively. Hospitals located in HTR regions were relatively less technically efficient (efficiency score = 0.76) than those in non-HTR areas (efficiency score = 0.84), and the differences were statistically significant (p-value < 0.10) when the VRS assumption was used. Similar significant differences in efficiency scores (efficiency score was 0.68 in HTR and 0.77 in non-HTR areas) were observed between these two groups of hospitals when CRS assumption was used (p-value < 0.10). According to multiple Tobit regression, the positive coefficient (Coeff = 0.258, p-value < 0.05) of the association between inefficiency scores and HTR areas implies that hospitals in HTR regions had lower efficiency scores than hospitals in non-HTR regions. Comparable patterns were identified in two additional models using characteristics of geographical locations of the hospitals. Findings revealed that hospitals located in wetland (Coeff = 0.295; p < 0.05), river island (Coeff = 0.372; p < 0.05), and hilly areas (Coeff = 0.422; p < 0.05) exhibited lower technical efficiency compared to those in plain land areas under the CRS assumption. Furthermore, the negative coefficients under CRS (Coeff = -0.004) and VRS (Coeff = -0.003) reflecting a negative association between the bed occupancy ratio (BOR) and inefficiency scores across all models. This suggests that higher BOR levels were associated with greater efficiency (p < 0.01). Results from sensitivity analysis observed no substantial differences in coefficients with the relationship between HTR characteristics and efficiency scores of the hospitals.ConclusionHospitals in HTR regions are found to be less technically efficient than non-HTR regions in Bangladesh. Policymakers should address the underlying causes of inefficiencies, implement targeted interventions such as improving healthcare access for vulnerable populations residing in HTR regions, optimize bed occupancy rates and reduce length of stays with proper referral systems and discharge planning in these particular areas. This indicates that further research on health systems in climate-vulnerable areas in Bangladesh should be prioritized to effectively address these challenges.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13119-4.Keywords: Technical efficiency, Data envelopment analysis, Secondary-level hospitals, Hard-to-reach areas, Bangladesh

Background

Hospital efficiency is paramount for sustainable healthcare systems while ensuring quality services at the lowest possible cost [1]. Optimal resource use and waste reduction in healthcare can significantly improve quality, accessibility, and cost-effectiveness [2]. Hospitals account for a significant share of overall health spending. Between 50% and 66% of total government health expenditures (GHE) are allocated to hospital care [3]. According to a systematic review on hospital inefficiency in the Eastern Mediterranean Region, inefficient hospitals use more resources than their peers do which accounted for 50–80% of total health expenditures [4, 5]. Globally, 20–40% of health funding is wasted due to hospital-related inefficiencies, accounting for US$ 300 billion economic loss [3, 6]. Thus, optimizing resource utilization is critical within the health system of low and middle-income countries (LMICs) to achieve improvement in hospital efficiency, sustainable health financing and universal health coverage (UHC) [7, 8].

Bangladesh’s health system is pluralistic, consisting of both public and private healthcare providers. The system is structured into a hierarchical framework which extend from the community level to the divisional level across the country. Secondary level hospitals, providing secondary healthcare services, often referred to as district or general hospitals, sadar hospitals or ‘250-bed hospitals’. These hospitals serve as referral facilities for the primary level health care centers. Treatment for specialized care in areas such as cardiology, neurology, and orthopedics including non-communicable diseases through outpatient, inpatient, and emergency departments are covered by these secondary hospitals [9]. Tertiary hospitals, which include medical college hospitals and specialized hospitals, support both primary and secondary health facilities while providing advanced specialized healthcare services [10]. The health system in Bangladesh is primarily financed through out-of-pocket payments made by households (68.5%), followed by central government, development partners, NGOs, and private sectors [9, 11]. In 2020, secondary hospitals accounted for approximately 21% of all outdoor consultations and 30% of indoor admissions at public health facilities. Over 23% of government health expenditures were managed by the central government as the financing agent [10, 11]. However, public hospitals in Bangladesh, on average, consumed approximately 49% of the total GHE in 2020 [12].

Several studies were conducted on hospital efficiency within LMICs settings. Countries such as India, Iran, Pakistan, Tunisia, the UAE, China, Cameroon, Cambodia, Namibia, Ethiopia, and Uganda assessed the efficiency of secondary level healthcare facilities [13–25]. Among them, studies in Cambodia [25], Ethiopia [26, 27], Namibia [23], and Uganda [14] assessed the efficiency of public district hospitals with output orientation. However, very few studies have been conducted evaluating hospital efficiency in Bangladesh. A recent study, evaluating public secondary hospitals’ efficiency in Bangladesh, revealed that factors such as size of population, poverty headcount ratio, bed occupancy ratio, and administrative divisions had a significant impact on the technical efficiency (TE) of these hospitals [28]. Some facilities exhibited notably low efficiency, highlighting a discrepancy between their utilization rates and efficiency levels [29]. Another study in 2016 reported one-third of the district hospitals (only 19.4%) as technically inefficient [30]. However, hospitals in the country suffer from insufficient data on operations and health service outputs that impedes the evaluation of efficiency, particularly in Hard-to-reach (HTR) regions [31].

Bangladesh is highly vulnerable to both the impacts of climate change and natural disasters. It is ranked as the seventh most disaster-prone country globally [32]. HTR regions in the country are also climate-vulnerable. It has been classified as both geographically isolated areas and populations beyond the scope of conventional development efforts [33]. It is evident that population in HTR areas face numerous challenges, such as poor communication, lack of health awareness, longer travel times, insufficient health professionals, limited medical supplies, and higher healthcare costs, which lead to greater hospital inefficiencies in the country [33, 34]. Regions that are accessible solely by country boat or on foot, account for 10% of the total population. This ultimately lead to an insufficient service delivery system for the population of rural HTR regions in the country [35]. Evidence suggests that approximately 28 million Bangladeshis, constituting around 21% of the total population, are living in HTR settings [33]. Around 1,114 unions in the country, that are difficult to access, spanning across 257 upazilas and 50 districts. This is an area in which approximately 25% of the country’s geographical area has a high concentration of poverty and even lacks basic communication systems [33, 36]. A district-level survey revealed that maternal and newborn care services are underutilized in HTR districts such as Bandarban, Sunamganj, Kurigram, and Satkhira, in contrast to the national average [37]. Several bottlenecks were further highlighted in HTR regions, such as insufficient drug availability, unskilled human resources, transportation, limited knowledge about essential services, and high out-of-pocket expenses [38].

Identifying any existing inefficiencies is crucial for policymakers and healthcare stakeholders to improve resource allocation, hospital performance, and healthcare delivery. Public healthcare facilities in Bangladesh play a pivotal role in delivering healthcare services to diverse socio-demographic group of populations. This encompasses even the most inaccessible regions, such as HTR areas. Considering its critical importance, the efficiency of public hospitals in HTR areas remains a matter of concern and inquiry. Furthermore, there is a dearth of evidence from a comparative efficiency analysis of hospitals in HTR versus non-HTR regions in the country. Therefore, this study aimed to estimate and compare the TE of secondary level hospitals in HTR and non-HTR regions in Bangladesh.

Methodology

The study used secondary data sources to conduct the efficiency analysis, employing an exploratory non-experimental study design. We used data envelopment analysis (DEA) with output-orientation to estimate the efficiency scores of secondary level hospitals [39]. We then employed multiple Tobit regression models to compare and analyze the correlation between the hospital efficiency and HTR characteristics and other relevant covariates.

Data envelopment analysis

DEA is a commonly employed nonparametric linear programming technique designed to evaluate the TE of a set of decision-making units (DMUs) by simultaneously considering multiple inputs and outputs [40, 41]. The DEA method has been suggested for assessing hospital efficiency [42–44] as well as evaluating hospital’s productivity [45, 46] in settings with insufficient and inadequate data on healthcare sector information and input prices. Given the non-linear aspects of the healthcare production process, variable returns to scale (VRS) TE assumption might be more appropriate in health service production [13, 14, 23, 26]. In evaluating the technical efficiency of secondary-level hospitals, this study emphasized VRS TE scores as they more accurately capture pure technical efficiency, isolating operational efficiency from scale-related effects [47]. The DEA method presume that a group of DMUs or hospitals is correlated with its equivalent number of inputs (e.g., doctors, nurses, beds, supplies) and outputs (e.g., number of services delivered) (see Fig. 1).

Relationship between inputs, production process, and the resulting outputs in analyzing the hospital’s efficiency. This diagram (Fig. 1) presents the efficiency analysis framework for secondary-level hospitals, showing the relationship between inputs, the production process, and outputs. Inputs such as labor (physicians, nurses), capital (hospital beds), and supplies, which are transformed through the production process by Decision-Making Units (DMUs) into outputs. Outputs comprise key healthcare services produced such as ANC recipients, deliveries, outpatient, number of admissions, PNC recipients etc. This framework evaluates how efficiently hospitals convert their resources into healthcare services

The efficiency score is assessed based on the proportional change in inputs or outputs, recognizing an efficiency boundary where just the efficient hospitals are put in place [48]. When a hospital can produce the highest level of output using the given number of inputs then it is considered as technically efficient hospital [39].

We illustrated two forms of technical efficiency of secondary level hospitals: constant returns to scale (CRS), estimated using the CCR model [39], and VRS, estimated using the BCC model [49]. This approach facilitates the comparison of results obtained from both models. The CRS assumption considers that in a production process, increasing the inputs by any amount will result in a proportional increase in output [39]. Conversely, the VRS assumption suggests that a change in input levels during production can lead to either an increase or a decrease in output [49]. Under the VRS assumption, a secondary hospital could exhibit either increasing returns to scale (IRS) or decreasing returns to scale (DRS). IRS occurs when the output increases at a higher rate than the increase in inputs, whereas DRS occurs when the output increases at a lower rate than the increase in inputs. Scale efficiency indicates how much a hospital deviates from the optimal operational scale. It is measured by the ratio between the score from CRS TE and VRS TE [50].

We have employed an output-oriented DEA model as it concentrates on maximizing the outputs without increasing any given level of inputs. Public health facilities such as secondary level hospitals lack the requisite authority over resource allocation, particularly staffing. Despite this, with a focus on primary and secondary healthcare and a strong emphasis on health promotion and disease prevention, secondary level hospitals have the ability, through community outreach efforts, to impact a wide range of individuals accessing services such as prenatal and postnatal care, family planning, childbirth assistance, vaccinations, and health education [9, 10]. Thus, an output-oriented DEA model was employed to analyze hospital efficiency. Other studies have used a similar approach to evaluate the efficiency of public health facilities [25, 26, 51, 52]. Our operational units or DMUs consist of 62 secondary level hospitals, for which we selected three input variables and six output variables for the analysis (as shown in Table 1).

Sources of data

The study utilized data from multiple secondary sources for the efficiency analysis to extract input, output, and explanatory variables, such as Local health Bulletin 2022 [10], Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics (BBS) [53] and The World Bank [54] (Table 1). The health bulletin features yearly statistics on hospital services and monitoring, published by the Directorate General of Health Services (DGHS) under the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare (MoHFW) of Bangladesh. Population information were sourced from the Population and Housing Census 2022 (Preliminary Report) of the BBS, and district-level poverty head count were obtained from The World Bank.

Finally, HTR area related data were extracted from the Water and Sanitation Program by the World Bank report 2012 [33]. The report used a robust methodological approach to classify the HTR regions of Bangladesh. However, geographical characteristics like wetland/haor, char/river island, coast, and hilly areas in Bangladesh are shaped by long-term geomorphological and climatic processes. Thus, we assume that the implications of using this data and its generalizability in the country’s local context remain valid within this relatively short timeframe.

Selection of variables

Variable selection was influenced by reviewing literature and the availability of data for efficiency analysis [10, 28, 55–57]. This is indeed a critical step in ensuring meaningful and accurate efficiency evaluation, particularly in healthcare, where effectiveness are crucial components [58, 59]. While DEA studies do not prescribe a standardized set of variables, guidelines have been recommended to support optimal variable selection based on analytic principles and context-specific relevance [60–62].

Input variables

Hospital production inputs are categorized into labor (e.g., number of physicians, number of nurses) and capital (i.e., number of beds). These are fundamental indicators of hospital capacity, resource availability, and human resource utilization. Previous studies have consistently emphasized the importance of staff-related inputs in DEA efficiency measurements, given the pivotal role human resources play in healthcare delivery [63–65]. Among staff-related factors, the actual number of staff and full-time equivalents are frequently used to capture both clinical and non-clinical contributions [60, 61]. Similarly, hospital beds, a key capital input, are widely acknowledged as a fundamental metric for assessing hospital efficiency, capacity, and competency across different settings [66–68].

Output variables

We selected the following as output variables for the DEA analysis: antenatal care (ANC) recipients, normal deliveries, cesarean section deliveries, postnatal care (PNC) recipients, outpatient department visits, and hospital inpatient admissions based on their alignment with core hospital services and their extensive application in previous DEA studies. Literature indicates that production-related outputs, such as inpatient and outpatient visits, are commonly employed in efficiency analysis due to their quantifiability [41, 69]. These outputs represent essential components of hospital services, making them highly relevant for evaluating healthcare efficiency [47, 70, 71].

Explanatory variables

The explanatory variables were selected based on the review of literature regarding efficiency analysis for Tobit regression [28, 72–75]. Several factors influencing the efficiency of secondary hospitals are categorized into environmental and institutional factors. Environmental factors include the population in catchment area, administrative divisions, HTR areas, and poverty head count. Institutional factors include the average length of hospital stay (ALoS), bed occupancy ratio (BOR), ratio of beds to physicians (RoBTP), and ratio of beds to nurses (RoBTN) (as shown in Table 1).

According to study objective, we estimated the association between hospital efficiency and hospital’s geographical characteristics (HTR areas). Since the HTR area is the core variable of interest in this study, a binary variable was generated initially to classify specific geographical areas, such as beel/wetland/haor, coastal/offshore island/saline, char/river island and hilly areas, as HTR areas, whereas all other areas (i.e., plain land and barind) were classified as non-HTR areas to facilitate the analysis. A categorical variable was subsequently generated using the same variable for model comparison. We used the estimated population living in HTR areas to examine river island/char areas. The river island areas were classified as HTR areas when these areas contained more than 25% of the total population of the districts. Barind areas play a significant role in Bangladesh’s agriculture and economy despite facing specific challenges related to soil fertility, water availability, and climate variability [33]. We considered the Barind areas as non-HTR areas since commuting to health facilities or secondary level hospitals is not challenging in these areas, similar to other HTR areas (e.g., hilly and water surrounded areas). However, we merged wetland, beel, and haor areas into one category i.e., wetland to facilitate the analysis.

Statistical analysis

We analyzed a descriptive statistic of input and output variables, including institutional and environmental characteristics of secondary level hospitals by HTR and non-HTR categories. To test the mean difference between the HTR and non-HTR areas, we employed the Wilcoxon rank-sum test also known as Mann-Whitney two-sample statistic test, with significance set at p < 0.01, p < 0.05, and p < 0.10 (95% CI) for all analyses.

Tobit regression analysis

We used multiple Tobit regression models to examine the relationship between inefficiency scores and the HTR areas while adjusting for the number of explanatory variables. Since DEA scores naturally range from zero to one, with some data clustering at these boundary values (i.e., censored for DMUs with a score of one), ordinary least squares regression is not suitable for estimating the relationship. To address this issue, we implemented a censoring limit at zero in our model to better align with the data distribution. Consequently, efficient hospitals are assigned a score of zero, whereas inefficient hospitals are assigned scores greater than zero. This transformation was carried out in accordance with previous studies [29, 76]. The censoring mechanism was determined to be exogenous, based on the study design and domain knowledge, ensuring its independence from the unobserved error terms. However, since the Stata statistical package does not directly support heteroscedasticity tests, such as the Breusch-Pagan/Cook-Weisberg test, for Tobit regression models, robust standard errors were employed to account for potential heteroscedasticity.

Sensitivity analysis

In DEA, the ratio of inputs and outputs to the number of DMUs can influence efficiency scores. Literature recommended that the number of DMUs should be at least three times the total input and output variables [70]. Our model adhered to this guideline, but variations in variable selection can significantly influence DEA outcomes and lead to inconsistent efficiency estimates. Since DEA is sensitive to outliers and may assign extreme or unrealistic weights to inputs or outputs in the absence of restrictions, we used maximum and minimum scores of the input and output variables in our sensitivity analysis and presented the changes in the technical efficiency scores. In addition, there is no established test to validate model specification in DEA [77]. To address this, we performed sensitivity analyses by running multiple DEA models with different combinations of input and output variables, including adjustments such as excluding certain input or output variables, efficient DMUs, DMUs with HTR characteristics, to assess the robustness of our findings. Additionally, the sensitivity of the Tobit regression model specification was examined by including and excluding variables. For all the analyses, STATA version 15.0 and Microsoft Excel were used.

Results

Descriptive statistics

In 2022, all 62 district hospitals had a total of 77,685 ANC visits, 1,19,934 deliveries (both normal and cesarean section), 81,536 PNC visits, 1,39,70,902 outpatient consultations, and 22,31,080 admissions. These hospital services or outputs were produced employing a total of 1,732 physicians and 7,683 nurses with 13,000 beds. Descriptive statistics of hospital’s input and output by the HTR area categories are shown in Table 2. In both the HTR and non-HTR areas, the number of beds ranged from 100 to 250; the average number of beds was 220 in the HTR areas (SD = 61) and 200 in the non-HTR areas (SD = 71). The average numbers of doctors and nurses were higher in the HTR area (30 and 130, respectively) than in the non-HTR area (26 and 119, respectively). However, these averages do not significantly differ (p-value > 0.10).

Technical efficiency scores

The estimated CRS TE and VRS TE scores for each secondary-level hospital are illustrated in the Bangladesh map using red and blue color gradients (Fig. 2a and b). These maps visually highlight the efficiency disparities across districts, with varying levels of efficiency concentrated geographically. Under the CRS assumption, out of 62 hospitals, 11 (17.74%) exhibited low efficiency (scores between 0.00 and 0.50), 30 (48.39%) exhibited moderate efficiency (scores between 0.50 and 0.85), and 21 (33.87%) demonstrated high efficiency (scores between 0.85 and 1.00). In contrast, under the VRS assumption, 7 hospitals (11.29%) were classified as low efficiency, 23 (37.10%) as moderately efficient, and 32 (51.61%) as highly efficient within the same score ranges.

Factors determining technical efficiency scores

The diagnostic tests favor the Tobit regression models. VIF test with its maximum value of 1.82 indicates that there is no significant multicollinearity in the regression models. The findings from the Tobit regression models are presented in Table 4. The regression results indicate that hospitals in HTR areas exhibited higher inefficiency levels than those in non-HTR areas, as evidenced by the positive coefficients in both Model 1 (Coeff = 0.258, p < 0.05) and Model 2 (Coeff = 0.149, p < 0.10). Our model comparison revealed that, hospitals located in wetland areas (Coeff = 0.295, p < 0.05), river island areas (Coeff = 0.372; p < 0.05), and hilly areas (Coeff = 0.422; p < 0.05) experienced significantly higher CRS inefficiency scores compared to hospitals in plain land areas in model 3. However, Model 4 indicated that hospitals in only wetland areas (Coeff = 0.195, p < 0.10) and hilly areas (Coeff = 0.356, p < 0.05) had significantly higher VRS inefficiency scores relative to those in plain land areas.

Sensitivity of the efficiency scores

We conducted sensitivity analysis using various combinations of input and output variables. In all of these cases, the average of the efficiency scores varied from 0.69 to 0.84 in VRS assumption suggesting a relatively stable efficiency frontier. The most sensitive case was found while excluding the number of hospital outpatient visit (OPD) as the outcome variable. The efficiency score changed from 0.80 (main model) to 0.69 (excluding the OPD visit as output) which is accountable for approx. 13.72% change from the main model (Fig. 3) indicating the importance of this variable in shaping the efficiency frontier. However, during the sensitivity analysis of the Tobit model specification by including and excluding variables, we observed no substantial changes with coefficients in the relationship between the HTR characteristics of the area covered by the hospital and the hospital’s TE scores.

Discussion

This study revealed that hospitals located in HTR areas had significantly lower CRS and VRS TE scores compared to non-HTR areas. The findings showed that the mean number of inputs e.g., hospital beds, physicians and nurses are higher in HTR areas than in non-HTR areas. The mean number of outputs e.g., ANC visits and outpatient consultations was lower in HTR areas than in non-HTR areas, although these findings were not statistically significant. This suggests that hospitals in HTR areas experienced some level of inefficiencies due to an imbalance in the input-output mix. HTR regions generally suffer from economic deprivation, small catchment populations, and a scarcity of both public and private hospitals in contrast to non-HTR regions [33]. The average TE scores of the hospitals imply that, hospitals in HTR areas could produce 32% and 24% more output considering the CRS and VRS assumptions respectively. While the hospitals in non-HTR areas, could produce 23% and 16% more output when the CRS and VRS assumptions are considered, respectively. This finding suggests that hospitals in HTR areas are more likely to increase output than non-HTR areas if they run efficiently. Ahmed et al. found secondary hospitals to be more technically efficient and could produce 21% and 8% more output considering the CRS and VRS assumption respectively [28].

The Tobit regression models revealed that HTR areas, BOR, and two administrative divisions (e.g., Sylhet and Rajshahi) had a significant influence on inefficiency in terms of both CRS and VRS TE scores. The positive coefficients for the associations between inefficiency scores and HTR areas imply that hospitals in HTR areas had lower efficiency scores than hospitals in non-HTR areas. This decrease in inefficiency may be partly due the reliance on traditional healers and home remedies, leading to delayed hospital visits and underutilization of hospital services [78]. Patients residing in these HTR areas may have limited financial means to afford hospital services (e.g., medicines and diagnostic services) compared with those in urban or better-off regions [79]. Our findings reflected this since the mean number of women who received ANC services (p-value > 0.10) and outpatient consultations (p-value > 0.10) from secondary hospitals were lower in HTR areas than in non-HTR areas. This could be attributed to cultural norms and beliefs that discourage women from seeking formal healthcare services, especially during pregnancy, leading to lower utilization of ANC services [80]. Individuals in low-income areas often opt for self-medication, over-the-counter remedies, or traditional care because of their affordability relative to hospital treatments [42].

On the other hand, the mean number of hospital admissions, c-section deliveries, and postnatal care visits in our study are higher among secondary hospitals in HTR regions than among those in non-HTR regions, although the differences are not statistically significant. This may be due to the tendency of patients in seeking hospital care only when conditions become severe, resulting in higher rates of emergency admissions and surgical interventions in HTR areas [81]. In addition, HTR areas often have limited access to primary healthcare facilities [35]. Patients are more likely to be referred to secondary hospitals or specialized care for complications, such as C-sections and PNC, due to the lack of adequately equipped local healthcare facilities [9]. Socioeconomic factors such as poverty, lower educational levels, and a lack of transportation options in HTR areas can lead to delayed healthcare seeking with more severe health conditions upon arrival at district hospitals, necessitating more intensive services such as admissions and C-Sects [9, 82].

However, many hospitals operate beyond their capacity, with an average bed occupancy rate of around 163%, ranging from 53 to 370%, as reported by Health Bulletins 2020 [10]. We also found the BORs of hospitals in HTR areas were relatively high (mean score = 159.02) compared with those in non-HTR areas (mean score = 155.86) (as shown in supplementary Table S2). Tobit models revealed that a higher level of BOR is associated with higher level of efficiency. This may be due to a higher level of hospital utilization as more people might rely on secondary hospitals for services that would typically be managed at primary level facilities, leading to higher admission rates. However, high BOR may not necessarily indicate efficiency, as cultural practices and lack of post-discharge support systems can lead to patients staying longer in hospitals, thereby occupying beds unnecessarily and reducing overall efficiency of hospitals in HTR areas. Evidence indicate that secondary hospitals are generally characterized by a high utilization rate, which is cost-efficient because of a fixed government budget for the facilities [28].

The AloS in the hospitals that are in HTR areas was also higher than in non-HTR areas (p-value > 0.10). Our data indicate that longer average stays in hospitals are associated with higher inefficiency scores. This increases healthcare costs, strains resources, and raises the risk of patient complications, ultimately reducing hospital efficiency and quality of care. Patients’ lack of trust in primary healthcare facilities and preference for extended hospital stays to ensure complete recovery is influenced by cultural beliefs and perceived quality of care [83]. To address this, hospitals must implement strategies to optimize the length of stay while maintaining positive patient outcomes and manageable workloads for healthcare providers.

Using the DEA approach, numerous studies have been carried out on the efficiency of secondary hospitals, and they have indicated varying levels of hospital efficiency in different settings, thereby suggesting a wider prevalence of hospital inefficiency. Even though no studies have compared the efficiency gains of hospitals between HTR and non-HTR areas. The average VRS TE of hospitals in Bangladesh was similar to the average VRS TE of hospitals in China (79%) [73], and Western and Northern Cape Provinces (82–82.8%) [57]. On the contrary, the average VRS TE score was higher than those of Burkina Faso (51%) [52], Pakistan (58-78%) [16], Angola (65.8–67.5%) [84], Morocco (67%) [85], Uganda (69%) [14], Zambia (67%) [57], Benin (63.3–85.8%) [86], Cameroon (75%) [22], and Namibia (62.7–74.3%) [23]. However, it is lower than those reported for Cambodia (87%) [25], Ethiopia (89%) [26], India (90%) [17] and The Kwazulu-Natal province of South Africa (90.6%) [87]. Thus the existing evidence indicates that TE scores vary across countries because of variations in environmental characteristics influencing the inputs and outputs of hospitals within each country.

Our study revealed that only 23% of the hospitals in the sample were operating at optimal scale efficiency. This score was slightly lower than that obtained in a previous study in similar setting (29%) [28]. The most significant scale inefficiency was observed in DRS, reaching a rate of 44%, which contradicts a previous study that, scale inefficiencies were the highest in IRS [28]. When DRS are present, increasing output leads to higher unit costs. If there is a demand for this increased output, the hospital administration must increase the output level accordingly [88]. However, increasing the number of beds rather than increasing output levels may be more practical, as demand is increasing, and existing capacity is being excessively utilized in secondary hospitals in the country [10]. Thus, the input-oriented expansion of hospitals could increase their scale efficiency.

Moreover, our data indicates AloS, RoBTN and RoBTP had no significant influence on either CRS or VRS TE scores. The coefficient was positive for RoBTP and negative for RoBTN. The negative coefficient for RoBTN suggests that having fewer beds per nurse was technically inefficient. On average, hospitals in HTR areas had a RoBTN of 2.07, while those in non-HTR areas had an average of 2.16, both significantly higher than the approximate rate of 0.33 observed in developed countries [2]. Additionally, the average RoBTP was somewhat higher in non-HTR areas (rate = 9.11) than in HTR areas (rate = 8.64). Previous research using the similar model reported consistent results. Hospitals should prioritize minimizing human resource costs (i.e., higher RoBTP and RoBTN) to increase efficiency [14, 19, 28, 51]. However, such a focus on minimizing human resources could compromise service quality in district hospitals, subsequently impacting doctor-patient relationships [73]. Efficiency of resource allocation could be enhanced and tailored by developing an input-mix method for secondary hospitals, taking into account health, socio-economic, and other physiographic indicators, such as HTR areas. As these areas in the country are often located in climate-vulnerable regions like in haors, chars, hilly and coastal areas.

Limitations of the study

The study used secondary cross-sectional data on hospital statistics from the government’s national repository which may not fully cover the range of factors influencing healthcare outcomes. Besides, we used the World Bank report 2012 data to examine the unique characteristics of hospitals situated in both the HTR and non-HTR regions as no updated geographical data on HTR areas were available since 2012, making it the most recent available source. There are potential influence of urban expansion, decentralization of health systems, and road infrastructure improvements on hospital catchment areas since 2012 in Bangladesh. We were unable to capture such changes in the catchment areas of secondary-level hospitals. However, these changes have not substantially been altered much the government-defined catchment areas or the functional distribution of secondary-level hospitals in Bangladesh. In addition, certain contextual factors, such as cultural norms and practices (e.g., health-seeking behavior, gender norms, and staffing preferences), remain unobservable within the scope of our study due to data unavailability. Using robust standard errors, to adjust for heteroscedasticity in variance estimation, do not correct for potential bias and inconsistency in parameter estimates that arises from model misspecification [89]. Their reliability depends on large-sample asymptotic properties, which may be less effective in smaller samples. Given these limitations, alternative approaches such as heteroscedastic Tobit models or bootstrapping could be considered for more robust inference [90]. Moreover, the results from DEA are sensitive to the subjective selection of inputs and outputs and its efficiency measurements relative to the best-performing units may not reflect absolute efficiency. These limitations suggest the need to complement DEA with other methods and broader contextual considerations in healthcare efficiency assessments.

Conclusion

The study revealed that secondary hospitals in HTR regions were significantly less technically efficient than those in non-HTR regions were, suggesting that these hospitals would need to improve their efficiency level. A hospital that operates with technical efficiency is more likely to use resources effectively, manage the costs of medical services, and, as a result, offer more affordable healthcare. Decision makers and administrators in the country should identify the underlying causes of these observed inefficiencies and take appropriate measures to increase efficiency. Targeted interventions could be implemented that are aimed at improving healthcare access and quality for populations residing in HTR regions. Policymakers can also develop context-based strategies for inefficient hospitals to optimize bed occupancy rates through better patient flow management and reduce the average length of stay by enhancing discharge planning, ensuring a proper referral system for better communication with different levels of health facilities, and encouraging post discharge follow-up initiatives. Currently discharge planning, referral systems, and patient flow strategies are not widely used in the Bangladeshi secondary hospital context. Therefore, implementation of such systems would require changes in the current practices in the health facilities, foundational investment in training, infrastructure, and standardized protocols to ensure effective hospital operations and continuity of care. Given the country’s poor health indicators and limited resources, ensuring the efficient operation of these hospitals is crucial for public health. Further investigation is needed into health system readiness in both climate-vulnerable and HTR regions to comprehend the operational disparities in service provision systems at the secondary-level hospitals that are operating at varying levels of inefficiency. The conclusions drawn from this study are specific to the input-output dynamics examined; thus, any policy recommendations should be contextualized within this framework.
