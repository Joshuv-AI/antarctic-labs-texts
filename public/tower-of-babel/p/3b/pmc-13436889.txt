Acute middle cerebral artery thrombosis in the early postoperative period after total knee arthroplasty under general anesthesia: A case report
Authors: Kwanghyuk Lee, Sang Il Lee, Jun Hyun Kim, Min Hee Heo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPostoperative stroke after noncardiac, non-neurologic surgery is rare but can lead to devastating outcomes. Although venous thromboembolism is a well-known complication after total knee arthroplasty (TKA), arterial thrombosis, particularly involving large cerebral vessels, is exceedingly uncommon. A 72-year-old woman with hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and hyperlipidemia underwent left TKA under general anesthesia. On postoperative day (POD) 2, she developed acute mental change and right-sided weakness temporally coinciding with transfusion of leukocyte-depleted red blood cells. Brain computed tomography angiography revealed left middle cerebral artery (MCA) occlusion. Mechanical embolectomy successfully retrieved a red thrombus and recanalized the occluded vessel, and the patient gradually recovered, being transferred for rehabilitation on POD 28. This case demonstrates that acute cerebral artery thrombosis can occur even in the absence of cardiac embolic sources after TKA. The early detection of the symptoms related to stroke and prompt evaluation for large-vessel occlusion are essential for the reduction of mortality.

Introduction

Postoperative stroke after noncardiac, non-neurologic surgery is rare but associated with significant morbidity and mortality.[12] Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is commonly performed in elderly patients, and although venous thromboembolism is a well-recognized complication, arterial thrombotic events involving cerebral vessels are exceedingly uncommon.[13] When perioperative stroke occurs, embolic mechanisms are often suspected; however, in some patients, no definite cardiac or paradoxical embolic source is identified despite standard evaluation.[12] Large-vessel occlusion after TKA is rarely reported and may present abruptly during the early postoperative period, when multiple prothrombotic triggers coexist.[123] We report a case of acute middle cerebral artery (MCA) thrombosis occurring in the early postoperative period after TKA, in which no definite cardioembolic or paradoxical embolic source within the performed evaluation was identified.

Case Report

Written informed consent for publication was obtained from the patient and family. The Institutional Review Board (IRB) waived the review of this case report (IRB No. 2025-11-013). This report follows the CARE guidelines.[4] A 72-year-old woman (155 cm, 63 kg) with hypertension, diabetes mellitus, angina, and hyperlipidemia underwent elective left TKA for degenerative osteoarthritis. Preoperative laboratory findings revealed hemoglobin (Hb) 9.7 g/dL and platelet count 206,000/μL. Electrocardiography (ECG) showed a sinus rhythm with first-degree atrioventricular (AV) block. On TTE, the ejection fraction was 66%, and Grade 1 diastolic dysfunction with left atrial enlargement was observed. No evidence of right-to-left shunt, such as atrial septal defect (ASD) or patent foramen ovale (PFO), was detected.

General anesthesia was induced with propofol and rocuronium and maintained with desflurane. Intraoperative blood pressure was stable without episodes of hypotension. A pneumatic tourniquet was applied to the left thigh for 95 minutes. Estimated blood loss was approximately 200 mL, and tranexamic acid was not administered. The patient was extubated uneventfully.

Postoperatively, mechanical prophylaxis with intermittent pneumatic compression was initiated. On postoperative day (POD) 2, Hb decreased to 7.5 g/dL, and transfusion of leukocyte-depleted red blood cells was started. During the second unit, the patient abruptly developed altered mental status and right-sided weakness. Vital signs remained stable. Brain computed tomography angiography (CTA) revealed acute occlusion of the distal M1 segment of the left MCA [Figure 1a]. The CTA covered the head-and-neck vessels and showed no evidence of carotid artery stenosis or thrombus source within the examined range, further supporting the absence of proximal embolic origin.

Cerebral angiography of left middle cerebral artery (MCA) thrombectomy. (a) Pre-thrombectomy angiogram demonstrating MCA occlusion. (b) Intra-procedural image showing the thrombectomy process. (c) Post-thrombectomy angiogram confirming successful recanalization

Because of recent surgery and anemia, intravenous thrombolysis was contraindicated. Mechanical thrombectomy was performed within 3 hours of symptom onset [Figure 1b], and a red thrombus was retrieved [Figure 2], with successful recanalization of the occluded vessel [Figure 1c]. Aspirin was initiated after the procedure. Follow-up imaging showed no residual occlusion. Twenty-four-hour Holter monitoring revealed no atrial fibrillation. Neurologic deficits gradually improved, and the patient was transferred for rehabilitation on POD 28.

Discussion

Perioperative stroke following TKA is uncommon, and when it occurs, embolic mechanisms are often considered.[123] Most vascular complications after TKA involve peripheral arteries, such as the popliteal or femoral vessels, rather than cerebral circulation.[5] Therefore, large-vessel cerebral thrombosis without an identifiable embolic source, as seen in this case, represents an unusual and clinically important presentation.[13]

The mechanisms of perioperative ischemic stroke are commonly discussed in terms of embolic events and in situ arterial thrombosis.[12] Paradoxical embolism through a right-to-left shunt has been reported after orthopedic surgery;[6] however, preoperative TTE in our patient showed no evidence of intracardiac communication, making this mechanism less likely. Cardioembolic stroke related to atrial fibrillation is another major cause of large-vessel occlusion, but continuous intraoperative monitoring and postoperative Holter evaluation did not reveal arrhythmia.[7] In addition, the head-and-neck CTA did not demonstrate carotid stenosis or an obvious proximal arterial source within the scanned range, further reducing the likelihood of an embolus arising from a proximal large artery.

Fat embolism is another embolic mechanism associated with orthopedic procedures involving intramedullary manipulation.[1] In the present case, fat embolism was considered less likely because angiography demonstrated a focal large-vessel occlusion rather than diffuse microvascular involvement, and the retrieved embolic material appeared grossly consistent with a red thrombus. Although histopathological confirmation was not available, the clinical and procedural findings favored thrombotic occlusion.

In contrast, in situ arterial thrombosis under transient hypercoagulable conditions represents a plausible mechanism.[12] Surgical stress and tourniquet-related ischemia–reperfusion may promote endothelial activation and platelet aggregation.[8] In addition, perioperative anemia and red blood cell transfusion have been associated with increased thrombotic risk in surgical patients.[9] In a patient with multiple vascular risk factors, these perioperative triggers may facilitate localized thrombus formation in a susceptible arterial bed. The temporal association between transfusion and symptom onset in this case raises the possibility that transfusion-related changes in viscosity or inflammatory and procoagulant pathways may have contributed, although causality cannot be established.

In this case, pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis was withheld because of the high risk of postoperative bleeding, and mechanical prophylaxis alone was selected based on population-specific evidence suggesting a lower incidence of venous thromboembolism in Asian patients undergoing TKA.[10]

This case has several limitations, including the absence of tranexamic acid administration and the lack of preoperative optimization of preexisting anemia, which may have contributed to postoperative anemia and the need for blood transfusion. The rationale for withholding tranexamic acid was not documented.

Conclusion

Acute large-vessel cerebral thrombosis may occur after TKA even in the absence of identifiable embolic sources.[13] Awareness of perioperative prothrombotic conditions and rapid diagnostic evaluation are essential for early intervention. When intravenous thrombolysis is contraindicated after recent surgery, prompt consideration of mechanical thrombectomy may improve neurologic outcomes.[12]
