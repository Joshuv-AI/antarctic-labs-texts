Periconceptional Dietary Quality and Metabolic Syndrome at 3 Years Postpartum
Authors: Lisa M Bodnar, Qianhui Jin, Ashley I Naimi, Hyagriv N Simhan, Janet M Catov, Sara M Parisi, Sharon I Kirkpatrick
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe period around pregnancy is a critical window in the primordial prevention of cardiovascular disease, but little is known about the role of dietary patterns in cardiometabolic health. Our objective was to determine the association between alignment of periconceptional diet with the 2020 to 2025 Dietary Guidelines for Americans and the risk of metabolic syndrome.Methods and ResultsWe used data from the Nulliparous Pregnancy Outcomes Study: monitoring mothers‐to‐Be Heart Health Study, a pregnancy cohort study that followed pregnant participants to a median of 3 years postpartum (n=4423). Usual dietary intake in the 3 months around conception was estimated from a Food Frequency Questionnaire. Alignment with the Dietary Guidelines was measured using the Healthy Eating Index‐2020, where higher scores represent greater alignment. Postpartum metabolic syndrome was defined using the American Heart Association/National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute guideline. The prevalence of metabolic syndrome at 3 years postpartum was 20%. After adjusting for confounders, the prevalence of metabolic syndrome was flat up to a periconceptional Healthy Eating Index‐2020 total score of ≈60, and then declined steeply as scores increased. Compared with a Healthy Eating Index‐2020 score of 60, having scores of 70, 80, and 90 were associated with 2, 4, and 7 fewer cases of metabolic syndrome per 100 individuals, respectively (prevalence differences: −0.02 [95% CI, −0.03, 0]; −0.04 [−0.08, −0.1]; −0.07 [−0.13, −0.02]).ConclusionsDietary interventions around conception and systems‐level changes to support high diet quality may be important for improving postpartum cardiometabolic health, and helping to reverse or slow the decline in women's cardiometabolic health.Keywords: cardiometabolic disease, dietary patterns, metabolic syndrome, postpartum, pregnancySubject Categories: Cardiovascular Disease, Diet and Nutrition, Epidemiology, Obesity, Pregnancy

The prevalence of metabolic syndrome, a cluster of risk factors for cardiometabolic disease, has risen among US women from 32% in 2011 to 37% in 2016, an increase that is most prominent among women aged 20 to 39 years. 1 Women with metabolic syndrome have a 2‐fold increase in atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease events and death, 2 , 3 and are more likely to report a poor quality of life than men with metabolic syndrome. 4 Primordial prevention of cardiovascular disease requires avoiding the development of its risk factors. 5 Cardiovascular damage, however, accumulates across the life course, and most prevention efforts start too late in life to be effective. 6

Recently, the American Heart Association and others issued statements on the critical need to target the period around pregnancy for women's cardiovascular disease prevention. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 Pregnancy acts as a “stress test” to women's cardiovascular systems, 11 and a poor response often results in adverse pregnancy outcomes 12 and postpartum subclinical cardiovascular disease. 13 Scientists and policymakers, however, have cautioned against generalizing known primordial prevention approaches, which have been primarily based on midlife and older adults, to pregnant individuals and other young adults. 14

Diet is a cornerstone of cardiovascular disease primordial prevention, 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 yet little is known about the role of dietary patterns in postpartum cardiovascular health. Periconceptional dietary patterns that align with the Dietary Guidelines for Americans are associated with a reduced risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes, 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 and hold promise in postpartum cardiovascular disease prevention. The current food environment, however, does not facilitate or support healthy diets, particularly for minority groups. 23 , 24 Understanding the relation between periconceptional dietary patterns and postpartum metabolic syndrome is a key first step in identifying structural changes needed to support women's long‐term health.

Our objective was to determine the association between alignment of periconceptional dietary patterns with the 2020 to 2025 Dietary Guidelines for Americans and the risk of metabolic syndrome at a median of 3 years postpartum in a racially and geographically diverse US cohort.

Methods

The Nulliparous Pregnancy Outcomes Study: Monitoring Mothers‐to‐Be (nuMoM2b) data described in this article, as well as the code book, are publicly and freely available without restriction at https://dash.nichd.nih.gov/study/226675. The study investigators and the Data Coordinating and Analysis Center have copies of the entire database but cannot release that version to outside investigators due to the permissions granted by the participants during the consent process and to protect participant confidentiality. The nuMoM2b‐Heart Health Study (nuMoM2b‐HHS) data described in the article are available from the data‐coordinating center upon reasonable request. Code for replication purposes can be found at https://github.com/smparisi/PeriDiet_MetS.

We used data from the nuMoM2b, a pregnancy cohort study at 8 US medical centers, and the nuMoM2b‐HHS, a 2‐ to 7‐year follow‐up of nuMoM2b participants. Both studies were approved by all local offices of human protections, and all participants provided informed, written consent in their native language. Details of each study's methodologies have been described previously. 25 , 26 Briefly, nulliparous pregnant individuals with singleton gestations were recruited from 2010 to 2013 at 6 to 13 weeks' gestation. Three study visits occurred during pregnancy, when data on usual dietary intake, medical history, demographics, behaviors, and psychosocial factors were collected. After delivery, information on pregnancy complications and birth outcomes was collected from the medical record.

Participants were eligible for the nuMoM2b‐HHS if they were at least 18 years of age with complete data on obstetrical delivery, and if they consented to being contacted for future research studies and did not later withdraw consent. Starting at 6 months after delivery of the index pregnancy, study personnel contacted consented participants every 6 months to maintain contact and collect health information. An in‐person study visit occurred 2 to 7 years after delivery of the index pregnancy. Participants were eligible for the in‐person visit if they were able to provide informed consent and not currently pregnant or <6 months postpartum from a subsequent pregnancy. Study staff performed clinical measurements and collected fasting blood and data on medical history, medication use, and self‐reported behavioral and psychosocial factors. All sites used trained and credentialed study personnel as well as a common protocol and manual of operations.

Of the 8838 nuMoM2b participants who were eligible for nuMoM2b‐HHS, 7003 were targeted for recruitment (Figure S1). The in‐person visit was completed by 4508 individuals who have been shown to have similar characteristics to the full, eligible nuMoM2b cohort. 27 We excluded participants who delivered their index pregnancy at <20 weeks (n=22) or who were found at the in‐person visit to be pregnant or <6 months postpartum from a subsequent pregnancy (n=63). The final analytic sample was 4423.

Exposure

Usual dietary intake in the 3 months around conception was assessed at 6 to 13 weeks' gestation using a self‐administered modified Block Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) including ≈120 food and beverage items. 28 , 29 The FFQ was modified to assess usual intake in the 3 months around conception and to add foods eaten commonly in Spanish‐speaking populations. 30 To facilitate recall of intake during the relevant period, a trained study staff member labeled each participant's FFQ with the months of interest: the month before their last menstrual period, the month of their last period, and the month following their last period. Portion size was queried for each item, and pictures of portion sizes were provided to improve accuracy. Study personnel checked all pages for completeness. The FFQ has acceptable validity (most correlations 0.5–0.6 for nutrients in comparison to 4‐day food records), including in diverse samples. 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 The complete dietary assessment methods have been published 35 , 36 and appear in Data S1.

Data from the FFQ were used to measure periconceptional diet quality using the Healthy Eating Index (HEI)‐2020, a measure that reflects adherence to the 2020 to 2025 Dietary Guidelines for Americans. 37 , 38 To calculate HEI‐2020 scores, NutritionQuest (Berkeley, CA) scanned FFQs and linked the line items to the United States Department of Agriculture Food and Nutrient Database for Dietary Studies 39 and Food Patterns Equivalents Database from the United States Department of Agriculture. 40 The HEI‐2020 total score (range 0–100) is calculated by summing the scores for 13 components, represented as densities (units per 1000 kcal), each aligning with key recommendations of the 2020 to 2025 Dietary Guidelines for Americans. 37 , 38 Nine components represent foods or dietary elements for which higher intakes are desirable (Total Fruits, Whole Fruits, Total Vegetables, Greens and Beans, Whole Grains, Dairy, Total Protein Foods, Seafood and Plant Proteins, and Fatty Acids). Four components represent foods or dietary elements for which lower intakes are advised (Refined Grains, Sodium, Added Sugars, and Saturated Fats). Scores for each component have a maximum of 5 or 10 points (Table S1).

Outcomes

Detailed methods for cardiometabolic measurements at the in‐person postpartum have been published previously. 25 , 27 Participants were weighed using regularly calibrated balance beam scales. Systolic blood pressure and diastolic blood pressure were measured after 5 minutes of full rest with the same type of automated device at each site. A manual sphygmomanometer was used for measurements if needed. 40 After an ≥8‐hour fast, participants' blood was drawn and analyzed via high‐performance liquid chromatography for high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, and triglycerides in serum, and glucose in whole blood. Insulin was measured using the Beckman ACCESS 2 ultrasensitive immune‐enzymatic assay. Medication use, including for high blood pressure, high cholesterol, and elevated glucose or diabetes, was ascertained by self‐report. Waist circumference was measured with a nonstretch measuring tape using National Institutes of Health guidelines. 41 Study personnel were instructed to measure waist circumference at the level of the iliac crest using the following protocol: “Palpate the hip area to locate the right ilium of the pelvis. Mark the spot to start the measurement with a cosmetic pencil by drawing a horizontal line just above the uppermost lateral border of the right ilium. Cross this mark at the midaxillary line, which extends from the armpit down the side of the torso. Extend the measuring tape around the waist from the right‐hand side and position the tape in a horizontal plane at the level of the top of the right ilium of the pelvis.” Waist circumference was also measured at the midpoint between the lower border of the rib cage and the iliac crest based on World Health Organization guidelines. 42 For both protocols, waist circumference was performed twice and averaged.

Postpartum metabolic syndrome was defined as the presence of at least 3 of the following conditions: (1) elevated waist circumference (≥88 cm measured at the iliac crest); (2) elevated blood pressure (systolic blood pressure ≥130 mm Hg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥80 mm Hg) or antihypertensive drug treatment for a history of hypertension; (3) elevated fasting glucose (≥100 mg/dL, drug treatment for elevated glucose, or previously diagnosed diabetes); (4) elevated triglycerides (≥150 mg/dL) or drug treatment for elevated triglycerides; or (5) reduced HDL‐C (<50 mg/dL) or drug treatment for low HDL‐C. 3 , 27 , 43 Because there is no universally accepted protocol for the measurement of waist circumference, 44 we performed a sensitivity analysis using the midpoint waist circumference ≥88 cm to define elevated waist circumference.

Covariates

At the first study visit, participants were asked “Are you of Hispanic or Latino origin or descent?” (yes/no) and “Which group or groups describe your racial background? You may answer yes to more than one. Check all that apply.” The choices were "White; Black, African American, or African Descent; American Indian or Alaska Native; Asian Indian; Other Asian; Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander; any other race.” We combined these into 4 mutually exclusive categories: non‐Hispanic White, non‐Hispanic Black, Hispanic, or other (due to small samples of American Indians or Alaska Natives (n=5), Native Hawaiians or Other Pacific Islanders (n=10) Asian Indians or Other Asian (n=136), any other race (n=6), or 2 or more races (n=157)).

Prepregnancy body mass index was calculated as self‐reported pregravid weight (kg) at enrollment divided by measured height (m) squared. Data on the participant's age, education, marital status, prepregnancy smoking, medical insurance, gravidity, and binge drinking were derived from self‐report at enrollment. Preexisting diabetes and chronic hypertension were collected via medical record abstraction after delivery. In the first trimester, standardized assessment tools were used to assess nausea and vomiting (Pregnancy‐Unique Quantification of Emesis and Nausea index 45 ), sleep satisfaction (Insomnia Rating Scale 46 ), anxiety (State–Trait Anxiety Inventory 47 ), and depressive symptoms (Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale 48 ).

Participants also self‐reported their preconception physical activity. We converted activity to metabolic equivalents and used current guidelines to define low activity, moderately active, and active based on the Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans. 49 We linked the census tract and block group of the participant's address at enrollment to the 2010 data from the US Census Bureau to calculate the percent of the neighborhood living below the poverty line. Preeclampsia, 50 , 51 gestational diabetes, 50 and preterm birth 26 were ascertained from the medical record by trained staff, and are described in Data S1.

Statistical Analysis

We compared the distribution of HEI‐2020 total scores for metabolic syndrome cases and noncases. To provide a visual representation of dietary pattern, 52 we created radar plots for metabolic syndrome cases and noncases, with each HEI‐2020 component score depicted as a mean of the percentage of maximum points on its respective axis.

We used multivariable logistic regression to estimate the association between HEI‐2020 total scores and postpartum metabolic syndrome and each of its 5 component health conditions. To allow smooth, nonlinear relationships between HEI–2020 total score and the outcomes, the total score was modeled as a restricted cubic spline with 3 knots in the default positions (10th, 50th, and 90th centiles of the HEI–2020 total score distribution). 53 A total of 1093 records were missing data on either dietary intake, any component of metabolic syndrome, or key covariates. To address the missing data, we used multiple imputation by chained equations with 300 iterations to create 20 imputed data sets. 54 The imputation model included the exposures, outcomes, and all informative covariates, as recommended. 55 , 56

We identified potential confounders using theory‐based causal diagrams 57 : age, education, race and ethnicity (markers of structural and interpersonal racism 58 ), prepregnancy body mass index, marital status, prepregnancy smoking, medical insurance, gravidity, preexisting diabetes, chronic hypertension, physical activity, nausea and vomiting, sleep satisfaction, depressive symptoms, and percent of the neighborhood in poverty. These covariates met the definition of confounding because they are hypothesized to have a causal relation with and are upstream from periconceptional diet and metabolic syndrome. 59 Covariates such as adverse pregnancy outcomes or postpartum factors were not included in models because they are downstream from periconceptional diet, and adjusting for them may induce bias. 60

After model estimation, we calculated and plotted average marginally adjusted prevalences and 95% CIs. We also estimated prevalence differences and 95% CI for HEI–2020 total scores from 38 (the 1st centile of HEI–2020 total score in our cohort) to 100 compared with an HEI–2020 total score of 60 (the score with the highest absolute prevalence). We calculated the excess or prevented number of cases per 100 births by multiplying estimates by 100.

We presented point estimates and 95% CI to avoid misleading significance threshold interpretations commonly associated with P values. 61 , 62

Results

The in‐person study visit occurred at a mean of 3.2 (SD 0.9) years postpartum. The cohort was predominantly 25 to 35 years old, normal weight before pregnancy, non‐Hispanic White, married, and nonsmokers and were privately insured (Table 1). Most of the cohort was sedentary before pregnancy and lived in a neighborhood with <20% poverty. Over half the cohort had an elevated waist circumference (64%) and one third had reduced HDL‐C (34%), while fewer had elevated blood pressure (23%), triglycerides (12%), or glucose (17%).

HEI‐2020 total scores were negatively associated with prevalence of reduced HDL‐C and elevated triglycerides, but not elevated waist circumference, blood pressure, or glucose (Table S3). For example, the adjusted prevalences of reduced HDL‐C for HEI‐2020 scores of 50 to 80 were 37% (95% CI 33%, 39%) and 31% (28%, 34%), respectively. These prevalences for elevated triglycerides were 15% (13%, 17%) and 9% (7%, 11%), respectively.

We performed sensitivity analyses replacing the iliac waist circumference measure with the midpoint waist circumference measure. The median (interquartile range) waist circumference and the prevalence of metabolic syndrome were lower when the midpoint waist circumference was used compared with the iliac measure (102 [19] versus 108 [23] cm; 16% versus 20%). While the prevalence of prepregnancy obesity, prepregnancy physical activity, and elevated postpartum blood pressure were higher among cases identified using the midpoint measure compared with the iliac measure, there were no other meaningful differences in maternal characteristics, pregnancy outcomes, or postpartum measures (Table S4). Furthermore, the risk curve for the association between HEI‐2020 total score and metabolic syndrome based on the midpoint measure to define elevated waist circumference was not different than when using the iliac measure (Figure S4).

Discussion

In this large cohort of pregnant individuals followed to 2 to 7 years postpartum, we found that higher periconceptional adherence with the 2020 to 2025 Dietary Guidelines, as measured by the HEI‐2020, was associated with a lower prevalence of postpartum metabolic syndrome. While the prevalence was constant up to total HEI‐2020 scores of 60, it sharply declined as scores increased. For components of metabolic syndrome, high HEI‐2020 scores were associated with a decline in the likelihood of reduced HDL‐C and elevated triglycerides. We observed these findings even after adjusting for preconception behavioral, demographic, psychosocial, and neighborhood factors.

We are unaware of prior research evaluating dietary patterns in periconceptional or pregnant individuals in relation to postpartum cardiometabolic health. Our results are consistent with findings from 2 recent meta‐analyses on dietary patterns and metabolic syndrome in adults. 63 , 64 They reported that the risk of metabolic syndrome increased by 16% to 19% with intake of a Western dietary pattern and decreased by 11% to 15% with a Healthy dietary pattern, with the suggestion of a stronger protective effect among women. 63 Additionally, a 2014 narrative review found that dietary patterns that have been promoted as consistent with health and chronic disease prevention, including the Mediterranean dietary pattern, Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension diet, and the Nordic Diet, were associated with a lower risk of metabolic syndrome and its components. 65 The vast majority of studies included in these reviews, however, sampled mid‐ or late‐life individuals, and may not apply to pregnant or other young adults. 14

Our results agree with the limited research on dietary patterns and metabolic syndrome in young adults. In a US cohort of 4161 18‐ to 30‐year‐olds, investigators found that a Prudent dietary pattern was associated with a 22% to 23% reduction in the 20‐year incidence of metabolic syndrome and elevated triglycerides, as well as small differences in risks of reduced HDL‐C and high blood pressure compared with a Western dietary pattern. 66 These results were similar to a cross‐sectional study of 955 US 19‐ to 39‐year‐olds, though reduced HDL‐C was the only component associated with diet. 67

Unlike the majority of prior research, which measured diet patterns using cluster analysis, factor analysis, or other a posteriori methods, our use of the HEI‐2020 and the modeling approach allowed a determination of the alignment of intakes with national recommendations and its inflection point in prevalence. We found meaningful reductions in prevalence of postpartum metabolic syndrome starting at an HEI‐2020 total score of 70. Because nearly all components of a healthy diet were below recommendations for individuals with metabolic syndrome, including low intake of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and plant proteins and excess intake of added sugars and saturated fat, there are many potential targets for dietary improvements.

Consistently, analyses of nationally representative data from the United States suggest suboptimal scores on the HEI, generally in the 50s. 68 While the mean HEI total scores in our cohort were somewhat higher, likely due to the greater proportion of college‐educated non‐Hispanic White women compared with the US population, national data and data from our cohort highlight the need for interventions to shift the food supply 23 and food environments 24 to make it more possible for individuals, including those of reproductive age, to follow dietary patterns that are consistent with guidance and with reduced disease risk. Importantly, unhealthy food environments disproportionately affect individuals with lower socioeconomic position and minority identities, and contribute to the long‐standing disparities in diet quality and cardiovascular disease. 69

The HEI‐2020 offers the advantage of a density‐based approach that aims to uncouple diet quality from quantity. 70 The HEI‐2020 scoring algorithm is not based on risk of particular outcomes, such as metabolic syndrome, but rather, broad evidence on dietary patterns that are consistent with health. HEI scores, however, have been associated with reduced risk of all‐cause mortality, cancer mortality, and cardiovascular disease mortality. 71 Nonetheless, the possible heterogeneity in dietary patterns that meet nutrient needs and are consistent with health is increasingly recognized, and the dietary guidance underlying the HEI‐2020 has been critiqued for not reflecting this heterogeneity. 72 As the application of data sciences to dietary intake data and the field of precision nutrition continue to evolve, it may be possible to develop guidance that is better tailored to variability in the numerous factors that shape eating patterns and disease risk.

Measurement error commonly associated with dietary data assessed via FFQ 73 , 74 may bias our results if the error is differential by metabolic syndrome. Our adjustment for energy intake, however, attenuates the potential bias in FFQ data because densities are more accurately measured than absolute intake of nutrients. 75 Because our work is intended to inform future dietary guidance, we used the most updated measure of adherence to current federal dietary recommendations: HEI‐2020. The differences, however, between the HEI‐2010 and HEI‐2020 are minor, and in our cohort their correlation was 0.97. Data were missing on dietary and outcome measures, but our use of multiple imputation, which assumes the missing data can be explained by observed data, lessens the likelihood of selection bias. While we believe this is a reasonable assumption, it cannot be tested. 76 We could not confirm that postpartum metabolic syndrome was new‐onset because we lacked data on all metabolic syndrome components before gestation‐related changes to metabolism and physiology. Nevertheless, the low prevalence of chronic hypertension and preexisting diabetes suggests this is not a major concern. Although we adjusted for a wide range of sociodemographic, behavioral, psychosocial, and environmental variables, we cannot conclude that the relationship between dietary patterns and postpartum metabolic syndrome is causal. We did not adjust for outcomes of the index pregnancy or factors occurring in intervening pregnancies because they are downstream consequences of periconceptional diet, but understanding their contribution to the association is an important next step.

The periconceptional period is a critical window in which dietary exposures may reduce the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes. 77 Our study provides evidence that its effects may extend to maternal cardiometabolic health several years postpartum. Because patterns of cardiometabolic disease progression are set early in life, with damage accumulating over the life course, 6 dietary interventions around conception and broader systemic changes to make high diet quality possible may be important for improving cardiometabolic health in the postpartum period and helping to reverse or slow the decline in cardiometabolic health in women.
