BASELINE: a CRISPR base editing platform for mammalian-scale single-cell lineage tracing
Authors: Evan Winter, Francesco Emiliani, Aidan Cook, Asma Abderrahim, Aaron McKenna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA cell’s fate is shaped by its inherited state, or lineage, and the ever-shifting context of its environment. CRISPR-based recording technologies are a promising solution for mapping the lineage of a developing system; however, challenges remain regarding single-cell recovery, engineering complexity, and scale. Here, we introduce BASELINE, which uses base editing to generate high-resolution lineage trees in conjunction with single-cell profiling. BASELINE uses the Cas12a adenine base editor to irreversibly edit nucleotides across target arrays built from 50 synthetic target sites, which are integrated multiple times into a cell’s genome. We demonstrate that BASELINE accumulates lineage-specific marks over a wide range of biologically relevant intervals, recording more than 4300 bits of information in a model of pancreatic cancer, a 50-fold increase over existing technologies. Single-cell sequencing reveals high-fidelity capture of these recorders, averaging 29 cell divisions captured per lineage, within the estimated range of mammalian development. We expect BASELINE to apply to a wide range of lineage-tracing projects in development and disease, especially those in which cellular engineering makes small, more distributed systems challenging.

Introduction

A shared lineage connects every cell within a multicellular organism. Efforts to trace these lineages date back to the 19th century [1, 2], when it was first established that cells arise from pre-existing cells. Since then, advances in microscopy [3], the introduction of fluorescent reporters [4, 5], and the development of high-throughput DNA sequencing [6, 7] have progressively improved our ability to reconstruct cellular phylogenies. More recently, CRISPR/Cas9-based lineage tracing systems have emerged, in which double-stranded DNA breaks are introduced within compact arrays of synthetic target sites. These breaks are repeatedly repaired and re-cleaved until DNA repair processes generate insertions or deletions (indels) that are inherited by daughter cells. First demonstrated in GESTALT [8] and MEMOIR [9], this approach has been applied to trace lineage reconstruction across diverse biological contexts [10–13].

However, double-strand-break-based recorders have notable limitations. When multiple targets within an array are cleaved simultaneously, the intervening DNA is lost, including any previously recorded edits. In addition, double-strand breaks can trigger a p53-mediated DNA damage response [14], which may alter the underlying cellular phenotype [15].

To address these challenges, researchers have explored alternative genome editing strategies that enable more precise and stable lineage recording. Base editors [16, 17] and prime editors [18], originally developed for targeted sequence modification without double-stranded breaks, have been adapted for CRISPR/Cas-based lineage tracing [19–21]. Other systems, including integrases [22] and homing endonucleases [23], are also capable of recording lineage information. Despite their promise, each approach faces limitations. Prime editors suffer from low efficiency in many cell types [18, 24], reducing tree resolution, though efforts to rectify this are rapidly evolving [25, 26]. Furthermore, current homing endonuclease systems require custom adaptations of single-cell sequencing approaches to capture lineage recordings [27], hindering phenotype-lineage integration; and integrase-based systems can be leaky or result in undesired recombination [22, 28, 29], while recorder recovery rates vary substantially across technologies [30]. These gaps highlight the need for a lineage tracing system with low cellular engineering requirements, high recorder diversity, efficient recovery, and high information density.

Here, we present BASELINE, a Cas12a adenosine base editing system for predictable, irreversible lineage recording (Fig. 1A). Our recorder contains 50 unique synthetic target sites with 272 adenines, representing 2272 (>7.58 × 1081) theoretical outcomes if all sites act independently. Cas12a enables multiplexed guide arrays, allowing both recorder and guides to be compactly packaged on a single construct (Fig. 1B). Recorder expression is driven by the CAG promoter and selectively amplified with T7 RNA polymerase through in vitro transcription (IVT) to maximize recorder recovery without polymerase chain reaction (PCR) bias [31]. The 1.5 kbp recorders are sequenced using Oxford Nanopore Technology (ONT) long-read sequencing, paired with consensus sequence generation to achieve high-accuracy base calls. Using mouse pancreatic cancer (KPCY) cells, we demonstrate the scalability, precision, and depth of BASELINE, reconstructing single-cell lineage trees with average depths exceeding 29 cell divisions.

Materials and methods

All product and kit-based reactions were performed as per the manufacturer’s instructions unless otherwise stated.

Cell lines culturing and maintenance

All experiments were performed using HEK293T (ATCC CRL-3216) and KPCY (Kerafast PDAC 6419c5) cell lines. HEK293T cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Corning™ 45000-316) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Cells were passaged at a 1:5 ratio every 2 days. KPCY cells were maintained in DMEM (Corning™ 45000-304) supplemented with 10% FBS. These cultures were passaged at a 1:10 ratio every 3 days, with a media change on day 2.

BASELINE plasmid

The BASELINE recording construct was designed from seven DNA fragments. These seven fragments consisted of five guide RNA (gRNA) cassettes driven by U6, one 50-target recording array, and one PiggyBac backbone containing blasticidin. The fragments were built from oligos and amplified (Supplementary File 2) to contain PaqCI (R0745S) restriction sites for GoldenGate Assembly. After the assembly reaction, 1 μl of the reaction was transformed into chemically competent Escherichia coli (C3019H). Colonies were screened for proper assembly.

Guide RNA screening construct

The backbone vector (Addgene 19 319) was digested with NdeI and a U6 promoter was inserted into the cut site. Using single-stranded Gibson Assembly, the guide expression cassette was modified to contain either the LbCas12a or AsCas12a DR sequence directly after the Twist oligo pool drop-in site. Single-stranded Gibson Assembly was used to generate the 1000-member gRNA library for both the LbCas12a DR backbone and AsCas12a DR backbone. Nuclease plasmids were purchased from Addgene (Supplementary File 1).

Unedited and pre-edited target array constructs

Unedited and pre-edited target arrays were synthesized as gBlocks from IDT and replaced the 50-target array in the BASELINE construct GoldenGate Assembly reaction.

TWIST oligo pool

A library of 10 000 candidate target sequences (20 nt, each with a TTTC PAM motif) was generated with FlashFry [36] (Supplementary Tables 7–9). The off-target discovery and scoring functions of FlashFry were used to sort the target library from low predicted off-target editing to high predicted off-target editing. Five scoring metrics from the FlashFry tool were used: doench2014ontarget, doench2016cfd, dangerous, hsu2013, and minot. Sequences were filtered out based on the number of mutations that they required to match genomic sites. The 4601 remaining sequences were then analyzed using the CRISPR/Cas target selection tool (http://www.rgenome.net/cas-offinder/). We used this tool for both ordering the sequences by their predicted editing efficiency as well as sanity-checking for any off-target effects. From this analysis, the top 990 predicted target sequences were selected. We included 10 targets that were tested in other published and unpublished works. We designed a pooled library to be synthesized by Twist Biosciences. Complete oligo designs are provided in Supplementary File 4. Oligos were amplified using primers labeled ETC and ETD in Supplementary File 4 and were digested with PaqCI and cloned into PaqCI-digested pLJM1-EGFP-modified backbones.

Lentivirus

Lentiviral particles were produced by co-transfecting HEK293T cells with pMD2.G, psPAX2, and the appropriate transfer plasmid. Culture medium was replaced at 24, 48, 72, and 96 h post-transfection. Supernatants collected at 48, 72, and 96 h were pooled and clarified by passage through a 0.45 µm syringe filter. Viral particles were concentrated by ultracentrifugation (Sorvall WX80+) and resuspended in complete DMEM. Aliquots were stored at −80°C until use.

Chemical transfection

Plasmid transfections were performed with PEImax (1 mg/ml) at a 3:1 ratio of PEI:DNA. For a 24-well format, 500 ng of plasmid was diluted in 25 µl of Opti-MEM SFM. PEI (1.5 µl) was added dropwise, and the solution was gently mixed by flicking the tube. After a 10 min incubation at room temperature, the mixture was added dropwise to cells in 300 µl total culture medium. At 24 h post-transfection, the medium was replaced with 500 µl complete DMEM. Cells were passaged into 12-well plates at 48 h, and antibiotic selection was applied at 72 h.

Electroporation

Electroporations were performed with the Neon™ Transfection System (Thermo Fisher) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. KPCY cells (5.6 × 106) were mixed with 5 µg plasmid DNA and electroporated using the following parameters: 1150 V, 30 ms, 2 pulses. Cells were plated into one well of a six-well plate and cultured in complete medium. After 24 h, medium was replaced with fresh medium containing selection.

PCR amplification and clean-up

All amplification reactions were performed using Q5 Hot Start 2× Master Mix (New England Biolabs, Cat. No. M0494L) with SYBR™ Green I Nucleic Acid Gel Stain (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. S7563) on a CFX Connect Real-Time Detection System (Bio-Rad). Reactions were manually stopped before the plateau phase of the quantitative PCR (qPCR) curve unless otherwise specified. For downstream applications, PCR products were purified using either the DNA Clean & Concentrator-5 Kit (Zymo Research, Cat. No. D4014) for cloning, or Mag-Bind® TotalPure NGS beads (Omega Bio-tek) for sequencing.

Illumina sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from cell lines using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Cat. No. 69 504). A total of 300 ng genomic DNA was tagged in two PCR cycles using primer ETQ, followed by purification with magnetic beads. Tagged amplicons were then amplified for 20 cycles using primers ETR and ERU and purified with magnetic beads. TruSeq i7 and Nextera i5 combinatorial indexes were incorporated in an additional four PCR cycles, followed by a final bead cleanup. Libraries were pooled and sequenced on the Illumina MiniSeq platform using the Mid Output 300-cycle kit.

Oxford nanopore sequencing

Amplicon samples were individually barcoded using the Native Barcoding Kit 24 V14 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Cat. No. SQK-NBD114.24), pooled, and sequenced on a PromethION Flow Cell R10.4.1 (FLO-PRO114M). Flow cells were washed and reloaded with additional libraries every 24 h until either the flow cell was no longer viable, or no libraries remained. For re-editing experiments, primers EFV and EFT were used to amplify 10-target fragments, which were then barcoded as described above. These barcoded samples were sequenced on a Flongle Flow Cell R10.4.1 (FLO-FLG114) and run until the flow cell was no longer viable. Sequencing output (POD5 files) was basecalled using Dorado v0.7.0, with results exported as FASTQ files.

Single cell dilution sorting

Images of 96-well plates were acquired at 20× magnification in the RFP channel. Images were analyzed using Fiji to estimate cell counts. Selected wells were trypsinized with 30 µl of 0.25% trypsin (Corning, Cat. No. MT25053CI), washed with culture medium, and resuspended to a final volume of 1 ml in a microcentrifuge tube. Approximately 0.5 cells per 100 µl were transferred into a reservoir containing 25 ml of complete medium, which was gently mixed three times using a serological pipette. After mixing, 100 µl of cell suspension was dispensed into fresh 96-well plates. Plates were left undisturbed for 4 days, after which 50 µl of fresh medium was added. On day 7, wells were scanned for cell growth, and wells with observable cells received fresh medium.

Tagging PCR for genomic amplicon consensus (pipeline-umi-nf)

An abbreviated protocol is described here; for full details, see Amstler et al. Extracted DNA was first tagged with four PCR cycles using primers FVO and FVP. Tagged products were purified using 0.6 × magnetic beads. Eluted products were then amplified for five cycles (short PCR) with primers FVQ and FVR, followed by a second 0.6 × magnetic bead purification. The eluted short PCR products underwent a 20-cycle amplification (long PCR) using primers FVQ and FVR. Long PCR products were purified with 0.45 × magnetic beads, and amplicon quality and size were verified via DNA electrophoresis.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

KPCY clones were stably transfected on day 0. Antibiotic selection was applied beginning on day 2 and maintained through day 10. On day 4, clonal populations were single-cell sorted into 96-well plates at ∼1 cell per well. A total of 10 colonies per plate were expanded until reaching confluency in six-well plates (day 19). Colonies exhibiting the most uniform RFP expression were selected for single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), with one population chosen per clone. Cells were prepared according to the 10X Genomics GEM-X single-cell protocol. Populations from expanded clones were mixed in equal proportions, diluted to 1100 cells/µl, and loaded for a targeted recovery of 20 000 cells. Library preparation and sequencing were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions on an Illumina NextSeq 2000. Resulting transcriptome libraries were analyzed using Seurat (v5.1.0).

BASELINE IVT and library preparation

The Chromium GEM-X Single Cell 3′ v4 Gene Expression with Feature Barcoding protocol (10X Genomics) was followed through step 2. From the 40 µl complementary DNA (cDNA) amplification output, 5 µl was reserved for IVT using the HiScribe® T7 Quick High Yield RNA Synthesis Kit (New England Biolabs, Cat. No. E2050). IVT reactions were incubated at 37°C for 4 h, followed by DNase I treatment at 37°C for 30 min. RNA was purified using the Monarch® Spin RNA Cleanup Kit (NEB, Cat. No. T2040L) and quantified with a NanoPhotometer® N50 (Implen). First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed with 100 ng of RNA and the FDZ oligo using the ProtoScript® First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (NEB, Cat. No. E6300), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Oligo (dT)-captured transcripts were amplified using 1/10th of the cDNA reaction with primers FUI and FDZ. Recorder transcripts captured with the CS1 oligo were amplified with primers FUI and EWE. All amplifications were stopped at 5000 RFU (≤6 cycles) and purified using 0.45× magnetic beads. Eluted products were checked for fragment size and purity via DNA gel electrophoresis. If RNA quantity or quality was insufficient, additional amplification and/or magnetic bead purification was performed.

Synthetic lineage tree

KPCY BASELINE monoclonal populations (clone 1, 33, 76, and 84) were electroporated with PiggyBac-transposase and PB-Cas12a-ABE on day 0. On day 4, electroporated clones were single-cell dilution sorted into three 96-well plates using 100 µl of selection medium (2 µg/ml puromycin and 10 µg/ml blasticidin). On day 7, 50 µl of complete medium was added to all wells. By day 12, some wells contained > 1 000 cells and were RFP-positive; cell counts were determined using Fiji. Two RFP-positive wells per clone were selected and single-cell sorted again into two 96-well plates. The dilution sorting step was repeated on day 19 for two additional wells. Growing wells were expanded into 48-well plates, and genomic DNA was extracted at >95% confluency on day 33 using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Cat. No. 69 504).

Time course experiment

BASELINE constructs with intIDs were electroporated into KPCY cells along with PiggyBac-transposase on day −7. Cells were propagated and selected with 20 µg/ml blasticidin for 7 days post-electroporation. On day 0, BASELINE cells were electroporated with PB-Cas12a-ABE and PiggyBac-transposase. Separately, on day −1, cells were passaged into eight wells of a 24-well plate at 80 000 cells per well. Approximately 20 h post-passaging on day 0, cells were transduced with lentivirus from pSLQ11438. For both conditions, aliquots of cells were collected on day 0, washed, and genomic DNA was extracted. Cells were propagated under selection with 20 µg/ml blasticidin and 2 µg/ml puromycin until day 7. Selection was removed for 3 days and then reapplied at the same concentrations. One million cell aliquots were collected on days 7, 12, and 21 for genomic DNA extraction using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit.

Re-editing experiment

KPCY cells were co-transfected with either the unedited or pre-edited constructs, along with PB-Cas12a-ABE and PiggyBac-transposase to generate stable lines. Cells were selected with 20 µg/ml blasticidin and 2 µg/ml puromycin until day 7, after which selection was reduced to 10 µg/ml and 1 µg/ml, respectively. One million cell aliquots were collected on days 7 and 14, and genomic DNA was extracted using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Cat. No. 69 504).

Hybridization capture and library preparation

The Chromium GEM-X Single Cell 3′ v4 Gene Expression with Feature Barcoding protocol (10X Genomics) was followed through step 2. From the 40 µl cDNA amplification output, 5 µl was reserved for amplification with primers EWI-biotin and FDZ. PCR products were purified using 0.45 × magnetic beads. Purified DNA was processed following the tube protocol of the IDT xGen™ Hybridization and Wash Kit v2 for high-throughput hybridization capture of long genomic fragments. After heating and reverse transcription (RT) wash steps, captured DNA-bead complexes were subjected to amplification using primers EWI (nonbiotinylated) and FDZ for 10–15 cycles. Amplification reagents were provided in the hybridization capture kit. Products were separated from streptavidin beads using a magnet, and PCR products were analyzed for purity and size via DNA gel electrophoresis (Supplementary Fig. 11D, E).

Editing analysis of raw and consensus reads

We used python’s regex, pandas, numpy, and pysam functions to analyze the CIGAR strings from our alignment files. Briefly, regions of interest are extracted from the reference file. These could be the intID or specific target sequences. Next, adenine positions are extracted and indexed from the reference. Then, the CIGAR string is used to align the aligned read with the reference sequence and the adenine positions are analyzed for reference correctness. Lastly, this is repeated for all reads in the alignment file and for all alignment files that are needed to be analyzed.

Analysis for umi-pipeline-nf

Consensus sequences were generated using umi-pipeline-nf [37]. Analysis and QC followed the developer’s manual, with modifications to the configuration file. Importantly, we set a consensus read limit at four and a minimum read length at 1200 bp. Output alignment files of consensus sequences were parsed using the CIGAR strings from each sequence. A-to-G mutations were tabulated in binarized CSV files.

Analysis for scNanoGPS

Consensus sequence generation of ONT-sequenced IVT products was performed using scNanoGPS [38] (https://github.com/gaolabtools/scNanoGPS). Reads were first aligned to the reference with minimap2 2.24-r1122 and filtered by alignment score and length. Preprocessed reads were then analyzed with the Scanner, Assigner, and Curator modules, with modified parameters for the 5′ adapter sequence (Supplementary File 2), scanning region length (200 bp), and minimum read length (1200 bp). To improve consensus reliability, only cell barcodes with sufficient unique molecular identifier (UMI) support were retained. Final curated sequences were converted to BAM format for downstream analysis. These output alignment files of consensus sequences were parsed using their CIGAR strings from each sequence. A-to-G mutations were tabulated in binarized CSV files.

Cell lineage simulation

All cell simulations were performed with Counterfeiter, a framework for simulating lineage trees (https://github.com/mckennalab/counterfeiter). Counterfeiter was either given (i) the established base editing results from the time course experiment, in which we approximated the per-cell, per-generation editing rate, or (ii) set editing rates. For the existing in vitro editing rates, we established a per-generation rate. Given the unidirectional nature of base editing and the interdependence of editing rates over the tree, we can approximate a per-cell editing rate by calculating the chance of 1 minus not observing any editing events in a lineage, given the normalized final editing rate: 1 – (1-×)(1/generations), which we validated with simulations. Each editing rate was then passed to Counterfeiter for all sites as a parameter for the editing simulations. Both simulations were run with varying drop rates, where recorders were simulated to the final generation, and individual recorder integrations were dropped randomly to the given proportion. Trees were assembled with IQ-TREE [39], and analysis and downstream processing were performed with R and TreeUtils (https://github.com/mckennalab/TreeUtils). Normalized Robinson–Foulds (RF) comparisons between simulated trees and ground truth were performed using the RF.dist function from the phangorn [40] package. Cas9 simulations were performed with the same framework, with 10 targets per integration, and varying numbers of integrations, editing rates, and recorder drop rates.

Each population was aggregated for the in vitro cell lineage data, and the mean editing rate per site was calculated across all recorders. This was used to create a correlation matrix between samples, and a lineage tree across samples was created using hierarchical clustering. The closest neighbor for each tip was then calculated using the cophenetic.phylo method in the Ape [41] R package.

Copy-number inference

Clone 76_1 cells were partitioned into their own Seurat object. A meta-data column was added to label the 1237 barcodes (BCs) that were found with missing intIDs in the hierarchical clustering data. The counts matrix for the clone 76_1 cells was extracted. The SCEVAN [42] CNV pipeline was run with the extracted count matrix and with an indication that the 1237 cells were abnormal (the remaining cells were norm_cells). The output file {file_prefix}_heatmap.png indicated the abnormality between the 1237 cells and the others.

To identify genes whose expression was structured by clonal lineage, we performed phylogeny-aware analyses on a downsampled single-cell dataset containing 200 cells from each of seven clonal populations. IQ-TREE2 [39] was used to generate trees for each downsampled clonal population, which were then combined to construct a single-cell lineage tree using the Ape [41] package in R. Gene expression values were obtained from the corresponding Seurat object and analyzed using two complementary approaches. First, SCOUT [43] was used to fit continuous-trait evolutionary models to each gene across the lineage tree, including Brownian motion, a single-optimum Ornstein–Uhlenbeck model, and a multi-optimum Ornstein–Uhlenbeck model. Genes were ranked within each model class using fitted model parameters, including σ for Brownian motion-like drift and α for lineage- or regime-specific constraint. Second, PATH [44] was used to quantify phylogenetic autocorrelation for each gene using Moran’s I, with diagonal entries representing each gene’s own phylogenetic signal. Top-ranked genes from SCOUT and PATH were visualized by projecting normalized expression values onto circular cladograms using ggtree and ggtreeExtra, with one heatmap tile per cell aligned to the corresponding tree tip. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) feature plots were generated from the same Seurat object and displayed alongside tree-based expression visualizations to compare transcriptional clustering with lineage-associated expression structure. To compare lineage-aware gene sets with conventional single-cell analyses, cluster markers were identified using Seurat’s [45] FindAllMarkers, filtered by corrected P-value, and the overlap with SCOUT and PATH-prioritized genes was assessed by gene-set enrichment analysis, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, and direct marker-overlap summaries.

Results

Building baseline

Base editing introduces precise, unidirectional nucleotide changes without inducing double-stranded breaks, unlike canonical CRISPR/Cas enzymes [16]. This approach dramatically reduces large deletions commonly observed in previous lineage recording technologies [8, 10, 46]. To develop our prototype recorder, we computationally generated and screened Cas12a target sequences containing between 3 and 9 editable nucleotides—adenines for ABEs and cytosines for cytosine base editors (CBEs)—against potential genomic off-targets, selecting a library of 990 novel target sequences. This set was combined with eight previously validated, unpublished targets from our lab and two known, active human targets [47], producing a final library of 1000 target sequences. Because published Cas12a base editors differ in design and reported activity, we sought to identify the most effective construct for our system without requiring modifications. We therefore evaluated editing efficiencies in HEK293T cells using six commercially available Cas12a base editors: three CBEs and three ABEs (Supplementary File 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1A and B). Across eight experimental conditions (see the ‘Materials and methods’ section), we recovered an average of 99.2% of target sequences (Supplementary Fig. 1C). The broadest base editing activity was observed with the ABE construct pSLQ11438, which edited 651 out of 1000 targets (Supplementary Fig. 1D), while among CBEs, construct 114 082 exhibited the widest activity, editing 252 targets.

A central goal in developing BASELINE was to maintain flexibility for end users, ensuring that the recorder could be deployed with either adenine or cytosine base editors, or in combination. To this end, we selected a final target set that maximized editing efficiency and recording longevity across modalities: 30 targets efficiently editable by ABEs, 19 compatible with both ABEs and CBEs, and one uniquely suited to CBEs (Supplementary Fig. 1F). Of these 50 targets, 40 ranked among the most editable by ABE (Supplementary Fig. 1E), comprising 272 adenines total, with 147 positioned within the high-activity Cas12a editing window [48] (Supplementary Fig. 1G). Conceptually, BASELINE spans ∼1000 bp (50 targets × 20 bp each), restricting sequencing options to third-generation, long-read platforms. To enhance accessibility, we optimized the cost-effective nanopore sequencing method.

Unlike traditional Cas9-based recorders, BASELINE uses Cas12a to efficiently edit multiple targets from a single multiplexed guide array. To demonstrate this, we constructed a minimal recorder containing 10 targets and tested it alongside a corresponding 10-guide cassette. Stable integration into HEK293T cells followed by transient transfection of the editor (pSLQ11438) and guide cassette (Supplementary Fig. 2A) revealed increased editing proportions at the most highly edited adenine across all targets. As expected, A-to-G edits were enriched in the +ABE condition compared to −ABE controls (Supplementary Fig. 2B). However, unexpectedly high cumulative edit rates per target in the −ABE sample—particularly at targets 4 and 8—likely reflected the inherent sequencing errors associated with low-quality ONT sequencing rather than true biological edits (Supplementary Fig. 2B and C). When we compared canonical base-editing sites to noncanonical sites using improved Nanopore chemistry, it was apparent that sequencing errors played a significant role (Supplementary Fig. 2D and E). To confirm that base editing outcomes could be measured with high fidelity, we sequenced a separate minimal recorder using Illumina sequencing following 14 days of editing. Transition matrix analysis revealed extremely low background error rates and clear enrichment of A-to-G edits (Supplementary Fig. 2F). These results show that Illumina reads capture the expected editing profiles with high accuracy, whereas ONT reads require additional error-correction strategies. Taken together, these findings indicate that Cas12a ABE-based lineage recording remains a viable approach, provided that sequencing errors are appropriately managed.

We then concatenated all 50 selected targets into a full-length array (Fig. 1A), arranging targets and PAMs head-to-tail without intervening buffer sequences to minimize the recorder length. Multiplexed 10-gRNA cassettes were integrated in reverse orientation to produce a single base-editing lineage tracing vector (Fig. 1B). This construct was stably transfected into the KPCY (6419c5) cell line, an established model of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma. Editing was induced via lentiviral transduction of the ABE construct. After 21 days, 4.5% of reference adenines were converted to guanines (A-to-G; Fig. 1C). Above a 1% editing-rate threshold, we identified 150 adenine positions exhibiting A-to-G conversions (Fig. 1D) across target positions and guide cassette identity (Fig. 1E).

Analysis of conversion matrices indicated that nonadenine positions exhibited a background mutation rate exceeding 2%, reflecting sequencing and other technical errors. Individual base substitution errors ranged 0.1%–0.9%, including nondeamination changes, consistent with the expected ONT error rate. Critically, simulations demonstrated that elevated sequencing error rates substantially diminished phylogenetic tree reconstruction accuracy (Supplementary Fig. 3A), highlighting the importance of precise basecalling and rigorous error correction for reliable lineage tracing. To lower our background noise, we implemented a computational consensus-building approach leveraging custom tagging and amplification oligos [37, 49]. This method clusters amplicons by terminal tags to generate consensus sequences, effectively lowering error rates from ∼Q18 to ∼Q46 (Fig. 1F and G, and Supplementary Fig. 3B). Unless otherwise stated, all subsequent ONT analyses utilize consensus-built sequences.

Encoding and decoding cellular memories from BASELINE recorders

Before characterizing the behavior of BASELINE, we first sought to determine whether an alternative method for introducing Cas12a-ABE could improve editing performance. To do this, we relocated the transducible ABE cassette from pSLQ11438 into a PiggyBac vector (Supplementary Fig. 4A). If this approach remained functional, it would allow us to simultaneously engineer our cell line of interest with both the editor (ABE) and the recorder (BASELINE). To compare editing accumulation and transgene silencing/variegation [50–52], KPCY BASELINE cells were transfected or transduced with PiggyBac- or lentiviral-delivered ABE constructs, and editing proportions were quantified after 21 days. Results showed higher average editing proportion and more edited sites using the PiggyBac-ABE construct (Supplementary Fig. 4B). At the least stringent editing threshold of 0.001 (0.1% edit rate needed to pass the filter in our consensus sequences), we observed 220 sites edited in the PiggyBac sample and 183 sites in the lentivirus sample, with average editing proportions at 0.160 and 0.067, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 4B). At a more conservative threshold (1%), 157 sites were edited in the PiggyBac compared to 129 in the lentivirus sample (average editing of 0.223 and 0.094). To compare the integration count per sample we performed qPCR on genomic DNA from 10 000 cell equivalents and found that the lentivirus sample appeared to have an average of ∼2 integrations per cell while the PiggyBac sample averaged ∼1.6 integrations per cell (Supplementary Fig. 4C). Despite the lower apparent integration count, the higher editing activity led us to use the PiggyBac construct in subsequent experiments.

After establishing PiggyBac as the optimal delivery method for BASELINE and Cas12a-ABE, we next examined the editing dynamics of our system. In particular, we asked whether editing of one adenine affects the ability to edit neighboring adenines within the same Cas12a target [53–56] (Supplementary Fig. 5A). To address this, we designed two target arrays corresponding to the first ten targets of BASELINE: one identical to the original sequence and another containing pre-edited targets. Each pre-edited target incorporated the most common individual base edit observed for that target in our 21-day dataset (Supplementary Fig. 5A and Supplementary File 2). We then generated stably integrated KPCY ‘WT’ and ‘pre-edited’ cell lines and exposed them to transient base editing. In our pre-edited recorder, most alternative sites were still actively edited, albeit at slightly lower rates than in the wild-type recorder (Supplementary Fig. 5B and C). These results recapitulate findings from other groups [53, 57], demonstrating that single-base mismatches do not fully inhibit Cas12a editing and thereby preserve editing independence among neighboring adenines. These data also suggest that partially edited targets accumulate additional editing events at a slower rate, further reducing the likelihood that a given recorder becomes fully saturated.

We next sought to measure the accumulation of these editing events. We performed a time course experiment, capturing genomic DNA from stable KPCY BASELINE cells on days post-transfection 0, 7, 12, and 21 (Fig. 2A). Across the length of the recorder, we saw steady increases in guanine accumulation from day 0 to day 21 (Fig. 2B). When comparing the 157 edits between day 21 and the 150 edits in the raw data from Fig. 1D, we found that 142 of them were shared, eight were unique to Fig. 1D, and 15 unique to the day 21 data (Supplementary Table 1). While the A-to-G changes are most prominent, at a significantly lower proportion, we observed the accumulation of T-to-C edits (Supplementary Fig. 6A). We hypothesized that the complement-strand adenines are being edited by Cas12a-ABE, albeit at a 45× lower rate. Noncanonical edits and deletions increase over time but remain rare (<1/1 000 bases). Analysis of the deletion events showed that the majority are one base in length and were evenly distributed across the recorder (Supplementary Fig. 6B and C). Such events are expected, as Cas12a and other CRISPR/Cas nucleases exhibit low levels of off-target editing and nicking [53]. At the target level, editing accumulated uniformly, with the exception of targets 16, 20, and 26, which displayed slower accumulation (Fig. 2C). The per-target and per-base editing proportions exhibited similar patterns, increasing by 1.8% from day 0 to day 7, by 16.7% from day 7 to day 12, and by 26.6% from day 12 to day 21. Collapsing the day 21 targets on top of one another allowed us to define the editing window of Cas12a-ABE, confirming that BASELINE recorders accumulate edits over an extended period in a manner consistent with the established behavior of Cas12a-ABE.

Lastly, we investigated variability in editing patterns between recorders. After filtering for edited recorders and quantifying their edits (Fig. 2E), we observed that most exhibited unique editing patterns (Fig. 2F), which were also evident within individual target sites (Supplementary Fig. 6D and Supplementary Table 2). For example, target 8 contains five editable adenines, allowing for 25 [32] possible editing outcomes; nine of these occurred in more than 1% of reads. Extending this analysis to all 50 targets and taking the product of per-target editing patterns, we estimated a total information recording capacity exceeding 1.90 × 1035 (Supplementary Table 3). While this estimated capacity assumes each editing outcome is equally likely and is inflated, it still greatly exceeds the necessary capacity for mammalian phylogenetic tree reconstruction (∼3.7 × 1012) [58–60]. In this time course experiment, BASELINE accumulates edits predictably and consistently over the 21 days, edits are spread across all targets of the recorder, and the recorders accumulate edits in a pattern that makes them unique and distinguishable from other edited recorders. Collectively, these results validate BASELINE as a robust and information-rich approach, providing a foundation for its application in experimental lineage tracing.

Simulations of BASELINE reconstruct highly accurate phylogenetic trees

Given the editing rates detailed above, we then set out to measure the reconstruction accuracy of BASELINE. We devised a computational framework to simulate lineage trees using both our observed editing rate (back-calculated to a per-generation rate, see the ‘Materials and methods’ section) and a range of potential editing rates (Fig. 3A). We included a dropout rate to simulate the challenges of recovering the recorder with single-cell sequencing technologies. We were also interested in how sparse sampling of a large lineage tree would affect reconstruction accuracy, for instance, subsampling 5000 cells from a whole organismal lineage tracing experiment. To that end, we generated lineage trees ranging in size from 24 (16) to 220 (∼1 million cells), randomly sampling 212 (4096) cells from each tree. Lastly, we varied the number of integrated recorders from 1 to 30, representing the range of integrations observed in our KPCY and HEK293T models.

Each simulation was run five times, and trees were reconstructed using IQ-TREE [39]. We then compared these reconstructed trees to the simulated ground-truth trees using the normalized RF distance [61]. We observe higher accuracies at moderate editing rates, with improved performance at higher integration counts (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. 7A). We achieved accurate (∼100%) reconstructions at 12 generations, with the lowest reconstruction accuracies observed with high per-generation editing rates, driving early saturation of the recorders (Supplementary Fig. 7A).

We then simulated trees using the editing rates established in our KPCY time course (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Fig. 7B), which again yielded high-accuracy reconstructions across most parameters. Despite even sparse sampling of individual cells (0.3% of cells recovered in the 20 cell division trees), we recovered 95% accurate lineage trees with three recorder integrations. We achieved ∼100% accuracy with ten or more integrations (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Fig. 7B). The most inaccurate reconstructions occurred when using <5 recorders, a 50% recorder dropout rate, and at early cell divisions, causing accuracy to drop to ∼50% in the three-recorder, six-generation simulations (Supplementary Fig. 7B). These results compared favorably to simulated Cas9 lineages both with and without the effect of large deletions commonly seen in Cas9 nucleases (Supplementary Fig. 8A and B).

We next created a cell-cultured lineage tree using our in vitro KPYC system to further validate BASELINE’s ability to record lineage relationships. We electroporated KPCY cell populations with our construct and isolated individual clones, which we characterized for the number of integrations using DNA amplicon sequencing (see the ‘Materials and methods’ section). High-confidence integration counts ranged from 3 to 32. We selected four populations (clones 1, 33, 76, and 84) to expand into a synthetic lineage tree, isolating single cells and expanding into a population twice over to create granddaughter populations (Fig. 3D and Supplementary File 3). We initially focused our lineage analysis on clone 1′s eight granddaughter populations. Clone 1 contains 1 088 sites over 4 recorders, of which 364 were fixed (>99% edited) in at least one of the granddaughter populations (Fig. 3E, bottom). Of these, 46 sites were fixed in at least one population and absent (<1%) in another, indicating a lineage-informative site (Fig. 3E, right). 24 of these sites statistically differed between the parental groups (Wilcox test < 0.05).

To ensure that BASELINE recorded lineage-specific information at all levels of the resulting tree, we compared the correlation of base editing outcomes across cousin populations (only sharing the grandparent cell) and within sibling populations (Supplementary Fig. 9). In clonal population 1, sibling cell recorders with a shared intID were > 90% correlated, which decays to 87% in cousin cells and to 74% in recorders with different IDs, representing lineage specificity over the background of shared editing patterns. We observed a similar relationship in clone 33, 76, and 84, where recorders are most similar between sibling cell populations and within shared intIDs (Supplementary Fig. 9).

We used these aggregated editing proportions in each granddaughter population to test tree reconstruction. We used average editing rates for each intID to create a population-level recorder for each granddaughter well (for instance, each clone 1 granddaughter well has four aggregated recorders with base modification proportions at each position), which was reassembled into a population-level tree that reconstructs the full set of clonal relationships (see the ‘Materials and methods’ section, Fig. 3E). Although our bulk DNA sequencing data does not allow us to assign recorders to individual cells, we were curious if aggregating recorders into synthetic cells would still enable accurate tree reconstruction. We simulated 600 cells from each granddaughter well, drawing recorders randomly for each integration in clone 33 (three integrations per cell). Across 100 simulated trees, 99.73% of simulated cells were assembled closest to a sibling cell on the resulting tree. There was sparse misassignment of cells to cousin populations, an example of which is shown in Fig. 3E. Our simulations demonstrate BASELINE’s high recording capacity and accuracy (>99%) with real-world base editing rates.

Accurate single-cell reconstruction of KPCY clonal populations

To characterize BASELINE dynamics in vitro and assess lineage recorder expression and recovery, we performed scRNA-seq on clonal KPCY BASELINE populations using the 10X Genomics platform. We evaluated three approaches to maximize BASELINE recorder recovery: (i) capture-sequence driven recovery [62], (ii) hybridization capture, and (iii) IVT (Supplementary Fig. 10). The capture-sequence (CS1) approach was unsuccessful, producing unintended amplicons and requiring excessive PCR cycles (Supplementary Fig. 11A–C). Hybridization capture improved the recovery of the target recorder but still yielded nonspecific products and necessitated over-amplification (Supplementary Fig. 11D and E). IVT created clean product bands, required fewer than 10 amplification cycles post RT, and enabled simultaneous isolation of BASELINE recorders from the oligo (dT) and the 10X capture sequence pools (Supplementary Fig. 11F). IVT was selected as the optimal recovery method due to its specificity, efficiency, and compatibility with multiple capture strategies.

With an established recorder recovery and isolation protocol, we selected six clonal populations for lineage reconstruction across two scRNA-seq experiments: clone 1, clone 32, clone 33, clone 52, clone 76 (two independent populations), and clone 84. Integration count information was again established using DNA amplicon sequencing (Supplementary Fig. 12A). Clones were transfected with PiggyBac-ABE on day 0 and single cells were sorted into individual wells of a 96-well plate on day 4. Wells were expanded and passaged until confluency was reached in a six-well plate (19 days post-transfection), after which cells were subjected to scRNA-seq (Fig. 4A). Following recorder recovery and isolation, BASELINE recorders were sequenced on a PromethION flow cell, while single-cell transcriptomes were sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq (Supplementary Fig. 12B). The transcriptional profiles of all clones showed divergence from one another with minor overlap (Fig. 4B), reflecting the expected heterogeneity of this KPCY parental cell line [10, 63].

We generated consensus BASELINE recorder transcripts using a custom pipeline adapted from the scNanoGPS toolset [38]. Comparison of the 10× cell barcode (BC) recovery across runs showed that of the 35 035 cells that passed Seurat filtering, nearly 98% (34 264) had associated lineage information that passed filtering by scNanoGPS (Supplementary Table 4). BCs were subsequently assigned to clusters based on the intIDs found in the consensus recorder sequences (Supplementary Fig. 13A, see the ‘Materials and methods’ section). Each cluster corresponded to a distinct clone, for which recorder recovery and editing statistics were quantified. Importantly, recorder recovery within each clone was effectively indiscriminate across cells, indicating that recorders were consistently captured regardless of the cell in which they resided (Supplementary Fig. 13B). This uniform recovery supports the reliability of recorder-based lineage reconstruction within clonal populations.

Recorder capture per cell varied across clones, with clone 52 exhibiting the lowest median capture rate at 72.7% and clone 1 achieving 100% (Fig. 4C). While capture remained generally consistent, the base-edit count per recorder showed greater variability. Notably, one run of clone 76 (76_1) displayed a median edit count of 30 per recorder (Fig. 4D). Given differences in recorder integration numbers among clones, total edit counts per cell differed markedly, ranging from a median of 193 edits in clone 33 (3 integrations) to 2140 edits in clone 84 (34 integrations) (Fig. 4E). Remarkably, over 75% of clone 84 cells contained more than 1 900 edits—representing a 50-fold improvement over current state-of-the-art systems [64]. This greatly improves clonal reconstruction when compared to our previous Cas9-based lineage tracing in the KPCY model (Supplementary Fig. 14). To better understand the remaining editing capacity of our clones, we calculated recording saturation per cell (Fig. 4F). Except for an outlier in clone 76_1, saturation percentages consistently clustered around 20%.

Further analysis of editing efficiency across all 272 adenine positions (Supplementary Table 5 and Supplementary Fig. 15) revealed large clusters of positions that contained similar editing patterns between clones. After filtering out the least informative datapoints (above 95% or below 5% edit rate in all datasets) as well as the clone 76_1 dataset, we found that 162 adenine positions are variable across the recorders. This variability is expected, as some positions are consistently edited while others exhibit stochastic editing, creating combinatorial diversity. These findings indicate that, despite overall consistency at many adenine positions, the large number of variably edited adenines provides sufficient diversity to preserve unique editing signatures across individual recorders.

Using IQ-TREE, we reconstructed phylogenetic trees for all clones (Fig. 4G and Supplementary Fig. 16). The reconstruction of clone 76_1 resulted in an asymmetric tree caused by the low levels of editing and a lack of uniquely edited recorders throughout the cell population (Supplementary Table 6). We also observed a large gap in the hierarchical clustering heatmap (Supplementary Fig. 17), indicating a large proportion of our cells where recorder recovery failed. Failure to recover some recorders is typical in a scRNA-seq experiment but the pattern is not typically seen so consistently across a large number of cells. To ensure that these cells were not responsible for the resulting tree structure, we mapped the 1237 BCs, that lacked recorders 1, 4, 5, 6, 7, and 8, onto the tree and quantified that over 98% of these BCs mapped to a single tree clade (Supplementary Fig. 18).

We removed these potentially confounding variables by generating the clone 76_1 data and tree structure without these six recorders. Mapping of the previously identified 1237 BCs to the new tree showed a near-identical projection, that these cells were highly related and all formed one clade of our tree indicating that the missing recorders had little effect on tree reconstruction (Supplementary Fig. 18A). Copy number variation inference analysis on the clone 76_1 dataset reveals that the 1237 cells have a shared loss of chromosome 2 (Supplementary Fig. 18B). Highlighting these 1237 cells on the UMAP of only clone 76_1 showed a high degree of variability (Supplementary Fig. 18C). This is not surprising given that no exogenous force is pushing or pulling the cells towards any transcriptomic landscape. Together, these data provided us with strong evidence that phylogenetic tree reconstruction was highly replicable despite varied missing recorder information, further compounding the evidence that BASELINE is a reliable lineage recorder.

Our most information-rich cell population was clone 84, containing 34 high-confidence integrations and a total of 9248 editable sites. Recorder recovery, editing counts, and saturation were relatively uniform compared to other clones and the resulting reconstructed tree remained highly balanced (Fig. 4G), with an average branch depth of 29, a maximum depth of 40, and with 91.7% of leaves representing ≥20 branch divisions (Fig. 4I). To assess phylogenetic signal, we took two approaches. First, we randomly sampled two clades and compared their editing patterns to evaluate branch-point support and divergence (Fig. 4H). Adenines unedited in both clades were excluded, leaving 4320 sites to serve as potential clade-defining markers. Of these, 2123 were jointly edited in both clades, 680 uniquely supported the substructure in each clade (340 per clade), and 1517 were partially edited in subclades, representing local lineage structure. Across these two clades, clone 84 cells retained 4928 unedited sites potentially available for future lineage recording. We next assessed transcriptional differences between clones using differential gene expression analysis and two single-cell phylogenetic tools, PATH [44] and SCOUT [43]. Both tools highlighted gene expression changes between clones that were not detectable by conventional differential expression analysis (Supplementary Fig. 19A), including several genes associated with pancreatic cancer progression (Supplementary Fig. 19B–F).

Discussion

This study introduces BASELINE, a Cas12a-based lineage tracing platform capable of reconstructing accurate phylogenetic trees over biologically relevant timescales. Compared with Cas9 systems, Cas12a offers a key advantage: the ability to multiplex large numbers of guide sequences into compact payloads [65–68]. Coupled with base editing, BASELINE avoids large intra-recorder deletions that can erase lineage information [69]. We demonstrate reliable recovery of a 1.5 kbp recording array using IVT, and we leverage ONT long-read sequencing to generate high-accuracy consensus sequences alongside single cell transcriptomes.

Other groups have recognized the advantages of Cas12a and developed both base editing [48] and canonical Cas12a nuclease-based [70, 71] recording technologies. The base editing work provided an important proof-of-concept showing that base edits can serve as stable and reliable markers of editing history. However, the system was limited to just four endogenous targets per cell, greatly restricting its recording capacity, and inducible guides suffered from leakiness that reduced control over editing sensitivity. Canonical Cas12a approaches, including DuTracer [71] and DAISY [70], demonstrated the feasibility of Cas12a for lineage barcoding, either alone or in combination with Cas9 [72]. Yet these methods remain constrained by a small number of available targets per cell, frequent target exhaustion, low integration counts in scRNA-seq experiments, and persistent problems with inducible control and engineering complexity. In contrast, BASELINE was designed to minimize these hurdles, providing scalable recording capacity in a format directly compatible with single-cell transcriptomics.

BASELINE recorders are robustly expressed and captured in droplet-based scRNA-seq workflows; we recovered at least one recorder in ∼100% of cells and ∼75% of all possible recorders. This broad recovery enables deep lineage reconstruction. In clone 84, the lineage tree reached an average tree depth of 29 cell divisions, with >4900 potential base edits still available for future recording. Despite the poor editing rates in one of the clone 76 results, we validated tree reconstruction using a shared chromosomal event, achieving >98% identical cell assignments. Together, these features position BASELINE as a system that advances the state of the art while reducing engineering and analysis complexity.

The long-term goal of lineage technologies is to capture comprehensive cell division histories alongside single-cell state measurements, ideally within complex developing systems such as organoids or in vivo models. While BASELINE represents a meaningful step towards this goal, limitations remain. As designed, the system requires at least two recorder integrations per cell to achieve ∼92% accuracy after 12 generations. With our current antibiotic selection-based methods, high-integration founder cells must be identified by single-cell isolation and screening. Although our modifications enable median recovery of ∼75% of recorders—substantially improving transcript capture—full recovery remains the target. Editing rate variability also affects reconstruction accuracy, as reflected in clone 76_1′s more asymmetric tree, and is consistent with our simulation results linking editing rate to phylogenetic resolution. We also only included canonical A-to-G conversions in our tree reconstruction, and future efforts could include all high-quality base modifications, as well as model the interdependence between adenines within a target to improve tree reconstruction. Additionally, because BASELINE assumes constitutive Cas12a activity, any expression silencing could curtail recording capacity and impact reconstruction accuracy. Furthermore, our consensus-building approach requires repeatedly sequencing each barcode to reduce sequencing errors, which increases the cost.

Looking ahead, applying BASELINE in vivo would offer exciting opportunities to capture lineage and state dynamics within developing tissues or disease models. Its compact, multiplexable design and compatibility with scRNA-seq and spatial profiling make it well-suited to these complex contexts. At the same time, in vivo use would introduce new challenges, including heterogeneous editing rates across cell types, the possibility of immune responses to the editor, and the difficulty of achieving robust recovery of recorder arrays from complex tissues. Finally, BASELINE has only so far been extensively tested in the KPCY cell line, with a doubling time of ∼16 h [63]. This relatively rapid proliferation rate likely contributes to the robust editing and lineage recording we observe. Extending these analyses to additional cell lines, with careful measurement of editing rates per cell division, could provide further insights and help optimize the broader application of BASELINE.

Despite these limitations, BASELINE offers an efficient, versatile, and accessible framework for lineage tracing. Our use of Cas12a-ABE builds upon prior work [72], while leaving Cas9 available for gene perturbation or as an independent recording channel. Moreover, base editing is inherently compatible with single-cell transcriptional profiling and hybridization-based spatial sequencing [73]. With high reconstruction accuracies across a range of integration counts, continued improvements in long-read sequencing, and advances in scRNA-seq transcript capture, BASELINE is well-positioned to drive increasingly detailed and accurate lineage maps and ultimately deeper biological insights.
