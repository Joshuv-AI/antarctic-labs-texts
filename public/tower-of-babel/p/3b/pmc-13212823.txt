Epidermoid cysts of the cerebellopontine angle in pediatric population—an institutional surgical case series and review of literature
Authors: Rozan Albanna, David Krieg, Vanja Cengija, Arild Egge, Radek Frič
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEpidermoid cysts (EC) are rare intracranial lesions, particularly uncommon in children, arising from ectodermal inclusions during neural tube closure. Although they are often located in the cerebellopontine angle (CPA), data on complication and recurrence rates after surgery for EC in this location in children remain limited.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed data from children (≤ 18 years) surgically treated for EC in CPA at the Oslo University Hospital-Rikshospitalet between 2004 and 2024, with > 1 year of follow-up. Clinical presentation, imaging, and surgical outcomes were assessed. A systematic review of the literature was conducted according to PRISMA guidelines.ResultsSix patients (mean age 15 ± 2 years, range 12–17; male/female ratio 1:2) were included. The most common presenting symptoms were headache (83%) and ataxia (33%). Mean diameter on preoperative MRI was 37 ± 5.4 mm (range 30–45). All patients were treated using the standard retrosigmoid approach. Gross total resection (GTR) was achieved in four patients (67%). Postoperative complications were limited to transient diplopia and hearing impairment, respectively, in two patients (34%). No mortality or permanent morbidity was observed. Recurrence was observed in two patients, in one of them after GTR.ConclusionEC of the CPA in children can be safely and effectively removed, yielding favorable outcomes with low morbidity. Although GTR should be attempted, residual tissue may persist in hidden anatomical recesses and predispose to recurrence after subtotal removal. Endoscopic assistance may enhance the completeness of resection. Small asymptomatic remnants can be monitored. Long-term MRI follow-up is recommended.Keywords: Epidermoid cyst, Cerebellopontine angle, Retrosigmoid craniotomy

Introduction

Epidermoid and dermoid cysts present as rare intracranial space-occupying lesions, accounting for 0.2% to 1.8% of all intracranial tumors [1]. The majority of epidermoid cysts (EC) in the central nervous system are found in adults, with a mean age at presentation of 40 years, while they are very rare in the pediatric population [1].

EC are thought to arise from inadvertent inclusion of ectodermal elements during neural tube closure, trapping them in the diploe, meninges, scalp, or suture lines. Intradiploic cysts may also occur secondary to trauma [2, 3]. Regardless of the mechanism underlying their formation, EC of the central nervous system are overwhelmingly intradural and rarely perforate the bone, even when located in the intradiploic space [1].

Specifically, EC located in the cerebellopontine angle (CPA), as one of the generally predilectionary locations [4–6], are extremely rare in children [7], representing approximately 5% of pediatric tumors encountered in CPA [8]. Accordingly, data on complication and recurrence rates after surgery for EC in this location in children remain limited. Based on the experience from our institution, we therefore present findings from the treatment of this specific group of patients.

Material and methods

The study was approved by the institutional personal data protection officer as a quality control study (reference number 25/08096), waiving the need for informed consent.

Pediatric patients (≤ 18 years) surgically treated for a radiologically identified and histopathologically verified EC at the Oslo University Hospital-Rikshospitalet between 2004 and 2024, and with at least a 1-year follow-up, were included. Patients’ medical records were reviewed by the first and senior author (R.A., R.F.), and pre- and postoperative radiological studies by a board-certified neuroradiologist (V.C.): here, the diffusion-weighted sequence (DWI) demonstrating restricted diffusion was the most specific for radiological diagnosis. A high-resolution, heavily T2-weighted sequence was used to accurately measure the diameter of EC.

A systematic review of literature was performed (D.K., R.A.) according to PRISMA guidelines, searching in PubMed, SCOPUS, and Embase using title/abstract and keywords (“epidermoid cyst”; “pediatric”; “children”; “cerebellopontine angle”; “CPA”; “tumor”). Only primary research articles mentioning intracranial EC in pediatric patients with individual data were included.

Results

Patient sample

The patient sample consisted of six patients (male/female ratio 1:2) with a mean age of 15 ± 2 years (range 12–17). Presenting symptoms were headache in 5/6 cases (83%), ataxia in 2/6 (33%), and dizziness, jaw pain, and reduced hearing in 1/6 cases each (17%). One child had unrelated psychiatric problems. Papilledema was observed on ophthalmoscopy in one patient.

MRI showed a typical, slightly hyperintense lesion on T2-weighted images with diffusion restriction on DWI in CPA in all cases (Fig. 1A, B): three of them were located on the right and three on the left side. The largest diameter was a mean 37 ± 5.4 mm (range 30–45 mm). In cranio-caudal direction, the extent of the lesion was from the cranial nerve III (in all cases) down to cranial nerves VI (2 cases), VII–VIII (1 case), and IX–XI (3 cases), respectively.

Despite a documented remnant on the first postoperative MRI (white arrow) (C), only one of these patients showed growth on the latest MRI (D)

All patients were operated using a conventional retrosigmoid approach, in park-bench position and with microsurgical, retraction-free technique (Fig. 2). According to surgical notes, gross total resection (GTR) was achieved in 4/6 (67%) cases, as also confirmed by immediate postoperative MRI (Table 1).

Follow-up

There was no mortality or serious/permanent morbidity related to surgery; two patients (34%) experienced transient diplopia and reduced hearing, respectively. Histopathological investigation confirmed the diagnosis of EC in all cases.

The duration of the follow-up was a median of 60 months (range 13–246). Despite a documented remnant of EC on the first postoperative MRI in 2/6 patients (33%), no patient required repeated surgery during the follow-up. On the latest MRI, only one of these two patients showed growth of the EC remnant (Fig. 1C, D). In addition, another patient showed a recurrence of the EC.

Review of literature

The systematic search of literature reporting EC in CPA encountered in pediatric patients (≤ 18 years of age) and containing individual data, yielded 17 studies including 18 patients in this age group (Fig. 3). While we found seven individual case reports on pediatric patients [9–15], other reports contained mixed study populations where only one pediatric patient was reported in each study (out of 1–36 patients included) [2, 3, 7, 16–21], except for one study where two out of three patients were children [22]. Median age of these 18 cases was 17 years (range 3–18), but retrieving data on rates of GTR, complications, and recurrences for pediatric patients only was not possible from these mixed study populations (Table 2). In addition, one study reported a case that was not operated on [23], and another one a 17-year-old patient who refused intervention [24].

(four cases) [8], Phi et al. (one case, but only mentioned in a figure without further details) [26], Tomita and Grahovac (four cases but not all in CPA) [27], and Massimi et al. Specific data from patients ≤ 18 years of age were also difficult to find in other mixed series [5, 6, 30–34].

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first case series on EC located in the CPA from an exclusively pediatric population. In previously published series, only individual pediatric cases were reported [9–15] or within predominantly adult case series (Table 2) [2, 3, 7, 16–18, 20–22].

Clinical characteristics

According to data from existing literature as well as the present case series, the vast majority of cases observed in the pediatric population occur in adolescents. As only one patient was reported to be younger than 10 years [14], the predominance of adolescents may raise questions about the possible role of hormonal changes during puberty in the growth of EC. However, although there were two-thirds of females in our own cohort, both sexes seem to be equally represented among reported cases in the literature (Table 2).

Although presenting as relatively large lesions (mean diameter 37 mm in our series) and often with radiologically evident compression of the brainstem, the symptoms are rarely dramatic, as EC grow slowly. Non-specific headache, ataxia, and dizziness seem to be the most common symptoms [2, 13, 20]. Presumably due to their typically soft consistency and slow growth, EC at this location—at least in our experience—do not usually cause hydrocephalus.

Surgical treatment

EC in CPA can be effectively and safely removed microsurgically using the conventional retrosigmoid approach, and the relief of symptoms without permanent morbidity can be expected in most patients [7, 13, 17, 21]. Several other different surgical approaches have also been utilized [4, 30, 34], but in our view, more complex skull base approaches would in pediatric cases only increase possible surgical morbidity and are rarely necessary. Pure endoscopic resection of EC has also been described as a feasible option [5, 32].

ECs are generally considered surgically feasible lesions due to soft consistency and usually manageable adhesion to neurovascular structures in the CPA. In general, GTR is advocated whenever feasible, to avoid recurrences [31]. We achieved GTR in 67% of our patients, which is consistent with previously published pediatric series, where GTR rates ranged between 60 and 80%. However, remnants of EC may sometimes persist in locations beyond the surgical field exposed by the standard retrosigmoid approach using a microscope [18]. In our series, such remnants were identified at the entry to Dorello’s canal and in Meckel’s cave (Fig. 1C, D), as well as in the prepontine cistern. Endoscopic inspection of the resection cavity has been recommended to minimize the risk of leaving these remnants behind [6, 17, 23], a technique we also utilized in some cases.

Traditionally, GTR has also been advocated because residual tumor may theoretically predispose to aseptic meningitis [2]. However, we did not observe this complication in our patients with documented EC remnants or recurrence. Asymptomatic small remnants of EC visible on postoperative imaging can therefore—at least in our experience—be monitored safely, while reoperation is reserved only for symptomatic or growing lesions. This strategy may reduce surgical risks in children, who are particularly vulnerable to morbidity from more extensive approaches [12].

Instead of pursuing GTR at all costs and knowing that recurrences can occur after all grades of resection [35], our experience favors a strategy that weighs surgical ambitions against patient safety. Lifelong follow-up serves as the safeguard, ensuring that recurrence is recognized early and reoperation is considered only when clinically justified.

Risk of complications

The complications observed in our cohort consisted only of transient cranial nerve deficits (diplopia and hearing impairment) in two cases, which completely resolved in both of them. This is in line with overall complication rates reported in the literature, where 10–30% of pediatric patients develop new neurological deficits, some of which may remain permanent [20]. However, the spectrum of complications reported after surgical resection of EC in CPA, based on the data from studies listed in Table 2, seems broader and more severe, including those leading to fatal outcomes [2, 7]. Recurrence

Recurrence occurred in two of our six patients (33%), including one case after supposedly achieving GTR, emphasizing the necessity of long-term surveillance. Reported recurrence rates range from 10 to 17% [2, 7], occurring after as long as 3, 5, 10, and 20 years, respectively [2, 7]. Importantly, recurrence has been shown to correlate with the extent of resection: the 5- and 10-year cumulative recurrence-free rates after initial resection were reported to be 64% and 32%, respectively, and better after gross total than subtotal resection (61% vs. 35%, p = 0.130) [2, 7].

However, even after apparently complete removal, recurrence may still occur due to microscopic residual epithelial remnants in anatomically concealed regions such as Meckel’s cave or the prepontine cistern. This is consistent with our findings and has also been highlighted in recent literature [2, 7]. Due to the slow-growing nature of EC and the potential for delayed recurrence, the true recurrence rate is likely underestimated in studies with limited follow-up duration. This highlights the importance of long-term MRI surveillance, particularly in pediatric patients. Moreover, malignant transformation of ECs has been occasionally reported in adult patients in recurrent or residual lesions and even many years after initial surgery [36–41]. However, no malignant transformation was documented among the pediatric cases included in this review.

Limitations

The main limitations of this study are its single-center, retrospective design as well as the small sample size, the latter reflecting the rarity of EC in CPA encountered in pediatric patients, even at a large tertiary-care center like ours. These limitations could be overcome in a future multicenter observational study, aiming at collecting data from a significantly larger cohort of patients. Furthermore, surgical notes and imaging could be subject to some interpretation bias.

Conclusions

EC of the CPA in children can be safely and effectively removed, yielding favorable outcomes with low morbidity. Although GTR should be attempted, residual tissue may persist in hidden anatomical recesses and predispose to recurrence after subtotal removal. Endoscopic assistance may enhance the completeness of resection. Small asymptomatic remnants can be monitored. Long-term MRI follow-up is recommended.
