Efficacy of Upadacitinib in Patients with Atopic Dermatitis of the Head and Neck Region
Authors: Kilian Eyerich, Pedro Mendes-Bastos, Gregor Holzer, Vipul Jain, Norito Katoh, Paula C Luna, Pablo Coto Segura, Michael Lane, Ayman Grada, Alvaro Moreira, Christopher G Bunick
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHead and neck atopic dermatitis (AD) is difficult to treat. We evaluated the efficacy of upadacitinib versus placebo in patients with head and neck AD in a post hoc analysis of Measure Up 1 (NCT03569293) and Measure Up 2 (NCT03607422).MethodsPatients with head and neck AD (overall and stratified by baseline head and neck Eczema Area and Severity Index [EASI]) received upadacitinib 15 mg, upadacitinib 30 mg, or placebo. Efficacy assessments included achievement of stringent targets (head and neck EASI ≤ 0.3 [almost clear], EASI 90 [near-complete clearance], EASI 100 [complete clearance], and patient-reported outcomes [PROs]) through week 16.ResultsAt week 16, more patients achieved head and neck EASI ≤ 0.3, EASI 90, and EASI 100 with upadacitinib 15 mg (46.8%, 41.9%, 29.1%) and upadacitinib 30 mg (58.0%, 56.5%, 42.4%) versus placebo (13.5%, 11.5%, 9.0%), respectively. Similar patterns, including PRO outcomes, were observed across head and neck AD severity subgroups. Total EASI head and neck median percentage reduction from baseline at week 16 was 87.8% for upadacitinib 15 mg and 96.3% for upadacitinib 30 mg.ConclusionsUpadacitinib substantially improved skin and PRO outcomes in patients with head and neck AD, including those with moderate or severe baseline involvement.Trial RegistrationClinicalTrials.gov Measure Up 1 (NCT03569293) and Measure Up 2 (NCT03607422).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13555-026-01793-z.Keywords: Eczema, Itch, JAK inhibitor, Minimal disease activity, Quality of life, Severity

Key Summary Points

Why carry out the study? Atopic dermatitis (AD) on the head and neck region is associated with increased disease burden and greater negative impact on quality of life than other AD-affected regions.This analysis evaluates the efficacy of upadacitinib, a once-daily oral Janus kinase inhibitor, compared with placebo in patients with moderate-to-severe AD with involvement of the head and neck region in the Measure Up 1 and Measure Up 2 studies, overall and stratified by baseline head and neck AD severity. What was learned from the study? At week 16, median percentage reduction from baseline in total Eczema Area and Severity Index head and neck was 87.8% with upadacitinib 15 mg and 96.3% with upadacitinib 30 mg in patients with moderate-to-severe AD with head and neck involvement.Upadacitinib demonstrated rapid improvement in patients with moderate-to-severe AD with head and neck involvement, with significant skin clearance and symptom relief observed as early as week 1 of treatment and sustained through week 16.Upadacitinib showed optimal efficacy in patients with moderate-to-severe AD with head and neck involvement, including minimal disease activity, not only in moderate cases but also in severe and very severe head and neck disease, addressing a population often refractory to other treatments.

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a chronic, relapsing, inflammatory, immune-mediated skin condition characterized by eczematous lesions, intense pruritus, and pain [1–3]. Individuals with moderate-to-severe AD experience considerable symptom burden and reduced quality of life (QoL) [4]. Head and neck AD is associated with increased disease burden and greater negative impact on QoL than other AD-affected regions, and may be embarrassing and stigmatizing [2, 5–7]. Treatment of head and neck AD can be challenging owing to continuous exposure to allergens and irritants [7, 8].

Long-term use of topical corticosteroids for AD on the face is discouraged owing to the risk of adverse effects [1]. Although topical calcineurin inhibitors—such as tacrolimus and pimecrolimus—are recommended for sensitive areas, local reactions causing discomfort may limit use [1, 6]. Biologics have been evaluated for head and neck AD; however, onset of beneficial effects may be slow. For example, head and neck AD persisted after 2 years of dupilumab treatment despite improvements in other regions [9]. Cytokine imbalance-related phenomena have been reported with biologics, including phenotypic switching [10, 11]. Additionally, dupilumab may cause de novo or worsening head and neck dermatitis or erythema in up to 10% of patients [11–13], potentially explained by dupilumab-associated dampening of type 2 inflammation and concomitant hyperactivation of interleukin (IL)−22-associated responses, possibly involving hypersensitivity to Malassezia in some cases [11]. With limited therapeutic options currently available, effective treatments for head and neck AD are needed.

Upadacitinib is a selective oral Janus kinase (JAK) 1 small-molecule inhibitor indicated for the treatment of patients with moderate-to-severe AD [14–16]. In a post hoc analysis of the Heads Up study, more patients achieved head and neck AD skin clearance as early as week 1 with upadacitinib than with dupilumab (nominal P ≤ 0.001) [17]. The proportion of patients achieving a ≥ 75% (marked improvement), ≥ 90% (near-complete clearance), or 100% (complete clearance) improvement from baseline in Eczema Area and Severity Index (EASI 75/90/100) in the head and neck regions was greater with upadacitinib than dupilumab through week 24 [17]. This analysis evaluates the efficacy of upadacitinib compared with placebo in patients with moderate-to-severe AD with involvement of the head and neck region in the Measure Up 1 and Measure Up 2 studies, overall and stratified by baseline head and neck AD severity.

Methods

Study Design and Patients

The Measure Up 1 (NCT03569293) and Measure Up 2 (NCT03607422) clinical trials evaluated upadacitinib in patients with moderate-to-severe AD; detailed methodology has been previously reported [16]. Both studies comprised a 35-day screening period, a 16-week double-blind treatment period, a blinded extension period (up to week 524), and a 30-day follow-up visit. Patients were randomly assigned (1:1:1) to receive once-daily orally administered upadacitinib 15 mg, upadacitinib 30 mg, or placebo for 16 weeks.

Eligible patients were adults (aged 18–75 years) or adolescents (aged 12–17 years; body weight ≥ 40 kg) with moderate-to-severe AD (≥ 10% of body surface area [BSA] affected by AD), an EASI ≥ 16, a validated Investigator Global Assessment for AD (vIGA-AD) [18] score ≥ 3, and a Worst Pruritus Numerical Rating Scale (WP-NRS) score ≥ 4. Patients were candidates for systemic therapy (i.e., using systemic treatment for AD, having a history of inadequate response to topical AD therapies, or for whom topical treatments were otherwise medically inadvisable) and had experienced AD symptoms for ≥ 3 years before baseline. Patients were ineligible if they had prior exposure to any JAK inhibitor or dupilumab (no other biologics were approved for use in AD during study enrollment for Measure Up 1 [August 2018–December 2019] and Measure Up 2 [July 2018–January 2020] [16]).

The protocols, informed consent forms, and recruitment materials were approved by Advarra (Columbia, MD, USA) and the independent ethics committee or institutional review board at each site before patient enrollment (Supplementary Table S1). Studies were conducted in accordance with International Conference for Harmonisation guidelines, applicable regulations, and the Declaration of Helsinki. Patients (or parents/legal guardians of adolescent patients) provided written informed consent before screening.

Assessments

In this post hoc analysis, the overall cohort of patients had head and neck EASI > 0 at baseline (based on the total EASI score weighted by the BSA of the head and neck region [10%]). Patients were also classified by EASI head and neck severity strata at baseline (adjusted by head and neck BSA) [19]. Baseline head and neck AD severity was classified as clear/almost clear (0–0.1), mild (0.2–0.7), moderate (0.8–2.1), severe (2.2–5.0), or very severe (5.1–7.2) [19].

Efficacy assessments during the 16-week double-blind treatment period were evaluated by treatment and visit in the overall cohort and by baseline head and neck AD severity in the moderate, severe, and very severe subgroups. Efficacy assessments included the proportion of patients achieving head and neck EASI score ≤ 0.7 (mild) or ≤ 0.3 (almost clear). Additional efficacy assessments in the overall cohort included improvement from baseline in head and neck EASI 90 and EASI 100, and percentage change from baseline in the head and neck EASI. Efficacy assessments by head and neck AD severity subgroup also included improvement from baseline in head and neck EASI 75, EASI 90, and EASI 100; WP-NRS improvement of ≥ 4 points; achievement of minimal disease activity WP-NRS 0 or 1; reduction from baseline in the Dermatology Quality of Life Index (DLQI) of ≥ 4 points; achievement of a DLQI score of 0 or 1; and achievement of minimal disease activity (MDA), defined as co-achievement of total EASI 90 and WP-NRS 0 or 1.

EASI thresholds were based on the Aiming High in Eczema/Atopic Dermatitis consensus recommendations, which specify moderate treatment targets of EASI 75 and EASI ≤ 7 and optimal targets of EASI 90 and EASI ≤ 3 [20], adjusted for head and neck BSA percentage.

Analysis

Pooled integrated data from the 16-week double-blind treatment period are reported, including the proportion of patients achieving endpoints and the treatment differences versus placebo, based on the intent-to-treat population (all patients randomized at baseline). The 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the proportions of responders are based on Wald limits without continuity correction. The 95% CIs for differences in proportions comparing active treatments with placebo are based on Mantel–Haenszel limits adjusted for baseline vIGA-AD and age. Nominal P values were generated for comparisons of active treatments with placebo. The P value for each comparison is based on a Mantel–Haenszel test of common rate difference equal to 0, adjusted for baseline vIGA-AD and age. Results are based on nonresponder imputation.

Results

Patients

Patient disposition has been previously reported [16]. Overall, 1682 patients with head and neck AD were included (clear/almost clear, n = 94; mild, n = 143; moderate, n = 510; severe, n = 776; very severe, n = 159). Efficacy data by severity subgroup are reported for the moderate, severe, and very severe subgroups.

Baseline patient demographics were generally balanced across head and neck AD severity subgroups (Table 1). There was a higher proportion of male patients with very severe head and neck AD (66.0%) than in the moderate or severe subgroups (53.3% and 56.4%, respectively). Patients with very severe head and neck AD had a lower mean body mass index (24.5 kg/m2) than those in the moderate (26.3 kg/m2) and severe (25.5 kg/m2) subgroups.

There were marked differences in disease characteristics at baseline, with a greater extent and disease severity in patients with more severe head and neck AD (Table 1). Patients with very severe head and neck AD had longer mean duration of AD symptoms (24.9 years) and greater BSA (64.9%) involvement than patients in the moderate disease subgroup (22.1 years and 40.8%, respectively). The percentage of patients with a baseline vIGA-AD of 4 (severe) increased across head and neck AD severity subgroups (moderate, 37.6%; severe, 56.4%; very severe, 84.9%). Mean baseline total EASI and EASI head and neck were higher in patients with very severe head and neck AD (43.2 and 6.0, respectively) than in those with moderate disease (25.4 and 1.5, respectively). Baseline EASI assessment also showed that 83.9% of patients with moderate disease had 10–49% of the head and neck area affected, 70.6% of patients with severe disease had 50–89% area affected, and 66.0% of patients with very severe disease had 90–100% area affected (Supplementary Table S2). The proportion of patients with severe baseline edema, erythema, excoriation, and lichenification increased across head and neck disease severity subgroups (Supplementary Table S2). At baseline, QoL (assessed by DLQI) was lower in patients with more severe head and neck AD, and itch (assessed by weekly WP-NRS) was similar across groups, regardless of baseline head and neck AD severity. Approximately half of patients reported prior use of systemic therapies, with higher use observed among those in the more severe head and neck AD subgroups (Table 1).

Efficacy

Skin Clearance

Overall Head and Neck AD Cohort

In the overall cohort, patients receiving upadacitinib 15 mg or upadacitinib 30 mg achieved greater improvements in head and neck skin clearance at weeks 1, 2, and 16 than those who received placebo (Table 2 and Supplementary Table S3). At week 16, a higher proportion of patients achieved EASI ≤ 0.7 (mild), EASI ≤ 0.3 (almost clear), EASI 90 (near-complete clearance), and EASI 100 (complete clearance) in the head and neck region with upadacitinib 15 mg (57.7%, 46.8%, 41.9%, and 29.1%, respectively) and upadacitinib 30 mg (66.7%, 58.0%, 56.5%, and 42.4%, respectively) than with placebo (20.0%, 13.5%, 11.5%, and 9.0%, respectively). The adjusted median percentage change from baseline in EASI head and neck score at week 16 in the overall cohort was reduced by 87.8% and 96.3% in the upadacitinib 15 mg and upadacitinib 30 mg groups, respectively, compared with 57.6% in the placebo group (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table S4).

Across all baseline head and neck AD severity groups, improvements in QoL and itch were achieved at greater rates with upadacitinib 15 mg or upadacitinib 30 mg than with placebo at week 16 (Table 3). Discussion

In this post hoc pooled analysis of Measure Up 1 and Measure Up 2, patients with head and neck AD at baseline receiving upadacitinib 15 mg or upadacitinib 30 mg experienced rapid, substantial improvements in head and neck skin clearance compared with those receiving placebo, with improvements observed by week 1 and sustained through week 16. Furthermore, substantial improvements were observed in skin clearance, overall AD severity, QoL, and itch across the subgroups of moderate, severe, and very severe head and neck AD among patients receiving upadacitinib 15 mg or upadacitinib 30 mg compared with those receiving placebo. The improvements across head and neck severity subgroups with upadacitinib 15 mg or upadacitinib 30 mg were rapid, starting at week 1, and sustained through week 16.

Upadacitinib 15 mg and upadacitinib 30 mg were efficacious across head and neck severity subgroups, even when evaluated against the optimal treatment targets (EASI ≤ 0.3 and EASI 90 [20]) in the head and neck region. Additionally, compared with placebo, more patients receiving upadacitinib 15 mg or upadacitinib 30 mg achieved MDA at week 16, even in the severe and very severe head and neck AD subgroups. Achievement of rigorous measures of clinical response, including EASI 90 and MDA, is associated with improved AD symptoms and QoL [21, 22], which is consistent with our findings showing increased response rates in EASI 90 and MDA, accompanied by higher response rates in DLQI (QoL) and WP-NRS (itch) with upadacitinib 15 mg and upadacitinib 30 mg, regardless of baseline head and neck AD severity. Given the burden of head and neck AD, rapid achievement of stringent targets, such as EASI 90 and MDA, is an important treatment goal [22].

Our findings reflect previous upadacitinib studies; for example, a network meta-analysis of up to 16 weeks’ treatment with six advanced therapies across 13 clinical trials of patients with moderate-to-severe AD found that upadacitinib was the most efficacious advanced systemic therapy for skin clearance and itch relief [23]. Specifically, for head and neck AD, more patients receiving upadacitinib than dupilumab achieved EASI 90 and EASI 100, with treatment differences evident from week 1 in the Heads Up study [17]. Real-world studies support these findings; for example, in an Italian compassionate use program, 31 patients with head and neck AD who did not respond to dupilumab treatment and subsequently received 52 weeks of treatment with upadacitinib experienced significant reductions in EASI at 52 weeks [7]. Additionally, in a retrospective study at specialist centers in Italy, 56% of patients with moderate-to-severe AD receiving upadacitinib achieved EASI 90 in the head and neck at week 16, increasing to 68% at week 104 [24]. Moreover, several case studies have reported improvements in head and neck AD among patients who switched to upadacitinib after inefficacy or worsening of head and neck AD with dupilumab [25, 26].

Upadacitinib is a selective JAK1 inhibitor, and its rapid efficacy may result from its broad modulation of relevant immune pathways implicated in acute and chronic AD (Th2, Th17, and Th1) and associated proinflammatory cytokines (IL-4, IL-13, IL-22, IL-31, interferon-γ, and thymic stromal lymphopoietin), which contrasts with biologics (such as dupilumab) that target narrower pathways and increase susceptibility to issues such as phenotypic switching [10, 14, 15, 27, 28]. Upadacitinib is associated with consistent and significant reduction in serum IL-22 levels [29, 30], a cytokine that is upregulated in patients with AD, particularly those with head and neck involvement [11, 31]. Reduced serum IL-22 following upadacitinib treatment has been reported in clinical trials versus placebo or dupilumab [29, 30] and in a real-world study that included patients with previous inadequate response to dupilumab [32]. Additionally, biomarker analyses of IL-22 have shown significant correlations with baseline AD severity and clinical improvements in skin clearance and itch in upadacitinib-treated patients [29]. Taken together, these findings suggest that reduced IL-22 levels may help drive the significant upadacitinib-associated clinical improvements seen in head and neck AD.

The increased disease burden that has been previously associated with head and neck AD [2] was also observed in this study, with higher mean EASI scores at baseline across disease severity subgroups; however, weekly average itch at baseline, as measured by WP-NRS, was similar across the severity subgroups. This apparent lack of variation in itch severity at baseline is consistent with the finding that there may only be a moderate association between itch severity and EASI scores; for example, approximately 30–52% of patients with clear/almost clear skin had moderate-to-very-severe itch in the previous week in a prospective observational study of 592 adults with AD [33]. Indeed, itch is a complex, heterogeneous symptom that may be an independent predictor of AD outcomes [34], and itch-dominant AD may be a specific, under-recognized subtype of AD [33]. In this study among patients receiving upadacitinib 15 mg or upadacitinib 30 mg, the proportions achieving WP-NRS 0/1 and a WP-NRS improvement of ≥ 4 at week 16 were similar across head and neck AD severity subgroups, suggesting that improvement in itch was consistent in patients with head and neck AD, regardless of the baseline severity of skin lesions.

There are some limitations to this study. EASI is a whole-body composite assessment and was not originally validated to measure outcomes or AD severity by body regions. Additionally, all patients with EASI head and neck score > 0 at baseline were included in the analysis; the inclusion of individuals with minimal baseline AD involvement in the head and neck region could have potentially influenced EASI 90 and EASI 100 response rates. Furthermore, this study was exploratory and descriptive and was not intended to be a confirmatory analysis. As this exploratory analysis was not prespecified, nominal P values are reported to mitigate the risk of drawing conclusions from multiple comparisons without predefined hypotheses or subgroup sample sizes. Despite these limitations, these findings highlight the potential therapeutic benefits of upadacitinib 15 mg and upadacitinib 30 mg for patients with moderate, severe, or very severe head and neck AD.

Future prospective studies to improve treatment options for people with head and neck AD should include head-to-head comparisons of systemic therapies and evaluate treatment responses stratified by head and neck AD phenotype, including classical AD, Malassezia-associated dermatitis, and allergic contact dermatitis.

Conclusions

Treatment with upadacitinib rapidly led to substantial improvements in head and neck outcomes, including skin clearance, itch, and QoL in patients with AD, including those with moderate and severe involvement. These findings support the use of upadacitinib to help manage head and neck AD. Understanding upadacitinib treatment outcomes for patients with head and neck AD will facilitate more informed, personalized decision-making for AD management.
