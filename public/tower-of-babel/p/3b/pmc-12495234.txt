Electroacupuncture at Huatuo-Jiaji acupoints combined with conductive hydrogel synergistically improves bladder and motor functions after spinal cord injury
Authors: Qiang Zhang, Feng Wei, Lulu Zhang, Can Liang, Yan Zhuang, Ning Jiang, Wenjie Weng, Xusheng Qiu, Huiru Wang, Yixin Chen, Jianwu Dai, He Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpinal cord injury (SCI) compromises the electrophysiological properties of tissues and disrupts the electrical transmission pathways, ultimately resulting in motor dysfunction and neurogenic bladder impairment. Therefore, restoration of normal electrophysiological functions in injured spinal cord tissues is crucial for SCI rehabilitation. Electrical stimulation (ES) exhibits therapeutic potential for neurological disorders via neuronal modulation, showing enhanced efficacy in combination with traditional Chinese medicine practices, such as targeted acupuncture. Specifically, acupuncture at Huatuo-Jiaji acupoints, located bilaterally along the vertebral column, exerts neurological regulatory effects. In this study, we developed a dual-modality therapeutic approach combining implantable biomimetic conductive hydrogels for internal ES with electroacupuncture at Huatuo-Jiaji acupoints for external ES to reconstruct the neural electrical microenvironment and synergistically enhance the electrophysiological recovery post-SCI. The engineered conductive hydrogels, fabricated by incorporating carbon nanotubes into photocrosslinkable gelatin matrices, showed dual functionality by facilitating endogenous electrical signal propagation and enhancing the exogenous ES efficacy. This combination therapy significantly attenuated the inflammatory responses and glial scar formation, concurrently promoting axonal regeneration and mitigating demyelination. Functional assessments revealed substantial improvements in both locomotor recovery and neurogenic bladder control in complete spinal cord transection model rats. Mechanistic investigations revealed the downregulation of the Janus kinase–signal transducer and activator of transcription signaling pathway as a key regulator of anti-inflammatory effects of the combination treatment. Overall, our innovative internal–external ES integration strategy offers a promising translational approach for SCI repair by simultaneously enhancing neural regeneration and facilitating functional restoration.Keywords: Spinal cord injury, Function recovery, Electrical stimulation, Conductive hydrogel, Jiaji acupoints

Introduction

Electrophysiological properties of spinal cord tissues and nerve cells play vital roles in mediating neural signal transmission, thereby governing motor coordination and sensory processing [1]. Following spinal cord injury (SCI), structural and functional disintegration of axonal integrity and neural circuit connectivity establishes a pathological electrical microenvironment characterized by nerve conduction blockade, sustained inflammatory cascades, and progressive glial scar formation. These pathophysiological changes lead to devastating functional deficits, including permanent motor impairment, neurogenic bladder dysfunction, and severe quality-of-life deterioration due to complications, such as detrusor–sphincter dyssynergia and recurrent urinary infections [2,3]. Consequently, restoration of neural circuit functionality, particularly reconstitution of the electrophysiological conduction capacity and neural circuit reorganization, is imperative for post-traumatic SCI repair and functional recovery.

Electrical stimulation (ES) is a promising neuromodulation strategy to restore or modulate the electrical signals post-SCI, thereby creating a favorable environment for neuronal regeneration and functional recovery. Internal and external ES are classified based on their action modes. The former utilizes electroconductive biomaterials to engineer biomimetic three-dimensional scaffolds replicating the native extracellular matrix architecture and providing topographical and electrotactic guidance for neural regeneration. These engineered constructs not only amplify endogenous bioelectrical signaling but also orchestrate cellular responses via spatiotemporally controlled activation of neurons and glia, culminating in accelerated axonal regrowth and circuit formation [4]. We previously revealed that gelatin (Gl)-based conductive hydrogels exhibit good electrical conductivity and bioactivity, promoting the recovery of electrophysiological activity after SCI [5].

External ES improves local blood circulation and metabolic activity, regulates neurotransmitter release, and effectively suppresses inflammatory responses and glial scar formation, thereby promoting neural circuit remodeling and restoring the physiological functions. However, targeting and systematic regulation capacities of ES require improvement. Electroacupuncture (EA) stimulation, synergistic integration of millennia-old acupuncture and modern bioelectronic principles, offers anatomical precision via meridian-specific stimulation. Notably, 50 Hz EA stimulation exerts neuroprotective effects predominantly via nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2-mediated oxidative stress suppression and lysosomal–autophagic flux potentiation in SCI model rats [6,7]. Moreover, EA stimulation demonstrates multiorgan regulatory capacity, effectively ameliorating neurogenic bladder dysfunction by restoring the detrusor–sphincter coordination [[8], [9], [10]]. EA stimulation also inhibits the pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 1/Akt/hyperpolarization-activated cyclic nucleotide-gated potassium channel 4 pathway to improve neurogenic urinary retention [11]. However, the system-level pharmacodynamic network underlying its therapeutic efficacy remains ambiguous. Anchored at the intersection of the governor vessel and bladder meridian, Huatuo-Jiaji acupoints constitute a neurovascular nexus with documented electrophysiological regulatory potency. Huatuo-Jiaji points are used to treat various disorders, including neural and urinary system disorders [12]. Jiaji points show significant therapeutic potential for post-ischemic motor impairments, possibly mediated via the upregulation of γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) receptor expression, exerting robust neuroprotective effects [13].

To enhance the therapeutic accuracy and effectiveness of ES, we developed an internal and external combination ES strategy by combining conductive hydrogel implantation at the defect site and EA stimulation at the Jiaji acupoints to synergistically reconstruct the neural electrical microenvironments at the injured site, thereby promoting SCI repair. The T8 and T10 Huatuo-Jiaji acupoints were selected based on their neuroanatomical relationship to the T9 injury site and principles of traditional Chinese medicine (TCM). Neuroanatomically, the Jiaji acupoints are located adjacent to the intervertebral foramina, where spinal nerves emerge to innervate both motor and sensory pathways relevant to the T9 lesion level and EA on Jiaji points has been proved can stimulate the appropriate posterior ramus of the spinal nerve originating from the lower vertebrae [14]. In TCM theory, they connect to the governor vessel and bladder meridian, making them ideal for simultaneous improvement of locomotor and bladder function after thoracic SCI [15]. The alternating 60 Hz and 2 Hz electroacupuncture parameters were chosen to leverage the distinct physiological effects of different stimulation frequencies. Low-frequency stimulation preferentially inhibits microglial activation, reduces pro-inflammatory cytokine release, and suppresses TLR4, HMGB1, and NF-κB signaling pathways in spinal cord [16,17]. In contrast, high-frequency stimulation can reduce the risk of urinary tract infection, decrease residual urine volume in neurogenic bladder dysfunction, and promote balanced bladder function [18]. By alternating between frequencies, these benefits are combined while avoiding the limitations associated with single-frequency stimulation. It's also been demonstrated that alternating low- and high-frequency stimulation alleviates neuropathic pain by inhibiting microglial activation and blocking the brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF)-tyrosine receptor kinase B (TrkB) signaling pathway [19]. The conductive hydrogels, prepared by incorporating carbon nanotubes (CNTs) into the photocrosslinking Gl hydrogel, enhanced the effects of EA stimulation by providing support and facilitating the transmission of local electrical signals due to addition of CNTs to the hydrogel. Moreover, EA stimulation synergistically promoted neural regeneration by enhancing the neural activity, connectivity, and functional neural networks. Combined EA and conductive hydrogel application induced the neuronal differentiation of neural stem cells (NSCs), axonal growth, and myelin regeneration, inhibited NSC differentiation into astrocytes, and reduced glial scarring. Biochemical and physical ES combination strategy effectively promoted the motor and bladder function recovery. RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis to assess the underlying mechanisms. Notably, EA stimulation at the Jiaji acupoints combined with conductive hydrogel application upregulated the neurotransmission- and synaptic plasticity-related signaling pathways and downregulated the inflammatory response-related signaling pathways, with both the neural protective and regenerative effects of this combination strategy contributing to SCI repair (Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

Conductive hydrogel synthesis and characterization

The Gl-C hydrogels were prepared as previously described [5]. Briefly, 20, 40, and 80 μg/mL polyethylenimine-modified CNTs were mixed with 6 % w/v photocrosslinkable Gl precursor solution (containing photo-initiator) and cured using a 395-nm light-emitting diode lamp for 2 min to form the Gl-C20, Gl-C40, and Gl-C80 hydrogels.

Morphologies, conductivities, and mechanical properties of the Gl-C hydrogels were examined using a scanning electron microscope (Quanta 400 FEG; FEI Company, Oregon, USA), electrochemical workstation (CHI660D; Chenhua Instrument Co., LTD, Shanghai, China), and rheometer (AR2000 MTS; TA Instruments, New Castle, Delaware, USA), respectively. Their biodegradation properties were evaluated as previously described [5].

NSC proliferation and differentiation assays

NSCs were extracted and cultured as previously described [20]. Briefly, NSCs were seeded in the tissue culture plates (control group), Gl, and Gl-C hydrogel surfaces, and cell viability was assessed using the cell counting kit-8 (Bimake, Houston, TX, USA) after culture in a proliferation medium [21]. NSC differentiation was assessed via immunofluorescence staining with anti-β-tubulin III (Tuj-1) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) after culture in a differentiation medium for 7 days [22]. Images were captured from three random fields of view using a confocal microscope (FV3000; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed to analyze the expression levels of Wnt9a, Wnt7a, and Ngn2 (neuronal differentiation markers). All information of primers and antibodies are listed in Tables S1 and S2, respectively.

Surgical procedure and combined Gl-C hydrogel implantation and EA stimulation at Jiaji acupoints

140 female Sprague–Dawley rats (8–10 weeks old; 200–220 g) were purchased from Shanghai Slaughter Laboratory Animal Co. (China). All animal experiments adhered to the National Institutes of Health Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Suzhou Institute of Nano-Tech and Nano-Bionics, Chinese Academy of Sciences (approval number: SINANO/EC/2023-YFC2412502). Complete transection SCI model rats were established as previously described [23,24]. Spinal cord tissues (4 mm) were completely removed at the 9th thoracic vertebrae, and 25 μL Gl and Gl-C solutions were injected into the damaged area and photocured for 120 s. For the subacute phase study, 50 rats were randomly divided into five groups: SCI, Gl, Gl-C20 (with 20 μg/mL CNTs in the scaffold), Gl-C40 (with 40 μg/mL CNTs) and Gl-C80 (with 80 μg/mL CNTs) groups. For chronic phase investigation, 75 rats were randomly divided into five groups: SCI, Gl, Gl-C40, Gl + EA, and Gl-C40 + EA groups. The animals were administered antibiotics daily for the first three days postoperatively and artificially urinated daily post-surgery.

Rats in the Gl + EA and Gl-C + EA groups were stimulated via EA at the Jiaji acupoints for 30 min per week from day 7 post-surgery. Two sets of acupuncture needles (0.25 mm × 40 mm; Research Taihe; Changchun Aikang Medical Equipment Co., Ltd., China) were vertically inserted into the Jiaji acupoints located adjacent to the 8th and 10th thoracic vertebrae (Fig. 1). The needles were advanced to a depth of 4–5 mm until the tips were in contact with the vertebral laminae. A low-frequency electronic pulse therapy instrument (model G6805-2A; Shanghai Huayi Medical Instrument Co., Ltd, China) was connected to the needles for EA stimulation. The parameters were set to alternate between 60 and 2 Hz frequencies at 1.05 and 2.85 s intervals, respectively, with the intensity maintained below 0.5 mA to induce mild twitching of the dorsal muscles in SCI rats.

Electric field detection in SCI rats

Neurostim-8 probe (Nanjing Changxiang Medical Technology Co., Ltd., China) was used to record the electrophysiological signals in the injured and treated spinal cord, and bladder tissues during applying EA at the Jiaji acupoints. Recordings from each SCI rat were repeated at least three times, and the waveform morphology consistency was verified in trials.

Histological analyses

Ten day and eight weeks after treatment, spinal cord samples were harvested as previously described [25]. The spinal cord sections were stained with primary and secondary antibodies listed in Table S2, detected using the Olympus confocal microscope (FV3000), and semi-quantified using the ImageJ software.

Evaluation of functional restoration

Locomotor function recovery was evaluated using the Basso–Beattie–Bresnahan (BBB) scale [26], inclined plate test [26], and footprinting assay [27]. Inflammation, hyperplasia, and fibrosis were assessed to determine bladder function recovery. Pathological changes in bladder specimens were classified into three grades: Grades I (mild inflammation or fibrosis in the bladder specimen visual field), II (moderate or significant inflammation or fibrosis in the bladder specimen visual field), and III (severe inflammation with dense field-wide inflammatory responses or fibrosis in the bladder specimen visual field).

Transcriptome sequencing analysis

After eight weeks of treatment, spinal cord tissue samples were collected from the injured site, spanning 8 mm rostral and caudal to the lesion site. RNA-seq was performed to elucidate the potential molecular mechanisms. Spinal cord samples of the Gl-C and Gl-C + EA groups were preserved using the TRIzol Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Transcriptome sequencing analysis was performed by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). RNA was extracted using standard methods, and quality control was performed using the NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). RNA integrity was assessed with the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Subsequently, libraries were constructed using the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit, and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, generating 150 bp paired-end reads. Gene expression levels were normalized to fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) using Cufflinks, while raw read counts were quantified with HTSeq-count. Differential expression analysis was conducted using the DESeq2 package, applying a significance threshold of p < 0.05 to identify statistically significant genes. To visualize expression profiles, hierarchical clustering was applied to the differentially expressed genes. Furthermore, functional enrichment analyses, including Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways, were carried out in R using a hypergeometric distribution-based approach.

Statistical analyses

Data are represented as the mean ± standard deviation. All results were statistically analyzed via independent t-tests for two-group comparisons and one-way analysis of variance for multi-group comparisons. Normality and log-normality tests were performed to assess the homogeneity of variance before performing the analysis of variance, followed by Tukey's post-hoc test. Statistical significance was indicated by *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001.

Results

Conductive hydrogels regulate the NSC fate

Physicochemical properties of the conductive Gl-based hydrogels modified with varying CNT concentrations were characterized. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis revealed that the Gl-C hydrogels exhibited an interpenetrating porous structure (pore size: 40–120 μm; Fig. 2A; Fig. S1A–D) that mimicked the topological features of the natural extracellular matrix and provided space for nutrient exchange and cellular proliferation. Integration of Gl bioactivity and CNT conductivity enabled the hydrogel scaffold to achieve synergistic electromechanical regulation. Specifically, electrical conductivity increased with increasing CNT concentration (Gl: 0.22 ± 0.02 S/m vs. Gl-C80: 0.36 ± 0.04 S/m; Fig. 2B), reaching values within the optimal range for neural tissue engineering, whereas compressive moduli (0.60–0.99 kPa) closely matched the mechanical properties of the native spinal cord tissues (0.1–3.0 kPa; Fig. 2C). Degradation studies confirmed enhanced stability due to CNT incorporation, as evidenced by the reduced mass loss from 60 % (Gl) to 35 % (Gl-C80) over three weeks (Fig. 2D). This improvement was possibly due to intermolecular interactions, such as hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions. Therefore, controllable degradation kinetics provide a critical temporal window to support tissue regeneration.

Biocompatibility assays confirmed the non-cytotoxicity of the Gl-C20/40/80 scaffolds to NSCs (Fig. S1E). After the seven-day differentiation culture of NSCs, immunostaining revealed that the Gl-C40 hydrogel supported the highest proportion of Tuj-1+ neurons (45.2 ± 4.9 %), which was significantly higher than those in the Gl-C20 (30.4 ± 7.3 %), Gl-C80 (17.7 ± 1.9 %), Gl (28.9 ± 5.8 %), and control (20.0 ± 5.3 %) groups (Fig. 3A–C). Gl-C40 group exhibited less astrocyte differentiation (GFAP+ cells: 13.5 ± 4.3 %), in contrast to the elevated gliogenesis observed in the Gl-C80 group (33.5 ± 9.7 %), and slight but not significant reduction in the Gl-C20 group (11.3 ± 3.3 %). qPCR analysis further revealed that the gene expression levels of Ngn2, Wnt7a, and Wnt9a, associated with neuronal differentiation, were upregulated in the NSCs cultured on the Gl-C20 and Gl-C40 substrates (Fig. 3D–F). Collectively, in vitro tests suggest that the Gl-C20 and Gl-C40 hydrogels effectively promote the neuronal differentiation of NSCs while suppressing their differentiation into astrocytes.

Next, we investigated the effects of Gl-C hydrogels on endogenous NSC migration and neurogenesis using complete transection SCI rat model. Nestin, an NSC marker, was used to detect the SCI-activated endogenous NSCs, which play a critical role in replacing the lost neural cells via proliferation and differentiation. Both immunofluorescence imaging and quantitative analysis revealed that the fluorescence intensity of nestin-positive cells in the lesion area was significantly higher in the hydrogel transplantation groups than in the SCI group ten days post-surgery (Fig. 3G and H). To assess the effects of the hydrogel scaffolds on endogenous NSC differentiation, newborn neurons were visualized via Tuj-1 staining. Fluorescence intensity in the lesion area of Tuj-1+ cells was significantly higher in the conductive hydrogel scaffold-treated groups than in the SCI group (Fig. 3I). Notably, fluorescence intensity of Tuj-1+ cells in the Gl-C40 hydrogel transplantation group is the highest among all the Gl-C groups, indicating enhancement of endogenous neurogenesis (Fig. 3J). This biomimetic dual-signal (electrical/mechanical) stimulation efficiently regulated the migration and differentiation of NSCs into neurons. Considering their superior abilities to promote endogenous NSC migration and neurogenesis, and biocompatibility, Gl-C40 hydrogels were selected for further SCI treatment studies.

EA stimulation enhances the conductive hydrogel-mediated electrical conduction at the spinal cord and bladder

Electrophysiological assessments were conducted to evaluate the spinal cord and bladder voltages by recording the electrical signals at both sites during EA treatment seven days post-surgery (Fig. 4A). As shown in Fig. S2, waveforms in the EA-treated groups reflected the neural activity evoked by therapeutic stimulation. The spinal cord-evoked potential amplitude was significantly higher in the Gl-C40 + EA group (10.15 ± 0.26 mV) than in the Gl + EA (5.60 ± 0.35 mV; p < 0.0001) and SCI + EA (8.33 ± 0.15 mV; p < 0.001) groups (Fig. 4B). This phenomenon was possibly because of the insufficient conductivity of Gl, which attenuated electrical signal propagation in the injured area, combined with the interfacial resistance between Gl and host tissues, further compromising the signal intensity recorded by the electrodes [28]. Notably, bladder-evoked potential amplitude was also markedly elevated in the Gl-C40 + EA group (8.42 ± 0.11 mV) than in the Gl + EA (1.18 ± 0.03 mV; p < 0.0001) and SCI + EA (6.14 ± 0.03 mV; p < 0.0001) groups (Fig. 4C). These findings suggest that the conductive hydrogels enhance local electrical conductivity at the SCI site and improve distal bladder innervation mediated by the spinal cord nerves. This electrophysiological evidence provides a foundation for further investigation of the combined effects of EA at the Jiaji acupoints and conductive hydrogel on the spinal cord and bladder functional recovery in animal models.

EA stimulation improves conductive hydrogel-mediated neural regeneration

After eight weeks of treatment with the conductive hydrogels and EA stimulation, major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys, were harvested from the rats, and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed to assess inflammation. As shown in Fig. S3–S6, neither the conductive hydrogel nor EA stimulation significantly altered the inflammation levels in the heart, liver, spleen, and lungs, confirming the biocompatibility of the combined treatment. Interestingly, both treatments significantly reduced kidney inflammation (Fig. S7) by possibly mitigating complications, such as urinary tract infections associated with neurogenic bladder dysfunction.

Conductive hydrogels were implanted into the spinal cords with complete defects, and spinal cord regeneration was evaluated eight weeks post-surgery. In the SCI, Gl, and Gl + EA groups, spinal cord defects remained clearly visible with distinct boundaries, indicating limited regeneration. In contrast, Gl-C40 group showed no obvious defects and smooth surface morphology at the injury site. Gl-C40 + EA group exhibited more extensive tissue regeneration in the defect area than the scaffold-only group with blurred boundary lines, suggesting improved tissue integration and repair (Fig. 5A).

Neurofilament (NF) staining was performed to histologically evaluate axonal regeneration. As shown in Fig. 5B and C, highest NF-positive staining was observed in the Gl-C40 + EA group. The conductive hydrogel significantly enhanced the effect of EA stimulation on axonal growth, as evidenced by the comparison of the Gl + EA and Gl-C40 + EA groups. Although fluorescence intensity revealed that the average NF-positive signal in the Gl-C40 group was slightly lower than that in the Gl group in Fig. 5C, the difference was not statistically significant. As demyelination is a critical pathological feature of SCI that significantly affects functional recovery, we assessed the demyelination levels in SCI rats treated with combined EA stimulation and conductive hydrogel implantation via Luxol fast blue (LFB) staining (Fig. 5D and E). In the SCI and Gl groups, only a small LFB-positive area was observed, whereas the Gl-C40 and Gl + EA groups exhibited larger LFB-positive regions, indicating that the Gl-C40 hydrogel reduced myelin degradation. Compared to the Gl-C40 group, Gl-C40 + EA group showed extensive LFB staining throughout the lesion area, confirming that EA stimulation synergistically suppressed demyelination.

Combined EA stimulation and Gl-C40 hydrogel application suppresses inflammation and scar formation

Inflammation typically induces secondary damage and impedes neural recovery following SCI. Histological analysis revealed that inflammatory cell infiltration, tissue edema, and fibrosis at the center of the spinal cord lesion were most severe in the SCI group but showed progressive attenuation in the Gl, Gl-C40, and Gl + EA groups, with no detectable pathological features observed in the Gl-C40 + EA group (Fig. 6A). Quantitative assessments further confirmed that the Gl-C40 + EA group exhibited the most significant suppression of inflammation, edema, and fibrosis (Fig. S8), suggesting that combinatorial therapy creates a favorable microenvironment for neural regeneration.

Formation of a chronic glial scar, a physical barrier to axonal regrowth [29], was evaluated via CS-56 immunostaining of chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans secreted by reactive astrocytes after SCI. SCI group exhibited extensive CS-56+ regions, whereas only a few CS-56+ regions were observed in the Gl and Gl-C40 groups. Notably, both EA-treated groups (Gl + EA and Gl-C40 + EA) exhibited markedly weaker CS-56 immunoreactivity than their non-EA counterparts (Fig. 6B and C). Quantitatively, Gl-C40 + EA group exhibited the lowest CS-56 staining intensity (180.81 ± 6.28 pixels), which was significantly lower than those in the SCI (200.04 ± 2.66), Gl (194.05 ± 4.08), Gl-C40 (197.33 ± 4.99), and Gl + EA (190.82 ± 2.73) groups (p < 0.05 for all comparisons). The average fluorescence intensity of CS-56 immunostaining in spinal cord tissue sections of Gl-C40 group was higher than that of the Gl group, but the difference was not statistically significant. This suppression of scar formation via EA stimulation and conductive hydrogel implantation possibly promotes axonal growth.

Combination treatment synergistically improves the locomotor and bladder functions

Functional motor recovery in rats was systematically evaluated via footprint analysis, BBB locomotor rating, and inclined plane testing. In the SCI group, hindlimb muscle strength was severely compromised, with a complete loss of anti-gravity function. Although rats in the Gl group demonstrated resistance movement at the distal joints, their overall muscle strength remained suboptimal. Moderate improvements were observed in the Gl-C40 and Gl + EA groups, with both the proximal and distal joints exhibiting resistance capacity. Remarkably, Gl-C40 + EA group showed intermittent weight-bearing locomotion in the hindlimbs accompanied by coordinated forelimb movement (Fig. 7A and B). BBB scores were higher in the Gl (6.3 ± 0.5), Gl-C40 (6.8 ± 0.7), Gl + EA (6.5 ± 0.5), and Gl-C40 + EA (7.7 ± 0.6) groups than in the SCI group (5.4 ± 0.5; p < 0.05) after eight weeks of treatment (Fig. 7C). To clearly show statistically differences of locomotor function outcomes among the groups at the same treatment time point, an ANOVA table is provided in the supporting information (Tabel S3). The angles of inclined plane test in the Gl (27.9 ± 3.2°), Gl-C40 (28.0 ± 3.2°), Gl + EA (26.6 ± 3.9°), and Gl-C40 + EA (32.2 ± 2.1°) groups significantly improved compared to that in the SCI group (26.4 ± 3.0°; Fig. 7D). These functional assessments collectively indicated that combined EA stimulation and conductive hydrogel implantation significantly enhanced the motor recovery in SCI model rats.

Normal micturition requires precise coordination between bladder detrusor contraction and urethral sphincter relaxation mediated by the supraspinal neural circuits [30]. Suprasacral SCI disrupts this coordination, leading to reflexic bladder dysfunction characterized by neurogenic detrusor overactivity and detrusor–sphincter dyssynergia [31]. Morphometric analysis revealed significantly lower bladder size and weight in the Gl, Gl-C40, Gl + EA, and Gl-C40 + EA groups than in the SCI group (Fig. 7E and F). Histopathological evaluation revealed extensive tissue fibrosis in the detrusors and sphincters of the SCI model rats, but the Gl-C40 + EA group showed minimal fibrotic changes, as indicated by Masson's trichrome staining (Fig. 7G and H; Fig. S9A and B). Consistently, H&E staining confirmed the marked suppression of inflammatory cell infiltration and tissue fibrosis in the Gl-C40 + EA group compared to those in the other treatment groups (Fig. 7I and J; Fig. S9C–F). These findings strongly suggest that combined EA and Gl-C40 hydrogel therapy synergistically ameliorates neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction following complete SCI in rats.

Transcriptomic characteristics of the EA and conductive hydrogel combination treatment in SCI repair

To investigate the potential therapeutic SCI repair mechanisms of the combined treatment, we performed RNA-seq analysis of the injured spinal cord tissues eight weeks post-treatment. Differential gene expression analysis revealed 850 significantly altered genes in the Gl-C40 + EA group compared to those in the Gl-C40 group (log2 FC > 1 or < −1; p < 0.05), with 501 upregulated and 349 downregulated genes (Fig. 8A). This pronounced transcriptional reprogramming suggests that EA potentiates the molecular effects of Gl-C40 via targeted pathway modulation.

(G) GSEA plot comparing the downregulated genes associated with inflammatory metabolism pathway activation in the Gl-C40 vs. Gl-C40 + EA groups. (H) GSEA analysis revealed that genes related to the Janus kinase (JAK)–signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) signaling pathway were significantly enriched in the Gl-C40-treated rats. (I) Expression levels of Il13ra2, Il23a, and Lif in the spinal cord tissue of different groups after SCI (n = 3; *p < 0.05 and **p < 0.01).

Multimodal functional analysis combining KEGG pathway enrichment and gene set enrichment analysis revealed the dual effects of the combined treatment on the neural and immune systems. KEGG analysis indicated that the downregulated genes were predominantly associated with immune response pathways (e.g., Th17 differentiation) and pathological processes, whereas the upregulated genes were clustered in neural circuit remodeling pathways, including neuroactive ligand–receptor interactions, synaptic vesicle cycling, and GABAergic/glutamatergic synapse regulation pathways (Fig. 8B–C and F). GO analysis further corroborated the enrichment of genes in the nervous system development, neuronal projection guidance, and synaptic plasticity pathways (Fig. 8D and E). Notably, the combination treatment increased mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)/BDNF–TrkB signaling-mediated GABAergic neurotransmission, thereby promoting synaptic plasticity and suppressing injury-induced neuronal hyperexcitability (Fig. 8F).

In addition to promoting neuroregeneration, Gl-C40 + EA regimen significantly attenuated secondary injuries via immune–inflammatory pathway regulation. Key inflammatory mediators (Il13ra2, Il23a, and Lif) showed marked downregulation (log2 FC < −1, p < 0.05; Fig. 8I). The GSEA analysis indicated a notable suppression of Janus kinase (JAK)–signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) signaling (Fig. 8H, normalized enrichment score = –1.92; p < 0.001), and the expression of genes associated with the JAK-STAT pathway in the Gl-C40+EA group was also reduced compared to the Gl-C40 group (Fig. S10). Significant decreases in pJAK2 and pSTAT3 positive stainings were observed at both the center and edge of the spinal cord lesions in the Gl-C40+EA group compared to the Gl-C40 group (Fig. S11). Therefore, the JAK-STAT signaling pathway might be potential targets for the combination treatment. Moreover, reduced interleukin-23/JAK2–STAT3 axis activity correlated with inhibited Th17 differentiation (Fig. 8G). The precise EA-mediated immunomodulatory mechanisms require further investigation.

These multi-omics findings establish a mechanistic framework wherein Gl-C40 + EA combination therapy downregulates the immune–inflammatory cascades to mitigate secondary damage and upregulates neural plasticity programs via 1) synaptogenesis enhancement by neuroactive ligand–receptor systems, 2) axon regeneration potentiation via MAPK/BNDF–TrkB signaling, and 3) GABAergic circuit stabilization to prevent pathological hyperexcitability. This dual-axis regulation creates a permissive microenvironment for structural and functional neural recovery. Through comprehensive transcriptomic deconvolution, we demonstrated that combined Gl-C40 hydrogel and Jiaji-EA stimulation promoted synergistic neural recovery via immunomodulation and neural restoration. Our findings provide a rationale for the development of novel bioelectronic hydrogel therapies targeting neural repair and immune modulation for SCI management.

Discussion

Despite concerns regarding their biocompatibility, carbon-based nanoparticles show promise for neural tissue engineering. In this study, CNTs were functionalized to improve their dispersibility and reduce cytotoxicity, as shown in our previous in vitro and in vivo tests [5]. H&E staining of major organs in rats treated with the Gl-C hydrogel revealed no inflammatory infiltration and tissue damage. Although unmodified CNTs have been linked to fibrosis and carcinogenicity in some studies [32], the surface-functionalized CNTs established in this study exhibited low biological risks. These findings, along with the biodegradability of the formed hydrogels that showed only a 35 % mass loss in three weeks, indicated mitigated CNT accumulation, supporting the clinical translatability of the combined therapeutic approach.

The naive spinal cord tissue exhibits electrical conductivity as endogenous bioelectrical signals are essential for maintaining the neuronal functions and facilitating axonal growth [33]. Conductive hydrogels possess electroactive properties enhancing the neuroelectric signal transmission within the spinal cord, thereby reconstructing the signaling pathways in damaged nerves and promoting neural regeneration. These hydrogels also establish an electrical microenvironment resembling the pre-injury spinal cord conditions, generating voltage gradients that modulate the ion flux across cell membranes [34]. This process activates the intracellular Ca2+ signaling cascades, driving spinal cord nerve regeneration by forming neural bridge networks and significantly improving motor function recovery. Carbon-based nanoparticles (e.g., CNTs, graphene, and carbon dots) exhibit high mechanical strength and electrical conductivity, serving as ideal candidates for modifying the conductive properties and mechanical moduli of hydrogels. This is achieved via crosslinking interactions between the polymer molecular chains and carbon nanoparticles [35]. We previously demonstrated the enhanced electrical conductivity and mechanical properties of a photocrosslinkable Gl hydrogel incorporating TCM-derived small molecule-loaded CNTs [5]. Gl-C20, Gl-C40, and Gl-C80 hydrogels were engineered to mimic the biomechanical and electrical conductivity profiles of the native spinal cord tissues. Their biomimetic properties highlight the potential of the developed hydrogels for spinal cord tissue engineering and neural differentiation. Besides, we also found that incorporated CNTs in the hydrogels exhibit a dose-dependent effect on neural differentiation, with higher concentrations inducing adversely affecting differentiation outcomes. It's probably because that CNTs interfere potassium channels and electrical signaling of excitable cells such as neurons, thereby impairing neuronal differentiation [36]. In contrast, appropriate concentration of CNTs, could upregulate neural-associated gene expression and trap neural growth factors, therefore creating a conductive and neurotrophic environment for neural differentiation [37]. Moreover, surface microstructures of the CNT-hydrogel composite might also impact neuronal differentiation [38]. The biphasic or dose-dependent effect of CNTs in directing neural stem cell fate will be investigated in future studies.

Conductive hydrogel combined with ES promotes NSC differentiation into neurons while inhibiting astrocyte proliferation [39], consistent with our findings. A previous study revealed that EA treatment improves the lower limb motor function and quality of life of patients with SCI via multifaceted mechanisms, including apoptosis inhibition, oxidative stress alleviation, inflammation modulation, autophagy regulation, and neurovascular unit repair [6,40]. Consistently, this study revealed that EA at the Jiaji acupoints effectively decreased the pro-inflammatory responses and glial scarring post-SCI, promoted neurogenesis via enhanced neuroelectric signal conduction. This combination therapy strategy showed remarkable advantages in neurogenic bladder function recovery compared to the existing conductive hydrogel-based approaches [41]. In the transection SCI model, combined EA stimulation at the Jiaji acupoints and Gl-C40 hydrogel application significantly mitigated bladder wall fibrosis and inflammatory cell infiltration. Mechanistically, upregulation of neuroactive ligand–receptor interactions [42] and GABAergic signaling [43] promotes synaptic plasticity remodeling and direct axonal regeneration to restore the neural conduction pathways responsible for bladder stretch sensing. This neural repair mechanism establishes a sensory foundation for the transmission of bladder distension signals. Moreover, coordinated modulation of calcium/potassium ion channel stabilization ensures the integrity of spinal electrical signaling, which precisely calibrates the rhythm of detrusor muscle contraction, balancing the delicate interplay between the bladder filling and voiding phases. Enhanced neuroactive ligand–receptor interactions and GABAergic signaling repair peripheral sensory circuits and enhance plasticity of the sacral micturition neural circuit [44]. Electrophysiological recordings further showed that the conductive hydrogels improved the bladder innervation conductivity, with the Gl-C40 + EA group exhibiting evoked potential amplitudes approaching the normal tissue levels.

The pro-inflammatory microenvironment post-SCI exacerbates scarring and impedes axonal regeneration. Our study demonstrated that EA at the Jiaji acupoints combined with conductive hydrogel markedly reduced neuroinflammation. Transcriptome analysis further revealed the downregulation of JAK/STAT signaling, a key macrophage/microglial polarization and inflammatory response regulator. Western blot analysis of the p-JAK2 and p-STAT3 will be carried out in future study. In addition, a recent study has proven that in a construction of rat SCI model, the electroacupuncture at Jiaji acupoints regulated JAK-STAT signaling pathway. The western blot results showed that the expression levels of p-JAK2 and p-STAT3 decreased in the Jiaji EA group compared to those in the SCI group, indicating the inhibition of the activation of the JAK2/STAT3 pathway [45]. These findings may offer supports to our transcriptomic results of JAK-STAT pathway downregulation. In this study, EA–hydrogel combination treatment achieved similar anti-inflammatory effects via endogenous regulation. Specifically, JAK/STAT suppression correlates with reduced inflammatory cytokines secretion, increased anti-inflammatory macrophage polarization [46], and attenuated reactive astrogliosis [47,48]. These synergistic mechanisms encompassing inflammation regulation and neurogenesis potentiation underlie the improved motor and bladder functional recovery in complete SCI transection models, underscoring the therapeutic value of combination of EA at the Jiaji acupoints with Gl-C40 conductive hydrogel scaffold for SCI. Future studies should elucidate the specific mechanisms underlying the synergistic effects of EA stimulation at the Jiaji acupoints and Gl-C hydrogel for neural defect repair.

Conclusion

In summary, a Gl-C40 hydrogel was synthesized and applied with EA stimulation at the Jiaji acupoints to facilitate tissue regeneration following SCI by establishing a continuous conductive circuit within the damaged spinal cord of rats. Combined application of the Gl-C40 hydrogel and EA treatment significantly modulated the inflammatory responses, enhanced endogenous neural regeneration, promoted axonal growth and myelination, ameliorated neurogenic bladder dysfunction by reconstructing the neural circuits, inhibited bladder fibrosis, and improved the locomotor function recovery. These effects were potentially mediated by the EA-induced upregulation of neural plasticity-related pathways and inhibition of the JAK/STAT signaling pathway. Collectively, our results suggest that maintaining a permissive biophysical microenvironment with electroactive stimulation is crucial for SCI repair. This innovative combinatorial approach integrating EA stimulation at the Jiaji acupoints with conductive hydrogel provides a novel avenue to enhance central nervous system regeneration for neural tissue engineering.
