Breakthrough serogroup Y meningococcal disease in a vaccinated patient with myasthenia gravis receiving ravulizumab: a case report
Authors: Silvija Šoprek Strugar, Paško Petrović, Iva Butić, Branimir Gjurašin, Marko Kutleša
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We report a case of fulminant invasive meningococcal disease caused by Neisseria meningitidis serogroup Y in a young, immunocompromised adult with myasthenia gravis receiving long-term immunosuppressive therapy including ravulizumab, despite prior meningococcal vaccination. Serogroup Y is increasingly recognized in epidemiological surveillance and may be associated with atypical clinical manifestations, including the uncommon coexistence of meningitis and pulmonary involvement, as observed in this patient. The illness was characterized by rapid progression to septic shock, meningitis, and presumed pleuropneumonia. Diagnosis was confirmed by both blood PCR and culture. This case highlights the increased susceptibility to invasive meningococcal infections in individuals receiving complement-inhibiting therapy and underscores the importance of comprehensive preventive strategies, including a carefully planned and implemented vaccination schedule and careful individualized consideration of antimicrobial prophylaxis.

Background

Invasive meningococcal disease (IMD) remains a rare but life-threatening condition, particularly among patients with impaired immune function. Monoclonal antibodies targeting complement component C5, such as ravulizumab, markedly increase susceptibility to Neisseria meningitidis infections [1, 2]. Reports of breakthrough IMD caused by vaccine-covered serogroup Y in patients receiving ravulizumab are extremely limited, and pulmonary involvement in this setting is even more unusual.

We report this case to highlight that IMD may occur and progress rapidly even in fully vaccinated patients receiving complement inhibitor therapy, may present with serogroup-associated atypical features, and requires prompt recognition supported by rapid molecular diagnostics and preventive strategies. Its novelty lies in the concurrence of vaccination, ravulizumab exposure, and severe serogroup Y infection manifesting not only with sepsis and meningitis but also with presumed pneumonia — a presentation linked to specific meningococcal serogroups — forming a rarely documented clinical constellation with important diagnostic and therapeutic implications, including careful consideration of antimicrobial prophylaxis.

Case description

Patient history and presentation

A 22-year-old female from coastal Croatia with a history of generalized myasthenia gravis (MG) diagnosed more than ten years earlier presented with acute febrile illness, confusion, and signs of septic shock. She had been receiving chronic immunosuppressive therapy consisting of prednisone 15 mg once daily, azathioprine 50 mg twice daily, and ravulizumab administered according to the standard weight-based dosing regimen, initiated on 30 January 2025, with the last dose administered two months prior to admission.

In accordance with current UK recommendations prior to initiation of complement-inhibitor therapy, the patient had been vaccinated against meningococcal disease with a quadrivalent conjugate vaccine (MenACWY), followed by a two-dose series of serogroup B vaccine (MenB) [3]. At the time of vaccination, the patient was receiving stable chronic immunosuppressive therapy without evidence of acute infection or disease exacerbation. The current illness began on 24 June 2025, one day prior to hospital admission, when the patient became febrile (≥ 38 °C) and experienced chills, nausea, vomiting, and anxiety. Emergency services were called, and the patient was transported to the nearest hospital`s emergency department. Initial clinical and laboratory evaluation did not reveal any alarming findings (CRP 15 mg/L; leucocytes 7 × 109/L, platelets 165 × 109/L; chest X-ray unremarkable), and she was discharged home with antipyretic therapy.

Overnight, the patient’s condition deteriorated, with the onset of mental confusion and purpuric skin lesions by early morning. She was urgently transferred to the University Hospital for Infectious Diseases in Zagreb, Croatia and admitted directly to the intensive-care unit due to septic shock.

The patient had not been receiving long-term antimicrobial prophylaxis before the onset of illness and had no prior clinical evidence of immunocompromise, with no documented opportunistic infections.

Clinical course and diagnostics

At admission the patient was somnolent, hypotensive (72/45 mmHg), and had a purpuric rash on the lower extremities and abdomen. Neck stiffness and a positive Kernig’s sign were present. Laboratory evaluation showed markedly elevated inflammatory markers (CRP 131 mg/L, procalcitonin 170.4 µg/L, IL-6 26 045 ng/L), leukocytosis (10.3 × 10⁹/L), thrombocytopenia (105 × 10⁹/L), coagulopathy (PT ratio 0.46; INR 1.48), and elevated lactate (4.55 mmol/L).

Blood samples for microbiological testing were obtained immediately, prior to antibiotic administration. Molecular analysis was performed using an in-house real-time PCR assay based on the methodology described by Corless et al. [4]. This PCR employed primers targeting the ctrA gene specific for Neisseria meningitidis was performed, followed by serogrouping using capsule biosynthesis gene–specific PCR targeting synF. The overall turnaround time was approximately 2 h. This assay enabled rapid pathogen detection from whole blood, and additional PCR targeting capsule biosynthesis genes confirmed the serogroup. Lumbar puncture was not performed because of coagulopathy and associated procedural risk; however, the diagnosis had already been established based on clinical presentation, neuroimaging findings, and confirmed meningococcal bacteremia.

Positive blood culture bottles were subcultured onto blood agar and incubated under appropriate atmospheric conditions. Gram stain from positive blood culture smears revealed Gram-negative diplococci. Given that growth was detected in blood cultures, colonies were identified as N. meningitidis using MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed according to the latest EUCAST guidelines [5]. Minimum inhibitory concentrations for penicillin, ceftriaxone, rifampicin, and ciprofloxacin were < 0.002 mg/L, indicating full susceptibility.

Initial chest radiography was normal. Follow-up imaging showed a new left basilar air-space opacity with a small pleural effusion, interpreted as presumed pulmonary involvement in the setting of meningococcal bacteremia (Fig. 2). However, no respiratory samples were obtained, and the pulmonary component was therefore not microbiologically confirmed. Brain MRI was largely unremarkable, except for several small punctiform hyperintense lesions in the cerebellum, frontal lobe, corpus callosum, and parietal cortico-subcortical region, interpreted as possible early microabscess formation (Fig. 3). However, in the absence of contrast-enhanced follow-up imaging, a definitive radiologic distinction from microinfarctions or microthrombotic lesions was not possible.

Treatment and outcome

Empiric antimicrobial therapy with intravenous ceftriaxone 2 g twice daily was initiated immediately. Ceftriaxone was continued as definitive therapy because of its reliable central nervous system penetration and convenient dosing. This approach is further supported by the more predictable cerebrospinal fluid penetration of ceftriaxone compared with penicillin, whose CNS concentrations depend more on the degree of meningeal inflammation, as well as by the common clinical practice of maintaining cephalosporins as definitive therapy even when isolates demonstrate in vitro susceptibility to penicillin [6]. Vasopressor support with norepinephrine was required for 24 h. Intravenous immunoglobulin was administered because of new-onset diplopia and ptosis suggestive of myasthenic exacerbation. Anticonvulsant prophylaxis with levetiracetam was initiated after electroencephalography (EEG) showed paroxysmal discharges. The patient also received mannitol, diuretics, and hydrocortisone for septic shock.

Clinical improvement followed stabilization. Diplopia resolved, inflammatory markers normalized, and follow-up imaging showed regression of pulmonary infiltrates. Follow-up brain MRI was not performed because the patient demonstrated complete clinical and neurological recovery and repeat imaging was therefore not considered clinically indicated. After completing a seven-day course of ceftriaxone consistent with international meningitis guidelines [7] and achieving clear clinical improvement, the patient was transferred to a tertiary neurology centre for optimization of therapy for her underlying myasthenia gravis. Long-term antimicrobial prophylaxis with amoxicillin 500 mg twice daily was recommended.

Discussion

Ravulizumab is a terminal complement component C5 inhibitor used for treatment of paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria, neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder, atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome, and MG [8]. It is a modified derivate of the first generation agent eculizumab, designed to offer a more convenient dosing schedule [9, 10]. By blocking cleavage of C5 into C5a and C5b, it prevents formation of the membrane attack complex, a key mechanism of innate defense against N. meningitidis [10, 11]. This mechanism leads to 1,000-fold increased risk of IMD, a complication well documented for eculizumab and increasingly recognized with ravulizumab [1, 2, 12]. Because of this risk, patients are advised to receive meningococcal vaccination at least two weeks before starting complement-inhibitor therapy [7]. Nevertheless, available evidence indicates that individuals receiving complement inhibitors remain at elevated risk for IMD despite appropriate vaccination [13].

Several studies have reported breakthrough invasive meningococcal infections in patients receiving complement inhibitors, most commonly caused by serogroup B, serogroup Y, or non-groupable strains [14], suggesting that complement blockade impairs serum bactericidal activity independently of vaccine-induced antibodies. This mechanism likely explains persistent susceptibility despite adequate vaccination.

According to current UK recommendations, a single dose of conjugate MenACWY vaccine is considered sufficient for primary immunization in adults [3]. In the present case, vaccination had been performed appropriately prior to initiation of complement-inhibitor therapy. Despite this, the patient developed serogroup Y disease, underscoring that vaccination alone may not fully mitigate risk in complement-inhibited individuals.

In Europe, serogroup B remains the predominant cause of IMD; however, surveillance data indicate a rising proportion of serogroup Y infections [15]. Serogroup Y has also been associated with atypical clinical presentations, including respiratory manifestations [16, 17].

This case demonstrates how early manifestations of invasive meningococcal disease may initially appear nonspecific and underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion in high-risk patients receiving complement inhibitors. Notably, it highlights a rare and clinically informative convergence of host susceptibility, pathogen characteristics, and atypical organ involvement, as illustrated by severe serogroup Y disease with presumed pulmonary manifestations occurring despite prior vaccination. Such presentations may delay recognition and complicate diagnostic reasoning, particularly if prior immunization leads clinicians to underestimate the likelihood of meningococcal infection. Current international recommendations differ regarding routine antimicrobial prophylaxis in complement-inhibited patients, and available evidence remains insufficient to support universal implementation, emphasizing the need for individualized preventive strategies and sustained clinical vigilance [3, 15, 18, 19]. Documenting and systematically reporting such cases is therefore essential to strengthen the evidence base and improve clinical awareness, risk assessment, and management strategies in this vulnerable population.

Conclusion

Patients receiving terminal complement inhibitors such as ravulizumab remain vulnerable to invasive meningococcal disease even when appropriately vaccinated. Serogroup Y infection may present with atyp ical features, including pulmonary involvement, which may delay recognition. This case further illustrates that prior vaccination may create a false sense of diagnostic reassurance and potentially divert clinical reasoning away from meningococcal disease in its early stages. Early molecular diagnostics and prompt empiric antimicrobial therapy are therefore essential to reduce morbidity and mortality in this population. Individualized preventive strategies — including vaccination, risk-adapted consideration of prophylaxis, and rapid access to care pathways — represent important components of management. Notably, current international recommendations are not fully aligned: UK guidance generally supports vaccination with MenACWY (and MenB where indicated) with booster dosing based on ongoing risk, without routine implementation of long-term antimicrobial prophylaxis, whereas US CDC recommendations endorse a more comprehensive approach, including both MenACWY and MenB vaccination, regular booster doses, and consideration of continuous antimicrobial prophylaxis in patients receiving complement inhibitors. In Croatia, specific national guidelines for this population are lacking, and clinical practice is largely guided by the adoption of international recommendations.

Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for invasive meningococcal disease in any febrile or nonspecific illness occurring in complement-inhibited patients, and systematic reporting of such cases remains crucial to improve evidence-based risk assessment and clinical decision-making.

Patient perspective

The patient reported a sudden onset of symptoms with rapid clinical deterioration, which was unexpected given prior vaccination and a previously stable clinical condition. Following recovery, she expressed increased awareness of the infection risk associated with complement-inhibitor therapy, as well as the importance of early recognition of symptoms and prompt medical evaluation in the event of future illness, highlighting the importance of awareness despite prior immunization.
