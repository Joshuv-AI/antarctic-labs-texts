Relationship between short-term exposure to ambient sulfur dioxide and length of hospital stay after surgery for osteoporotic fractures: a distributed lag non-linear analysis
Authors: Yong-xu Liu, Jia-qi Liang, Guo-ji Lin, Hong-yi Jiang, Min-jun Zhang, Lin Chen, Shan‑jun Yan, Ting Wang, Xi Qian, Ya-qin Gong, Chong Li, Ke Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile long-term air pollution is a known risk factor for fracture, its short-term impact on prognosis, particularly length of stay (LOS) in hospital following surgery, remains unclear.ObjectivesThis study aimed to assess the relationship between short-term exposure to ambient sulfur dioxide (SO2) and LOS among patients undergoing surgery for osteoporotic fractures.MethodsA retrospective observational analysis of secondary hospital records from 3567 patients treated in Kunshan, China, between 2017 and 2024 was conducted. Individual clinical records were linked to daily area-level ambient SO₂ and meteorological data, and a distributed lag non-linear model was used to assess the association between short-term SO₂ exposure and LOS.ResultsA non-linear, J-shaped association was identified between SO2 exposure and LOS, with an apparent inflection point around 8.1 µg/m3. However, the timing of the effects, namely, the lag structure, shifted according to the degree of exposure. Moderate levels (P75) were linked to a transient, inverted U-shaped impact that reached its peak at lag 5 (percentage increase: 1.03%; 95% CI: 0.46, 1.59). By contrast, high-intensity exposure (P95) appeared to trigger a more persistent form of toxicity, with risks peaking at lag 12 (percentage increase: 2.37%; 95% CI: 1.00, 3.76). Stratified analyses suggested higher susceptibility among female patients and those admitted in winter.ConclusionsShort-term ambient SO₂ exposure was associated with prolonged LOS after osteoporotic fracture surgery, particularly at higher levels of exposure. These findings highlight the potential relevance of ambient air pollution to postoperative outcomes in vulnerable patient populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27742-1.Keywords: Air pollution, Sulfur dioxide, Osteoporotic fracture, Length of hospital stay, Distributed lag non-linear model

Introduction

Osteoporotic fractures (OPFs) are a significant public health concern, driven by the rapid aging of the global population and changes in epidemiological patterns. The effects of these fractures are not confined to the skeletal system but also contribute significantly to morbidity, mortality, and economic burdens on healthcare systems worldwide. The clinical outcomes for patients suffering from OPFs are often complicated by prolonged hospitalization, the need for complex surgical procedures, and extensive rehabilitation, imposing a heavy strain on medical resources [1, 2].

Although traditional clinical risk factors, such as hormonal changes, nutritional deficiencies, mechanical unloading, and genetic predispositions, are well-documented, they do not fully account for the variability in fracture outcomes observed in different populations [3]. Orthopedic research is undergoing a shift in emphasis from strictly biological perspectives to consideration of environmental and clinical factors, focusing on the “exposome,” a term describing the cumulative environmental influences and biological responses throughout a person’s life [4].

The detrimental effects of ambient air pollution on bone health have been increasingly recognized, and recent metabolomics evidence has provided further support for the link between air pollution and bone damage [5]. However, this issue is generally not routinely addressed in standard orthopedic practice [6]. Air pollution has also been linked to adverse skeletal outcomes, including reduced bone mineral density, osteoporosis, and increased fracture risk [7]. Recent studies have identified particular air pollutants, including sulfur dioxide (SO₂), as potential “bone disruptors,” associated with systemic oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, and endocrine disruptions that can influence bone remodeling [5, 8]. SO₂ is produced primarily from the combustion of fossil fuels, and is notable for its tissue bioavailability and potential harmful effects even at concentrations below regulatory standards [9].

Short-term exposure to SO₂ may induce inflammation and disrupt endothelial function, both of which are crucial for fracture healing and vascularization during recovery. However, compared with the large body of evidence on fracture incidence and other incident skeletal outcomes, relatively few studies have examined the influence of gaseous pollutants such as SO₂ on post-fracture prognosis and recovery [10].

Despite the growing interest in environmental osteology, there are important evidence gaps, limiting the development of effective preventive strategies [11]. Most current studies have focused on fracture etiology, investigating how long-term, chronic exposure to air pollution affects bone mineral density (BMD) loss and increases the risk of incident fractures [12]. However, relatively little is known of the effects of short-term exposure on patient recovery and prognosis following fracture [13]. Retrospective epidemiological studies typically assess the speed and effectiveness of postoperative recovery using the length of stay (LOS) in hospital. The LOS represents a reliable proxy for physiological recovery, as it reflects both the speed of functional recovery and an absence of perioperative complications that would otherwise delay discharge [14].It is currently unknown whether transient exposure to ambient SO₂ during the critical “perioperative window” can disrupt the acute phase of biological healing, thereby prolonging hospitalization [15]. Furthermore, previous studies examining the effects of environmental factors on bone health have often relied on ecological designs with aggregate-level data, which suffer from the ecological fallacy and cannot adequately adjust for individual-level confounders, such as specific fracture sites, types of surgery, and comorbidities. This methodological limitation indicates an urgent need for further studies to dissect the complex relationship between acute exposure to air pollution and surgical outcomes [16].

To address these knowledge gaps, this study investigated the association between short-term exposure to ambient SO₂ and LOS among patients following surgery for osteoporotic fractures in Kunshan, Jiangsu Province, a developed region in eastern China. This study is a retrospective observational analysis based on secondary hospital records, together with the linking of individual clinical data with area-level daily ambient SO₂ concentrations and meteorological data and using a distributed lag non-linear model (DLNM) to evaluate the lagged association with the length of hospital stay after treatment for osteoporotic fracture, thereby providing a methodological advancement over previous ecological analyses [17]. The specific objectives were: (1) quantification of the exposure-lag-response relationship between daily environmental SO₂ concentrations and LOS, to identify potential non-linear patterns and safety thresholds; (2) elucidation of the temporal lag structure of these effects, specifically distinguishing between immediate acute impacts and delayed cumulative effects; and (3) identification of potentially vulnerable subpopulations stratified by sex and season to inform targeted interventions [18, 19]. This shift in research focus from fracture incidence to the prognosis of patients with fractures provides novel, policy-relevant evidence, specifically to support the incorporation of environmental quality standards into standard protocols for perioperative care, thereby helping to improve clinical outcomes for this sensitive patient population within the broader framework of environmental health [20].

Materials and methods

Study design, secondary data source, and study population

This retrospective observational study analyzed individual-level clinical data from the Affiliated Kunshan Hospital of Jiangsu University, the largest tertiary medical center in Kunshan, Jiangsu Province, eastern China. The included patients were treated between January 1, 2017, and March 24, 2024, covering both pre-pandemic and pandemic phases. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Affiliated Kunshan Hospital of Jiangsu University (approval No. 2024-03-053-H00-K01) and was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design of the study and the strict anonymization of personal information during data collection and analysis.

Study population

A total of 5,240 potentially eligible patients aged ≥ 50 years who were admitted with a primary diagnosis of osteoporotic fractures between January 2017 and March 2024 were identified from the electronic medical record system. Candidate patients were classified using the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes for fractures of the rib/sternum (S22), lumbar spine (S32), shoulder/upper arm (S42), forearm (S52), and femur (S72) [21]. Sex was defined according to the biological sex documented in the electronic medical records. The patients were then screened using prespecified exclusion criteria to improve clinical comparability and the validity of exposure assignment. Specifically, 263 patients were excluded due to fractures resulting from high-energy trauma or pathological causes [22] ; 773 because of a Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score ≥ 3 [23, 24]; 573 as they resided outside the Kunshan region or had incomplete demographic data, and 64 because of extreme LOS values (≤ 1 or ≥ 90 days) [19]. Ultimately, after the application of these criteria, 3,567 patients were enrolled in the final analysis. Details of the selection process, including the number of patients identified, included, and excluded at each stage, are shown in Fig. 1, and the baseline characteristics of the final cohort are presented in Table 1. As this study was a retrospective observational analysis based on secondary hospital records, no formal a priori calculation of sample size was performed; instead, all eligible patients identified during the specified study period were considered for inclusion. All included patients had complete hospitalization records from admission to discharge; therefore, no additional loss to follow-up occurred after inclusion.

Environmental and meteorological data

Daily average concentrations of five critical air pollutants, including SO2, nitrogen dioxide (NO2), ozone (O3), and particulate matter (PM2.5 and PM10), were obtained from the Kunshan Ecology and Environment Bureau. Data were collected from three state-controlled monitoring stations (No. 2 Middle School, Zhenchuan Middle School, and Kunshan Deng-yun College), all located within an 11-km radius of the hospital. Kunshan comprises approximately 927 km2, with a flat terrain, and pollutant dispersion is considered relatively homogeneous. The daily arithmetic mean of the three stations was therefore used as a city-level indicator of ambient background exposure on each calendar day. As only patients residing in Kunshan were included in the analysis, this station-averaged metric was considered a reasonable proxy for short-term area-level ambient exposure in the study population. This approach was informed by previous monitoring-station-based exposure assessment methods [25], although the monitoring stations used in the present study were concentrated to a greater extent around the study hospital. Meteorological data, including the daily mean temperature (°C) and relative humidity (%), were acquired from the Kunshan Meteorological Service to control for weather-related confounding. The daily environmental data were matched to individual patients based on their specific date of admission.

To link the environmental data with observations on individual patients, a daily environmental dataset was first constructed using the calendar date as the time index. The reference dates used for patients were the dates of their admission to hospital, and the corresponding same-day ambient pollutant and meteorological values were then assigned to the patients’ records. Lagged exposure variables were subsequently generated from the daily time series and linked to each patient using the admission date. Thus, each patient was assigned area-level ambient exposure estimates for the day of admission as well as the preceding lag days, instead of using personal or residential address-based exposure measurements.

Outcome measures and covariates

The primary outcome was the LOS, representing an indicator of postoperative recovery and healthcare resource utilization among surgically treated patients with OPFs. LOS was defined as the interval in days between hospital admission and discharge. The raw LOS data exhibited a right-skewed distribution, which is typical of healthcare utilization outcomes. Therefore, LOS values were natural log-transformed (ln[LOS]) before modeling to improve distribution normality and stabilize variance. Individual-level covariates were extracted from the electronic medical records, including demographic characteristics (age, sex, and body mass index [BMI]), clinical features (primary diagnosis/fracture site), and temporal factors (season of admission, day of the week) [26]. To account for potential changes in healthcare delivery and hospitalization practices during the COVID-19 pandemic, a binary indicator variable was also included. The COVID-19 period was defined as the interval between January 23, 2020, corresponding to the initiation of the Level 1 public health emergency response in China, and December 7, 2022, when national COVID-19 control measures were substantially relaxed. All remaining dates were classified as falling within the non-COVID-19 period [27].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the “dlnm” package in R software (version 4.5.2). Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients were first calculated to assess collinearity among air pollutants and meteorological variables [28]. A DLNM combined with a generalized linear model (GLM) was used to quantify the non-linear and delayed effects of short-term SO₂ exposure on LOS [29]. Specifically, the log-transformed LOS was modeled using a Gaussian family distribution, which was appropriate for the approximately normal distribution of the transformed outcome [30]. A natural cubic spline (ns) with 3 degrees of freedom (df) was used for both the exposure and lag dimensions, with a maximum lag of 14 days (lag 0–14) to capture the short-term cumulative effects during the acute hospitalization phase. This lag window was selected based on the observed LOS distribution (median: 7.0 days; IQR: 6.0 days), ensuring coverage of the hospitalization period for more than 75% of patients and the biologically relevant window for the early phase of fracture healing [32].

The model adjusted for potential confounders was as follows: (1) Individual-level covariates (): these included the categorical variables, sex and the primary diagnosis (fracture site), and the continuous variable, BMI. Age was modeled non-linearly using a natural cubic spline with 3 df; (2) Temporal covariates (): these included the day of the week (DOW), season (astronomical definition) to control for long-term trends and seasonality. A binary indicator for the COVID-19 pandemic period was also included; (3) Meteorological controls: Natural cubic splines with 3 df were used for the 14-day moving averages (MA14) of ambient temperature () and relative humidity () to control for weather-related confounding.

Sensitivity and stratified analyses

A directed acyclic graph (DAG) was constructed to provide a both robust identification of potential confounders and a theoretical justification of the covariate selection (Supplementary Figure S1). The DAG mapped the assumed causal relationships among short-term SO2 exposure, LOS, and potential covariates (including age, sex, BMI, season, day of the week, temperature, and humidity). The DAG was used to verify the main and sensitivity models following adjustments for these key confounders without introducing collider bias. This causal framework was used to perform comprehensive sensitivity analyses to verify the robustness of the primary findings. First, two-pollutant models were constructed by incorporating PM2.5, PM10, NO2, and O3 into the primary model to assess potential confounding by co-pollutants and ensure the independence of the SO2 effect. Second, the sensitivity of the model parameters was evaluated by varying the df values for the natural cubic splines in both the exposure and lag dimensions. A range of df values (2 to 5) was assessed, and the model fit was compared using the Akaike information criterion (AIC). The final model specification (3 df for both dimensions) was selected based on a balance between model parsimony and biological plausibility, as detailed in Supplementary Table S1. Stratified analyses were performed to identify potential subpopulations susceptible to SO2 exposure. The models were stratified by sex (male vs. female) and season (spring, summer, autumn, winter) to explore variations in risk patterns across different groups. The overall statistical significance of the SO2-LOS association within each stratum was determined using ANOVA F-tests, comparing the full model containing the cross-basis terms against a null model.

Results

Descriptive statistics of study population and environmental conditions

Overall, 3567 patients who had undergone surgery for osteoporotic fractures were included in the final analysis. As detailed in Table 1, the study population was predominantly female (69.2%), with a mean age of 68 ± 11 years and a mean BMI of 23 ± 3.3 kg/m2. There was a high prevalence of femoral (29.4%) and lumbar-vertebral (29.3%) fractures. The median LOS was 7.0 days (IQR: 6.0 days), exhibiting a right-skewed distribution. During the study period (2017–2024), the ambient air quality in Kunshan was generally compliant with local standards (Table 2). The median daily average concentration of SO2 was relatively low at 8.0 µg/m3 (IQR: 3.0 µg/m3), while the median concentrations of PM2.5, PM10, and NO2 were 23.0, 44.0, and 30.0 µg/m3, respectively. The meteorological data indicated a temperate climate with a median daily temperature of 18.4 °C.

Correlation analysis

Spearman’s rank correlation analysis (Supplementary Figure S2) revealed distinct relationships between pollutants. SO2 levels showed a moderate positive correlation with those of particulate matter (r = 0.47 for PM2.5; r = 0.53 for PM10) and NO2 (r = 0.39), but were negatively correlated with relative humidity (r=-0.43). Notably, strong collinearity was observed among co-pollutants, particularly between PM2.5 and PM10 (r = 0.89), and between particulate matter and NO2 (r > 0.60). As these correlations exceeded conventional thresholds for multicollinearity, which could compromise model stability, a two-pollutant model strategy was adopted in the sensitivity analyses to isolate the independent effects of SO2 rather than incorporating all pollutants simultaneously.

Overall exposure-response association

The complex non-linear dependency of LOS on short-term SO2 exposure is illustrated in the 3D surface plot (Fig. 2). A distinct “high-risk zone” is visible in the quadrant, corresponding to the combination of high concentrations and extended lag periods. The overall cumulative exposure-response curve provides a further delineation of this relationship, indicating a J-shaped trajectory with an apparent inflection point around 8.1 µg/m3. The association was negligible below this threshold, while above 8.1 µg/m3, the cumulative risk of prolonged LOS increased markedly before plateauing at higher concentrations. Crucially, the lower region of the 95% CI remained consistently above the reference line (log-RR = 0) throughout this elevated range, confirming a robust positive association between supra-threshold SO2 exposure and extended LOS.

Lag-specific response patterns

The temporal dynamics were characterized by stratification of exposure intensity (Fig. 3). Low-exposure levels (P5 and P25) showed no significant associations. In contrast, moderate (P75) and high (P95) levels of exposure resulted in distinct lag-response patterns. Moderate exposure (10 µg/m3) was associated with a transient inverted U-shaped trajectory, with visible effects at lag day 1, a peak at lag day 5 (percentage increase: 1.03%, 95% CI: 0.46, 1.59), and a decrease after the first week. Conversely, high exposure (14 µg/m3) led to a sustained and escalating risk. Significant effects began at lag day 1 (2.11%, 95% CI: 0.24, 4.03) and did not decline; instead, the magnitude increased progressively, reaching a delayed peak at lag day 12 (percentage increase: 2.37%, 95% CI: 1.00, 3.76). This contrast indicates that while moderate exposure disrupted early recovery phases, high-intensity exposure was linked to reduced physiological resilience for two full weeks (Table 3).

Stratified analysis by potential effect modifiers

Stratified analyses indicated distinct susceptibility patterns (Fig. 4). The sex-stratified models showed a statistically significant overall association between SO2​ exposure and LOS in female patients (P < 0.001), whereas the association in males did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.062). Stratification according to season identified winter and summer as critical periods for vulnerability (both P < 0.001). Conversely, no significant overall associations were observed for admissions during spring (P = 0.214) or autumn (P = 0.097). Analysis of the exposure-response curves showed that winter admissions were associated with consistent risks across all SO2 concentrations, while the adverse effects in summer were driven predominantly by high-exposure episodes.

Sensitivity and robustness checks

Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the primary findings. First, in the two-pollutant models with separate adjustments for PM2.5, PM10, NO2, and O3 (Supplementary Figure S4), the estimated effect of SO2 on LOS remained stable, ranging from 6.48% (95% CI: 3.82, 9.21) to 6.58% (95% CI: 3.93, 9.31). The estimates for the relative change in effect were less than 5% compared to the single-pollutant model (6.80%), indicating that the observed association was independent of co-pollutants. Second, varying the degrees of freedom (df: 2–5) for the splines (Supplementary Table S1) had no substantial effect on the direction or statistical significance of the associations. Third, to evaluate the influence of the exposure window, the cumulative effects of various lag periods (0–7, 0–14, and 0–21 days) were compared while adjusting for co-pollutants using corresponding moving averages. The exposure-response curves increased consistently across these windows (Supplementary Figure S3). Although the AIC values varied among the models, the direction and statistical significance of the associations remained robust, indicating that the primary findings were not influenced by the specific choice of the lag window (Supplementary Table S2).

Discussion

This observational secondary-data analysis linking individual clinical records with area-level ambient SO₂ data demonstrated an association between short-term SO₂ exposure and prolonged LOS in patients undergoing surgery for OPFs. The association followed a non-linear, J-shaped trajectory with an apparent inflection point around8.1 µg/m3. Notably, marked differences in the temporal patterns of exposure were observed: while moderate exposure (P75​) elicited a transient, inverted U-shaped effect that peaked at lag day 5, high-intensity exposure (P95​) resulted in sustained toxicity that continued to increase for almost two weeks, reaching a maximum at lag day 12.

Additionally, female patients and winter admissions were found to be particularly vulnerable. These associations remained robust after adjustment for co-pollutants (PM2.5​, PM10​, NO2​, O3​), confirming the independent effects of gaseous SO2​ on the acute post-fracture recovery phase.

To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies in environmental osteology to focus on prognosis (recovery) rather than etiology (incidence). While it is well-documented that air pollution is a risk factor for a loss of bone mineral density and incident osteoporotic fractures, the influence of exposure to environmental pollutants on the post-fracture recovery has remained underexplored [33]. The present findings bridge this gap by demonstrating that environmental factors may continue to influence physiological outcomes even after a fracture has occurred [34]. Unlike particulate matter, which has often been addressed in cardiovascular studies, this study showed that SO₂, a highly soluble gaseous pollutant, independently influenced LOS in multi-pollutant models [35]. This suggests that gaseous pollutants may have enhanced bioavailability, potentially enabling them to enter systemic circulation and interfere with healing processes. This finding extends the current “PM-focused” view in orthopedic environmental research [36].

Based on these findings, this study proposes a “triple hit” hypothesis, in which systemic inflammation, impaired angiogenesis, and oxidative stress combine to explain the observed lag patterns. First, the inhalation of particulate matter may act as an initial “environmental hit,” potentially contributing to systemic inflammation [37]. Superimposition of this effect on the “traumatic hit” of the fracture itself and the subsequent “surgical hit” may exacerbate the inflammatory response, characterized by an increased release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6 and TNF-α [38]. This systemic inflammation could potentially disrupt the early inflammatory phase of bone healing, as well as the balance between osteoclasts and osteoblasts, shifting the metabolic balance away from anabolic repair toward catabolic resorption [39], thereby potentially prolonging the time required for clinical stability and discharge.

The distinct temporal patterns observed, specifically, the peak at lag day 5, align with the physiological timeline of fracture healing, particularly in terms of the functions of specific pro-angiogenic signaling pathways during this period [40]. The first week after surgery represents a critical window for soft tissue repair and the initiation of angiogenesis, as demonstrated in studies investigating factors influencing fracture healing [41]. SO2 derivatives (e.g., sulfite and bisulfite) can disrupt endothelial function and may inhibit the expression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), potentially impairing angiogenesis [42]. It is possible that SO2 exposure may interfere with angiogenic processes around day 7, potentially delaying re-establishment of the vascular supply required for callus formation. Furthermore, the observed effects at high concentrations suggest that the antioxidant defense mechanisms of the body may be overwhelmed, leading to persistent oxidative stress [43]. This “saturation effect” could delay physiological recovery into the second week, consistent with the prolonged times documented in clinical observations [44].

The results of the stratified analysis indicated potential sex-related heterogeneity in susceptibility. Although no statistically significant interaction by sex was observed, the association appeared stronger among women. This pattern may be plausible given the higher baseline susceptibility to osteoporosis in women, particularly after menopause, and prior evidence linking ambient air pollution to poorer bone health in female populations [45]. Synergy between pre-existing respiratory compromise (e.g., undiagnosed COPD) and acute SO₂-induced irritation may lead to an erosion of physiological reserves, thereby increasing the risk of postoperative pulmonary complications, such as pneumonia, which are linked to extended hospitalization in surgical patients [46]. Additionally, the observed increased vulnerability during winter may reflect a “dual burden” mechanism, in which the physiological stress of cold-induced vasoconstriction impairs local hemodynamic perfusion to the fracture site, while inversion layers in the atmosphere also trap pollutants at ground level, collectively impeding the early phase of recovery [47].

These findings may have potential clinical and public-health relevance. However, due to the observational nature of this study and the use of area-level ambient exposure estimates, the findings should not be interpreted as direct evidence for the effectiveness of specific exposure-reduction interventions. Instead, the delayed lag pattern observed suggests that short-term environmental conditions around the time of admission may influence postoperative recovery. Further prospective studies with more refined individual-level exposure assessments are needed to determine whether targeted strategies to reduce pollutant exposure before or during hospitalization can improve recovery outcomes in vulnerable patients.From a public health perspective, the finding that adverse effects can manifest at concentrations as low as 8.1 µg/m3, well below current WHO guidelines, challenges the adequacy of present safety limits for vulnerable, post-surgical populations [48]. Stricter control of sulfur emissions could yield substantial co-benefits by improving patient recovery outcomes and reducing the economic burden on the healthcare system.

This study has several methodological strengths. First, the analysis involved individual-level clinical outcomes and covariates derived from secondary hospital records, rather than relying solely on aggregated daily event counts. This enabled adjustment for several patient-level covariates, such as age, fracture site, and surgical procedure, although the area-level exposure assignment indicates that ecological bias cannot be fully discounted. Second, the application of the DLNM enabled capture of the complex temporal dynamics of the exposure-response relationship, specifically identifying the delayed “lag 5” peak that would likely be missed by standard linear models. Third, the study was conducted in a low SO2 environment where the median level was only 8.0 µg/m3. The observation of significant adverse effects in this context provides critical evidence that “legal” does not necessarily mean “safe,” supporting the view that there is no safe threshold for exposure to air pollution in sensitive populations.

Several limitations warrant careful interpretation. First, SO₂ exposure was operationalized as an area-level contextual variable derived from fixed-site monitoring stations and was assigned to patients based on their date of admission, rather than being measured for each individual. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as associations with short-term ambient background SO₂ exposure rather than personal exposure. This assignment of exposure may have introduced a degree of measurement error and limited the ability to capture fine-scale spatial variations among individuals. Second, because the analysis was restricted to patients undergoing surgery for osteoporotic fractures, the findings may not be generalizable to non-surgical patients or to other fracture populations. In addition, the use of a restricted analytic sample in a retrospective secondary-data analysis may have introduced some degree of selection-related bias that cannot be fully excluded. Third, there were no explicit adjustments for calendar year or specific healthcare policies (e.g., the implementation of the Diagnosis-Related Groups [DRG] payment reforms and Enhanced Recovery After Surgery [ERAS] protocols). However, as these systemic interventions represent long-term, gradual shifts, they are unlikely to confound short-term associations between daily fluctuations in air pollution and LOS. Instead, the study controlled for the COVID-19 period and season to account for major temporal variations in healthcare availability. Fourth, detailed data on certain in-hospital complications (e.g., wound infections) and inflammatory biomarker levels were not included as consistent and systematic recording of these variables was lacking in the electronic medical records, and the timing of biomarker measurement was not standardized among individuals. Therefore, these factors could not be reliably incorporated into the overall analysis. Future studies with greater standardization of recording and biomarker assessment may help clarify the potential inflammatory mechanisms underlying the observed association. Future prospective studies incorporating personal dosimetry and biomarker information are needed for empirical confirmation of the biological pathways proposed in the “triple hit” hypothesis.

Conclusion

In this retrospective observational analysis of secondary hospital records, short-term exposure to area-level ambient sulfur dioxide was independently associated with prolonged hospital stay in patients undergoing surgery for osteoporotic fractures. The exposure-response relationship followed a non-linear J-shaped curve with an apparent inflection point around 8.1 µg/m3. Lag structures showed variations according to exposure intensity, with moderate exposure associated with a transient peak increase of 1.03% (95% CI: 0.46%, 1.59%) at lag day 5, while high-intensity exposure led to a more persistent impact, escalating to a 2.37% increase (95% CI: 1.00%, 3.76%) by lag day 12. Susceptibility was significantly higher in female patients and during winter and summer. Since these adverse effects occurred at levels below the current regulatory limits, it is recommended that air quality be strictly controlled for postoperative patients. The incorporation of environmental risk stratification into perioperative protocols, may optimize recovery outcomes.
