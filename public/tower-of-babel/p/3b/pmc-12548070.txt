Pan-immune-inflammation value predicts survival of patients with oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma receiving immunotherapy and chemoradiotherapy: a pooled analysis of two phase II trials
Authors: Xingyuan Cheng, Kongjia Luo, Yilu Gao, Ruixi Wang, Shiliang Liu, Qiaoqiao Li, Sha Zhou, Hong Yang, Baoqing Chen, Qianwen Liu, Mian Xi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the prognostic role of pan-immune-inflammation value (PIV) in patients with oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) receiving chemoradiotherapy (CRT) combined with anti-programmed cell death 1 (PD-1) immunotherapy, and to explore the underlying mechanisms and dosimetric parameters that affect PIV zenith.MethodsIn this pooled analysis, 86 patients from two phase II trials who received toripalimab plus CRT were analysed. PIV was calculated as follows: (neutrophil count × platelet count × monocyte count)/lymphocyte count. The optimal cut-off value was determined using the receiver operating characteristic curve. Survival analysis was conducted using the Kaplan–Meier method and Cox regression models. Univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses identified predictors of high PIV zenith. Pretreatment tumour samples from 46 patients were subjected to RNA and whole-exome sequencing (WES). Gene set enrichment analysis was performed on RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data, and somatic mutations were assessed using WES to further explore molecular correlates.ResultsSignificant changes in immuno-inflammatory biomarkers were observed during CRT, which gradually normalized post-radiotherapy. After a median follow-up of 35.5 months, patients with high PIV zenith during CRT exhibited significantly poorer progression-free survival (p = 0.007) and overall survival (p = 0.015). In multivariable analysis, high PIV zenith remained a significant prognostic indicator for survival. Mean lung dose (MLD) was identified as an independent predictor of high PIV zenith. Patients with high PIV zenith had decreased interferon α response, interferon γ response, transforming growth factor-β signalling and more frequent mutations in the Hippo pathway genes, resulting in pathway downregulation.ConclusionsHigh PIV zenith during CRT strongly predicts poorer survival outcomes in patients with ESCC treated with combined immunotherapy and CRT. These peaks are associated with higher MLD, reduced interferon α response, interferon γ response and increased prevalence of Hippo pathway mutations.Keywords: Oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma, PD-1 inhibitor, chemoradiotherapy, immuno-inflammatory biomarkers, pan-immune-inflammation value

Introduction

Immunotherapy has emerged as an important research field in recent years, particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting the programmed cell death 1 (PD-1)/programmed cell death ligand-1 pathway, which have provided new therapeutic options for patients with oesophageal cancer [1,2]. Several studies have demonstrated that the addition of PD-1 inhibitors can improve survival outcomes for patients with advanced or locally advanced oesophageal cancer [3–6]. Despite these advances, some patients still exhibit poor responses and have unfavourable prognoses. The complete response (CR) rate of patients with oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) received immunotherapy varies between 40% and 60% [7–10], while patients who failed to achieve CR demonstrated worse survival [11,12]. Thus, there is an urgent need to identify indicators that can predict patient outcomes and aid in clinical decision-making.

Immunity and inflammation play critical roles in both the initiation and progression of cancer. Several readily measurable immuno-inflammatory markers derived from routine blood tests – such as the neutrophil–lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet–lymphocyte ratio (PLR) and systemic immune-inflammatory index (SII) – have been linked to the prognosis of oesophageal cancer in multiple studies [13–15]. However, these markers vary in their predictive power and the results across studies are inconsistent. Considering the complex interactions between immunity, inflammation and cancer, a composite biomarker incorporating various components might better represent the overall immune-inflammatory status and provide stronger prognostic insight. The pan-immune-inflammation value (PIV), which integrates all the elements of commonly used peripheral blood immune-inflammatory markers, has shown promising predictive efficacy in several solid tumours, including colon, lung and breast cancers [16,17]. As for ESCC, several studies have found that pre-treatment PIV is a good prognostic predictor [18–20]. However, these studies did not involve patients receiving chemoradiotherapy (CRT) and immunotherapy. The studies also did not analyse PIV during treatment.

Two phase II clinical trials, EC-CRT-001 and NEOCRTEC1901, demonstrated promising efficacy and acceptable toxicity in patients with locally advanced ESCC who received definitive or neoadjuvant CRT combined with anti-PD-1 immunotherapy (toripalimab) [9,10]. The main objectives of this pooled analysis of the two trials were to evaluate the prognostic value of the PIV in patients with ESCC treated with toripalimab plus CRT. Additionally, we aimed to explore the clinical factors and dosimetric parameters influencing PIV and to analyse the possible underlying mechanisms affecting PIV through transcriptome and whole-exome sequencing (WES) data.

Materials and methods

Patients

Clinical data from the EC-CRT-001 and NEOCRTEC1901 trials were pooled for this analysis, totalling 86 patients with locally advanced ESCC (Supplementary Figure 1). The EC-CRT-001 study (NCT04005170) enrolled 42 patients with ESCC between November 2019 and January 2021, exploring the efficacy, safety and potential biomarkers of toripalimab combined with definitive CRT. The NEOCRTEC1901 trial (NCT04006041) enrolled 44 patients with ESCC from December 2019 to July 2021, assessing the efficacy and safety of toripalimab with neoadjuvant CRT.

Both trials, along with this pooled post-hoc analysis, adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Written informed consent was secured from all participants prior to enrolment, and the Institutional Review Board of Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center approved the study protocols.

Procedures

The detailed eligibility criteria, pre-treatment evaluation and treatment procedures have been previously reported [9,10]. Briefly, 240 mg of toripalimab was administered intravenously every 3 weeks in both trials. In the EC-CRT-001 trial, patients received 50.4 Gy of radiotherapy in 28 fractions, concurrent paclitaxel and cisplatin for 5 weekly cycles, and toripalimab for up to 1 year or until disease progression or unacceptable toxicity occurred. In the NEOCRTEC1901 trial, patients underwent neoadjuvant radiotherapy of 44.0 Gy in 20 fractions, concurrent paclitaxel and cisplatin for 4 weekly cycles, and toripalimab for two cycles. Oesophagectomy was performed within 6–8 weeks after neoadjuvant therapy.

Predictor and outcome definitions

Study endpoints included progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS) and CR. PFS was defined as the time from randomization to the first occurrence of tumour progression or death. OS was defined as the time from randomization to death from any cause. For patients who underwent surgery, pathological CR, as assessed on the surgical specimen, was considered CR. For patients receiving definitive CRT, the achievement of CR was assessed 3 months after radiotherapy [9].

Blood test results were collected at baseline, weekly during CRT, and 1 month post-CRT. Immuno-inflammatory biomarkers were defined as follows: absolute lymphocyte count (ALC) (×103/mL); NLR, absolute neutrophil count (×103/mL)/ALC (×103/mL); PLR, absolute platelet count (×103/mL)/ALC (×103/mL); lymphocyte–monocyte ratio (LMR), ALC (×103/mL)/absolute monocyte count (×103/mL); SII, absolute platelet count (×103/mL) × NLR. The PIV was calculated with the following equation: absolute neutrophil count (×103/mL) × absolute platelet count (×103/mL) × absolute monocyte count (×103/mL)/ALC (×103/mL).

Various dosimetric parameters for the lung and heart were analysed, including mean doses and the percentage of total lung and heart volume receiving more than x Gy (Vx).

RNA and whole-exome sequencing

Baseline frozen tumour samples from 46 patients (19 from the EC-CRT-001 trial and 27 from the NEOCRTEC1901 trial) were used for RNA and WES. All samples used for RNA and DNA sequencing were obtained through endoscopic biopsy. Pre-treatment snap-frozen biopsies were collected, and total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). RNA purity was assessed with a NanoPhotometer (IMPLEN, Westlake Village, CA), and concentration and integrity were measured using the Agilent 2100 RNA Nano 6000 assay kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) libraries were prepared using the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NR604-01/02) (San Diego, CA), following the critical assessment of library quality, paired-end sequencing was performed using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Differential expression profiles of genes distinguishing high- and low-risk subgroups were systematically analysed using the R package ‘limma’. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted using the R package ‘fgsea’ with the hallmark gene set [21]. Single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA) and ESTIMATE algorithm were also used to estimate the tumour microenvironment states [22,23]. Total DNA was extracted from tumour tissues and matched normal blood using the TIANamp DNA Mini Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China). DNA quality was assessed for degradation and purity with a Nanodrop spectrophotometer and Qubit® 3.0 Fluorometer. Libraries were prepared using the VAHTS Universal Plus DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina V2, involving fragmentation, purification and end-repair processes. The libraries underwent hybridization-based capture for target enrichment using the SureSelect XT (Agilent SureSelect XT Reagent kit, Santa Clara, CA). Library quantification and length assessment were performed using Qubit 3.0 and Agilent 2100, respectively. Sequencing was conducted using a paired-end 150 bp (PE150) approach on a high-throughput platform. Somatic mutations in ESCC tumours were identified using normal blood as a control, followed by filtering and annotation with ANNOVAR.

Statistical analysis

The data collection cut-off date for this pooled analysis was 12 March 2024. Baseline patient characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were utilized to determine optimal cut-off values to classify patients into high or low immuno-inflammatory subgroups. Kaplan–Meier curves were used to estimate PFS and OS, and log-rank tests were applied to evaluate differences between groups. Relationships between clinical or immuno-inflammatory biomarkers and survival outcomes were examined using univariate Cox regression analysis. Univariate analysis was conducted to identify risk factors for high PIV zenith, followed by multivariate logistic regression with backward-stepwise selection to optimize the models. Spearman’s rank correlations were performed to evaluate multicollinearity among dosimetric parameters before multivariate analysis. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 20.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL) and R software (R Core Team, Indianapolis, IL, version 4.2.1). p Values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

All 86 patients from the two trials were included in this study. The clinical characteristics of the patients are presented in Table 1. The median age at diagnosis was 58 years (range, 41–69 years). Most patients (76.7%) were male. The median tumour length was 5.0 cm (range, 1.0–15.0 cm). Regarding the clinical TNM stage, only eight (9.3%) patients were in stage I/II, 53 (61.6%) in stage III, and 25 (29.1%) in stage IVa. After a median follow-up of 40.4 months (range, 1.5–50.8 months), 37 patients (43.0%) had died and 42 (48.8%) experienced disease progression. Kaplan–Meier curves for PFS and OS are shown in Supplementary Figure 2. The 3-year OS rate was 57.9% (95% CI: 48.3–69.4%), and the 3-year PFS rate was 51.1% (95% CI: 41.6–62.9%) for the entire cohort.

Regarding haematological immuno-inflammatory biomarkers, the median baseline values for ALC, NLR, PLR, LMR, SII and PIV were 1.8, 2.6, 147.1, 3.7, 682.4 and 750.0, respectively (Table 1). ALC and LMR decreased rapidly during CRT, reaching a median nadir of 0.3 and 0.7, and returned to 1.3 and 2.6 one month post-CRT (Figure 1(A,D)). In contrast, NLR, PLR, SII and PIV significantly increased during CRT, with the median zenith values of 17.6, 520.3, 2949.1 and 1131.5, respectively, and declined to near baseline levels 1 month after CRT (Figure 1(B,C,E,F)).

The optimal cut-off values for each immuno-inflammatory biomarker were determined based on ROC analysis of CR. Patients were then categorized into high and low subgroups according to these optimal cut-off values (Supplementary Table 1).

Association between immuno-inflammatory biomarkers and survival

We investigated the relationship between haematological biomarkers and survival outcomes. Patients with a high PIV zenith (PIV zenith >813.1) during CRT exhibited significantly shorter PFS (p = 0.007; Figure 2(B)) and OS (p = 0.015; Figure 2(E)) compared to those with a low PIV zenith, while PIV at other time points cannot distinguish survival outcome (Figure 2(A,C,D,F)). Table 2 summarizes the prognostic factors for PFS. In the univariate analysis, age, tumour length, N stage, PD-L1 expression and PIV zenith during CRT were significantly associated with PFS. All potential prognostic factors were included in the multivariable analysis, which identified age <58 years (hazard ratio (HR), 2.34; 95% CI, 1.20–4.63; p = 0.013) and high PIV zenith during CRT (HR, 2.36; 95% CI, 1.15–4.83; p = 0.019) as independent prognostic factors for unfavourable PFS (Table 2). Tumour length, N stage and PIV zenith during CRT were significantly correlated with OS in the univariate analysis, with only high PIV zenith during CRT (HR, 2.46; 95% CI, 1.16–5.22; p = 0.019) emerging as an independent prognostic factor (Supplementary Table 2). As for CR, patients with high PIV zenith had a CR rate of 51% (26 of 51) versus 67% (22 of 33) in those with low PIV zenith, but this difference was not significant (p = 0.181).

Clinical and dosimetric factors associated with PIV

Given that PIV zenith during CRT was a risk factor for both PFS and OS, we conducted univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses (Table 3) to identify clinical factors influencing PIV zenith. The univariate analysis identified smoking history, tumour length and N stage as predictors of PIV zenith. Multivariate logistic regression identified smoking history (OR, 0.34; 95% CI, 0.13–0.89; p = 0.028) and tumour length (OR, 4.24; 95% CI, 1.51–11.88; p = 0.006) as independent predictors.

To further explore the impact of radiation dose on PIV, dosimetric factors were included in the analysis. Mean lung dose (MLD) and V5–30 of the lung were associated with PIV zenith. To prevent multicollinearity, Spearman’s correlation analysis of the dosimetric factors was performed before the multivariate analysis, revealing strong correlations between V5–30 of the lung and MLD (γ > 0.800, p < 0.001, Supplementary Figure 3). As illustrated in Supplementary Figure 4, higher MLD as well as higher V20 and V30 of the lung were associated with an increased likelihood of high PIV zenith. To avoid multicollinearity, only MLD was included in the multivariate analysis. MLD (OR, 1.29; 95% CI, 1.01–1.65; p = 0.044) and tumour length (OR, 1.39; 95% CI, 1.06–1.82; p = 0.018) were independently associated with PIV zenith, and the effect of smoking history was no longer significant. Additionally, 10 Gy for MLD, 20% for lung V20, and 7% for lung V30 were determined as the optimal cut-off values using the ROC curve (Supplementary Figure 5).

Biological basis of immuno-inflammatory biomarkers predicting survival

To further explore the biological basis of immuno-inflammatory biomarkers in predicting survival outcomes, pre-treatment biopsy samples for RNA-seq and WES were collected from 46 patients. The patients were divided into low- and high-PIV groups, with 21 and 25 in each. The GSEA plots from RNA-seq data indicate a downward trend in interferon α response, interferon γ response and transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) signalling in the high-PIV group (Figure 3(A–C)). In addition, compared to low-PIV patients, immune cell infiltration analysis using the ssGSEA method showed significantly lower intratumoural infiltration of type 2 helper T cells (Supplementary Figure 6), while the ESTIMATE score revealed no significant difference between the two groups (Supplementary Figure 7). Regarding nonsynonymous somatic single nucleotide variants, the most frequently mutated genes were TP53 and TTN, observed in 87% (40 of 46) and 52% (24 of 46) of patients, respectively (Figure 3(D)). The Hippo pathway was most frequently altered in patients with high PIV, with significant differences in RTK-RAS signalling between patients with high or low PIV (Figure 3(D)). Due to the low mutation rate of the RTK-RAS signalling pathway, we only analysed the specific mutation types of the Hippo pathway components. The most common type of mutation in the Hippo pathway was missense mutation, suggesting that patients with high PIV have downregulation in this pathway (Supplementary Figure 8).

Discussion

Based on a patient cohort from two phase II prospective trials, our study determined that PIV zenith during CRT is a strong predictor of survival in patients with ESCC treated with anti-PD-1 immunotherapy combined with CRT. Dosimetric parameters such as MLD, lung V20 lung and V30 were positively correlated with high PIV zenith. Additionally, patients with high PIV zenith had downregulation of interferon α response, interferon γ response and more frequent mutations in Hippo pathway.

Originally proposed in colorectal cancer, PIV has been proved to outperform other immuno-inflammatory markers and therefore been recognized as a robust prognostic indicator in patients with metastatic colon cancer [17]. With the advent of immunotherapy, various studies have highlighted the prognostic value of PIV in patients receiving such treatments. For instance, in patients with small-cell lung cancer receiving immunotherapy, high PIV was significantly linked to poorer clinical outcomes [24]. Yekedüz et al. suggested that pretreatment PIV could serve as a prognostic biomarker for patients with metastatic renal cell carcinoma receiving nivolumab as second-line therapy or later [25]. Although studies on PIV in oesophageal cancer are scarce, Feng et al. reported that pretreatment PIV predicted the treatment efficacy of neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy in ESCC, and patients undergoing radical surgery with low preoperative PIV experienced longer cancer-specific survival [18,20]. However, these studies did not involve radiotherapy and focused solely on baseline PIV. Corti et al. dynamically assessed changes in PIV scores, finding that an increase in early PIV relative to baseline was associated with poor response to immune checkpoint inhibitors and survival outcomes in patients with microsatellite destabily-high colorectal cancer [26], highlighting the significance of monitoring immune-inflammatory markers during treatment. Our study is the first to evaluate the prognostic value of PIV in patients with ESCC treated with combined CRT and immunotherapy, demonstrating that on-treatment PIV zenith is an important prognostic indicator.

In previous studies, immunoinflammatory biomarkers such as the PLR and NLR were reported as prognostic predictors in patients with ESCC treated with CRT combined with immunotherapy [15,27]. In this study, these markers were also evaluated but only the PIV was identified as a prognostic indicator. PIV, which comprises lymphocytes, neutrophils, monocytes and platelets, offers a more comprehensive assessment of a patient’s immunoinflammatory status than other markers. Biologically, each component significantly impacts anti-tumour immunity. Myeloid-derived suppressor cells, associated with resistance to PD-1/PD-L1 inhibition, originate from monocytes and neutrophils [28]. Neutrophils promote tumour growth and metastasis through multiple mechanisms: release reactive oxygen species and nitrogen species, and enzymes to induce DNA damage, secrete granule proteins that promote angiogenesis and suppress T cell activation, facilitate tumour cell proliferation and migration via extracellular traps, mediate tumour cell intravasation and subsequent dissemination, and establish pre-metastatic niches to reactivate dormant cells [29]. Within the tumour microenvironment, monocytes differentiate into cell populations such as tumour-associated macrophages and myeloid-derived suppressor cells. These cells impede immune function by the depletion of essential amino acids, the production of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, and the expression of immune checkpoint molecules. Furthermore, they directly drive tumour growth via non-immune pathways, which encompass promoting angiogenesis, remodelling the extracellular matrix, facilitating metastasis, sustaining tumour metabolism, supporting cancer stem cells and reversing cellular senescence [30]. Platelets form microaggregates that shield circulating tumour cells from immune surveillance and secrete cytokines that inhibit antigen presentation and promote tumour cell proliferation [31]. In contrast, lymphocytes, crucial to the mechanism of immune checkpoint inhibitors, play a vital role in anti-tumour responses by secreting interleukin-17 and initiating cytotoxic immune actions [32].

In this study, we analysed, for the first time, factors influencing PIV during treatment, including radiotherapy dosimetric parameters, and found that MLD, lung V20 and lung V30 were significantly associated with high PIV. Lymphocytes are notably radiosensitive, with a D50 as low as 2 Gy [33], making them vulnerable in patients with thoracic malignancies where the lungs, rich in circulation, often fall within the radiation field. Previous studies have demonstrated a significant correlation between lung dose and volume histogram parameters, such as MLD, V5 or V10, and lymphopenia [34–36]. Besides lymphocyte counts, PIV also reflects the inflammatory status, which can be exacerbated by tumour necrosis after radiation and radiation pneumonitis. Radiation pneumonitis, in contrast to treatment-associated lymphopenia, correlates more strongly with the percentage of lung volume receiving higher doses [37,38]. These findings partially explain our results, but further investigation is needed to fully understand the specific effects of radiation dose parameters on the immunoinflammatory state of patients. We have determined that 10 Gy of MLD, 20% of lung V20, and 7% of lung V30 are the optimal cut-off values. Adjusting the radiotherapy plan to reduce these indices may mitigate the adverse immune-inflammatory state induced by radiation.

The specific mechanisms whereby immunoinflammatory markers predict patient prognosis remain unclear. We found that interferon α response and interferon γ response were downregulated in patients with high PIV. Interferon α has long been utilized in the treatment of malignant tumours due to its ability to block cell cycle progression, induce apoptosis in tumour cells, enhance the expression of major histocompatibility complex molecules on tumour cells, and promote tumour recognition by immune cells [39]. Additionally, interferon α can restore glucose levels in the microenvironment, facilitating the activation of CD8+ T cells and effective tumour killing. Additionally, the combined use of interferon α and PD-1 inhibitors has significantly improved responses to immunotherapy in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma [40]. Interferon γ exerts its antitumor effects by enhancing both the cytotoxic functions of natural killer cells and tumour-specific cytotoxic CD8+ T lymphocytes, and the antigenicity of tumour cells, inhibiting cell proliferation, and promoting apoptosis [41]. Additionally, it increases endothelial cells’ sensitivity to TNF, inducing TNF to kill tumour endothelial cells and thereby inhibiting tumour growth [42]. Its role in shaping the tumour microenvironment can be improved by the therapeutic efficacy of immunotherapy by modulating immune cell responses [43]. Administering interferon α or interferon γ to patients with high PIV could be a viable strategy to enhance their response to PD-1 therapy.

In this study, patients with high PIV exhibited downregulation of the TGF-β pathway. TGF-β plays a dual role in tumorigenesis and progression. On the one hand, TGF-β inhibits tumorigenesis by suppressing cell proliferation and promoting apoptosis. On the other hand, TGF-β promotes tumour progression and metastasis by inducing tumour cell epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, promoting tumour angiogenesis and inhibiting the cytotoxic effects of the host immune system [44,45]. Some clinical studies suggest that TGF-β inhibition has controllable safety and efficacy in gastric and oesophageal cancer. However, in a few patients, tumour size increased by more than 100% one month after treatment, a phenomenon classified as hyperprogression [46,47]. In summary, the dual role of TGF-β in oesophageal cancer should be noticed, and patients who may benefit from pathway interference should be carefully selected.

We also found that patients with high PIV zenith have more frequent mutations in the Hippo pathway that lead to pathway downregulation. As tumour suppressor gene or pathway, Hippo and p53 act synergistically to maintain oesophageal homeostasis and inhibit the formation of ESCC [48]. Dysregulation of this pathway leads to enhanced activity of YAP/TAZ, promoting tumorigenesis and progression. Hippo signalling contributes to drug resistance in various cancers by modulating tumour immunogenicity and intrinsic cancer cell regulation [49]. Additionally, its characteristics have been identified as predictive biomarkers of prognosis and treatment response in gastric cancer [50]. This pathway has become an attractive target for anticancer therapy development. Unfortunately, our RNA-seq results failed to confirm downregulation of the Hippo pathway in patients with high PIV, which may be due to complex transcriptional regulation and post-transcriptional modifications, as well as the limited sample size of this study.

The data for this study were sourced from two prospective clinical trials, ensuring high-quality. However, the study still has several limitations: a relatively small sample size; a lack of external validation; factors such as infection, steroid treatment or other drugs that could affect immune-inflammatory markers were not explored; and cut-off values for inflammatory biomarkers varied across studies and therefore require further validation.

Conclusions

Our study found that high PIV zenith during CRT is a strong predictor of survival in patients with ESCC treated with combined anti-PD-1 immunotherapy and CRT. High PIV zenith was associated with higher MLD, lung V20 and lung V30. Patients with high PIV zenith had decreased interferon α response, interferon γ response, transforming growth factor-β signalling and more frequent mutations in the Hippo pathway that lead to pathway downregulation. Future studies should focus on optimizing radiotherapy regimens and combination treatment strategies to improve the immune-inflammatory status of patients. Additionally, research should explore potential complementary therapeutic strategies for patients who are non-responsive and have poor immune-inflammatory status.
