Intranasal versus intravenous administration of analgesia — authors’ reply
Authors: Michael A Smyth, Joyce Yeung, Gavin D Perkins
Published: 2025

We agree with Nicolas Py and colleagues that rapid and effective pain relief is a priority for emergency medical services (EMS).1 The required steps following EMS arrival to administration of analgesics include patient assessment, obtaining medical history, recording vital signs, securing vascular access, and formulating a treatment plan. The time spent obtaining vascular access will vary between patients and practitioners, but it is likely to be a small component of the overall interval from arrival to drug administration.

The effectiveness of analgesia is influenced by the route of administration and both the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties of the drug, which together determine how quickly and reliably pain relief is achieved. The intranasal route, although attractive as rapidly accessible, does not necessarily ensure more effective analgesia. For example, in one randomised trial of post-operative pain among patients undergoing spinal surgery, Striebel and colleagues reported that intranasal fentanyl was inferior to intravenous fentanyl over the first 30 min,2 which might be an important timeframe for prehospital populations. In another randomised trial among post-operative surgical patients, the same group found that intranasal fentanyl demonstrated longer time to onset and longer time to peak analgesia, compared with intravenous administration.3 Similarly, Toussaint and colleagues4 reported no significant difference in Numeric Rating Score (NRS), time to onset of pain relief, or time to peak pain relief when comparing intranasal and intravenous fentanyl. However, a prehospital study by Rickard and colleagues5 did demonstrate a larger reduction in Verbal Rating Score (VRS) among patients receiving intranasal fentanyl compared with those receiving intravenous morphine. It is uncertain, however, whether this difference was related to the administration route or the pharmacodynamic properties of the drugs under investigation.

In a systematic review, Bowman and colleagues6 found that the intranasal route generally reduces time to drug administration, might be particularly helpful in paediatric populations, and seems to be effective for analgesia. They also found that some drugs given intranasally will require more frequent redosing than when administered intravenously and that the intranasal route is not always more effective than the intravenous route.

In relation to the PACKMaN trial,7 the intranasal route might have reduced time to drug administration, but it is unclear how substantial the time reduction would have been. It is also unclear whether intranasal administration would have affected our findings. It is certainly possible that both drugs would have been administered a little quicker, with no change in analgesic efficacy.

In closing, we are supportive of a multimodal approach to analgesia, but we do not necessarily agree that our findings indicate the need for a multimodal approach. One potential advantage of intravenous over intranasal administration is the ability to titrate to effect, which might be of greater importance in the prehospital population, where the interval for treatment is short, and who may be physiologically unstable with limited monitoring capability. Finally, we wholeheartedly agree that achieving adequate analgesia, in a timely manner, is a key priority when treating patients with moderate to severe pain.
