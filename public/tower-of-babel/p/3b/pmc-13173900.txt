Dual T-cell exhaustion and hyperactivation profiles identify patients with advanced HIV at high risk for fungal co-infections
Authors: Xiaowen Jiang, Yuanrui Liu, Danhua Li, Yanxia Liu, Yishan Chen, Hongya Jiang, Zhanfeng Zhang, Rong Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo characterize immune alterations in PLWH across disease stages and identify laboratory indicators for diagnosing fungal co-infections.MethodsWe conducted a comprehensive cross-sectional study comparing laboratory immunological parameters—including lymphocyte subsets, activation/exhaustion markers, and plasma cytokines—across patient groups stratified by co-infection type and CD4⁺ T cell count. Statistical significance was assessed using false discovery rate correction.ResultsThe most severe immunological compromise was observed in patients with advanced HIV disease (CD4⁺ ≤199 cells/µL) and co-infections, who exhibited profound pan-lymphopenia and a dissociated T cell profile featuring both hyperactivation (HLA-DR/CD38) and exhaustion (loss of CD28). Compared to patients with other opportunistic infections, those with fungal co-infections displayed a distinct immune phenotype characterized by significantly lower CD4⁺ T cell counts (51.32 ± 47.41 vs. 134.27 ± 130.12 cells/µL, q = 0.004) and B cell counts (65.74 ± 56.13 vs. 108.30 ± 101.10 cells/µL, q = 0.024), alongside elevated CD8⁺ T cell activation. Patients with localized Talaromyces marneffei infection had significantly higher CD4⁺ T, CD8⁺ T, and natural killer cell counts than those with disseminated disease, underscoring the importance of preserved cellular immunity in pathogen containment. No significant differences were observed across fungal pathogens or sites of cryptococcal disease. Correlation analysis revealed a strong positive association between HIV viral load and interleukin-10(R² = 0.587, r = 0.632, 95% CI: 0.473 to 0.755, P = 0.014).ConclusionSpecific immunological parameters—particularly severe CD4⁺ and B cell lymphopenia with concurrent CD8⁺ T cell hyperactivation—provide an objective basis for identifying PLWH at high risk for fungal infections. These findings highlight the potential value of adjunctive immunomodulatory strategies alongside antiviral therapy in this patient population.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13223-8.

Introduction

Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS), caused by the Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV), remains a global health crisis, with an estimated 39 million people living with HIV worldwide [1, 2]. The pathogenesis of HIV is defined by chronic immune activation and progressive CD4⁺ T cell depletion [3, 4], leading to severe immune compromise and heightened susceptibility to a spectrum of opportunistic infections (OIs) [5, 6]. Among these, fungal co-infections represent a major cause of morbidity and mortality [7, 8], particularly in advanced disease stages.

The evolving immune landscape during HIV infection profoundly alters conventional laboratory parameters. Although standard inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) are elevated during co-infections, their patterns are often dysregulated and may not reliably reflect the severity of specific OIs. Underlying this dysregulation is a complex disruption of the cytokine network [9], creating a state of chronic inflammation that paradoxically coexists with immune cell depletion and functional exhaustion [10]. Flow cytometric analyses consistently reveal progressive deterioration of the immune architecture, marked not only by the hallmark decline in CD4⁺ T cells but also by pan-lymphopenia, skewed T cell differentiation, and concurrent T cell hyperactivation and exhaustion in advanced disease [11].In this context, “exhaustion” refers to a state of T cell dysfunction characterized by progressive loss of effector functions (e.g., cytokine production, proliferation), sustained expression of inhibitory receptors, and altered transcriptional programs, often resulting from chronic antigen exposure as seen in persistent viral infections like HIV.

However, a critical question remains poorly understood: Are there distinct immunological signatures that differentiate HIV patients with fungal co-infections from those with other OIs [12]? Current evidence linking specific patterns of lymphopenia, T cell subset al.terations, and systemic immune activation to the risk and type of fungal infection remains fragmented [13, 14]. Moreover, the immunological determinants governing the clinical manifestations of specific fungal diseases—such as localized versus disseminated Talaromyces marneffei infection—are not well defined [15, 16].

Therefore, this study was designed to systematically characterize and compare hematological and immunophenotypic profiles—including lymphocyte subsets, activation/exhaustion markers, and cytokine levels—across a spectrum of HIV patients stratified by infective status and CD4⁺ T cell counts. By elucidating these immune signatures, we aim to identify objective laboratory correlates that may aid in risk stratification and differential diagnosis of co-infections in this vulnerable population.

Materials and methods

Study population

This prospective observational cohort analyzed 420 hospitalized and outpatient cases (174 PLWH cases, 219 AIDS cases with co-infections and 27 AIDS cases without co-infections) at Guangzhou Eighth People’s Hospital from January 2022 to February 2023, along with 25 age- and sex-matched healthy controls (HCs).

Patients were further divided into three groups based on CD4⁺ T cell count: ≥500 cells/µL, 499–200 cells/µL, and ≤ 199 cells/µL. PLWH/AIDS participants were recruited according to the following criteria:

Exclusion criteria included active malignancy, pregnancy, hepatitis B/C coinfection, and recent vaccination (< 3 months). The cohort included 345 male (19–75 years) and 75 female (27–72 years) participants. Demographic characteristics, clinical data, and comprehensive lymphocyte subset profiles were collected for all subjects. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Guangzhou Eighth People’s Hospital (Approval No: K202474376).

Diagnostic criteria and timing of sample collection

All co-infections were diagnosed using standard clinical, microbiological, and/or radiological criteria within one week prior to study enrollment. Blood samples for immunological analyses were collected prior to the initiation of any targeted antimicrobial (antifungal or antibacterial) therapy for the diagnosed co-infection. Peripheral blood samples were collected in EDTA anticoagulant tubes and processed for flow cytometry analysis within 24 h using fresh whole blood. The “AIDS with co-infections” group (n = 219) included patients with the following: fungal infections (n = 182, detailed below), bacterial pneumonia (n = 18, e.g., Streptococcus pneumoniae, Klebsiella pneumoniae), tuberculosis (n = 12), cytomegalovirus disease (n = 5), and syphilis (n = 2).

Fungal pathogen distribution

Among the 182 patients with fungal co-infections, the identified pathogens and their frequencies were: Talaromyces marneffei (n = 87, 47.8%), Pneumocystis jirovecii (n = 41, 22.5%), Cryptococcus neoformans (n = 25, 13.7%), and mixed fungal infections (n = 29, 15.9%).

Lymphocyte subset enumeration (absolute counts)

Lymphocytes were gated based on high CD45 expression and low side scatter (SSC). Within the lymphocyte gate, CD3⁺ T lymphocytes and CD3⁻ non-T lymphocytes were identified. CD3⁺CD4⁺ and CD3⁺CD8⁺ T cell subsets were quantified among CD3⁺ T cells. Among CD3⁻ lymphocytes, CD19⁺ B cells and CD3⁻CD16⁺CD56⁺ NK cells were quantified. Absolute counts were calculated using fluorescent counting beads (Trucount™ or equivalent) with the following formula:

Absolute Count (cells/µL) = (Number of Gated Subset Events / Number of Acquired Bead Events) × (Total Beads per Tube / Sample Volume Analyzed (µL)).

T cell immunophenotyping for reconstitution

Lymphocytes were gated based on high CD45 expression and low SSC. CD3⁺ T cells were gated within the lymphocyte population, and CD3⁺CD4⁺ T cells were further gated. Naïve T cells were defined as CD3⁺CD4⁺CD45RA⁺CD45RO⁻, and memory T cells as CD3⁺CD4⁺CD45RO⁺CD45RA⁻.

T cell activation analysis

Lymphocytes were gated based on high CD45 expression and low SSC. CD3⁺ T cells were gated, and the frequency of CD3⁺HLA-DR⁺ cells was determined. Within the CD3⁺ T cell gate, CD3⁺CD4⁺ (helper) and CD3⁺CD8⁺ (cytotoxic) T cells were analyzed for activation markers: HLA-DR⁺ and CD28⁺ frequencies in CD4⁺ T cells, and HLA-DR⁺, CD28⁺, and CD38⁺ frequencies in CD8⁺ T cells.

Other tests

Complete blood count analysis was performed using a Mindray BC-6800Plus automated hematology analyzer (Shenzhen Mindray Bio-Medical Electronics Co., Ltd., China). Plasma levels of CRP were quantified using the Mindray CRP-M100 kit (latex-enhanced immunoturbidimetric method) on the corresponding analyzer (Shenzhen Mindray Bio-Medical Electronics Co., Ltd., China). Procalcitonin (PCT) concentrations were measured using the electrochemiluminescence immunoassay with the Elecsys BRAHMS PCT kit on a Cobas e 411 analyzer (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Germany). HIV-1 viral load was determined by the COBAS® AmpliPrep/COBAS® TaqMan® HIV-1 Test, v2.0 (PCR-fluorescence method) on the respective Roche Molecular Systems instruments (Roche Molecular Systems, Inc., USA). The plasma levels of Th1/Th2 cytokines were quantified using a commercial human Th1/Th2 cytokine kit (Cytometric Bead Array, CBA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Saili Biotechnology, Jiangxi, China). Analysis was performed on a BD FACS Lyric™ flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 21.0. Normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using independent samples t-tests. For multiple comparisons, the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied to control the FDR. P-values from omnibus tests (ANOVA/Kruskal–Wallis) for each parameter in Tables 1, 2 and 3 were adjusted to q-values, with q < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted only for variables with significant omnibus tests; these p-values are indicated with superscripts in the tables. Effect sizes are reported as Cohen’s d for t-tests (small |d| ≈ 0.2, medium |d| ≈ 0.5, large |d| ≈ 0.8) and rank-biserial correlation r for Mann–Whitney U tests (small |r| ≈ 0.1, medium |r| ≈ 0.3, large |r| ≈ 0.5), with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) provided. Viral load data were log₁₀-transformed for all correlation analyses due to their right-skewed distribution. Descriptive statistics for viral load are reported as median and interquartile range (IQR).

Results

Synergistic exacerbation of lymphocyte exhaustion, aberrant differentiation, and dysregulated activation by HIV disease progression and co-infection (Table 1)

Comparative analysis revealed a stepwise deterioration in immunological parameters across study strata (all reported findings FDR-corrected q < 0.05; Table 1). PLWH exhibited significant perturbations relative to HCs, including lymphopenia, reduced CD4⁺ T cell counts, and an inverted CD4/CD8 ratio. This immune deficit progressed in patients with AIDS, who showed further declines in CD4⁺ T cells. The most profound immunological collapse was observed in AIDS patients with co-infections, who developed severe pan-lymphopenia, with near-complete ablation of CD4⁺ T cells (70.74 ± 58.41 cells/µL) and significantly lower counts of all other major lymphocyte subsets compared to all other groups (all q < 0.001). Immunologically, this advanced stage was defined by a shift toward a memory CD4⁺ T cell phenotype and a hyperactivated/exhausted T cell profile, evidenced by marked upregulation of HLA-DR and CD38, coupled with severe loss of CD28 on both CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells (all q < 0.001).

Progressive hematopoietic suppression correlates with severity of CD4⁺ T cell depletion (Table 2)

Hematologically, patients with preserved immunity (CD4⁺ ≥500 cells/µL) showed parameters largely comparable to HCs. A significant decline in leukocyte and lymphocyte counts emerged once CD4⁺ T cells fell below 500 cells/µL (both q < 0.05). The most profound disturbances were observed in the severely immunocompromised group (CD4⁺ ≤199 cells/µL), which exhibited marked pan-cytopenia. Lymphocyte counts were the most severely affected, plummeting to 0.70 ± 0.49 × 10⁹/L compared to 2.42 ± 0.69 × 10⁹/L in HCs (q < 0.001). In contrast, the plasma cytokine profile remained largely stable. Among the six cytokines analyzed, only interleukin-2 (IL-2) demonstrated statistically significant variation across CD4⁺ strata (q = 0.018), displaying a non-linear trajectory that fell to its lowest level in the most severely immunocompromised group. Notably, all measured cytokine concentrations, including IL-2, remained within normal reference ranges. No other cytokines—IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), or interferon-gamma (IFN-γ)—showed significant alterations after FDR correction (all q > 0.05).

Progressive immune deterioration and phenotypic dissociation across CD4⁺ T cell strata (Table 3)

Patients with preserved immunity (CD4⁺ ≥500 cells/µL) exhibited absolute counts of total T、B and NK cells that were comparable to healthy controls. However, a significant decline in the proportion of CD4⁺ T cells and a reciprocal rise in CD8⁺ T cells were already apparent at this stage (both q < 0.001). Once the CD4⁺ T cell count fell below 500 cells/µL, a progressive and severe pan-lymphopenia ensued. The most profound reductions in absolute counts of CD3⁺ T, CD8⁺ T, B, and NK cells were observed in the CD4⁺ ≤199 cells/µL group, which were significantly lower than all healthier patient groups and HC (all q < 0.001). This resulted in a severely inverted CD4/CD8 ratio, with CD8⁺ T cells dominating the lymphocyte pool (58.58% ± 15.24%, q < 0.001).

This numerical decline was coupled with profound functional T cell alterations. The remaining CD4⁺ T cells exhibited a terminally differentiated phenotype, characterized by a steep, stepwise decline in naïve cells and a corresponding expansion of memory cells as CD4⁺ counts dropped (both q < 0.001). Most strikingly, the CD4⁺ ≤199 cells/µL threshold marked the onset of a state of dissociated T cell activation and exhaustion. Distinct Immunological profiles in HIV-positive patients with fungal versus other opportunistic infections

Divergent Immunological Signatures in HIV Patients with Fungal versus Non-Fungal Opportunistic Infections (Table 4)HIV-positive patients with fungal co-infections exhibited a more severely dysregulated immune phenotype compared to those with other OIs, encompassing bacterial (Streptococcus pneumoniae, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa), mycobacterial, viral (e.g., cytomegalovirus), and syphilitic infections, characterized by profound CD4⁺ and B cell lymphopenia alongside heightened systemic T cell activation (Table 4). Specifically, the fungal group demonstrated significantly lower CD3⁺CD4⁺ T cell counts (51.32 ± 47.41 vs. 134.27 ± 130.12 cells/µL, q = 0.004) and CD19⁺ B cell counts (65.74 ± 56.13 vs. 108.30 ± 101.10 cells/µL, q = 0.024). Concordantly, the percentage of CD3⁺CD4⁺ T cells was significantly lower (5.36% ± 5.51% vs. 12.26% ± 11.06%, q = 0.004). In contrast, the percentage of CD3⁺CD8⁺ T cells was significantly higher (61.02% ± 14.89% vs. 53.38% ± 15.47%, q = 0.012) in group AIDS with fungal compared to group AIDS with othter OIs. Furthermore, a state of heightened immune activation was evident, with significantly higher expression of HLA-DR on total CD3⁺ T cells (41.11% ± 20.03% vs. 30.33% ± 15.80%, q = 0.004) and on the CD3⁺CD4⁺ T cell subset (48.61% ± 21.88% vs. 35.15% ± 21.72%, q = 0.004). Activation of the CD8⁺ T cell compartment was particularly pronounced, reflected in increased co-expression of CD38 (90.99% ± 9.20% vs. 83.77% ± 15.75%, q = 0.020) and HLA-DR (48.23% ± 19.34% vs. 39.27% ± 17.93%, q = 0.020).

HIV RNA viral load correlates with hematological decline, inflammatory surge, and divergent T Cell activation profiles (Table 8 and Fig. 1).

Correlation analyses revealed distinct patterns of association between HIV viral load and immunological parameters. The most robust relationship was a strong positive correlation between viral load and the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 (R² = 0.587, r = 0.632, 95% CI: 0.473 to 0.755, P = 0.014).

In contrast, higher viral loads were inversely correlated with absolute counts of multiple lymphocyte subsets. Significant, albeit weak, negative correlations were observed with CD3⁺CD4⁺ T cells (r = − 0.640, CI: -0.709 to -0.559, P = 0.015), CD3⁺CD8⁺ T cells (r = − 0.270, CI: − 0.383 to − 0.148, P = 0.044), and CD16⁺CD56⁺ NK cells (r = − 0.430, CI: − 0.528 to − 0.321, P = 0.013). A comparable weak negative correlation was also found with platelet counts (r = − 0.272, CI: − 0.386 to − 0.149, P < 0.001).

Weaker positive correlations were identified between viral load and markers of systemic inflammation and immune activation, including PCT (r = 0.144, CI: 0.004 to 0.383, P = 0.042) and the CD8⁺ T cell activation markers CD38 (r = 0.692, CI: 0.619 to 0.752, P = 0.006) and HLA-DR (r = 0.242, CI: − 0.118 to 0.357, P = 0.025). It is notable that the confidence intervals for these associations crossed or approached zero, indicating uncertainty in the estimate of their true strength. No significant correlations were detected between viral load and lymphocyte subset percentages, T cell differentiation markers, or the majority of other cytokines measured.

Discussion

Our data clearly illustrate the immunological trajectory of HIV disease progression. As CD4⁺ T cell counts decline, the immune system undergoes qualitative changes: from initial selective CD4⁺ T cell loss to eventual pan-lymphopenia, accompanied by severe T cell dysregulation. The observed phenotypic dissociation—concurrent loss of the co-stimulatory molecule CD28 alongside dramatic upregulation of CD38 and HLA-DR [17]—represents a state of simultaneous hyperactivation and exhaustion. This phenomenon was most prominent in patients with advanced disease and co-infections [18], suggesting that persistent antigenic stimulation plays a crucial role in driving immune dysregulation.

Within this context, our data identify a distinct profile of immunological exhaustion in patients with fungal co-infections, characterized by significantly more profound depletion of both CD4⁺ T cells and B cells compared to those with other OIs. This finding is consistent with established clinical evidence that severe CD4⁺ T cell lymphopenia, particularly at counts ≤ 50 cells/µL, is a key risk factor for invasive fungal diseases [19]. However, the concomitant, severe loss of B cells suggests a broader combined immunodeficiency. As HIV infection is known to cause profound B cell dysregulation—including aberrant activation, functional impairment, and immunosenescence [20–22]—this deficit likely compromises critical humoral responses and antigen presentation, further crippling the antifungal defense.

We therefore posit that fungal infections in this setting may not be merely a passive consequence of pre-existing immunosuppression. Instead, they could actively exacerbate the immunopathologic trajectory. The absence of CD4⁺ Th cells cripples the orchestration of cellular immunity [23], while the B cell deficiency undermines humoral defense. This creates a vicious cycle: the persistent antigenic burden from the fungal pathogen, superimposed upon a background of chronic immune activation driven by residual HIV transcription [24], may actively accelerate the loss of these key effector lymphocytes. This synergy between a specific immunodeficiency and ongoing microbial stimulation fosters an environment exceptionally permissive to fungal persistence and dissemination [25].

Vertical comparison with established literature, including large-scale cohort studies, confirms the central role of CD4⁺ T cell count as the primary indicator of disease progression and OI risk [26]. Our study extends this knowledge by empirically demonstrating that a CD4⁺ T cell count below 200 cells/µL represents a critical threshold. This aligns with findings in lymphocytopenia patients, where a CD4 count of < 100 cells/µL, compared to 101–300 cells/µL, was associated with a markedly increased risk of opportunistic infections [27].

Horizontal comparison with contemporary research yields novel insights. Whereas generalized immune activation in PLWH is well-documented, our controlled comparison—directly contrasting fungal with non-fungal opportunistic infections—specifically links fungal co-infections to a more severe immunophenotype, defined by exaggerated T cell exhaustion and systemic activation. This observation partially aligns with earlier reports by Kaw et al. [28] but is strengthened by our study’s matched design within a uniform patient cohort. Furthermore, our stratification of Talaromyces marneffei cases confirms that pathogen containment is closely associated with preserved cellular immunity, reflected in significantly higher absolute counts of CD3⁺ T, CD4⁺ T, CD8⁺ T, and NK cells—consistent with the model proposed by Stolbova et al. [29]. Mechanistically, effective antifungal immunity appears to require functional T-B cell collaboration, a process disrupted in HIV infection [5]. Most notably, the absence of marked immunological differences across major fungal pathogens (Pneumocystis jirovecii, Cryptococcus, Talaromyces marneffei) constitutes a novel finding. This suggests that severe combined immunodeficiency affecting both T and B cell lineages rather than pathogen-specific immune responses [30], serves as the common underlying driver of severe fungal disease progression in this population.

A significant positive correlation was observed between HIV viral load and the immunosuppressive cytokine IL-10 (r = 0.632, P = 0.014). This finding suggests that high-level viral replication may trigger excessive IL-10 production [29]. We hypothesize that this represents a maladaptive immunoregulatory response, which could further suppress the already compromised antiviral and antifungal immunity [31]. Such a mechanism would create a vicious cycle, perpetuating the severe immune exhaustion phenotype observed in our cohort.

Study limitations and strengths: This study has several limitations. Its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the relationship between immune phenotypes and infection risk. The single-center setting may affect generalizability, and despite rigorous phenotyping, we did not perform functional assays on the exhausted T cell populations. Furthermore, while we correlated cytokine levels with CD4 strata (Table 2), a direct comparison of cytokines between the AIDS group with fungal vs. non-fungal co-infections was not performed; such an analysis could provide further mechanistic insight in future longitudinal studies. Despite these limitations, our study possesses notable strengths, including a well-phenotyped cohort, the use of stringent FDR correction for multiple comparisons, a systematic head-to-head comparison of fungal and non-fungal OIs within the same advanced disease context, and the analysis of clinically actionable, routinely available laboratory parameters.

Conclusion

In summary, our study elucidates the multi-stage pathway of immune deterioration in HIV progression and identifies the distinct immunological exhaustion phenotype associated with fungal co-infections. These findings not only deepen our understanding of the pathophysiology of HIV–OI interactions but also provide a scientific basis for precision risk stratification and the development of novel immunotherapeutic strategies in clinical practice.
