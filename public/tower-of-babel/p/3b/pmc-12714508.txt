Patient-Specific Virtual Patch Planning Workflow for a Hypoplastic Aortic Arch

History of Presentation

Our patient was a preterm female infant (born at 34 weeks of gestation) with prenatal diagnosis of aortic arch hypoplasia (AAH), coarctation, and suspected duodenal atresia. A postnatal transthoracic echocardiogram (echo) confirmed long-segment transverse AAH, a bicuspid aortic valve, and a small perimembranous ventricular septal defect. A computed tomography angiogram showed juxtaductal coarctation with a posterior shelf just distal to the origin of the left subclavian artery and a triangular-shaped aortic arch with a sharp angle between the ascending aorta and the transverse arch with long-segment AAH (3 × 3 mm, Z-score: −3) (Figure 1, Video 1). Because of low birth weight (1.41 kg) and comorbidities, she was medically managed with prostaglandins until 1.8 kg. At 2 months of age, her repeat echo showed a worsening arch gradient of 50 mm Hg (previously 33 mm Hg) and mildly reduced left ventricular function (ejection fraction: 50%). The decision was made to proceed with median sternotomy and arch augmentation.

Investigations

Pertinent anatomical structures were segmented from the previously acquired computed tomography angiogram. Using our in-house virtual reality (VR) platform (VR3S), the surgeon visualized the anatomy and considered various surgical reconstructions. All-autologous techniques were less suitable in this case because of associated long-segment transverse AAH and the triangular shape of the arch. Patch augmentation allows uniform enlargement of the entire hypoplastic segment and intentional remodeling of the arch curvature, thereby minimizing anastomotic tension and risk of residual obstruction.1 After deciding on aortic advancement and patch augmentation, incision lines were drawn on the 3-dimensional aorta model in VR. The model was then virtually cut open along those lines, and a virtual patch was created that would mimic the actual size and curvature of the reconstructed patch intraoperatively. Once approved by the surgeon, the virtual patch was flattened into a 2-dimensional surface, and a 10% extra margin was added to accommodate the suture line (Figure 2, Video 2).

Interventions

Under general anesthesia, arch augmentation was performed through median sternotomy, cardiopulmonary bypass, and moderate hypothermia using antegrade cerebral and lower body arterial perfusion. After controlling the arch vessels, the descending aorta, and the ductus, an aortic cross clamp was applied and antegrade cardioplegia was administered. Coarctectomy and ductal division were performed. The remaining transverse arch was incised onto the mid-ascending aorta. Based on intraoperative findings, a slightly modified version of the virtual patch was fashioned from a pulmonary homograft and sewn to the posterior wall of the aortic arch. The descending aorta was brought up to the underside of the arch and sutured 3-quarters of the way circumferentially. Finally, the anterior descending aorta was incised, and the remaining homograft patch was sutured. Echo showed no transverse arch or descending aortic obstruction with a normal flow pattern.

Follow-Up

Six-month follow-up echo showed a moderately dilated transverse aortic arch that tapers to normal dimensions at the level of the aortic isthmus. There was no residual gradient (Figure 3).

Discussion

There is variable consensus on how to manage coarctation with associated AAH, and the recent clinical practice guidelines by the Society of Thoracic Surgeons highlight that. The heterogeneity stems from how AAH is defined, imaged, and quantified across studies. This leads to discrepancies in the surgical approach, risk stratification, and reported outcomes.2 With recent advancement in automated image segmentation and labeling, virtual surgical planning for long-segment AAH repair can facilitate intraoperative decision-making, enabling precise preoperative visualization and a tailored personalized surgical approach.
