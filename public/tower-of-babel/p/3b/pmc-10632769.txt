Giant isolated profunda femoris artery aneurysm treated with aneurysmal ligation without profunda femoris artery revascularization
Authors: Naveed A Rahman, Zachary E Williams, Asad Choudhry, Scott Surowiec
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractIsolated nonruptured profunda femoris artery (PFA) aneurysms are exceedingly rare. We present the case of an 80-year-old man who initially presented with a large pulsatile mass in the right groin. Computed tomography angiography identified a giant 6-cm PFA aneurysm. This was a truly isolated PFA aneurysm without any evidence of concomitant abdominal aortic or popliteal aneurysms. Potential surgical options included reconstruction with an interposition graft, endovascular coil embolization, and aneurysmal ligation. We elected to perform aneurysmal ligation of the PFA aneurysm without revascularization given the aneurysm's massive size and patency of the superficial femoral artery.Keywords: Aneurysm, Femoris artery aneurysm, Giant aneurysm, Profunda

True aneurysmal development isolated to the profunda femoris artery (PFA) is an extraordinarily rare condition, comprising just 0.5% of all peripheral artery aneurysms.1 These cases are typically asymptomatic but can produce symptoms such as distal edema, acute limb ischemia, and thromboembolism preceding aneurysmal rupture in severe cases.1,2 PFA aneurysms are often discovered incidentally given their asymptomatic nature and typically occur in conjunction with polyaneurysmal disease.3 In the present case, the PFA aneurysm was classified as a type IV femoral artery aneurysm according to the classification reported by Amer et al,4 being limited solely to the PFA. Type IV femoral artery aneurysms are the rarest type, accounting for only 5% of femoral artery aneurysms.4 We report our experience managing a true giant PFA aneurysm via surgical intervention. The patient provided written informed consent for the report of his case details and imaging studies.

Case report

An 80-year-old male veteran with no prior trauma or operative procedures presented to the outpatient clinic with a palpable mass in the right groin area and associated thigh paresthesia. Subsequent imaging via computed tomography angiography revealed a 5.9 × 6-cm right PFA aneurysm with arteriomegaly (Figs 1 and 2). No evidence of common femoral artery involvement was found. A small right internal iliac artery aneurysm with patent flow was also elucidated on imaging. Because this giant PFA aneurysm measured approximately twice the typical threshold for operative repair, we elected for immediate open surgical excision of the aneurysm.

Three-dimensional reconstruction of computed tomography angiogram of isolated profunda femoris artery aneurysm (PFA; A).

We obtained proximal control of the right common femoral and superficial femoral arteries via a right groin incision. The neck of the PFA was controlled with a Rummel tourniquet, and additional branches were controlled using vessel loops. The femoral nerve was identified and preserved. The PFA aneurysm sac was completely dissected out, and we were able to establish proximal and distal control (Fig 3). The distension was not suspected to be mycotic in origin because no pus or drainage was appreciated on dissection nor at the final pathologic assessment. The patient's history also provided no evidence of prior arterial access that would substantiate suspicions of systemic infection. We incised the aneurysmal sac and found dense mural thrombi with no back bleeding (Fig 4). The sac contents were extracted and sent for pathologic examination, which returned negative infectious workup findings. The sac was plicated with 3-0 Prolene sutures in running fashion, and the aneurysmal neck was ligated in a similar manner (Fig 5). Revascularization of the PFA was not performed. Hemostasis was obtained, and palpable distal pulses were noted. The patient was discharged the following day and reported symptom resolution with good healing of the incision. The PFA aneurysm sac was found to have regressed in size and was completely excluded at his follow-up visit.

Discussion

The rarity of aneurysms confined solely to the PFA cannot be overstated, because these specific classifications comprise only 1% to 2.6% of all femoral aneurysms.2 These aneurysms are most commonly identified in male patients presenting at an age >70 years with a history of hypertension and long-term tobacco consumption.3,5 True PFA aneurysms often occur concurrently in patients with synchronous abdominal and popliteal aneurysms and are present in 65% to 75% of diagnosed cases.2,6 This unique PFA aneurysm was not associated with either abdominal or popliteal aneurysms, because only an additional concomitant aneurysm in the right iliac artery was found. The potential symptoms associated with considerable PFA aneurysms include limb pain, paresthesia, and deep vein thrombosis from local venous compression due to an aneurysmal mass effect.2,7 Our patient reported limb pain and paresthesia symptoms anteceding ligation, although all were alleviated after treatment.

Multiple imaging modalities, including Doppler ultrasound, computed tomography angiography, and magnetic resonance angiography, exist to substantiate a presumed PFA aneurysm diagnosis.8 Cases of suspected aneurysmal development in the PFA are most commonly investigated using computed tomography angiography as the primary imaging modality. Computed tomography angiography is effective in identifying aneurysms located deep within tissue and assisting with detecting incidental aneurysms outside the area of focus.9 Surgical intervention is recommended for most patients able to tolerate the procedure, especially those with PFA aneurysms measuring >3.5 cm in diameter because of the heightened complication risk.10 The options for surgical treatment include ligation and resection of the aneurysm, with or without revascularization, venous autografting, embolization, and endovascular therapies.11 Because these particular pathologic variants are difficult to detect, allowing them to overdevelop and reach extreme sizes, they are conventionally treated via open ligation.1,9 For patients with distal perfusion to the lower extremity maintained via the superficial femoral artery (SFA) and collateral vessels, especially when the pathology is isolated exclusively to the PFA, arterial reconstruction becomes nonessential.2,12 We elected against implementing a common femoral interposition graft to revascularize the PFA, because no suitable distal targets for bypass were present. This aneurysm was large and incorporated the entire PFA, from its origin down to the distal branch points, as noted in the images. We concluded that proximal ligation of the PFA would be sufficient owing to the patient's considerably sized SFA, which measured >1 cm in size with present inline flow and triple vessel runoff to the foot. Furthermore, a lateral PFA branch, which diverged proximally to the aneurysm, was left intact. Prior literature has also demonstrated the success of PFA aneurysm treatment via proximal ligation, specifically for cases involving a patent SFA.1,12,13 Patency of the SFA was observed on preoperative imaging of our patient, and his distal pulses were palpable on examination, further substantiating our treatment decision.

Conclusions

True PFA aneurysms are elusive, both within the population and on clinical observation. Proper recognition and treatment of these pathologies is essential before the onset of distal limb complications. We report the case of an 80-year-old man who presented with a massive right PFA aneurysm causing pain and thigh paresthesia. The unique aspects of this case include that this was a truly isolated PFA aneurysm without any common femoral artery involvement. We further demonstrated that this PFA aneurysm can be effectively treated with aneurysmectomy and ligation without revascularization, provided that the blood flow through the SFA and distal vessels remains uninhibited.
