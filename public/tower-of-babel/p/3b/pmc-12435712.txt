A Comprehensive Review of Epidemiology, Environmental Factors, and Control Strategies for Zoonotic Cutaneous Leishmaniasis in Iran: Challenges and Prospects
Authors: Mohammad Hossein Feiz-Haddad, Soleiman Khorami
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Zoonotic cutaneous leishmaniasis (ZCL) remains a significant public health challenge in Iran, which reported an incidence rate of 15.8 cases per 100,000 individuals in 2019. Among the countries of the Eastern Mediterranean region, Iran bears a disproportionate burden of this neglected tropical disease, with 18 out of its 31 provinces affected. ZCL is especially prevalent in rural and semiurban areas, where ecological and environmental factors create suitable conditions for disease transmission. Rodent reservoirs, particularly Meriones species, and specific vectors, such as Phlebotomus sandflies, play critical roles in the epidemiology of ZCL in Iran. Recent studies have highlighted dynamic changes in the distribution of ZCL in Iran, with newly identified hotspots in regions such as Beyza district (Fars province), Isfahan, and Razavi Khorasan. These shifts are attributed to climate change, urbanization, and altered land use patterns, which influence vector habitats and reservoir dynamics. Notably, the COVID-19 pandemic has exacerbated the spread of ZCL, redirecting health priorities and increasing the vulnerability of already high-risk areas. The interplay between these ecological factors underscores the importance of understanding localized transmission patterns. Despite the growing prevalence of ZCL, research remains limited in its focus on epidemiological aspects specific to Iran. This review aims to address this gap by examining the geographic distribution, reservoirs, vectors, and ecological drivers of ZCL within Iran. By identifying high-risk provinces and emerging trends, such as those in Fars, Isfahan, and Razavi Khorasan, this study provides a foundation for designing targeted control strategies. Effective interventions, informed by these findings, are crucial in mitigating the future burden of ZCL, particularly as vector habitats are expected to expand due to climatic changes.

INTRODUCTION

Leishmaniasis is a significant global health issue impacting over 350 million individuals across 98 countries. Annually, new cases of cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL) are recorded in worldwide. Iran is among the nations heavily burdened by this disease. The primary forms of CL in Iran are zoonotic cutaneous leishmaniasis (ZCL) and anthroponotic cutaneous leishmaniasis (ACL), which are prevalent in distinct areas. Recognizing the CL is essential for crafting potent prohibition and control measures. The World Health Organization identifies Leishmania major as the main reason for ZCL in Iran, while Leishmania tropica drives ACL. Additionally, Iran has faced significant public health challenges due to the COVID-19 pandemic, which has further strained efforts to manage leishmaniasis. A thorough awareness of the disease’s epidemiology and transmission dynamics in Iran is essential for developing targeted interventions to reduce its impact on the population [Figure 1].[123]

Phlebotomus sandflies serve as the primary vectors for CL transmission in Iran. The disease is reported in multiple regions, including the provinces of Isfahan, Fars, Khorasan, Khuzestan, and Kerman. Other notable CL-endemic areas include parts of Mazandaran, Ilam, Bushehr, Hormozgan, Sarkhs, Kashmar, Lotfabad, Kashan, and Damghan. ZCL is particularly common in rural settings, while epidemiological shifts, such as the emergence of new endemic zones and the disease’s expansion into previously unaffected areas, have been noted in various studies. Urban growth in major cities like Tehran, Isfahan, Shiraz, Mashhad, Tabriz, and Ahvaz, driven by rural-to-urban migration, has increased the need to monitor the spread of CL in these densely populated areas. Of Iran’s 31 provinces, 18 have regions where CL is prevalent, with ZCL accounting for approximately 80% of cases and ACL making up the remaining 20%.[456]

Iran is a key region in Asia where CL is widespread, with a recent rise in cases and the disease’s spread to new areas. Given the multifaceted risk factors, prioritizing effective public health strategies is critical to managing existing cases and preventing further spread.[78]

ZCL poses a major health challenge in Iran’s rural areas, where wild rodents, such as gerbils, act as significant reservoirs for this vector-borne disease. Studies indicate that 97.8% of ZCL cases are found in dry and semidry areas below 725 meters in elevation, often in areas with suboptimal living conditions. Identifying these high-risk zones is essential for implementing targeted interventions and allocating resources to curb ZCL transmission.[89]

Iran has consistently included leishmaniasis control in its national health strategies. However, despite national and international efforts, the disease remains uncontrolled, with new outbreaks and geographic expansion reported. Understanding the epidemiological profile of CL is critical for designing effective control programs to enhance their success.

ZCL is prevalent across many parts of Iran, with rural Jahrom hosting key animal reservoirs like Tatera indica and Meriones persicus. In urban Jahrom, Mus musculus and Rattus rattus also contribute to disease persistence, though to a lesser extent.[101112131415161718192021]

Globally, ZCL is a pressing public health concern, particularly in the Middle East. The interplay between vectors like the sandfly Phlebotomus papatasi, animal reservoirs, humans, and environmental factors complicates disease control. Understanding these dynamics is key to managing this neglected tropical disease.[14]

The ZCL, caused by the parasite Leishmania major, is a prevalent health concern in Iran. Transmission to humans often occurs through close proximity to infected animals, particularly rodents, which serve as primary reservoir hosts. Four gerbil species—Rhombomys opimus, Meriones libycus, Meriones hurrianae, and Tatera indica—are the main hosts for ZCL in various regions of Iran, with Nesokia indica also showing notable infection rates.[22] Research has mapped the spread of these reservoir hosts across Iran, highlighting the influence of climatic and geographical factors on their habitats.[23] Given the impact of climate change, these predictive models require regular updates with fresh data to support effective disease monitoring and control programs.[24]

Rodents are the primary vectors for ZCL transmission. Studies initiated in 1953 in northeastern Iran identified Rhombomys opimus, Meriones libycus, and Tatera indica as key species in disease spread. These studies detected Leishmania major, L. gerbilli, and L. turanica in wild gerbil populations, with L. major being the only strain pathogenic to humans, underscoring the critical link between these rodents and human infections.[2526]

The geographical range of ZCL in Iran has been expanding, with new endemic areas emerging, such as the Beyza district in Fars province and Aran and Bidgol in Isfahan province, indicating a growing public health challenge in both southern and central Iran.[27] These findings emphasize the need for targeted interventions to control rodent populations and sand flies to prevent further spread. Inadequate or poorly coordinated control measures may drive rodents toward human settlements, potentially sparking new infection waves. Environmental factors, including rodent density, temperature, rainfall, and humidity, significantly influence ZCL prevalence, with a noticeable time lag in their impact.[28] The disease is widespread across 18 Iranian provinces, including Fars, Isfahan, Kerman, Khuzestan, and Lorestan, affecting both rural and urban communities. ZCL cases typically surge in autumn, aligning with peak sand fly behavior, suggesting that preventive actions should be prioritized earlier in the year. Economic challenges, such as poverty, displacement, and inadequate infrastructure, further exacerbate ZCL transmission risks.

METHODOLOGY

This analysis gathers information from scientific research and reports concerning ZCL in Iran, with a focus on studies published from 2012 to 2023. The objective is to evaluate patterns in ZCL epidemiology, explore factors contributing to its transmission, and examine the behavior of its vectors and reservoirs in Iran’s endemic areas. The study is grounded in an extensive review of reputable databases such as PubMed, Scopus, ScienceDirect, Web of Science, and Google Scholar, incorporating only pertinent, peer-reviewed publications.

Literature search strategy

To compile relevant data on ZCL in Iran, a systematic search was conducted using controlled vocabulary and keyword queries across selected databases. Key terms such as “Zoonotic Cutaneous Leishmaniasis,” “Iran,” “Epidemiology,” “Vector,” and “Reservoir” were employed, combined with Boolean operators to enhance search precision. The following search strategies were implemented across various databases:

PubMed: The query included “Zoonotic Cutaneous Leishmaniasis AND Epidemiology AND Iran,” restricted to publications from 2012 to 2023.

Scopus: Searches targeted titles containing “Zoonotic Cutaneous Leishmaniasis,” with results filtered to articles published between 2012 and 2023.

ScienceDirect: The focus was on articles addressing ZCL epidemiology, limited to the 2012–2023 timeframe.

Web of Science: Queries used “Epidemiology of Zoonotic Cutaneous Leishmaniasis,” with filters for language and research domain.

Google Scholar: Searches emphasized ZCL, its vectors, and reservoirs in Iran, prioritizing relevant studies.

The goal was to gather all pertinent studies on ZCL in Iran, excluding irrelevant research or publications outside the 2012–2023 period.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Studies included were English-language publications from 2012 to 2023 that addressed the epidemiology, vectors, or reservoirs of ZCL in Iran. Excluded were non-peer-reviewed works, non-English articles, studies published outside 2012–2023, or those lacking relevant ZCL data. Research on other leishmaniasis forms or unrelated topics was also omitted.

Data extraction

Information was gathered from the chosen studies, encompassing details like study location, sample size, ZCL prevalence, and the identified vector and reservoir species. Notable findings were recorded, emphasizing the impact of environmental and human factors on the disease’s transmission.

Screening procedure

The screening process adhered to rigorous inclusion and exclusion criteria. Out of 123 initially reviewed articles, 96 studies satisfied the criteria for inclusion. Two authors independently evaluated each article, addressing any discrepancies through discussion.

The findings

To accurately detect Leishmania species in sandfly vectors, it is crucial to map their distribution and identify the specific Leishmania species prevalent in Iran’s endemic areas. Hedgehogs, common rodents, and feral rodents are recognized as potential reservoir hosts for ZCL. ZCL poses a growing public health challenge in Iran, driven by the country’s distinct climate and socioeconomic conditions. Several provinces, including Isfahan, Yazd, Fars, Khuzestan, Ilam, Kerman, Golestan, Semnan, and Sistan va Baluchestan, are highly endemic for ZCL. Rural populations, especially those engaged in agriculture or residing near forests and animal reservoirs, face the highest risk. Inadequate housing and limited preventive measures further elevate transmission risks in these communities.[91622242930]

ZCL cases in Iran exhibit regional differences, with higher incidence in arid and semiarid regions. Annual case fluctuations suggest environmental factors significantly influence sandfly populations and disease transmission. Young males are primarily affected, likely due to greater exposure to sandfly habitats through outdoor activities. Research consistently identifies rodents as key reservoir hosts, underscoring the complex epidemiology of ZCL. Following a major outbreak in 2013, vector identification efforts intensified in emerging endemic areas like Beiza District, using sticky traps and aspirating tubes, emphasizing the need for ongoing surveillance and improved diagnostic tools for human and animal infections.

Geographical analysis indicates ZCL’s potential expansion in northwest Iran. Climate change projections suggest that previously unsuitable areas may become conducive to ZCL transmission, necessitating early detection and adaptive control measures. Studies in northeastern Iran, particularly Razavi Khorasan province, reveal an expanding disease distribution, highlighting the need for long-term ecological research to understand reservoir host and sandfly vector behavior. This knowledge is vital for predicting outbreaks and implementing targeted interventions. Factors such as deforestation, urbanization, and climate change are altering sandfly and reservoir host habitats, further complicating disease dynamics.[83132333435363738]

In Iran, several rodent species, including Rhombomys opimus, Meriones libycus, Meriones persicus, Tatera indica, and Nesokia indica, serve as reservoir hosts for ZCL, sustaining its zoonotic cycle. In provinces like Golestan, Isfahan, and Fars, these rodents exhibit varying infection rates with Leishmania major and Leishmania turanica. Notably, Rhombomys opimus and Meriones libycus show elevated infection rates, with one study in an endemic province reporting 18.4% and 13.3%, respectively, highlighting their critical role in ZCL transmission dynamics.

Understanding the ecological context of these reservoirs is essential for predicting outbreaks. Research on vector behavior, particularly Phlebotomus papatasi, and the influence of climate, terrain, human activities, and urbanization is vital for devising proactive control measures. Continuous monitoring and updated disease models are necessary to address changing environmental and climatic conditions.

Public health interventions in Iran can reduce ZCL prevalence through vector control, monitoring reservoir host populations, and educating communities on avoiding vector contact. These preventive strategies are crucial for curbing ZCL transmission.

Phlebotomus papatasi is a primary ZCL vector in Iran, with rodents like Rhombomys opimus, Meriones libycus, and Psammomys obesus playing significant roles in transmission. Emerging carriers, such as hedgehogs and calomyscid rodents, indicate evolving transmission dynamics. DISCUSSION

The ZCL remains a major societal health problem in Iran, reflecting its prevalence across the Middle East and Southwest Asia. Caused primarily by Leishmania major and transmitted by the sandfly Phlebotomus papatasi, ZCL persists due to ecological and climatic conditions that support its vectors and reservoirs. This study highlights the importance of thorough epidemiological research and targeted interventions to control its spread and reduce its impact.

Patterns of spread and emerging endemic areas

Recent research highlights a concerning expansion of ZCL in Iran, with new endemic areas emerging alongside established ones. Provinces such as Fars (notably Beyza, Jahrom, and Darab), Isfahan (Aran and Bidgol), and Razavi Khorasan (Kashmar and Neyshabur) have seen significant case increases, while Pakdasht in Tehran Province has emerged as a high-risk area. This spread is driven by climate change, urbanization, and human migration, which reshape habitats for sandfly vectors and rodent reservoirs.[9183867]

Mapping data indicate that Fars reports the highest annual ZCL cases (>1,000), followed by moderate incidence in Khuzestan, Isfahan, and Sistan va Baluchestan (500–1,000 cases annually). Provinces like Yazd, Semnan, and Golestan record lower rates (100–500 cases annually), though underreporting and inadequate surveillance systems may skew these figures. These findings underscore the critical need for enhanced monitoring and region-specific public health strategies.[3968]

Environmental and climatic factors are pivotal in ZCL transmission. Temperature, vegetation, and proximity to rodent burrows heavily impact vector and reservoir distribution. Central and southeastern provinces like Isfahan, Fars, and Khuzestan are especially prone due to conditions favoring sandfly proliferation. Predictive modeling incorporating these elements is crucial for crafting effective control strategies.[89]

Key vectors and reservoirs

Phlebotomus papatasi is the primary vector in Iran, transmitting Leishmania major to humans. Key rodent reservoirs, including Rhombomys opimus (great gerbil), Meriones libycus (Libyan jird), Tatera indica (Indian bush rat), and Nesokia indica (short-tailed bandicoot rat), frequently harbor coinfections of L. major and L. turanica. This coinfection complicates ZCL dynamics and heightens transmission risks.[319] Provinces such as Golestan, Isfahan, Yazd, Fars, and Khuzestan report the highest concentrations of these reservoir species, correlating with ZCL incidence rates.

Control challenges and the role of human activity

Human activities such as urbanization and agricultural expansion intensify ZCL transmission by modifying vector habitats and increasing human exposure. In certain regions, ZCL spread is associated with human migration and environmental changes. For instance, northwest Iran is increasingly suitable for ZCL vectors and reservoirs due to shifting climatic conditions.

Broader implications and future directions

The ZCL patterns in Iran reflect broader regional trends, underscoring their sensitivity to climate and ecological shifts. Expanding research to address socioeconomic impacts, enhance diagnostic tools, and improve treatment options is essential. Strengthening surveillance systems to collect accurate incidence data will support effective resource allocation and intervention planning.

This study emphasizes the urgent need for ongoing monitoring and adaptive strategies to reduce ZCL’s growing burden in Iran. By tackling environmental and climatic drivers, bolstering public health infrastructure, and promoting international collaboration, policymakers can effectively address this neglected tropical disease and safeguard at-risk communities.

Limitations

Difference in data standard and reporting across Iran’s area may skew epidemiological assessments, highlighting the critical requirement for improved data collection and reporting practices to bolster future ZCL management efforts. This study primarily focused on Iran. Additionally, the lack of comprehensive research from many endemic areas, especially outside Iran, resulted in some regions having no studies that met the inclusion criteria for this review.

CONCLUSION

The ZCL continues to pose an important public health issue in Iran, shaped by a complex array of environmental, biological, and socioeconomic determinants. Effective disease management necessitates a complete plan that integrates vector control, reservoir management, and community education. The trajectory of ZCL in Iran differs from global trends due to distinctive environmental shifts, political complexities, and rapid urbanization. Consequently, ongoing surveillance and adaptation of control strategies are imperative as epidemiological dynamics evolve.

By translating the key insights from this study into actionable policies, Iranian decision-makers can formulate evidence-informed interventions aimed at curtailing the spread of ZCL and reducing its impact on affected populations. This review underscores the pivotal function of ecological and climatic factors in disease transmission, particularly in regions like Iran, and advocates for region-specific control measures bolstered by predictive modeling to preempt and mitigate disease propagation.

ZCL should be regarded not only as a medical challenge but also as a socioeconomic and public health priority. Efforts in research, prevention, and control should be coordinated across disciplines to alleviate the burden on vulnerable communities in endemic areas. A thorough understanding of the disease ecology—including reservoirs, vectors, and ecological interactions—is vital for the development of successful control strategies.

Given the intricate transmission ecology of ZCL, a holistic approach is required—one that addresses reservoir hosts, sandfly populations, community behaviors, and environmental factors within a One Health framework. Considering the influence of climate change and environmental variability on disease dynamics, adaptive management and continuous monitoring are particularly crucial in Iran.

Finally, to address the gaps identified in this study, further research is warranted. Priorities should include vaccine creation, enhanced evaluative methodologies, specific mapping of ZCL hotspots, and a deeper understanding of vector behavioral patterns. Moreover, future investigations should examine the sociopolitical and financial dimensions that are consequences of expansion of ZCL both within Iran and globally.
