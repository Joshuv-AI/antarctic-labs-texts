Neighborhood air quality, psychobiological reactivity, and mental health across adolescence
Authors: Jessica L. Buthmann, Ian H. Gotlib
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Researchers are paying increasing attention to the environmental aspects of the neighborhood in which children and adolescents are raised and their role in psychobiological development. Factors that are distal to the home environment, such as green spaces and pollution levels, have been shown to be associated with mental health in youth, even when accounting for variance explained by more proximal home factors (e.g., family socioeconomic status). Researchers have posited that individuals vary in their susceptibility to adverse conditions, which can be operationalized as psychobiological reactivity; this formulation, however, has not been tested with respect to air quality. Here, we assessed internalizing and externalizing symptoms in 133 adolescents at four time points, each approximately two years apart, beginning in early puberty. Psychobiological reactivity was assessed during the Trier Social Stress Test. We found that adolescents living in neighborhoods with poorer air quality, measured by particulate matter, ozone levels, and days with temperatures over 90°F, had higher levels of internalizing and externalizing symptoms if they were also less psychobiologically reactive. These findings support the formulation that levels of psychobiological reactivity index susceptibility to the environment and highlight the importance of reducing air pollution to help promote optimal adolescent health.

A growing literature is documenting associations between aspects of the community in which youth live and their subsequent psychological well-being. In particular, researchers have described associations of neighborhood socioeconomic disadvantage (NSD) with psychopathology (Elovainio et al. 2020; Gard et al. 2021), although null findings have also been reported (Gresko et al. 2024; King & Waldman 2024). These inconsistent findings may be due, in part, to the fact that many aspects of the community are related to NSD, including pollution, greenspaces, and community safety, any or all of which may contribute to mental health (Burt 2022). While NSD is frequently used as a proxy for a host of community characteristics, it may be more informative to focus on specific components of NSD.

In this context, although air quality is considered to be one metric of NSD, it may have unique properties that warrant investigation independent of other aspects of neighborhood disadvantage. Socioeconomically disadvantaged communities often have more sources of pollution than do their more advantaged counterparts (i.e., industrial sites, traffic, waste facilities; Cushing et al. 2015; Nunez et al. 2024; Pratt et al. 2015); it is important to recognize, however, that air quality in a community can worsen when wildfire smoke carries to the region from other places or when temperatures reach extreme highs, independent of NSD (Reid et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2017). Further, air pollution may affect psychological well-being directly through numerous biological systems (e.g., immune, endocrine, and cardiovascular systems; see Miller et al. 2016; Thomson 2019), in contrast to the more indirect pathways through which NSD can influence well-being, such as lack of resources, educational opportunities, or low community safety.

Poor air quality has been documented to have wide-ranging effects on mental health; researchers have found that exposure to air pollution is associated with more externalizing symptoms in children (Qi et al. 2023; Smolker et al. 2024), with more depressive symptoms in adolescents (Manczak et al. 2022; Roberts et al. 2019), and with higher rates of hospitalizations for mental health problems in adults (Nguyen et al. 2021; Qiu et al. 2022). Importantly, however, not everyone exposed to poor air quality develops mental health difficulties (Ezpeleta et al. 2007). In this context, investigators working from several frameworks based in evolutionary adaptation theories (e.g., biological sensitivity to context and differential susceptibility) posit that some individuals are more psychobiologically reactive than others, leading them to be more likely to be affected by characteristics of the environment (e.g., Belsky & Pluess 2009; Boyce & Ellis 2005). In tests of this formulation, researchers have found that adolescents living in neighborhoods with greater NSD are more vulnerable to developing symptoms of psychopathology if they are more psychobiologically reactive than their peers (e.g., Cai et al. 2024; Fletcher et al. 2019); interestingly, however, other investigators have found that this vulnerability is indexed by lower levels of reactivity (Portnoy et al. 2020). These ostensibly equivocal findings are consistent with the adaptive calibration model, which proposes that high reactivity increases adolescents’ risk for internalizing symptoms in the context of adverse environments, whereas low reactivity increases their risk for externalizing symptoms (Del Giudice et al. 2012; Ellis & Del Giudice 2019). To date, researchers have not tested the formulation that when exposed to poor air quality, some adolescents are more susceptible to mental health difficulties than others because of their different levels of psychobiological reactivity. Highlighting the potential importance of air quality in this formulation, Gao et al. (2023) recently found that adults with a stronger genetic risk score who were exposed to poorer air quality were at higher risk for developing mental health difficulties.

Examining differential susceptibility to poor air quality across adolescence is particularly important given the dynamic developmental changes in psychobiological functioning that occur during this time. Adolescence is a period during which symptoms of psychopathology emerge amid a changing social and biological context (Merikangas et al. 2010). Puberty-related increases in gonadal hormones are associated with changes in the hypothalamic-adrenal-gonadal (HPG) axis that can affect stress reactivity in adolescents (Romeo 2010). Characterizing changes in mental health symptoms from adolescence through early adulthood as a function of poor air quality exposure and level of stress reactivity will help to elucidate the interactive roles of these two constructs in placing adolescents at increased risk for developing symptoms of psychopathology.

In the current study, we examined associations between neighborhood air quality and mental health symptoms across adolescence as a function of individuals’ level of reactivity to social stress. We predicted broadly that adolescents who live in neighborhoods with poorer air quality will have larger increases in internalizing and externalizing symptoms. More specifically, however, drawing on Del Giudice’s (Del Giudice et al. 2012; Ellis & Del Giudice 2019) findings, we predicted that among adolescents living in neighborhoods with poorer air quality, those who are more psychobiologically reactive will have greater increases in internalizing symptoms across adolescence, whereas those who are less reactive will have greater increases in externalizing symptoms.

Methods

Participants

We obtained data from adolescents at four time points approximately two years apart, beginning in early puberty. Participants who were fluent in English, had no contraindications for participating in neuroimaging procedures, had no serious psychiatric conditions, and were in the early stages of puberty were recruited via online and print advertisements. Participants gave informed written assent and their legal guardians gave written informed consent for participation in this study. All study procedures were approved by the Stanford University Institutional Review Board.

At baseline, 229 participants were recruited (Mage = 11.46, SD = 1.06, 58.7% assigned female at birth). A social stress test (see below) was administered to 169 participants at baseline or at a follow-up assessment 2 years later; of these participants, 15 had data that were not suitable for analysis due to data artifacts in skin conductance (n = 10), heart rate (n = 6), or cortisol data (n = 5), yielding a sample of 154 participants with complete social stress data. Measures of emotional and behavioral problems were completed by 171 participants at baseline, by 160 participants at the second time point approximately two years later, by 157 participants at the third time point approximately two years after that, and by 136 participants at the fourth time point approximately seven years after baseline. One hundred thirty-six participants had complete data, including address information and covariates, available for analysis in the current study (see Supplementary Figure 1 for visualization of participation across timepoints).

Measures

Demographic characteristics

At baseline, participants and their guardians reported their address, sex, race, age, household income, and household size. In addition, we assessed pubertal status by having participants report their Tanner Staging, for which they rate pubertal development on a scale from 1 to 5 based on illustrations of pubic hair and breast/testes growth (Marshall & Tanner 1968).

Early life stress

At baseline, participants were administered the Traumatic Events Screening Interview for Children (Ribbe 1996). This structured interview assesses whether participants experienced 30 potentially traumatic events in their lifetime. Participants reported when and for how long the event occurred, as well as their objective and subjective rating of the severity of the event. Subsequently, a panel of graduate students and postdoctoral researchers, blind to the subjective information provided by the participants about each event, rated the objective severity of the event on a scale from 0 to 5. The objective panel severity rating for each type of event was then summed for analysis in this study.

Psychobiological stress reactivity

Psychobiological reactivity was operationalized as heart rate, heart rate variability, and cortisol reactivity during the Trier Social Stress Test for children and adolescents (Allen et al. 2017; Kirschbaum et al. 1993; Seddon et al. 2020). The TSST was administered individually at baseline to half of the participants and at the second assessment to the other half of the participants. Participants abstained from food and drink for 30 min before providing a saliva sample. The first saliva sample was collected before the TSST began, and electrocardiogram electrodes were applied to the participants. Participants had a five-minute rest period before the TSST began, and then a second saliva sample was collected. An experimenter told the participants the beginning of a story and instructed them to come up with an exciting ending, which would be judged over the next five-minute period. The experimenter judging the story ending maintained a neutral expression, took notes, and told the participants that they would be recorded. Participants then completed a five-minute serial subtracted task in front of the judge, who interrupted them and instructed them to start over if a mistake was made. A third saliva sample was collected after this. Participants then viewed a thirty-minute neutral video, during which two additional saliva samples were collected.

Saliva samples were collected with SalivaBio Children’s Swabs (Salimetrics, LLC) and stored at −20 °C. Samples were assayed for cortisol levels with a high-sensitivity (0.004 μg/dL) immunoassay kit from Immuno-Biological Laboratories Inc. (Hamburg, Germany; intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation 3–5%). Consistent with expert recommendations (Stalder et al. 2016) and our prior work (Kircanski et al. 2019), we winsorized cortisol values for each sample that exceeded 2 standard deviations above the mean (4.12% of the samples). We calculated the area under the curve with respect to increase (AUCi) to assess total cortisol production (Pruessner et al. 2003) for baseline-to-peak stress reactivity (i.e., from Samples 2 to 4) based on our previous work (Kircanski et al. 2019) showing that peak cortisol levels were observed 30 min from stressor onset (i.e., Sample 4) in this sample.

We obtained electrocardiogram data using the electrocardiogram amplifier module and three electrodes in the lead II configuration. Data were recorded at a sampling rate of 500 Hz with the Biopac MP150 system and AcqKnowledge software (Biopac systems, Goleta, CA). Data obtained during the 5-min baseline, storytelling, and 5-min subtraction tasks were divided into 60-s intervals and inspected for artifacts and missing R-peaks. In the case of one missing R-peak, a peak was inserted midway between the two adjacent peaks. In the case of multiple missing R-peaks, the 60-s period was excluded. Heart rate and the root mean squared successive differences (RMSSD) were extracted, with the latter representing heart rate variability (HRV). As a measure of reactivity, we calculated the slope of heart rate and HRV from baseline through the end of the subtraction task.

As reported previously (Buthmann et al. 2025), cortisol, heart rate, and HRV reactivity, along with skin conductance and self-reported mood reactivity, were entered in a principal components analysis. Cortisol (λ = 0.459), heart rate (λ = 0.779), and HRV (λ = −0.495) formed one component (variance explained = 34%), and skin conductance (λ = 0.382) and mood reactivity (λ = 0.318) formed another (variance explained = 22%). We used the primary cortisol and cardiac component as our measure of psychobiological reactivity for analysis in the current study.

Psychopathology symptoms

Participants completed the Youth Self-Report (Achenbach & Rescorla 2001) at each of the four assessments. This self-report questionnaire has 112 items assessing emotional and behavioral problems to which participants respond on a scale from 0 (not true) to 2 (very true). Scores on the anxious/depressed, withdrawn/depressed, and somatic complaints subscales were summed to form an internalizing symptoms score, and scores on the rule-breaking and aggressive behavior subscales were summed to form an externalizing symptoms score. Raw scores were used for analysis.

Neighborhood socioeconomic disadvantage

We geocoded participants’ addresses reported at baseline to their census tracts. The California Communities Environmental Health Screening Tool (CalEnviroScreen 3.0, Faust et al. 2017) developed by the California State Office of Environmental Health Hazard Assessment aggregates data from 2005 to 2016 across the state for a number of socioeconomic indicators and pollutants. We derived scores for each participant’s census tract. Socioeconomic indicators include rates of poverty, linguistic isolation, educational attainment, unemployment, and housing burden. Health indicators include the rate of infants born at low birth weight and hospital admissions for asthma and cardiovascular conditions. NSD was operationalized as the population characteristics score, a composite of the socioeconomic and health indicators.

Air quality

The CalEnviroScreen also includes measures of pollutants, including particulate matter less than 2.5 μm (PM2.5), ozone, particulate matter from diesel, traffic density, drinking water contaminants, groundwater threats, impaired water bodies, pesticide use, toxic release sites, cleanup sites, hazardous waste, and solid waste sites. In addition, we obtained data at the census tract level from the National Environmental Public Health Tracking Network at the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention indexing the number of days per year for which the maximum temperature exceeded 90°F. The number of days meeting this temperature threshold was averaged across years 1999–2020. Given research showing that high temperatures amplify the health risks posed by air quality, we used the average number of days that the temperature exceeded 90°F for the year corresponding to participants’ baseline assessment as well as the CalEnviroScreen ozone and PM2.5 variables to measure air quality. To increase normality, we log transformed ozone, PM2.5, and average days over 90°F and then used a z-score transformation to standardize the scale of the variables. Finally, we summed the scores for these three variables to compute a composite air quality score.

Analysis

We used the mgcv package (Wood 2011) for RStudio to estimate generalized additive models predicting psychopathology symptoms across adolescence. Separate models were estimated for internalizing and externalizing symptoms. Age was centered and entered as a smooth term with restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Smooth terms were applied to the random effects variables and estimated for each participant. We estimated main effects and interaction terms between air quality and stress reactivity, and included income to needs ratio, sex assigned at birth, white race, ELS severity, whether symptoms were reported before (coded 0) or after (coded 1) the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, and NSD as covariates. Race was coded as 1 (white race) or 2 (other racial identity) due to the smaller number of participants in other racial groups, and sex was coded as 0 (male) or 1 (female).

Internalizing symptoms

The overall model predicted 14.0% of the variance in internalizing symptoms (see Table 2). Internalizing symptoms increased significantly as a function of older age (B = 0.72, 95% CI = 0.03, 1.41), greater severity of ELS (B = 0.35, 95% CI = 0.17, 0.54), being assigned female at birth (B = 4.59, 95% CI = 2.76, 6.43), and being a race other than white (B = 2.12, 95% CI = 0.29, 3.95). Household income to needs ratio and NSD were not associated with internalizing symptoms. The interaction between air quality and stress reactivity predicting internalizing symptoms was statistically significant (B = −0.84, 95% CI = −1.37, −0.30), such that participants living in neighborhoods with poor air quality who were also less reactive had more internalizing symptoms (see Fig. 1). The interaction of air quality, stress reactivity, and age was not statistically significant, indicating that the interaction between air quality and stress reactivity did not differ as a function of age. Externalizing symptoms

The overall model predicted 12.5% of the variance in externalizing symptoms (see Table 2). Like internalizing symptoms, externalizing symptoms increased significantly with age (B = 0.67, 95% CI = 0.37, 0.98) and with the severity of ELS (B = 0.41, 95% CI = 0.27, 0.56). Household income to needs ratio and NSD were not associated with externalizing symptoms. The interaction between air quality and stress reactivity was statistically significant (B = −0.68, 95% CI = −1.09, −0.27) but was subsumed by a significant interaction of air quality, stress reactivity, and age (B = −0.20, 95% CI = −0.36, −0.05). Discussion

In this study, we examined the associations among neighborhood air quality, stress reactivity, and symptoms of internalizing and externalizing problems across adolescence in order to test whether susceptibility to poor air quality varied as a function of levels of psychobiological stress reactivity. Contrary to our hypothesis, we found that adolescents living in neighborhoods with poorer air quality who were less, rather than more, psychobiologically reactive reported higher levels of internalizing symptoms, independent of age. Further, adolescents who lived in neighborhoods with poorer air quality and who had lower levels of psychobiological stress reactivity also had larger increases in externalizing symptoms across adolescence. Together, these findings highlight the potential role of the physical environment in shaping psychological well-being in adolescents and underscore the importance of efforts to improve air quality.

We hypothesized that we would find main effects of neighborhood air quality predicting internalizing and externalizing symptoms; however, these effects were not statistically significant. This was unexpected given prior work that has documented higher internalizing symptoms in the context of poorer air quality (Manczak et al. 2022; Roberts et al. 2019). Findings concerning the main effect of air quality on externalizing symptoms have been less consistent, with some researchers finding an effect (Qi et al. 2023) and others reporting null results (Zubizarreta-Arruti et al. 2025). Poor air quality has been associated with increases in oxidative stress, inflammation, and cortisol levels (Li et al. 2017; Miller et al. 2016), which have also been reported among people with internalizing disorders (Belem da Silva et al. 2017; Wadsworth et al. 2019), suggesting the potential for shared mechanisms in the emergence of internalizing symptoms. Further, given that findings of associations between elevated inflammation and cortisol levels are less common among people with externalizing disorders (Flouri et al. 2019; Kushner et al. 2016), it is not surprising that we did not find a main effect of poor air quality predicting externalizing symptoms in this study. The fact that some researchers have found overall NSD to be associated with externalizing symptoms (e.g., Burt et al. 2020) suggests that factors other than air quality that are related to NSD may drive the development of externalizing symptoms. Notably, we did not find in this study that NSD was associated with externalizing or internalizing symptoms. This may be due to the relative affluence of the area from which the sample was drawn or the relatively low endorsement of externalizing symptoms in this community sample of adolescents. Future work with samples of adolescents that are representative of a more socioeconomically diverse population is needed before strong conclusions can be drawn concerning the adverse effects of neighborhood socioeconomic disadvantage.

Notably, we found that participant race was significantly associated with internalizing symptoms, such that white participants had lower symptoms than did those who were not white. We were not powered to examine associations by race in a more specific manner than this broad categorization. Researchers have found that racial and ethnic minorities in the United States report higher levels of internalizing symptoms than do their white counterparts (Hargrove et al. 2020; McLaughlin et al. 2007). This may be related to experiences of discrimination and acculturative stress (Ramos et al. 2022), as well as to disparities in neighborhood disadvantage (Cushing et al. 2015; Nunez et al. 2024). Future studies with larger, more racially heterogeneous and representative samples are needed to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the associations among neighborhood context, stress reactivity, and symptoms of internalizing and externalizing in adolescents with different racial and ethnic backgrounds.

We had hypothesized that adolescents in neighborhoods with poor air quality would have higher levels of internalizing symptoms if they were more psychobiologically reactive and higher levels of externalizing symptoms if they were less reactive. We generated this hypothesis based both on the adaptive calibration model, which posits that individual reactivity levels are an adaptation to adversity (Del Giudice et al. 2012; Ellis & Del Giudice 2019), and on a large literature linking internalizing symptoms with potentiated stress reactivity (Perry et al. 2020) and externalizing symptoms with lower reactivity (Chen et al. 2015; Wade et al. 2024). Our hypothesis with respect to internalizing symptoms was not supported; rather, lower reactivity moderated the association between air quality and internalizing symptoms. Prior work examining the association between reactivity and internalizing symptoms has been mixed. Although some researchers have reported that greater reactivity is linked with internalizing symptoms, others have found that lower reactivity is associated with internalizing symptoms (Daches et al. 2019), especially among women (Zorn et al. 2017). This lack of reactivity may represent an impaired ability to adapt and respond to environmental cues, potentially due to chronic over-activation of the stress response system (Duprey et al. 2021; McLaughlin et al. 2015). Future studies that yield data from repeated measurements of stress reactivity and longer-term environmental exposures may help to elucidate these associations.

Our hypothesis was supported with respect to externalizing symptoms. Specifically, youth who lived in neighborhoods with poor air pollution had larger increases in externalizing symptoms across adolescence if they were also less psychobiologically reactive. In prior work, exposure to both psychosocial (Brindle et al. 2022) and physical (Wing et al. 2018) adverse environments has been associated with lower psychobiological functioning which, in turn, has been shown to predict psychopathology symptoms (Chen et al. 2015; Gao et al. 2015; Portnoy et al. 2020). Thus, there may be common mechanisms that play a role in the development of psychopathology symptoms in the context of both psychosocial and environmental adversities. This may be particularly salient during adolescence, when heightened plasticity and pubertal changes in the context of a dynamic psychosocial landscape pose new challenges for adolescents (Romeo 2010). Adolescents who attempt to navigate these challenges with a diminished capacity to respond to stimuli may develop more externalizing behaviors (e.g., rule-breaking and aggression), especially for those youth who are already experiencing high allostatic load from neighborhood pollutants.

Our finding that lower stress reactivity moderates the association between air quality and internalizing and externalizing symptoms should be considered in the context of several limitations. First, our measure of air quality was temporally coarse and assumes that all adolescents in the same census tract are exposed to the same air quality. Future work should consider the use of air quality sensors that track participant exposure more directly and on a finer timescale and should attempt to assess the use of air filtration systems in homes and schools. Second, our sample was drawn from a relatively affluent community, which may have limited the range of air quality and socioeconomic disadvantage experienced, as well as the number of externalizing symptoms reported. Future studies conducted with participants from a wider range of neighborhoods and backgrounds are necessary to determine the generalizability of our findings. Third, although participant race was a significant variable in our analyses, this variable was binarized (White and not-White) because of the limited representation in each non-White racial group. We were not powered to test differences between non-White groups and caution readers not to generalize our findings to all non-White racial groups. Fourth, we measured psychobiological stress reactivity once, early in adolescence. Although the Trier Social Stress Test cannot be repeated because participants are informed of the actual role of the judges, having additional data points about stress reactivity obtained through other procedures would allow stronger conclusions to be drawn about the association between reactivity and psychopathology symptoms and the nature of their interaction. Finally, our findings are based on adolescents’ self-reports of their internalizing and externalizing symptoms; although we administered widely used and well-validated measures, future studies should examine the effects of air quality and stress reactivity on interview-based diagnosed disorders or incorporate reports of symptoms from third-party informants, such as parents or teachers.

Despite these limitations, our findings underscore the importance of reducing air pollution to help promote optimal adolescent health. Reducing air pollution, increasing green spaces, and encouraging the use of air filtration systems in homes and schools may offset the adverse effects of poor air quality on mental health difficulties in adolescents. Further, screening for abnormal levels of stress reactivity may inform the need for psychosocial intervention aimed at improving regulatory capacity, particularly for youth who are living in more polluted neighborhoods. Future work is needed to probe the epigenetic, molecular, and neural underpinnings of our findings, which will also advance our knowledge of the specific pathways that are affected by poor air quality and inform the development of more effective interventions.
