Decentralized adaptive task allocation for dynamic multi-agent systems
Authors: Elizaveta Tarasova, Victoria Erofeeva, Oleg Granichin, Kirill Chernikov
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We present a decentralized two-layer architecture for dynamic task assignment in multi-agent systems, designed to operate under partial observability, noisy feedback, and limited communication. The system consists of adaptive controllers that predict task parameters via recursive regression with forgetting and selectively broadcast tasks to a small subset of agents based on relevance and availability. To ensure consistency of task models across the network, we introduce a distributed optimization procedure that combines Simultaneous Perturbation Stochastic Approximation (SPSA) with consensus-based synchronization. The proposed approach enables scalable, online task allocation without centralized coordination. As a representative application, we evaluate the system on simulated workloads involving prompt-based tasks assigned to a diverse set of large language models (LLMs), demonstrating its robustness across varying noise levels, task dynamics, and input arrival patterns.

Introduction

Decentralized task allocation in large-scale, heterogeneous multi-agent systems has emerged as a critical challenge in domains ranging from robotic swarms and sensor networks to distributed computing and human–AI collaboration. In such systems, agents independently execute tasks without centralized oversight, enabling resilience to failures, scalability to large populations of agents, and adaptability to dynamic environments. However, achieving efficient and adaptive task distribution under purely decentralized control is nontrivial: agents possess diverse capabilities, tasks exhibit varying urgencies and processing requirements, and timely feedback is often delayed or noisy. Conventional approaches–such as auction-based mechanisms, heuristic protocols, or market-inspired bidding schemes–typically assume static agent performance models or rely on system-wide broadcasts, leading to reduced efficiency and robustness when agent behavior drifts or task types evolve.

Dynamic environments pose three principal challenges for decentralized task allocation:Behavioral drift: Over time, an agent’s processing speed, reliability, or resource availability may change due to wear, load fluctuations, or context shifts. Static performance models fail to capture such non-stationarity, resulting in mismatches between tasks and agents.Non-stationary feedback: Task completion outcomes may not reflect historical patterns–e.g., the same agent may exhibit different success rates under varying network conditions or environmental contexts–requiring continual model updates.Delayed observability: In realistic settings, agents’ contributions and task outcomes are observed with latency, preventing immediate system-wide adaptation and potentially causing local controllers to operate on stale information.Addressing these challenges without resorting to centralized coordination requires lightweight, fully decentralized mechanisms for both adaptation and synchronization. In the next section, we review prior works on decentralized allocation schemes and adaptive coordination strategies.

Related works

Decentralized task allocation in multi-agent systems has been addressed through a wide range of methods. Classical approaches rely on auction-based or rule-driven protocols1–3, which allow agents to make local decisions with limited coordination. More recent developments include hierarchical or streaming strategies4,5, enabling large-scale deployment and partial visibility. Industrial studies have also emphasized ontology-based frameworks for adaptive resource management, where reusable task models support flexibility across domains6. While effective in static or well-specified environments, such methods often lack robustness under dynamic conditions, noisy feedback, or behavioral drift.

Recent advances in consensus control emphasize prescribed-time convergence and event-triggered/self-triggered mechanisms, ensuring guaranteed agreement within finite horizons while reducing communication load. Dynamic and self-triggered protocols have been proposed for nonlinear MAS, achieving prescribed-time consensus without Zeno behavior7. Further extensions address leader-following consensus with input saturation under a time-varying dynamic event-triggered mechanism, providing explicit lower bounds on inter-event intervals8. Related studies have also proposed accumulative-error-based event-triggered schemes for discrete-time linear systems, where looped functional methods enable co-design of control gains and event parameters under input saturation9. These results underline the importance of resource-efficient and resilient coordination, complementary to decentralized task-allocation research.

Taken together, these studies illustrate the breadth of existing approaches to decentralized task allocation, ranging from control-theoretic consensus protocols to market-based negotiation and machine-learning-driven scheduling. To structure the discussion, we next group related work into several methodological categories–fog computing and distributed scheduling, market-inspired coordination, deep reinforcement learning and embedding-based methods, hybrid heuristic and fuzzy logic schemes, metaheuristic optimization, privacy-aware models, and LLM-based multi-agent coordination. This organization highlights both the diversity of solutions and the common limitations, particularly reliance on static assumptions, centralized training, or the absence of continuous adaptation to drift and delayed feedback.

Fog computing and distributed scheduling. The fog-computing paradigm epitomizes the very challenges we target – resource dynamics, unstable performance parameters, and delayed observability – since micro-data centers, routers, and edge devices must self-organize task execution without global coordination10,11. We review insights from fog scheduling–including selective task offloading to reduce broadcast overhead, lightweight local performance models to accommodate behavioral) drift, and intermittent synchronization under communication latency–that are critical for decentralized adaptive task allocation research.

Fully decentralized, multi-agent frameworks have been introduced to handle dynamic workloads and unexpected events in fog environments, avoiding single points of failure by local negotiation and self?coordination12. In the context of the Internet of Underwater Things (IoUT), a hybrid framework has been developed that combines clustering, ant-colony optimization, and a pseudo-trajectory homotopy method for unmanned marine vehicles (UMVs)13. The two-layer architecture with surface and underwater vehicles illustrates distributed task planning under severe communication constraints, which is conceptually close to fog-like environments with intermittent connectivity. In smart manufacturing, hybrid algorithms that combine rule?based heuristics with metaheuristics improve makespan and energy efficiency in fog?enabled pipelines14. A recent taxonomy classifies these methods by objectives–latency, energy, or load balance–highlighting delay and energy minimization as most common11. Machine learning for resource prediction, classification, and adaptive control is increasingly adopted in fog nodes15. Federated and distributed learning extend these gains to wireless?heterogeneous settings, sharing model updates without central aggregation16. Fair task distribution has been modelled as a Distributed Envy Minimization Problem (DEMP), where cooperative algorithms ensure Pareto efficiency while minimizing envy among agents with indivisible resources17.

Most fog scheduling schemes either rely on static heuristics–failing to track long?term performance drift–or incur high communication costs through system?wide broadcasts. For example, dynamic multi?criteria schedulers balance urgency, data size, and complexity but lack decentralized feedback loops and predictive modelling18.

Decentralized market-inspired coordination. We also examine decentralized market?inspired coordination approaches, which apply economic principles to decentralized task allocation. The seminal Contract Net Protocol laid out announce–bid negotiation between task issuers and executors, and has been adapted for smart homes and multi?robot teams19,20. Recent auction schemes focus on scalability and resilience to communication delays. Greedy decentralized algorithms enable real?time bidding with minimal overhead1, while delay?aware frameworks incorporate bid quality and timing into winner selection3. Although auctions reduce conflicts and support heterogeneity, they often require centralized bid aggregation or global broadcasts, creating bottlenecks under high churn or partial observability.

Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) and embedding-based methods. A twin-delayed DDPG model has been proposed for offloading delay-sensitive tasks in static fog topologies, where each agent learns task-to-node mappings based on fixed observability scopes21. GraphSAGE embeddings have been combined with decentralized DRL, but this approach requires a global training phase and prior knowledge of the network graph22. Both methods excel in their narrow settings, but struggle to accommodate changing agent sets and partial visibility in real time. Task allocation has also been formulated as a potential game, where reinforcement learning with graph neural networks enables scalable coordination across large agent populations23. While effective in structured environments, this approach depends on centralized training and full task observability, limiting adaptability in dynamic and partially observable systems.

Hybrid heuristic and fuzzy logic schemes. Hybrid approaches integrate rule?based heuristics or fuzzy logic with metaheuristics or DRL for domain?specific pipelines. For example, fuzzy controllers have been combined with genetic algorithms or DRL for smart manufacturing, typically under the assumption of static network configurations14,24. Extensions to dynamic fog workloads have also been proposed using fuzzy rules, but these approaches lack online learning or feedback-driven adaptation25. These systems optimize makespan or energy use but do not generalize well to fully dynamic, non?preemptive environments.

Metaheuristic and optimization-based algorithms. Metaheuristic methods such as the artificial hummingbird algorithm focus on minimizing global cost metrics under known workload distributions26. DCOP?based schedulers solve constraint networks through precomputed bounds and binary constraints27, while envy?minimization models ensure fairness among agents with indivisible resources17. These techniques provide strong theoretical guarantees but typically require central coordination, offline computation, or persistent agent–task mappings, limiting their applicability in rapidly changing settings.

Privacy-preserving and energy-aware models. Secure task assignment via encrypted search protects sensitive constraints but prioritizes confidentiality over allocation efficiency28. EcoTaskSched integrates CNNs with BiLSTM to capture spatial and temporal workload features, validated on DeFog workloads and an Azure-based COSCO testbed29. While it demonstrates significant energy savings and SLA improvements, it relies on centralized training and fixed task assignment, limiting adaptability to dynamic feedback in decentralized settings. Such approaches are unsuitable when global visibility is unavailable or the environment evolves faster than model retraining cycles.

LLM-based multi-agent coordination. Recent studies have explored the use of Large Language Models (LLMs) as autonomous agents capable of self-organizing task assignments through role prompting and self-selection30. Such frameworks demonstrate the potential of LLMs for distributed negotiation and resource allocation in multi-agent settings. However, the coordination mechanism remains primarily linguistic and high-level, lacking lower-level adaptive control or predictive task modeling under dynamic feedback and partial observability.

Research highlight & contributions

This work addresses the problem of decentralized task allocation in large-scale, heterogeneous agent systems. We propose a novel architecture and supporting algorithms that enable scalable, adaptive, and communication-efficient task allocation without centralized coordination.

Across existing paradigms, three recurring limitations remain unresolved:Static assumptions. Many approaches require fixed network topologies, predefined offloading points, or full observability.Offline training or heavy computation. DRL, embedding, and metaheuristic methods often involve centralized preprocessing or extensive retraining.Lack of continuous adaptation. Fuzzy, heuristic, and optimization algorithms rarely incorporate feedback loops to handle behavioral drift and delayed observability.Our framework directly addresses these limitations through a two-layer decentralized architecture with adaptive controllers and SPSA-based consensus synchronization, enabling fully decentralized, online adaptation in dynamic environments with noisy and delayed feedback.

Specifically, to manage incoming tasks in a decentralized manner, we propose a two-layer architecture. The outer layer consists of adaptive controllers – dedicated decision-making units responsible for analysing tasks, predicting behavioral) parameters (e.g., expected processing time, urgency), and selecting a relevant subset of agents. The controllers do not directly assign tasks; instead, each task is made visible only to a restricted group of agents, who autonomously decide on execution. This limited visibility enables scalability, supports agent heterogeneity, and avoids full system broadcast.

Conventional task allocation mechanisms – such as auction-based schemes and heuristic protocols – tend to degrade in performance when operating in dynamic environments characterized by changing agent capabilities, evolving task types, or inconsistent feedback. These methods typically rely on fixed rules or assumptions about system behavior, making them vulnerable to behavioral drift, where agent performance varies over time; non-stationary feedback, where task outcomes no longer reflect earlier patterns; and delayed observability, where task results are not immediately available for adaptation. In contrast, our framework incorporates mechanisms for real-time adaptation through localized feedback loops, continuous tracking of behavioral parameters, and coordination among controllers. Each controller incrementally updates its predictive models based on the outcomes of tasks it observes, enabling robust performance under uncertainty and change.

To balance the distribution of task-handling responsibilities among controllers, we apply a load balancing strategy based on a consensus protocol. This mechanism provides a lightweight mechanism that assigns tasks to controllers based on real-time estimates of workload and urgency. It operates without centralized coordination and is resilient to changing network conditions, making it well-suited for dynamic, large-scale systems.

Once a task is assigned to a controller, local models are used to predict relevant parameters (such as expected processing time), and agents are scored based on availability and task fit. Behavioral prediction is performed using recursive models with exponential forgetting, which allow each controller to adapt to drift while using only its local history.

To maintain coherence between local models across controllers, a decentralized synchronization mechanism is introduced. Specifically, we employ decentralized SPSA-based consensus31, which allows each controller to refine its prediction parameters via randomized local perturbations and shared function-value observations. This synchronization step ensures that controllers do not diverge in their interpretation of similar task types, despite working under decentralized and noisy conditions. Importantly, the applied SPSA-based consensus method demonstrates consistency under almost arbitrary bounded disturbances, in the sense formalized in32. This robustness property is especially critical in real-time online systems, where tasks arrive continuously and it is often impossible to repeat experiments or accurately characterize the distribution of uncertainties. The importance of developing methods that remain stable and consistent in such non-reproducible, dynamic environments has been further emphasized in our prior work33.

Additionally, we evaluate the system in a large-scale simulated setting involving task generation from an LLM, highlighting potential applications in knowledge work (e.g., document generation, translation, and analytical content creation), content routing, and decentralized language service orchestration.

Two-layer decentralized architecture

This section outlines the system structure and task representation, followed by its operational dynamics. The proposed two-layer architecture decouples task interpretation from execution, enabling adaptive coordination among heterogeneous agents with limited visibility and communication.

Agents and local state

Execution agents do not have global access to incoming tasks. Instead, each controller selects a subset of agents to whom a task is made visible, based on relevance, availability, and internal policies. This visibility mechanism is analogous to geolocation-based task assignment in ride-hailing systems.

Each agent \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_k$$\end{document} makes an autonomous decision about whether to accept a visible task, based solely on local information and predefined heuristics. In decentralized multi-agent systems, such local decision-making is typically based on utility maximization under partial observability. Common strategies include:Greedy selection, based on minimizing estimated cost or finish time27.Earliest Finish Time (EFT), used in dynamic scheduling with local estimates28.Threshold-based filtering, where agents accept tasks only if predicted utility exceeds a threshold 34.Auction-style or contract-net mechanisms, enabling agents to bid or negotiate over tasks2,3.Reputation-aware policies, where agents use historical success or delay statistics to guide selection 35.These decentralized policies enable agents to coordinate without global synchronization, often achieving near-optimal task distribution through emergent behaviour. In our model, agents use a simplified local policy combining the earliest availability estimates and relevance scores provided by the controller.

Decentralized adaptive controllers

Task assignment via load balancing protocol

When a new task \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_i$$\end{document} arrives into the system, it must be assigned to one of the adaptive controllers \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_j \in \mathcal {C}$$\end{document}. This controller becomes responsible for analysing the task, predicting its parameters, and selecting a relevant pool of execution agents. Since the system operates in an online decentralized regime without a central dispatcher, task allocation must rely solely on local observations and limited communication between neighbouring controllers.

To redistribute incoming tasks among controllers and balance the queues of tasks awaiting assignment, we employ a load balancing protocol. This protocol is an iterative and fully distributed mechanism originally designed for multi-agent systems operating under dynamic and stochastic communication topologies. The protocol enables controllers to reach agreement on load allocation without centralized coordination, while remaining robust to partial observability, communication delays, and asynchronous updates.

Applying this strategy requires first defining what constitutes the load of a node (controller), followed by a description of the load balancing mechanism.

Controller communication. We assume that controllers are able to exchange control information (e.g., workload estimates, task-urgency metrics) and collaboratively rebalance task assignments among neighboring controllers. In fog-computing environments, permanent all-to-all connections are typically infeasible: links may be unavailable at certain times due to bandwidth limitations, interference, or traffic constraints. Even when multiple neighbors are accessible, maintaining simultaneous communication with all of them is often impractical. Instead, it is convenient to model inter-controller communication through a time-varying undirected graph \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {G}(t) = (\mathcal {C}, \mathcal {E}(t))$$\end{document}, where active links represent the subset of neighbors exchanging information at time t. Randomized neighbor selection is commonly used in this context to limit communication overhead, which is consistent with the justification for randomized topologies discussed in31.

The communication weights are captured by an adjacency matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B(t) = [b^{j,j'}(t)] \in \mathbb {R}^{m \times m}$$\end{document}, defined as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} b^{j,j{\prime}}(t) = {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \displaystyle \frac{2}{\deg (j) + \deg (j{\prime})}, & \text {if } (j,j{\prime}) \in \mathcal {E}(t), \\ 0, & \text {otherwise}. \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The expected adjacency matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B_{\text {av}} = \mathbb {E}[B(t)]$$\end{document} represents the averaged communication graph \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {G}_{\text {av}}$$\end{document}, where the weight of each edge corresponds to its expected presence over time. The graph \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {G}_{\text {av}}$$\end{document} is assumed to be connected, enabling long-term coordination through distributed updates.

Load balancing protocol.

We seek an optimal task-distribution protocol by equalizing loads across controllers. These protocols are well suited to our decentralized setting for several reasons. First, both LVP and ALVP require only neighbour-to-neighbour exchanges of load estimates, eliminating the need for any central coordinator or global broadcasts. Third, by using constant or adaptively tuned step-sizes, these protocols maintain stability and responsiveness in the presence of time-varying workloads and link delays. Task parameter adaptation via recursive regression with forgetting

After tasks have been assigned and rebalanced among controllers using the protocol above, each controller proceeds to predict the key parameters of its tasks – such as expected processing time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{p}_i$$\end{document} and wait tolerance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_i$$\end{document} – using the adaptive techniques detailed in this section.

To adaptively estimate task parameters, each controller \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_j$$\end{document} maintains a local linear model updated using a recursive forgetting-based regression scheme. The model assumes a prediction of the form:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{p}_i = \phi _i^\top \theta _j, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi _i \in \mathbb {R}^d$$\end{document} is the feature vector for task \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_i$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta _j \in \mathbb {R}^d$$\end{document} is the weight vector maintained by the controller \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_j$$\end{document}.

After observing the actual processing time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_i^{\text {real}}$$\end{document}, the controller updates its model by minimizing a quadratic residual functional with exponential forgetting:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} J_j^{(t)}(\theta ) = \sum _{s=1}^{t} \beta ^{t - s} \cdot \left( p_s^{\text {real}} - \phi _s^\top \theta \right) ^2, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta \in (0,1]$$\end{document} is the forgetting factor, which assigns more weight to recent observations.

The model parameters are updated recursively as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \theta _j^{(t+1)} = \theta _j^{(t)} - Each controller independently applies this scheme to estimate local parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta _j$$\end{document} based on its own stream of task observations.

The resulting local model captures short-term task dynamics and adapts to parameter drift. These local estimates are subsequently synchronized across controllers using the decentralized SPSA+consensus algorithm described in the next section, ensuring network-wide coherence without requiring gradient access or centralized coordination.

This scheme allows for robust local tracking of parameter drift using a lightweight update per task. The adapted vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta _j$$\end{document}, representing the local model parameters of the controller \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_j$$\end{document}, undergoes periodic synchronization with other controllers using a decentralized variant of the SPSA-based consensus algorithm. This process is analogous to distributed learning, where each node maintains its own model and parameters are synchronized across the network without a central coordinator.

A similar online adaptation procedure is applied to estimate the wait tolerance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_i$$\end{document}, using \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_i^{\text {real}}$$\end{document} in place of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_i^{\text {real}}$$\end{document}.

Global parameter correction via decentralized SPSA

Having described how each controller predicts task parameters locally, we now turn to ensuring that these prediction models remain coherent across the network. To enable consistency across task parameter models maintained by distributed controllers, we employ the Simultaneous Perturbation Stochastic Approximation-based consensus method. This approach allows for gradient-free optimization of prediction models using only two observations of the objective per iteration, making it highly suitable for decentralized and bandwidth-constrained systems.

In contrast to gradient-based methods requiring assumptions on the noise distribution, our SPSA-based consensus approach only assumes that the noise is bounded: deterministically bounded in the case of deterministic disturbances, or mean-squared bounded, regardless of internal dependence structure, in the case of random noise. This enables robust operation in realistic decentralized environments without the need to specify a probabilistic noise model.

Theoretical convergence guarantee

In the proposed architecture, each controller j performs local optimization using a gradient-free SPSA update with an additional consensus correction from neighboring controllers. Although the observations may be noisy, Appendix A shows that the algorithm remains stable as long as the noise is bounded in magnitude, regardless of its distribution. This is particularly important for uncertain environments, such as human-centered or data-driven systems, where the noise model may be unknown.

We build upon the theoretical framework of31, which provides convergence guarantees for distributed SPSA algorithms with consensus dynamics under mild assumptions. The following theorem presents an adaptation of the main result from that work applied to our setting.

Synthetic simulation

To evaluate the core properties of our task allocation system in a controlled setting, we conducted a synthetic simulation. The environment allowed us to explicitly define task characteristics, agent capabilities, and observation noise, enabling a transparent assessment of system behaviour under varied conditions.

Task and agent setup. Each task was represented as a prompt of a certain type, sampled from one of six categories: Data Analysis, Programming, Translation, Summarization, Q&A, and Creative Writing. Tasks arrived dynamically in batches of size 1 to 8, and each batch could contain a heterogeneous mix of task types. Each task was associated with a release time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_i$$\end{document}, complexity score \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h(T_i)$$\end{document}, and type \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\texttt {type}_i$$\end{document}. The feature vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varvec{\phi }_i$$\end{document} was generated as a real-valued vector of fixed dimensionality.

The system simulated five synthetic agents representing major LLM classes: GPT-4, Claude-3.5, Gemini-1.5, LLaMA-3, and Mistral-7B. These agents acted as behavioural proxies with adaptive performance characteristics. Rather than using static efficiency profiles, each agent’s success probability and response latency were dynamically updated during the simulation based on observed task outcomes.

Controller logic. Four adaptive controllers were used, connected in a sparse communication graph. For each task, a controller predicted the expected execution time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{p}_i$$\end{document} and acceptable waiting window \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{w}_i$$\end{document}. The deadline was then computed as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D_i = r_i + \hat{w}_i + \hat{p}_i$$\end{document}.

Based on availability and current relevance, a candidate pool of agents was formed. The controller did not assign a specific agent; instead, agents in the pool independently claimed tasks, mimicking multi-agent selection dynamics. Controllers also coordinated through a Local Voting Protocol (LVP) for distributing tasks among themselves. Model parameters were updated via SPSA with consensus.

Conclusion

This paper has introduced a decentralized architecture for task allocation in dynamic multi-agent systems, combining adaptive controllers, predictive modelling, and a local voting protocol (LVP). The system operates in an online setting with limited visibility and heterogeneous agents, and is designed to perform robustly in high-uncertainty environments.

The proposed framework integrates predictive models for task parameters, relevance- and availability-based agent selection, and decentralized coordination among controllers. We further enhance adaptability through simultaneous perturbation stochastic approximation (SPSA), enabling each controller to refine its model based on noisy feedback and local information. A key theoretical contribution is the proof of convergence under unknown-but-bounded noise and minimal assumptions, extending the applicability of SPSA-based consensus algorithms to two-layer decentralized settings.

Simulation results demonstrate the effectiveness of the architecture on real-world prompt-like tasks distributed among diverse large language models (LLMs). The system outperforms baseline approaches in both average performance and robustness across a variety of task categories. The second set of experiments confirms that the proposed adaptation mechanism enables stable online learning in the presence of structural drift and noise.

Beyond its empirical performance, the novelty of this work lies in explicitly addressing three persistent gaps in decentralized task allocation: (i) enabling online adaptation under behavioral drift and noisy feedback without centralized retraining, (ii) introducing a two-layer architecture with selective task visibility to support scalability and heterogeneity, and (iii) providing decentralized synchronization via SPSA-based consensus under bounded disturbances. Furthermore, by validating the framework on LLM-driven workloads, we demonstrate its applicability to emerging domains of decentralized knowledge work, where adaptability and robustness are critical. These aspects distinguish our approach from conventional auction-based, heuristic, or DRL-heavy methods and establish a foundation for scalable and resilient task allocation in dynamic multi-agent environments.

Future work will focus on integrating hierarchical control and reward-based feedback, enabling agent selection not only based on availability and predicted accuracy but also on long-term outcomes. Additionally, we plan to explore task allocation in resource-constrained or partially observable settings, further pushing the boundary of adaptive multi-agent coordination. While the current architecture focuses on decentralized adaptation and agent selection, it can be further extended toward orchestration-level control. By introducing additional coordination layers or hierarchical planning mechanisms, the system could support more complex task dependencies, agent negotiations, and global workflow management – enabling orchestration without compromising its decentralized foundation.
