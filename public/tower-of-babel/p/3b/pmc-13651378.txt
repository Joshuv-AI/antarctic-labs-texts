Effectiveness of an MDT-CDIO integrated teaching model in orthopedic clinical education: a controlled study
Authors: Zhixuan Nian, Mingchun Li, Junwen Liang, Yijia Li, Ziting Wei, Liqiang Pan, Sen Fang, Xudong Yang, Wenjia Du, Xiangdong Yun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOrthopedic clinical education requires the integration of theoretical knowledge, practical skills, and multidisciplinary clinical decision-making. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of a multidisciplinary team (MDT)-integrated Conceive–Design–Implement–Operate (CDIO) teaching model in orthopedic clinical education.MethodsIn this prospective randomized controlled study, 60 orthopedic interns at Lanzhou University Second Hospital, enrolled between March and August 2024, were randomly assigned to either an experimental group (n = 30) or a control group (n = 30). The experimental group received orthopedic instruction based on the MDT-CDIO model, whereas the control group received traditional teaching. Teaching effectiveness was evaluated after the instructional intervention using theoretical examinations, practical skills assessments, and structured questionnaires.ResultsThe experimental group achieved significantly higher scores than the control group in the theoretical assessment (47.23 ± 0.72 vs. 45.40 ± 1.16), practical assessment (46.03 ± 0.76 vs. 44.63 ± 0.89), and overall performance (93.18 ± 1.00 vs. 90.03 ± 1.37; all P < 0.001). In the questionnaire assessment, the experimental group also reported significantly higher scores across all evaluated dimensions than the control group (all P < 0.001).ConclusionsThe MDT-CDIO integrated teaching model appears to be a valuable approach for orthopedic clinical education and may improve students’ learning outcomes, clinical practice skills, and perceived educational experience. Further large-scale, multicenter studies are warranted to validate its educational value and generalizability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09758-8.Keywords: MDT teaching model, CDIO teaching model, Joint teaching model, Orthopedics, Teaching methods, Education

Introduction

Medical education has long been a cornerstone of the medical profession [1], and clinical teaching in orthopedics is particularly critical for the cultivation of high-quality medical professionals. Traditional orthopedic teaching models typically adopt a disease-centered framework, emphasizing systematic instruction that progresses from etiology and pathogenesis to diagnosis and treatment, while guiding students’ clinical practice primarily through structured case presentations [2]. However, this approach often fails to sufficiently stimulate students’ sustained interest in orthopedics, thereby rendering the subject relatively abstract and monotonous. The CDIO teaching model [3]—Conceive, Design, Implement, Operate—embodies the translation and adaptation of competency-based engineering education principles into the medical education context. By emphasizing “learning by doing” and project-based learning, the CDIO model has been increasingly incorporated into orthopedic clinical education in recent years. By simulating the entire clinical process, this model establishes a closed-loop clinical reasoning cycle—from case analysis to treatment implementation—thereby effectively cultivating medical students’ clinical reasoning and procedural skills [4]. Su X et al. [5]reported that the application of the CDIO model in standardized orthopedic training significantly increased students’ satisfaction with the teaching process, improved assessment performance, and enhanced overall teaching effectiveness. Our previous research [6] also demonstrated that a combined PBL–CDIO approach produced favorable educational outcomes in the teaching of medical imaging interpretation. However, compared with other medical specialties, orthopedics is characterized by a high degree of subspecialization, a wide range of treatment modalities, and its nature as a highly multidisciplinary clinical field [7, 8]. The traditional CDIO model therefore exhibits certain limitations when applied to orthopedic clinical education. Its single-discipline-oriented instructional design struggles to accommodate the multidisciplinary knowledge system required for orthopedic diseases and does not place sufficient emphasis on the development of teamwork competencies. Research indicates [9] that many students experience a sense of helplessness when confronting urgent situations involving multidisciplinary diagnosis and treatment, thereby revealing deficiencies in their emergency medical response competencies. Addressing these challenges requires the development of an innovative orthopedic education model that aligns with contemporary healthcare and educational demands. The Multidisciplinary Team (MDT) model, a well-established clinical approach that integrates expertise across multiple specialties, has gained increasing and widespread adoption in clinical education in recent years [10]. Its core advantage lies in dismantling disciplinary barriers through simulated real-world clinical scenarios, thereby enhancing students’systems thinking and comprehensive clinical decision-making abilities, and it has shown particular value in the instruction of complex disease management [11]. The present study innovatively integrates the MDT teaching model with the CDIO (Conceive–Design–Implement–Operate) teaching model. By simulating real-world multidisciplinary consultations, the MDT approach effectively addresses inherent limitations of the CDIO model, particularly with respect to managing complex cases and fostering interdisciplinary thinking. This integrated model retains the strengths of the CDIO framework in systematic competency training while incorporating the MDT’s collaborative decision-making mechanisms, thereby aligning more closely with the “student-centered” philosophy of modern orthopedic education.

On this basis, the study proposes the following scientific hypotheses: (1) the MDT–CDIO integrated teaching model, through the integration of multidisciplinary resources and simulation of the entire clinical process, can significantly enhance students’ performance in assessments of orthopedic theoretical knowledge and clinical operational skills; and (2) this model can achieve higher levels of student satisfaction owing to its systematic cultivation of comprehensive competencies, such as communication, collaboration, and problem-solving. To test these hypotheses and to explore the educational value of this novel teaching model in orthopedic clinical education, the corresponding research findings are presented below.

Materials and methods

General information

From March to August 2024, a total of 60 orthopedic internship students from the Second Hospital of Lanzhou University were enrolled in this study. All participants were medical students who had completed basic medical science courses and had entered the clinical internship phase. Participants were randomly assigned to either the experimental group (n = 30) or the control group (n = 30) using a computer-generated random number table at a 1:1 ratio. To ensure strict randomization, the random sequence was maintained by a third-party researcher not involved in recruitment or teaching processes. Group assignment information was contained in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes that were only opened after students formally commenced their internships. Statistical analysis demonstrated that there were no statistically significant differences in baseline characteristics between the two groups (P > 0.05).Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The research has been performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and all methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Second Hospital of Lanzhou University(Approval No:2026 A-129).

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) orthopedic clinical internship duration of at least 12 weeks; and (2) voluntary participation in this study with provision of written informed consent.

The exclusion criteria included: (1) participants who withdrew during the study or were unable to complete the entire internship due to illness or other reasons; and (2) individuals who had previously received training in MDT- or CDIO-related teaching models.

After completion of their orthopedic clinical rotations, both groups of students underwent exit assessments administered by the Residency Training Office. These evaluations were conducted by senior orthopedic faculty members who were not involved in the study design. However, the examiners were not formally blinded to students’ group allocation.To improve objectivity and reliability, all examiners underwent standardized pre-service training and utilized standardized, objective scoring rubrics. When scoring discrepancies among examiners exceeded 10%, a review by the monitoring team was required to achieve consistent scoring. This process ensured inter-examiner reliability remained within acceptable ranges for medical clinical assessments. The content of the assessments strictly adhered to the National Standardized Residency Training Curriculum for Orthopedics and primarily evaluated students’ mastery of orthopedic knowledge and their ability to integrate multidisciplinary knowledge in the management of complex orthopedic cases. The difficulty and discrimination indices of the examination papers were pre-evaluated by a panel of three independent education experts to ensure content validity. The assessments consisted of two components: a theoretical examination and a practical skills assessment. The theoretical examination included multiple-choice questions, case-analysis questions, and radiographic interpretation items. The practical skills assessment comprised five stations: specialized orthopedic physical examination, physician–patient communication, conservative management of Colles’ fracture, manual reduction of radial head subluxation, and knee joint aspiration. These assessments were conducted using a combination of standardized patients (SPs) and simulation models, with on-site scoring performed by trained proctors. The theoretical examination and the practical skills assessment each accounted for 50% of the total score, yielding a maximum overall score of 100 points.

Teaching methods

Traditional teaching model (control group)

In the control group, a traditional disease-centered, lecture-based teaching approach was implemented as follows. To ensure baseline consistency, all instructors were senior specialists with over 5 years of experience and underwent standardized orientation to ensure the curriculum aligned with the National Standardized Residency Training guidelines. Weekly theoretical lectures were delivered using PowerPoint presentations to explain the etiology, pathology, diagnosis, treatment, and principles of postoperative recovery for orthopedic diseases. Case discussions were led by instructors, who analyzed diagnostic and treatment approaches, while students primarily engaged in passive knowledge acquisition with minimal exposure to multidisciplinary collaboration. Clinical practice primarily consisted of surgical observation and ward rounds, and clinical treatment plans were predominantly formulated by the instructors.

MDT combined with CDIo teaching model (experimental group)

(1) Composition of the Teaching Team. The team consisted of attending physicians and senior staff from orthopedics, radiology, rehabilitation, anesthesiology, nursing, and other relevant disciplines. Prior to the study’s commencement, all multidisciplinary team (MDT) members first participated in a joint workshop to standardize the instructional objectives and feedback mechanisms for each phase of the Conceptualization-Design-Implementation-Operation (CDIO) model. Orthopedic surgeons were responsible for determining case-specific treatment plans; radiologists provided specialized imaging evaluation and diagnosis; rehabilitation physicians designed individualized postoperative rehabilitation programs; anesthesiologists evaluated patients and formulated anesthesia protocols for complex surgeries; and head nurses developed perioperative nursing care plans.

(2)MDT Combined with CDIO Teaching Model.

MDT Combined with CDIO Teaching Model.

Conception Phase: Select an “open-ended case” from the standardized clinical teaching database. (e.g., a 65-year-old woman with right shoulder pain and restricted movement for six months) is presented to students without a direct diagnosis being provided. Students are required to (1) list at least three possible diagnoses (e.g., rotator cuff injury, frozen shoulder, cervical spondylosis) and explain the rationale for the differential diagnosis (e.g., location of pain, physical examination findings, imaging characteristics), and (2) develop a diagnostic testing plan (e.g., X-ray, MRI, bone density scan) with a clear explanation of the purpose of each test, such as using MRI to assess the severity of soft tissue injury.

Design Phase: Students are divided into groups of five to develop a comprehensive treatment plan for a patient with a full-thickness rotator cuff tear and type 2 diabetes. Each group member assumes one of the following clinical roles: orthopedic surgeon, radiologist, physical therapist, specialist nurse, or anesthesiologist. They are required to conduct a 30-minute simulated MDT consultation meeting. The orthopedic surgeon focuses on analyzing surgical indications and procedure selection; the radiologist provides detailed interpretation of MRI findings regarding the severity of the rotator cuff tear, including classification of partial-thickness versus full-thickness tears and their correlation with clinical symptoms; the physical therapist develops a phased functional rehabilitation program, including the planned duration of abduction brace use and progressive muscle-strengthening protocols; specialist nurses formulate perioperative management priorities, emphasizing the frequency of blood glucose monitoring and pressure-injury prevention measures; anesthesiologists assess anesthesia risk and propose intraoperative blood glucose control protocols. Each professional role participates in interdisciplinary consultations (e.g., discussing the impact of the arthroscopic approach on the timing of early rehabilitation interventions) to reach a multidisciplinary consensus treatment plan through collaborative discussion.

Implementation Phase: First, MDT physicians identify and critique gaps in the students’plans (e.g., failure to consider the impact of diabetes on wound healing) and assist students in revising these plans to finalize the treatment strategy. Next, students actively participate in bedside care in order to master diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for the relevant cases. They also participate in surgical procedures to deepen their understanding of orthopedic surgery and to enhance their clinical skills. Each student must complete an “MDT Full Process Log” to document key milestones in their learning journey. Throughout the process, students should systematically record treatment progress and engage in structured reflection to identify areas for improvement.

Operation Phase: Students conduct follow-up visits for discharged patients, collecting relevant metrics (e.g., fracture healing status, visual analogue scale [VAS] pain scores, complication rates) to compare their initial treatment plans with actual clinical outcomes. Subsequently, MDT physicians organize weekly debriefing sessions, during which students present analyses of deviations between the designed treatment plans and the actual clinical outcomes.The instructor combines cutting - edge content such as clinical hotspots and new guidelines, integrates 3D models, new AI technologies, etc., and proposes specific improvement measures. At the same time, it can also broaden everyone’s horizons and enable students to gradually adapt to the professional role of orthopedic surgeons serving as hubs of multidisciplinary collaboration during their internship phase.

Student satisfaction survey

Based on our research team’s previous studies [6, 12], and published MDT-related educational evaluation research [13], we designed and refined a survey questionnaire better suited to our current research. It assesses five dimensions: teaching model engagement, learning initiative, knowledge integration effectiveness, clinical reasoning development, and clinical practice competency. Each question is scored on a 20-point scale (0 = Very Dissatisfied, 20 = Very Satisfied), totaling 100 points.( See Supplementary Material 1 for the full questionnaire).

The reliability and validity of the self-designed satisfaction questionnaire were assessed. Cronbach’s α coefficient was used to evaluate internal consistency, yielding a scale-wide value of 0.921. All subscales exceeded 0.80, indicating good to excellent reliability (Table 1).

Reliability Analysis Results

The content validity index (CVI) of this questionnaire was assessed by a panel of five senior orthopedic education experts, yielding a score of 0.92. This confirms that the questionnaire accurately represents the intended educational domains. Due to the large number of questionnaire items (40 items) and relatively limited sample size (60 participants), the overall exploratory factor analysis failed to converge. Therefore, we employed a dimension-by-dimension approach to assess the questionnaire’s construct validity. Exploratory factor analysis was conducted separately for each of the five dimensions. Results showed KMO values of 0.73 (A), 0.77 (B), 0.81 (C), 0.86 (D), and 0.75 (E), with Bartlett’s sphericity tests all statistically significant (p < 0.001), indicating suitability of each dimension’s data for factor analysis. Further analysis revealed that each dimension extracted one factor with an eigenvalue greater than 1. The cumulative variance explained was 51.3%, 54.8%, 59.2%, 61.5%, and 55.7%, respectively. In addition, the factor loadings for all items within their respective dimensions were greater than 0.6.

However, given the relatively low subject-to-item ratio, particularly for the 10-item dimensions, this dimension-by-dimension EFA should be regarded as a pragmatic but limited approach necessitated by sample size constraints. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary evidence supporting construct validity rather than definitive validation of the questionnaire structure. Future studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to further confirm the factor structure of the questionnaire using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA)(Table 2).

Qualitative analysis of open-ended questions

The open-ended question at the end of the questionnaire (“What feedback or suggestions do you have regarding the MDT-CDIO teaching model?”) underwent thematic analysis. Two researchers independently coded the responses and identified recurring themes. Disagreements were resolved through discussion.

Statistical methods

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 statistical software. Quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test before between-group comparisons. For approximately normally distributed data, the independent-samples t-test was used. For data showing deviation from normality, the Mann–Whitney U test was additionally performed as a sensitivity analysis. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Performance evaluation

Students in the MDT–CDIO group achieved significantly higher scores than those in the control group in the theoretical assessment, practical assessment, and total score (47.23 ± 0.72 vs. 45.40 ± 1.16, 46.03 ± 0.76 vs. 44.63 ± 0.89, and 93.18 ± 1.00 vs. 90.03 ± 1.37, respectively; all P < 0.001; Table 4). To further characterize score variability, we examined the score ranges. In the control group, the score ranges were 43.5–48.5 for the theoretical assessment, 43.0–46.0 for the practical assessment, and 87.5–92.5 for the total score. In the experimental group, the corresponding ranges were 46.0–48.5, 45.0–47.5, and 91.0–95.0. No student achieved the maximum possible score in any of the three assessments (50, 50, and 100, respectively), indicating that an absolute ceiling effect was not observed, although the scores were concentrated within a relatively high-performance range. Shapiro–Wilk testing showed that the control group scores and the experimental group total score did not significantly deviate from normality, whereas the experimental group theoretical and practical scores showed mild departures from normality. Additional Mann–Whitney U tests were therefore performed as sensitivity analyses, and the between-group differences remained statistically significant for all three outcomes.

Qualitative analysis of open-ended questionnaires

Qualitative thematic analysis of open-ended responses revealed three core themes regarding the MDT-CDIO teaching model:

Student endorsement of the teaching model: 87% of students reported enhanced interdisciplinary collaboration and clinical reasoning skills. One respondent noted: “This model significantly boosted my confidence in communicating with professionals from different disciplines.” (2) Existing challenges: 18% of students encountered difficulties integrating diverse expert perspectives during the initial training phase, while 12% suggested increasing hands-on clinical practice time to better apply theoretical knowledge; (3) Optimization recommendations: Key suggestions included increasing the frequency of 3D simulation training to enhance understanding of orthopedic conditions and establishing an online MDT discussion platform to foster teamwork and knowledge sharing.

Discussion

The results of this study indicate that, following the introduction of the MDT combined with CDIO teaching model in clinical orthopedic education, students outperformed those in the traditional teaching group in theoretical assessments(47.23 ± 0.72 vs. 45.40 ± 1.16), practical assessments(46.03 ± 0.76 vs. 44.63 ± 0.89), and overall grades(93.18 ± 1.00 vs. 90.03 ± 1.37). Furthermore, satisfaction scores across all dimensions also showed a significant improvement. These results suggest that the MDT-CDIO model not only improves students’ mastery of orthopedic theoretical knowledge but also promotes the simultaneous enhancement of their clinical skills, knowledge integration abilities, and subjective learning experiences.

Compared with traditional disease-centered, lecture-based teaching methods [14], the strengths of the MDT-CDIO model lie primarily in its seamless integration of multidisciplinary collaboration with comprehensive clinical training. On the one hand, through the closed-loop training cycle of Conceive-Design-Implement-Operate, the CDIO model enables students to move beyond passive absorption of knowledge points and instead internalize knowledge through case analysis, treatment planning, hands-on practice, and reflection on outcomes [15]. Previous studies have also shown that CDIO-based teaching helps improve learners’ knowledge retention, learning satisfaction, and readiness for clinical practice [16, 17]. On the other hand, the introduction of MDT breaks away from a single-discipline perspective, prompting students to understand complex cases within a multidisciplinary collaborative framework encompassing orthopedics, radiology, rehabilitation, anesthesia, and nursing, thereby bringing them closer to real-world clinical decision-making scenarios [18]. Orthopedics is characterized by its high specialization, complex diagnostic and treatment pathways, and the need for multi-stage collaboration [19]. Therefore, this integrated model is highly adaptable to orthopedic education and aligns more closely with the developmental direction of modern medical education, which emphasizes the cultivation of comprehensive competence.

The mechanism behind the improvement in the experimental group’s performance in this study may primarily stem from a shift in students’ learning roles. In traditional teaching, students primarily act as observers and executors [20], whereas under the MDT-CDIO model, students are required to propose differential diagnoses, formulate examination plans, participate in simulated consultations, revise treatment plans, and review actual outcomes. This shift from passive learning to a cycle of analysis, decision-making, implementation, and reflection helps bridge the gap between theoretical knowledge and clinical application, enhancing students’ abilities in problem identification, information integration, and clinical reasoning [21]. At the same time, structured multidisciplinary role assignments and 30-minute simulated MDT consultations [22] provide students with opportunities to systematically practice communication, collaboration, and consensus-building skills that are often underdeveloped in traditional orthopedic education [23] but are critical in managing complex cases.

The qualitative findings of this study further support the above conclusion. The open-ended questionnaire revealed that 87% of students believed this model enhanced their interdisciplinary collaboration skills and clinical reasoning abilities, while also boosting their confidence in communicating with professionals from different disciplines. This finding aligns with the higher scores obtained in the quantitative questionnaire across dimensions such as knowledge integration, clinical thinking development, and clinical practice skills, suggesting that the advantages of the MDT-CDIO model are reflected not only in exam scores but also in students’ subjective perceptions of their own skill development. Previous studies have similarly indicated that multidisciplinary-oriented education helps improve learners’ comprehensive abilities, teamwork performance, and clinical decision-making levels [24, 25]; therefore, the findings of this study are consistent with existing evidence in medical education.

Although the overall results were positive, the qualitative analysis also revealed that the model still faced certain challenges during the initial implementation phase. 18% of students reported difficulties in integrating the opinions of experts from different disciplines during the early stages of training, suggesting that interdisciplinary collaboration skills cannot be developed solely through situational exposure but still require more explicit, structured guidance from instructors—such as a unified discussion framework, clear objectives for each discipline’s contributions, and enhanced feedback sessions. Relevant research also indicates that in clinical education, clear, structured feedback and guidance are crucial for learners to develop effective clinical judgment and collaboration skills [26]. Additionally, 12% of students suggested increasing the duration of hands-on clinical practice, indicating that even within the MDT-CDIO model, students still desire more opportunities to translate treatment plans into actual clinical procedures. In other words, while the integrated teaching model has improved students’ understanding of “how” and “why” to perform procedures, their proficiency in execution still requires further reinforcement through more extensive practical training opportunities.

The optimization suggestions put forward by the students also provide direction for future teaching improvements. Some students recommended increasing the frequency of 3D simulation training; this suggestion is not at odds with the findings of this study but is highly consistent with the requirements of orthopedic learning for spatial anatomical understanding, visualization of surgical pathways, and structural visualization skills. In orthopedic education, many injury types, joint structures, and surgical approaches possess distinct three-dimensional characteristics [27]. Consequently, students hope to compensate for gaps in their understanding of complex structures through increased visualization and simulation training, which aligns with the emphasis in this study on the need to enhance clinical understanding and procedural skills. Previous research has shown that technology-enhanced teaching, 3D printing, and multimodal visualization tools can help learners better understand complex orthopedic structures and improve spatial cognition [28]. At the same time, the suggestion to establish an online MDT discussion platform reflects students’ desire to extend interdisciplinary communication beyond fixed classroom sessions to include pre-class preparation, post-class debriefing, and continuous collaborative learning; this aligns with current trends in digital medical education and online collaborative teaching [29, 30]. Therefore, these suggestions should not be viewed as “additional topics” separate from the research findings, but rather understood as improvement directions proposed by students based on their actual learning experiences and closely related to the learning needs identified in this study.

Overall, the value of the MDT-CDIO teaching model lies not only in improving short-term exam scores, but also in providing a training framework for clinical orthopedic education that more closely mirrors real-world clinical practice. Through open-ended case-driven learning, multi-role collaboration, multi-stage debriefing, and comprehensive reflection, this model promotes the simultaneous development of students’ knowledge, skills, and non-technical competencies. Particularly in orthopedics—a specialty that relies heavily on multidisciplinary collaboration while emphasizing spatial understanding and procedural execution—traditional lecture-based teaching alone can no longer fully meet competency development needs [31], whereas the MDT-CDIO model is able to address this gap to a significant extent.

Of course, this study still has several limitations. First, this was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size; although the results were statistically significant, their generalizability requires further validation in larger, multicenter studies. Second, this study assessed short-term teaching outcomes at the conclusion of the clinical rotation and cannot yet determine whether this model leads to long-term knowledge retention, sustained clinical performance advantages, or a stable impact on actual performance during the subsequent residency phase. Third, although this study employed standardized scoring scales, rater training, and a review mechanism to maximize assessment objectivity, both instructors and students were aware of the teaching model, and raters were not formally blinded to group assignments. Therefore, the influence of the Hawthorne effect and potential detection bias cannot be completely ruled out. Fourth, the satisfaction questionnaire used in this study was a self-developed scale. Although it demonstrated good internal consistency and preliminary evidence of construct validity, due to sample size limitations, the evidence for construct validity should still be considered preliminary. Further confirmation through confirmatory factor analysis in a larger sample is required in the future.

Despite the aforementioned limitations, this study suggests that the MDT combined with the CDIO model holds promising prospects for application in clinical orthopedic education. This model effectively addresses the needs of modern medical education to cultivate interdisciplinary communication, systems thinking, clinical reasoning, and the ability to integrate theory with practice; it may also serve as a reference for educational reforms in other clinical specialties involving complex collaborative processes. If implementation strategies can be optimized on a larger scale in the future and combined with long-term follow-up evaluations, the educational value of this approach is expected to be further validated.
