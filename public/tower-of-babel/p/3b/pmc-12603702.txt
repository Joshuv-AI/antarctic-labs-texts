Early intervention guidelines for infants at risk of neurodevelopmental delay using traditional childcare practices from India
Authors: Tanochni Mohanty, Mallikarjunaiah H S, Umme Uzma M, Kavitha Raja
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIndia is witnessing a growing concern around neurodevelopmental disorders, with nearly one in eight children potentially affected. The early years of life, especially the first 1000 days offer a crucial window to support brain development and positive long-term outcomes. Global early intervention (EI) strategies focus on caregiver involvement, enriched environments and learning through meaningful repetition. However, applying these approaches in everyday community settings remains challenging due to the need for specialised training which is inaccessible for grassroots-level workers. Interestingly, many traditional Indian caregiving practices echo these global principles, though they remain undocumented in scientific literature.ObjectiveThis study aimed to explore traditional Indian child-rearing practices aligned with evidence-based EI and developing culturally rooted, easy-to-follow video resources to train grassroots-level workers (Accredited Social Health Activists (ASHA)).MethodsWe conducted 11 focus group discussions with 121 mothers and grandmothers from varied regions and cultural backgrounds across India. Using open-ended prompts, we gathered insights into daily caregiving routines for infants under 1 year of age. Conversations were recorded, transcribed and thematically analysed by age group (0–3, 3–6, 6–9 and 9–12 months). Based on recurring patterns, a series of EI videos was created and validated through community and expert feedback.ResultsFindings revealed that many common Indian practices—such as massage, floor play, lullabies and face-to-face interaction—align closely with global EI principles. The videos developed from these practices were well-received by caregivers and experts for their clarity, cultural relevance and practical usefulness.ConclusionsIndian families already practise many nurturing routines that support early development. By capturing these practices in simple, accessible videos, we can bridge traditional wisdom with modern science—empowering parents and ASHA workers to support children’s development meaningfully in the home environment.Keywords: Cerebral Palsy, Health Policy, Low and Middle Income Countries, Qualitative research, Child Health

Traditional Indian child-rearing practices—such as massage, cosleeping and face-to-face interaction—naturally align with these evidence-based strategies.

Introduction

Neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) are conditions that arise when brain development is disrupted during the fetal or early neonatal period.1 These conditions can affect how a child moves, learns, behaves or communicates. Some of the common NDDs include autism, attention deficit hyperactive disorder, intellectual disabilities and cerebral palsy.2 A recent study from India reported that one in eight children may be affected by any one of the NDD, highlighting NDD as a major public health concern.3 NDDs can potentially result in physical disability and may have negative long-term consequences for children and their parents. A review reported that rural, urban and mixed rural–urban Indian population demonstrated a pooled prevalence for NDD of 1.83 (95% CI 0.41 to 3.25), 2.29 (95% CI 1.43 to 3.16) and 4.37 (95% CI 2.24 to 6.51), respectively.4 The first 1000 days of life are a critical window for brain development, when interventions are particularly effective. Diagnosing NDD in early infancy maximises the opportunity for appropriate management strategies and can positively impact the child’s transition to schooling, thereby improving their quality of life.5 A review of the early diagnosis and management of NDD highlights key differences between high-income countries (HICs) and low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs).6 In HICs, accurate diagnosis is routinely made before 6 months of corrected gestational age through a combination of neuroimaging and standardised tools.7 8 However, in LMIC, implementing these assessments is challenging due to poor awareness and accessibility of assessment. Furthermore, there are considerable data gaps regarding the prevalence, epidemiology and causes of developmental disabilities in LMIC, which acts as a challenge to guide policymakers. Timely identification of developmental disabilities is crucial for professional early intervention (EI) strategies which can help children with definite NDD to acquire new skills and behaviours, reinforcing their ability to learn and thrive. In HICs, EI for high-risk infants is often initiated right from the neonatal period, with more targeted support for developmental disabilities beginning as early as 3 months of age.9 Research consistently shows that early and timely intervention not only enhances a child’s developmental outcomes and daily functioning but also significantly improves overall quality of life and participation in social settings. The global EI guidelines emphasise a family-centred approach such as face-to-face nurturing interaction with vocalisations, joint attention from adults and infants, reciprocal interaction, and continuity of intervention across the early childhood period.9 NDD prevalence and distribution in recent years has been linked to impoverished environment. These guidelines are in line with the Indian child rearing practices as per observation and anecdotal evidence.10,12 Traditional Indian child-rearing practices for infants typically emphasise exclusively breastfeeding for the first 6 months, daily massages, close physical contact with the mother, a strong focus on family values, early exposure to language through talking and singing, and a belief in the importance of touch and nurturing, often with extended family members playing a significant role in the child’s upbringing. Despite the close resemblance to EI strategies, there is no formal resource available for mothers or grassroots-level workers to adopt into the routine of childcare. With urbanisation and nuclearisation of the families, traditional childcare practices are often not passed down to the newer generation. Moreover, dependence on digital resources may create conflict between Indian traditional child-rearing practices and modern practices, creating confusion in the minds of young parents.13 Hence, the objective of this paper was to develop EI strategies aligned with Indian child-rearing practices, video record them and validate them with global EI guidelines as the frame of reference.

Methodology

Study design and ethical consideration

This study used a qualitative research design, with focus group discussions (FGDs) as the primary mode of data collection in order to explore Indian child-rearing practices in depth.

Participants and samplings

The study participants comprised mothers and grandmothers of children aged at least 3 years selected from multiple cultural and geographical backgrounds across India table 1 and included various religious affiliations. This diversity allowed for a comprehensive understanding of child-rearing practices across different regions and traditions in India. A purposive sampling method was employed to recruit participants, ensuring that those with direct experience in traditional caregiving and child-rearing were included in the study.

Patient and public involvement

Participants were approached through trusted personal networks including colleagues, friends and relatives to help ensure a respectful and familiar setting. Before any involvement, the purpose and goals of the research were explained clearly and openly, using language that was accessible and sensitive to different backgrounds. Individuals were encouraged to ask questions, express concerns and take time to consider their participation. FGDs took place only after participants gave informed consent and agreed to take part on their own terms. While patients and the public were not directly involved in designing the study or analysing data, their lived experiences played a vital role in shaping the conversations and the insights that emerged. Ethical approval was secured through the relevant institutional review board, and every effort was made to ensure participants felt heard, respected and safe throughout the process. Although they were not involved in writing or disseminating the findings, their contributions remain central to the story this research aims to tell.

Development of the research instrument

A thorough literature review was conducted to gather existing knowledge on Indian child-rearing practices. Based on the findings, a set of open-ended questions was developed to guide the discussions. The questionnaire underwent content validation by experts in paediatric physiotherapy and child development and expert validation to ensure clarity, relevance and cultural sensitivity. The final questionnaire consisted of guided prompts (detailed in a separate table), enabling an in-depth exploration of child-rearing customs, beliefs and practices.

Data collection process

The FGDs were conducted in a calm, well-lit and distraction-free environment to facilitate meaningful discussions. Each group was interviewed in a location that they were already familiar with such as their home, workplace or a local community hall.

Each participant was given an ample amount of time to understand the questions and respond comfortably. The discussions were conducted in the participants’ local languages to ensure accurate and uninhibited responses. Each FGD was video recorded with digital single-lens reflex camera (EOS 1500D, Canon, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with EF-S 18–55 mm and EF 55–200 mm lenses to ensure clear visual documentation of the procedures with prior informed consent from all participants. The seating was arranged in a semicircle, allowing all participants to have a clear view of one another and ensuring that the camera could capture their body language and all non-verbal cues. The recordings captured both verbal responses and non-verbal expressions, which were crucial for understanding the contextual nuances of responses. Data collection continued until data saturation was achieved, which was determined as the point where no new themes emerged in three successive FGDs. A total of 11 FGDs were conducted, involving 121 mothers and grandmothers. All the participants were offered reimbursement of expenses for their participation in the study.

Data processing and analysis

Following data collection, the video recordings were transferred to a laptop, assigning unique identification codes for confidentiality and organised storage. The recordings were repeatedly reviewed and transcribed verbatim by two people who were native speakers of the target language. Furthermore, it was transliterated using the India Typing portal, which supports multilingual input and script conversion across Indian languages. Thereafter, two persons fluent in the target language and English translated the material into English. The audio was meticulously analysed to ensure transcription and translation accuracy by native language experts. This meticulous process helped preserve the original meanings and cultural contexts. After this thematic analysis was performed, categorising repeated themes into age groups 3–6 months, 6–9 months, 9–12 months. Thereafter, the researchers performed a detailed analysis of the themes and identified those that aligned closely with globally recognised EI practices (EIPs). Author 1 conducted an in-depth review of the content to identify key sections and become thoroughly familiar with the dataset, ensuring the extraction of the most relevant quotations. Author 3 was responsible for transcribing and transliterating the audio recordings. This process of data immersion facilitated the identification of critical information pertinent to the study’s objectives, particularly focusing on keywords frequently associated with the EIP within the transcribed scripts. A word cloud analysis was employed to highlight these keywords, excluding conjunctions and prepositions while retaining nouns, verbs, adverbs and adjectives. Author 4 then conceptualised the analytical framework by interpreting the identified keywords, codes and emerging themes. The finalised framework comprised four overarching themes—bathing, sleeping, playing and nursing/feeding—structured across the developmental age groups of 0–3 months, 3–6 months, 6–9 months and 9–12 months. These findings are presented in the results section.

Development of EI videos

Based on the common child-rearing practices identified in the study, a series of age-specific EI videos was developed. The developed videos underwent a rigorous multistep validation process to ensure their accuracy, cultural relevance and practical applicability.

Face validation

The initial versions of the videos were reviewed by a group of caregivers, community level grassroots health workers, Accredited Social Health Activists (ASHA) workers and parents to assess their clarity, comprehensibility and cultural appropriateness. Feedback was collected on aspects such as ease of understanding, relevance to real-life caregiving situations and visual/audio clarity.

Expert validation

The videos were then evaluated by a panel of subject-matter experts, including paediatric physiotherapists who are authors 1, 2 and 4, child development specialists and EI professionals. The experts assessed the accuracy of the demonstrated techniques, alignment with global EIPs and suitability for the target audience (parents and ASHA workers). Inputs from both face validation and expert review were systematically analysed, and necessary modifications were made. These included adjustments in content, narration, demonstration techniques, repetitions of crucial information and visual presentation to enhance the effectiveness and clarity of the videos. Any identified issues, such as misinterpretations, technical inconsistencies or unclear demonstrations, were rectified before finalising the videos. The revised versions were re-evaluated by the experts to confirm that all concerns had been adequately resolved. After completing all validation steps and ensuring that the content met scientific, practical and cultural standards, the final versions of the age-specific EI videos were produced. This thorough validation process ensured that the videos were scientifically robust, culturally appropriate and practically useful for their intended audience. These videos were designed to educate and support parents and ASHA workers in home-based childcare and promote culturally relevant EI strategies tailored to Indian caregiving practices.

Results

A total of 11 FGDs were conducted with 121 participants, comprising mothers and grandmothers of children aged 3 years and above. Participants represented diverse cultural, linguistic and geographical regions across India, including both rural and urban contexts. Most were the primary caregivers of infants and young children. Each FGD lasted 45–70 min (median 56 min). Discussions yielded rich narratives highlighting how caregiving practices in early childhood integrate sensory, motor and socioemotional stimulation within routine family activities.

Through thematic analysis, four major caregiving domains emerged that were bathing, sleeping, feeding/nursing and playing, each encompassing activities aligned with developmental principles of EI. Representative quotations are provided to illustrate key concepts and contextual variations.

Bathing as a multisensory and motor-stimulating routine

Across all regions, bathing was described not merely as hygiene but as an opportunity for bonding and developmental stimulation. Caregivers universally emphasised prebath oil massage as essential for strengthening the baby’s body, improving flexibility and promoting sleep. Gentle joint movements and midline positioning were commonly incorporated:

When we massage and stretch their hands and legs, the baby becomes strong and sleeps better. (Mother, West Bengal)

Grandmother is the best, because she puts oil and massages the hands and feet gently. This helps the child a lot and improves their growth. Hands become warm and soft. Sesame oil, we use coconut oil, sesame oil, castor oil, almond oil, also almond oil. (Mother, Karnataka)

Postmassage sun exposure was widely practised to ‘make bones strong’. Many families combined bathing with songs, rhythmic chants or naming body parts, promoting language and sensory awareness. Regional differences were evident, such as herbal oil use in southern states and turmeric application in western India. These practices demonstrate an intuitive integration of motor, sensory and communicative stimulation within a culturally embedded daily routine:

Midline activities usually, holding the hand, moving it downwards, upwards, from top to bottom, also rotating and flexing the hand. Then, moving the legs up and down, actually doing knee-to-chest kind of exercise to help with gas (Grandmother, Telangana)

Moving the hands and legs alternately. When the baby is on their back, they move one hand up while the other moves down. Moving the legs in a cycling motion, one leg bends while the other straightens. Simultaneous but opposite movements of arms and legs, like pushing one arm forward while pulling the other back. (Mother, Rajasthan)

Sleeping practices fostering comfort and attachment

Sleep was viewed as essential to physical and emotional well-being. Cosleeping with mothers or grandmothers was considered critical for warmth, breastfeeding convenience and emotional reassurance. Rhythmic movements, lullabies and gentle patting were consistently used to soothe infants:

When a mother does skin-to-skin contact with the baby, the baby feels safe and protected. They understand whom they are with. Secondly, some changes happen in the mother’s body, like the lactation reflex forming, which helps the baby feed faster and better without having to wait for the milk to come. (Mother, Karnataka Urban)

Minimal light exposure, we use zero or dim bulb in night to avoid eye strain (Mother, Kerala)

Traditional swaddling and hammock sleeping (jhulla) were common, particularly in southern and northeastern regions. While caregivers valued these for comfort, some expressed awareness of safety concerns. Eye contact, rhythmic rocking and voice modulation during lullabies were recognised as enhancing security and attachment—elements consistent with EI principles of self-regulation and sensory modulation.

Feeding and nursing as sensory and communicative acts

Feeding was described as a deeply interactive process encompassing tactile, auditory and visual engagement. Exclusive breastfeeding was nearly universal, typically accompanied by talking or stroking to maintain eye contact and emotional connection:

When I feed, I talk to him and touch his face. He listens to my voice and looks at me. That’s how he learns to respond. (Grandmother, Gujarat)

Introduction of semisolid foods around 6–8 months was frequently paired with storytelling, naming foods and pointing to surroundings, fostering joint attention and communication. Feeding by hand was preferred over spoons for enhancing texture awareness and nurturing closeness. Caregivers often seated infants on their lap or hip, providing proximal postural support. These observations align with family-centred feeding approaches that emphasise responsiveness and multisensory interaction:

When we sit near the plate, we make the child sit with us and the child automatically sees how we are eating. When the child starts sitting, when he can sit on his own, he sits with us. Now for the child, eating with hands, putting some food on the hands. (Mother, North-eastern)

Play as the foundation for motor, sensory and social development

Play was viewed as the core of learning and development. Caregivers described daily floor play, tummy time, clapping and peek-a-boo as promoting movement, curiosity and communication:

During the day, when the baby is awake and playing, we place the baby on their stomach, lying face down on a soft mattress. So, when we put the baby on their tummy, we stay close by to encourage them to try to lift their head by holding onto their hands. (Mother, Karnataka)

Commonly used play materials included household items such as spoons, bangles and cloth dolls, reflecting creativity and cultural relevance. Verbal imitation and repetition of sounds were typical, encouraging early speech. Outdoor play and participation in household rituals were valued for social exposure. Families in joint households highlighted the advantages of multigenerational interaction, whereas those in nuclear families reported fewer opportunities for shared play.

Gently ringing bells to stimulate the baby’s hearing. Using soft, soothing bell sounds to encourage calmness and attention. (Mother, West Bengal)

First, we make them sit on the lap, then place them on the thighs with their legs hanging down in a supported position…After that, with a chair in front, he began to go forward, started that, then hit the wall and began walking. (Mother, Rajasthan)

So the child is standing confidently with support then you know we just tell them how to put one leg in front, we do it a little, the child has to know that the foot goes like this forward. So after doing it once or twice, then we leave, again we put some toy or the mother or father is there, they keep calling and playing with the child, the child will take one or two steps forward, then 2 steps, then 3, and gradually the child learns to walk. (Mother, Gujarat)

The person who feeds, like if it’s the grandmother, ‘say grandma, say grandma, grandma’, that’s how you interact with the child. Father will introduce himself as ‘see, I am papa’, or grandmother also introduces herself. (Grandmother, Gujarat)

My baby’ ‘good baby’ ‘okay baby’—when a smile is given then ‘good baby’ like this we say, small one (Mother, Kerala)

Integration with EI principles

Across all domains, caregivers demonstrated an intuitive understanding of how daily routines could promote developmental milestones. Activities such as oil massage, cosleeping, responsive feeding and interactive play mapped naturally to recognised EI domains—motor control, communication, sensory processing and socio-emotional bonding (tables25). These findings illustrate that traditional caregiving practices inherently possess therapeutic potential for early child development.

Participants also noted shifts in family structure and work patterns that restrict continuation of such traditions, underscoring the need for culturally adapted EI materials that are feasible in contemporary Indian households.

Validation of video modules

Face validation

The face validation phase involved mothers, grandmothers and ASHA workers who viewed the preliminary video modules. Participants affirmed that the content was clear, realistic and culturally authentic. They particularly valued the inclusion of local caregiving settings, familiar language tone and demonstrations using Indian infants and caregivers. Minor suggestions included slowing down narration, using close-up shots for massage techniques and repeating key developmental cues for clarity.

Caregivers appreciated that the videos validated their existing practices while adding scientific explanations.

“These videos show what we already do at home but explain why it helps the baby—now we understand better.” (ASHA worker, Karnataka)

Feedback confirmed the acceptability and contextual fit of the material for parental education and community-level dissemination.

Expert validation

Following face validation, a panel of subject-matter experts, including paediatric physiotherapists (authors 1, 3 and 4), child development specialists and EI professionals, reviewed the content for developmental accuracy, alignment with global EI principles and appropriateness for the target audience (parents and community workers).

Experts endorsed the scientific integrity, clarity and alignment with functional developmental milestones depicted in the videos. Suggested modifications included refining the sequencing of activities, enhancing demonstration precision and standardising terminology used in narration. These recommendations were systematically incorporated into the final versions.

Finalisation of culturally appropriate EI videos

Based on cumulative feedback from caregivers, community workers and experts, the video modules were finalised. Adjustments were made in content flow, visual clarity and repetition of demonstration segments to ensure comprehensibility across literacy levels. The final suite of videos demonstrated the integration of traditional caregiving wisdom with evidence-based EI techniques, offering a model for home-based developmental stimulation applicable across diverse Indian contexts.

The resulting videos were thus validated for face and content accuracy, ensuring that they are developmentally sound, culturally grounded and operationally feasible for dissemination through ASHA networks and community rehabilitation programmes.

Discussion

One of the central aims of this study was to explore how Indian child-rearing practices can be meaningfully integrated into EIP that align with global principles. At its core, EI involves enriching a child’s everyday environment to support their natural drive to learn, encouraging confidence through guided, caregiver-supported exploration.14 Global EIP emphasises active skill training, nurturing environments and strong parent–child interactions.15 These are not just theoretical; but are repeated, purposeful experiences that shape brain development in significant ways.16 17 Early, targeted intervention has shown positive outcomes, but most existing data comes from older children or adults, as assessing preverbal infants remains challenging.18 However, the aim of this research is to provide guidance for an enriched environment for child rearing and is not aimed at intervention for children with specific diagnoses. Moreover, this research does not presume to take the place of professional intervention but is aimed merely at providing family-centric care. Interestingly, traditional Indian caregiving already includes many practices that naturally promote early development. For instance, stacking objects while sitting common between 6 and 9 months supports postural control, hand function, coordination and early problem-solving.19 Research indicates that the corticospinal tract, a key motor pathway, is largely developed by 6 months,20 making this an ideal window to introduce movement-rich experiences that strengthen neural connections.20,22 Prone play, a typical part of bathing or floor time in Indian homes during 0–3 months, is another example. Globally recommended for gross motor development, it also promotes brain–body coordination as babies learn to bear weight and reach for toys. Studies show that about 90 hours of child-led practice over 6 weeks can induce measurable neuroplastic changes.23 While this might seem intensive, it becomes feasible when woven into daily routines. Traditional caregiving excels here—face-to-face play, rattles, rocking, singing and gentle touch are naturally embedded in Indian homes and repeated often. These emotionally rich, sensory experiences are not only bonding but critical for brain development.24 Retraining and empowering parents to intentionally use these culturally familiar methods helps bridge generational wisdom with modern science. Another cornerstone of EIP is that learning must be joyful and meaningful. Motivation and attention are key drivers of neuroplasticity,25 and babies tend to repeat what they enjoy. Even without words, they express preferences through gestures, smiles or eye contact. Parents who tune into these cues create a learning environment that is both effective and emotionally secure.15 Traditional play like clapping, imitating siblings or singing during routines fosters communication, movement and social connection, all within the safety of the home. Such enriched environments benefit not only typically developing infants but also those recovering from early challenges. Research from neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) supports this—structured, developmentally supportive care improves outcomes across motor, cognitive and sensory domains.16 21 Globally used NICU interventions such as facilitated tucking,26 Kangaroo Mother Care,27 infant massage,28 music therapy,29 newborn individualised developmental care and assessment programme,30 non-nutritive suckling31 and parent-delivered physiotherapy32 closely mirror Indian traditions like swaddling, skin-to-skin contact, rhythmic movement and massage. India is currently undergoing a marked transition from the traditional joint family system to predominantly nuclear family structures.13 As a result, children today have fewer opportunities for interaction with extended family members. Concurrently, the rising educational attainment of women and the increasing economic necessity for dual-income households mean that both parents are often engaged in full-time employment.13 This reduces the time parents can devote to direct engagement with their children. The absence of extended family support during this critical phase of early brain development further limits children’s exposure to sensory-rich and socially stimulating environments.15 Evidence suggests that, unlike animals who begin exploring their surroundings shortly after birth, human infants rely almost entirely on parents or caregivers to provide enriched environments that foster exploration. Consequently, the responsibility of ensuring exposure to emotionally supportive and sensory-rich experiences rests primarily with parents and caregivers. Such environments are essential for optimising intellectual development and promoting holistic growth across motor, cognitive, social and sensory domains.15 Moreover, epidemiological data indicate a higher prevalence of CP in urban populations compared with rural ones,4 lending further support to the notion that reduced environmental enrichment and limited familial support in urban settings may contribute to adverse developmental outcomes. In today’s nuclear, educated families, many parents hesitate to use them, worrying they lack scientific backing. Generational differences in child-rearing beliefs and practices often create tension within families. The older generation often advocates traditional methods, while younger parents lean towards modern approaches, and again, staying in a nuclear set-up furthermore supports not following the traditional childcare practices.13 These professional-led, evidence-based educational videos are expected to play a transformative role in revalidating traditional wisdom and building parental confidence.

Strength

The study followed a strong and careful process to ensure reliable results. It brought together 121 participants across eleven FGDs, allowing voices from different ethnic and cultural backgrounds to be represented meaningfully.

Limitation

The qualitative study design is not a robust study design, and these results must be validated with a longitudinal study to conclusively report if the strategies are effective.

Future direction

To truly understand if this educational resource can be an effective EI programme for children diagnosed/at risk for NDD, a well-conducted randomised controlled trial (RCT) would be valuable. A well-designed RCT could be structured with two groups, using block randomisation to ensure balanced allocation. One group would receive a culturally adapted, video-based EI programme developed from this current study, while the comparison group would be provided with only a verbally instructed home exercise programme. The required sample size should be calculated to achieve adequate statistical power. Every stage of the study that is from participant recruitment and group allocation to the delivery of the intervention should be conducted under strict blinding procedures. This approach would help minimise bias and allow for a more accurate evaluation of the effectiveness of the video-based intervention.

Conclusions

The study found that many Indian child-rearing practices closely reflect those used in well-established global EIPs. There was similarity in child-rearing practices from across various groups in India, which is encouraging as this would promote acceptability of the video resources in various parts of India.
