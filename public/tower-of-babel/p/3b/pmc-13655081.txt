Chromosome-level genome assembly and DNA methylation data of the oleaginous astaxanthin-producing alga Chromochloris zofingiensis
Authors: Yu Zhang, Yibo Wang, Shuangyu Li, Liqin Sun, Jin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChromochloris zofingiensis has emerged as a promising economic microalgal strain owing to its capacity for the integrated production of triacylglycerol (TAG) and astaxanthin. To advance its development as a robust model alga for studying lipid metabolism and astaxanthin biosynthesis, we performed a comprehensive genome sequencing strategy integrating Nanopore long-reads and Illumina short-reads, together with Hi-C and PacBio full-length transcriptome sequencing. This enabled an improved chromosome-level genome assembly comprising of 17 chromosomes with a total size of 57.09 Mb and a scaffold N50 of 3.18 Mb. In total, 94.85% of the assembled sequences are anchored on 17 chromosomes. Compared to the sole published genome draft, the new assembly exhibits substantial enhancements in completeness and accuracy, with 12,480 protein-coding genes predicted. Furthermore, we presented the first genome-wide DNA methylation landscape of C. zofingiensis through the combined analysis of Nanopore and Bisulfite sequencing data. In summary, this improved, chromosome-scale genome provides a valuable genetic resource for in-depth molecular research and genetic engineering of this oleaginous astaxanthin-producing alga.

Background & Summary

The freshwater unicellular green microalga Chromochloris zofingiensis has been considered as a promising candidate for the integrated production of triacylglycerol (TAG) and astaxanthin. It has garnered considerable attention due to its robust growth, high biomass yield under multi-trophic growth conditions, and simultaneous accumulation of TAG and astaxanthin. C. zofingiensis is haploid and reproduces asexually. Its life cycle is simple, generally consisting of three stages: growth, maturation, and division. During its life cycle, it exhibits a multinucleate cell stage, with the number of nuclei determined by the frequency of DNA replication and nuclear division1.

With the development of a nuclear transformation system2, the release of the first whole-genome sequence3, the establishment of random mutagenesis systems4,5, and the increasing availability of omics data6,7, C. zofingiensis is emerging as a novel model alga for studying lipid metabolism and astaxanthin biosynthesis mechanisms. However, the first whole-genome sequence3 published in 2017, which was generated using second-generation Illumina HiSeq sequencing technology, was highly fragmented and incomplete, with 52 gaps totaling 3.28 Mb, reflecting the inherent limitations of short-read sequencing. Consequently, studies have since reported that numerous genes involved in lipid metabolism and astaxanthin biosynthesis are either incomplete or misannotated in the current reference genome7–10. Notably, Mao et al.8 identified approximately 35 Kb of identical sequence shared between chromosomes 3 and 9, which was confirmed to be an assembly artifact. Zhang et al.7 found that 18 of 34 carotenoid biosynthesis-related genes were either incomplete or misannotated, accounting for over 50% of this gene set. These findings collectively underscore the need for further sequencing and assembly of the C. zofingiensis genome to ensure its accuracy and utility in functional genomics research.

Furthermore, DNA methylation is a key aspect of epigenetics, and studies have shown that epigenetic regulation plays an important role in the adaptation of the model alga C. reinhardtii to abiotic stress11. Methylation can regulate gene expression and thereby affect cellular metabolic processes, including lipid metabolism and astaxanthin biosynthesis12–14. To date, however, no studies have reported on DNA methylation in C. zofingiensis.

Here, to obtain a well-annotated chromosome-level genome of C. zofingiensis, we employed a hybrid approach of long-reads sequencing (Nanopore) and short-reads sequencing (Illumina), together with assistance of the high-through chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) technology and full-length transcriptome sequencing (PacBio). Compared with the previously published C. zofingiensis genome, our version showed significant improvements in genome sequencing, assembly, and annotation. However, it should be acknowledged that some gaps still remain in the current assembly, and future efforts will focus on further improving and refining the genome. Additionally, we probed the spatial distribution characteristics of genome-wide DNA methylation (N6-adenine and 5-methylcytosine) through an integrated analysis of Nanopore and bisulfite sequencing data. Future work should aim to capture dynamic epigenetic changes across different environmental and physiological states. In summary, our work provides an improved, chromosome-level genome, and for the first time, profiles the genome-wide DNA methylation landscape of this industrially relevant microalga.

Methods

Algal strain and sample materials

The Chromochloris zofingiensis strain ATCC 30412 was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection and maintained on Kuhl agar plates, with the medium composition described previously15. Seed cultures used for subsequent experiments were obtained after 48 hours of cultivation under standard conditions: aeration with 1.5% (v/v) CO₂-enriched air at 25 °C and continuous illumination at 80 μmol photons m−2 s−1. C. zofingiensis cells used for whole-genome sequencing were collected after 48 hours of cultivation under standard conditions. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4500 rpm for 5 minutes, washed twice with deionized water, and rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen. For full-length transcriptome sequencing, a mixed sample was prepared by pooling cells grown under ten different culture conditions. Samples were collected at 0, 6, 12, and 24 hours, respectively. The specific culture conditions were as follows: Control (standard conditions), High light (300 μmol photons m−2 s−1), Salt stress (0.2 M NaCl added), Nitrogen deprivation (KNO₃ omitted), Sulfur deprivation (MgSO₄·7H₂O replaced with MgCl₂), Phosphorus deprivation (NaH₂PO₄·H₂O and Na₂HPO₄·2H₂O omitted), No CO₂ supply, Heterotrophic (5 g/L glucose added, cultured in darkness with orbital shaking at 150 rpm), Mixotrophic (5 g/L glucose added), and BG-11 medium(used in place of Kuhl medium).

Sequencing strategy

DNA and RNA were extracted using the Plant Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (Cat. No. DP305-02, Tiangen, China) and the Plant RNA Extraction Kit (Cat. No. 9769, Takara, Japan), respectively. All procedures related to whole-genome sequencing were conducted strictly according to the protocols provided by Oxford Nanopore Technologies. Following library quality control, third-generation Nanopore sequencing was performed using the PromethION48 platform. Raw sequencing data were then subjected to quality control and contamination assessment. High-resolution chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) sequencing was performed on the Illumina platform using paired-end 150 bp reads (PE150) based on sequencing-by-synthesis technology. The Hi-C library was constructed using the in situ Hi-C protocol, which primarily consists of the following steps: cell crosslinking, restriction enzyme digestion, end repair, proximity ligation, DNA purification, fragment enrichment, and high-throughput sequencing16,17. Full-length transcriptome sequencing was conducted using PacBio third-generation technology in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols. After passing library quality control, Single-Molecule Real-Time sequencing was conducted using the PacBio SMRT platform18. In brief, the analysis process for obtaining the full-length transcriptome mainly consists of three stages19: identification of full-length sequences, isoform-level clustering to obtain consensus sequences, and polishing of the consensus sequences. Both Nanopore third-generation sequencing and bisulfite sequencing were employed for DNA methylation analysis. The raw electrical signals obtained from Nanopore sequencing were basecalled using Guppy20 to generate raw sequence files. These raw sequences were then subjected to quality assessment and filtering to obtain clean reads for subsequent bioinformatics analyses. Bisulfite sequencing was conducted on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with paired-end 150 bp reads (PE150).

Genome assembly

A total of 2,053,968 reads (17.08 Gb) passed quality filtering and were retained for subsequent assembly, with an average read length of 8,313 bp and an N50 of 9,496 bp (Table 1). Genome assembly was performed using Canu21 for error correction and Smartdenovo for de novo assembly, followed by three rounds of polishing with third-generation and second-generation sequencing data using Racon22 and Pilon23, respectively. The final assembly yielded a genome size of 57.93 Mb comprising 32 contigs, with a contig N50 of 2.75 Mb and a GC content of 50.52%. Alignment of second-generation sequencing data showed that 93.25% of clean reads could be mapped to the assembled genome, with 92.62% of read pairs properly aligned. Genome completeness was assessed using the CEGMA v2.524 and BUSCO v2.025 databases. CEGMA identified 425 out of 458 core eukaryotic genes (92.79%) and 226 out of 248 highly conserved genes (91.13%), while BUSCO detected 266 out of 303 conserved core genes (87.79%). C. zofingiensis represents a relatively divergent green algal lineage, and some conserved BUSCO genes may have undergone substantial sequence divergence, thereby reducing the detection sensitivity of BUSCO analyses. In addition, residual limitations in genome assembly and/or annotation may still exist. Together, these factors likely contribute to the relatively lower BUSCO completeness score. Nevertheless, the high CEGMA score, combined with the high mapping rate of Illumina reads, supports the overall reliability and improved completeness of the C. zofingiensis genome assembly.

Statistics of Sequencing Results.

A total of 15.74 Gb clean reads were obtained from the Hi-C sequencing data after quality control (Table 1). The Q20 and Q30 scores were 98.79% and 96.10%, respectively. Read alignment was performed using BWA26 (v0.7.10-r789; mode: aln), showing that 92.55% (97,251,910) of the reads could be mapped to the genome, of which 78.83% (41,420,263) of these were uniquely mapped, meeting the requirements for downstream analyses. Of the uniquely mapped reads, 31,586,077 read pairs were identified as valid Hi-C contacts, accounting for 76.26% of the total, thereby confirming that the Hi-C library construction satisfied the required quality standards.

After Hi-C assembly and manual refinement, the C. zofingiensis genome was assembled to a total size of 57.09 Mb, with 17 chromosomes. Of the assembled genome, 54.15 Mb (94.85%) could be anchored to the 17 chromosomes. Among these, 52.60 Mb (97.12%) were oriented and ordered with high confidence. Post-assembly statistics showed 56 scaffolds and 90 contigs, with a scaffold N50 of approximately 3.18 Mb and a contig N50 of approximately 1.40 Mb. The final assembly contained 34 gaps and had a GC content of 50.51% (Table 2). As visualized in the Hi-C chromosomal interaction heatmap (Fig. 1), the 17 chromosomes exhibited clear and distinct interaction signals, further validating the quality and accuracy of the genome assembly.

Genome annotation

PacBio full-length transcriptome sequencing yielded approximately 10 Gb of clean reads after quality control. From these, 7,302 non-redundant transcript sequences were identified for downstream genome annotation. The C. zofingiensis genome contains 6,939 repetitive sequences, with a total length of 2,386,161 bp, accounting for only 4.15% of the genome, indicating a low level of repeat content. A total of 12,480 protein-coding genes were identified, representing a substantial reduction compared with the 15,274 genes reported in the 2017 draft genome. This reduction primarily reflects the elimination of erroneous scaffolding and redundant gene annotations present in the earlier assembly. For example, in the 2017 draft genome, an identical ~35 kb genomic fragment encoding 10 genes was present on both chromosomes 3 and 9. Our chromosome-level assembly demonstrates that this fragment was incorrectly scaffolded onto chromosome 9 in the previous version, a conclusion that is further supported by experimental evidence8. In addition, several key genes involved in lipid metabolism were previously misannotated as multiple genes. Specifically, the diacylglycerol acyltransferase gene DGAT1A (Czo05g01100) was incorrectly annotated as two separate genes (Cz06g05010 and Cz06g04190); DGTT3 (Czo02g03170) was split into Cz03g14070 and Cz03g14080; the fatty acids desaturase gene FAD4 (Czo11g02150) was split into Cz12g10230 and Cz12g10220; FAD6B (Czo10g04690) was split into Cz11g21120 and Cz11g21110; and the long-chain acyl-CoA synthetase gene LACS6 (Czo08g01840) was split into Cz08g09130 and Cz08g09120. Annotation of non-coding RNAs and pseudogenes further revealed the presence of 33 rRNAs, 128 tRNAs, and 78 pseudogenes, while no miRNAs were detected.

The protein-coding genes of C. zofingiensis were functionally annotated using multiple databases. Among them, 85.89% of the genes could be annotated based on databases such as NR, GO, KOG, and KEGG. NR annotation revealed that C. zofingiensis shares the highest number of homologous genes (2,919 genes) with Volvox carteri, followed by Chlamydomonas reinhardtii, with 2,376 homologous genes (Fig. 2a). GO analysis indicated that genes related to catalytic activity were the most abundant in the C. zofingiensis genome (Fig. 2b). According to KOG functional classification, the most represented category was genes with general function prediction, followed by those involved in posttranslational modification, protein turnover, and chaperones, and then genes associated with signal transduction mechanisms (Fig. 2c). In addition, 597 predicted motif structures and 9,912 domain structures were identified in the C. zofingiensis genome.

Genomic feature analysis and comparison with previous versions

The first genome sequencing and annotation of C. zofingiensis was released by Roth et al. in 20173. This remains the only reference genome available to date, and, as noted in the background section, its assembly was incomplete and requires further improvement. In this study, the third-generation Nanopore sequencing technology was used, achieving an average read length of 8,313 bp, which effectively overcomes the limitations of short-read assemblies. Furthermore, Hi-C technology was employed for chromosome-level scaffolding, resulting in only 34 assembly gaps, significantly fewer than the 52 gaps reported in Roth’s genome (Table 3). We further analyzed the genomic distribution of these 34 gaps in relation to gene density (Fig. 3). The majority of the gaps are located in gene-poor regions (i.e., repetitive sequence regions). A small number of gaps (approximately 7) are situated in intergenic regions, which also exhibit relatively low gene density. Future efforts to filling these gaps may involve integrated analysis using multiple assembly tools, cross-platform data integration, or PCR amplification with specific primers designed at gap boundaries to further enhance genome completeness and assembly accuracy. The contig N50 of the assembled genome in this study reached 2.75 Mb, nearly twice that of Roth’s version, indicating a substantially improved assembly quality. Telomeres, which are specialized DNA sequences located at the ends of chromosomes, play a crucial role in chromosomal stability and complete replication27. In C. zofingiensis, the 5′ telomeric repeat is (CCCTAAA)n and the 3′ telomeric repeat is the reverse complement (TTTAGGG)n. In this study, among the 17 chromosomes assembled, 13 chromosomes were successfully assembled at both 5′ and 3′ telomeric ends, while 4 chromosomes were assembled at only one end, totaling 30 out of 34 telomeric regions successfully assembled (Fig. 4). In contrast, Roth’s genome included 19 chromosomes, but only 25 out of 38 telomeric regions were successfully assembled. Specifically, 8 chromosomes were assembled at both ends, 9 chromosomes were assembled at only one end, and 2 chromosomes were not assembled at either telomeric end. These comparisons further support the superior completeness and structural accuracy of the present genome assembly.

DNA methylation sequencing

Nanopore sequencing raw data were generated in binary FAST5 format, which contains the complete raw electrical signal for each read (one read per FAST5 file). Basecalling was performed using Guppy to convert FAST5 files into FASTQ format for downstream quality control and analysis. After filtering the raw data to remove adapters, short fragments less than 500 bp, and low-quality reads, a total of 2,514,524 clean reads were obtained, comprising 19,575,437,974 bp. The N50 and N90 read length was 9,329 bp and 5,091 bp, respectively. Analysis of sequencing depth and alignment efficiency revealed that 2,382,679 clean reads (94.76%) could be aligned to the C. zofingiensis reference genome.

In C. zofingiensis genome, Nanopore sequencing identified 26,929,107 N6-methyladenine (6 mA) methylation sites and 28,283,445 5-methylcytosine (5mC) methylation sites, including 7,761,764 CHG, 17,551,896 CHH, and 2,969,785 CpG methylation sites. Genome-wide methylation level analysis revealed that CpG methylation exhibited the highest overall methylation levels (Fig. 5). The distribution patterns of four DNA methylation types (6 mA, CpG, CHH, and CHG) across repetitive regions, CpG islands, and genic regions of the C. zofingiensis genome are shown in Fig. 6. To explore sequence-specific methylation preferences, motif analysis was performed on the 9 bp flanking regions of the top 1,000 most highly methylated CpG, CHG, and CHH cytosines (Fig. 7a–c). The results showed that CpG methylation exhibited no clear sequence preference, whereas CHG and CHH methylation showed preferred motifs, GCTGC and GCAA, respectively. Analysis of the 21 bp flanking regions of the top 1,000 highly methylated 6 mA sites further identified a conserved CAAC motif (Fig. 7d). Given that Nanopore sequencing indicated CpG methylation as the most abundant modification in the C. zofingiensis genome and its depletion near transcription start sites (TSS), potentially associated with gene expression, we further validated CpG methylation patterns using bisulfite sequencing. Genomic features were divided into different regions according to the genome annotation file, and the number of CpG sites and their methylation levels within each region were quantified (Fig. 8). The results showed that CpG sites were highly methylated (>50%) across most regions except the 5′ UTR, where methylation levels were relatively low, consistent with Nanopore-based observations.

Data Records

The Nanopore sequencing dataset (used for both genome assembly and DNA methylation analysis), Illumina short-reads, Hi-C sequencing reads, PacBio full-length transcriptome data, and bisulfite sequencing data generated in this study have been deposited in the National Genomics Data Center (NGDC), with the corresponding Genome Sequence Archive (GSA) accession numbers are CRA03105028, CRA03090429, CRA03089530, CRA03091331, and CRA03091732, respectively. Additionally, the final chromosome-level genome assembly and annotation results have been deposited in GenBank at NCBI under BioProject accessions https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject/PRJNA145257333.

Technical Validation

Genome completeness was assessed using the CEGMA v2.524 and BUSCO v2.025 databases. The CEGMA analysis showed 92.79% and 91.13% completeness for core eukaryotic genes and highly conserved genes, respectively, while the final BUSCO results showed 87.79% completeness. The Hi-C sequencing data were aligned to the genome using BWA26 (v0.7.10-r789; aln mode), revealing that 92.55% of the reads could be mapped to the genome. Overall, the C. zofingiensis genome assemblies obtained in this study showed excellent completeness and high accuracy.
