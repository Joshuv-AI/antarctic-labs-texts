Predictive factors of successful tympanoplasty and quality of life outcomes in adults with dry perforation: a randomised clinical trial
Authors: Adel Azar, Samer Mohsen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding clinical outcomes following tympanoplasty is essential for effectively counseling patients and providing a comprehensive perspective on its benefits and limitations. To evaluate the effects of tympanoplasty on ear-related clinical symptoms, hearing function, health-related quality of life (HRQol), and symptoms of depression and anxiety. A randomized, non-blinded clinical trial was conducted. Patients scheduled for tympanoplasty were compared with a control group of individuals on the surgical waiting list. A single academic tertiary care center. The study included 111 participants (41 males [36.94%], 70 females [63.06%]; mean age = 34.41 ± 12.78 years) with dry tympanic membrane perforations. Among them, 63 underwent tympanoplasty, with success and failure rates of 33:18 (64.71%: 35.29%) at 3 months, and 22:16 (57.89%: 42.11%) at 6 months. The remaining 48 served as controls, with 43 (89.58%) followed up at 3 months. Tympanoplasty was evaluated at three time points—preoperatively (T0), and postoperatively at 3 months (T3) and 6 months (T6). Primary outcomes included scores from the Arabic version of the Ear Outcome Survey-16 (EOS-16) and the Arabic Version of the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), assessed at T0, T3, and T6. For successful tympanoplasty: the OverallQ1-14 score ((30.41 ± 12.09), (15.36 ± 6.97) and (15.55 ± 6.88), n = 22, p < 0.001), for failed tympanoplasty: the OverallQ1-14 score ((25.69 ± 11.84), (21.94 ± 9.81) and (21.31 ± 11.85), n = 16, p = 0.3) and for controls: the OverallQ1-14 score ((27.02 ± 12.88) and (26.49 ± 13.26), n = 43, p = 0.59). Almost all distinct items showed statistical significance p < 0.05 after successful tympanoplasty against none after failure tympanoplasty while controls remained unchanged. Multiple linear regression equation of depression score (T3-T0) in successful tympanoplasty “Ydepression = (2.44 + 0.162xOverall + 0.156xAC) + Ɛdepression”. Successful tympanoplasty significantly improved ear symptoms, hearing function, psychosocial well-being, and overall health-related quality of life. Although it had no immediate or direct effect on depression or anxiety, improvements in hearing and quality of life were predictive of reductions in depressive symptoms. These findings highlight the broader psychosocial benefits of tympanoplasty and support its role in holistic patient care.Trial registration This study was registered with ANZCTR (ACTRN12625000436471) on 09/05/2025.Keywords: Otology, Tympanoplasty, Success, Predictive Factors, Chronic otitis media (COM), Quality of life (Qol), Health-related quality of life (HRQoL), Depression, Anxiety, Patient-related outcome measure (PROM)

Introduction

Tympanoplasty is a surgical procedure used to repair perforations of the tympanic membrane, with or without ossicular chain reconstruction, aiming to restore conductive hearing and prevent middle ear infections. Middle ear reconstruction is categorized into five types based on the extent of anatomical damage:

Type I: Reconstruction of the tympanic membrane with an intact ossicular chain.

Type I: Reconstruction of the tympanic membrane with an intact ossicular chain.

Type II: Repair involving minor defects in the ossicles.

Type II: Repair involving minor defects in the ossicles.

Type III: Establishing a direct connection between the tympanic membrane and the stapes head.

Type III: Establishing a direct connection between the tympanic membrane and the stapes head.

Type IV: Protecting the round window while the oval window remains open to the external auditory canal.

Type IV: Protecting the round window while the oval window remains open to the external auditory canal.

Type V: Treating a fixed stapes by creating a new fenestra (fenestra novovalis).

Type V: Treating a fixed stapes by creating a new fenestra (fenestra novovalis).

A good outcome is typically defined as a postoperative air–bone gap of less than 15 dB across frequencies from 500 to 2000 Hz, which are critical for speech understanding. Outcomes with hearing loss between 15 and 30 dB are considered satisfactory. However, accurately determining the appropriate tympanoplasty type before surgery is often challenging due to factors such as residual tympanic membrane tissue, adhesions, and granulation tissue1.

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines quality of life as an individual’s perception of their position in life, within the context of the culture and value systems in which they live, and in relation to their goals, expectations, standards, and concerns 2. Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) focuses on specific dimensions of overall quality of life, typically evaluating physical functioning (e.g., daily activities and hobbies), symptoms (both general and disease-specific), treatment side effects, and performance in everyday life, including social interactions, communication, and alertness3.

Several factors predict HRQoL outcomes following ear surgery, including complications such as wound infections, tympanic membrane perforations, retractions, and worsened postoperative air conduction thresholds. Demographic variables such as gender, socioeconomic status, and educational level also play a role4.

Patients with chronic otitis media (COM) exhibit a higher prevalence of preoperative depressive disorders, which are modifiable predictors of HRQoL following middle ear reconstructive surgery5. Addressing these psychological factors is crucial, as untreated depression can lead to significant personal and societal costs. For instance, depression increases the risk of social disability by up to 23 times, even when physical illnesses are considered 6. Therefore, the diagnosis, treatment, and prevention of depression in COM patients are essential for improving postoperative HRQoL and reducing the overall burden of disease.

Numerous studies have investigated clinical symptoms, hearing improvement, psychological distress, and quality of life before and after both microscopic and endoscopic tympanoplasty—including primary and revision procedures—at various follow-up points (commonly T0, T3, T6, and T12), using a variety of questionnaires5,7–14.

The objective of this study is to evaluate the impact of tympanoplasty on clinical outcomes, quality of life, and levels of depression and anxiety. Additionally, it seeks to compare subjective outcomes across different demographic groups among patients with chronic otitis media.

Uniquely, this is the first study to directly compare successful and failed tympanoplasty cases with a control group of patients who have dry tympanic membrane perforations. Furthermore, the study explores the interrelationships between depression, anxiety, quality of life domains, and specific hearing frequencies.

Methods

Study design

A randomized, non-blinded clinical trial was conducted from January to December 2024 at Al Mouwasat University Hospital, affiliated with Damascus University, to investigate clinical outcomes and quality of life after tympanoplasty. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine at Damascus University (MD-140824-H16).

Simple randomization was implemented using a computer-generated randomization table, assigning 48 participants to the control group, with the remaining participants allocated to the surgery group. Allocation concealment was ensured through centralized randomization conducted via phone Table 1.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated based on a similar study, using a Type I error (two-tailed) of 5% (zₐ = 1.960) and a statistical power of 90% (zᵦ = 1.282). From Table 2 of the referenced study7, which reported overall preoperative and postoperative Chronic Ear Survey (CES) and Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-21 (DASS-21) results, the observed difference between the two repeated measures was D = 13.5 (standardized effect size = 0.734), and the population standard deviation was σ = 18.4. Substituting these values into the standard formula15: N = 4σ2(zcrit + zpwr)2/D2 yields a total required sample size of N = 78.

For a design with one pre-treatment and one post-treatment measurement, approximately 60 patients per group would be needed. With two post-treatment measurements, this number is reduced to fewer than 40 patients per group. Since this study included three repeated measurements and assumed homogeneity of the data, we considered the lower sample size threshold to be acceptable16.

Additionally, because the study used Likert-type scales and pure tone audiometry (PTA) that increase in 5 dB increments, the data distribution is constrained and ordinal in nature. Therefore, non-parametric statistical tests were deemed appropriate for analysis17.

Measurements

The Arabic version of the Ear Outcome Survey-16 (EOS-16) is a simple, clear, reliable, reproducible, and valid tool for assessing health-related quality of life (HRQoL). It consists of four subscales, each using a 5-point Likert-type response format:

Ear symptoms (Questions 1, 2, 3, 4, and 6; Cronbach’s alpha = 0.78)

Ear symptoms (Questions 1, 2, 3, 4, and 6; Cronbach’s alpha = 0.78)

Hearing impairment (Questions 5, 7, 8, and 9; Cronbach’s alpha = 0.85)

Hearing impairment (Questions 5, 7, 8, and 9; Cronbach’s alpha = 0.85)

Psychosocial impact (Questions 10–14; Cronbach’s alpha = 0.89)

Psychosocial impact (Questions 10–14; Cronbach’s alpha = 0.89)

Need for care (Questions 15 and 16; Cronbach’s alpha = 0.79)

Need for care (Questions 15 and 16; Cronbach’s alpha = 0.79)

However, the corrected item-total correlations for Questions 15 and 16 were below 0.3, indicating a limited contribution to the overall HRQoL assessment18. Furthermore, potential bias may arise due to variations in postoperative care—such as patients returning for stitch removal one week after surgery and applying ear drops two weeks later to moisturize the aural pack, which is typically removed in the third week. Given these factors, the overall EOS-16 score in this study was calculated using only Questions 1 through 14. This is the first clinical trial to apply the EOS-16 with a three -month recall period, capturing the chronic nature of the disease course.

The Arabic version of the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) is also a reliable and valid instrument for assessing mood in non-psychiatric hospitalized patients and for identifying the severity of anxiety and depression. It consists of 14 items scored on a 4-point Likert scale—7 items assess anxiety (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.83), and 7 items assess depression (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.77). Total scores are interpreted as follows:

Normal: 0–7, Borderline: 8–10, and Case (clinical anxiety or depression): 11–21

The tool has a low rate of false positives and false negatives (1–5%) across the categories of cases, borderline cases, and non-cases. The cut-off point for identifying a “case” can be set either the upper or lower limit of the borderline range, depending on the clinical context19,20.

Participants

Participants aged between 18 and 55 years with dry tympanic membrane perforations were included in the study. Patients scheduled for tympanoplasty were evaluated at three time points: preoperatively (T0), and at 3 months (T3) and 6 months (T6) postoperatively. A control group consisting of patients on the surgical waiting list, was also assessed at baseline and re-evaluated after 3 months. All data were collected by the same physician to ensure consistency across assessments. Exclusion criteria included individuals younger than 18 years; patients with active squamous chronic otitis media (cholesteatoma); acute perforation; severe, uncontrolled chronic illnesses; use of hearing aids; physical disabilities; current use of psychiatric medications; and pregnancy.

Procedure

All surgeries were performed using the postauricular approach under general anesthesia. The procedure involved harvesting temporalis fascia or conchal cartilage, refreshing the edges of the perforation and the medial surface of the tympanic membrane, elevating the meatal flap and annulus, and preparing the middle ear space. Ossicular reconstruction, when required, was carried out using cartilage grafts, incus interposition, or partial ossicular replacement prostheses (PORPs). The underlay grafting technique was employed in all cases.

The Eustachian tube and tympanic cavity were packed with gelfoam to support graft placement. Patients were administered acetaminophen as needed and were observed in a day-surgery unit for approximately 24 h postoperatively.

All surgeries were performed by senior otolaryngology residents under the supervision of ENT and otologic surgeons. The anticipated duration of each procedure was approximately 2 h.

Postoperative complications monitored included wound infection, recurrent perforation, persistent or recurrent otorrhea, tympanic membrane retraction with an air–bone gap exceeding 10 dB, postoperative vomiting, granulation tissue formation in the external auditory canal, and facial nerve paresis.

Successful tympanoplasty is defined by an intact tympanic membrane upon examination at three months postoperatively; conversely, finding a perforation indicates a failed tympanoplasty.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26 and GraphPad Prism version 8.0.1 for statistical computation and graph generation, respectively. Descriptive statistics, including means, medians, and standard deviations, were used to provide a comprehensive summary of the data.

Group comparisons were performed using non-parametric tests due to the ordinal nature and non-normal distribution of the data. These included:

Friedman’s Two-Way Analysis of Variance by Ranks (for related samples),

Friedman’s Two-Way Analysis of Variance by Ranks (for related samples),

Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test (for within-group comparisons),

Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test (for within-group comparisons),

Mann–Whitney U Test (for between-group comparisons), and

Mann–Whitney U Test (for between-group comparisons), and

Kruskal–Wallis Test (for comparisons across more than two independent groups).

Kruskal–Wallis Test (for comparisons across more than two independent groups).

Mean ranks were reported when data distribution across groups differed, as indicated by a Two-Sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test result of p < 0.05.

Mean ranks were reported when data distribution across groups differed, as indicated by a Two-Sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test result of p < 0.05.

Effect sizes were calculated for all relevant statistical tests to quantify the strength of observed relationships and enhance the interpretability and comparability of results 21. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 with a 95% confidence interval.

The Chi-squared (χ2) test was used to assess independence between categorical variables. Binary logistic regression was employed to identify predictors of successful tympanoplasty outcomes.

Correlation analyses were performed using Spearman’s rho to examine relationships between HADS-D, HADS-A, overall EOS-16 scores, and air conduction (AC) threshold scores at each time point. Additionally, Pearson correlation was used to assess the linear relationships between changes in hearing, quality of life, depression, and anxiety over time.

Finally, multiple linear regression was applied to predict changes in depression scores (T3–T0) based on changes in overall EOS-16 scores and changes in AC threshold scores over the same interval.

Results

Participants

A total of 111 participants were included in this study, with a mean age of 34.41 years (SD = 12.78). Of these, 63 underwent tympanoplasty, while 48 served as controls. At the 3-month postoperative examination (T3), 33 patients had a successful tympanoplasty, and 18 had a failed procedure. These numbers changed at the 6-month follow-up (T6), with 22 successful and 16 failed cases. Of the 48 controls, 43 completed the examination at 3 months. The graft material, ossicular chain reconstruction, and complications observed 3 months post-surgery are detailed in Table 1.

The dropout rate in the tympanoplasty group was 19.05% (12/63) at T3, increasing to 39.68% (25/63) at T6. In the control group, 10.42% (5/48) dropped out by T3. Our analysis assumes that missing data were “missing completely at random”, primarily due to participants’ inability to travel for follow-up appointments. A complete case analysis revealed no systematic differences between dropouts and completers, suggesting that the results remain valid 22.

Regarding audiometric data, the mean air conduction (AC) threshold for the contralateral ear (whether retracted or previously treated with tympanoplasty) was significantly higher in the untreated ear (mean = 29.27 dB, SD = 10.98) compared to the intact contralateral ear (mean = 17.19 dB, SD = 7.18), with a statistically significant difference (U = 219.50, z = −4.97, p < 0.001). Similarly, the mean air–bone gap was also significantly greater in the contralateral ear (mean = 17.08 dB, SD = 7.41) compared to the intact ear (mean = 4.83 dB, SD = 4.59), with a significant difference (U = 107.50, z = −6.10, p < 0.001). There was no sensorineural hearing loss across time points. An overview of the audiometric data for the treated ear is provided in Table 2.

Regarding recurrent perforation, 10 out of 26 participants with an intact contralateral ear experienced a recurrent perforation, compared to 2 out of 10 participants with a non-intact contralateral ear. However, this difference was not statistically significant (Pearson Chi-Square = 1.11, df = 1, p = 0.44). Similarly, 12 out of 37 participants with a unilateral perforation experienced recurrent perforation, compared to 6 out of 14 participants with bilateral perforations. This difference was also not statistically significant (Pearson Chi-Square = 0.48, df = 1, p = 0.53).

Discussion

Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) plays a crucial role in patient management decisions, complementing clinical evaluations by assessing changes in a patient’s condition and their ability to adapt within societal contexts. HRQoL encompasses various dimensions, providing a comprehensive measure of the impact of disease and its treatment on an individual’s life. In this study, patient experiences and suffering were thoroughly evaluated by comparing surgical and control groups, offering a detailed overview of the outcomes associated with different treatment strategies. Our findings indicate that successful tympanoplasty performed in an academic center significantly improves overall quality of life by addressing both physical and psychological aspects of health. These results underscore the importance of such procedures in enhancing patient well-being, particularly when the surgery is successful.

Patients often report pain, which is uncommon in chronic otitis media (COM), as a means to encourage increased care from doctors during the examination to seek relief 25. Direct treatment targeting potential causes of ear fullness 26,27 and addressing directional horizontal nystagmus 28 have been shown to significantly improve sensations of pressure and dizziness following successful surgery through perforation closure, which eliminate the triggers responsible for these symptoms.

Persistent itching after successful surgery can occur due to ongoing skin inflammation and the wound healing process, with potential therapeutic agents to alleviate this symptom 29. Additionally, increased moisture levels have been linked to isolated ear itching, even when the external ear canal examination appears normal 30. Chronic discharge and infection may result from elevated heat and humidity within the external canal, highlighting the need for control measures 31, such as limiting the use of ear packing and preventing moisture exposure. Although recommendations from ear surgeons regarding water precautions vary 32, their impact on patients largely depends on individual lifestyle factors.

Unpleasant clinical manifestations, hearing loss, and poor speech discrimination in noise can all adversely impact a patient’s social communication and psychological well-being. These issues may lead to embarrassment and social withdrawal, as patients often need to ask repeatedly for clarification to understand speech correctly. Such challenges tend to improve significantly following successful surgery. Furthermore, achieving normal hearing across all frequencies is crucial for accurate sound localization 33, which is essential for effective communication and overall quality of life.

Hearing loss was identified as the most significant factor prompting patients to undergo surgery. Following successful tympanoplasty, hearing improved by an average of 10.5 dB, and the associated psychological distress from other symptoms was alleviated. As a result, tinnitus was no longer perceived as a personal concern six months postoperatively. In contrast, patients who experienced failed surgery showed only a modest hearing improvement of 4–6 dB, which did not translate into meaningful personal benefit; in fact, tinnitus complaints increased in this group. These findings support the role of successful surgery as a key factor in the effective management of tinnitus 34. The degree of patient-reported benefit was more closely related to the magnitude of improvement in air-conduction thresholds rather than the attainment of a specific threshold level 35, highlighting the subjective importance of perceptible hearing gains over absolute audiometric outcomes.

Our study demonstrated a significant postoperative decrease in the air–bone gap, from 27.35 ± 6.36 dB preoperatively to 17.65 ± 9.72 dB after successful tympanoplasty. This improvement was closely associated with enhanced patient-reported hearing outcomes, indicating that surgical restoration of hearing plays a crucial role in improving overall health-related quality of life (HRQoL). However, in patients with surgical failure, the hearing improvement—and the associated HRQoL benefit—was diminished, underscoring the central role of hearing restoration in driving perceived benefit. These results align with findings from other studies, such as one reporting notable improvements in the Chronic Otitis Media Outcome Test-15 (COMOT-15) scores and a decrease in the air–bone gap from 28.85 ± 7.99 dB to 12.42 ± 7.26 dB, along with substantial improvements in self-reported hearing loss and speech understanding difficulties 36. Additionally, another research observed a decrease in the mean air–bone gap from 28.92 ± 8.83 dB before surgery to 16.47 ± 5.86 dB afterward. Both the Chronic Ear Survey (CES) and COMOT-15 scores showed significant enhancements postoperatively. Overall, ear surgery in patients with chronic otitis media (COM) significantly improves hearing function and quality of life 37.

The impact of tympanoplasty on both physical and psychological functioning was most clearly demonstrated by the significant improvement in the item Impact on HRQoL (Q14). More importantly, the overallQ1–14 score proved to be the most accurate indicator of health-related quality of life in patients with chronic otitis media. This score improved by 15 points following successful surgery, compared to only a 4-point increase after failed surgery, and remained unchanged in the control group. Persistent complications—such as recurrent infections, perforations, and worsening hearing loss—combined with limited public awareness about ear health, contribute to patients’ ongoing fear of symptom deterioration. This fear can have a profound psychological impact; for example, studies in multiple sclerosis have shown that fear can significantly reduce quality of life and exacerbate symptoms of depression and anxiety 38. These findings highlight the importance of actively engaging with and supporting patients to mitigate fear and promote both physical recovery and psychological well-being.

No significant demographic differences were observed among participants, indicating that the sample was homogeneous and that the data are likely generalizable. Interestingly, women reported greater disturbances from ear symptoms and other health-related quality of life domains, despite having better hearing thresholds. This may be linked to generally higher anxiety levels reported among women. These results align with prior research indicating that all subdivisions—including symptoms, hearing, psychosocial, and resources—as well as the total scores of the Chronic Otitis Media Outcome Test-15 (COMOT-15) and Zurich Chronic Middle Ear Inventory (ZCMEI-21), tend to be higher in women compared to men. This may potentially reflect differing coping strategies 39, with women being more distressed by health issues. However, there were no significant gender differences in the scores of the COMOT-15 and CES assessments among patients with cholesteatomatous otitis media who underwent either canal wall down or canal wall up tympanomastoidectomy, with an average preoperative pure-tone average (PTA) of 50 ± 18 dB 40. This suggests that the gender-related disparities in quality of life measures may be more pronounced in less severe or different clinical contexts, or that the type of surgical intervention and degree of hearing impairment influence perceived quality of life similarly across genders in more severe cases.

Smokers also exhibited lower social interaction scores, likely influenced by stigma-related experiences such as humiliation, blame, and punishment 41, in addition to the burden of hearing loss. When the contralateral ear was not intact—due to retraction or a history of tympanoplasty—bilateral perforation emerged as a major factor affecting patients’ perception of ear-related problems. This was reflected in higher scores across all subdivisions, especially in the domains of hearing impairment and overall quality of life. However, unlike findings from a comparable study 42, our results did not show a statistically significant difference in depression and anxiety scores among patients with bilateral perforations.

Several factors may contribute to the high rate of recurrent perforations observed. These include Eustachian tube dysfunction and the involvement of senior residents performing the procedures, although they may achieve good auditory outcomes 43. However, the most significant factor appears to be inflammation and infection, as all cases of discharge followed failed tympanoplasty. Increased moisture, likely exacerbated by the 21-day packing, may have played a role in this recurrence. Additionally, patient non-compliance with medical advice—such as avoiding Valsalva maneuvers and other activities that increase middle ear pressure—may have contributed to the higher failure rate.

The success rate of tympanoplasty varies across institutions and tends to decline over time; for instance,Bhatia et al 11 reported that 82% of patients maintained an intact graft at six months postoperatively. In a tertiary teaching hospital where all procedures were performed by residents under supervision, the graft closure rate was 84.12% at one month and decreased to 72.72% after twelve months. Additionally, perforation closure rates differed based on surgical approach, with an 85% success rate for endoscopic techniques compared to 57.14% for microscopic-assisted procedures 44. Predictors of surgical success included the use of endoscopy, cartilage grafting, and longer preoperative periods without otorrhea. Factors such as gender, age, perforation size, contralateral ear disease, operated side, reoperation status, and degree of hearing loss did not significantly impact outcomes 44.

This study is the first to compare subjective patient responses between successful and failed tympanoplasty procedures. Patients who underwent successful tympanoplasty showed clear and significant improvements in ear symptoms and overall quality of life. In contrast, those with failed procedures were more likely to experience persistent otorrhea. Interestingly, there was no significant difference in hearing impairment between the two groups, with audiometric improvements differing by only 5 dB. Although the six-month outcomes still favored the successful surgery group, most differences were not statistically significant—except for a notable improvement in depression scores following successful surgery. One possible explanation for this may be the link between local inflammation, neurotransmitter levels, microbiota imbalances, and the severity of depression 45. These physiological factors could influence both the healing process and psychological responses, potentially impacting tympanoplasty outcomes. While direct effects of surgery on depression and anxiety scores were not significant, improvements in hearing thresholds and overall quality of life were predictive of reductions in depression scores, particularly after successful surgery. This finding highlights the potential role of inflammatory modulation and auditory rehabilitation in improving mental health outcomes postoperatively.

Study limitations include the dropout rate and the difficulty in assessing Eustachian tube patency. Despite these limitations, the study benefitted from consistent surgical methods, as procedures were performed by senior residents under supervision, resulting in satisfactory audiological outcomes—even with variability in perforation closure rates 43.

Conclusion

Successful tympanoplasty significantly improves ear symptoms, hearing thresholds, and health-related quality of life in patients with chronic otitis media. These benefits were most evident in patients with successful surgical outcomes. Our findings support the central role of hearing restoration in enhancing quality of life following middle ear surgery.
