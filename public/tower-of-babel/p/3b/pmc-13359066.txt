Unruptured distal anterior cerebral artery branch pseudoaneurysm in the setting of intracranial pressure monitor placement: illustrative case
Authors: Andrew L. Waack, Christina N. Feller, Gerard A. Mackinnon, Stephanie K. Cheok, Francis J. Jareczek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDIntracranial pressure monitors (IPMs) are commonly placed in patients with severe traumatic brain injury to diagnose and guide treatment for intracranial hypertension. Complications of IPM placement include infection, hemorrhage, and malposition. While rare case reports describe ruptured iatrogenic pseudoaneurysms associated with IPM, the incidence and implications of unruptured pseudoaneurysms are poorly understood.OBSERVATIONSA 19-year-old female sustained a severe traumatic brain injury, leading to IPM placement. After a prolonged hospital course, delayed noninvasive imaging to characterize potential cerebrovascular injury raised concern for a carotid-cavernous fistula. Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) ruled out this pathology but revealed an unruptured distal right anterior cerebral artery pseudoaneurysm at the site of the previously placed IPM, which was later successfully treated with endovascular embolization after short-interval repeat DSA.LESSONSWhile pseudoaneurysm formation is a rarely described complication of ICM placement, they are associated with a high rate of rupture (60%) and, subsequently, high mortality (20%). Thus, a high index of suspicion of potential pseudoaneurysm rupture should be maintained in the setting of a delayed rapid decline in neurological examination or delayed intraparenchymal hemorrhage after IPM placement. Urgent evaluation using CT angiography and/or catheter angiography should be considered in addition to standard noncontrast imaging. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE251022

Guidelines recommend the use of intracranial pressure (ICP) monitoring in patients following a survivable traumatic brain injury with a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 3–8 after resuscitation.1 A variety of IPMs are available, although external ventricular drainage and intraparenchymal monitoring (intracranial pressure monitor [IPM] or “bolt”) systems are the two most commonly utilized. IPM placement is a routinely performed neurosurgical procedure that involves trephination with fiber-optic lead insertion into the brain parenchyma. Although complications are relatively rare, those most commonly described include infection and intracranial hemorrhage, with approximately 10% of IPM placements leading to intraparenchymal hemorrhage.2,3 Pseudoaneurysm formation is an extremely rare, and likely delayed, complication of IPM placement, with only 4 prior cases reported in the literature, which were discovered after pseudoaneurysm rupture (Table 1).2–5 Unruptured IPM-associated pseudoaneurysms have not been reported in the literature and, as such, the incidence and potential clinical implications of these pathologies are poorly understood. Illustrative Case

A 19-year-old female without a significant past medical history was brought to the hospital after jumping from a moving vehicle traveling at approximately 30 mph. She was reported to have a GCS score of 3 in the field and was intubated immediately on arrival to the emergency department. CT imaging of the head demonstrated a right-sided acute 5-mm convexity subdural hematoma, a 3-mm anterior parafalcine subdural hematoma, a scattered right frontotemporal subarachnoid hemorrhage with intermixed cerebral contusions, and multiple skull base fractures (Fig. 1A and B). Initial evaluation by the neurosurgical service documented a GCS score of 8T. A right frontal IPM was placed without difficulty in a standard location at Kocher’s point, with no intraprocedural concerns or obvious complications. Postprocedural repeat imaging showed stability of prior hemorrhages, no evidence of new hematoma, and adequate placement of the right frontal IPM without complication (Fig. 1C–E). Her initial ICP was elevated but responded to medical therapy. She was admitted to the neurosciences intensive care unit for ongoing management. On hospital day 5, the IPM was incidentally dislodged, and a new frontal IPM was placed on the contralateral (left) side. Postprocedural repeat imaging again demonstrated no evidence of complication at either IPM site. Ongoing management of intracranial hypertension included placement of a right frontal external ventricular drain (EVD) on hospital day 6 for CSF diversion. This device was placed via a new twist drill hole posterior to the prior IPM burr hole. The EVD and the left-sided IPM were both replaced on hospital day 13. Notably, this is the first imaging study in which a tract hemorrhage along the initial right-sided IPM catheter trajectory was visualized. In retrospect, this likely represents hemorrhage from the inciting vascular injury, which ultimately led to pseudoaneurysm formation (Fig.2A–C). Maximal medical management was continued, and once the patient’s ICP had normalized, the left-sided IPM was removed on hospital day 23.

As part of her initial evaluation, the patient underwent CT angiography (CTA) of the head and neck, which demonstrated luminal narrowing of the left internal carotid artery concerning for Biffl grade 2 blunt cerebrovascular injury, and CT venography, which demonstrated nonocclusive narrowing of the left transverse sinus attributed to a small intraluminal thrombosis versus external compression. After the patient had stabilized clinically, repeat imaging was performed on hospital day 24. This study demonstrated resolution of the ICA injury and improved transverse sinus patency but also some early filling of the left cavernous sinus and superior ophthalmic vein concerning for development of a possible traumatic left carotid-cavernous fistula (CCF) in the sinus, which prompted neuroendovascular consultation. Also noted was a tiny hyperdensity just anterior to the EVD catheter. This hyperdensity was located presumably close to or at the site of the prior right-sided IPM but was overall improved from a prior CT imaging study of the head obtained on hospital day 13 and thus was thought to be a resolving tract hemorrhage (Fig. 2D–F). The patient subsequently underwent a diagnostic digital subtraction angiography (DSA) study on hospital day 27 for workup of her potential CCF. DSA did not demonstrate any findings concerning for CCF but did reveal an incidental finding of an unruptured distal right anterior cerebral artery (ACA) pseudoaneurysm, 3 × 3 × 3.5 mm, arising from a branch of the pericallosal artery and suspected to be at the location of the previous right-sided IPM (Fig. 3A). After multidisciplinary review, the decision was made to perform short-interval repeat DSA to evaluate for interval change in the lesion. In the meantime, the patient subsequently underwent tracheostomy and percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy placement on hospital day 28. Her EVD was removed on hospital day 30. Her neurological examination began to improve dramatically, and she was weaned from the ventilator and transferred to the floor on hospital day 33. At that time, her neurological examination was notable only for disinhibition; she otherwise had no cranial nerve deficits and was able to follow instructions in all four extremities with full and symmetric strength.

The patient returned to the angiography suite for repeat DSA on hospital day 37, 10 days after her initial diagnostic study, which demonstrated persistence of the pseudoaneurysm. Given the poorly understood natural history of such lesions, the potentially high risk of hemorrhage, and its colocation with already injured tissue, the decision was made to proceed with treatment. Both endovascular and open surgical approaches were considered. Given the relatively distal location of the lesion, embolization with a liquid embolic agent was one option offered to the patient. Open surgical management via craniotomy and direct vessel ligation was also considered but not pursued given the subcortical location of the lesion, concern for friable surrounding tissue, and more invasive nature of the procedure. Postprocedure, her neurological examination remained stable, and she did not demonstrate any acute deficits following Onyx 18 embolization of the distal ACA branch (Fig. 3B). She was then discharged to inpatient rehabilitation 41 days after her initial presentation.

She returned to our clinic approximately 1 month after treatment. She denied any new neurological symptoms and remained free of any focal neurological deficits. Repeat CTA revealed embolization material at the site of the treated pseudoaneurysm without evidence of recurrent or new lesions (Fig. 4). Discussion

Observations

IPM placement is a routine neurosurgical procedure performed to monitor ICP in patients with severe traumatic brain injury with the goal of guiding subsequent management and ultimately improving neurological outcomes. Pseudoaneurysm formation is an extremely rare complication of IPM placement, with only 4 prior cases reported in the literature.2–5 Locations of reported pseudoaneurysms after IPMs include branches of the superficial temporal artery (extracranial),2 ACA,4 and middle cerebral artery.3,5 All reported cases describe intraparenchymal hemorrhage associated with these pseudoaneurysms. To our knowledge, this is the first report of an unruptured pseudoaneurysm associated with IPM placement.

A pseudoaneurysm results from an injury to all three layers of the arterial wall with an associated hematoma contained by the perivascular connective tissue.6–8 Intracranial pseudoaneurysm is an uncommon pathology, accounting for less than 1% of intracranial aneurysms.9 Pseudoaneurysm formation has been described in association with penetrating or blunt trauma, infection, connective tissue disorders, and prior radiation treatment, with rare reports of spontaneous lesions.3,6–8 Iatrogenic pseudoaneurysms are uncommon in the literature but have been described following ventriculostomy (external ventricular drainage or ventricular shunt placement),6,9,10 craniotomy for tumor resection,11 tumor biopsy,12 subdural tap,13 ventricular tap,14 burr hole for subdural hematoma evacuation,15 and IPM placement.2–5 Iatrogenic pseudoaneurysm formation may occur as a direct result of mechanical trauma to the vessel or as a delayed response (up to several months after inciting event) presumed due to chronic inflammation related to permanent ventriculostomy catheter juxtaposition to a cerebral vessel.6 During IPM placement, there are several possible maneuvers that may cause vessel trauma, including inadvertent deep displacement of the twist drill or with durotomy, or due to direct injury from the fiber-optic lead itself.3 Additionally, IPM dislodgment (whether inadvertent, as in our case, or purposeful discontinuation at the end of monitoring) could also cause vascular trauma and result in pseudoaneurysm formation. Lack of bleeding at the time of IPM placement may be attributable to vessel tamponade by the fiber-optic cable, with pseudoaneurysm formation and rupture occurring after IPM removal.4 Notably, IPM placement is a “blind” procedure performed without direct visualization of the brain and its overlying vasculature, thus increasing the risk of hemorrhage and vascular injury.

The natural history of intracranial pseudoaneurysms is poorly understood, but the limited available literature suggests that these lesions are considered unstable with a high risk of expansion and rupture.7 Most reported cases of iatrogenic pseudoaneurysm were discovered following intraparenchymal hemorrhage on CT, with only approximately 20% of cases being discovered incidentally (as in our case).4 Notably, we believe our case to be the first unruptured pseudoaneurysm from IPM placement specifically; the prior IPM-related pseudoaneurysms reported were all discovered with ruptured and associated hemorrhage (Table 1). It is possible that pseudoaneurysm may be an underreported cause of tract hemorrhage immediately following IPM placement, and tract hemorrhage may be an indicator of developing pseudoaneurysm.16 The risk of hemorrhage and death in relation to pseudoaneurysm has been reported to be up to 20%–50%.3,5,7–9 As such, treatment is often recommended.9 These lesions can be treated surgically or endovascularly, with the technique guided by aneurysm characteristics and patient condition.5,6 Superficial cortical locations, small-vessel diameters, and associated hematoma are more amenable to open surgery as opposed to endovascular techniques.5,9 Surgical options include wrapping, suturing, and trapping—with or without bypass (depending on collateral supply; with low-flow bypass for distal pseudoaneurysms or high-flow bypass for proximal pseudoaneurysms).7 There are several endovascular techniques that may be used, including embolization, flow diversion, or vessel sacrifice.5–7 Lastly, patient-specific factors, such as age, comorbidities, and goals of care, should be incorporated into treatment decisions. The case reported here was initially managed conservatively with plans for short-interval repeat imaging; however, given the persistence of the lesion, the relative uncertainty in its expected clinical course, the patient’s dramatically improved neurological examination, and the desire to progress with inpatient rehabilitation to facilitate her recovery, treatment was ultimately pursued with liquid embolization. In contrast to other reported cases,4,5 there was no associated hematoma that required evacuation, so the patient was able to forgo craniotomy with open surgery. Additionally, it is paramount to consider that pseudoaneurysm formation occurs in the setting of traumatic brain injury, with the inherent increased risk of vasospasm, loss of autoregulation, and delayed cerebral ischemia.16 Although not as well established as with aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage, there is evidence that calcium channel blockers, milrinone, papaverine, and intravascular angioplasty may be effective following trauma to prevent these complications.16

Lessons

Iatrogenic pseudoaneurysm formation, although rarely described, should be recognized as a potential complication of IPM placement. Pseudoaneurysm formation may result from inadvertent extension of the twist drill deep into the intracranial space, passage of a dural opening tool, or during IPM catheter placement or removal. Although it is difficult to avoid passing in the vicinity of small intracranial vessels during blind neurosurgical procedures such as IPM or EVD placement, careful manipulation of the twist drill and catheters may help reduce the risk of vascular injury. Additionally, robustly securing these catheters and attention by hospital staff to avoid their inadvertent, uncontrolled removal may also decrease the risk of this complication. Pseudoaneurysm rupture has the potential for catastrophic consequences, which could be avoided with early treatment. A high index of suspicion for this complication should be maintained in the setting of intraparenchymal hemorrhage after IPM placement, and further investigation should be considered with either noninvasive or, ideally, catheter angiography.4 The true incidence of this complication is unknown, as patients do not routinely undergo cerebrovascular imaging after placement or removal of IPM. However, the very limited number of cases reported, despite the high frequency of IPM placement, suggest that it may indeed be a rare, although important, phenomenon.
