Rationale and protocol for a prospective clinical trial enrollment improvement hybrid study within a trial
Authors: Patrick Lewicki, Sabrina Clark, Elaina Shoemaker, Bingkai Wang, Jerison Ross, Stephanie Daignault-Newton, Noelle Carlozzi, Adam Martin-Schwarze, William Meurer, Anne Sales, Khurshid Ghani, Casey Dauw, Kristian Stensland
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionEnrollment to clinical trials is challenging, and few evidence-based interventions to improve enrollment exist. Provider-facing advertising campaigns could help increase enrollment, but this type of intervention has not been prospectively evaluated in a clinical trial.MethodsWe designed a randomized clinical trial to evaluate the effect of a month-long email advertising campaign on enrollment to a clinical trial. This hybrid implementation-effectiveness Study Within a Trial tested the effect of precision audit and feedback techniques enrollment effectiveness outcomes (enrollment) and measured implementation outcomes (email opens, link clicks). This design is an application of a Study Within a Trial (SWAT) to evaluate an intervention intended to improve enrollment. The goal of this study was to inform both this advertising campaign and refine SWAT methods for evaluating future trial improvement interventions.DiscussionThis Study Within a Trial provides preliminary data on the effectiveness of email advertising campaigns for improving clinical trial enrollment. Additionally, the infrastructure built through this SWAT will inform future studies of clinical trial enrollment improvement. This protocol can serve as a template for other investigators seeking to evaluate enrollment improvement interventions.

Introduction

Enrollment to clinical trials is complex, often challenging, and the primary reason for premature trial termination [1,2]. In fact, enrollment has remained low and slow for decades [[3], [4], [5]]. Despite these challenges, there are very few interventions proven to help improve enrollment to clinical trials. The interventions that do exist require drastic changes to trial design, have small effect sizes, do not apply to many clinical trials, or have not been rigorously evaluated or reproduced [6]. As a result, there is not a clear answer for what trial enrollment improvement strategies work, what interventions to use to help struggling trials, or what the relative cost effectiveness of these interventions is. In other words, the field of recruitment science is limited by understudied interventions resulting in a trial enrollment knowledge gap.

This knowledge gap comprises three key components: (1) trialists do not often report what they are doing to improve enrollment, and (2) when they do the methods are suboptimal for evaluating causality, (3) perhaps because existing methods of evaluating enrollment improvement are limited [7]. To address this tripartite knowledge gap, we designed a Study Within a Trial (SWAT), applying a rigorous, yet pragmatic, method of prospectively evaluating a clinical trial enrollment improvement intervention within an ongoing clinical trial.

Specifically, we identified an opportunity to evaluate advertising within our ongoing clinical trial. Many trials include recruitment efforts, including email advertisements and announcements, that are planned and exist outside of the trial protocol. However, these efforts are often launched without prior specification, so it is unclear how they impact enrollment [7]. For example, within one of our trials, a monthlong email based “recruitathon” was launched that sent emails to physicians at participating trial sites that included recruitment leaderboards, trial information, and encouraged enrollment to the clinical trial. Based on anecdotal data from coordinators, it seems that this intervention may have been effective in improving screening and enrollment rates. However, disentangling the effectiveness of the email intervention from other concurrent efforts and events (e.g., other advertising, word of mouth, incentives, secular trends in eligible patients) is not possible given the pre-post study design. With the reported effectiveness, however, another recruitment effort was planned for the month of March. To assess how well this intervention worked, and determine if this intervention should be used in the future, we designed a prospective SWAT embedded within existing trial operations to evaluate the intervention's effectiveness. Additionally, we will gain preliminary knowledge on the effects of specific implementation strategies implicit in the email advertising strategy (e.g., audit and provide feedback, remind clinicians, distribute educational materials) on participant recruitment [8].

Overall, this pragmatic iteration of a “Study Within a Trial” (SWAT), could contribute to both the general recruitment science literature and directly inform future recruitment and quality improvement work for our group [9]. This approach can produce rigorous results while not interfering with ongoing trial recruitment, requiring limited time and resources, and building infrastructure for the future evaluation of quality improvement and enrollment improvement interventions.

Methods

The intervention in this study was a minimal risk behavioral intervention delivered only to participating physicians as part of a larger clinical trial, with a process outcome (enrollment in a trial). Given the prospective evaluation of the advertising push and lack of a reporting guidelines statement for quality improvement testing, we have formatted our protocol to adhere to the Consolidated Standards for Reporting Trials (CONSORT) statement [10].

Host trial

This study was embedded within the ongoing Stent Omission after Ureteroscopy and Lithotripsy (SOUL) trial run in the Michigan Urologic Surgery Improvement Collaborative (MUSIC) [11]. The SOUL trial (NCT05866081) aims to compare patient-centered outcomes after surgical procedures for kidney and ureteral stones that leave a temporary urinary drainage stent versus similar procedures that do not leave a stent. The SOUL trial comprises a randomization arm, wherein patients are randomized at time of surgery to receive a stent or not, and an observation arm, wherein patients are not randomized but do report outcomes. The total goal enrollment of the study is 792 participants between June 2023 and December 2025, and is conducted at 14 clinical trial sites. At the time of the recruitment intervention, 43 individual urologists had ever submitted patients for potential enrollment in the trial.

Study design

Our Study Within a Trial (SWAT) was a prospective trial with random parallel group assignment with crossover to evaluate the effectiveness of a physician-facing email advertising campaign compared to standard trial communication on absolute trial recruitment. In this crossover design, we delivered the email campaign to one group for a month, then delivered the email campaign to the other group for a month. We evaluated absolute enrollment while the intervention was being delivered and for the month afterward, then compared the change from baseline for each group.

Participants and setting

This study was embedded within the SOUL clinical trial, comprising 14 sites, with 43 urologists eligible to enroll participants. We excluded this SWAT study team members from randomization, as well as one substantial recruiting outlier, resulting in 38 urologists eligible for randomization. This study was run over a 3-month period, from March 1 – May 31, 2025, with intervention delivered to Group 1 in March, Group 2 in April, and followup data collection through May.

Intervention

The core intervention was a one-month email advertisement campaign with thematic, tailored messaging to encourage screening and enrollment in the SOUL trial (Fig. 1). Emails were sent weekly from a single trial coordinator email address. Message content included (1) motivational content, including trial recruitment leaderboards, advertising enrollment goals, and encouraging recruitment messages; (2) trial relevant content, including links to trial education content, contact information for trial coordinators; and (3) deliberately designed entertainment content to encourage engagement with the email and trial, such as thematic images and sports-related language (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Example of email advertising intervention.Fig. 1

We centered our content primarily on the principles of audit and feedback. We compared each individual urologist's recruitment performance to either their own prior performance, a top enrolling performer, or the average performance. We provided encouraging framing surrounding under-performers (i.e., those enrolling fewer than average patients) to increase enrollment, and appreciation framing surrounding over-performers (i.e., those enrolling more than average) to sustain enrollment. Sample messaging is in Fig. 1.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was absolute number of participants enrolled in SOUL per urologist over the study period. Secondary outcomes included the proportion of eligible patients seen (assessed from the MUSIC kidney stone registry) who are enrolled in SOUL, and the absolute number and proportion of eligible patients who are screened for SOUL participation.

Additionally, we measured implementation outcomes to assess process measures leading to success (or failure) of the email intervention. These included email opens (tracked via an institutionally approved mail merge application) and trial URL link clicks (tracked through an institutionally approved URL shortener and click tracker). We also estimated cost of the intervention by prospectively recording staff time spent developing and implementing the intervention. Finally, we measured sustainment of the intervention by evaluating trial enrollment for the months after this study concludes (April and May 2025 for the first intervention group, May 2025 for the second intervention (crossover) group).

Sample size

There was a fixed sample size, given the limited number of participating urologists and sites. We set our sample size at 38 (19 in each intervention arm), with 27 urologists (13 in one group and 14 in the other) who had enrolled at least 1 participant in the SOUL trial in the last 6 months, an additional 3 urologists (2 in one group and 1 in the other) who had ever enrolled a patient but no enrollments in the last 6 months, and an additional 8 (4 in each arm) urologists who treat at least 1 eligible patient per month and are eligible to enroll participants but have never enrolled to the trial.

We then paired urologists based on this ordered ranking of number of participants enrolled and randomized within the pair to assign one urologist to treatment and one to control using the blockrand package in R [12]. It was not possible to blind the allocation, as we needed email addresses to distribute content, and urologists would know if they are reading emails or not. However, we did not actively advertise performing this study given the potential for urologists to selectively behave differently (ie, the Hawthorne effect) [13]. We will blind the statistician to group allocation during statistical analysis.

Crossover

After 1 month of weekly emails, we crossed the groups over to the other intervention arm. The group randomized to receive the advertising emails only in March, and the second randomized group only received the advertising campaign emails in April. Notably, no all-trial emails were sent during the study period, so our study communication was the only systematic trial communication made.

Statistical analysis

Following the intention-to-treat principle, results will be compared using count outcomes (number of patients enrolled) and binary outcomes (proportion of eligible patients enrolled) between the intervention and control groups. Generalized estimating equations (GEEs) with appropriate link functions will be employed to account for within-subject correlation across treatment periods and to adjust for baseline measures of the outcome to improve precision [14]. Carryover effects will be assessed by testing the interaction between treatment and period.

Discussion

We built a pragmatic study within a trial (SWAT) to evaluate the effectiveness of an email-based advertising campaign on enrollment to a clinical trial. This study fills multiple knowledge gaps surrounding a lack of evidence for effective enrollment improvement interventions, and builds a platform for evaluating future enrollment and quality improvement interventions nested within quality improvement collaboratives. Specifically, this study addresses 3 key knowledge gaps by (1) reporting an intervention designed to improve enrollment, (2) describing the application of a randomized design to evaluate for causality, and (3) prospectively applying these methods in a reproducible way. These data and methods can be applied to other contexts, and could inform the development and evaluation of future trial enrollment improvement interventions.

In addition to the trial methodologic components, our study also explores a specific trial enrollment strategy that has previously been poorly described. Our intervention centers on trial advertising and applies the implementation strategy of audit and feedback [8]. While audit and feedback has been successful in improving the uptake of evidence-based practices, its effectiveness as a strategy for improving trial enrollment is less well studied [15]. There has been one prospective randomized trial of audit and feedback for trial enrollment, but the fidelity to the intervention was not well reported, feedback was embedded within existing feedback reports, and was of lower intensity than in our study [16]. There are to our knowledge no prospective trials of the effect of email advertising campaigns on enrollment.

Our protocol has multiple major strengths. First, we apply rigorous implementation science methods to integrate hybrid implementation-effectiveness methods within our study. This will inform our understanding of both how well the intervention is working and how well the intervention is being used. Specific to our email intervention, we have designed infrastructure to measure how frequently email advertisements are being opened and interacted with, and by which urologists. If the recruitment push does not have an overall effect, we will be able to tell whether emails are actually being opened and/or URL links are clicked, informing strategies for future enrollment interventions. We also have valuable information on the number and type of patients eligible for the SOUL trial to establish a denominator of patients, as our MUSIC collaborative abstracts data on kidney stone patients undergoing surgery at all participating sites.

There are some limitations to our study. The SOUL trial is embedded within our quality improvement collaborative MUSIC, which hinges on engagement from its members. This level of buy-in from individual members, and sense of community within MUSIC, could mean that results from this study are not transferable to other settings with less esprit de corps. Additionally, the logistical resources available to our study team, such as investigator and study team effort to design and disseminate recruitment materials and analyze results, may not be feasible in some settings. Next, our sample size was necessarily limited by the number of potential participating urologists in our study. As such, we may not be able to detect differences in the intervention effect size at conventional levels of statistical hypothesis testing given power constraints. This is a documented issue facing trial recruitment science and SWATs [17]. Additionally, it is possible that there is a carry-over effect of the advertising intervention, so there could be increased enrollment in April based on an intervention delivered in March. The understanding of effect size of the intervention may be limited by this carry-over effect. We elected to perform this crossover design to increase sample size and allow for 2 time points of initiating the intervention in exchange for a less precise estimate of effect size. In future studies, including a washout period prior to crossover, or accessing larger sample sizes to obviate the need for crossover, could address this limitation.

These limitations notwithstanding, our study stands to inform trial recruitment science, both evaluating an enrollment improvement intervention and building a platform for evaluating future recruitment and quality improvement interventions.
