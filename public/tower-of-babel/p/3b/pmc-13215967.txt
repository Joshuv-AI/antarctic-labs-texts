Unveiling the cryptic diversity and distribution of elements related to virophage mavirus through deep mining of pPolB proteins
Authors: Yutong Wei, Qiyan Liu, Zhen Gong, Guan-Zhu Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVirophages are unique double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) viruses that parasitize viruses of Nucleocytoviricota (NCVs). While conventionally viewed as a viral group, growing evidence suggests that “virophage” is better understood as a parasitic lifestyle, rather than a natural group. Despite this conceptual shift, their diversity and evolution remain largely obscure and contentious. Through deep mining of protein-primed type B DNA polymerase (pPolB) in 7041 eukaryotic genomes and 12,053 metagenomes sampled globally, we expand the diversity of pPolB-carrying mavirus virophage-related elements (pMVREs), which include virophages, transpovirons, and Polinton-like viruses (PLVs). Our phylogenomic and metagenomic mining reveals the widespread distribution of pMVREs in eukaryotic genomes (97/7041, 1.38%) and global environments (2450/12053, 20.33%). pMVREs possess genome architectures of high plasticity and promiscuity. The presence of pMVREs and NCVs is statistically correlated in both eukaryotic genomes and global metagenomes, supporting a specific co-occurrence association between pMVREs and NCVs. Moreover, pMVRE diversity and composition exhibit strong heterogeneity across global ecosystems. Together, this study unveils a vast diversity of virophage-related elements and provides insights into the intricate relationship among virophages, transpovirons, PLVs, pMVREs, and NCVs.Keywords: Virophages, Virus evolution, Virus ecology, Metagenomes

Introduction

Virophages are unique double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) viruses that depend on giant viruses, which belong to Nucleocytoviricota (NCVs), for their propagation (Fischer and Suttle, 2011; La Scola et al., 2008). Virophages have been formally classified by the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV) into the Virophaviricetes class (phylum Preplasmiviricota, kingdom Bamfordvirae, realm Varidnaviria) with three recognized members, namely Cafeteriavirus-dependent mavirus, Sputnikvirus mimiviri, and Mimivirus-dependent virus Zamilon (Krupovic et al., 2016a), although more than 40 putative virophages have been described (Fischer and Suttle, 2011; Gaia et al., 2014; La Scola et al., 2008; Roitman et al., 2023; Sheng et al., 2022; Tokarz-Deptuła et al., 2023). The recent isolation of Gezel-14T (Roitman et al., 2023), a Polinton-like virus (PLV) that displays a typical virophage lifestyle, further challenges a strictly taxonomic view. This finding reinforces the perspective that “virophage” is better defined as a parasitic lifestyle rather than as a viral group. Recent studies have also uncovered virophage-related sequences in protist genomes and environments mainly through mining major capsid proteins (MCPs) (Bellas and Sommaruga, 2021; Bellas et al., 2023; Paez-Espino et al., 2019; Roux et al., 2017; Yutin et al., 2015a; Zablocki et al., 2014; Zhou et al., 2013, 2015). However, the diversity and evolutionary placement of these virophage-related elements remain largely unclear.

Virophages encode highly diverse genome architectures but share a very limited set of conserved genes with certain mobile genetic elements, including NCVs, Polintons, PLVs, transpovirons as well as some phages and plasmids. These mobile genetic elements form an extremely complex evolutionary network (Bellas and Sommaruga, 2021; Koonin and Krupovic, 2017; Krupovic and Koonin, 2015; Wei et al., 2023). One of the conserved proteins is the pPolB. While some virophage lineages (such as maviruses) encode the pPolB protein, the replication of others (such as sputnikviruses) might rely on a PolA-S3H fusion protein (Krupovic et al., 2024). Moreover, pPolBs encoded by maviruses lose most of their exonuclease domain but retain the core palm domain. Transpovirons, another class of elements that parasitize giant viruses (Desnues et al., 2012; Koonin and Krupovic, 2017), encode pPolB-S1H fusion proteins (Krupovic et al., 2016b, 2024). Analyses of metagenomic databases using MCPs of Polintons led to the discovery of a highly diverse group of PLVs (Krupovic and Koonin, 2015; Krupovic et al., 2024). PLVs differ from canonical Polintons, and most PLVs lack pPolB (Bulzu et al., 2025; Koonin and Krupovic, 2017). However, certain PLVs (including the Tlr1 element) encode pPolB-S1H fusion proteins (Krupovic et al., 2024). The pPolB proteins that only possess the core palm domain are referred to as palm-retaining truncated pPolBs (PRT-pPolBs) in this study. PRT-pPolB proteins possess the characteristic TPR1 and TPR2 subdomains in the palm domains. The strong conservation of palm domains among pPolB proteins enables robust phylogenetic analyses (Krupovic et al., 2024).

In this study, we analyzed 7041 eukaryotic genomes and 12,053 metagenomes sampled globally through mining the hallmark pPolB proteins, and expanded the diversity of pPolB-carrying elements encompassing virophages, transpovirons, and PLVs, which we designated pMVREs. We explored the diversity and distribution of pMVREs in both eukaryotic genomes and global metagenomes. Moreover, in both eukaryotic genomes and global metagenomes, the occurrence of pMVREs is correlated with that of NCVs, revealing their specific association. This study discovers the vast diversity of pMVREs and provides insights into the intricate relationship of virophages, transpovirons, PLVs, pMVREs, and NCVs.

Results

Widespread presence of pMVREs in the eukaryotic genomes

To interrogate the diversity of virophages and their related genetic elements, we first mined the genomes of 7041 eukaryotes (Supplementary Dataset 1) for the elements closely related to virophages based on the hallmark pPolB proteins. We identified a monophyletic lineage of pPolB-carrying elements that encompasses virophages, transpovirons, PLVs, and virophage-related sequences previously discovered in the alga Bigelowiella natans (Blanc et al., 2015) (Fig. 1A), which we designated as pMVREs. We identified the presence of pMVREs in the genomes of at least 97 eukaryotes, accounting for 1.38% of the eukaryotic genomes analyzed here. pMVREs were dispersed in the genomes across the major supergroups of eukaryotes, including fungi, animals, protists, and plants (Supplementary Dataset 2). We found the presence of pMVREs exhibits significant phylogenetic clumping in eukaryotes using D statistics (Supplementary Table S1), indicating that the presence of pMVREs is not evenly distributed across the eukaryote phylogeny. Indeed, we observed that pMVREs were distributed in a high proportion of certain eukaryote groups, such as Ochrophyta (17/60, 28.33%), Amoebozoa (14/56, 25.00%), Cnidaria (10/67, 14.93%), and Chlorophyta (18/127, 14.17%) (Fig. 1B). Phylogenetic analysis classified pMVREs into four major groups with strong support (ultrafast bootstrap (UFBoot) = 100%), termed pMVRE groups 1 to 4 (Fig. 1A). pMVRE group 1 included maviruses and was primarily distributed in protists and fungi. pMVRE group 2 included PLVs previously discovered in the alga Bigelowiella natans and appeared to be distributed across eukaryotes. pMVRE group 3 consisted of certain PLVs (Bellas and Sommaruga, 2021; Sheng et al., 2022; Yutin et al., 2015b) and was mainly distributed in algae and protists (Fig. 1A). pMVRE group 4 only included transpovirons. Together, our phylogenetic mining revealed the uneven distribution of pMVREs in the eukaryotic genomes.

Plastic genome architectures of pMVREs

To explore the genome architectures of pMVREs, we identified and annotated 358 complete endogenous pMVRE genomes characterized by the presence of two flanking terminal inverted repeats (TIRs) (Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. S1, Supplementary Dataset 3 and 4). Besides the hallmark pPolB, more than a third of pMVREs possessed CD_CSD (accession: cd00024), RVE (accession: PF00665), the major capsid protein (MCP) (accession: PF04451), GIY-YIG_SF (accession: cl15257), and DUF5679 (accession: cl40933) (Fig. 2B–D), among which DUF5906 (accession: pfam19263) was specific to pMVRE group 1. In addition to pPolB, the domain encoded most frequently by members of pMVRE group 1 was DUF5906, a protein family whose proteins are thought to be SF3 helicases (Fig. 2B–D, Supplementary Fig. S1C). This observation is consistent with the feature of maviruses and some PLVs encoding an S3H helicase (Krupovic et al., 2024). The superfamily 1 (SF1) helicase-related domains were found across pMVRE group 2 and 3 (Fig. 2B–D). Many pMVRE group 2 and 3 elements encoded PRT-pPolB fused with SF1 helicases (Supplementary Fig. S1), as reported previously (Krupovic et al., 2016b, 2024). We found that the occurrence of protein domains appeared to follow a power law distribution (Fig. 2B and D, Supplementary Dataset 5). Interestingly, not all the complete pMVREs encoded viral structural proteins, as exemplified by transpovirons (Desnues et al., 2012; Krupovic et al., 2016b) and a large lineage identified from fungi within pMVRE group 1, which were likely to be plasmids. Together, our results indicated that pMVREs possessed a highly plastic and promiscuous repertoire of gene contents.

Correlated presence of pMVREs and NCVs in the eukaryotic genomes

Given the dependence of virophages on NCVs, we further mined NCVs in the eukaryotic genomes based on their hallmark proteins, namely family B DNA polymerase (PolB) proteins. We identified endogenous NCVs in the genomes of 128 eukaryotes. Interestingly, we found that pMVREs and NCVs often appeared to be present simultaneously in the genomes across eukaryotes (Fig. 1B). We tested the correlation between pMVREs and NCVs using their presence and absence as traits across 249 eukaryote taxonomic groups. Evidence favoring correlation was found between NCVs and pMVREs as well as NCVs and pMVRE groups 1 to 3 (group 4 was too small to generate meaningful results) (Supplementary Table S2). We also tested the correlation between pMVREs and NCVs across 4290 species with known phylogenetic positions. Evidence favoring correlation was found between NCVs and pMVRE groups 1 to 3, although we did not find an overall correlation between NCVs and pMVREs (Supplementary Table S2). Together, these results indicated that the presence of pMVREs and NCVs was statistically correlated in the eukaryotic genomes, supporting their specific association.

Expanding diversity of pMVREs in global metagenomes

Next, we investigated the diversity of pMVREs in 12,053 metagenomes (Supplementary Dataset 6) sampled globally from diverse environments by mining the hallmark pPolB proteins (Fig. 3, Supplementary Fig. S2). Our global metagenomic mining found that pMVREs were present in at least 2450 out of 12,053 (20.33%) metagenomes, highlighting the widespread distribution of pMVREs in global environments (Fig. 3B and D). Phylogenetic analysis showed that pMVREs identified in eukaryotic genomes and global metagenomes were intermixed, and could be classified into the four aforementioned groups (Fig. 4A). Moreover, we reconstructed sequence similarity networks (SSNs) of pMVREs identified in this study and previously based on pPolB proteins (Barreat and Katzourakis, 2023; Paez-Espino et al., 2019). Based on the analysis of pPolB proteins, the major diversity of pMVREs was not appreciated previously (Fig. 4B). pMVREs identified from global metagenomes were classified into 4,771, 3,137, 1,515, and 181 clusters based on the pPolB amino acid identity (AAI) thresholds of 0.9 (approximately at viral species level), 0.7, 0.5 (approximately at genus level), and 0.3 (approximately at family level), respectively (Nayfach et al., 2021). For all four AAI thresholds, the number of clusters increased monotonically with the number of metagenomes investigated, and no saturation was observed (Fig. 4F–I). Moreover, no saturation was observed in metagenomes sampled from different environment types either (Fig. 4F–I). Therefore, our results unveiled the cryptic diversity of pMVREs on Earth.

Global biogeographical patterns of pMVREs

Our metagenomic mining revealed that pMVREs were widely distributed across global ecosystems (Fig. 3A and B). The number of pMVRE clusters varied across the ten ecosystem types investigated (Fig. 4D and E). 95.16%, 89.99%, 79.21%, and 47.51% of viral clusters at AAI thresholds of 0.9, 0.7, 0.5, and 0.3 were specific to a single ecosystem type. Notably, 81.14%, 75.26%, 64.55%, and 38.67% of viral clusters at AAI thresholds of 0.9, 0.7, 0.5, and 0.3 were specifically identified in aquatic ecosystems. In contrast, a relatively low proportion of viral clusters (4.84%, 10.01%, 20.79%, and 52.49% at AAI thresholds of 0.9, 0.7, 0.5, and 0.3, respectively) was shared among ecosystem types (Supplementary Fig. S3). Together, these results indicated that pMVREs exhibited heterogeneity across global ecosystems.

We found pMVREs most prevalent in freshwater environments, detected in 49.52% of samples (1141/2304), followed by plant litter and peat (41.50%, 61/147) and marine systems (21.95%, 569/2592). In contrast, pMVREs were rare or absent in other biomes, such as animal microbiomes (2.28%) and plant rhizospheres (4.99%). Overall, we found that pMVREs were present in a relatively high proportion of metagenomes sampled in aquatic ecosystems (Fig. 3A and B, Supplementary Table S3). The number of pMVRE pPolB copies was also generally high in aquatic ecosystems (Fig. 3C). We further explored the sharing of pMVRE communities among aquatic metagenomes sampled from low (from 0° to 30°), mid (from 30° to 60°), and high latitude (from 60° to 90°) regions using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA). For pMVRE clusters with AAI thresholds of 0.3 and 0.5, we observed a significant overall difference (P-value = 0.001 and P-value = 0.001, respectively), indicating substantial dissimilarity in pMVRE composition across aquatic ecosystems at different latitudes (Supplementary Fig. S4A). The difference remained significant for both freshwater (P-value = 0.001, AAI = 0.3; P-value = 0.001, AAI = 0.5) and marine samples (P-value = 0.001, AAI = 0.3; P-value = 0.001, AAI = 0.5) (Supplementary Fig. S4B and C). Together, our results suggested that pMVRE composition also varied across aquatic ecosystems.

Evolutionary placement of pMVREs based on MCPs

Many pMVREs encoded MCPs. Therefore, we also clarified the relationship among virophages, NCVs, Polintons, PLVs (Barreat and Katzourakis, 2023; Bellas and Sommaruga, 2021; Bellas et al., 2023; Paez-Espino et al., 2019; Sheng et al., 2022; Yutin et al., 2015b) previously identified and pMVREs identified in this study through phylogenetic and sequence similarity network analyses based on MCPs. Our analyses showed that pMVREs, virophages, PLVs, and Polintons neither clustered together in the reconstructed SSNs nor formed monophyletic groups, but instead exhibited extensive intermixing, indicating that the major diversity of pMVREs had not been appreciated previously (Fig. 4C, Supplementary Fig. S5). These results confirmed that these pPolB-carrying elements are closely related to each other. Several virophages, such as Gezel (Roitman et al., 2023), encode MCPs that do not cluster with other representative virophage-like maviruses in the phylogenetic tree, but instead cluster with some PLVs. Nevertheless, pPolB and MCPs appeared to have undergone distinct evolutionary histories, and thus complex recombination might have occurred among these pPolB-carrying elements.

Correlated presence of pMVREs and NCVs in the global metagenomes

To explore the relationship between pMVREs and NCVs, we further mined NCVs in global metagenomes based on the hallmark PolB proteins. We found that NCVs were present in at least 3797 out of 12,053 (31.50%) metagenomes. Both pMVREs and NCVs were identified in 1893 out of 12,053 (15.71%) metagenomes, revealing a specific coexistence of pMVREs and NCVs across the global metagenomes (P-value = 0.000; Chi-squared test) (Supplementary Table S4). The presence of NCVs was also correlated with that of pMVRE group 1 to 4 in the global metagenomes (P-value = 0.000 for pMVRE group 1; P-value = 0.000 for pMVRE group 2; P-value = 0.000 for pMVRE group 3; P-value = 0.000 for pMVRE group 4; Chi-squared test) (Supplementary Table S4). When stratified by ecosystem, our analysis revealed that this specific association remained robust, showing statistical correlations across major ecosystem types and most subtypes, particularly in aquatic ecosystems (Supplementary Tables S5 and S6). Moreover, we tested the correlation between NCVs and pMVREs at the cluster level (AAI thresholds of 0.5 and 0.3) for the global metagenomes. The pPolB proteins of pMVREs were classified into 1515 and 181 clusters based on the AAI thresholds of 0.5 and 0.3, respectively, whereas the PolB proteins of NCVs were classified into 1875 and 298 clusters based on the AAI thresholds of 0.5 and 0.3, respectively. We found specific correlation in approximately 1.79% (50,853/2,840,625) of pMVRE and NCV cluster pairs at the AAI threshold of 0.5 and in approximately 10.48% (5652/53,938) of pMVRE and NCV cluster pairs at the AAI threshold of 0.3 (Fig. 5A and B, Supplementary Table S7). Specific correlation between pMVRE and NCV clusters was observed for all four pMVRE groups (Fig. 5C and D). Together, these results revealed the specific co-occurrence relationship between pMVREs and NCVs.

Discussion

In this study, through mining eukaryotic genomes, we expanded the diversity of pMVREs, which encompass virophages, virophage-related sequences, transpovirons, and PLVs identified previously (Barreat and Katzourakis, 2023; Bellas et al., 2023; Paez-Espino et al., 2019; Roux et al., 2017; Yutin et al., 2015b; Zablocki et al., 2014; Zhou et al., 2013, 2015). Our study unveils the cryptic diversity and distribution of pMVREs through deep mining of pPolB proteins. Our study used pPolB as hallmark proteins and inherently excluded some virophages that lack pPolB, such as sputnikviruses. Thus, our findings do not aim to capture the full diversity of virophages. The definition of pMVREs also bridges the evolutionary gap between virophages, virophage-related sequences, and PLVs (Barreat and Katzourakis, 2023; Bellas et al., 2023; Paez-Espino et al., 2019; Roux et al., 2017; Yutin et al., 2015b; Zablocki et al., 2014; Zhou et al., 2013, 2015).

We found that pMVREs were widely distributed not only in eukaryotic genomes but also in metagenomes sampled globally from diverse ecosystems. pMVRE communities vary substantially across the globe and appear to be highly abundant in aquatic systems, indicating that pMVRE diversity and composition exhibit heterogeneity across global ecosystems. We think that our study might still under-estimate the diversity of pMVREs, because pPolB may have been replaced by alternative polymerases (Krupovic et al., 2024). We identified complete endogenous pMVRE genomes characterized by terminal inverted repeats (TIRs) (Supplementary Dataset 4). Their 5′- and 3′-TIRs exhibited high sequence identity, averaging 96.82% in eukaryotic genomes and 95.30% in metagenomes (Supplementary Fig. S6). The consistently high sequence identity between 5′- and 3′-TIRs supports their authenticity as boundaries of single, integrated mobile elements, rather than independent assembly artifacts, but the assembly problems cannot be formally excluded. Following this genomic characterization, we annotated 358 complete endogenous pMVRE genomes and found that pMVREs encoded a highly plastic and promiscuous repertoire of gene contents. In essence, pMVREs might represent a “cloud” of mobile genetic elements, in which a proportion of them encode a proportion of genes.

Our analyses identified a statistical correlation between the presence of pMVREs and NCVs across both eukaryotic genomes and global metagenomes. This co-occurrence pattern suggests a potential ecological or evolutionary association between these elements. NCVs harbor highly diverse mosaic genomes, and their endogenization introduces novel genetic material into eukaryotic genomes, significantly shaping host genomic architecture (Aylward et al., 2021; Moniruzzaman et al., 2020a, Moniruzzaman et al., 2020b; Sarre et al., 2024). Moreover, numerous metabolic genes identified in NCVs, including key components of glycolysis and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, suggest these viruses may reprogram their hosts' central carbon metabolism (Moniruzzaman et al., 2020a, Moniruzzaman et al., 2020b; Schulz et al., 2020). Based on our correlation data, we hypothesize that some pMVREs may specifically interact with certain NCVs. The specific pMVRE-NCV-host interplay may exert profound influences on both global ecosystems and host genome evolution. Future studies are needed to determine whether and how this potential interplay influences global ecosystem dynamics and host genome evolution.

Recent studies indicate that virophages can integrate into the eukaryotic genomes (Fischer and Hackl, 2016; Blanc et al., 2015). Coinfecting with CroV and its host (Cafeteria roenbergensis), maviruses were inserted into the genome of CroV. The activation of integrated maviruses does not prevent the replication of CroV, but the released maviruses protect related neighboring C. roenbergensis from CroV infection (Fischer and Hackl, 2016). However, virophage integration has been thought to be rare (Koslová et al., 2024). In this study, we found pMVREs that include virophage-related elements are widespread in the genomes of eukaryotes. It is possible that these pMVREs integrated in the host genomes play roles in defending against the infection of NCVs, which merits further experimental validation. Moreover, further research is needed to characterize the biological nature of pMVREs, which might have important implications for understanding the interaction among pMVREs, NCVs, and eukaryotes.

Conclusions

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive mining of pPolB across 7041 eukaryotic genomes and 12,053 global metagenomes. Our findings significantly expand the known diversity of pMVREs, a group encompassing virophages, transpovirons, and PLVs, revealing their widespread distribution in both eukaryotic genomes and diverse ecosystems. We demonstrate that pMVREs possess highly plastic genome architectures and that their occurrence is statistically correlated with the presence of NCVs in both eukaryotic genomes and environmental samples. Although our pPolB-centric approach may underestimate the full spectrum of virophage diversity, this study provides a robust phylogenetic framework for understanding the evolutionary relationships among pMVREs. Our results lay a foundation for future experimental studies aimed at deciphering the biological interactions between pMVREs, NCVs, and their eukaryotic hosts, and their collective impact on ecosystem dynamics.

Materials and methods

Identification of pMVREs across eukaryotes and metagenomes

We used similarity search and phylogenetic analysis combined approaches to identify pMVREs based on the hallmark pPolB protein within the genomes of 7041 eukaryotes and 12,053 metagenomes sampled globally. The eukaryotic genomes were retrieved from NCBI (Sayers et al., 2024) genome resources (Supplementary Dataset 1). The global metagenomes were obtained from Joint Genome Institute Integrated Microbial Genomes (JGI-IMG) (Markowitz et al., 2012) (Supplementary Dataset 6). We first employed the tBLASTn algorithm to search against the eukaryotic genomes and the global metagenomes using representative pPolB proteins as queries with an e cut-off value of 10−5 and a length cut-off value of 150 amino acids. Representative pPolB proteins included both full-length, classic pPolBs and known, truncated variants that possess the core palm domains (Supplementary Dataset 7). Then, we performed initial large-scale phylogenetic analyses of non-redundant pPolB homologs and representative pPolB proteins from virophages, Polintons, PLVs, plasmids and phages. Protein sequences were aligned using MAFFT 7.505 (Katoh and Standley, 2013). Large-scale phylogenetic analyses were performed using an approximate maximum likelihood method implemented in FastTree 2.1.10 (Price et al., 2010).

Phylogenetic analyses of pPolB proteins

To establish the evolutionary relationship among pMVREs, virophages, Polintons, PLVs, plasmids and phages, we performed phylogenetic analyses of representative pPolB proteins. Representative pPolB sequences were selected based on the initial large-scale pPolB phylogenetic analyses. The pPolB proteins were aligned using MAFFT 7.505 (Katoh and Standley, 2013). Phylogenetic analysis was performed using a maximum likelihood (ML) method implemented in IQ-tree 2 (Minh et al., 2020). ModelFinder implemented in IQ-tree 2 was used to determine the best-fitting substitution model (Kalyaanamoorthy et al., 2017). The node supports were evaluated using an ultrafast bootstrap method with 1000 replicates (Hoang et al., 2018). Phylogenetic trees were annotated using iTOL (Letunic and Bork, 2019).

Gene contents analyses of pMVREs identified in eukaryotes

To identify complete pMVREs within eukaryotic genomes, we used gt-tirvish implemented in GenomeTools to identify TIRs flanking pMVREs (Supplementary Dataset 3 and 4) (Gremme et al., 2013). Complete pMVREs with flanking TIRs were retrieved and annotated using domain search and similarity search tools, including Conserved Domain search (Lu et al., 2020), HMMER (Eddy, 1998; Gao et al., 2018), tBLASTn, and BLASTp (Altschul et al., 2005). Structures of representative pPolB proteins and SF1 helicases were predicted by AlphaFold (Varadi et al., 2022). Template modeling score (TM-score) between protein structure pairs was calculated using Pairwise Structure Alignment tool in RCSB Protein Data Bank (Berman et al., 2000).

Clustering and phylogenetic analysis of MCPs

We employed the tBLASTn algorithm to search against pMVREs identified in the eukaryotic genomes and metagenomes using representative MCPs as queries with an e cut-off value of 10−5. Phylogenetic analyses of MCPs were performed using a ML method implemented in IQ-tree 2 (Minh et al., 2020). Protein sequences were aligned using MAFFT 7.505 (Katoh and Standley, 2013). ModelFinder implemented in IQ-tree 2 was used to determine the best-fitting substitution model (Kalyaanamoorthy et al., 2017). The node supports were evaluated using an ultrafast bootstrap method with 1000 replicates (Hoang et al., 2018). Phylogenetic trees were annotated using iTOL (Letunic and Bork, 2019). MCPs were clustered using MCL (Van Dongen, 2008) with thresholds of 95% identity and 70% coverage.

Identification of NCVs across eukaryotes and metagenomes

We also used similarity search and phylogenetic analysis combined approaches to identify NCVs based on the hallmark protein PolB (Gong et al., 2020) (Supplementary Dataset 8) within the genomes of 7041 eukaryotes and 12,053 metagenomes. First, we employed the tBLASTn algorithm to search against the eukaryotic genomes and the global metagenomes using representative PolB proteins as queries with an e cut-off value of 10−5 and a length cut-off value of 150 amino acids. Then, large-scale phylogenetic analyses of non-redundant PolB homologs and representative PolB proteins were performed using an approximate maximum likelihood method implemented in FastTree 2.1.10 (Price et al., 2010). The PolB homologs clustered with NCVs were retrieved.

Sequence similarity network reconstruction

We reconstructed sequence similarity networks (SSNs) based on the genetic distances of complete pMVREs in eukaryotic genomes. Moreover, separate SSNs were constructed for pPolB and MCPs. These proteins were clustered using MCL (Van Dongen, 2008) with thresholds of 95% identity and 70% coverage. All networks were visualized using the Fruchterman-Reingold algorithm in Gephi (Bastian et al., 2009), with edge weights set as the inverse of genetic distance (1/distance).

D statistics

D statistics represents a robust measure of phylogenetic signal for binary traits independent of trait prevalence and phylogeny size (Fritz and Purvis, 2010). D statistics was used to assess the phylogenetic signal of pMVREs and NCVs across 249 taxonomic groups of eukaryotes. The phylo.d function (Fritz and Purvis, 2010) in the caper package v1.0.2 was used to estimate D statistics. The phylogenetic tree was retrieved from TimeTree (Kumar et al., 2022).

Correlation analysis of pMVRE and NCV distribution in eukaryotes

To quantify the correlation of pMVRE and NCV distribution in the genomes of eukaryotes, we used the presence and absence of pMVRE and NCV as traits. The correlation between pMVREs and NCVs was tested across 249 eukaryote taxonomic groups and 4290 species with known phylogenetic positions using BayesTraits (v4.0.0) (Pagel and Meade, 2006). Two models were compared: the independent model where traits evolved independently, and the dependent model where traits evolved together. The marginal likelihood of each model was estimated using the stepping stone sampler with 250 stones and 5000 iterations per stone (Xie et al., 2011). Log Bayes factors (Log BF) were calculated by comparing the likelihoods of the two models: Log BF = 2 × (log marginal likelihood of the dependent model – log marginal likelihood of the independent model). Log BF values greater than 2 indicate positive evidence of trait correlation, while values greater than 5 indicate strong evidence of trait correlation.

Geographical distribution analysis of pMVREs in global metagenomes

Geographical distribution analysis of pMVREs across various ecosystems was analyzed using ggmap (Kahle and Wickham, 2013), scatterpie (Yu, 2023), and ggplot2 (Wickham, 2016) packages in R. The richness of pMVREs identified in metagenome samples was analyzed using ggplot2 (Wickham, 2016) package in R.

Diversity analysis of pMVREs in global metagenomes

Both pPolB proteins and PolB proteins identified in global metagenomes were clustered using MMseqs2 (Steinegger and Söding, 2017) with AAI thresholds of 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, and 0.9. Each pPolB protein cluster was then assigned to one or more ecosystems. Rarefaction and extrapolation curves were generated using the iNEXT (Hsieh et al., 2020) package in R.

Correlation analysis of pMVRE and NCV distributions in metagenomes

We assessed the correlation between pMVRE and NCV distributions at the protein cluster level. Phylogenetically validated pPolB (hallmark protein of pMVREs) and PolB (hallmark protein of NCVs) proteins were clustered separately using MMseqs2 (Steinegger and Söding, 2017) at AAI thresholds of 0.3 and 0.5. We then generated a binary presence/absence matrix for each cluster across all metagenomic samples. Next, for each AAI threshold, pairwise association tests were performed between every pMVRE cluster and every NCV cluster. A 2 × 2 contingency table was constructed for each pair by cross-tabulating their presence/absence patterns, and statistical significance was determined using the Chi-squared test or Fisher's exact test. Visualization of the pairwise test results was generated using the pheatmap package in R (Kolde, 2019).

Community analysis of pMVREs

pMVRE communities were identified based on pPolB proteins at the cluster level (AAI thresholds of 0.5 and 0.3). pPolB proteins were clustered using MMseqs2 (Steinegger and Söding, 2017) at AAI thresholds of 0.3 and 0.5. PCoA analysis and PERMANOVA were performed based on the Bray-Curtis distance using the Vegan package (Oksanen et al., 2012). The Bray-Curtis distance was calculated based on the presence of different communities in each ecosystem group. Data were visualized using the tidyverse (Wickham et al., 2019) and ggplot2 (Wickham, 2016) packages in R.
