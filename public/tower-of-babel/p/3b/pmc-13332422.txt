Navigating Alzheimer’s Disease in Primary Care: Practical Strategies for Diagnosis and Management
Authors: Scott T Larson, Jeffrey L Susman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlzheimer’s disease (AD), the most common form of dementia, is a neurodegenerative disorder that primarily affects older adults. Because the number of Americans 65 years of age and older living with AD is expected to increase and the availability of specialty physicians, such as neurologists and geriatricians, is limited, the role of primary care in diagnosing and managing patients with AD is expected to grow. Several guidelines for primary care providers are available for the evaluation, diagnosis, and treatment of patients with AD. However, there is no single concise and convenient reference tool for use in the primary care setting. In the primary care setting, clinicians are responsible for identifying patients at risk or demonstrating signs of cognitive impairment, administering cognitive tests, identifying appropriate referrals, and, in some cases, ordering blood-based biomarker testing. Although definitive testing and treatment identification may occur in the specialty care setting, in addition to serving as the hub to coordinate the multidisciplinary care team, primary care clinicians remain responsible for guiding patients and caregivers through shared decision-making regarding treatment, as well as the myriad responsibilities related to comorbidities, patient and caregiver psychological well-being, social support, and safety. This review aims to provide practical recommendations to primary care clinicians for the diagnosis, management, and long-term care of patients with AD.Keywords: Alzheimer’s disease, guideline, diagnosis, treatment

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a complex neurodegenerative disorder characterized by the presence of amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles that primarily affects older adults and is the most common cause of dementia.1,2 Approximately 6 million Americans 65 years of age and older are living with AD.3 Worldwide, according to the World Health Organization, 34 to 40 million people lived with dementia in 2021, and the prevalence of dementia due to AD is expected to rise.4 Globally, the prevalence of AD varies but is consistently highest in regions with older populations and stronger local healthcare infrastructure for detection. For example, in the United States, the number of people 65 years of age and older living with AD is projected to reach approximately 14 million by 2060, and almost half of this population will comprise individuals 85 years of age and older.3 Dementia also increases with age, affecting 5.0% of people 65–74 years of age, 13.2% of people 75–84 years of age, and 33.4% of people 85 years of age and older.3 The availability of specialty physicians, such as neurologists, psychiatrists, and geriatricians, is already limited, with wait times for a first visit of 6 months or more. Therefore, as the population ages, the role of primary care in diagnosing and managing people with AD is likely to grow. Primary care clinicians (PCCs) will increasingly be faced not only with evaluating cognition but also with establishing the etiology of cognitive changes when detected.

Several factors may impact the trajectory of cognitive impairment in asymptomatic individuals. Research on these factors has brought forth the concept of cognitive reserve, which describes individual differences in susceptibility to cognitive decline due to aging or brain disease.5 Directly modifiable risk factors influence the risk of cognitive impairment by affecting brain integrity and neuroplasticity, such that reducing these risks may preserve or enhance cognitive reserve, allowing the brain to better compensate for underlying pathology, thereby delaying clinical symptoms. Lifetime experiences, such as educational and occupational attainment, general cognitive ability or intelligence, and social and cognitive engagement, in combination with genetic factors, may allow individuals to better cope with cognitive decline due to aging or brain disease.5 In the primary care setting, the serious cognitive changes of AD and Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias (ADRD) should be differentiated from normal changes in cognitive abilities that occur with age, such as difficulty retrieving names or words, lapses in attention, and slight slowing of cognition.6 However, cognitive and behavioral symptoms due to AD or ADRD are often undiagnosed, undisclosed to the provider or undisclosed by the provider to the patient, or misattributed, even by neurologists and neurospecialists.7–9 Only a small proportion of PCCs report feeling confident in making a diagnosis of AD or ADRD and, therefore, rely on specialty physicians for diagnosis and treatment of patients with AD.10,11

When evaluating cognition, AD is conceptualized as a clinical continuum from asymptomatic with evidence of AD neuropathologic change to mild cognitive impairment (MCI) to dementia.12 MCI is defined as impairment that is not severe enough to interfere with independent daily functioning, with some but not all people progressing to dementia.12 Per the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), dementia is defined as a significant decline in one or more cognitive domains that interfere with a person’s independence and daily activities.13 Among patients with MCI, approximately 15% develop dementia after 2 years, and approximately 33% develop dementia after 5 years.3 Interestingly, approximately 26% of individuals with MCI do not show signs of additional cognitive decline or revert to a normal cognitive state.3 Current research is aimed at better understanding which individuals are more likely to develop dementia.

Emerging disease-modifying therapies require diagnostic confirmation with imaging or laboratory markers before use in practice.14 In general, the diagnostic process involves excluding alternative neurologic disorders and contributing factors to cognitive decline, and assessing amyloid burden.15 These areas are evolving rapidly, and currently, there is conflicting guidance.3,14 There are multiple guidelines and review articles regarding the evaluation, diagnosis, and treatment of patients with cognitive impairment, including AD and dementia. These include guidelines from the National Academy of Neuropsychology;16 the US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF);13 the Gerontological Society of America;17 the Alzheimer’s Project;6 the Alzheimer’s Association guidelines for the Diagnostic Evaluation, Testing, Counseling, and Disclosure of AD and ADRD (DETeCD-ADRD);10 the Lancet Standing Commission;18 and guidelines on the use of blood-based biomarkers (BBMs) from the Alzheimer’s Association.19 Guidelines for evaluation of mild cognitive impairment from the American Academy of Neurology were published in 2018 but have since been retired.20 There is currently no single concise and convenient reference for the diagnosis, disclosure, treatment, and long-term care of patients with AD for the PCC. Therefore, within this review we provide guidance based on our clinical experience that we hope will serve as a resource in the primary care setting for managing patients with AD.

Beginning The Conversation: Normalize Discussion Of Brain Health

The first step in identifying individuals with AD is to increase people’s awareness of the importance of brain health and the signs and symptoms of cognitive impairment that may require further evaluation (Figure 1).17 The topic of brain health can be covered in concert with other health discussions during routine clinical visits. For example, broach the topic of brain health as part of the one-time initial preventive physical examination during the first 12 months under Medicare, or the annual wellness visit, which requires direct cognitive assessment as one of its components, to broach the topic of brain health.21,22 Many people are reluctant to express concern about cognitive impairment because of fear and stigma associated with dementia. Therefore, regularly raising the topic of brain health may help normalize the topic for both the provider and the patient, encourage individuals to be more aware of changes to their cognition or other brain health–related changes, and create an environment where individuals may feel more comfortable sharing their concerns.17 Healthcare teams can use brain health–related discussions to encourage individuals to adopt lifestyle changes that can potentially improve their brain health.17 Several potentially modifiable risk factors for dementia have been identified (Box 1).10,18,23–25 Studies have suggested that adopting brain-healthy behaviors may prevent or delay nearly half of dementia cases.18,23,25

Diagnostic Evaluation Process

Screening and Case Finding

The USPSTF on screening for cognitive impairment in older adults concluded that there was insufficient evidence to recommend screening of asymptomatic, community-dwelling adults 65 years of age and older. Clinicians should remain alert to early signs or symptoms of cognitive impairment and evaluate as appropriate.13 While screening the entire population of adults 65 years of age and older in the US may not be feasible,16 practical assessment tools are available to assess the risk of MCI and dementia, in addition to regular conversations about brain health that may identify signs of cognitive impairment. The annual wellness visit is a practical office visit when PCCs can consider asymptomatic screening. Furthermore, a case-finding approach, in which individuals may raise concerns about cognitive impairment, may reduce the number of individuals to be screened in the primary care setting.26 Identifying individuals most likely to be affected with cognitive impairment is a practical approach and may optimize resource allocation.16

Individuals at high risk for cognitive impairment (eg, patients with traumatic brain injury), those with suspected cognitive impairment identified by the provider during a regular exam, and those who have raised concerns about cognitive impairment should be evaluated further16 Short cognitive screening tests can be administered, scored, and interpreted by clinical staff with minimal training and no prior experience, and will not substantially disrupt workflow.27 Brief cognitive screening tests include the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and the Mini-Cog.6,10,16,17 In individuals without signs of cognitive impairment, clinicians can continue to promote brain-healthy behaviors and evaluate the need for a cognitive assessment at the next annual visit (Table 1).10

Steps After Identifying Signs of Cognitive Impairment

For patients who show signs of cognitive impairment via a cognitive assessment tool, a full diagnostic evaluation involves a multitiered evaluation across multiple visits (Figure 2).6,10,16,17 The first step of the diagnostic evaluation is a detailed history, including the perspective of an informant (eg, spouse, caregiver, or other representative), interviewed separately from the patient, if possible.43 Careful characterization of symptoms of concern, exploration of plausible relations between symptoms and pertinent events, and a comprehensive survey of all major domains (cognition, daily function, behavior/neuropsychiatric, sensorimotor) using a structured approach is important for sensitive detection and accurate delineation of potentially clinically relevant changes and syndromes.10 Evaluations include developmental and educational, social, and family history, and changes in cognition; activities of daily living (ADLs), mood, and other neuropsychiatric symptoms; and sensory and motor function. The clinician should gather reliable information about individualized risk factors for cognitive decline.10 Finally, the workup should look to exclude potentially reversible causes of cognitive decline, such as depression, medication side effects, metabolic disturbance, normal pressure hydrocephalus, and infectious disease.44

The next step of the evaluation process involves tiers of assessments and tests based on individual presentation, risk factors, and profile to establish a diagnostic formulation, including the overall level of impairment, the cognitive-behavior syndrome, the likely causes, and contributing factors.10 This evaluation includes a delineation of the patient’s cognitive status; characterization of the specific clinical profile of the patient’s cognitive-behavioral syndrome through examination of cognition, mood, and behavior (mental status examination); and a dementia-focused neurologic examination. Further, the clinician establishes the most likely brain disease (or condition) causing the clinical syndrome (etiological diagnosis) and delineates other conditions or factors that may be contributing to the illness.10 Laboratory testing (typically a laboratory panel with thyroid-stimulating hormone, vitamin B12 tests, and a basic metabolic profile) will uncover potential occult causes for cognitive decline.10 Brain MRI without contrast (or CT), when available and not contraindicated, is appropriate for the evaluation of AD. Results are useful to exclude other etiologies, such as stroke, normal pressure hydrocephalus, subdural hematoma, benign masses, or malignancy, to identify features of unusual forms of dementia, such as prion disease, or to reveal atrophy patterns supportive of a neurodegenerative disease diagnosis.10

Tracking of functional status with the patient and an informant over time is important. Clinicians should assess independent activities of daily living (IADLs) and ADLs depending on the severity of cognitive impairment (Table 1). Several instruments have been validated to assess daily function,45 including the Lawton and Brody IADL scale (an 8-item assessment of IADLs for individuals with and without cognitive impairment)41 and the short version of the Amsterdam IADL (a 30-item assessment of IADLs for individuals with early signs of dementia).42 In practical terms, analysis of functional status may help to determine the level of support needed from caregivers and for documentation in patients who may have long-term care insurance. It is also critical to consider the time course of functional status, as this will deteriorate over time in patients with AD.

Validated Assessment Instruments

Patients and informants may struggle to explain how symptoms first appeared, how they evolved, how frequently they occurred, or when they became noticeable or troubling. Therefore, an important aspect of assessing individuals for cognitive impairment is administration of practical questionnaires to assess symptoms and the level of cognitive impairment. Several brief, validated cognitive test instruments are available (Table 1), such as the Mini-Cog, a composite of 3-item recall and the clock drawing test;28 the MMSE, the best-known and most often used short screening tool for providing an overall measure of cognitive impairment;29 Clinical Dementia Rating scale Sum of Boxes (CDR-SB), an assessment of cognition and function widely used in clinical trials;30 the St. Louis University Mental Status, an 11-item questionnaire to detect MCI and dementia;31 the General Practitioner Assessment of Cognition, consisting of cognitive test items and historical questions asked of an informant;32 and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment, a 20-item assessment of multiple cognitive domains that may be more accurate at detecting MCI than MMSE.45 Several validated instruments offer structured questionnaires to assist in organizing and reporting cognitive impairment and are simple to administer. These include the Informant Questionnaire on Cognitive Decline in the Elderly, a short questionnaire of 16 questions designed to assess cognitive decline and dementia in elderly people filled out by an informant;34 AD8, a brief screening interview for patient and informant;35 and Cognitive Function Instrument, a 14-item questionnaire used to detect change in cognitive and functional abilities within the previous year for patient and informant.36,37 No guideline has included formal recommendations on specific cognitive tests. Therefore, the choice of cognitive test should consider cost, ease of testing, familiarity with interpretation, and local practice patterns. Finally, there is evidence to suggest that disparities in the accuracy and interpretation of cognitive tests depend on race, ethnicity, level and quality of education, and socioeconomic status.46–48 Therefore, care should be taken to consider demographic, socioeconomic, and psychosocial factors when interpreting cognitive assessment results.

Neuropsychiatric symptoms often precede cognitive decline and commonly increase in frequency and intensity as neurodegenerative dementing conditions progress. Approximately 35% to 85% of individuals with MCI also have neuropsychiatric symptoms, and an initial psychiatric diagnosis, such as depression, may occur before a diagnosis of a neurodegenerative disorder in as many as 28% of cases.49 The Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI) is the most widely used instrument to survey many of these symptoms. Other validated instruments offer structured questionnaires that can assist in the organization and reporting of symptoms of mood and behavior changes. These include the NPI-Q, a general instrument for neuropsychiatric symptoms composed of a 12-item questionnaire completed by an informant;38 the Patient Health Questionnaire, a 9-item tool for depressive symptoms and depression in older adults that is self-administered by the patient and verified by the clinician,39 and the Geriatric Depression Scale, a 15-item tool that is self-administered by the patient (but can be administered to the patient by a clinician, if needed) and that is suitable for detection of depression in patients with MCI and mild dementia.40

The Role of Biomarkers in the Diagnosis of AD

The use of biomarkers in the diagnosis of AD is a rapidly evolving field. The goal of biomarker testing is to aid in the diagnosis of AD and to inform treatment because newer treatment options require amyloid confirmation for use. Per the AA, the presence of abnormal core 1 biomarkers is sufficient for confirming AD pathology in symptomatic individuals, but it does not preclude the search for other contributors to the clinical symptoms, particularly other comorbidities.12 Core 1 biomarkers include Aβ proteinopathy (Aβ42) and phosphorylated and secreted tau (p-tau217, p-tau181, and p-tau231); positron emission tomography (PET) and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) are routinely used to analyze these biomarkers.12 Only biomarkers proven to be accurate with respect to an accepted reference standard should be used for clinical diagnostic purposes.12 Clinical practice guidelines from the Alzheimer’s Association provide guidance on clinical scenarios that are appropriate for the use of amyloid and tau PET in patients with MCI or dementia.50

BBMs offer a less invasive and widely available technique that may be more feasible compared with PET and CSF collection, and are more accessible to nonspecialists.14 The recent release of an FDA-cleared BBM by Lumipulse makes this decision of importance to PCCs. A consensus statement by the BBM Workgroup recommends the use of BBM tests for confirmation of amyloid pathology in primary care for individuals 65 years of age and older, as the rate of amyloid positivity in this age group is highest.51 Because confirmation of amyloid pathology is required before initiation of anti-amyloid therapy, the potential use of a biomarker (as opposed to a more invasive or less widely available confirmatory test) could expedite care.12 A recent systematic review and clinical practice guideline from the Alzheimer’s Association assessed the diagnostic accuracy of BBMs and suggested thresholds for the sensitivity and specificity of testing based on their purpose: triage or diagnosis (in place of amyloid PET imaging or CSF biomarker testing).19 Due to their relatively low cost and potential for widespread use, a concern with BBMs is the potential for false positives and false negatives. In a setting with overtesting or poor testing implementation, individuals with a low pretest probability of having AD pathology could be just as likely to have a true positive test result as a false positive result, and this could cause undue harm if patients are erroneously started on therapy. Alternatively, false negatives could delay confirmatory testing and treatment.19 To minimize risk, biomarker testing should always be part of a comprehensive clinical evaluation and should never be interpreted in isolation.

Currently, there is considerable debate regarding the diagnosis of AD based on the presence of certain biomarkers (endorsed by the NIA-AA),12 or as a clinical-biologic entity (endorsed by the International Working Group).52 Regarding this controversy, the American Geriatrics Society raised concerns over the proposal by the NIA-AA that AD be defined solely based on amyloid pathology, stating that this may place many older individuals at risk of overdiagnosis and lead to increased potential from harm from initiation of treatments where clinical benefits remain to be proven.12,19,53 It is the perspective of the authors that clinical use of AD biomarkers should be reserved for the evaluation of symptomatic individuals contemplating anti-amyloid therapy and not for diagnosis in cognitively unimpaired individuals.12

Diagnostic Disclosure: A Compassionate And Iterative Approach

Timely disclosure of a diagnosis of AD is crucial to provide appropriate care to improve patient well-being.54 Diagnostic disclosure and treatment planning may need to occur over several visits for patients and care partners to completely understand the diagnosis and the level of care required. Throughout disclosure and treatment planning, a triadic relationship—involving the patient, their care partner/family, and the clinician—facilitates better understanding of the nature and severity of cognitive impairment, informed decision-making, and the delivery of appropriate assistance with daily activities.54 For patients who may be unable to identify a trusted care partner, engagement of a social worker, outreach to the Alzheimer’s Association Helpline, or contact with a local agency on aging to obtain care support may be necessary.54 Finally, understanding the patient and care partner’s treatment goals and daily needs is critical when disclosing a diagnosis and initiating a management plan.

Several approaches have been developed to aid in the structure of information for a clinician to deliver a diagnosis of cognitive impairment due to AD.55–57 For example, the SPIKES protocol can be used to disclose a diagnosis of AD. SPIKES is a 6-step process: (1) Set up interview, (2) assess the patient’s Perception, (3) obtain the patient’s Invitation, (4) give Knowledge and information, (5) address Emotions with empathic responses, and (6) present Strategy and Summary.56 We have adapted the SPIKES approach for disclosing a diagnosis of AD (Figure 3). Another comprehensive approach to the communication of diagnosis news that can be used across any illness, including AD, is the Serious Illness Care Program.57

Treatment

Several treatments are available for patients with AD to address the symptoms of AD or to potentially slow disease progression. Patient goals will vary based on the stage of AD. Therefore, it is important to understand treatment goals prior to recommending a specific treatment approach. Cholinesterase inhibitors (such as donepezil, rivastigmine, and galantamine) and memantine may be used to treat the cognitive symptoms in patients with AD.6,18,58 In patients with mild to moderate AD dementia or in those with advanced disease, cholinesterase inhibitors may provide a temporary improvement in cognition (eg, memory and thinking skills), neuropsychiatric symptoms, and ADLs58 Cholinesterase inhibitors do not cure or stop cognitive decline but may have short-term, modest positive effects.18 While often prescribed to treat MCI, there is no evidence supporting the clinical benefit of cholinesterase inhibitors in patients with MCI.58,59 However, cholinesterase inhibitors can provide symptomatic benefits in early-stage AD, including cognitive function and improvement of daily activities.60 When dementia has progressed to a severe stage, there is general agreement that stopping cholinesterase inhibitor treatment should be considered.61 A meta-analysis of cholinesterase inhibitors for the treatment of AD demonstrated that, compared with placebo, the number needed to treat in order for 1 additional patient to demonstrate a global response or a cognitive response was 12 (95% CI, 9–16) and 10 (95% CI, 8–15), respectively, and the number need to harm was 12 (95% CI, 10–18).62 Cholinesterase inhibitors are well tolerated; common side effects are gastrointestinal in nature, and greater tolerability is expected in clinical practice than in clinical trials, as the dosage is tailored to the individual.62 Memantine, alone or in combination with cholinesterase inhibitors, appears to have modest benefits in patients with moderate to severe AD and leads to modest improvements in cognition, delayed worsening in ADLs, and improvements in global outcomes, such as reduced agitation and improved interactions with others in patients with advanced disease.58

Anti-amyloid antibodies selectively target and remove abnormal forms of amyloid-β aggregates implicated in AD pathophysiology and are considered disease-modifying.6 Currently, three anti-amyloid antibodies have been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration. Aducanumab is a recombinant monoclonal antibody targeting amyloid beta (Aβ), but its manufacturer discontinued production of the drug in 2024 based on the manufacturer’s stated decision to reprioritize AD franchise resources.58 Lecanemab is a humanized monoclonal antibody targeting aggregated Aβ that is approved for the treatment of patients with early-stage AD with confirmed brain amyloid pathology.63 In the Phase 3 Clarity AD study, lecanemab demonstrated a 27% delay in clinical decline at 18 months compared with placebo, slowing the progression of clinical decline in patients with early AD.64 This was based on a treatment difference of 0.45 points in adjusted mean change in CDR-SB score (lecanemab, 1.21; placebo, 1.66; treatment difference, −0.45).64 While this difference was significant in the context of the trial, it is unknown how it will translate to clinical practice. The principal side effects associated with lecanemab treatment are infusion reactions and amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA) that are associated with edema and hemorrhagic changes.63 Monitoring via serial MRI screening is recommended during treatment to detect asymptomatic ARIA, which typically leads to at least short-term cessation of therapy.63 Donanemab is an IgG1 monoclonal antibody directed against Aβ present in brain amyloid plaques that is approved for the treatment of patients with MCI or mild dementia due to AD and biomarker-confirmed Aβ pathology.65 In the phase 3 TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 study, donanemab demonstrated a 35% delay in clinical decline at 76 weeks compared with placebo (based on a treatment difference of 3.25 points in least squares mean change from baseline in integrated Alzheimer Disease Rating Scale [iADRS] score).66 For context, it should be noted that a meaningful change in iADRS in patients with AD and MCI is 5 points, and in patients with AD and mild dementia is 9 points.66 Infusion reactions and ARIA are common side effects, which require routine monitoring.65 A critical review by Espay et al demonstrated that the number needed to harm for both lecanemab and donanemab was approximately 3, and the number needed to treat was not possible to accurately calculate due to a lack of patient-level data.67 The authors concluded that it is important for clinicians and patients to understand that these drugs are associated with small and uncertain clinical benefits, worrisome and poorly understood risks, and high cost of treatment.67 It is the opinion of these authors that the role of amyloid-targeting therapy in early AD is still evolving, and the decision to pursue treatment should involve a shared decision-making process to fully understand the potential risks (both medical and financial) and benefits of treatment. For patients who are referred to a specialty clinic (memory care or neurology), the primary care provider’s role remains important for regular check-ins with patients regarding goals of continued treatment and monitoring for side effects.

Referrals And Long-Term Care Planning

A patient with atypical findings or in whom there is uncertainty about how to interpret the evaluation, or who is suspected of having an early-onset or rapidly progressive cognitive-behavioral condition, should be further evaluated by a specialist.10 Delirium and rapidly progressive dementia warrant rapid assessment and treatment. Atypical, rapidly progressive, or early-onset dementias pose unique diagnostic and care challenges that may require comprehensive neuropsychiatric evaluation and specialist assessment, interpretation, and management. Patients with atypical forms of neurodegenerative dementias may have substantially different care and management needs and considerations regarding safety than patients with typical presentations of dementia due to AD.10 Types of non-AD dementia include, but are not limited to, behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia, dementia with Lewy bodies, and vascular dementia/vascular cognitive impairment. The diagnostic criteria for these types of non-AD dementias are out of the scope of this review but can be found in published guidelines.68–70

Referral will vary by health system but could include one or more of the following specialties: neurology, neuropsychology, or geriatrics/aging center (Box 2). The type of referral and the eventual makeup of the multidisciplinary care team will depend on the patient’s characteristics, including factors beyond AD, such as comorbidities, psychological well-being, existing social support, and safety. A good referral should include a summary of the evaluation for cognitive impairment with a clear explanation of the reason for the referral and the patient’s goals (eg, whether the patient is a candidate for anti-amyloid therapy or has an atypical presentation) As symptoms of AD progress, communication among the PCC, the patient, and care partners will evolve. Nonphysician care providers, such as social workers, are important members of the support system for individuals living with AD and their caregivers and can serve as a hub for emotional support and counseling, disease education, and resource navigation. In the early stages of the disease, many patients can maintain their independence but may benefit from changes to their living space to increase safety. Ability to drive should be reevaluated, and financial and legal planning should be considered. As AD progresses, patients may require more assistance with daily activities. Some patients may need support from specialized units within assisted living or nursing homes to provide additional support for AD, such as nutritional support and safety planning. In later stages of AD, 24/7 care may be required, along with increased emotional support for the patient and caregivers. Several resources are available to help with planning for future health scenarios; resources can be found through Alzheimers.gov, the Alzheimer’s Association, and the US Department of Veterans Affairs.

Referrals and Long-Term Care Planning

Conclusions

As the population of Americans with AD increases, PCCs will likely play a primary role in the diagnosis of AD, continue to be involved in the long-term care planning for their patients, and serve as the hub to coordinate the multidisciplinary care team. The conversation about brain health should be initiated before signs of cognitive impairment, to normalize the topic of cognitive function and encourage patients to disclose their concerns when cognitive changes occur. Because treatment is most effective when implemented in the earliest stages of disease, it is important to identify patients at high risk of AD as early as possible. While screening the entire population may not be feasible, short cognitive screening tests can be used in at-risk patients when cognitive impairment is suspected. We have provided a multitiered evaluation across multiple visits to assess the history of changes in cognition, ADLs, and safety concerns, mood and other neuropsychiatric symptoms, and sensory and motor function.

Once an individual has tested positive for cognitive impairment and other neurologic, psychologic, metabolic, or infectious causes have been ruled out, a comprehensive tiered evaluation—with the patient’s and their informant’s agreement—which may include consideration for noninvasive BBM testing, is necessary to confirm a diagnosis. Once confirmed, the clinician discloses the diagnosis and develops a care plan that meets the needs and goals of the patient and their care partner. For atypical cases or if there is uncertainty with the diagnosis, patients should be referred to specialty care. As AD progresses, the PCC’s role will change to assist with the patient’s long-term care plan.

Several issues will need to be addressed in the future to improve the management of patients with AD. There is ongoing debate regarding the role of biomarkers in the diagnosis of AD. There is reasonably convincing evidence that high levels of amyloid biomarkers can guide the appropriateness of anti-amyloid therapy alone (in lieu of confirmatory imaging or CSF analysis). The benefits and risks associated with anti-amyloid therapies, and how to present this information appropriately to patients, need to be better elucidated. Finally, as new BBMs and treatments emerge, it will be important for PCCs to remain current with changing clinical assessment tools and emerging expectations for specialty referrals.
