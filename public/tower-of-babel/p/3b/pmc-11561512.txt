Risk Stratification With Fractional Flow Reserve and Fractional Flow Reserve Gradient From CCTA
Authors: Tonghui Zhao, Jinlong Zhang, Xinyang Hu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Over the past 2 decades, substantial clinical evidence has shown the value of coronary physiological assessment in risk stratification and treatment decision-making for patients with coronary artery disease (CAD).1,2 Current guidelines recommend the use of fractional flow reserve (FFR) and other physiological indices, such as non-hyperemic pressure ratios, to evaluate myocardial ischemia and guide percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI).3,4 These physiological indices can reflect the vessel-level hemodynamic condition of the stenotic coronary artery. However, coronary atherosclerosis may exhibit a wide spectrum of plaque phenotypes and varying distributions. Although similar in terms of FFR or other physiological indices, the severity of focal coronary stenosis can vary among different coronary arteries.5 It has been reported that different coronary atherosclerosis patterns (ie, focal or diffuse lesion, presence or absence of major physiological gradient) have distinct prognoses and clinical outcomes after PCI.6 Therefore, assessing the functional patterns of coronary atherosclerosis may provide valuable information to clinicians in treatment decision-making and in planning or optimizing PCI.

Currently, several tools are available to assess the distribution of physiological stenosis and quantify patterns of CAD, including FFR-based pullback systems (eg, pullback pressure gradients), non-hyperemic pullback systems, and angiography-derived FFR virtual pullback systems.7 However, these methods typically involve pressure wire–based techniques or require invasive procedures, which may limit their clinical applicability. Coronary computed tomography angiography (CTA)-derived fractional flow reserve (FFRCT) is a noninvasive method to evaluate the physiological status.8 Previous studies showed that FFRCT virtual pullback seems to be accurate for evaluating pressure gradients, similar to FFR gradients.9 This technology can provide both FFRCT values on a vessel level and local gradients of FFRCT (FFRCT gradient) on a lesion level without invasive procedures.

In this issue of JACC: Asia, Hong et al10 present an analysis from a case-control study, the DESTINY (Prediction and Validation of Clinical Course of Coronary Artery Disease With CT-Derived Noninvasive Hemodynamic Phenotyping and Plaque Characterization) study, aimed to evaluate the additive prognostic value of FFRCT gradient in addition to FFRCT to predict future coronary events. The study cohort consisted of 181 patients (469 vessels) from the coronary event cohort and 64 patients (165 vessels) from the negative control cohort, who had undergone coronary CTA within 6 to 36 months before invasive coronary angiography. The primary outcome was defined as a composite of vessel-specific myocardial infarction or urgent revascularization. Preexisting disease patterns were classified according to FFRCT ≤0.80 and FFRCT gradient ≥0.025/mm. Large FFRCT gradients define focal disease, whereas the absence of focal gradients characterizes diffuse CAD. The authors compared plaque characteristics and clinical outcomes based on preexisting disease patterns and concluded that patients exhibiting high FFRCT gradients showed an elevated risk of future coronary events independent of FFRCT values. Integrating both FFRCT and FFRCT gradient provided greater predictive ability for future coronary events compared with using FFRCT alone.

The study10 should be commended for evaluating clinical outcomes in medically treated patients classified by an emerging technique of noninvasive coronary physiology, which increased our understanding of the natural course of vessels according to disease patterns. Previous studies have reported the relationship between coronary disease patterns and subsequent post-PCI physiological outcomes or clinical outcomes in patients who underwent coronary intervention.6,11 Patients with diffuse disease patterns present more frequently with suboptimal post-PCI physiological results than patients with focal patterns. The subsequent clinical events rate after PCI was significantly higher in patients with predominant diffuse disease. Prior studies established the clinical value of disease pattern evaluation before PCI. The results from Hong et al10 add to the evidence supporting the use of FFRCT and FFRCT gradient for risk stratification before invasive procedures.

Coronary CTA is an accurate, noninvasive alternative to invasive coronary angiography that provides additional information on clinical decision-making and patient management, particularly when complemented with functional FFRCT.12,13 By integrating the FFRCT gradient, it may be possible to further identify patients who are appropriate candidates for coronary revascularization, while also further consolidating its role as a “gatekeeper” of the catheterization laboratory.

However, some evidence gaps need to be addressed before large-scale clinical application. First, the current study10 was an observational case-control study, which may have selection bias. The primary endpoint of this study was a composite of vessel-specific myocardial infarction or urgent revascularization. It is worth considering whether this endpoint accurately reflects the risk of future cardiovascular events in patients. The results of the study need to be validated in further prospective studies. Second, the FFRCT gradient critical value used in this study was derived from receiver-operating characteristic curve analysis. This value should be validated in larger data sets. Third, following the use of FFRCT and FFRCT gradient for patient risk stratification, it remains to be validated through further clinical studies whether subsequent changes in clinical decision-making can lead to patient benefit. In addition, the optimal treatment strategy to improve patient outcomes based on different types of lesions suggested by these techniques remains unclear.

The report by Hong et al10 underscores the importance of coronary atherosclerosis distribution measurement using FFRCT and FFRCT gradient. With continued advancements in image acquisition and data processing, physiological techniques derived from coronary CTA may further evolve to include methods such as virtual PCI, providing additional support for preoperative planning in patients. In addition, artificial intelligence technology may advance the development of coronary CTA-based functional indicators and help them better meet the demands of clinical practice in the future. We look forward to seeing more evidence in future large-scale randomized controlled trials and hope it can gradually become an essential tool in the workflow of coronary physiology assessment and be more widely adopted in clinical practice.
