Sugar restriction during the first 1000 days after conception and its association with decreased risk of anxiety and depression
Authors: Xiuping Dou, Yang Su, Yao Huang, Man He, Bingying Zhang, Xuejin Li, Hualiang Lin, Yin Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDepression and anxiety pose a major challenge to global public mental health, and early life exposures are known to exert substantial long‐term effects. However, evidence on the long‐term effect of sugar restriction during the first 1000 days after conception on mental health remains limited, and it is still unclear whether such restriction reduces the subsequent risk of anxiety and depression in later life.AimsTo examine whether sugar restriction during the first 1000 days after conception is associated with a reduced risk of anxiety and depression in later life, compared with no sugar restriction.MethodsWe leveraged a natural experiment created by the cessation of the United Kingdom's sugar rationing policy in September 1953, under which sugar intake prior to abolition was constrained to modern recommended levels before abruptly doubling after rationing ended. Using data from the UK Biobank (n = 66 223), we compared individuals conceived just before and after the end of rationing to isolate the effect of early life sugar restriction on anxiety and depression.ResultsEarly life sugar restriction was associated with significantly lower risks of both anxiety (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.765, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.732–0.799) and depression (HR = 0.786, 95% CI 0.749–0.824) over the lifetime. The protective effect showed duration dependence with the greatest benefit observed when the restriction spanned the entire first 1000 days. In addition, sugar rationing during this period delayed the first diagnosis by approximately 2 years for anxiety and 4 years for depression.ConclusionsSugar restriction during the first 1000 days after conception significantly reduces the risk of anxiety and depression and delays their first diagnosis, highlighting the importance of early life dietary interventions for long‐term mental health.

INTRODUCTION

Depression and anxiety affect over 300 million individuals globally, representing a major challenge to public mental health. 1 These disorders not only impair social functioning and quality of life but also increase the risk of long‐term chronic diseases such as obesity, diabetes, dementia and cardiovascular diseases. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 Given their substantial disease burden and lasting health implications, identifying modifiable risk factors is critical for formulating early preventive strategies.

The first 1000 days, from conception to age 2, represent a critical window for neurodevelopmental programming, with profound implications for lifelong mental health. 8 During this period, exposure to high levels of dietary sugar has emerged as a key nutritional factor that may influence the risk of developing mental disorders in later life. 9 , 10 The pathways linking sugar exposure to mental health outcomes are likely to differ between prenatal and postnatal periods, reflecting time‐specific mechanisms of neurodevelopmental programming. In utero, such exposure can influence foetal brain development through mechanisms including altered placental function, epigenetic modifications and programming of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, processes that may shape lifelong stress responsiveness. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 During infancy, sugar exposure exerts its effects primarily through the gut–brain axis while also contributing to the programming of the dopaminergic reward system and the establishment of long‐term dietary habits that reinforce sustained high sugar intake. 11 , 15 These distinct yet complementary pathways converge on neural systems central to emotional regulation, core domains implicated in the pathogenesis of common mental disorders such as anxiety and depression.

Accumulating evidence from animal studies also supports this hypothesis, demonstrating that intrauterine and perinatal high‐sucrose exposure could induce anxiety‐ and depression‐like behaviours in rodents. 16 , 17 However, population‐based studies regarding the impact of early life sugar restriction on mental health outcomes remain limited. 18 To the best of our knowledge, only one study reported that a maternal high‐sugar diet during pregnancy significantly increased the risk of anxiety and depressive behaviours in offspring by age 4. 19 Given the currently high levels of sugar consumption among pregnant women and young children, 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 there is a compelling need to rigorously investigate the long‐term mental health consequences of early life sugar exposure.

The United Kingdom's food rationing programme (1939–1954) offers a unique natural experiment to examine these associations. This policy enforced scientifically designed nutritional allocations that strictly limited sugar availability while ensuring adequate intake of essential nutrients. During the rationing period, dietary patterns were closely aligned with modern World Health Organization (WHO)‐recommended guidelines, maintaining childhood sugar intake within currently advised limits. The abrupt termination of sugar rationing in September 1953 led to a sharp and specific rise in sugar consumption, more than doubling intake, with documented deteriorations in metabolic and dental health. 25 , 26 This historical setting provides a rare opportunity to investigate the long‐term mental health effects of early life sugar restriction. Leveraging this natural experiment, our prospective cohort study aimed to examine associations between sugar restriction during the first 1000 days and risks of anxiety and depression later in life, and to assess the influence of early sugar restriction on the age at first recorded diagnosis of a mental disorder.

METHODS

Study design

We employed a quasi‐experimental design informed by the regression discontinuity framework to investigate the association between sugar restriction during the first 1000 days after conception (from conception to age 2) and subsequent risks of anxiety and depression in adulthood. The termination of sugar rationing in September 1953 served as a natural experiment, creating a sharp discontinuity in sugar availability, whereas other food supplies remained stable. This setting allowed us to compare individuals exposed to sugar rationing (conceived or born before September 1953) with those unexposed (conceived or born after), approximating local comparisons around this policy cutoff.

The abrupt and isolated rise in sugar consumption following the end of rationing, without a corresponding increase in the availability of other foods, supports two key assumptions underlying this design. First, we assumed that other factors associated with mental health (e.g., socioeconomic conditions, access to healthcare and nonsugar dietary patterns) remained continuous across the rationing cutoff and did not change abruptly within a narrow window around the policy change. Second, we assumed that the risk of anxiety and depression among adults who were never exposed to sugar rationing would be similar, regardless of their birth date relative to the end of rationing. By treating the end of rationing as a quasi‐random assignment of sugar exposure, we compared individuals born just before and after this critical date to isolate the effects of sugar intake. The details of the study design can be found in the online supplemental methods. 25

Data source and study sample

The United Kingdom's National Food Survey (NFS, 1940–present) provides longitudinal household‐level data on food consumption, including sugar‐derived calories. We digitised and analysed NFS records to quantify dietary shifts before and after rationing ended. Health outcomes were collected from the UK Biobank cohort study, which enrolled approximately 500 000 participants aged 37–73 from England, Wales and Scotland between 2006 and 2010. This large‐scale prospective study design provides a robust platform for investigating risk factors for a wide range of diseases. Each participant in the UK Biobank provided written informed consent.

We defined exposure based on birthdate relative to the rationing cessation. The first 1000 days, spanning from conception to approximately age 2, are roughly equivalent to 11 quarters (1001 days). We defined the study window as the 11 quarters before and after the end of sugar rationing, covering the period from 1 October 1951 to 30 June 1956. Among the 78 283 participants born during this period, we excluded 11 009 individuals who were born or lived outside the UK, were from a multiple birth (e.g., twins, triplets), were adopted or were immigrants. An additional 1051 participants were excluded due to the missing data of their birthplace, resulting in 66 223 participants included in the final analysis (figure 1).

Exposures

We assessed sugar rationing using multiple classification schemes to capture different dimensions of exposure during the first 1000 days. Individuals born between October 1951 and June 1954 were classified as the rationed group, whereas those born between July 1954 and June 1956 were classified as the nonrationed group. We further categorised exposure into broader groups: never, in utero only, in utero and up to 1 year and in utero and up to 2 years. Moreover, the transition at approximately 6 months from exclusive milk‐based nutrition to complementary feeding signifies a pivotal inflexion point in dietary patterns. 27 To align with recommendations that complementary feeding begins at 6 months of age, we stratified exposure as follows. Among the rationed participants, we defined exposure as occurring in utero only or in utero combined with postnatal exposure lasting 6, 12, 18 or 24 months. Among the nonrationed participants, we defined exposure based on birth occurring within 9–15, 15–21, 21–27 or 27–32 months after the end of rationing. This stratification served two key purposes. First, it allowed us to examine the relationship between the duration of sugar exposure and the risk of outcomes among those rationed. Second, it enabled us to test our assumption that the risk among those nonrationed would be similar across different birth dates, effectively serving as an internal negative control. The distribution of births among participants is detailed in Supporting Information S1: table S1.

Outcomes

Cases of anxiety and depressive disorders were ascertained using the UK Biobank's curated ‘First Occurrence’ fields (Category 2405). These fields integrate diagnostic records from self‐reports (Field 20002), primary care (Read codes), hospital admissions (the International Classification of Diseases, 9th Revision [ICD‐9] and the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision [ICD‐10]) and death registers (ICD‐10). The UK Biobank maps all source codes to unified 3‐character ICD‐10 codes and identifies the earliest recorded date for each condition. We utilised the fields for anxiety (ICD‐10 F40–F43, Fields 130904–130911) and depressive disorders (ICD‐10 F32–F33, Fields 130894–130897). In this study, follow‐up for each participant began at their date of birth and continued until the earliest of the following events: the first recorded diagnosis of anxiety or depression from any data source, death, loss to follow‐up or the administrative censoring date. Age at diagnosis was defined as the date of the earliest diagnosis. Censoring dates were source‐specific: October 2022 for England, August 2022 for Scotland and May 2022 for Wales. Mortality data were available up to November 2022. Further details on case definitions and distributions are provided in the Supporting Information S1: methods and tables S2 and S3.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics are presented as mean (standard deviation) for continuous variables and as counts (percentages) for categorical variables, stratified by sugar rationing exposure status. To examine the continuity of key covariates at the policy cutoff, relative balance tests were conducted. To visualise unadjusted temporal relationships, we constructed Kaplan–Meier survival curves and cumulative hazard curves for time‐to‐diagnosis of anxiety and depression. Given the severe violation of the proportional hazards assumption (Schoenfeld residuals test, p < 0.001) and the superior fit of the parametric hazard model (lower Akaike Information Criterion; see Supporting Information S1: table S6), we conducted an event study analysis using multivariate parametric hazard models with a Gompertz distribution to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for anxiety and depression risk. Regarding the time scale, ‘time zero’ for all survival models in this study is defined as the participant's date of birth. Three models were fitted: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (adjusted for sex, ethnicity and birthplace) and Model 3 (additionally adjusted for the calendar month of birth, survey year and the north and east deciles of birthplace coordinates).

To further examine whether the associations between sugar rationing and anxiety/depression risk were influenced by temporal confounding, we conducted negative control analyses using outcomes unlikely to be related to sugar intake. Specifically, we assessed the relationship of sugar rationing with migraine, type 1 diabetes (T1D), menarche and the first use of prescription glasses—all putative negative control outcomes unlikely to share causal pathways with dietary sugar exposure. We also conducted a placebo cutoff analysis among nonrationed participants born between July 1954 and June 1956, using 1 July 1955 (the midpoint) as a placebo cutoff to divide participants into two groups. These analyses included the same covariates as our primary models to evaluate whether observed mental health associations might reflect residual confounding from secular trends. To further evaluate the effects of sugar rationing on the first recorded diagnosis of anxiety and depression, we employed an accelerated failure time (AFT) model with a Weibull distribution. We reported time ratios (TRs), median age at first recorded diagnosis, delays in age at first recorded diagnosis and their corresponding 95% CIs based on the AFT model.

To assess the robustness of our findings, we conducted several sensitivity analyses by (1) removing birthplace coordinates from Model 3; (2) additionally adjusting for food affordability, in utero tobacco exposure, being breastfed as a baby, antidepressant/anxiolytic use, family history of depression, and traumatic events (which were associated with an increased risk of depression 28 and are presented in Supporting Information S1: table S4); (3) adjusting for several baseline covariates, including socioeconomic status (Townsend deprivation index, annual household income and educational attainment), physical health conditions (obesity, diabetes and cardiovascular disease) and lifestyle factors (physical activity, smoking status and alcohol consumption); (4) employing alternative model specifications by switching the distribution assumption to Weibull and utilising the estimated semi‐parametric Cox proportional hazards model to estimate hazard ratios under these alternative configurations; (5) setting the upper age limit for follow‐up and disease diagnosis at 66 years to ensure cohort age overlap; (6) excluding participants with a prior diagnosis of anxiety or depression at study enrolment and re‐estimating the association; (7) excluding cases based solely on self‐reported data; (8) using anxiety and depression cases defined exclusively by self‐report data, primary care records and hospital inpatient data to evaluate the consistency of our primary findings across these individual sources (detailed numbers and proportions of cases from each source are presented in Supporting Information S1: table S3); and (9) employing a more narrowly defined birth window, spanning from one year before to one year after the end of sugar rationing (1 October 1952–30 September 1953 vs. 1 July 1954–30 June 1955). Individuals born between 1 October 1953 and 30 June 1954 were excluded due to the ambiguity of their in utero exposure status.

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.2.1). A two‐tailed p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. A detailed description of the statistical analyses is provided in the supplemental methods.

RESULTS

Characteristics of study participants

The study included 66 223 UK Biobank participants born in the United Kingdom during the 1951–1956 study window with a mean age of 54.48 (1.63) years; 56.45% were female, 99.01% were White, 30.74% had in utero tobacco exposure and 17.29% reported childhood traumatic experiences (table 1). Participants born between October 1951 and June 1954 (conceived before September 1953) were classified as the ‘rationed’ group (n = 39 775), whereas those born between July 1954 and June 1956 were classified as the ‘non‐rationed’ group (n = 26 448). During follow‐up, 7663 (11.57%) cases were diagnosed with anxiety and 9197 (13.89%) with depression. As shown in Supporting Information S1: table S5, all standardised mean differences (SMDs) are below 0.1, indicating no meaningful imbalance and supporting the local randomness assumption of the study.

Dietary patterns during and after sugar rationing

After the end of sugar rationing, both total caloric intake and sugar consumption exhibited immediate and sustained increases (Supporting Information S1: figure S1). Daily per capita sugar intake among adults rose from about 43.31 g/day in the first quarter of 1953 to 74.03 g/day by the third quarter of 1954, where sugar‐derived calories accounted for 77% of the total caloric increase. The elevated consumption level remained stable throughout the observation period with no regression towards pre‐cessation baselines. No concurrent changes were observed in the intake of other foods or nutrients (Supporting Information S1: figures S2 and S3).

Association of sugar rationing with anxiety and depression

Prolonged exposure to sugar rationing during the first 1000 days after conception was associated with an attenuated risk of anxiety and depression compared to nonrationed counterparts (Supporting Information S1: figure S4). Individuals exposed to sugar rationing exhibited significantly higher survival probabilities compared to nonrationed counterparts (Supporting Information S1: figure S5).

Sugar restriction during the first 1000 days was associated with decreased lifetime risk of anxiety and depression (tables 2 and Supporting Information S1: S7). In the fully adjusted models, exposure to sugar rationing was associated with an approximately 17% reduction in anxiety risk (HR = 0.831, 95% CI 0.794–0.870) and an approximately 14% reduction in depression risk (HR = 0.862, 95% CI 0.827–0.899) compared to the nonrationed group. A dose–response relationship was evident; the HRs of anxiety were 0.922 (95% CI 0.873–0.974) for in utero exposure, 0.851 (95% CI 0.815–0.888) for in utero to 1 year exposure and 0.765 (95% CI 0.732–0.799) for in utero to 2 years exposure. Similar results were observed for depression.

Using the first post‐rationing birth cohort (born in July–December 1954) with no prenatal exposure to sugar rationing as the reference, we observed that longer duration of sugar rationing was associated with a distinctly reduced risk of anxiety and depressive disorders among those with early exposure to sugar rationing (figure 2, Supporting Information S1: table S8). The protective associations remained stable at 18 months postpartum, demonstrating adjusted HRs of 0.745 (95% CI 0.670–0.828) for anxiety and 0.747 (95% CI 0.678–0.823) for depression. HRs for anxiety and depression in the nonexposed cohort remained statistically indistinguishable from 1 (all p > 0.05) across successive conception cohorts in the post‐rationing period. The consistent null associations across multiple conception intervals provided robust epidemiological validation for the continuous assumption of affective disorder risk profiles in this population. A placebo cutoff analysis performed in nonrationed participants yielded null associations (anxiety: HR = 0.993, 95% CI 0.985–1.002; depression: HR = 0.999, 95% CI 0.991–1.007) (Supporting Information S1: table S9). Association of sugar rationing with delays in age at first recorded diagnosis of anxiety and depression

Accelerated failure time (AFT) analysis indicated that sugar rationing during the first 1000 days after conception was associated with a delay in age at first diagnosis of both anxiety and depression (table 3). Among nonrationed individuals, the median age at first diagnosis was 29.882 years (95% CI 28.060–31.823) for anxiety and 46.283 years (95% CI 42.797–50.053) for depression. Exposure to sugar rationing in utero, in utero to 1 year and in utero to 2 years was associated with delays in the age at the first diagnosis of anxiety of 0.578 (95% CI −0.007 to 1.174), 1.217 (95% CI 0.692−1.751) and 2.049 (95% CI 1.505−2.602) years, respectively. Corresponding delays for depression were 1.004 (95% CI −0.120 to 2.156), 1.773 (95% CI: 0.770−2.798) and 4.141 (95% CI 3.066−5.240) years.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses yielded generally similar results across alternative approaches, including parametric survival models with Weibull distribution, semi‐parametric Cox proportional hazards regression, age‐restricted analyses (≤ 66 years), alternative covariate adjustment strategies, varied case definitions and exclusion criteria, and consistently demonstrated comparable protective associations across all model specifications (Supporting Information S1: tables S11–S20).

DISCUSSION

Main findings

In this large‐scale prospective cohort study, we employed the termination of sugar rationing as a natural experiment to investigate associations between sugar restriction during the first 1000 days (from conception to age 2) and the subsequent risk of anxiety or depression. Our findings indicated that sugar restriction during the first 1000 days was associated with a 17% reduction in anxiety risk and a 14% reduction in depression risk over the lifetime. The protective effect demonstrated a duration–response relationship, with continuous restriction throughout this period associated with a 24% decreased risk of anxiety and a 21% decreased risk of depression. Furthermore, sugar restriction was associated with a delay in the age of first diagnosis, by approximately 2 years for anxiety and nearly 4 years for depression.

We observed that in utero exposure to sugar rationing alone accounted for approximately one‐third of the total reduction in anxiety and depression risk and contributed to about a one‐quarter delay in age at first diagnosis. Two previous studies have also suggested that maternal adherence to a sweet dietary pattern is associated with an increased risk of anxiety and depressive symptoms in offspring. 19 , 29 By employing a quasi‐experimental design with a multi‐source case definition for anxiety and depression, our study provides a robust framework to help clarify this association. Although the association between early life sugar exposure and subsequent mental health outcomes remains underexplored, existing evidence has generally demonstrated significant links between sugar consumption and elevated risk of anxiety and depression, which is consistent with our findings. 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 For instance, the NutriNet‐Santé web‐cohort study found that high daily intake of added sugar was associated with a significantly increased risk of anxiety in adults. 37 Similarly, a Spanish cohort study reported that individuals in the highest quartile of sugar consumption had a 35% greater risk of depression compared to those in the lowest quartile. 32 A cross‐sectional study further quantified a 28% increase in depression risk per 100‐g daily increment in dietary sugar intake. 30 Another prospective cohort study found that a higher intake of both sugar‐sweetened and artificially sweetened beverages was associated with an increased risk of depression, by 26% and 40%, 36 respectively. These convergent findings underscore the consistency of the observed associations across diverse populations and study designs.

Our findings underscore the importance of sugar restriction during the first 1000 days to mitigate lifelong mental disorder risks, suggesting the need for multilevel strategies. At the individual level, evidence‐based guidance on limiting added sugar should be integrated into standard prenatal and postnatal counselling for both parents, emphasising their role in shaping the early life dietary environment. 12 At the policy level, national dietary guidelines should be strengthened to include age‐specific, actionable limits on free sugar intake. This includes clear recommendations for pregnant women, infants and children under 2 years to avoid all added sugar in alignment with WHO recommendations. 38 Moreover, interventions should focus on reducing added sugars in manufactured foods, applying fiscal measures such as taxation, facilitating the switch to low‐calorie sweeteners and improving the accessibility and affordability of healthier complementary foods. 39

Several biological mechanisms may explain the observed associations. Our findings align with the developmental origins of health and disease (DOHaD) hypothesis, which posits that early nutritional disturbances can induce persistent epigenetic modifications in stress‐response pathways, particularly through the HPA axis. 40 Excessive sugar consumption may heighten HPA axis reactivity, stimulating glucocorticoid production and pro‐inflammatory cytokine release. This cascade can lead to oxidative stress, in utero metabolic dysfunction and increased stress sensitivity. Besides, maternal dietary patterns may influence offspring neurodevelopment through changes in DNA methylation, thereby altering neuronal plasticity and inflammatory responses. 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests that dietary patterns during gestation and early lactation can programme offspring's lifelong food preferences and eating behaviours. 12 , 45 Notably, prenatal exposure to a high‐sugar diet has been shown to permanently alter taste preferences, promoting a sustained preference for sweet foods in adulthood and potentially compromising long‐term health outcomes. 12 , 45 , 46 , 47

This study has several strengths. First, we employed a natural experimental design using a limited‐time‐window approach, which significantly reduced potential confounding from secular trends in examining the association between sugar rationing and mental disorders. This design supports causal inference regarding the relationship between sugar restriction and mental health risk, consistent with another study that ruled out potential reverse causation between sugar intake and mood disorders. 33 Additionally, we conducted negative control and sensitivity analyses to validate the robustness of our analysis.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, the sugar intake of the children was not directly quantified during and after rationing in this study. However, multiple sources recorded a significant deterioration in children's dental health after the end of rationing, which may serve as a clinical marker of increased sugar consumption. 22 , 26 , 48 Second, exposure measurement relies on a policy‐driven proxy (birth date relative to the end of sugar rationing) rather than individual‐level sugar intake, which may introduce misclassification bias. Nondifferential misclassification (some post‐rationing individuals have low sugar intake, and some rationed individuals have high intake) would likely bias effect estimates towards the null, rendering our findings conservative. Differential misclassification is unlikely given the exogenous nature of the policy change. Third, although we adjusted for several covariates, potential unmeasured confounders, including maternal diet beyond sugar, and other prenatal nutritional exposures during and after the rationing period, could still introduce bias. However, the natural experimental design (exogenous policy change) mitigates concerns about these factors, as they are unlikely to be systematically associated with birth date around the rationing endpoint. To further address this concern, we plotted the trajectory of diagnosis rates across a wider range of calendar time (from 2000 to 2020, see Supporting Information S1: figure S6), restricted our analysis to a narrow birth window and thereby obtained consistent results. In addition, we conducted multiple sensitivity analyses adjusting for childhood characteristics and baseline socioeconomic and lifestyle covariates, which further validated the robustness of our findings. Fourth, the ‘healthy volunteer’ bias and the 50‐year ration‐recruitment gap might induce survivorship bias, resulting in limitations to the generalisability of the findings. This likely under‐represents those most adversely affected by early life diets, which could influence the observed associations. Our sensitivity analyses, which adjusted for baseline health conditions, partially mitigated the bias. Fifth, we defined the start of the observation period as the date of birth and included all first‐recorded diagnoses from birth through the end of follow‐up. However, early onset cases occurring before recruitment may not have been recorded in healthcare systems, potentially introducing a left truncation bias. Although we conducted several sensitivity analyses to confirm the robustness of our findings, caution is still warranted when interpreting this relationship. Sixth, the predominantly White composition and the historical nature of the UK Biobank sample limit the generalisability of our findings. Our results should be interpreted with caution when applied to contemporary or ethnically diverse populations, and replication in future cohorts is warranted.

Implications

Our study suggests that sugar restriction during the first 1000 days was associated with decreased risks of anxiety and depression, as well as a delayed first diagnosis of these disorders. These findings support dietary guidelines recommending limited sugar exposure during pregnancy and early childhood. While requiring replication in future studies, our results underscore the importance of early nutritional programming for long‐term mental health.
