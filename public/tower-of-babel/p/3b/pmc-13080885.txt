Patient views on blood‐based biomarker tests for Alzheimer's disease in primary care
Authors: Andrea Russell, Abigail Vogeley, Rebecca Lovett, Rachel O'Conor, Stephanie Batio, Jeimmy Hurtado, Morgan Bonham, Julia Yoshino Benavente, Adam M Brickman, Jeff D Williamson, Chinedu Udeh‐Momoh, Michelle M Mielke, Michael Wolf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONBlood‐based biomarkers could offer accessible, cost‐effective detection of Alzheimer's disease (AD) in primary care. This study examined patient attitudes toward AD biomarker testing.METHODSCrosssectional surveys were conducted with 572 adults from Chicago‐area clinics. Participants received brief education on biomarker tests before completing the survey.RESULTSMost participants (83.8%) were unfamiliar with biomarker tests; 1.8% previously completed testing. After brief education, most (94.5%) supported offering tests to patients with memory complaints, and 85% were willing to complete testing if recommended by their clinician. Facilitators included tests informing clinical care, coverage by insurance, and education in advance. Barriers included cost and reliability concerns. Participants commonly expected they would experience emotional distress and motivation to improve brain health after a positive test.DISCUSSIONPrimary care patients educated about biomarker testing were generally willing to undergo testing and support its use. After a positive result, informational and psychological support may be needed.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, biomarker testing, blood‐based biomarkers, dementia, early detection, primary care

BACKGROUND

An estimated 7.2 million U.S. older adults are living with Alzheimer's Disease (AD), a number projected to reach 13.8 million by 2060. 1 Early detection of AD allows those diagnosed access to disease‐modifying therapies when they may be most effective, to reduce progression risk factors, and to prepare for the future. 2 Because most patients with memory complaints present initially to primary care providers (PCPs), and many have difficulty accessing specialty aging‐related care, 1 accurate and affordable AD diagnostic tools compatible with resource‐limited primary care are needed.

When patients report memory concerns, current medical society position statements and recommendations are used to assess history, exam, and routine exclusion of other causes of potential cognitive impairment before testing AD. 3 , 4 Blood‐based biomarkers (BBMs) may determine AD etiology by examining the presence of amyloid beta (Aβ) and/or phosphorylated tau (p‐tau). 5 BBMs have several advantages over alternative methods of determining AD pathology, including positron emission tomography (PET) or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers, which are expensive, more invasive, and less accessible, particularly in primary care. 5 , 6 , 7 Recent data from Sweden showed that in primary care settings, the addition of BBMs (specifically p‐tau217) to clinical examination and imaging increased diagnostic accuracy from 61% to 91%. 8 Despite their advantages over CSF and PET, BBM tests do not yet have widespread uptake due, in part, to unclear accuracy and predictive validity. 9 The systematic and clinical barriers to widespread use of BBMs in routine clinical practice, including primary care, have been well characterized. 10 , 11 Yet, to date, patient awareness and attitudes toward BBMs are underexamined.

Existing studies have been published assessing the public's attitudes to BBMs, one among AD caregivers in the Netherlands 12 and another among a general sample of U.S. adults age 65–80. 13 Both studies found generally positive attitudes toward BBM testing, especially if treatment options were available. Few studies have examined awareness, attitudes, and acceptability of AD BBMs among U.S. primary care patients with diversity by age and morbidity. To inform future strategies for effective communication and education on AD BBMs, our objective was to examine attitudes toward testing, as well as perceived facilitators and barriers, among a sample of middle‐ and older‐aged primary care patients in the United States.

METHODS

We conducted a rapidly deployed crosssectional survey among active participants in one of three ongoing cohort studies who consented to be contacted for ancillary studies. Full details of the cohort study inclusion criteria have been published previously 14 , 15 , 16 and summarized in Table S1. Briefly, two studies addressed longitudinal aging beginning in midlife (R01AG070212, “MidCog”) and later life (R01AG030611, “LitCog”). The third cohort study was designed to examine chronic disease self‐management and mental health among primary care patients and their access and use of health care services (R01AG075043, “COVID‐19 & Chronic Conditions (C3) Study”). Each of the studies used uniform collection of demographics and social determinants of health. All study procedures were approved by the Northwestern University Institutional Review Board.

Sample

Parent study eligibility criteria are described in Table S1. Across studies, all participants were 21 year of age or older, English speaking, living with 1+ chronic conditions, and followed mostly in primary care. To recruit parent study participants, queries of medical records identified eligible participants. Letters were mailed with study information and opt out instructions. Research assistants phoned participants to complete screening. Individuals with severe or uncorrectable cognitive, visual, or hearing impairment were excluded. At in‐person or telephone baseline interviews, informed consent was obtained and patients were enrolled. Consent forms invited to participants to opt into being contacted for future research studies.

Procedure

For this survey, emails with a Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) link were sent to participants who agreed to be contacted from prior studies. Five days after the first email, a follow‐up email and text message about the study was sent with the survey link. Two weeks from the initial email, coordinators called participants to offer to complete the study via telephone. The questionnaire length was 15 min. Participants were entered into a lottery for three $500 gift cards. Data collection occurred between November 18, 2024, and January 6, 2025. In total, 1431 parent study participants were contacted to participate. Of those, 572 completed the survey, 8 consented but did not start the survey, 6 partially completed the survey, 60 declined, and 5 were ineligible. The remaining 779 passively declined, with 85 lost to follow‐up and 694 who did not respond, yielding a 40.1% response rate. Survey responders were older, had higher education levels, higher health literacy (HL), higher patient activation, and more chronic conditions than non‐responders.

Measures

Uniform descriptive information (e.g., age, education, chronic conditions) was available from parent studies. New questions assessed immediate family member history of AD and self‐reported cognitive status. The Newest Vital Sign (n = 568) or the Brief Health Literacy Screen (n = 4) assessed HL, 17 , 18 measures that are highly concordant in primary care populations. 19 The Consumer Health Activation Index was used to determine health activation. 20

Survey

Initially, participants were provided with brief information explaining AD BBMs and their potential use in primary care. It was noted that AD BBMs identify individuals at higher risk of developing AD but would not give a definitive answer. To support generalizability, our questionnaire items were based on a poll distributed from University of Michigan. 13 The survey questions are available in Appendix A of the Supplemental File. The dependent variables are detailed below.

Awareness and attitudes

Participants were asked about their familiarity with BBMs, if they had previously completed BBM testing, and if they would undergo BBM testing. They were also asked to rate the importance of offering BBM testing to (1) patients with memory and thinking problems and (2) annually for all adults age 65+.

Facilitators and barriers

Items examined how specific factors influence the acceptability of BBM testing, specifically if testing was: accompanied by comprehensive information in advance, followed with adequate prevention and treatment options for AD, recommended by PCPs, easy and convenient, covered by insurance, completed by someone they knew who had a positive experience, or able to inform medical care. We assessed potential barriers including fear of a positive result, concern about test accuracy and reliability, belief that others would view or treat them differently, being unsure if doctors or the test were trustworthy, needle phobia, or cost. Response options were: strongly agree, agree, neither agree nor disagree, disagree, strongly disagree or I don't know.

Anticipated impact of positive results

Participants rated how likely they expected to: (1) believe they would probably develop AD, (2) experience significant distress, or (3) be concerned others would view them differently after a positive test. Response options were very, somewhat, not likely, or I don't know.

Analyses

Descriptive statistics, including means with SDs and frequencies, were calculated for each survey response and by patient characteristics. Response options of “I don't know” were treated as missing, and remaining responses were collapsed dichotomously based on data distribution. Chi‐square test examined associations between patient characteristics (age, gender, race, education, HL, health activation, comorbidities, AD family history, and self‐reported cognitive status) and dependent variables, omitting missingness. Only statistically significant bivariate associations are presented (data not shown for nonsignificant associations). Multivariable modified Poisson regression models with robust error variance estimators were used to estimate adjusted risk ratios and 95% confidence intervals rather than odds ratios. 21 Patient characteristics were included in multivariable models. 22 , 23 , 24 Statistical analyses were completed using SAS version 9.4.

RESULTS

Sample characteristics (N = 572) are presented in Table 1. Participants were 61.9 years old (SD 12.8), and two‐thirds were female (63.8%). Participants were racially diverse; 27.7% identified as Black and 10.7% self‐identified as Latino. Almost a third (32.0%) had less than a college degree and 40.1% had a household income of less than $50,000 per year. Slightly less than a third of participants (28.7%) had limited HL and 50.7% had low health activation. About half of participants (47.5%) had 3+ chronic conditions, and most reported excellent (16.7%) or good (61.2%) overall memory. Over half of participants (56.8%) had at least one close blood relative diagnosed with AD. To complete the questionnaire, 474 participants used the online survey and 98 surveys were completed via telephone. Phone completers were more likely to be older, female, identify as Black, have 3+ chronic conditions, or limited HL.

Awareness and attitudes

The majority of participants (83.7%) were unfamiliar with BBMs for AD, and 1.8% reported having a prior BBM test. After receiving brief education, 76.8% and 17.7% of participants viewed it as very or somewhat important, respectively, to offer tests to individuals with memory or thinking problems. Fewer described it as very (60.4%) or somewhat (26.8%) important to offer BBM tests to any adult 65 or older. Overall, if BBM testing was recommended by their PCP, 85.0% were willing to undergo testing, 12.4% didn't know, and 2.9% were unwilling.

Facilitators

The most endorsed facilitators (Figure 1A) by participants were if testing informed medical care (94.2%), was covered by insurance (93.4%), if comprehensive education was received prior to testing (88.5%), and if testing was easy/convenient (88.1%). In bivariate analyses (Table S2), participants younger than 50 years of age were more likely to agree that comprehensive education was important prior to testing. Black individuals were more likely and Latino individuals were less likely to agree that adequate treatment/prevention options for AD would increase test acceptability. Similarly, those with limited HL were more likely to agree to testing if there were treatment options. Ease and convenience were more important to Black versus White individuals, those with limited HL, those younger than 50 years, and without family history of AD. Those with 3+ chronic conditions were more likely to identify insurance coverage for BBM tests as a facilitator. Finally, Black versus White individuals, those with limited HL, and those without AD family history were more likely to report knowing someone with a good experience would be a facilitator.

Alzheimer's disease blood‐based biomarker test facilitators and barriers in the overall sample (N = 572).

In multivariable models (Table 2), younger age remained associated with a greater need for comprehensive education prior to testing (age 50–64 vs <50: risk ratio [RR] = 0.88, 95% confidence Interval [CI] 0.82–0.95, p ≤ 0.001; age 65+ vs <50: RR = 0.86, 95% CI 0.77–0.97, p = 0.01) as well as greater acceptance of testing if there were adequate treatment or prevention options (age 50–64 vs <50: RR = 0.86, 95% CI 0.77–0.97, p = 0.01; age 65+ vs <50: RR = 0.77, 95% CI 0.63–0.93, p = 0.01). Relatedly, limited HL remained associated with greater acceptance of testing if adequate treatment options were available (RR = 1.13, 95% CI 1.03–1.24, p = 0.01). In addition, having 3+ chronic conditions was significantly associated with accepting testing if it was covered by insurance (RR = 1.05, 95% CI 1.01–1.09, p = 0.02). Finally, Black individuals (RR = 1.16, 95% CI 1.01–1.32, p = 0.04) and those with limited HL (RR = 1.19, 95% CI 1.06–1.34, p = 0.004) were more likely to endorse that someone else sharing a positive experience with BBMs would increase their acceptance of testing, whereas this was relatively less important to those with a family member with AD (RR = 0.86, 95% CI 0.77–0.96, p = 0.01).

Barriers

Overall, the most endorsed perceived barriers (Figure 1B) were test cost (49.3%), concern about test reliability (35.3%), concern about being treated differently after a positive test (24.2%), and fear of positive result (21.7%). In bivariate analyses (Table S3), compared to Whites, Latinos would be less likely to decline BBM testing due to fear of a positive result, concern the test would be inaccurate, or because they believed they would be treated differently after a positive test. In addition, male participants were less likely to report concern of being viewed differently after a positive result. Compared to Whites, Latino and Black individuals were more likely to be unsure if they could trust their doctor or the test. Similarly, individuals aged 50+ were more skeptical about trusting doctors or BBM tests compared to younger adults. Finally, individuals without family AD history were more likely to cite test costs as a barrier.

In multivariable models (Table 2), female participants demonstrated greater concern about being viewed differently following a positive result (RR = 1.57, 95% CI 1.12–2.21, p = 0.01) and about test costs (RR = 1.34, 95% CI 1.10–1.62, p = 0.003). Cost remained less of a barrier to those with family AD history (RR = 0.82, 95% CI 0.7–0.97, p = 0.02). Finally, Latino individuals were less likely to endorse being treated differently following a positive test as a barrier (RR = 0.36, 95% CI 0.16–0.8, p = 0.01).

Anticipated impact of positive results

Approximately 87% of participants said they were very likely (11.3% somewhat likely) to take steps to improve brain health and nearly three in four participants anticipated experiencing emotional distress (25.5% very likely; 47.2% somewhat likely). A quarter (25.8%) believed it was very likely (52.6% somewhat likely) they would probably develop AD, and 13.3% described they would be very likely (35.0% somewhat likely) to be concerned others would view them differently. In bivariate analyses (Table S4), adequate HL was associated with stronger beliefs in probable development of AD, more emotional distress, and greater likelihood to take steps to improve brain health following a positive test. Alternatively, low health activation was linked to a lower likelihood of taking steps to improve brain health and greater belief they would develop AD. White individuals were more likely to believe a positive test indicated probable development of AD and report likely emotional distress than Latino or Black individuals. In addition, female participants were more likely to anticipate distress following a positive test and individuals with 3+ chronic conditions were less likely to believe they would seek to improve brain health.

In multivariable models (Table 3), limited HL remained significantly associated with a lower likelihood of taking steps to improve brain health (RR = 0.92, 95% CI 0.84–1.00, p = 0.04) after a positive test. In addition, anticipated distress following a positive test was significantly higher among female participants (RR = 1.76, 95% CI 1.21–2.54, p = 0.003) and those with 3+ chronic conditions (RR = 1.40, 95% CI 1.04–1.89, p = 0.03), whereas it was significantly lower among Latino individuals (RR = 0.50, 95% CI 0.26–0.97, p = 0.04).

DISCUSSION

In this sample of middle‐aged and older adults, most of whom were recruited from primary care clinics, over 80% were unaware of AD BBMs, and only 1.8% had undergone BBM testing. After brief education, 94% believed BBM testing should be offered to older adults with memory impairment and 85% would agree to testing if recommended by their PCP. Overall, the most common perceived facilitators of undergoing BBM testing were if results had the ability to inform medical care, cost was covered by insurance, comprehensive education prior to testing was received, and if the test was easy/convenient. The top barriers were test cost, reliability concerns, fears of being treated differently if test was positive, and fear of a positive result.

These findings can be compared to the same questionnaire administered by Cox et al. to a nationally representative, general sample in January 2023. 13 They found that half agreed that BBMs should be offered annually for all adults 65+, whereas this sample was much more likely to agree. Although AD BBMs are not currently recommended for annual screening due to unclear reliability, accuracy, and in the absence of effective treatment, AD BBMs could have more widespread use in the future if these are resolved. 10 As our data were collected from a sample of existing research participants, it is possible that they were more open to newer health care solutions compared to the general population in Cox et al. Second, our population was actively engaged in health care, and it is well‐known that those engaged in primary care have up to twice a greater likelihood of engaging in preventive health. 25 , 26 , 27 Finally, in the last 18 months there has been increased media attention to AD BBMs, in addition to approval of disease‐modifying therapies, which may explain these findings.

In our sample, rates of motivation to improve health, likelihood of experiencing emotional distress, and belief they would develop AD after a positive test were higher than Cox et al., whereas the concern about being treated differently was lower—all differing by between 8%–25%. A diagnosis of dementia has a large impact on a patient's identity and can causes feelings of loss, anger, fear, and frustration, 28 and prevalence of depression and anxiety in dementia are 25% and 14%, respectively. 29 , 30 Although positive BBM tests are not official diagnoses, a positive test places patients on a path to potential diagnosis, and this heightened emotional vulnerability is a concern needing monitoring. Alternatively, high endorsement of improving brain health means some have action‐oriented coping interests and may benefit from interventions capitalizing on their motivation to be healthier and to have realistic expectations for AD risk going forward.

Adequate support following positive BBM tests will require follow‐up testing, referrals, and potentially primary care–based mental health resources and/or treatment. This could be challenging for primary care, where providers often report limited preparedness to support care of patients with dementia, short visits, lack of reimbursement, limited funding and staff for care coordination, or may presume dementia care falls to specialists. 31 , 32 , 33 In addition, clinical guidelines on when AD BBM testing should be conducted are limited and urgently needed to avoid creating practice variability. 10 , 34 Systems‐level interventions to support patients following diagnosis, like weighted reimbursement for comprehensive care plans and the Guiding an Improved Dementia Experience (GUIDE) could mitigate some challenges 35 , 36 ; however, there is a notable gap in resources for the period between identification of AD risk and the diagnosis. 28 , 34

Our data have implications for future possible widespread use of BBM tests for AD. Female, Black, and younger individuals, and those with limited HL, multiple chronic conditions, or without family history of dementia may face additional needs or barriers to accept testing. This could exacerbate health disparities, as these groups are disproportionately at risk for dementia or experience delayed diagnosis. 1 , 37 This trend has been observed already in the utilization of recently FDA‐approved monoclonal antibodies. 38 Our findings may be used to tailor public health or health system messaging, as the literature has shown effectiveness at education campaigns in HIV/AIDS, smoking cessation, and vaccine uptake. 39 , 40 , 41

Unsurprisingly, the best test facilitator was if the test results informed medical care. As AD research and development investment is in the billions, 42 , 43 the need for AD treatment is universally acknowledged. However, even in the absence of treatment, there are real benefits of early diagnosis, including an explanation for symptoms, access to monoclonal antibodies, time for patients and families to plan for the future, access to available treatments for risk reduction, ability to participate in shared decision‐making while cognition is preserved, and resources to address safety concerns 31 , 44 , 45 These are important benefits of which patients and families considering AD BBMs should be aware. Notably, potential benefits should also be described with the risks, including test error (i.e., false positive, false negative), the considerable uncertainty about if or when AD may onset following a positive test, and potential downstream complications with health insurance or employment. 46 Complete benefit versus risk conversations will be important when AD BBMs become more widely available, and both clinicians and patients may benefit from shared decision‐making tools when discussing testing.

This study had limitations. Our response rate was suboptimal. In addition, there were differences between completers and non‐completers. However, those who completed the survey were more likely to be at risk of AD (e.g., older, with more chronic conditions) and thus are more likely to have BBMs offered to them. All participants had previously participated in health‐related aging research projects and could be more open‐minded toward AD BBM tests compared to the general primary care population or the U.S. population overall. Alternatively, the prevalence of limited HL (≈30%) and lower health activation (≈45%) in the sample was common, indicating adequate representation of individuals who might be expected to be less engaged with the health care system. In addition, the education about AD BBMs provided within the survey was brief; however, this was intentional, as primary care visits are often short and time for in‐depth discussions can be lacking. Other limitations include those inherent to crosssectional research.

In conclusion, most middle‐aged and older adults in primary care would be willing to undergo AD BBM testing after receiving a brief explanation of the purpose of testing and its limitations. Specific facilitators or barriers to accepting testing emerged, which varied based on participant characteristics. Some barriers require innovations in medicine, like more AD treatment options. Other concerns could be addressed with high quality communication and resources before or after testing.
