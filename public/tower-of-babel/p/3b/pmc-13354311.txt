Relationship Between Problematic Internet Use and Health Literacy with Health-Promoting Lifestyle among Students
Authors: Sajad Javanbakht, Faeze Sobhanifar, Reza Ahmadi Jirandeh, Mohsen Vahedi, Abolfazl Rahgoi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Studying at university is a transitional and dynamic period, which provides an optimal opportunity for fostering healthy behaviors. But the high prevalence of problematic internet use (PIU) among students may hinder the development and establishment of healthy behaviors and a health-promoting lifestyle (HPL). PIU is important due to its impact on academic performance, social relationships, and policymaking on this issue. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between PIU and health literacy (HL) with HPL among students.Methods:This cross-sectional descriptive-analytical study was performed on 525 students of the University of Social Welfare and Rehabilitation Sciences in 2024, who were selected by convenience sampling. An electronic questionnaire was sent to students, which consisted of demographic, Walker’s HPL, Caplan’s PIU, and Tavousi’s HL questionnaire. Data were analyzed using SPSS Statistics version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).Results:The mean score of PIU was 55.55 ± 15.63. The mood regulation dimension had the highest score, and negative outcomes had the lowest score. HPL score was at a moderate level, and the severity of PIU was at a mild level. There was a significant negative correlation between HPL and PIU. Also a positive correlation was observed between HL and HPL.Conclusions:PIU and HL are significantly related to HPL. Managers and policymakers are encouraged to develop special programs to reduce the negative effects of PIU by enriching students’ free time, teaching media literacy, as well as increasing HL and health-promoting behaviors to improve students’ quality of life, which affects their academic performance and careers.

Introduction

The growing prevalence of noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) has recently been recognized as a major public health concern. NCDs, also known as chronic diseases, are long-term conditions that arise from a combination of genetic, physiological, environmental, and behavioral factors.[1] According to an international report, NCDs are not limited to adults; they also affect the younger generation.[2] The rising prevalence of NCD risk factors among young people presents a significant challenge to global health systems.[3] Behaviors such as unhealthy dietary habits, lack of physical activity, and stress increase the susceptibility to NCDs. Therefore, focusing on lifestyle modifications is an effective strategy for managing chronic conditions.[4]

Lifestyle refers to the habits and behaviors that individuals adopt, which may have an impact on their health status.[5] In line with this definition, health-promoting lifestyle (HPL) is characterized by the spontaneous and multifaceted beliefs and behaviors that individuals adopt in order to maintain their health and achieve self-satisfaction and self-realization.[6] HPL, as a concept, consists of health responsibility, physical activity, nutrition, interpersonal relations, and stress management.[67]

A significant portion of the young population is formed by university students, and their role as future leaders renders them vital to organizations, communities, and the whole country. During their time at college, students make more independent decisions about their lifestyle and health performance, which may have an impact on their long-term life approaches.[8] While early childhood significantly influences lifestyle choices, university years represent a critical period for shaping these behaviors. The lifestyle choices adopted during this time, if unaddressed, can negatively impact students’ health and well-being. This period is marked by significant internal and external changes affecting the individual’s physical, psychological, and social development.[9] The eating habits of university students, which are marked by irregularity in meals, reliance on fast food, increased intake of sugary drinks, low consumption of fruits and vegetables, and nutritionally inadequate diets, can have a negative impact on students’ lifestyles.[1011] Research findings show that health-promoting behaviors among students are at an unfavorable level. A study in China demonstrated that the mean scores of all dimensions of healthy lifestyle behaviors of students, except for exercise, are at an average level.[12] Almutairi et al.’s study (2018)[13] in Saudi Arabia showed that university students are leading unhealthy lives, where the majority of them have unhealthy eating habits and a poor physical activity level. Other studies in Iran also show that the HPL of students is at an average level.[1114]

One of the factors that affects HPL is problematic internet use (PIU).[15] As an important element of modern technology, the internet plays a crucial role in changing the lifestyle of individuals within a society, to the extent that it is impossible to eliminate it from everyday life.[16] Currently, it is estimated that around 5.3 billion people, representing 66% of the global population, use the internet.[17] With the rise in internet use, a significant issue that has emerged is its misuse. The scientific community remains divided on the best term to describe the use and abuse of new technologies. Therefore, different models and terminologies have been used to refer to the excessive behavior of internet use in addition to PIU, including internet addiction, pathological internet use, and internet dependence. According to Fernandes et al. (2019),[18] PIU appears to be the most appropriate term. Proper use of the Internet is characterized by employing it to achieve a certain goal within a specific timeframe that does not result in cognitive or behavioral issues. Any type of Internet use that does not meet the aforementioned criteria, i.e., is not purposeful, is not done within a specific timeframe, or causes behavioral and cognitive problems, is considered PIU, and it appears that a significant number of individuals are using the Internet in a way that is not consistent with this definition.[19]

Another factor that influences HPL is health literacy (HL). HL is defined as the ability of a person to obtain knowledge and information and use them in order to maintain and improve health in a way that is appropriate to the individual and system contexts.[20] HL has been identified as one of the main elements for leading a healthy lifestyle[21] and is a key determinant of health in society. Inadequate HL can be the cause of many adverse health outcomes.[22] Nowadays, HL is crucial for young people, as individuals possessing high HL levels tend to experience more favorable health outcomes compared to others. Also, the main causes of mortality and disability in young people and adolescents have shifted from infectious diseases to behavioral issues, and evidence from Iran points to an upward trend in chronic diseases among this demographic group.[23]

Students form a significant portion of Internet users, and studies show that the level of Internet use, especially PIU, is increasing among these users. According to a study, the prevalence of PIU among students was 0.08% to 47.7%.[24] In Iran, studies that have addressed the concept of PIU indicate that the prevalence of PIU in students is between 20% and 33.8%.[252627] Several studies have demonstrated that excessive use of the Internet is associated with numerous negative consequences in various aspects of life, including sleep, nutrition, physical activity, academic and professional success, and family relationships. These issues can lead to various physical and mental disorders such as sleep disorders, back pain, depression, anxiety, loneliness, and low self-esteem.[15282930]

Students form a significant portion of the young population, and the college period is known as a transitional and dynamic period, which provides an optimal opportunity for fostering healthy behaviors. Considering the unfavorable lifestyle habits of students and the increasing rates of PIU among them, along with the limited body of research investigating the relationship between HPL, HL, and PIU, this study was designed to investigate the relationship between PIU and HL with HPL among students of the University of Social Welfare and Rehabilitation Sciences.

Methods

This study used a cross-sectional design and was conducted between February and June 2024. A convenience sampling method was used, and 525 students from the University of Social Welfare and Rehabilitation Sciences in Tehran from three faculties and four levels of education (bachelor’s, master’s, doctorate, and residency) participated in this study by completing a questionnaire. The data collection tool in this study was a web-based questionnaire, which consisted of a demographic questionnaire, Walker’s HPL questionnaire, Caplan’s PIU questionnaire, and Tavousi’s HL questionnaire.

The demographic questionnaire included eight questions related to age, gender, marital status, Ministry related to the field of study, educational level, year of education, place of residence, and amount of daily Internet use.

The PIU scale has 15 items that assess internet use in five subdimensions of preference for online social interaction, mood regulation, cognitive preoccupation, compulsive internet use, and negative outcomes. Items are designed based on a seven-point Likert scale and are scored from 1 to 7, and the total score ranges from 15 to 105. A score of 15–45 indicates a normal state, a score of 46–65 shows a mild disorder, a score of 66–85 suggests a moderate disorder, and a score of 86–105 reveals a severe disorder.[3132] This scale was first designed by Caplan in 2002 and was then revised in 2010. This questionnaire was translated into Persian by Ahmadpour et al.[33] in Iran, using the forward-backward translation method, and then its content validity was examined by ten experts using the content validity ratio (CVR). The overall validity of the instrument was reported to be 85.3% and the overall reliability of the questionnaire was 92.5% based on the alpha coefficient.

Walker’s Health-Promoting Lifestyle Profile (HPLP-II) consists of 52 items that measures health-promoting behaviors in six dimensions: Self-actualization (items 1–9), health responsibility (items 10–18), interpersonal relationships (items 19–27), stress management (items 28–35), physical activity (items 36–43), and nutrition (items 44–52). It is scored on a four-point Likert scale (always = 4, often = 3, sometimes = 2, and never = 1). The score for each dimension indicates the intensity of the behavior in that dimension, with a total score ranging from 52 to 208 across all dimensions. No cut-off point has been established for this questionnaire.[34] HPLP-II was first designed by Walker, and the overall reliability of this questionnaire in its English version was 94%.[3435] This questionnaire was translated into Persian by Mohammadi Zeidi et al.[36] in Iran, using the forward-backward translation method, and then its validity was examined by 11 experts. The alpha reliability coefficient was 0.82 for the total scale and ranged from 0.64 to 0.91 for the subscales. The confirmatory factor analysis related to the six-factor model represented an acceptable fit. Also, a test-retest method was performed with an interval of two weeks, and the correlation coefficient between the scores of the test–retest was 0.91.

Tavousi et al.’s[37] HL questionnaire consists of nine items that assesses HL in two dimensions: Basic skills and decision-making skills. It is scored on a five-point Likert scale (completely easy/always = 5, easy/often = 4, neither easy nor difficult/sometimes = 3, difficult/rarely = 2, completely difficult/never = 1). This questionnaire was designed in 2022 by Tavousi et al.[37] in the general population. The content validity of the questionnaire was desirable (the CVR was more than 0.56, and the content validity index (CVI) was more than 0.79). Also, the face validity, as assessed by ten adults, was satisfactory. The results obtained from exploratory factor analysis showed a two-factor structure for the questionnaire, namely, basic skills and decision-making skills, that jointly accounted for about 61% of the variance observed. Furthermore, the confirmatory factor analysis indicated acceptable fit indexes for the data. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient and intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for each of the two dimensions were satisfactory (alpha = 0.84 and 0.81, and ICC = 0.85 and 0.82). The values for the whole items were 0.91 and 0.81, respectively.

To ensure the reliability and accuracy of responses, several control questions were added to the web-based questionnaire, and after obtaining the code of ethics, the link to the questionnaire was sent to students via short message service (SMS) and online university groups that belonged to the students.

After the sampling phase was completed, the data were analyzed using the statistical package for social sciences Data were analyzed using SPSS Statistics version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). at a significance level of 0.05. Descriptive statistics (mean and standard deviation, frequency, and percentage) were used to present the demographic characteristics of the participants and HPL, PIU, and HL scores. Pearson correlation coefficient was used to determine the relationship between PIU and HL with HPL. An independent samples t-test was used to compare the mean scores of study variables in terms of the two-state qualitative variables. Analysis of variance (Anova) (equality of variances was met) or Welch Anova (equality of variances was not met) was used to compare the mean scores of study variables in terms of the multistate qualitative variables. Homogeneity of variance was assessed using Levene’s test. In the post hoc analysis, the Tukey test (equality of variances was met) or the Games-Howell test (equality of variances was not met) was used. Multiple regression was used to determine the predictors of HPL.

Discussion

This study was designed to investigate the relationship between PIU and HL with HPL among students of the University of Social Welfare and Rehabilitation Sciences.

According to the results, PIU had a significantly positive correlation with age. This finding was not consistent with the study of Layegh and Abazari[38] and another study,[33] which reported no significant relationship between these two variables. In other studies, with the increase in age, PIU decreased,[39] which does not support the result of the present research. This inconsistency could be due to the participation of students who have a limited age range in this study.

Also, there was a significant difference between PIU and all of its dimensions based on education level. This finding in the Iranian population was in line with the results of the study by Lashgarara et al.[40] but was not consistent with the study by Maheri et al.[14]

There was no significant relationship between PIU and place of residence. Results of Abdi et al.[41] supports this finding. However, Farhadinia et al.[42] and other research in this area[4344] are not in line with the present study. Therefore, future studies need to investigate this relationship further.

According to the results of this study, the average amount of Internet use by students was 3 h per day, and 33.1% of the participants spent 2 to 4 h per day using the Internet, which is consistent with the findings of Layegh and Abazari in the study of students at Ardabil University of Medical Sciences, despite a slight difference with the present study.[38] The severity of the PIU disorder in the present study was mild in most participants (39.5%). This finding is consistent with the results of the study by Ahmadpour et al.[33] and Kumar et al.[45] in range of mild and moderate disorder, but the results regarding the severity of PIU disorder in the severe range differ significantly, which may be due to the use of different instruments, because in the Internet addiction test, the problematic use range is divided into three parts: Mild, moderate, and severe, while the Kaplan PIU questionnaire which was used in this study also has a normal range in its classification.

The results indicate that there is a significant and direct relationship between the amount of Internet use and the overall score of PIU, meaning as the amount of daily internet use increases, the PIU score also increases, which is consistent with the results of the study by Zheng et al.,[46] Viseshfar,[47] and implicitly with the findings of the study by Langarizadeh et al.[48] This finding highlights the need for education about reducing the amount of Internet use in order to prevent PIU and its consequences.

In this study, the mean score of HPL was 135.12 ± 15.48, indicating a moderate level of HPL, which is consistent with the studies of Ebadi et al.,[49] Heidari et al.,[50] and Peker and Bermek in Turkey.[51] In the study by Tabee Burdbar et al.,[52] the mean score of HPL was not desirable, which is consistent with the aforementioned results. Interpersonal relationships had the highest score, and physical activity had the lowest score, which is consistent with the studies conducted in Iran and England.[49505354] According to the findings, students with unhealthy lifestyles, compared to others, have a higher risk of experiencing depression, anxiety, and adopting unhealthy behaviors.[55] These consequences can be prevented with interventions such as HPL education. On the other hand, the status of physical activity among students requires special attention and short-term and long-term planning in universities.

HPL is inversely related to PIU and all its dimensions. This finding is in line with the study by Abdi et al.[41] and other studies conducted in Korea and Turkey.[565758]

The results of the present study showed that there is a direct and significant correlation between HL and HPL, which means that as the HL and its dimensions increase, the HPL score also increases, which is consistent with the findings of the study by Tabee Burdbar et al.[52] and García et al.[59] The study by Lee et al.[60] also indicates that there is a direct and significant correlation between HL, HPL, and general health, which is consistent with the findings of the present study.

Conclusions

The findings indicate that the average score of PIU among students is in the range of mild disorder, and the average score of HPL can be described as moderate. PIU and HL are significantly correlated with HPL. Therefore, it is recommended that administrators and policymakers take steps to improve the quality of life of students by developing programs to reduce problematic use of the Internet through enriching students’ leisure time and teaching media literacy and health-promoting behaviors, as well as increasing HL, which is likely to affect the quality of their services.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. Due to the cross-sectional nature of the research, the ability to accurately describe the relationship between variables and establish a cause-and-effect relationship is limited. On the other hand, the study population consists of a group of young people studying in health-related fields, which requires caution in generalizing the results based on age and field of study.

Future studies

According to the limitations, prospective and experimental studies are recommended to examine the relationship between variables. Also, conducting similar studies in different age groups and economic levels can facilitate data generalization and be helpful for developing strategies.
