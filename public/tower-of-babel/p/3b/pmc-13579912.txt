Including randomized and non-randomized studies of interventions in evidence synthesis for harms: a meta-epidemiological study
Authors: Fan Mei, Minghong Yao, Yu Ma, Jiayidaer Huan, Liu Yang, Kang Zou, Ling Li, Xin Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNon-randomized studies of interventions (NRSIs) provide important evidence on harms, especially for rare adverse events that randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are often underpowered to detect. Evidence synthesis is therefore needed to integrate findings across study designs and to inform a comprehensive assessment of harms. However, synthesizing evidence from RCTs and NRSIs remains methodologically challenging. We examined how evidence from RCTs and NRSIs is synthesized in practice and how conclusions were drawn when findings conflict.MethodsThe meta-epidemiological study included systematic reviews indexed in PubMed between 1 January 2017 and 31 December 2024 that synthesized evidence from both RCTs and NRSIs for the same outcome. We evaluated methodological practices across four synthesis scenarios. For reviews that combined RCTs and NRSIs in a meta-analysis, we assessed key methodological components of the review process. For reviews that meta-analyzed RCTs and NRSIs separately, we assessed qualitative agreement between RCTs and NRSIs based on the magnitude, direction, and statistical significance of the estimates. When qualitative disagreement was observed, we further evaluated whether the review conclusions were reasonable, taking into account the certainty of evidence and the heterogeneity of the estimates.ResultsOf 42,341 records screened, 195 systematic reviews were included. 49 (25.1%) conducted only qualitative syntheses of both RCTs and NRSIs. 11 (5.6%) meta-analyzed only RCTs, with NRSIs synthesized qualitatively; and 7 (3.6%) meta-analyzed only NRSIs, with RCTs synthesized qualitatively. Among the 91 reviews (46.7%) that combined RCTs and NRSIs in a single meta-analysis, important methodological gaps were identified: 72.5% included NRSIs at moderate or high risk of bias, 49.5% used unadjusted estimates, and 53.8% did not conduct subgroup analyses by study design. Separate meta-analyses for RCTs and NRSIs were conducted in 37 reviews (19.0%), of which 67.6% showed qualitative disagreement between the two study designs, and 20.0% were judged to have inappropriate conclusions according to our assessment criteria.ConclusionsSystematic reviews synthesizing RCTs and NRSIs for harms frequently overlook essential methodological considerations and often draw conclusions without adequately addressing conflicting findings across study designs. These practices risk compromising the credibility of harm assessments used in clinical, regulatory, and policy decision-making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-026-05022-4.

Background

For regulatory approval and clinical decision making, accurate knowledge of benefits and harms is essential [1]. Although randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are the reference standard for evaluating intervention benefits [2, 3], they have important limitations in the assessment of harms because some harms may occur beyond the follow-up period, and RCTs frequently lack sufficient statistical power to detect rare events [4, 5]. Non-randomized studies of interventions (NRSIs), often drawing on real-world data, can therefore provide valuable complementary evidence [6, 7]. Evidence synthesis, which integrates findings from multiple studies on the same topic [8], should therefore incorporate and critically appraise evidence from both RCTs and NRSIs to obtain a comprehensive assessment of harms. A Cochrane review further highlighted that, across a range of healthcare interventions, effect estimates from RCTs and NRSIs are often similar [9], strengthening the feasibility of integrating these two sources of evidence.

A key methodological question is whether evidence from RCTs and NRSIs should be synthesized separately or pooled in a meta-analysis, given that inappropriate synthesis may distort conclusions about harms [10, 11]. However, authoritative guidelines offer conflicting recommendations on when and how evidence from RCTs and NRSIs should be combined [7, 12–14]. For example, the Cochrane Collaboration discourages pooling RCTs and NRSIs in a single meta-analysis [14], whereas the International Society for Pharmacoepidemiology (ISPE) supports quantitative synthesis alongside RCTs when NRSIs are well adjusted [12]. Conflicting guidance on how to currently integrate evidence from RCTs and NRSIs undermines the effective use of both study designs to support decision-making. In the context of harm assessment, for example, researchers may be inclined to directly pool RCTs and NRSIs to obtain a statistically significant result, without accounting for the influence of risk of bias in NRSIs on the certainty of the evidence [15].

Although previous studies have highlighted methodological challenges in combining RCTs and NRSIs [16–19], they are limited by selective samples, outdated evidence, or a narrow scope, such as nutrition research. Moreover, no empirical study has systematically examined these issues in harm assessment. This gap is important because our recent meta-epidemiological study, based on the full medical database, showed that incorporating NRSIs into meta-analyses altered RCT results in about one third of reviews, and that harms were the most common scenario in which RCTs and NRSIs were combined in a meta-analysis [16, 20, 21]. We therefore conducted a large-scale investigation to examine how evidence from RCTs and NRSIs is synthesized in practice and how conclusions are reached when findings differ. We also compared the methodological characteristics of reviews published before and after 2022, after the publication of key methodological guidance, to assess whether methodological practice had improved [7, 12, 13].

Methods

The protocol was prospectively registered on the Open Science Framework [21]. We followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [22] to structure our manuscript (Additional file 1).

Data sources

We searched PubMed for systematic reviews published between 1 January 2017 and 31 December 2024 that incorporated both RCTs and NRSIs. The search strategy combined terms pertaining to RCTs, NRSIs, evidence synthesis, and harms (Additional file 2: Table S1).

Eligibility criteria and study screening

We included pairwise systematic reviews, with or without meta-analysis, that specifically evaluated harms associated with pharmacological interventions used in humans, including vaccines, and that reported at least one dichotomous harms outcome informed by both RCTs and NRSIs. No restrictions were applied regarding age, sex, or population. We defined a study as a systematic review according to Cochrane Handbook criteria as reviews with predefined eligibility criteria, systematic searching, and risk-of-bias assessment [23]. RCTs were defined as studies in which participants were randomly allocated to intervention groups [23]. NRSIs were defined as quasi-randomized trials, non-randomized controlled trials, cohort studies, case-controlled studies, controlled before-after studies, case series, case reports, or cross-sectional studies [13].

Eligible studies were required to have a primary objective of evaluating harms, defined as “exclusively an unintended effect or effects of an intervention”, including adverse event, adverse effect, adverse reaction, complications [24]. We limited inclusion to English-language reviews published in journals indexed in the Journal Citation Reports (JCR) based on their 2024 ranking. We excluded network meta-analyses, dose-response meta-analyses, meta-analyses of proportions, scoping reviews, protocols, conference abstracts, editorials, comments, and short reports. Titles, abstracts, and full texts were independently screened by pairs of reviewers (F.M., Y.M., J.Y., L.Y.). Disagreements were resolved by consensus or adjudication by a third reviewer (M.Y. or L.L.).

Data extraction

For each review, we selected one harm outcome informed by both RCTs and NRSIs. When multiple primary outcomes were reported, we selected the first primary outcome presented in the methods section. We extracted data on disease area, comparator and outcome types, numbers and overall sample size of RCTs and NRSIs, NRSI designs, risk of bias assessment, synthesis approach, additional analyses (subgroup analyses, sensitivity analyses, and publication bias), and reasons for not conducting meta-analysis. We extracted the journal and publication year of each systematic review and subsequently classified journals as JCR Q1 or non-Q1 and reviews as published before or after 2022. Detailed definitions for all extract parameters are provided in Additional file 2: Table S2. If the type of NRSIs contributing to the selected outcome was not specified, we deduced them by consulting the original studies cited in the review.

To determine which source of evidence (i.e., derived from RCTs, NRSIs, or pooled estimates) supported the review conclusions, we compared the results from each evidence source against the conclusions presented in the review. This judgement was based on both the quantitative findings and the authors’ narrative interpretation, with unclear cases resolved by reviewer consensus. As abstract often serve as a key source of information for readers to assess the trustworthiness of review conclusions [25], we further evaluated whether effect estimates were clearly reported and whether their source was specified in the abstract.

For reviews that pooled RCTs and NRSIs in a single meta-analysis, we additionally extracted information on pre-synthesis assessments of similarity in populations, interventions/exposures, comparisons, and outcomes (PI/ECO), as well as assessments of heterogeneity and risk of bias. We also recorded the statistical models and effect measures used, how confounding was addressed, reported certainty of evidence, and the meta-analytic results.

Included systematic reviews were assigned to four reviewer teams (each team with two members). Within each team, two reviewers independently extracted data using a standardized, pilot-tested form with detailed written instructions to ensure consistency. Disagreements were resolved by discussion or third-party adjudication (M.Y. or L.L.), and data accuracy was subsequently verified by two reviewers (F.M. or Y.M.).

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as means with standard deviations (SDs) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). As high-impact journals are widely perceived to show better methodological practice [26], we compared key characteristics of systematic reviews published in JCR Q1 versus non-Q1 journals [27]. For reviews that conducted separate meta-analyses of RCTs and NRSIs, we identified and recorded reported Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) ratings when available [23]. When GRADE ratings were not reported, we independently assessed the certainty of evidence based on risk of bias (RoB, using the results reported by authors), inconsistency, imprecision, indirectness, and publication bias, using the reported RoB assessments. In line with GRADE guidance [29], evidence from NRSIs assessed with the Newcastle-Ottawa scale (NOS) was initially rated as low certainty, whereas evidence assessed with the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool was initially rated as high certainty, as ROBINS-I is designed to evaluate individual NRSIs against a hypothetical target RCT and explicitly evaluates selection bias and confounding [30]. For reviews with qualitative disagreement between RCTs and NRSIs, we then assessed the reasonableness of the review conclusions using criteria that considered certainty of evidence, direction and magnitude of effect estimates, and statistical significance (Fig. 1). These criteria drew on a suggestion from one reviewer, together with relevant work from the GRADE Working Group [31] and existing methodological guidance [7, 13, 14].

Results

Our initial literature search yielded 42,341 records, of which 1,139 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. After screening, 195 systematic reviews met the inclusion criteria and were included in the analysis (Fig. 2). The most common reasons for exclusion were failure to include both RCTs and NRSIs for the same outcome, non-pairwise systematic reviews, lack of pharmacological interventions, or absence of a primary safety objective. Among included reviews, 118 were published in JCR Q1 journals and 77 in non-Q1 journals.

General characteristics of included systematic reviews

The general characteristics of the included systematic reviews are summarized in Table 1. Overall, 81 reviews (41.5%) did not report registration information, and 134 (68.7%) focused on morbidity outcomes (Additional file 2: Table S3). Eligibility criteria for NRSIs were variably reported: 46 reviews (23.6%) did not prespecify the eligible NRSI design, whereas 22 (11.3%) imposed no restrictions on NRSI design, and 111 (56.9%) ultimately included multiple NRSI designs (Additional file 2: Fig. S1). The median number of RCTs per outcomes was 3 (IQR, 1–5), encompassing a median of 1,309 participants (IQR, 371 − 10,140). For NRSIs, the median number per outcomes was 6 (IQR, 4–11), with a median of 21,425 participants (IQR, 1,618 − 308,249). No significant differences were observed when comparing these characteristics between Q1 and non-Q1 journal.

Among the 195 systematic reviews, 33 (16.9%) applied the same RoB tool to both RCTs and NRSIs. For RCTs, the most commonly used tools were the Cochrane RoB tool (49.2%) and RoB2 (26.7%), whereas the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale was most frequently used for NRSIs (60.0%) (a complete list is provided in Additional file 2: Table S1). Risk of bias was rarely explicitly considered in meta-analyses, with only 30 reviews (15.4%) reporting this for RCTs and 36 reviews (18.5%) for NRSIs. Approaches included sensitivity analyses incorporating risk of bias, exclusion of studies at high risk of bias, and refraining from meta-analysis when risk of bias was deemed high. Assessment of the certainty of evidence was conducted in only 37 reviews (19.0%).

Synthesis approaches

Among the 195 reviews, 49 (25.1%) synthesized evidence from both RCTs and NRSIs qualitatively only. Meta-analyses were conducted only for RCTs in 11 reviews (5.6%) and only for NRSIs in 7 reviews (3.6%). In 91 reviews (46.7%), RCTs and NRSIs were pooled in a single meta-analysis, whereas 37 reviews (19.0%) conducted separate meta-analyses by study design.

Among the 49 reviews that synthesized evidence qualitatively only, 33 (67.3%) reported a rationale for not conducting a meta-analysis. Among the 91 reviews that pooled RCTs and NRSIs in a single meta-analysis, 42 (46.2%) performed subgroup analyses by study design, whereas 49 (53.8%) did not. No significant differences were observed between reviews published in Q1 and non-Q1 journals.

Characteristics of reviews combining RCTs and NRSIs in a meta-analysis

As shown in Table 3, assessment of comparability between RCTs and NRSIs before pooling was infrequent. Among the 91 pooled meta-analyses, 32 (35.2%) did not evaluate PI/ECO similarity, and only 12 (13.2%) assessed heterogeneity from RCTs and NRSIs. Few reviews (17.6%) accounted for RoB in the meta-analysis, while most included NRSIs at moderate or high RoB (72.5%). Nearly half (49.5%) relied on unadjusted effect estimates from NRSIs. Among reviews reporting adjusted estimates, only a minority specified the confounding adjustment method (n = 6, 20.0%), prespecified the confounders (n = 7, 23.3%), or listed adjusted variables (n = 13, 43.3%). In reviews including multiple NRSI designs, 13 (40.6%) combined evidence across designs without distinction. Random-effects models were used in most pooled meta-analyses (90.1%).

Characteristics of additional analyses

Characteristics of additional analyses are summarized in Table 4. Among the 146 studies that conducted meta-analyses, 30 (20.5%) assessed heterogeneity separately for RCTs and NRSIs. Subgroup analyses were performed in 82 reviews (56.2%), most commonly to explore sources of heterogeneity, including differences in study design (53.6%). Meta-regression was infrequently used (10.9%). Sensitivity analyses were conducted in 75 reviews (51.4%), with discrepancies between primary and sensitivity analyses observed in 17.3%. Assessment of publication bias was not reported in 59 reviews (40.4%), primarily owing to small numbers of included studies (Additional file 2: Table S4). Among the remaining reviews, publication bias was most often evaluated by pooling RCTs and NRSIs (74.7%) (Table 4).

Changes in methodological characteristics of systematic reviews including RCTs and NRSIs published up to 2022 and after 2022

A total of 142 reviews were published in 2022 and before, and 53 reviews were published in 2023 and after (Additional file 2: Table S5). More recent reviews showed modest methodological improvements, including higher rates of protocol registration, greater adherence to PRISMA reporting guidelines, and increased use of contemporary RoB tools (Cochrane RoB 2 for RCTs and NOS or ROBINS-I for NRSIs). However, among reviews pooling RCTs and NRSIs, assessment of comparability between RCTs and NRSIs remains limited. Key aspects, including PI/ECO similarity, heterogeneity, and RoB, showed no significant differences.

Drawing conclusions across synthesis scenarios

Table 5 summarizes how different synthesis strategies informed review conclusions. Among meta-analyses including only RCTs, most reviews (63.6%) based their conclusions exclusively on RCT findings. When only NRSIs were meta-analyzed, 42.9% of reviews relied solely on NRSI findings. Among reviews that conducted separate meta-analyses of RCTs and NRSIs and found qualitative disagreement (n = 25), 32.0% based their conclusions on RCT evidence alone, whereas 16.0% relied on NRSI evidence alone. After considering statistical significance, the direction and magnitude of the effect, and certainty of evidence (Additional file 2: Table S6), 5 reviews (20.0%) were judged to have reached unreasonable conclusions (Additional file 2: Table S7).

Among 49 reviews that pooled RCTs and NRSIs without subgroup analysis by study design, qualitative disagreement between the RCT-specific and pooled estimates was observed in 25 (53.2%), yet all conclusions relied exclusively on the pooled estimates. Discussion

Principal findings

This large meta-epidemiological study examined how RCTs and NRSIs are used in evidence synthesis of harms and how such evidence informs conclusions. We identified substantial methodological issues across reviews. More than one-quarter did not report registration information, and approximately 20% lacked clarity regarding the types of NRSI included. Among reviews that pooled RCTs and NRSIs in a single meta-analysis (46.7%), nearly half relied exclusively on unadjusted estimates, the most did not address comparability between study designs, including PI/ECO similarity, RoB, or heterogeneity.

Our analyses further highlighted the decision-making challenges arising from heterogeneous synthesis strategies. When RCTs and NRSIs were meta-analyzed separately, 67.6% of comparisons showed qualitative disagreement between RCTs and NRSIs. However, the conclusions of systematic reviews were commonly based solely on statistical significance, without considering the certainty of evidence. Based on our prespecified assessment criteria, we judged the conclusions of nearly one fifth of these meta-analyses to be inappropriate. These challenges were amplified when RCTs and NRSIs were pooled in a single meta-analysis. A major concern was that the two types of evidence were often pooled primarily to achieve statistical significance. Such practices are rarely justifiable, as NRSIs typically have larger sample sizes and more precise estimates, which directly influence pooled results [16, 32]. When subgroup analyses by study design were not conducted, pooled estimates conflicted with RCT results in nearly half of reviews, yet conclusions continued to rely primarily on pooled effects without considering the potential bias introduced by NRSIs [33]. A similar pattern was observed even when subgroup analyses were performed.

Differences between RCTs and NRSIs were also commonly overlooked in assessments of publication bias. Nearly half of the meta-analyses evaluated publication bias by pooling RCTs and NRSIs, a practice rarely justified given the greater susceptibility of NRSIs to selective publication and exaggerated effect estimates [12, 31, 34, 35]. Although modest improvements in characteristics such as protocol registration and adherence to reporting guidelines were observed following the release of methodological guidance in 2022 [7, 12, 36], critical issues that threaten the validity of harm-related decision-making—particularly assessment of comparability between RCTs and NRSIs - remain largely unaddressed.

Comparison with other studies

Previous methodological research has examined related issues in systematic reviews, but most studies have focus on general processes, such as search strategies or data extraction, or were limited by small sample sizes [18, 19, 37–39]. For instance, Cheurfa et al. reported that 33% of reviews pooled RCTs and observational studies in a single meta-analysis; [37] however, their analysis was based on systematic reviews of healthcare interventions published between 2015 and 2019 may not reflect current practice in harm assessment. A recent Cochrane methodology review comparing effect estimates from RCTs and NRSIs found generally small differences between study designs, supporting the potential comparability of these sources of evidence [9]. However, the review did not address how such evidence is used and integrated in systematic reviews of harms, nor how conclusions are drawn when findings from RCTs and NRSIs are combined or conflict.

Methodological literature has consistently emphasized the important of NRSIs in harms evaluation [40], yet no prior study has provided empirical evidence. To our knowledge, this study provides the first comprehensive meta-epidemiological study of systematic reviews of harms that incorporate both RCTs and NRSIs. We highlighted methodological issues across synthesis approaches and examined how conclusions were derived under different scenarios - particularly when evidence was conflicting - and explored the impact of methodological guidelines on subsequent synthesis practices.

Implications for future evidence synthesis practice

The use of RCTs and NRSIs in systematic reviews of harms has expanded rapidly. Our findings provide valuable insights into how evidence synthesis of RCTs and NRSIs inform harm-related decision-making and the reliability of review conclusions. However, there has long been debate about how to integrate RCTs and NRSIs (e.g., in separate meta-analysis, combined in a single meta-analysis, or a combination of qualitative and quantitative syntheses) [7, 12]. Given the diversity of these synthesis scenarios, it is difficult to recommend any one approach as preferable. More importantly, regardless of the integration approach adopted, differences between RCTs and NRSIs should be addressed with caution.

As such, we provided the following suggestions for future evidence synthesis of harms. Firstly, when both RCTs and NRSIs are synthesized qualitatively, review authors should clearly justify why meta-analysis was not undertaken, such as substantial statistical heterogeneity, important differences in PI/ECO, or concerns about risk of bias. Secondly, when meta-analysis is conducted for only one study design, either RCTs or NRSIs, the role of the other study design should be explicitly stated, for example, as complementary evidence or to support interpretation of the findings. Thirdly, when separate meta-analyses are conducted for RCTs and NRSIs, the consistency of findings across study designs should be carefully assessed. Where findings are inconsistent, results from RCTs and NRSIs should be presented separately, and conclusions should take into account the certainty of evidence, as well as the magnitude, direction, and statistical significance of effect estimates. Fourthly, when RCTs and NRSIs are combined in a single meta-analysis, their comparability should be carefully assessed, including PI/ECO similarity and qualitative agreement in effect estimates [18, 41]. Combining RCTs and NRSIs directly to achieve statistical significance, without addressing the risk of bias in NRSIs, should be avoided [14]. When RCT evidence on harms is limited, including NRSIs alongside RCTs may be warranted to safeguard patient interests. However, NRSIs at high risk of bias should be excluded, and adjusted estimates should be employed when available [14, 39]. In such cases, subgroup analyses by study design, sensitivity analyses assessing the impact of including NRSIs on pooled results, and exploration of sources of statistical heterogeneity are all suggested.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of our study include a comprehensive and representative sample of evidence synthesis of harms, duplicate assessment at all stages, and rigorous data collection and analysis. We used prespecified criteria to assess qualitative agreement in results and the reasonableness of conclusions across different synthesis strategies, based on the direction and magnitude of effect estimates and changes in statistical significance. We also thoroughly provided detailed practical, scenario-specific suggestions that may improve the credibility of future evidence syntheses and inform methodological guidance.

Several limitations warrant consideration. Firstly, our analysis was restricted to pairwise meta-analyses with dichotomous outcomes, and findings may not generalize to other types of meta-analyses or continuous outcomes. Secondly, our search was limited to PubMed; however, we consider PubMed to provide broad coverage and sufficient representativeness to address the objective of the current study. Thirdly, we examined only the reporting of effect estimates and source of evidence in abstracts, as these often serve as the primary source of information for readers to appraise the trustworthiness of review conclusions. However, abstracts may not capture important methodological details. Our findings may, therefore, underestimated the extent to which authors accounted for methodological issues when synthesizing evidence from RCTs and NRSIs. Finally, our criteria for assessing the reasonableness of review conclusions and qualitative agreement represent a preliminary methodological exploration intended to identify potential methodological issues. As such, some degree of subjectivity cannot be entirely ruled out, and the findings based on these criteria should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

Our empirical findings highlight substantial methodological shortcomings in the synthesis of harm evidence from RCTs and NRSIs, for example, inadequate assessment of comparability between study designs prior to meta-analysis. Inconsistencies between estimates from RCTs and NRSIs are often overlooked when drawing conclusions, potentially undermining the validity of the evidence. Drawing on these findings, we developed tailored suggestions for each synthesis scenario to improve practice. Our findings underscore the need for more detailed guidance to support evidence synthesizers, clinicians, and policymakers.
