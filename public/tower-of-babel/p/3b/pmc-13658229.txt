Treatment-aware deep learning enables counterfactual prediction of individual benefit from PARP inhibitors in ovarian cancer
Authors: Jean-Sébastien Frenel, Pierre-Etienne Heudel, Emmanuelle Guinaudeau, Céline Bossard, Carmela Pisano, Giuseppe Caruso, Joseph Rynkiewicz, Sanae Salhi, Yahia Salhi, Jérôme Chetritt, Eric Pujade-Lauraine, Isabelle Ray-Coquard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) assays are used to select patients with ovarian cancer for PARP inhibitors, but they do not fully capture the heterogeneity of treatment benefit. Using data from the phase III PAOLA-1 randomized trial, we developed a deep learning model based on diagnostic hematoxylin and eosin whole-slide images from treatment-naive primary tumors to estimate individual benefit from maintenance olaparib plus bevacizumab versus bevacizumab alone. The model combined image-derived features with randomized treatment assignment and progression-free survival data to estimate, for each patient, the risk of progression under both treatments. The difference between these risks defined a continuous Estimated Treatment Improvement (ETI) score, which quantifies patient-level treatment effect. In 421 patients with available pre-treatment slides, ETI showed a strong treatment-biomarker interaction for first progression-free survival (interaction HR 0.36, 95% CI 0.22–0.59; p < 0.005), while HRD status showed a similar interaction magnitude (HR 0.41, 95% CI 0.25–0.64; p < 0.005). ETI remained independently associated with outcome after adjustment for clinical covariates and HRD status. Subgroup and STEPP analyses indicated that ETI further stratified benefit within both HRD-positive and HRD-negative tumors. Attention maps suggested an association between multifocal tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes and predicted benefit from olaparib. These findings suggest that histology-based modeling of heterogeneity of treatment effects may complement HRD testing for treatment decision-making, although external validation remains necessary.

Introduction

Olaparib, a first-in-class PARP inhibitor, has become a cornerstone of maintenance therapy for advanced epithelial ovarian cancer following a response to platinum-based chemotherapy. Its efficacy is closely linked to HRD status, including BRCA1/2 mutations, yet current HRD assays, designed to detect static “genomic scars”, may not capture the tumor’s real-time DNA-repair status at the time of therapy initiation. Although landmark trials such as SOLO-11 and PAOLA-12 demonstrated olaparib’s benefit, clinicians still face uncertainty: some HRD-positive patients relapse early despite maintenance2–4, whereas a subset of HRD-negative tumors derive meaningful benefit1,3. This imperfect stratification can lead to overtreatment of HRD-positive individuals and missed opportunities in HRD-negative patients. Moreover, molecular HRD testing introduces workflow bottlenecks, limited tissue availability, high cost, and long turnaround times5 while exposing patients to potential toxicities when selection is suboptimal.

Deep learning analysis of routine whole-slide histopathology images (WSIs) offers a promising source of real-time biomarkers. WSIs capture rich morphological and microenvironmental patterns invisible to the human eye and attention-based multiple-instance learning (ABMIL)6 now enables end-to-end outcome prediction without expert annotations7,8. However, most computational pathology models ignore treatment context and key clinical variables. Here, we introduce a treatment-aware ABMIL framework that conditions tile-level representations on both HRD status and treatment arm, yielding an interpretable, patient-specific Estimated Treatment Improvement (ETI) score that quantifies predicted survival gains with olaparib versus placebo. Using diagnostic PAOLA-1 trial slides, we show this artificial intelligence (AI)-driven histology score refines stratification beyond conventional HRD subgroups, paving the way for better patient selection for PARPi therapy. Here, the primary objective is to develop and characterize DiaThera, a histology-based AI model that yields a clinically useful predictive biomarker of individual benefit from PARP inhibition (the ETI score), rather than to propose a general-purpose treatment-effect estimation method.

Results

Discriminative performances of DiaThera

As a measure of overall risk discrimination rather than treatment-effect prediction, the concordance index (c-index) is reported here to characterize the baseline performance of the model. Predictive evidence is provided in subsequent sections through treatment-biomarker interaction analyses, STEPP, and risk-difference contrasts. The model achieved a concordance index (c-index) of 0.64 (95% CI: 0.59–0.67) when evaluated on the pooled test predictions across the study cohort derived from the PAOLA-1 cohort (n = 421). This performance was consistent across folds. Models trained without conditioning on treatment or HRD status yielded lower performance (c-index 0.58, 95% CI: 0.56–0.61), underscoring the relevance of integrating clinical context into the histology-based prediction model. To evaluate whether the model captures biological signals aligned with current biomarkers, we analyzed the association between the ETI and HRD status. Interestingly, stratification based on ETI revealed a substantial, but not complete, overlap with HRD status.

Among HRD-positive patients (n = 243), 195 (80.3%) were classified as responders and 48 (19.7%) as non-responders (Table 1). Conversely, among HRD-negative patients (n = 178), 6 (3.4%) were classified as responders. Focusing on HRD-positive patients treated with olaparib who were predicted as non-responders (n = 28), 67.9% (n = 19) were BRCA-mutated (BRCAm) and 32.1% (n = 9) were BRCA wild-type; 96.4% (n = 27) had FIGO stage IIIC-IV disease, with 53.6% (n = 15) stage IV (Table 1). Most had a partial response to first-line chemotherapy (PR: 75.0%) and lacked complete macroscopic resection (71.4%), while complete responses were less frequent (CR: 21.4%) (Table 1). In a Cox PH multivariate model excluding ETI, both BRCAm (HR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.41–0.81, p < 0.005) and HRD-positivity (HR = 0.49, 95% CI: 0.36–0.66, p < 0.005) were independently associated with prolonged PFS-1 (Table 2). After including the continuous ETI score, BRCA (HR = 0.59, 95% CI: 0.42–0.83, p < 0.005) and HRD status (HR = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.47–0.98, p = 0.04) remained significant, with a reduced effect size for HRD-positive. This attenuation is consistent with the partial overlap between ETI and HRD: ETI captures predictive variation that correlates with HRD, yet a subset of HRD-positive patients are classified as ETI non-responders. Moreover, age was not significantly associated with survival in either model. Post-chemotherapy response, particularly partial response (PR), showed a trend toward worse prognosis compared to complete response (CR), but did not reach statistical significance after adjustment (HR = 1.38, p = 0.08). FIGO stage IV was associated with a higher risk of progression compared to stage IIIB, with statistical significance retained in the model including ETI (HR = 1.66, 95% CI: 1.0–2.74, p = 0.05). Upfront debulking surgery remained strongly protective in both models (HR = 0.46 without ETI, HR = 0.47 with ETI; p < 0.005), while interval debulking showed no significant association. Finally, macroscopic resection was consistently associated with better outcomes (HR = 0.42 without ETI, HR = 0.41 with ETI; both p < 0.005). Comparison with alternative approaches

To characterize the predictive value of DiaThera beyond classical survival discrimination, we quantified treatment effect heterogeneity using a contrast in progression-free survival between olaparib and control arms within biomarker-defined subgroups. At 36 months, DiaThera achieved a treatment effect contrast of 36.0% (95% CI: 17.7–54.2, p < 0.001), comparable to HRD status (35.8%, 95% CI: 18.9–54.2, p < 0.001). At 60 months, confidence intervals for DiaThera (43.0%, 95% CI: 26.6–58.7) and HRD status (35.9%, 95% CI: 20.1–51.3) overlapped substantially, indicating comparable predictive performance at this time horizon despite a 7.1 percentage-point numerical difference. In contrast, a WSI-derived prognostic score trained without treatment conditioning failed to stratify treatment benefit, yielding non-significant and near-zero predictive contrasts at both 36 months (−8.6%, 95% CI: −25.3–10.0, p = 0.36) and 60 months (−6.4%, 95% CI: −23.4–12.2, p = 0.49). We next compared DiaThera with a range of established treatment-effect estimation approaches based on clinical variables, as well as variants incorporating WSI-derived prognostic features. While several clinical meta-learners demonstrated moderate predictive contrasts, treatment-effect contrasts in this dataset were generally smaller than those observed for DiaThera across both time horizons (Supplementary Table 1). Augmenting these methods with WSI-derived prognostic scores did not materially change this pattern. The strongest WSI-augmented baseline (X-learner with WSI-based prognostic features) achieved predictive contrasts of 15.0% at 36 months and 18.0% at 60 months, compared with 36.0% and 43.0% for DiaThera, corresponding to absolute differences of 21.0 and 25.0 percentage points, respectively.

Consistent with this interpretation, purely prognostic survival models exhibited consistently negative predictive contrasts. Prognostic Cox and RSF models, trained without treatment interaction, preferentially selected patients with better baseline prognosis rather than greater relative treatment benefit, resulting in negative contrasts at both time horizons (Supplementary Table 1). This confirms that prognostic risk stratification does not translate into treatment effect prediction and reinforces the conceptual distinction between prognostic and predictive modeling.

Interaction analyses further supported the predictive nature of the ETI score. The Gail-Simon test demonstrated significant qualitative interaction between treatment and ETI strata at both 36 months (p < 0.001) and 60 months (p < 0.001), indicating heterogeneity of treatment effects across ETI subgroups. Similarly, STEPP analysis revealed a monotonic relationship between ETI score and treatment benefit, with estimated effects ranging from approximately −5% in the lowest ETI stratum to greater than 40% in the highest (Fig. 1c). Predictive value of DiaThera ETI

The Cox PH model revealed a strong treatment-biomarker interaction for ETI-based stratification (HR = 0.36; 95% CI: 0.22–0.59; p < 0.005). HRD status showed a comparable interaction (HR = 0.41; 95% CI: 0.25–0.64; p < 0.005), with overlapping confidence intervals indicating similar predictive magnitude. In both models, the main effect of treatment alone was not statistically significant (DiaThera model: HR = 0.95; 95% CI: 0.70–1.28; p = 0.72; HRD model: HR = 0.99; 95% CI: 0.71–1.39; p = 0.96), highlighting that the benefit of olaparib is largely confined to the biomarker-positive subgroups. Both biomarkers showed a prognostic main effect (biomarker-positive patients tended to have longer PFS irrespective of treatment), while both biomarkers showed strong treatment-by-biomarker interactions of comparable magnitude, the ETI score additionally enables stratification within HRD-defined subgroups, identifying potential non-responders among HRD-positive patients and potential responders among HRD-negative patients. These results suggest that both biomarkers are predictive of benefit from olaparib, with ETI offering additional stratification within HRD-defined subgroups by integrating morphological information from routine diagnostic slides

We evaluated whether the DiaThera ETI-based classification predicts survival outcomes among patients treated with olaparib (Fig. 2). In the ETI responder group (n = 133), the median PFS‑1 was 75.2 months (95% CI: 60.7-NR), substantially longer than in ETI non-responders (n = 142), who exhibited a median PFS‑1 of 17.3 months (95% CI: 15.9–20.3). HRD-positive patients treated with olaparib had a median PFS‑2 of 75.2 months (95% CI: 61.3-NR), compared to 25.1 months (95% CI: 21.2–30.2) in HRD-negative patients. Patients on placebo had a median PFS‑2 of 33.2 months (95% CI: 29.8–37.9). The analysis of the OS reinforced the findings. Interpretability

The model learned to assign a weight to each tile, discriminating within the WSI the most informative tiles under each scenario, viewable in a heatmap, with the most informative ones highlighted in yellow to red and the less informative appearing in blue/green tones. The DiaThera model provides novel insights into olaparib response, identifying subtle histological features overlooked by conventional methods and offering a path beyond current genomic classifiers (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Figs. 3–6). The common histological trait among both positive and negative HRD patients who responded to olaparib appears to be the presence of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), either focal or multifocal, distributed within the stroma or in direct contact with tumor cells. Robustness to staining variability and color normalization

To assess the robustness of the proposed model to staining and acquisition variability, we evaluated the impact of WSIs color normalization on model predictions. Reinhard normalization9 was applied to each tile prior to encoding, using external references originating from independent pathology centers and scanner platforms (see Supplementary Fig. 2). DiaThera ETI scores were compared before and after normalization on a subset (n = 100) of patients for whom both normalized and non-normalized WSIs were available. Across all evaluated cases, ETI values remained highly consistent (median |ΔETI | = 0.15, IQR: 0.08–0.24), with preserved sign and patient ranking. While minor quantitative variations in ETI were observed after normalization, no patient crossed clinically meaningful decision boundaries and no reversals in treatment-benefit stratification were detected.

Discussion

In this study, we introduce a treatment-aware deep learning multimodal model that predicts individual benefit from PARPi based on WSI analyses of pre-treatment tumor tissue, as well as HRD status. By conditioning on both treatment and biomarker status, the DiaThera model enables counterfactual inference and provides an individualized ETI score that stratifies patients beyond HRD status. Applied to the PAOLA-1 trial cohort, this newly promising approach in this field successfully identified a subset of patients likely to benefit from olaparib. Indeed, ETI-based stratification of patients revealed biologically plausible subgroups, including HRD-positive patients predicted as non-responders, who presented with advanced stage, suboptimal post-chemotherapy response, and incomplete macroscopic resection; BRCA status was heterogeneous. Our results demonstrate that ETI score is independently associated with survival outcomes. The c-index of 0.64 for PFS-1 reflects the model’s discriminative capacity for progression risk; predictive value is established through the treatment-biomarker interaction analyses, STEPP plots, and risk-difference contrasts described above. The inclusion of ETI in multivariate survival models attenuated the predictive effect of HRD and BRCA statuses, suggesting that histology-derived features provide complementary information to genomic assays, with the added value of integrating morphological information from routine diagnostic slides to refine stratification within HRD subgroups.

Importantly, the Cox PH model demonstrated a strong predictive value of DiaThera ETI-based stratification for identifying treatment responders (HR = 0.36; 95% CI: 0.22–0.59; p < 0.005), comparable to that of HRD status (HR = 0.41; 95% CI: 0.25–0.64; p < 0.005). These overlapping effect sizes indicate that the histology-based biomarker captures a predictive signal of equivalent magnitude to genomic HRD, without claiming superiority. Rather, ETI offers three complementary advantages over HRD-based stratification alone: (i) it is derived from routinely available diagnostic slides without the need for dedicated molecular assays; (ii) it provides interpretable morphological correlates of predicted benefit, including the observed association between multifocal tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte patterns and olaparib response; and (iii) it enables refined stratification within HRD-defined subgroups, identifying potential non-responders among HRD-positive patients and potential responders among HRD-negative patients who would otherwise be excluded from treatment under current molecular criteria. Also, while conditioning was performed on HRD status and treatment, this dependence could ultimately be overcome by predicting HRD status directly from histology slides, thus removing the need for external molecular testing10. Beyond HRD, incorporating additional covariates may further refine prediction, with particular potential for integrating primary chemotherapy response as a key determinant of subsequent PARP inhibitor benefit7.

Most deep learning models in pathology have focused on prognostic modeling, predicting disease evolution irrespective of treatment. In contrast, our model estimates individualized treatment effects by simulating both observed and counterfactual outcomes. This predictive framing aligns more directly with clinical decision-making and has the potential to guide therapeutic allocation. Importantly, our architecture explicitly models treatment context, a feature rarely incorporated in deep learning for cancer outcomes11,12. Our approach builds upon individualized treatment effect modeling in machine learning13, extending it to histology and survival modeling. This shift from prognostic to predictive modeling is a critical advance for clinical utility and the clinical implications of ETI-based stratification are substantial. For HRD-positive patients unlikely to benefit from PARPi, the model could help reduce exposure to toxicity and unnecessary cost. Conversely, for HRD-negative patients with a prediction of significant benefit, ETI could support more confident personalized treatment decisions. As oncology increasingly seeks to balance benefit and burden, integrating AI-derived predictors could enhance therapeutic precision at an individual level. These findings align with a growing body of evidence suggesting that genomic scars, while informative, may not fully reflect functional HRD at the time of treatment initiation2,3. Functional assays assessing real-time DNA repair activity, such as RAD51 foci formation, have been proposed as complementary tools to HRD status, but remain challenging to implement broadly in clinical practice14–16. In this context, histopathology-based models offer a practical and scalable alternative, leveraging routinely collected diagnostic slides without the need for additional tissue or complex laboratory assays15,16. Indeed, histopathological images integrate tumor-intrinsic architecture and stromal context, providing a unique window into the biological landscape at diagnosis. Our results suggest that deep learning models can extract latent morphological signatures of treatment benefit. This supports prior work in breast and lung cancer showing that features such as tumor-stroma ratio or nuclear morphology may inform therapeutic outcomes17,18.

Although our model is trained in a weakly supervised manner, attention maps highlight for prediction, tumor bed and especially presence of dispersed small foci of TILs and small necrosis and vascular congestion, suggesting early tumor stress and inflamed tumor microenvironment in accordance with recent findings in explainable pathology AI. Despite their clear biological relevance, these immune cell infiltrates form subtle “hot spots” occupying only 10 to 15% of the overall tumor surface, making them nearly undetectable to the pathologist under routine examination. This morphological observation aligns with the known biological effects of PARP inhibitors, which are recognized for enhancing antitumor immune responses through mechanisms such as neoantigen-specific T-cell receptor (TCR) clonal expansion and the induction of immunogenic cell death19. In triple-negative breast cancer, higher levels of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) have been associated with improved response to olaparib20, suggesting that increased intratumoral lymphocyte density, regardless of HRD status, may enhance sensitivity to PARPi therapy. Furthermore, recent evidence indicates that combining PARPi with immune checkpoint inhibitors may amplify this immune activation and improve clinical outcomes in ovarian cancer21. These findings support the exploration of immune-related histological signatures, such as multifocal TILs, both as predictive markers of response and as potential targets for combination therapies.

A major strength of this work is that the model was trained on a rigorously curated clinical trial population, enabling the implementation of counterfactual learning in a controlled setting. Beyond cohort composition, robustness to technical variability is a key prerequisite for clinical deployment of histology-based AI models. The observed stability of ETI scores under WSI color normalization suggests that the predictive signal learned by the model is resilient to staining and acquisition variability. Importantly, normalization was derived from external datasets originating from different centers and scanner platforms, indicating that the model does not rely on site-specific color signatures. This robustness is likely attributable, at least in part, to the properties of the image encoder, which was pretrained to learn representations invariant to non-biological sources of variability. Together, these findings suggest the potential generalizability of the approach across heterogeneous histopathology acquisition settings.

However, a limitation of this study is the absence of external validation on an independent cohort. Although our nested cross-validation framework (CV-CT) with strict fold separation was designed to minimize optimism, it cannot fully account for all sources of variability encountered in prospective real-world settings. In addition, calibration-for-benefit analyses, which assess the agreement between predicted and observed individualized treatment effects, were not performed. Such approaches are increasingly recommended for evaluating treatment-effect models22–24, but can be challenging in single-cohort settings and may benefit from larger sample sizes, independent validation datasets or dedicated resampling strategies. External validation of DiaThera and the ETI score in independent randomized trial cohorts and real-world datasets is an essential prerequisite before any clinical implementation can be considered.

Methods

Cohort description

This study included 467 patients from the PAOLA-1 phase III randomized controlled trial, a multicenter study evaluating the efficacy of maintenance therapy with olaparib or placebo combined with bevacizumab in newly diagnosed, advanced high-grade ovarian carcinoma2,25. All patients selected for this analysis had digitized histopathology slides available, obtained either at initial surgery or biopsy, prior to any systemic therapy. As such, all were treatment-naïve. HRD status was assessed using the Myriad MyChoice® CDx assay, with a genomic instability score (GIS) ≥ 42 defining HRD-positivity according to the trial protocol. Patients with inconclusive or failed HRD testing (n = 46) were excluded from the analysis. Among the remaining patients, 57.7% were classified as HRD-positive and 42.3% as HRD-negative (Table 3). The median age at diagnosis was 60 years (range: 26–78). All patients presented with advanced-stage disease: 75% were FIGO stage III and 25% were stage IV. BRCA mutational status was negative in 66% of patients, while 34% had pathogenic BRCA1/2 mutations. Of the included patients, 65.3% received olaparib and 34.7% received placebo, both in combination with bevacizumab. Follow-up was available for three survival endpoints. For PFS-1 (time to first progression), 288 events (68.4%) were recorded with a median follow-up of 21.02 months (range: 0.03–76.44). For PFS-2 (time to second progression), 248 events (58.9%) occurred, with a median follow-up of 35.34 months (range: 0.03–76.44). Preprocessing of whole-slide images

All WSIs were digitized using a Hamamatsu NanoZoomer S360 scanner (Hamamatsu Photonics, Japan) at 40x optical magnification, corresponding to a native resolution of 0.23 µm per pixel. First, tissue regions were identified using a U-Net26 segmentation model trained to distinguish tissue from background (see Fig. 4a) using WSIs downsampled to 1024 × 1024 pixels. The resulting binary mask was upscaled and used to guide tile extraction and thus excludes background regions and pen marks without requiring explicit artifact detection filters. No additional blur or focus quality filtering was applied. Preliminary experiments indicated that the ABMIL framework was robust to moderate variability in tile quality and overly aggressive filtering risked discarding biologically relevant but morphologically heterogeneous regions. Using the tissue segmentation mask, WSIs were tessellated into non-overlapping tiles of 512 × 512 pixels at ×20 magnification (0.46 µm/pixel). Tiles with less than 10% tissue coverage, as determined by the segmentation mask, were discarded. Each tile was encoded into a 384-dimensional feature vector using a Vision Transformer (ViT-S/14) pretrained with the DINOv227 self-supervised learning framework. The encoder was trained on a curated dataset of approximately 5 million tiles extracted from over 50,000 WSIs spanning multiple organs, scanner manufacturers, tissue preparation protocols and staining conditions. Encoder weights were frozen throughout all experiments; no fine-tuning was performed (see Supplementary Note 1 for more details).Fig. 4Model pipeline and training strategy.a WSIs are preprocessed to isolate tissue areas and are tessellated into fixed-size tiles (512 × 512 pixels). Model architecture

A treatment-aware ABMIL model6, designed by DiaDeep is used to predict treatment-specific survival outcomes from WSIs, see Fig. 4a. The model operates on sets of tiles extracted from diagnostic slides, where each tile is represented by a 384-dimensional vector. These embeddings are projected into a common latent space of 512 dimensions using a shared linear transformation followed by a ReLU activation. This ensures that all tiles from a patient are encoded in a comparable feature space, regardless of morphological heterogeneity. In parallel, we introduce clinical information into the model in the form of two binary variables: the treatment arm (olaparib or placebo) and HRD status (positive or negative), resulting in a multimodal model with three inputs (WSI, HRD status and treatment arm). Rather than treating the four possible combinations of these variables as discrete categories, we represent them independently. Each variable is embedded through a learnable embedding layer and the two resulting vectors are combined and linearly projected to produce a patient-specific context vector. This context is then added to each tile embedding before attention computation, effectively conditioning each tile on the patient’s therapeutic and genomic background. This additive conditioning mechanism was designed to avoid overfitting. Indeed, when modeling treatment response in ovarian cancer, HRD status alone explains a substantial fraction of the variability in outcomes1–3. In early experiments, we observed that when the model was allowed to learn a joint embedding of HRD and treatment, it often collapsed onto HRD as a dominant signal. This led to models that primarily replicated known stratification based on HRD status rather than discovering histological features associated with response. By disentangling the clinical inputs and conditioning tiles through addition rather than concatenation or joint embedding, the model is forced to learn how morphology interacts with treatment rather than bypassing the visual data entirely. Attention weights are then computed for each tile using a gated mechanism and the slide-level representation is obtained as a weighted sum of the contextualized tile embeddings. The final prediction is a single survival risk score per patient, which is optimized using a Cox proportional hazards (PH) loss28, allowing the model to learn from right-censored survival data. This architecture encourages the discovery of new morphological patterns that may correlate with treatment sensitivity beyond standard genomic biomarkers. In particular, it aims at the identification of HRD-negative patients who nevertheless benefit from olaparib, which is an important clinical subgroup that current molecular stratification fails to capture29. During training, up to 5000 tiles were randomly sampled per slide to form fixed-size bags, enabling efficient batch processing. When fewer than 5000 eligible tiles were available, sampling with replacement was used to maintain constant bag dimensions. The number of eligible tiles per WSI varied according to tissue area. Across the 421 WSIs, the median number of eligible tiles was 4841 (interquartile range (IQR): [3230–6413], see also Supplementary Fig. 1). During inference, all eligible tiles were retained to preserve complete slide information.

Model training

DiaThera is trained in a supervised fashion: WSI-derived tile embeddings, conditioned on treatment arm and HRD status, are used as inputs, while time-to-event outcomes (PFS-1 with censoring indicators) serve as the training target through a Cox partial likelihood loss. Survival outcomes are not used as model inputs. To robustly evaluate model performance and generalizability on unseen data, we implemented a cross-validation and cross-testing (CV-CT) protocol8 (see Fig. 4b). First, the whole dataset was partitioned into five non-overlapping stratified folds, preserving the distribution of treatment arms, HRD status, and clinical outcomes. For each of these five folds serving sequentially as an independent test set, the remaining four folds underwent an additional 4-fold stratified cross-validation to train and validate four independent models, whose predictions were averaged into a single ensemble. This ensemble was then evaluated on the held-out fifth fold, ensuring strict data leakage prevention. This process was repeated until each fold served exactly once as an independent test set, resulting in comprehensive out-of-sample predictions across the entire cohort. This CV-CT procedure ensured rigorous separation between training, validation, and test sets, thereby accurately reflecting model generalizability.

Response to treatment

To estimate the individual benefit from olaparib, we computed an Estimated Treatment Improvement (ETI) score for each patient. Although only one treatment arm is observed per patient in the clinical trial, our conditional model enables counterfactual inference by simulating both possible treatment outcomes from the same baseline data30,31. For each patient, the model was applied twice, once assuming placebo and once assuming olaparib, using the same diagnostic slide and HRD status as input. Since all WSIs were obtained from treatment-naive primary tumors, predictions reflect intrinsic properties of the untreated tumor tissue. The ETI was defined as the difference between the predicted survival risk under placebo and under olaparib, effectively quantifying the counterfactual gain from treatment. Patients were then stratified in two groups (responders and non-responders), above or below the median ETI value, computed using cross-validation predictions. This counterfactual framework enables individualized estimation of treatment effect using data from randomized arms and provides a complementary stratification axis beyond HRD status alone.

Statistical analysis

The concordance index (c-index) was used to evaluate the discriminative performance of the model with respect to survival outcomes. All performance metrics were computed using 5-fold stratified cross-validation with group constraints to ensure that individual patients never appeared in both training and test sets (cross-validated cross-testing, CV-CT). Bootstrap resampling was used to derive 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for all point estimates. First, to evaluate whether ETI-based score provides added predictive value beyond established clinical markers, a multivariable Cox PH including ETI alongside known predictive factors: response to first-line chemotherapy according to RECISTv.1.132 (no evidence of disease [NED], partial response [PR], or complete response [CR]), HRD status, BRCA status, age (dichotomized at 65 years), FIGO stage (IIIB, IIIC and IV), the timing of cytoreductive surgery (upfront, interval debulking surgery or no debulking surgery) as well as the complete macroscopic resection after last debulking surgery. Hence, ETI was compared to standard survival baselines and treatment-effect estimation approaches using clinical variables, in order to isolate the incremental value of treatment-aware conditioning. Standard survival baselines included Cox PH models with clinical covariates and random survival forests (RSF)33. Treatment-effect estimation methods included T-learners, using Cox and RSF models per treatment arm, X-learners with propensity-weighted pseudo-outcome regression34, uplift forests and an adaptation of CFRNet35 (see Supplementary Note 3). To directly quantify the value of treatment-aware conditioning, we additionally compared DiaThera against a WSI prognostic model trained on extracted features but without conditioning. The primary metric for comparing biomarker performance was the treatment effect contrast, defined as the difference in Kaplan–Meier (KM) estimated treatment benefit between positive (responders) and negative (non-responders) subgroups. Treatment benefit was defined as the difference in PFS-1 probability between olaparib and control arms at fixed time horizons (36 and 60 months), estimated from KM curves. For continuous scores, patients were stratified using median splits. A positive contrast indicates that biomarker-positive patients derive greater treatment benefit, consistent with predictive biomarker behavior. CIs for treatment effect contrasts were estimated using non-parametric bootstrap resampling at the patient level. Two-sided p-values were derived from the bootstrap distribution under the null hypothesis of zero contrast. We should note that because treatment-effect estimation algorithms can show limited agreement even in large randomized trials36, these comparisons are exploratory and intended to assess the robustness of the ETI signal rather than to claim methodological superiority.

To establish ETI as a predictive rather than purely prognostic biomarker, three complementary analyses were performed. First, Subpopulation Treatment Effect Pattern Plots (STEPP)37 were generated using overlapping sliding windows across the continuous ETI distribution to visualize treatment effect gradients with 95% bootstrap confidence bands. Second, qualitative interaction testing was performed using the Gail-Simon method38. Third, decision curve analysis (DCA)39 was conducted as a decision-analytic summary of the potential clinical utility of ETI-guided treatment selection across a range of decision thresholds. In this context, DCA was used to compare threshold-based ETI-guided decision strategies with “treat all”, “treat none”, and HRD-guided strategies, without implying that the net benefit estimates represent directly observed treatment effects as generally the case in the literature23,40. The primary evidence for treatment-effect heterogeneity was derived from the interaction analyses, the STEPP analysis, and the treatment-effect contrasts within the randomized trial.

Finally, in accordance with the statistical framework used in the PAOLA-1 trial, the predictive value of dichotomized ETI scores was further evaluated using a Cox PH interaction model quantifying the interaction between treatment effect and ETI-based prediction on PFS-1 and compared against HRD alone. Survival analyses were conducted using the KM estimator for the three main survival outcomes: PFS-1, PFS-2, and OS. Differences between subgroups were assessed using log-rank tests. For each stratification (HRD status, treatment arm, or ETI-based prediction), we reported median survival outcomes along with 95% CIs computed from the KM survival function. The p-values and HR with 95% CIs were reported for each covariate, including the ETI-based group. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted on held-out cross-testing folds only, using predictions generated by models that were not trained on those patients, in order to preserve the exogeneity of the evaluation.
