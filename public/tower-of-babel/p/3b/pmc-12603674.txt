A Proposal for Homoskedastic Modeling With Conditional Auto‐Regressive Distributions
Authors: Miguel A Martinez‐Beneito, Aritz Adin, Tomás Goicoa, María Dolores Ugarte
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTConditional auto‐regressive (CAR) distributions are widely used to deal with spatial dependence in the geographic analysis of areal data. These distributions establish multivariate dependence networks by defining conditional relationships between neighboring units, resulting in positive dependence among nearby observations. Despite their practical convenience and well‐founded principles, the conditional nature of CAR distributions can lead to undesirable marginal properties, such as inherent heteroskedasticity assumptions that may significantly impact the posterior distributions. In this paper, we highlight the variance issues associated with CAR distributions, particularly focusing on edge effects and issues related to the region's geometry. We show that edge effects may be more pronounced and widespread in disease mapping studies than previously anticipated. To address these heteroskedasticity concerns, we introduce a new conditional autoregressive distribution designed to mitigate these problems. We demonstrate how this distribution effectively diminishes the practical issues identified in earlier models.Keywords: CAR distributions, disease mapping, edge effect, homoskedasticity

Introduction

Disease mapping has been a highly active area of research over the past three decades, with numerous methodological advancements and practical applications constantly published in the field of epidemiology. The primary objective of disease mapping is to address the small area estimation challenges inherent in public health analyses. Epidemiologists aim to analyze study units as small as possible to ensure that underlying spatial risks are not obscured by aggregation across larger areas. However, smaller units typically have fewer individuals, which can compromise the reliability of risk estimates, particularly for rare diseases.

The administrative areal units used in these studies often differ significantly in population size and composition, adding complexity to making accurate inferences about the spatial distribution of risks. If risks were treated as independent across different areal units, their variances would be inversely proportional to the number of expected cases [1]. As a result, risk estimates for sparsely populated areas tend to be more variable and often exhibit extreme values, irrespective of the actual risks. The primary goal of disease mapping is to stabilize these risk estimates in less populated areas, mitigating the mentioned issues and providing more accurate representations of the underlying risks.

Although early models in disease mapping did not account for spatial dependence between areal units [2], spatial dependence soon emerged as a crucial tool for addressing the small area estimation issues inherent in disease mapping studies. Among the various approaches, conditional auto‐regressive (CAR) distributions, specifically designed for areal data, have become the most widely used tool for inducing spatial dependence in these problems.

CAR distributions can be formulated either multivariately, defining the joint multivariate distribution P(θ) for a random vector θ (commonly referred to as Gaussian Markov Random Fields or GMRF), or conditionally, through the full conditionals P(θi|θ−i) for each unit i. In the first case, θ follows a multivariate Normal distribution with a given precision matrix τQ. In the second, the conditional distributions are a set of univariate normal distributions, where the means depend on neighboring locations. Both approaches explicitly model the spatial relationships between units and their neighbors. In the multivariate case, these relationships are encapsulated in the precision matrix, which defines the conditional dependencies between neighboring units.

Continuous Gaussian fields have also been applied to disease mapping problems [3, 4, 5]. Although Gaussian fields are not inherently suited for areal data analysis due to their continuous spatial nature, they can be adapted for this purpose. These models assume that the covariance between spatial units is a parametric function of their distance, often measured as the distance between centroids in the case of areal data. Unlike GMRFs, which model spatial dependence using precision matrices, Gaussian fields induce spatial dependence through the covariance matrix. Since covariance and precision matrices are mathematical inverses of each other and the dependence structure defined with any of these approaches may not make full sense from the other point of view. Consequently, some properties of CAR‐based random effects, designed with a conditional variance perspective, may exhibit less desirable behavior when viewed in terms of marginal variances. Similarly, Gaussian fields, optimized for marginal covariance, may not perform so well when evaluated from a conditional variance perspective.

The edge effect is a well‐documented pathological feature of CAR‐distributed random vectors [6, 7]. This effect causes the estimates (usually posterior means) of θ at the borders of the study region to exhibit greater variability than those within the interior. This extra variability introduces issues in spatial risk smoothing models, similar to those observed in low‐population areas, potentially leading border regions to display extreme risk estimates regardless of their actual risk. This is clearly an undesirable effect of spatial smoothing models that would be convenient to minimize for practical applications, the values of some estimates depend on their position on the lattice, beyond their data or those observed in neighboring regions. Several approaches have been developed to address the edge effect, including weighting schemes for boundary areas to account for the degree of missing information at each location [8] and the construction of external buffer zones with either missing observed data [9] or real observed data when available [10, 11]. Despite significant attention to the edge effect in the 1990s and early 2000s, methodological proposals have been scarce over the past two decades, with much less focus on this issue in recent years.

This paper aims to evaluate, from a marginal perspective, certain features of CAR‐distributed random vectors, either previously described edge effects or marginal issues of other kinds, that the conditional definition of this distribution could have induced. In this manner, we seek to unveil those side or undesired effects, from a marginal perspective, that these distributions could have been inherited from their conditional conception. Based on this evaluation, we will propose a modification of the traditional Intrinsic CAR distribution to address some identified issues, particularly those related to edge effects and some additional features linked to the geometry of the graph representing the geographic structure of the study region.

The paper is structured as follows: In Section 2, we introduce intrinsic conditional autoregressive (ICAR) random effects and assess them from a marginal perspective across various real‐world contexts, specifically mortality studies in different Spanish regions. This study will show some geometric features that CAR‐based spatial analyses show in practice. Section 3 introduces a methodological modification to the ICAR distribution designed to resolve the problematic features identified in Section 2. In Section 4, we assess the proposed modification through both simulated scenarios and the same real‐world contexts discussed earlier. Finally, Section 5 concludes with a discussion of the findings of the paper and their implications.

The ICAR Distribution and Its Marginal Variances

The ICAR Distribution

From a full‐conditional point of view, we say that a random vector θ follows an ICAR distribution if each of its components, given the rest, follows the univariate distribution

where θ‾i=(∑{j:j∼i}wijθj)/(∑{j:j∼i}wij) is a weighted mean of the values of θ in the neighboring areas of unit i. In this expression, the values wij are intended to weight the influence of the neighbors j of each areal unit i, which we denote as j∼i. In the previous expression, and from now on, the variability in normal distributions will be parameterized as a function of precisions, or precision matrices in the multivariate case. In general, a set of full conditionals P(θi|θ−i) such as those in Equation (1) does not, by itself, guarantee a valid joint distribution for θ. To obtain a proper joint distribution with a symmetric precision matrix, the precisions in Equation (1) must take the specific form shown; this is therefore not an arbitrary choice but the result of a principled and reasoned mathematical construction. As a consequence, areal units i with more neighbors have larger conditional precisions, which is sensible: The conditional mean is based on more information, so θi is expected to lie closer to that mean.

The implications of this assumption for the marginal variances are, however, less well known. Prior work [12, 13] reports higher marginal variances at locations with fewer neighbors—an unsurprising consequence of their lower conditional precision—but also notes the puzzling fact that units with the same number of neighbors (and equal weights) can still exhibit different marginal variances. Thus, the mapping from the conditionals in Equation (1) to the marginal variance structure of the ICAR remains imperfectly understood. Although heterogeneous conditional precisions are required for the ICAR to be well defined, the resulting heteroskedastic marginal variances may be undesirable, as the data‐generating mechanism need not produce such differences. For this reason, the inherent heteroskedasticity of ICAR has been described as “undesirable” [14], and alternative weighting schemes have been proposed to mitigate it [12].

For the rest of this section, we will follow the typical assumption of adjacency for the spatial structure so that wij=1 if i∼j and 0 otherwise. We will refer to Q, the precision matrix without the precision parameter τ, as the structure matrix of the ICAR distribution. As defined in Equations (1) and (2), the ICAR distribution is improper and has infinite marginal variances so, to be well defined, we will assume the common restriction ∑iθi=0 which makes it possible, between other advantages, to talk properly about the (finite) marginal variances of the distribution.

Conditional properties of the ICAR distribution are encoded in its precision matrix τQ, which is well understood and, by construction, sparse (hence computationally convenient). By contrast, marginal covariance properties depend on (τQ)−, the process covariance, which typically lacks a simple analytical structure because dependence in ICAR models is imposed through Q. As noted by Rue and Held, page 23 [13], “The interpretation provided by Q is hard (or nearly impossible) to interpret marginally … by definition Q−1=∑, and ∑ii depends generally on all elements in Q”. In other words, the useful and intuitive analytical structure of the precision matrix is largely lost upon inversion, making marginal properties difficult to study mathematically. Closed‐form analyses of the covariance matrix exist for the proper CAR distribution, of which ICAR is a limiting case [15, 16], but comparable results for the improper ICAR distribution are, to our knowledge, not available, leaving its marginal covariance structure less well understood.

An Empirical Assessment of the Marginal Variances of the ICAR Distribution

Marginal variances in the ICAR distribution have already been pointed out [12, 13], but always attributed to the distinct number of neighbors of each spatial unit. Moreover, this effect has been examined only at the level of the prior dependence structure of the ICARs, not its potential impact on posterior estimates in applied analyses. We now study some properties of the marginal variances of the ICAR distribution in several real contexts, and beyond its known relationship with the number of neighbors of each areal unit.

A more detailed discussion on this issue is given in the following section. Without loss of generality, the precision parameter τ has been fixed to 1 for this whole study.

The irregular shapes of the regions in Figure 1 could have made us guess that the marginal municipal variances in that figure, which do not depend on any data at all, had an unpredictable character. However, those maps show clear geographical patterns whose recurrence points to a general effect of the ICAR distribution. First, we notice a clear common edge effect for all of them, which makes the municipalities placed at their borders show, in general, higher variances than the municipalities in their inner side. This should make the risk estimates of those units to be less shrunk, and therefore less smoothed (more variable), than the rest. This edge effect may be partly explained by the smaller number of neighbors for areal units located on the study‐region boundary. However, many near‐boundary units that are not on the boundary also exhibit this effect, suggesting that factors beyond the number of neighbors may be at play. This seems the most remarkable feature in these maps; nevertheless, Figure 1 shows some additional common characteristics. For example, variances at the center of each region show much less variability, and their estimates will therefore be more smoothed than at any other location of the regions of study. However, some municipalities with high variance can be noticed in these regions. Those municipalities typically correspond to units with a low number of neighbors, usually no more than one or two. This may be understood as a consequence of the conditional character of ICAR distributions, which makes the conditional variance of municipalities with few neighbors higher, and that high conditional variance could also make the corresponding marginal variances also high. While the effect of the number of neighbors has already been pointed out in the literature, it does not appear to be the most striking effect shown in Figure 1.

An interesting feature of the geographical distribution of the marginal variances is that it may also depend on the geometric shape of each region of study. For example, for the Valencian Region and Aragon, with long‐shaped geographic structures, the municipalities with higher variability are at the north and south of those regions, which correspond to regions with higher eccentricity values (greater distances to the farthest area) in the corresponding graph. Therefore, in those cases, the long‐shaped structure of the region of study may affect the geographical distribution of the marginal variance as clearly as the previously mentioned edge effect. On the other hand, both Castile and Leon and Castile‐La Mancha are more round‐shaped than the Valencian Region and Aragon, so we cannot talk about any long axis traversing these regions. However, we can notice another effect for both, coming from the geographic shape of those regions. Thus, for Castile and Leon, we notice several regions of high variance close to the border but no so on the border. The most evident of these, although not the only one, is at the eastern protrusion of the region, where its isolated character leads to increased variance, beyond the edge effect that might appear in municipalities located at the borders. Similar effects can be noticed for Castile‐La Mancha, particularly evident in the western part of this region. Therefore, in summary, beyond the previously known and documented influences of the number of neighbors and edge effects, the conditional character of ICAR distributions gives rise to additional patterns in the marginal variances of the process. These are linked to the specific geometry of each study region and may ultimately shape the final risk estimates.

The illustrated effects are intrinsic to the ICAR distribution, frequently used in hierarchical Bayesian models to allow for geographical variability and dependence of some quantity of interest. Thus, an interesting question arises here: Are the mentioned side effects of the ICAR distribution noticeable in practice within the estimates of hierarchical models that incorporate it? In other words, beyond the different prior marginal variances for each municipality, do those different variances have any impact on the posterior risk estimates of the corresponding hierarchical models? As previously mentioned, having lower or higher marginal variance could make the corresponding estimates more or less shrunk, respectively, which should have an impact on those estimates. Thus, the question now is whether that impact is noticeable, in practical terms, in real settings.

To investigate this, we considered the 102 causes of death from the Spanish National Statistical Institute across the four regions depicted in Figure 1 in the period 1989–2014. For these causes, we have selected those with at least 16.000 deaths during that period, either for men or women, separately. Since Spain is composed of about 8.000 municipalities, this means that our selection contains the causes of death with at least 2 expected deaths per municipality, on average, during the period of study. This yields a collection of 100 causes of death, 50 for men and 50 for women. For each of these causes and regions, we have run the corresponding Besag, York and Mollié (BYM) model [17]. More details on the BYM model can be found in the following section. All the models in this paper have been run in INLA. For each municipality, and for the set of 100 analyses carried out, we have calculated the variance of the linear predictor estimates (posterior means) over the 100 considered data sets. Doing this, we estimate the variability of the posterior mean estimates for each municipality for this large collection of causes of death, which is intended to randomize the geographical spatial patterns that could be found over these regions (see Figure 2). In this manner, we try to explore the effect that the different marginal variances could also have on the corresponding estimates, in particular, if they are more or less variable in some particular areas of the study region.

The maps in Figure 2 resemble those in Figure 1 in different aspects. In particular, the correlation between the prior variances (Figure 1) and the variances of the estimates (Figure 2) is 0.38 for the Valencian Region, 0.52 for Castile and Leon, 0.19 for Aragon, and 0.34 for Castile‐La Mancha. In addition, the patterns shown in the prior marginal variances can also be noticed in the variability of the estimates. Thus, it seems that the heteroskedastic features that we saw in the prior dependence structure of ICAR distributions are also having an effect in practice on the corresponding posterior estimates, which are more variable, across causes of death, wherever the prior marginal variance was higher. The variances of the posterior estimates (Figure 2) may depend on other aspects besides the geometry of the region of study, for example, the distribution of more or less populated municipalities over the regions of study. The most populated municipalities show, in general, higher variances, which are probably due to an increased statistical power in these regions. In addition, the inclusion of the independent random effect in the BYM model, which does not take any effect on the plots shown in Figure 1, may also have an impact. However, despite these factors, the resemblance of the maps in both figures suggests as if heteroskedasticity may also be influencing the variability of posterior estimates.

In this section, we have referred to the issues observed in both prior variances and posterior estimates of ICAR‐based spatial analyses as side effects. These side effects are not expected to be part of any underlying data‐generating mechanism, but rather are consequences of the prior assumptions inherent to the ICAR distribution. As such, being extrinsic to the data, we aim to eliminate their influence from any posterior estimate. Regarding border effects, one might argue that the observed excess variance is natural, as geographical units near the borders typically have less information available, leading to greater uncertainty. This is very reasonable, but efforts to mitigate such exogenous effects should be tried and are entirely legitimate, as they seek to correct phenomena that are ultimately external to the data. These efforts are comparable to, and as justified as, the use of natural splines in spline regression modeling to improve estimates near the boundaries of an explanatory variable's range, an approach widely supported by extensive literature. However, border effects are not the only anomalies unveiled. Geometric effects have also emerged, which are not necessarily attributable to locally scarce information. Addressing these issues, which stem from the prior conditional structure, will be the main objective, from now on, of this paper.

A Proposal of CAR Distribution Favoring Homoskedasticity

As described, regular ICAR‐distributed random vectors show, by construction, important undesirable features in terms of (prior) marginal variances and variability of posterior estimates inherited from their conditional formulation. We are going to propose now a modification of the ICAR distribution intended to fix those effects.

Let us assume that θ follows an ICAR distribution with variance‐covariance matrix ∑=(τQ)−, given by the Moore‐Penrose pseudo‐inverse of its precision matrix (see the appendix for a detailed explanation of the need for this pseudo‐inverse for that variance‐covariance matrix). Let us also denote Λ=diag(σ12,…,σI2) the diagonal matrix whose diagonal elements coincide with those of ∑, the marginal variances of each spatial unit. In that case, let us consider now the modified ICAR distribution defined by NI(0I,τ(Λ1/2QΛ1/2)). This distribution also has a sparse precision matrix, as sparse as the original matrix Q, since they will share the same zero cells. The difference between Q∗=Λ1/2QΛ1/2 and Q will be that the non‐zero cells of Q∗ will be weighed but those of Q, generally, will be not (although if they were, Q∗ could be similarly defined as a reweighed version of Q). If Q was not singular, then (Q∗)−=(Q∗)−1=(Λ−1/2Q−1Λ−1/2)=Λ−1/2∑Λ−1/2 would have all its diagonal terms equal to 1, what would make this new modified CAR distribution homoskedastic. Regretfully, for ICAR distributions Q will be singular and, in that case, as mentioned at the end of the appendix (Q∗)−=(Λ1/2QΛ1/2)−≠Λ−1/2Q−Λ−1/2. Therefore, our proposed transformation, in contrast to the non‐singular case, would not necessarily lead to a pure homoskedastic process. However, this transformation should enhance homoskedasticity since the conditional variance is decreased(/increased) where the original ICAR showed particularly high(/low) marginal variances. This should balance the initially more heterogeneous variances of the ICAR distribution, leading to more similar values. Therefore, we will call from now on this new CAR distribution as HomCAR, as an abbreviation of homoskedastic CAR, although the homoskedastic term here should be intended in a broad, not so strict, manner.

In this expression, the conditional expected value of each observation is the mean of some rescaled version of the neighboring values. The rescaling factor σj/σi overweighs(/underweighs) the contribution of the neighboring locations with high(/low) marginal variance. This makes the high variance of the observations with that feature to be transferred to their neighboring sites, balancing the variances between those locations. Aditionally, in contrast to regular ICARs, the conditional means in Equation (3) are no longer weighted means of the neighbors' values θj, since the weights in the new linear combinations (ni−1σj/σi) do not add up to 1. In relation to this issue, if a specific region i exhibits particularly high (or low) marginal variance σi in the original ICAR model, the weights in the linear combination used to compute its mean in the HomCAR distribution will be correspondingly low (or high). This adjustment reduces (or increases) the variability of this region, thereby achieving a leveling effect. Regarding the conditional precisions in expression (3), regions with higher (or lower) marginal variance in the original model will now exhibit higher (or lower) conditional precision. This increased conditional precision causes θi to align more closely with its neighbors, further reducing its marginal variance.

A Practical Assessment of the HomCAR Distribution

In this section, we are going to assess in practice the performance of the HomCAR distribution introduced in the previous section. First, we compare the (prior) marginal variances of the HomCAR distribution with those of the ICAR for each of the 4 regions of study considered in Section 2 (Figure 1). For the HomCAR distribution, the minimum, median and maximum municipal variances are 0.81, 0.98, and 1.13 for the Valencian Region, 0.93, 1 and 1.06 for Castile and Leon, 0.92, 1, and 1.05 for Aragon and 0.87, 0.99 and 1.09 for Castile‐La Mancha. These values are very similar for all 4 regions and show some variability that illustrates how the HomCAR distribution, as previously commented, is not purely homoskedastic. However, the ranges for the marginal variances for the HomCAR distribution are far lower than for the original ICARs. For the ICARs, the (prior) maximum municipal marginal variance is always at least 10 times higher than the minimum, in contrast to the HomCAR, where that relationship is always lower than 1.4. This illustrates the improvement in terms of homoskedasticity achieved by the HomCAR distribution.

We will explore now in practice the performance of the HomCAR distribution, focusing on the corresponding empirical posterior variances of the risk estimates instead of their prior variances, since the prior variances could take an effect also on the shrinkage of those estimates. First, we will do it over a collection of simulated data sets, and finally, we will revisit the analysis of the Spanish mortality data set (Section 2), now using the HomCAR distribution.

As can be appreciated in Figure 4, variances may increase or decrease even more than 50% from one model to the other, illustrating how the HomCAR model reduces the effects evidenced by the ICAR distribution in Section 2. Specifically, the variances of the estimates increase in the center of the regions of study and decrease in their borders and in more isolated areas. In fact, the variances of the estimates found for the HomCAR model no longer correlate with the prior ICAR variances since those correlations are now equal to 0.05 for the Valencian Region, 0.12 for Castile and Leon, −0.15 for Aragon, and 0.02 for Castile‐La Mancha. Note that the HomCAR distribution also produces an increase in the variance of the estimates at the municipalities in the center of the region of study. This is something in principle unexpected that possibly deserves further research.

Concluding Remarks

Disease mapping, as a research area, has as its main goal the control of the variance of the risk estimates, mainly for the smaller areas, so that they allow to estimate the underlying geographical risk patterns in a reliable manner. However, little attention has been previously paid to the marginal variance side effects that traditional disease mapping modeling tools, such as ICAR distributions, could induce on the estimated patterns. Tiefelsdorf et al. [20] studied this issue, but for spatial moving average and simultaneous autoregressive distributions. Some other works [21, 22] have also proposed to scale the ICAR distribution with different goals; however, that scaling is homogeneous for the whole region of study, so the heteroskedasticity issues unveiled in this paper remain after that scaling. This work sheds light on heteroskedasticity in ICAR distributions, which arises from their conditional nature. Although the ICAR distribution has a principled and legitimate formulation from a conditional perspective, that definition leads to certain unintended features. As shown, these effects go beyond the previously known influence of the number of neighbors on marginal variances, generating additional edge‐ and geometry‐related patterns. Moreover, our results demonstrate that such structural irregularities in the prior persist in practice within posterior estimates from standard analyses of real mortality data. Regretfully, these effects are very hard, if even possible, to detect from isolated (univariate) studies, so their effects could be easily spread throughout a large number of studies in the literature. This work proposes a manner to fix the edge and geometric effects unveiled throughout this paper, which may be more relevant and widespread in the outcomes of disease mapping studies than previously anticipated.

According to the results in this paper, one could perhaps conclude that the use of CAR distributions for disease mapping, or for the analysis of areal data in general, should be discouraged in comparison to the use of (continuous) Gaussian fields. In our opinion, this is not necessarily true. Gaussian fields show the advantage of being homoskedastic, unless different marginal variances are explicitly considered in the corresponding model. In fact, since they induce spatial dependence directly over the covariance matrix, they should have very good properties in marginal terms. However, since they are defined under a marginal perspective, their performance will be possibly not be as good under a conditional point of view. For example, for the simulated setting considered (Subsection 4.1) in the Valencian Region, the conditional precision of one municipality given the rest may be up to 15.8 times larger than in others (20.0 for Castile and Leon, 10.3 for Aragon, or 12.4 for Castile‐La Mancha). This is a function, mainly, of the proximity of its neighbors, which depends mostly on the geographical size of the municipalities. In general, we should not want geographically smaller units to have different conditional variances, so this is an unpleasant conditional effect of Gaussian random fields that we would prefer to avoid.

Along this work, we have found that the weighting of the structure matrix of ICAR distributions could be an appropriate solution to their heteroskedasticity problems. This procedure has important connections with that in Corpas‐Burgos and Martinez‐Beneito [23] where a weighting of the structure matrix was also sought, but in that case for the Leroux et al.'s CAR distribution [24]. They also reached a similar weighting scheme of the structure matrix, where wij=cicj if i∼j, and 0 otherwise. In that case, {ci,i=1,…,I} were parameters of the model that were estimated by following a multivariate approach where several diseases were jointly studied and those parameters were estimated as a function of the common features shown by those diseases. They found that, in accordance with our results, high values for ci make wij to be high for j∼i and this makes the random effect θi to be closer to those of their neighbors. This is similar to what we find, for example, for the municipalities on the border of the regions of study, which showed high marginal variances and therefore the corresponding weights wij take high values. Interestingly, the authors find also in their work that areas with large ci values are often placed at the border of the region of study: “Note that several of these units are located in spatial units at the borders of the graph where the geometry of the graph would impose lesser spatial dependence” [23]. Therefore, both works agree for different reasons in putting higher weights in the more eccentric municipalities of the regions of study.

As future lines of research of this work, on one hand, it remains how to adapt this homoskedastic proposal for other CAR distributions, such as, for example, the Proper or Leroux et al.'s CAR distribution [1]. For these distributions, the matrices with the marginal variances Λ depend on their spatial parameters, so they are not fixed, in contrast to the ICAR case worked in this paper. Thus, the proposal of homoskedastic alternatives to these distributions entails additional challenges already to be solved. On the other hand, the current proposal only partially enhances the homoskedasticity of ICAR distributions. Developing alternative distributions that further improve homoskedasticity, or even yield fully homoskedastic processes, would represent a meaningful advancement in this area. Additionally, gaining a deeper understanding of the HomCAR distribution, such as uncovering the reasons behind the increased variance of estimates at the center of the study region, and determining whether this effect is beneficial or not, would be valuable contributions that could enhance our current understanding of homoskedasticity in ICAR models. This work tries to be a first step in that direction.
