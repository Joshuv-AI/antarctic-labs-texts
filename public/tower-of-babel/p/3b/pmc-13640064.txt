Burden of Epstein-Barr virus in gastric cancer, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, and Burkitt lymphoma in Africa: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Saleamlak Sebsibe, Sisay Desale, Sisay Kassa, Yabsira Asmare, Yonas Erkihun, Zewudu Mulatie, Afewerk Habtamu, Bruktawit Eshetu, Emiyamrew Getnet, Endashaw Belayhun, Kalu Tsegaye, Mahider Shimelis Feyisa, Marye Wondimnew Tefera, Mikiyas Shemelis Achame, Rahel Birhanu Arage, Mihret Tilahun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEpstein-Barr virus (EBV) is implicated in nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC), gastric cancer (GC), and Burkitt lymphoma (BL), but its burden across Africa remains poorly characterized due to fragmented evidence. This systematic review and meta-analysis estimated the pooled prevalence of EBV in NPC, GC, and BL across Africa, stratified by cancer type, region, and detection method.MethodsPubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and African Journals Online were searched for studies published between January 2000 and March 2025 reporting EBV prevalence in histologically confirmed NPC, GC, or BL in African populations. Study quality was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute checklist. Pooled prevalence was estimated separately for each cancer type using a random-effects model (DerSimonian-Laird), with subgroup analyses by region and detection method. Publication bias was assessed using Begg’s and Egger’s tests.ResultsTwenty studies comprising 2,763 participants from 11 African countries were included. Pooled EBV prevalence was highest in BL 86.8% (95% CI: 82.2, 91.5) followed by, NPC 66.0% (95% CI: 65.8, 66.2), and lowest in GC 35.4% (95% CI: 19.9, 50.9), with substantial heterogeneity throughout (I² >95%, p < 0.001). Prevalence of NPC was highest in East Africa 81.8% (95% CI: 81.4, 81.7) and lowest in North Africa 50.0% (95% CI: 49.9, 50.1). The subgroup analysis of NPC by detection method, EBER-ISH yielded the highest pooled prevalence 80.4% (95% CI: 67.0, 93.3), compared with DNA PCR 60.3% (95% CI: 36.8, 83.8). No significant publication bias was detected for any cancer type (all p > 0.05), and sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of the pooled estimates.ConclusionsEBV was frequently detected in Burkitt lymphoma, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, and gastric cancer among African populations included in this review. Prevalence estimates varied substantially according to cancer type, geographic region, and diagnostic method. The limited use of EBER-ISH, the current reference standard for confirming EBV involvement in tumor cells, represents an important evidence gap that should guide future research and diagnostic standardization efforts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-13980-6.

Introduction

Epstein-Barr virus, also known as human herpesvirus 4 (HHV-4), is a ubiquitous double-stranded DNA virus belonging to the family Herpesviridae, subfamily Gammaherpesvirinae [1, 2]. EBV is one of the most common human viruses, infecting more than 90% of the global population, usually during childhood or adolescence [3]. Following primary infection, EBV establishes lifelong latency in B lymphocytes, with the potential for intermittent reactivation throughout life [4]. While primary infection often presents as infectious mononucleosis in adolescents and adults, the virus is also strongly linked to multiple malignancies, including nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC), gastric carcinoma (GC), Hodgkin lymphoma, Burkitt lymphoma, and post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorders [5–7].

EBV-associated cancers result from a complex interplay of viral oncogenes, host immune responses, and environmental or genetic cofactors. Latent membrane protein 1 (LMP1), latent membrane protein 2 A (LMP2A), Epstein-Barr nuclear antigens (EBNAs), and small non-coding RNAs such as EBERs are key viral molecules known to drive malignant transformation [8, 9]. EBV contributes to carcinogenesis through multiple mechanisms, including immune evasion, activation of NF-κB signaling, inhibition of apoptosis, genomic instability, and epigenetic reprogramming of infected cells [10–12].

Globally, EBV is implicated in approximately 98% of endemic NPC cases and 8–10% of all gastric cancers, classified as EBV-associated gastric carcinoma (EBVaGC) [13, 14]. While NPC has its highest burden in East and Southeast Asia, significant cases also occur in North and East Africa [15]. Similarly, EBVaGC is documented across Africa, but epidemiological data remain limited and highly variable between regions [16].

In Africa, socio-demographic factors such as early childhood EBV infection, household crowding, co-infections, exposure to smoke, and certain dietary practices are believed to increase susceptibility to EBV-associated malignancies [17–19]. Furthermore, immune-modulating conditions such as HIV infection and malnutrition may contribute to increased EBV reactivation and oncogenesis in African populations [20, 21]. Despite this, comprehensive epidemiological mapping of EBV-associated NPC and gastric cancer in Africa is lacking.

While several African studies have evaluated EBV prevalence in tumor tissues using diagnostic markers such as EBER in situ hybridization, LMP1 immunostaining, and PCR for EBV DNA, the available evidence is fragmented, limited to small sample sizes, or restricted to specific cancer centers [21–23]. Lack of comprehensive systematic review or meta-analysis has evaluated the burden of EBV infection in nasopharyngeal carcinoma and gastric cancer across Africa. Given the significant oncogenic role of EBV and the rising cancer burden on the continent, understanding its contribution is essential for improving prevention, early detection, and clinical management. Therefore, this study aims to estimate the pooled prevalence of EBV in these malignancies across Africa and to identify key epidemiological and diagnostic gaps relevant to cancer control.

Methods

Study design and reporting

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted to estimate the prevalence of Epstein-Barr virus in nasopharyngeal carcinoma, gastric cancer, and Burkitt lymphoma across Africa. The study protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under registration number CRD420261283753. The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines [22].

Data sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed in PubMed, Google scholar, Scopus, Web of Science, and African Journals Online to identify relevant studies published between January 2000 and March 2025. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms related to “Epstein-Barr virus,” “nasopharyngeal carcinoma,” “gastric cancer,” “Burkitt lymphoma,” and “Africa,” using Boolean operators. Reference lists of all eligible studies were manually screened to identify additional relevant articles.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they reported EBV prevalence in histologically confirmed cases of NPC, GC, or BL conducted in African populations and utilized laboratory-confirmed EBV detection methods. Studies were excluded if they were reviews, editorials, case reports, duplicate datasets, or lacked sufficient data for extraction.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data were extracted using a standardized data collection form, including study characteristics (author, year, country, region), cancer type, sample size, number of EBV-positive cases, detection method, and specimen type. Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion. The methodological quality of included studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) [23] critical appraisal checklist for prevalence studies. Studies scoring between 6 and 8 out of 9 were considered to have moderate to high methodological quality.

Statistical analysis

The pooled prevalence of EBV infection was estimated using a random-effects model (DerSimonian-Laird) to account for expected heterogeneity among studies. Results were expressed as proportions with 95% confidence intervals. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and quantified using the I² statistic [24]. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on cancer type, geographic region, and laboratory detection method to explore sources of heterogeneity. Publication bias was assessed using Begg’s and Egger’s tests, and a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate the robustness of the pooled estimates. All statistical analyses were performed using STATA17 software.

Ethical considerations

As this involved secondary analysis of published data, ethical approval was not required.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

A total of 1,247 records were identified through database searching. After removal of duplicates, 835 studies were screened based on titles and abstracts, of which 798 were excluded. Subsequently, 37 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, and 17 were excluded based on predefined criteria. Finally, 20 studies were included in the meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

The included studies comprised 2,763 participants from 11 African countries, representing East, West, North, and Southern Africa. Of these, 11 studies focused on (NPC), five on (GC), and four on (BL). EBV detection methods included DNA polymerase chain reaction (PCR, n = 13), EBV-encoded RNA in situ hybridization (EBER-ISH, n = 4), immunoassays (n = 2), and genome sequencing (n = 1). Based on the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist, all studies were of moderate to high methodological quality (Table 1).

Pooled prevalence of EBV

By Cancer Type: A statistically significant difference in EBV prevalence was observed across cancer types (I²-test for subgroup differences, p < 0.001). The pooled prevalence was highest in Burkitt lymphoma 86.8% (95% CI: 82.2, 91.5; 4 studies), followed by nasopharyngeal carcinoma 66.0% (95% CI: 65.8, 66.2; 11 studies), and lowest in gastric cancer 35.4% (95% CI: 19.9, 50.9; 5 studies) (Fig. 2).

Country-level distribution of evidence

Country-level analysis revealed substantial geographic heterogeneity. East Africa contributed the largest proportion of evidence 60.7% of participants, n = 1,677, driven by large BL studies from Uganda/Kenya/Tanzania (n = 730) and Ethiopia (n = 512). In contrast, West Africa was represented only by Ghana (n = 235, 8.5%), and Central Africa was entirely unrepresented. Notably, only South Africa and Tunisia contributed EBER-ISH-based evidence (gold standard), while all other countries relied on PCR or immunoassays, reflecting country-level differences in diagnostic capacity [50, 51]. Country-specific pooled prevalence estimates were as follows: NPC ranged from 50.8% (Egypt) to 100.0% (Kenya), BL ranged from 80.3% (Ethiopia) to 95.6% (Uganda/Kenya/Tanzania); and GC ranged from 14.8% (Tunisia, EBER-ISH) to 67.3% (Ghana, PCR) (Table 2).

Subgroup analysis of EBV among nasopharyngeal carcinoma

Pre-specified subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity across the included studies. Stratification was performed by geographic region, and laboratory detection methods.

By geographic region

Subgroup analysis by geographic region also demonstrated significant variation (p < 0.001), with the highest combined prevalence observed in East Africa 81.5% (95% CI: 81.4, 81.7), followed by Southern Africa 70.6% (95% CI: 24.7, 100), and West Africa 53.0% (95% CI: 23.7, 82.3) while North Africa showed the lowest prevalence 50.0% (95% CI: 49.9, 50.1) (Fig. 3).

By detection method

EBV prevalence varied significantly according to laboratory detection method (p 0.01). Studies using DNA PCR reported a pooled prevalence of 60.3% (95% CI: 36.8, 83.8; 8 studies), and EBER in situ hybridization 80.4% (95% CI: 67.0, 93.8; 3 studies) (Fig. 4).

Quality assessment and risk of bias

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the JBI critical appraisal checklist for prevalence studies. Two reviewers independently evaluated each of the 20 studies, and disagreements were resolved by consensus. The quality scores ranged from 6 to 8 out of 9, indicating moderate to high methodological quality (Fig. 5). Overall, the risk of bias was considered moderate. The main concerns were related to selection bias due to hospital-based sampling and limited representativeness of study populations. In addition, variability in EBV detection methods, particularly the predominance of DNA PCR (13/20 studies) and limited use of EBER-ISH (4/20 studies) and genome sequencing (1/20 studies), may have introduced measurement bias. Despite these limitations, the included studies were deemed sufficiently robust for meta-analysis.

Publication bias

Publication bias was assessed independently using Begg’s test, and Egger test which showed no statistically significant evidence of bias for NPC, (p = 0.2758), GC (P = 04830), and BL (P = 0.0894).

Visual inspection of the funnel plot suggested slight asymmetry, which may indicate potential small-study effects (Fig. 6). Neither Egger’s test nor Begg’s test demonstrated statistically significant evidence of publication bias for NPC, GC, or BL. Therefore, the observed asymmetry is more likely attributable to the substantial heterogeneity across studies rather than true publication bias.

Sensitivity analysis

A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of our findings. For NPC, recalculated pooled prevalence estimates ranged from 62.38% to 70.20% after omitting each individual study, all remaining within the 95% confidence interval of the overall pooled estimate 66.0% (95% CI: 65.8, 66.2). For GC, estimates ranged from 27.50% to 40.63%, all within the confidence interval of the overall estimate 35.4% (95% CI: 19.9, 50.9). For BL, estimates ranged from 83.85% to 87.22%, all within the confidence interval of the overall estimate 86.8% (95% CI: 82.2, 91.5). Although omission of two NPC outlier studies (Aswani, 2022 and Adam, 2014) yielded lower estimates, all p-values remained significant (p < 0.001), confirming that no single study disproportionately influenced the pooled estimates (Table 3).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provide comprehensive pooled estimate of EBV burden across three major EBV-associated malignancies in Africa, based on 20 studies comprising 2,763 participants from 11 African countries. The meta-analyses findings reveal a clear and biologically coherent hierarchy of EBV prevalence, highest in Burkitt lymphoma 86.8% (95% CI: 82.2, 91.5), followed by nasopharyngeal carcinoma 66.0% (95% CI: 65.8, 66.2), and lowest in gastric cancer 35.4% (95% CI: 19.9, 50.9). This gradient is consistent with EBV’s classification as a Group 1 carcinogen [14] and reflects the fundamentally different oncogenic roles it plays across these three histologically and immunologically distinct tumour types rather than a uniform viral effect.

The highest pooled EBV prevalence was observed in Burkitt lymphoma 86.8% (95% CI: 82.2, 91.5), a finding that is biologically expected given the well-established triad of EBV infection, holoendemic Plasmodium falciparum malaria, and chronic B-cell hyperstimulation favoring MYC translocation in the pathogenesis of endemic BL [19, 43]. This pooled estimate is consistent with global meta-analytic [43] reported EBV attribution exceeding 80–90% in endemic BL cohorts from malaria-holoendemic settings, confirming that the African pattern observed here is part of a reproducible global pattern specific to this lymphoma subtype. The concentration of all four BL studies in East Africa region with the highest combined prevalence in this review 81.5%, 95% CI: 81.4, 81.7) reflects the geographic convergence of all three etiological drivers of endemic BL and lends biological coherence to the regional clustering of the evidence. The substantial heterogeneity observed within the BL subgroup (I² > 95%) despite the narrow confidence interval suggests that differences in specimen type and processing conditions, particularly the variable use of fresh versus formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPET) tissue, meaningfully influence detection sensitivity [51].

EBV prevalence in nasopharyngeal carcinoma was substantial at the meta-analytic level 66.0%, (95% CI: 65.8, 66.2), though notably lower than the approximately 98% positivity rate reported for endemic undifferentiated NPC globally [13, 14]. This divergence from the canonical global figure is itself a scientifically meaningful observation. A key explanatory factor is the strong influence of detection method on the meta-analytic estimate: the subgroup analysis by laboratory method demonstrated that studies using EBER-ISH yielded a substantially higher pooled prevalence 80.4%, (95% CI: 67.0, 93.8) compared to studies relying on DNA PCR 60.3%, (95% CI: 36.8, 83.8). Because 8 of the 11 NPC studies used DNA PCR while only 3 used EBER-ISH, the overall pooled NPC estimate is heavily weighted toward the lower, less tumour-specific PCR-derived signal. This pattern directly parallels the conclusion of Hirabayashi et al.[44], who demonstrated that detection-method selection systematically shifts pooled EBV estimates in gastric cancer meta-analyses, and reinforces that the comparatively lower African NPC pooled estimate partly reflects a methodological dilution effect rather than a genuine biological ceiling on EBV involvement in African NPC.

The pooled EBV prevalence in gastric cancer 35.4% (95% CI: 19.9, 50.9) was markedly higher than the 8–10% typically reported in global EBV-GC meta-analyses [45, 46], representing a more than three-fold elevation that demands scientific explanation. Three converging and independently supported mechanisms help account for this elevated pooled estimate. First, Helicobacter pylori, which is highly prevalent across Africa, may act synergistically with EBV to promote gastric epithelial transformation through chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and epigenetic dysregulation [47]. The very early primary EBV infection common in Africa, where most children acquire EBV before age five has been shown to result in poorly controlled viral replication and impaired T-cell responses compared to infection in adolescence or adulthood [19], this altered immune priming may sustain a higher viral load in epithelial tissues over decades. The host genetic variation in immune surveillance and EBV-specific restriction pathways may modulate population-level susceptibility to EBV-driven gastric carcinogenesis in ways that remain incompletely characterized in African populations [47].

The subgroup analysis by geographic region revealed substantial variation in NPC prevalence across African sub-regions (p < 0.001), with East Africa recording the highest pooled estimate 81.5% (95% CI: 81.4, 81.7), followed by Southern Africa 70.6% (95% CI: 24.7, 100.0), West Africa 53.0% (95% CI: 23.7, 82.3), and North Africa with the lowest estimate 50.0% (95% CI: 49.9, 50.1). These regional differences are biologically and epidemiologically interpretable. East Africa high pooled NPC-associated EBV prevalence aligns with its position as the region with the most intensive overlap between holoendemic malaria, early childhood EBV infection, and high HIV prevalence cofactors collectively associated with impaired immune surveillance of EBV-infected cells [19, 20, 48]. North Africa comparatively lower pooled estimate may reflect genuine differences in NPC etiology, with stronger proposed contributions from dietary nitrosamines, occupational exposures, and genetic predisposition [15, 18].

Country-specific prevalence estimates also varied considerably. However, these differences should be interpreted cautiously because they may reflect variations in pathology infrastructure, diagnostic capacity, specimen processing, and laboratory methods rather than true epidemiological differences. Furthermore, the uneven geographical distribution of studies, particularly lack of evidence from some regions, limits continent-wide generalizability.

The subgroup analysis by detection method revealed one of the most clinically and methodologically consequential findings of this review. The EBER-ISH-based studies reported a substantially higher pooled EBV prevalence in NPC 80.4% (95% CI: 67.0, 93.8) compared to DNA PCR-based studies 60.3% (95% CI: 36.8, 83.8), a difference of approximately 20% points. This detection-method effect is biologically expected and has direct implications for interpreting the overall pooled NPC estimate. EBER-ISH detects EBV-encoded small RNA transcripts directly within the nuclei of malignant epithelial cells and is widely regarded as the most tumour-specific method for confirming EBV involvement in carcinoma tissue [8, 49]. DNA PCR, by contrast, amplifies EBV DNA without spatial localization, making it unable to distinguish viral genomes residing within tumour cells from those in EBV-latently-infected B lymphocytes that routinely infiltrate tumour microenvironments [11, 49].

Importantly, the prevalence estimates presented in this review should be interpreted as measures of EBV detection rather than definitive EBV attribution. The PCR based assay detects viral DNA without demonstrating localization within malignant cells, PCR positivity alone cannot establish a causal role for EBV in tumorigenesis. Consequently, prevalence estimates derived from PCR-based studies may either overestimate or underestimate the true attributable fraction of EBV-associated cancers. EBER-ISH remains the preferred approach for confirming EBV involvement in tumor cells and should be prioritized in future African studies [7, 43, 44, 49].

Specimen handling and tissue processing represent an additional, often underappreciated, source of variability in EBV detection. FFPET tissue the most widely used specimen type in this review was subject to DNA fragmentation, cross-linking, and chemical modification during formalin fixation, all of which reduce the sensitivity of downstream assays relative to fresh or optimally cryopreserved tissue [50, 51]. Strengthening pathology infrastructure to support EBER-ISH on well-preserved tumour tissue would address both the methodological gap and the specimen-quality gap simultaneously, bringing African EBV-cancer epidemiology closer to the diagnostic standard required for accurate attributable-fraction estimation.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths, including adherence to PRISMA guidelines, protocol registration in PROSPERO, comprehensive database searching, inclusion of multiple EBV-associated malignancies, and extensive subgroup analyses by region and detection method.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be considered. Substantial statistical heterogeneity was observed across studies. Evidence was unevenly distributed across Africa, with no eligible studies identified from Central Africa and limited representation from several regions. Additionally, most studies were hospital-based and may not represent national populations. Moreover, considerable variation existed in specimen types and EBV detection methods. Because PCR was the predominant diagnostic approach, the pooled estimates may not fully reflect true EBV-attributable disease burden. Finally, the limited number of studies available for some subgroup analyses reduced statistical power for exploring sources of heterogeneity.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrate that EBV is frequently detected in 86.8% Burkitt lymphoma, 66.0% in nasopharyngeal carcinoma, and 35.4% in gastric cancer in the African countries represented in the available evidence. Prevalence varied substantially by cancer type and detection method, highlighting the importance of methodological standardization and expanded surveillance across underrepresented regions of Africa. The GC estimate is notably higher than global benchmarks, suggesting Africa-specific cofactors may amplify EBV’s oncogenic role. EBER-ISH-based studies report higher NPC prevalence 80.4% than PCR-based studies 60.3%, highlighting the critical influence of detection method on pooled estimates. Future efforts should priorities expanding EBER-ISH capacity, conducting population-based studies in under-represented regions, and investigating cofactors including malaria, H. pylori, HIV, and host genetics. These efforts will strengthen the evidence base for EBV-targeted prevention and vaccine strategies in Africa.
