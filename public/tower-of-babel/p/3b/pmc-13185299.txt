The effect of stressful life events on adolescents with depression: the chain mediating role of basic psychological needs and self-esteem
Authors: Furong Gou, Xin Tian, Xinrong Ma, Ling Dang, Yuelan Zhang, Mengyuan Zhang, Liqi Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAdolescent depression has emerged as a critical global public health concern, with stressful life events identified as key environmental risk factors. However, the underlying psychological mechanisms in clinically diagnosed adolescents remain insufficiently explored. This study aims to examine whether basic psychological needs and self-esteem serve as sequential mediators in the relationship between stressful life events and depressive symptoms.Patients and methodsA total of 312 adolescents (aged 12–19 years; 68.8% female) diagnosed with depressive disorder were recruited from a psychiatric hospital in Northwest China. Participants completed validated self-report measures including the Adolescent Self-Rating Life Events Checklist (ASLEC), the Basic Psychological Needs Scale (BPNS), the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (SES), and the Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS). A serial mediation model was tested using SPSS PROCESS (Model 6), controlling for gender.ResultsStressful life events positively correlated with adolescent depressive symptoms (B=0.305, p<0.001). In the chain mediation model, basic psychological needs and self-esteem acted as sequential mediators, explaining 47.25% of total variance (indirect B=0.144, 95% CI [0.088, 0.202]). Decomposition showed basic needs alone mediated 20.62% (B=0.063, CI[0.027,0.107]), self-esteem 19.57% (B=0.060, CI[0.027,0.096]), and the chain pathway (needs→self-esteem) 7.05% (B=0.022, CI[0.009,0.038]).ConclusionFindings support a theoretically grounded chain mediation model in which stressful life events are associated with lower levels of adolescents’ basic psychological needs satisfaction, which in turn relates to reduced self-esteem and more severe depressive symptoms. These results integrate Self-Determination Theory and Maslow’s hierarchy of needs, offering evidence-based insight into the process through which environmental stress is associated with. Clinically, this suggests that interventions targeting need satisfaction and self-esteem restoration may may be associated with reduced depression in adolescent patients.Keywords: Stressful life events, Depressive symptoms, Basic psychological needs, Self-esteem, Adolescents

Introduction

Depressive disorders are a category of mood disorders primarily characterized by persistent low mood, diminished interest, and impaired cognitive function [1]. Depressive symptoms are quite common among adolescents and show an upward trend year by year [2]. Adolescence, marked by rapid changes in emotional regulation, cognitive maturation, and social role adaptation, makes it a high-risk period for depressive symptoms [3]. Data from the World Health Organization (WHO) indicates that the prevalence of depression among adolescents aged 10–14 is approximately 1.4%, rising to 3.5% among those aged 15–19 [4]. In China, the prevalence of depressive disorders among adolescents aged 12–18 is even higher, ranging from 1.8% to 7.8%, significantly exceeding the average rate among adults [5]. This data underscores the prevalence and severity of depression within the adolescent population. In fact, depression persistently impacts adolescents’ cognitive, academic, and social functioning [6]. As symptoms worsen, it increases high-risk behaviors such as suicide and self-harm, causing long-term consequences for their future lives, studies, and social interactions [7, 8]. This underscores the necessity of identifying key risk factors. Therefore, this study integrates key psychological factors to determine the underlying mechanisms influencing depressive symptoms.

Stress is one of the most important risks leading to mental health problems [9]. Stressful life events refer to threatening or disruptive external occurrences encountered by individuals in contexts such as academic, interpersonal, or family settings, which impose sustained strain on the emotional regulation system [10]. For adolescents with depressive disorders whose emotional regulation abilities remain underdeveloped, such events are more likely to be related to intense negative emotional responses [11]. According to the stress-vulnerability model, when external stressors exceed an adolescent’s psychological resources, depressive symptoms are more likely to be related to or exacerbated [12]. Existing research confirms a significant positive correlation between stressful life events and symptom severity in adolescents with depressive disorders, indicating that stressful life events constitute a major risk factor for worsening depression in this population [13]. According to li et al., interpersonal relationship and gender mediates the relationship between stressful life events and depression [14]. Ji et al. found that social support partially mediates the relationship between negative life events and depressive symptoms [15]. Although existing research has revealed the roles of stressful life events, interpersonal relationship, and social support in adolescent depressive symptoms, the mechanisms underlying the impact of stressful life events on depressive symptoms in adolescents with depressive disorders remain unclear. In particular, the mediating pathway of basic psychological needs and self-esteem warrant further exploration. Therefore, this study aims to explore the mediating pathway of basic psychological needs and self-esteem in the relationship between stressful life events and depressive symptoms among adolescents with depressive disorders, based on the stress-vulnerability model and basic psychological needs theory. This research seeks to provide theoretical foundations for relevant clinical interventions.

Stressful life events and depressive symptoms in adolescents

Common stressful events include academic stress (e.g., failing a test, excessive workload), peer bullying (e.g., verbal abuse, hitting), family stress (e.g., parental conflict, divorce, neglect), and traumatic experiences such as bereavement and victimization [16, 17]. Stressful events often overwhelm individual adaptive capacity, being associated with psycho-physiological stress responses. Adolescents, in their critical developmental stage, are more susceptible to stressful events’ adverse impacts [18]. Extensive investigations reveal a robust connection between stressful experiences during the adolescent period and the increased likelihood of developing depression [19–22]. Longitudinal studies have identified major life events as important prospective predictors of emotional and behavioral functioning in adolescents [23]. From a theoretical perspective, the cognitive-behavioral model of depression suggests that stressful life events can activate negative core beliefs and be associated with negative automatic thoughts, which intensify negative emotions and promote behavioral avoidance [24]. This process may create a self-reinforcing cycle that exacerbates and maintains depressive symptoms. In light of the robust connection between stressors in daily life and depressive symptoms, the following hypotheses are put forward:

H1: Stressful life events significantly predict the severity of depressive symptoms in adolescents.

H1: Stressful life events significantly predict the severity of depressive symptoms in adolescents.

The mediating pathway of basic psychological needs

Self-Determination Theory (SDT) states that human beings are born with three basic psychological needs - autonomy, competence and belonging - that are essential for healthy psychological development [25, 26] whereas prolonged failure to meet them can lead to negative consequences such as depressive symptoms. This psychological mechanism is particularly pronounced during adolescence, a developmental stage in which not only is emotional sensitivity significantly heightened, but it is also a critical period for identity formation [27, 28]. Research on adolescent populations has confirmed that the absence of basic psychological needs predicts a higher likelihood of depressive symptoms [29, 30]. The susceptibility-stress model of depression suggests that stressful events in the external environment (e.g., major life changes) and intrinsic individual susceptibility factors (e.g., chronic unmet psychological needs) combine to increase the risk of depression [31]. Results demonstrated that greater exposure to stressful events correlated with reduced basic psychological need satisfaction in adolescents, and this relationship subsequently predicted elevated depression levels via diminished satisfaction [32]. Therefore, basic psychological needs were selected as mediators because they represent a core psychological mechanism linking external stressors to internal cognitive–emotional processes [33]. Stressful life events may deplete adolescents’ psychological resources and undermine autonomy, competence, and relatedness, while need frustration may impair emotion regulation resources, making adolescents less capable of coping with stress-induced negative emotions and ultimately contributing to depressive symptoms [34]. Guided by the model’s theoretical postulates, this study formulates the following hypotheses based on the observed consistency between findings and theory:

H2: Basic psychological needs serve as a mediating pathway between stressful life events and depressive symptoms in the adolescents.

H2: Basic psychological needs serve as a mediating pathway between stressful life events and depressive symptoms in the adolescents.

The mediating pathway of self-esteem

Rosenberg defines self-esteem as an individual’s overall appraisal of his or her own worth, encompassing perceptions of personal competence, value, and acceptance, which is a central component of the self-concept and is particularly malleable during adolescence, a critical period for self-identity formation [35]. Beck’s cognitive model of depression views low self-esteem as a key cognitive vulnerability at this stage, characterized by negative appraisals of the self, the world, and the future, which are associated with the way adolescents interpret stressful experiences as well as regulate their emotions [36]. Empirical research has further confirmed this mechanism in adolescent populations. Choi noted that among adolescents, higher self-esteem buffers the psychological impact of stress by promoting constructive interpretations (“I can cope with this”), whereas low self-esteem amplifies feelings of helplessness and reinforces the link between stress and emotional distress [37]. This is consistent with findings from studies of vulnerable groups of adolescents (e.g., rural left-behind children), in which stress-induced decreases in self-esteem are strongly associated with rises in depressive mood [38]. Based on the above theoretical models and existing empirical evidence, self-esteem is regarded as a key cognitive mechanism through which stress affects depressive symptoms. Stressful life events may impair adolescents’ self-evaluation, and low self-esteem, as a cognitive vulnerability factor, can activate negative cognitive schemas and amplify the adverse association associated with stress, thereby increasing the risk of depression. In summary, the following hypotheses are proposed to delve into the intricate relationship among between self-esteem in stressful life events and depressive symptoms:

H3: Self-esteem mediates the relationship between stressful life events and adolescents depression symptoms.

H3: Self-esteem mediates the relationship between stressful life events and adolescents depression symptoms.

The roles of basic psychological needs and self-esteem

A substantial body of research has demonstrated a close association between the satisfaction of basic psychological needs and self-esteem [39, 40]. An empirical study on Chinese junior high school students with depression found that the satisfaction of basic psychological needs could effectively predict their self-esteem [41]. Similarly, a study conducted among students at San Sebastián University in Chile indicated that satisfaction of basic psychological needs fosters the development of higher self-esteem [42]. Maslow’s hierarchy of needs provides a theoretical framework for understanding the relationship between basic psychological needs and self-esteem, suggesting that self-esteem develops on the foundation of more fundamental psychological needs such as autonomy, competence, and relatedness. When these needs are adequately met, individuals are more likely to perceive themselves as capable and valuable, thereby promoting higher self-esteem [43]. However, in real-life contexts, adolescents are commonly exposed to various stressful life events [44], and these adverse environmental factors may disrupt the satisfaction of basic psychological needs and erode self-esteem as a core psychological resource, thereby increasing the risk of depressive symptoms. Therefore, it is necessary to further explore the underlying psychological mechanisms through which stressful life events are associated with depressive symptoms. Nevertheless, few studies have integrated basic psychological needs and self-esteem into a unified framework to explain how stressful life events are associated with depressive symptoms among adolescents with depressive disorders. To address this gap, the present study innovatively constructs a chain mediation model incorporating both basic psychological needs and self-esteem. Based on this, we propose the following hypotheses:

H4: Basic psychological needs and self-esteem levels in the adolescent population act as chain mediators between stressful life events and depressive symptoms.

H4: Basic psychological needs and self-esteem levels in the adolescent population act as chain mediators between stressful life events and depressive symptoms.

Materials and methods

Participants

This study included 312 adolescents aged 12 to 19 years (M = 16.0, SD = 1.75), who were consecutively recruited from the Ningxia Mental Health Center between March 2024 to February 2025. All participants and their legal guardians (if underage) provided informed consent prior to participation.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) meeting the DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for depressive disorder (confirmed by two attending psychiatrists); (2) age between 12 and 19 years, consistent with the WHO definition of adolescence; (3) receiving stable medication for at least four weeks; and (4) ability to complete self-report questionnaires independently. Exclusion criteria included: (1) comorbid psychotic or neurodevelopmental disorders; (2) severe physical illness or cognitive impairment; and (3) incomplete or invalid questionnaire responses (> 10% missing data on any main variable).

According to Fritz and MacKinnon [45], detecting mediated association of medium to small magnitude often requires a sample size of 200 or more to achieve adequate statistical power. Given that this study focuses on the overall association of chain mediation, the 312 valid samples identified during the design phase fall within the commonly recommended range for detecting small to medium associations in this field. In addition, an a priori power analysis using G*Power (multiple regression, f² = 0.02, α = 0.05, power = 0.80, four predictors) indicated a minimum required sample size of 198, confirming that the current sample size was adequate. Furthermore, Monte Carlo power analysis based on the observed path coefficients showed that the power to detect all key indirect associations exceeded 0.98, surpassing the conventional threshold of 0.80. This sample size provides robust statistical power for bootstrap confidence interval estimation and statistical testing of mediation associations. Therefore, the current study’s sample size of 312 is sufficient and aligns with established recommendations for mediation analysis involving multiple variables.

This study was conducted at Ningxia Ning-An Hospital in the Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region, China and performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee at Ningxia Mental Health Center, Yinchuan, China (protocol no. 2023-WS-022), and was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2300076548). All participants provided written informed consent prior to involvement in the study.

Measures

Stressful life events

The Adolescent Self-Rating Life Events Checklist (ASLEC) is a 27-item tool assessing stressful life events across six domains (interpersonal conflicts, academic pressure, punishment, bereavement, academic difficulties, other negative events) using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = minimal to 5 = extreme) to rate frequency/severity over the past six months [46]. Higher total scores reflect greater perceived severity of stress. The ASLEC has been validated for use in Chinese pediatric populations [47]. This study reported a Cronbach’s α of 0.933, showing excellent internal consistency reliability.

Depressive symptoms

Assessment of depressive symptoms using the Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS) by Zung (1965) [48]. This scale comprises 20 items reflecting four specific symptom clusters of depressive states: psychic-emotional symptoms, somatic disturbances, psychomotor disturbances, and psychological disturbances of depression. The SDS uses a 4-point Likert scale to measure weekly symptom frequency, with total scores reflecting depressive severity (higher scores = greater severity). Validated in Chinese populations, it shows strong reliability and validity compared to clinical evaluations [49]. In this study, Cronbach’s α was 0.872.

Basic psychological needs

The Basic Psychological Needs Scale (BPNS) consists of 21 items, divided into three subscales: Competence Needs, Autonomy Needs, and Belongingness Needs [50]. It assesses core psychological need fulfillment via a 7-point Likert scale, where higher total scores indicate greater need satisfaction. Cronbach’s α = 0.861, indicating strong internal consistency.

Self-esteem

The Self-Esteem Scale (SES), a common tool for assessing general self-esteem, uses a 4-point Likert scale for responses [51]. Item scores were averaged for individual self-esteem scores, with higher scores indicating stronger self-esteem. The SES is validated across diverse populations, including Chinese samples [52]. The internal consistency of the scale was assessed with a Cronbach’s α of 0.847.

Control variables

Given that gender exhibits significant differences across all three variables—depression, basic psychological needs, and self-esteem—it will be included as a control variable in all subsequent model analyses, encompassing direct associations, indirect associations, and each path within the chained mediation model.

Common method bias control

All variables were measured using self-report instruments. Measurement of shared method bias was assessed through Harman’s single-factor test [53]. The results showed that the first common factor explained 20.20% of the variance, a value less than the 40% threshold. This suggests that shared method variance is carried negligible significance as a concern in this study.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using SPSS 27.0. During the data cleaning phase, we first conducted completeness checks on all questionnaires. Cases exhibiting obvious outliers or severe missing data that prevented calculation of scale scores were excluded based on scoring rules and ethical requirements. Following this step, a total of 312 valid questionnaires were included in the final statistical analysis. Pearson correlations were computed to examine bivariate associations. Serial mediation analysis was performed using PROCESS (Model 6), with stressful life events as the predictor, depressive symptoms as the outcome, and basic psychological needs and self-esteem as mediators. All variables were entered into the model based on the total scores of each scale, with no standardization procedures applied prior to analysis. To examine whether the proposed serial mediation model provided a better fit than competing models, a parallel mediation model was tested as an alternative model. Model fit was compared using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), with lower values indicating better model fit. Gender was controlled. Indirect associations were estimated using 5,000 bootstrapped samples and 95% bias-corrected confidence intervals.

This suggests that gender may play an important role in adolescents’ psychological functioning. Overall, the findings suggest that gender was the sociodemographic factor most associated with psychological differences in this sample. These findings support the inclusion of gender as a covariate in subsequent mediation analyses to control for potential confounding associations.

Discussion

This study, guided by the stress-vulnerability model and basic psychological needs theory, explored how stressful life events are associated with depressive symptoms through basic psychological needs and self-esteem. The findings revealed a clear chain: environmental stress undermines adolescents’ satisfaction of autonomy, competence, and relatedness, which in turn reduces self-esteem and intensifies depressive symptoms. Maslow emphasized that unmet basic needs hinder self-worth, while SDT highlights emotional distress under prolonged need frustration. By confirming this sequential mechanism, our model offers a theoretical explanation for how external stress is internalized into emotional distress in depressed adolescents, providing a foundation for targeted psychological interventions. The results of this study show that stressful life events are related to the severity of depressive symptoms in adolescents. In other words, adolescents experiencing more stressful life events tend to report higher levels of depressive symptoms. This finding supports Hypothesis H1 and is consistent with previous research [54]. Shi suggested that stressful life events are a high risk factor for depressive symptoms [55]. In addition, a longitudinal study found that adolescents who experienced more stressful events in a short period showed higher levels of depressive symptoms in the following months [56]. These studies indicate that stressful life events are associated with higher depressive symptoms in adolescents. The stress-vulnerability model provides theoretical support for this observation [12]. According to the model, stressful life events are important external factors related to emotional difficulties, and adolescents may be more sensitive because their emotional regulation and coping skills are still developing [57]. Overall, both empirical evidence and theory support the link between stressful life events and depressive symptoms in adolescents. In clinical practice, assessing adolescents’ recent stressful experiences and providing emotional support and stress management strategies, such as identifying stressors and practicing relaxation techniques, may help reduce depressive symptoms. The results of this study show that basic psychological needs play a mediating pathway between stressful life events and depressive symptoms. Stressful life events were negatively associated with basic psychological needs, and basic psychological needs were negatively associated with depressive symptom levels. This finding supports Hypothesis H2 and is consistent with previous research. Zhang noted that adolescents whose basic psychological needs are not met are more likely to experience depressive symptoms [58]. Liu emphasized that meeting basic psychological needs is critical to reducing the severity of depression during the pandemic. Self-determination theory provides a theoretical framework for this result, indicating that environmental factors are associated with mental health by facilitating or thwarting the satisfaction of basic psychological needs, including autonomy, competence, and relatedness [59]. Stressful life events may undermine the satisfaction of these needs, thereby linked to emotional dysregulation and associated with depressive symptoms. Based on this, interventions for adolescents with depression may focus on enhancing the satisfaction of basic psychological needs, such as involving adolescents in setting personal goals during treatment to increase their sense of autonomy and better buffer the negative impact of stressful events. The results of this study show that self-esteem plays a partial mediating pathway between stressful life events and depressive symptoms. Stressful life events were negatively associated with self-esteem, and self-esteem was negatively associated with depressive symptom levels. This finding supports Hypothesis H3 and is consistent with previous research. Woods noted that low self-esteem is an important cognitive risk factor for adolescent depression [60]. In addition, studies have shown that adolescents who experience stressful life events such as parental divorce, academic failure, or peer rejection tend to report lower self-esteem, and this decrease in self-esteem is associated with higher depressive symptoms [61]. According to the stress–cognitive vulnerability model, when individuals encounter stressful life events, existing cognitive vulnerabilities are activated, and low self-esteem is a central aspect of adolescents’ cognitive vulnerability [12]. Therefore, stressful life events may exacerbate depressive symptoms by lowering self-esteem and increasing negative self-appraisals. Based on this, clinical interventions can include providing adolescents with depressive disorders with affirmative feedback, emotional acceptance, and opportunities to participate in decision-making, which may strengthen self-recognition, enhance self-esteem, and help mitigate the negative impact of stressful events on depressive symptoms.

This study further confirmed that basic psychological needs and self-esteem jointly form a chained mediating pathway between stressful life events and depressive symptoms, thereby supporting Hypothesis H4. This finding suggests that stressful life events may not only exert direct associations or single indirect associations, but may also be associated with depressive symptoms through a sequential mechanism. According to the stress–vulnerability model, external stress does not directly lead to depression but operates through individuals’ internal vulnerabilities and psychological resources [12]. Frustration of basic psychological needs and low self-esteem are two typical vulnerability factors: compromised need satisfaction reduces adolescents’ capacity to cope effectively, while diminished self-esteem increases their tendency to engage in negative self-evaluation, pessimistic attribution, and feelings of helplessness, thereby amplifying the emotional consequences of stress. Over time, stressful life events accumulate their negative impact through the pathway of “need frustration → reduced self-esteem,” resulting in higher levels of depressive symptoms. In sum, stressful life events do not induce depression directly; instead, they are associated with a continuous psychological depletion process by undermining basic psychological needs and further lowering self-esteem. The chained mediation model constructed in this study is grounded in this theoretical mechanism, proposing that stressful life events are associated with depressive symptoms among adolescents with depressive disorders through the sequential mediating associations of basic psychological needs and self-esteem. Furthermore, the comparison results of the goodness of fit between the parallel mediation model and the chain mediation model further verify the rationality of this sequential mechanism of action. The comparison with the parallel mediation model supports the hypothesized sequential mechanism, suggesting that stressful life events may be associated with depressive symptoms through basic psychological needs and subsequently self-esteem. Although moderation models were not tested in the present study, future research should examine potential moderating factors to further clarify these relationships.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. We used a cross-sectional design, and although the mediation paths were statistically supported, we cannot infer causal relationships between variables. Future studies should use longitudinal or experimental designs to examine temporal changes. All data were self-reported, which may lead to common method bias and reporting bias, and future studies can include multi-source data to improve objectivity. The sample was from a single psychiatric hospital in Northwest China, and the gender distribution was unbalanced (about 70% female). Thus, the generalizability of the findings and the model’s applicability across genders need to be verified in more representative and gender-balanced samples. This study did not systematically collect participants’ treatment history or current psychological or medical intervention status. This factor may directly affect adolescents’ self-esteem and their ability to cope with stressful events, and thus significantly association scores and model pathways. We therefore did not control for this key variable, and future studies should include treatment history to improve the accuracy and interpretability of the results. Finally, this study used process regression to test the mediation associations without explicitly considering measurement error. Future studies can use structural equation modeling with larger samples and integrate machine learning methods to explore more complex psychological mechanisms.

Conclusion

This study examined the relationship between stressful life events and depressive symptoms among adolescents with depression, with a focus on the chain mediating pathway of basic psychological needs and self-esteem. The findings demonstrated that stressful life events were significantly associated with higher levels of depressive symptoms, and that this association was sequentially mediated by the frustration of basic psychological needs and reduced self-esteem. These results provide empirical support for theories such as the Self-Determination Theory and the diathesis-stress model, highlighting key psychological mechanisms that contribute to depression in clinical adolescent populations.

The findings of this study suggest that clinical interventions for adolescents with depression should focus on restoring core psychological resources, including basic psychological needs satisfaction and self-esteem. Based on the present results, several actionable strategies can be considered: supporting adolescents’ participation in decision-making to enhance autonomy; implementing staged goal-setting by breaking down tasks or rehabilitation objectives into achievable steps to strengthen competence and a sense of accomplishment; and promoting belongingness through positive therapeutic alliances or peer support activities. In addition, cognitive-behavioral techniques may help adolescents identify and reduce self-critical thinking, while self-affirmation training or structured mastery experiences can consolidate a stable and positive self-concept. Overall, interventions should not only target symptom reduction but also aim to restore core psychological resources, thereby potentially enhancing adolescents’ capacity to cope with stress and reducing the severity of depressive symptoms.
