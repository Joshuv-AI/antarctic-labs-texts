Laser for lower-urinary tract symptoms
Authors: Peng-Hui Wang, Szu-Ting Yang
Published: 2025

Lower-urinary tract symptoms (LUTS), including urinary frequency, urgency, nocturia, incontinence, weak urine stream, and a sensation of incomplete emptying—commonly affect women during their reproductive and postmenopausal periods, resulting in a debilitating condition negatively affecting women, secondary to LUTS-related troublesome and bothersome clinical presentations, significantly compromising quality of life (QoL) and social functioning.1–4 LUTS represent a broad spectrum of disorders, are classified as a type of pelvic floor disorders (PFD).4,5 The underlying pathophysiology of LUTS is complex and mixed, which may result from functional disorders, psychosomatic problems, and true organic and anatomical structural disorders, or previous trauma (obstetric or gynecologic surgery) and/or other etiologies.6–9 Among LUTS, stress urinary incontinence (SUI) may be one of the most frequent problems, affecting one-fifth of Taiwanese women.6–9 The strategies to treat SUI vary greatly, ranging from the traditional pelvic floor physical therapy, topical estrogen creams, vaginal inserts or pessaries (a removable device inserted into the vagina to support the pelvic organs), and surgeries, including urethral injections or urethral slings to modern technology, such as vaginal lasers, extracorporeal shock wave therapy, and application of platelet-rich plasma, among others.10–12 We are very happy to introduce Dr. Hsiao’s study published in the February 2025 issue of the Journal of Chinese Medical Association (JCMA) to discuss the value of using modern technology-vaginal lasers, in the management of women with SUI.13

Dr. Hsiao prospectively enrolled 75 women with SUI treated with single-session vaginal CO2 laser therapy to determine the predictors of treatment effectiveness.13 The results showed that low voided volume (dL, hazard ratio [HR]: 0.71), high postvoid residual volume (dL, HR: 2.174), and low functional profile length (HR: 0.956) were predictors for subjective failure (ie, global response assessment [GRA] ≤0 at the last follow-up survey).13 Additionally, the authors identified voided volume (coefficient = −0.5, p = 0.005) and postvoid residual volume (coefficient = 0.9, p = 0.04) as predictors of subjective failure with a intercept of 1.0 (95% CI, −0.3 to 2.3, p = 0.12).13 Furthermore, there was a significant improvement in the International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire (ICIQ), Incontinence Impact Questionnaire (IIQ7), Overactive Bladder Symptom Scores (OABSS), Urogenital Distress Inventory (UDI-6), and Urgency Severity Scale (USS) scores at 3 and 6 months, compared with those at baseline.13 By contrast, there was no change in the Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI) from baseline.13 Moreover, the authors found the median value of the subjective improvement interval was 18.3 months (95% CI, 12.2-18.3 months), contributing to recommending that additional therapy may be considered 18 months after treatment to maintain therapeutic effects.13 This study is interesting and worthy of discussion.

In fact, it is a relatively new strategy to use laser therapy for treating women with SUI, because the first paper in Taiwan addressing this topic was derived from the same author group (Drs. Lin and Hsiao).14 The results are relatively different from the current study, although no hypothesis has been proposed to explain the difference. In fact, the effect for mild SUI was moderate at 6-month follow-up, but was not effective in women with pad weight >10 g in the earlier report.14 One study from Kaohsiung Veterans General Hospital showed that laser therapy, either by Erbium:YAG laser or CO2 laser, seemed to be useful for female PFDs, especially for improvement of SUI symptoms.15 The newest study in Taiwan was also performed by the same group (Drs. Lin and Hsiao) to show that low-level laser therapy is a safe, cost-effective, and straightforward treatment for alleviating genitourinary syndrome of menopause and SUI.16 Compared to all other studies in Taiwan, characterized by a smaller number of participants and a retrospective nature, the design of the current study is prospective. Although the current study is also limited by a small number of subjects and of the most importance, absence of controls, the value is still superior to those aforementioned retrospective studies in Taiwan.14–16 Although the current study seems to be of more value, the result seems to be against the recent concept that vaginal lasers may have little to no effect on SUI as measured by both clinical assessment in the short term, compared with sham, control, or topical treatments.10,17 However, although evidence is uncertain, meta-analysis shows that laser may improve patient-reported measures of SUI.10,17 Additionally, fortunately, there is no doubt that the safety of laser for treating SUI women is confirmed.10,17

To clearly assess therapeutic effectiveness between vaginal laser and other controls, including sham, control or topical treatment, a recent meta-analysis showed no difference between the number of continent women between women who underwent vaginal laser compared with sham (risk ratio [RR]: 1.50, 95% CI, 0.72-3.10; I2 = 81%; three studies, 196 women; very low-certainty evidence) in the short-term follow-up.10,17 There may be more continent women among those who underwent vaginal laser compared to those who underwent sham or control treatments in the medium term (1-5 years, RR: 2.88, 95% CI, 1.48 to 5.60; I² = not applicable; one study, n = 76; very low-certainty evidence).17 However, vaginal lasers may improve patient-reported incontinence measures (ICIQ and Urinary Incontinence Short Form [UISF]) compared to sham or control treatment in the short term (mean difference [MD]: −1.42 points, 95% CI, −2.41 to −0.43; I² = 54%; eight studies, n = 632; very low-certainty evidence).17 All these findings suggest that the value and effectiveness of laser for treating women with SUI require further studies incorporating more medium- and long-term data, and, most importantly, data stratified by different types of incontinence, symptom severity, and patient comorbidities will better guidance for using laser for the aforementioned purpose (SUI treatment).

Taken together, Dr. Hsiao’s study provides domestic data to support the potential benefits of laser therapy for SUI.13 As with many previous studies,18,19 their data are limited by a small sample size, and the absence of controls, the use of lasers in the management of SUI in Taiwan remains immature. We hope for more and more studies, particularly those using RCT design, to clarify the feasibility of utilizing lasers for treating SUI in women. We believe that the recent trend toward minimally invasive or less traumatic procedures19,20 for treating SUI women may become increasingly popular and acceptable, although the evidence of its effectiveness is still uncertain.
