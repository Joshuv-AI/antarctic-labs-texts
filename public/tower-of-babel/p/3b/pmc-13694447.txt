Examining the chronicity of suicidal thoughts and behaviors among Australian males
Authors: Tilahun Haregu, Shaosen Zhang, Jesse Young, Gregory Armstrong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeResearch on chronic suicidal thoughts and behaviors (STB) in Australia is limited. This study aimed to determine the prevalence and identify predictors of chronic STB among Australian males.MethodsA nine-year retrospective cohort study was conducted, analysing data from the first four waves of the Ten to Men Study. The study included 3,070 males who reported lifetime STB at Wave 1. Chronic STB was defined as the presence suicidal thoughts, plans, or attempts over subsequent waves. Modified Poisson and mixed-effects Poisson regression models were used to identify predictors.ResultsThe prevalence of chronic suicidal thoughts and attempts were 34.5% and 13.6%, respectively, with an overall chronic STB prevalence of 37.2%. At Wave 2, depression (IRR = 1.59; 95% CI [1.28, 1.98]; P < 0.001), homosexuality (IRR = 1.50; 95% CI [1.06, 2.14]; P = 0.023), and alcohol use disorder (IRR = 1.21; 95% CI [1.01, 1.46]; P = 0.044) were significantly associated with chronic STB. Over nine years, depression (IRR = 2.57; 95% CI [2.05, 3.22]; P < 0.001), alcohol use disorder (IRR = 1.53; 95% CI [1.27, 1.85]; P < 0.001), disability (IRR = 1.43; 95% CI [1.12, 1.82]; P = 0.004), and marijuana use (IRR = 1.33; 95% CI [1.05, 1.67]; P = 0.016) were key predictors.ConclusionAmong Australian men who report lifetime STB, more than one in three experience chronic STB over a nine-year period. Depression, alcohol use disorder, disability, illicit substance use, and socioeconomic factors emerged as primary predictors. Further research is needed to explore intervention strategies addressing these factors.Keywords: Chronicity, Suicidal ideation, Prevalence, Risk factors

Introduction

Suicide is a significant public health concern in Australia, being the leading cause of death among men aged 15–44 [1]. Approximately 75% of suicidal deaths in Australia occur among men [2]. Psychiatric disorders and harmful substance use (e.g., heavy alcohol and illicit drug consumption) confer a greater risk for suicidal behaviour worldwide. In Australia, higher suicide prevalence is found in more remote regions and Indigenous Australian communities [3, 4].

Suicidal behaviour is often conceptualized through the suicide cascade, a model that illustrates how various factors interact and escalate, ultimately leading from suicidal ideation to planning and, eventually, to an attempt or completion of suicide [5, 6]. The cascade represents a progression that typically occurs along a continuum. However, it is important to note that this progression is not always linear or inevitable. Several factors, such as an individual’s psychological state, external circumstances, and available support systems, influence this process [7].

A significant proportion of individuals who experience suicidal ideation, make suicidal plans, or attempt suicide will exhibit chronic suicidal thoughts and behaviors [8]. The 10-year follow-up of National Comorbidity Survey (NCDs) showed persistence of suicidal ideation among ideators at baseline to be 35%, while the prevalence for chronic suicidal planning and attempts were 21.2% and 15.4%, respectively [9]. Another study in a Danish Territory among those 15 years and older found that 30% of those who attempt suicide repeat the attempt within a year [10]. A Japanese study among suicide attempters admitted to emergency departments reported that the rate of chronic suicidal attempts in males within 5 years was 16.8% [11]. However, most of this evidence comes from clinical populations - individuals who are receiving treatment or care for mental health conditions, physical illnesses, or other medical conditions that may be associated with a higher risk of suicidal behaviour- and there is a lack of estimates on chronic suicidality and its predictors in longitudinal general population samples.

The causes of chronic suicidal thoughts and behaviors (STB) are multifaceted and complex, varying widely between regions, communities, and individuals. Some studies have identified factors associated with chronic suicidal behaviour, such as the lethality of suicide methods [12], the time elapsed since the previous suicide attempt [13], the number of previous suicide attempts [11], and the lack of mental health intervention after an attempt [10].

While studies generally consider relevant aspects contributing to chronic suicidal behaviour, they often focus on mental health disorders. The role of socioeconomic factors, such as income and employment, and substance use has not been explored. Moreover, factors known to be associated with a higher risk of any suicidal behaviour and especially relevant to Australia, such as rurality [14] and Index of Relative Socio-Economic Disadvantage and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander status [15, 16], are also absent from many studies on chronic suicidal thoughts and behaviors.

To enhance public health strategies aimed at reducing chronic STB in males, it is essential to investigate all potential risk factors, including those specific to Australia. Identifying and addressing these factors is crucial for optimizing suicide prevention measures among Australian males. Accordingly, our study aimed to (1) determine the rates of chronic STBs in Australian males; and (2) identify predictors of chronic STB using a nationally representative population-based cohort of males in Australia.

Methods

Study population

This study used data collected from the Ten to Men study, a large-scale cohort study focusing on the health of Australian males, which has been conducted in discrete survey waves since 2013. The Ten to Men cohort provides a nationally distributed snapshot of Australian males aged 10–55, capturing substantial diversity in geography, age, socioeconomic circumstances, cultural background, and health status. Its stratified, multi-stage sampling design ensures strong representation of urban and regional communities, a broad adolescent-to-midlife age range, and wide variation in education, employment, and wellbeing [17].

To date, four waves have been completed, with a unique questionnaire administered at each wave, involving the participation of over 16,000 men and boys in wave 1. Detailed information on the study’s design and methods have been published elsewhere [18, 19]. For the purposes of this study, we analyzed data from all four waves. Wave 1 was conducted from 2013 to 2014, while Wave 2 took place from 2015 to 2016. Waves 3 and 4 were conducted in 2020 and 2022/23, respectively. The questionnaire included sections covering various aspects of the participants’ lives, including socioeconomic factors, lifestyle choices, beliefs, and emotional well-being. Variables from various sections across these questionnaires were analyzed in relation to chronic STB. We conducted a retrospective cohort study involving males with history of lifetime suicidal thought, plan or attempt at Wave 1 and followed up in the subsequent three waves over a 9-year period.

Study variables and measurement

Outcomes

In this study, suicidal thought and behaviour (STB) includes suicidal thought, plan and/or attempt as defined as a “Yes” response to questions regarding suicidal thoughts, plans, or attempts. At Wave 1, STB was assessed using lifetime questions about suicidal thoughts, plans, and attempts. In Waves 2–4, it was evaluated using questions about suicidal thoughts, plans, and attempts within the past 12 months. Chronic STB was then defined as the presence of STB (in the past 12 months) during follow-up in Waves 2–4 among males who reported lifetime STB at Wave 1. Chronic STB in this study includes both persistence and recurrence of STBs.

In this study, we examined chronic suicidality using suicidal thoughts and suicide attempts as the primary outcomes. These variables represent the core cognitive and behavioural dimensions of chronic suicidality and are the most stable and clinically consequential indicators over time. Suicide plans were not included because they represent an intermediate and often transient stage between thoughts and behaviour, are less consistently measured across waves, and do not align as directly with the chronicity construct central to our research aims.

Predictor variables

Informed by interpersonal theory of suicide [6], we considered basic demographic factors, socioeconomic factors, health and substance use factors, and social and environmental factors as potential predictors of chronic suicidal thoughts and behaviors (STBs). Sociodemographic factors included age of the respondent at time of the surveys (in completed years), marital status (never married, divorced/separated/widowed, currently married), highest educational qualification (No completed qualification, certificate or diploma, bachelor degree, postgraduate, others), employment status (employed vs. not employed), and combined annual household income in Australian Dollars (categorized to low (< 40,000), middle (40,000–80,000), and high (above 80,0000)) based on the distribution of income within the sample to ensure adequate cell sizes across all four waves. These thresholds were selected for analytic stability rather than to represent socioeconomic advantage in the broader Australian context. Area-level factors included the remoteness of residence (categorized as major cities, inner regional and outer regional) and the Index of Relative Socio-Economic Disadvantage (IRSD), a measure used to assess the socio-economic disadvantage of a geographic area based on factors such as income, education, and employment, with lower scores indicating greater disadvantage [20]. Substance use factors considered were current smoking, alcohol use, and illicit substance use. Health-related factors included history diagnosis of depression and anxiety disorders in the past 12 months, and disability measured by Washington Group Short Set on social functioning [21]. Personal Wellbeing Index [22] and MOS (Medical Outcomes Study) social support score [23] were also included.

Statistical analysis

We conducted all data analyses using Stata v18.0 [24]. Descriptive statistics were calculated for all measures. To ensure adequate statistical power across waves, we used STB, defined as the presence of suicidal thoughts, plans, or attempts in the past 12 months, as the outcome variable. To identify baseline predictors of chronic STB at Wave 2 among males who reported lifetime STB at Wave 1, we used a modified Poisson regression model. To assess predictors of chronic STB across Waves 2–4 over the nine-year follow-up period, we applied a mixed-effects Poisson regression model with an unstructured covariance matrix and participants included as a random effect. This model included only males who were followed across all four waves. The outcome was defined as the presence of STB in the 12 months preceding each of Waves 2–4, with predictor variables assessed during the same waves. Both the outcome and predictor variables were allowed to vary over time. Selection of variables for the final models was based on theoretical considerations, sample size, and an assessment of multicollinearity using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). To enhance statistical power, we combined suicidal thoughts, plans, and attempts into a single outcome variable (STBs) for the regression analysis. Results were presented as incidence rate ratios (IRRs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and p-values.

Results

Our study included 3,070 males who reported any lifetime history of STBs at wave 1. The majority were married, employed, and resided in major cities. The prevalence rates for alcohol use disorder, a history of depression, and disability were 48%, 35%, and 14.9%, respectively. Suicidal thoughts

At wave 1, a total of 2,745 participants (17.1%) reported lifetime suicidal thoughts. Among these, 519 (18.9%) reported experiencing suicidal thoughts during the 12 months prior to wave 2. Overall, 362 (34.5%) of participants with lifetime suicidal thoughts at wave 1 who were followed up through wave 4 reported experiencing suicidal thoughts during the 12 months preceding at least one of waves 2–4.

Suicidal attempt

A total of 801 participants (5.0%) reported a lifetime suicide attempt at wave (1) Of these, 36 (4.5%) reported a suicide attempt in the 12 months prior to wave (2) Nearly 14% of males with a lifetime suicide attempt at wave 1, who were followed up through wave 4, reported experiencing a suicide attempt during the 12 months preceding at least one of waves 2–4.

Predictors of chronic STBs

Among males with lifetime STB at Wave 1 who were followed up at Wave 2, a history of depression was strongly associated with chronic suicidal thoughts and behaviors (STBs), with those reporting depression having nearly 1.6 times higher rates of chronic STB at Wave 2. Homosexual males had a 1.5-fold higher rate of chronic STB. Males with alcohol use disorder and those with disabilities both had a 21% higher rate of chronic STBs compared to their counterparts. Homosexual men had 50% higher rates of chronic STB at Wave 2 as compared to heterosexual men (IRR = 1.50; 95% CI:1.06,2.14; P = 0.023). Men with “other” sexuality had significantly higher rates of chronic suicidality (IRR = 2.54; 95% CI:1.34, 4.80; P = 0.004). Other predictors included the SEIFA index, where individuals with lower socioeconomic disadvantage (third quartile) had higher rates of chronic STBs, as well as the Personal Well-being Index and social support, both of which were negatively associated with chronic STBs. Discussion

We found that, among Australian men who report lifetime STB, more than one-third experience chronic STB over a nine-year period. The main predictors of chronic STBs were history of depressive disorders, alcohol use disorder, and disability. Additionally, social disadvantage, personal well-being, and social support played a role in the chronicity of suicidal behaviour.

Our findings are consistent with the findings of existing research which demonstrate a similarly high level of chronicity of such behaviour in more than a third of people with a baseline history of suicide ideation continuing to have it at some time over the next decade [9, 11]. A cross-national study demonstrated that 60% of transitions from ideation to plan and attempt occur within the first year after ideation onset [25]. This highlights the significant risk of eventual suicidal attempts in individuals with active suicidal thoughts, underscoring the need to not only study factors leading to suicidal attempts, but also those contributing to the chronicity of suicidal thoughts and planning. The same study found that almost all mental health disorders considered in the study, including depression and post-traumatic stress disorder, were found to be predictors of chronic suicidal behaviour [16].

Prior research has found that alcohol and drug use, depression, and disability are associated with STBs. Our study extends this by providing novel evidence that these risk factors are also linked to the chronicity of STBs. Depression, substance use, and alcohol use disorder had the largest effect effects on chronic STBs in our study. Depression is closely linked to chronic STB, with individuals experiencing recurrent or untreated depression at higher risk for persistent suicidal thoughts and actions. This interplay increases the likelihood of prolonged STB [8, 14, 26–28]. In our study, marijuana use significantly predicted chronic STBs. Repeated use of marijuana use often leads to depression, anxiety, and irritability during withdrawal, increasing the risk of chronic suicidality [29].

Homosexual men showed substantially higher rates of chronic suicidal thoughts and behaviours (STBs) at Wave 2, with rates approximately 50% higher than heterosexual men. Men identifying with “other” sexual orientation demonstrated even greater elevation, with rates more than twice those of heterosexual men. These findings align with extensive evidence that sexual minority groups experience elevated risk for suicidal distress, often attributed to minority stress processes, discrimination, and reduced access to affirming support services [30–32]. The particularly high rates among men in the “other” category may reflect additional vulnerabilities associated with less clearly defined or more socially marginalised identities.

Alcohol use disorder (AUD) is also strongly associated with chronic STB. AUD increases the risk of persistent suicidal thoughts and actions by exacerbating mental health issues like depression and anxiety, impairing judgment, and heightening impulsivity [9, 33, 34]. Similarly, disability is associated with higher rates of chronic STBs. This could be due to the psychological, social, and physical challenges it entails.

Our study has identified protective factors against chronic suicidal thoughts and behaviors (STBs). We found that males experiencing lower socioeconomic disadvantage, as indicated by being in the third SEIFA quartile, had higher rates of chronic STBs. This contrasts with previous research, which has typically reported a negative association between SEIFA and suicidal behavior [16, 35]. Additionally, personal well-being has been linked to a reduced risk of suicidal behavior [36, 37],, while social support has also been shown to have a protective effect [38, 39]. Our study builds on this evidence by demonstrating that these protective factors extend to the chronicity of suicidal behavior among males.

We observed a negative association between smoking and chronic suicidal thoughts and behaviours (STBs), even though this relationship did not meet the conventional P < 0.05 threshold. It has been well established that smoking increases the risk of suicidal ideation and attempts [40–42], and our prior analyses with the same data also reported a positive association between smoking and suicidal behaviour [41]. Additionally, research suggests that population-level smoking interventions may help reduce suicide risk [43]. The negative association observed in our study has resulted from adjusting for depression and substance use. Controlling for the relationship between mental illness, substance use and smoking, our analysis showed the independent effect of smoking on chronic suicidality. Nonetheless, this finding should be treated with strong caution, given the strength of prior findings on the positive association between smoking and suicide [40–42].

This study has some limitations. First, all data were self-reported, making recall bias and social desirability bias possible. Second, substantial dropout, which was associated with baseline STB, across the waves of the Ten to Men study may impact the generalizability of our findings. Due to this dropout, some subgroups, such as Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people, could not be included in the analysis due to insufficient statistical power. Stigma surrounding suicidal disclosure among men may have contributed to under-reporting of chronic STBs and to selective dropout across waves, particularly among those experiencing greater distress. As a result, prevalence estimates and longitudinal patterns may underestimate the true burden of chronic STBs in this population. Third, our definition of chronic STBs was broad, encompassing suicidal thoughts, plans, and attempts. However, it did not capture the transition from suicidal thoughts to plans and attempts over time.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study revealed that chronic STBs affect over a third of Australian males who have previously exhibited such behaviors. We identified four predictors of chronic STBs: depression, alcohol use disorder, disability, and illicit substance use. These findings emphasize the need for targeted interventions that address these factors to help reduce chronic STBs in this population. Further research is needed to explore how modifying these predictors may prevent future incidents of STB and contribute to more effective suicide prevention strategies.
