Low-Voltage Resistive Switching and Synaptic Plasticity in Cs2AgBiBr6 Memristors
Authors: Zhuang Miao, Ahmed Kadid, Alexander Löwen, Naveen Kolluru, Christian Tückmantel, Timo Maschwitz, Cedric Kreusel, Jamal Aziz, Stefan Janicke, Boqi Wu, Pang Wang, Thomas Riedl, Daniel Neumaier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Synaptic devices that can store and process data are imperative to mimic the operation of the human brain. In this work, lead-free double perovskite Cs2AgBiBr6 memristors were fabricated on ITO/glass substrates with thermally evaporated Ag top electrodes and systematically investigated for their resistive switching (RS) and neuromorphic functionalities. The devices exhibit stable, low-voltage (+0.239 ± 0.018 V) volatile resistive switching with good cycle-to-cycle reproducibility. A maximum ON/OFF ratio exceeding 105 is maintained for 24 cycles, while values above 102 persist for 1100 cycles. Electrical analysis reveals that the switching behavior is governed by ion migration-induced filament formation, primarily involving mobile Br– ions, VBr + vacancies, and Ag+ ions injected from the electrode. A detailed conduction model is proposed based on space charge-limited conduction and ion-induced electric field screening. Furthermore, the volatile RS characteristics enable biologically inspired synaptic functions, including spike amplitude-dependent plasticity (SADP), spike number-dependent plasticity (SNDP), and paired-pulse facilitation (PPF) with a maximum facilitation index of 169%, benchmarking competitively among perovskite memristive synapses. Based on experimentally extracted synaptic weight-update rules, an artificial neural network simulation achieved a peak recognition accuracy of 98%.

Introduction

Regardless of whether current artificial intelligence development continues toward ever-larger neural networks or shifts to compact models enabled by optimized algorithms, global energy demand from computing systems is expected to continue rising. Neuromorphic computing, inspired by the brain’s extraordinary energy efficiency, offers a promising approach for overcoming the limitations of the traditional von Neumann architecture. To emulate the key components of the brain, biological neurons and synapses, various chip-level implementations have been developed, including complementary metal oxide semiconductor (CMOS)-compatible silicon transistors, perovskite photodetectors, or transition metal oxide memristors.

Among all the possible solutions for neuromorphic computing systems, perovskite-based memristors attracted great interest due to their added optoelectronic opportunities, the facile preparation, and the electronic–ionic transport properties similar to those of biological synapses. However, widely studied lead halide-based perovskites, such as MAPbI3, raise the concern about the toxicity of Pb. Another issue is their rapid degradation under various kinds of stresses, such as ambient air or elevated temperatures. , On the contrary, the lead-free double perovskite compound Cs2AgBiBr6 of the form A2BB/X6 has shown superior thermal and moisture stability. Previous research has demonstrated that memristors configured in an Au/Cs2AgBiBr6/ITO sandwich-like structure exhibit remarkable robustness under harsh environmental conditions, even when exposed to high temperatures of 180 °C or upon direct exposure to a flame from an alcohol burner. Besides the outstanding stability, Cs2AgBiBr6 reveals its potential as an artificial synapse with synaptic characteristics under electrical pulses, self-storage visual sensors coupling light detection and memory functions, or even an all-in-one system integrating sensing, memory, and computing together.

Despite the rapid expansion of proposed applications, the core device performance of Cs2AgBiBr6 memristors, especially their ON/OFF ratio and maximum cycling endurance, remained limited (Table S1). Although several approaches have been followed, such as doping Cs2AgBiBr6 with Nd or substituting Br with Cl, the intrinsic material stability alone does not provide a reliable basis for optimizing the endurance of the memristive behavior. For MAPbI3-based memristors, the resistive switching behavior can originate from the formation of a conductive filament (CF) based on iodine vacancies, while the activity of the metal electrode plays a crucial role. However, these mechanisms cannot be directly applied to memristors based on Cs2AgBiBr6, given substantial differences in the crystal structure, defect chemistry, and ion migration dynamics. Consequently, in double perovskite-based memristive devices, the underlying operation and degradation mechanisms remain an open and actively investigated research area.

In this work, we have fabricated volatile memristive devices based on the Ag/Cs2AgBiBr6/ITO/glass structure with up-to-date performance, including their applicability as electrical synapses. The devices exhibit volatile filamentary RS behavior with a low operating power of 204 pW at Vth = 0.20 V, together with a high ON/OFF ratio of ∼105. Furthermore, a reversible RS property (>1100 cycles) while keeping the ON/OFF ratio as stable as ∼102 is achieved, which allows us to analyze the degradation mechanism originating from the formation and increment of an AgBr layer. Furthermore, based on the synaptic performance indicated by the spike amplitude-dependent plasticity (SADP), spike number-dependent plasticity (SNDP), and a paired-pulse facilitation (PPF) index of 169%, indicating good plasticity among ever-reported perovskite-based synapses, an accuracy of 98% was achieved via simulation of a simple artificial neural network (ANN) on a handwritten number classification recognition test.

Results and Discussion

The Cs2AgBiBr6-based memristors were fabricated on a glass substrate with an indium tin oxide (ITO) back electrode and a Ag top electrode as described in the method section. The double perovskite film composition was initially investigated using energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopy, which yielded a composition of Cs:Ag:Bi:Br = 2:1:1:6 (see the Supporting Information). Additionally, the layers were characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM), atomic force microscopy (AFM), and X-ray diffraction (XRD). From the SEM and AFM images in Figure a,b, we observed that a homogeneous, uniform, and pinhole-free Cs2AgBiBr6 film with a root-mean-square (RMS) roughness of 10.2 nm has been formed. A cross-sectional SEM image and EDX mappings for Cs, In, and Si are shown in Figure c. The corresponding XRD spectrum is labeled according to the work of Greul et al., as illustrated in Figure d, where no additional reflections of side phases are detected. After depositing the perovskite film, Ag electrodes were deposited by thermal evaporation using shadow masks. With and without the electrode, the perovskite film shows no optically observable changes after 2 weeks as shown in Figures S1 and S2, confirming the excellent stability of the films.

To investigate the RS characteristics, I–V sweeps were performed on Cs2AgBiBr6 devices by biasing the top Ag electrode while keeping the bottom ITO electrode grounded. Figure a shows a sweep with the sequence of 0 V → −1.8 V → 0 V→ + 1.4 V→ 0 V. The devices exhibit a clear RS behavior at negative and positive bias. From 0 V to ∼ −0.5 V, only a low current (in the nA range) is observed, followed by a sudden rise during the “activation/conditioning” sweep reaching the compliance current (C.C) of 0.1 mA. This transient current at ∼ −0.5 V diminishes in subsequent sweeps. The current then increases again near −1.1 V, reaching C.C, and decreases during the back sweep to 0 V (arrow 2). In the positive region, a distinct “set” process appears at ∼ +0.3 V, where the device switches from a high-resistance state (HRS) to a low-resistance state (LRS). During the reverse sweep back to 0 V (arrow 4), the current decreases gradually. The observed voltage for the set process is then defined as threshold voltage (Vth) for volatile switching, given the absence of the reset process. To assess scan rate effects on RS behavior, the sweep rate was varied from 0.1 to 10 V s–1 by adjusting the per-point dwell time while keeping other measurement parameters constant (Figure S3). Increasing the scan rate shifts Vth from 0.25 to 0.325 V and markedly suppresses the HRS current at 0.4 V (from 9.58 to 2.76 mA), suggesting a strong influence of time-dependent processes such as ion migration and accumulation. 1 V s–1 was selected for all subsequent I–V measurements throughout this work. Previous reports indicated that a transition of volatile to nonvolatile RS behavior can appear with increased C.C. To assess volatility, sweeps at 0.1, 1, and 10 mA C.C were performed. The switching trend remains similar, with higher C.C increasing the current level but leaving Vth nearly unchanged. The retention test (Figure S4) further supports a volatile RS mechanism. The ON/OFF ratio extracted at 0.1 V was 71, 247, 701, 605, and 241 for C.C. of 100 μA, 350 μA, 1 mA, 3.5 mA, and 10 mA, respectively (Figure S5). We therefore selected 1 mA C.C with the highest ON/OFF ratio for the following endurance test.

(a) Volatile resistive switching (RS) sweep under different C.C; (b) multiple volatile RS switching cycles under identical conditions; (c) extracted HRS and LRS for 1100 cycles at Vread = 0.1 V (consecutive sweeps with a C.C of 1 mA); (d) double logarithmic plot for the positive-biased region; and (e) double-log plot for the negative-biased region of the sweep with 10 mA C.C in (a).

To evaluate the operational stability, a multiple biasing cycling test was performed on fresh devices with a reduced sweep range of −1.2 to +0.8 V at 1 mA C.C, inserting a 5 s pause between two cycles to minimize crosstalk between cycles. A clear memory window persists in the positive-biased region for up to 1100 cycles, whereas the current states in the negative-biased region fluctuate (Figures b and S6). Notably, by the 1000th cycle, the current toward the negative voltage vertex exceeds the current measured in backward sweep to 0 V (arrow 1*); a current peak appears at −0.425 V, which was absent in the first and 100th cycles (arrow: “Reduction”). After 1100 cycles, the device maintains its RS behavior with an ON/OFF ratio of >102 (Figure S6). Across all 1100 cycles, the LRS remains stable, while the HRS conductance arises (Figure c).

Device-to-device variability was examined to identify fabrication-related variations. First cycle I–V curves from 105 devices produced across independent fabrication batches show highly uniform switching behavior and Vth (Figure S7). The Vth values across the devices follow a normal distribution centered at +0.261 ± 0.021 V by the first batch and +0.239 ± 0.018 V by the second batch, indicating good reproducibility. This consistency is further reflected in the yield rates of 87 and 96%, respectively. Moreover, the initial RS response of the devices from the second batch yields a high ON/OFF ratio (1.11 ± 0.15)*106, along with low switching power consumption down to 204 pW enabled by a low HRS current of 1.017 nA at Vth +0.2 V. After continuous 24 switching cycles on a single device, the HRS conductance increased from 10–8 S to >10–4 S (Figure S8), mirroring the behavior seen after the “activation/conditioning” sweep in Figures b and S6. This indicates that the activation sweep of forming the conduction pathways initially is unnecessary, confirming the intrinsically forming-free nature of the devices.

On the basis of the observations stated previously, we next analyze the RS mechanisms. Depending on the device configuration, conventionally, RS in perovskites is classified as either filamentary or an interface type. Filamentary switching occurs via CFs governed by either electrochemical metallization (ECM) reactions of metal ions/atoms or oxygen/halide vacancies (valence change memory, VCM), often with inert electrodes. Interface-type switching originates from ion/defect accumulation at the perovskite/electrode interface, e.g., with inert electrodes or organic interlayers. In Cs2AgBiBr6, VAg – and VBr + vacancies are the dominating defects depending on initial growth conditions, , and halide ions such as Br– play a key role in ionic conduction. Compared to the low diffusion barrier of 0.17 eV for iodine vacancies (VI +) in MAPbI3, VBr + vacancies in MAPbBr3 exhibit a slightly higher diffusion barrier of 0.20 eV. In the double perovskite Cs2AgBiBr6, while the diffusion barrier for VBr + increases further to 0.33 eV, these vacancies remain the dominating mobile species. Therefore, filament formation and annihilation of the devices can be attributed primarily to VBr + migration. Because switching to the LRS occurs when the Ag electrode is positively biased, the principal CF constituent should be positively charged carriers, e.g., Ag+ ions and VBr + vacancies, similar to prior research on the MAPbI3 memristor. , Due to the high activation energy for the migration of VAg –, Ag from the perovskite lattice is likely immobile, while injected Ag+ feasibly migrates via filling the generated VAg – sites. The low Vth of the device further suggests the source of Ag+ ions from spontaneously grown AgBr instead of a pure Ag electrode. The physical form of the spontaneously formed AgBr is difficult to determine, that is, whether it exists as a continuous ultrathin interfacial layer or as localized flakes at grain boundaries. Nevertheless, the presence of AgBr prior to electrical biasing is consistent with the observed low Vth and the forming-free resistive switching behavior of the devices. The absence of a characteristic reduction reaction in the I–V response in Figure a suggests that CFs are mainly composed of VBr + at the initial state. The observed current peak for the 1000th cycle at −0.37 V in Figure b can be attributed to the electrochemical reduction of Ag+ because the resulting AgBr accumulation at the top electrode/perovskite interface leads to an increase of conductance of the HRS during cycling in Figure c.

To distinguish the kinetics in our devices, I–V curves were analyzed using piecewise log–log fitting as shown in Figure . In the positive-biased region (Figure d), current initially increases linearly with a slope of 1.05, showing ohmic conduction, before a transition at Vth ≈ +0.2 V, which leads to a slope of 3.91, indicating halide and active metal ion migration. Backward sweep yields a slope of 1.21, suggesting ohmic conduction with moderate carrier trapping or detrapping. In the negatively biased region (Figure e), the conduction varies through several regimes. At the low-bias region (0 to −0.35 V), a slope of 1.41 indicates a trap-controlled space charge-limited conduction (SCLC) regime, consistent with the field-driven drift of VBr + and Br– toward the Ag and ITO electrodes, respectively. The trap states in the Cs2AgBiBr6 film are gradually filled with carriers. Between −0.35 and −1 V, described as the medium-biased region (blue-shaded), the slope drops to 0.33, suggesting that accumulated VBr + and Br– at the perovskite/electrode interface generate an inner electric field, which screens the external electric field like in a capacitor. Such ion-induced field screening is well studied in perovskite-based solar cells and is recognized as a key factor influencing operational stability. Beyond −1 V, a short transition phase with a slope value of 2.1 (trap-filled SCLC) is followed by a rapid current increase, indicating trap-filled limited (TFL) conduction with its slope of 9.36. This sharp rise suggests that once the external field exceeds the ion-induced screening, migration of VBr+ and Br– inside the perovskite (bulk) proceeds more rapidly than at the interface, causing redistribution of the inner electric field and a pronounced current increase. On the reverse sweep, slopes of 7.10, 1.5, and 0.99 correspond to TFL conduction, trap-filling SCLC, and ohmic conduction, respectively. These regimes reflect the migration of halide ions and vacancies in the direction opposite to that of the forward sweep, without the formation of a screening electric field as the applied bias decreases.

We propose an ion migration-driven RS mechanism as shown in Figure , involving three dominant processes: (i) ion drift under applied field polarity; (ii) diffusion of local aggregated ions/vacancies during both the brief pause at 0 V (intracycle pause) and the longer pause between two cycles (intercycle pause); and (iii) electrochemical reaction between Ag+ and Br–. Since the reduction of Ag+ was observed when the top silver electrode is negatively biased (Figure b), oxidation of the Ag atom into Ag+ ions happens under positive biasing. The presence of the reactant AgBr layer could reduce the Vth, leading to less energy consumption as reported in metal/MAPbI3 21; however, continuous AgBr growth depletes Br–, accelerates perovskite degradation, and further damages the devices. Importantly, further formation of AgBr, i.e., redox reaction of Ag+ with Br–, requires an applied bias as reported in Ag/MAPbBr3.

Figure illustrates the proposed conduction mechanism. In the “initial” state of a pristine device, Br– ions and VBr + vacancies are uniformly distributed as shown in Figure a. During the negative bias, the resistance remains stable and is only governed by Br–/VBr + drifting, with no evidence of Ag+ redox activity as shown in Figure b,c. When the voltage sweep crosses 0 V, the mobile ionic species reverse their drift direction and must traverse the full perovskite layer thickness (Figure d,e). The long path reduces Br– accumulation at the Ag/perovskite interface. Once the bias exceeds the necessary potential of the activation energy of Ag+ in AgBr, Ag+ ions migrate toward the ITO electrode, eventually connecting with VBr + clusters to form CFs and switch the device to the LRS, as depicted in Figure f. As mentioned previously, the CF is composed mainly of VBr +, while the ratio of injected Ag+ rises during cycling. During intracycle pauses, Ag+ ions injected during the positive bias get diffused into the VAg – site, accompanied by redistribution of Br–/VBr + clusters, which destabilizes and ruptures the CF, giving rise to the observed volatile response (Figure g). Although an ideal cycle would start from a fully recovered initial state, residual Ag+ can persist and react with Br– near the Ag/perovskite interface under the subsequent negative bias (Figure h). The intracycle pauses lower Ag+/Br– concentration at the Ag/perovskite interface and further suppress the AgBr growth by the next cycle. In summary, the degradation of Cs2AgBiBr6 memristors primarily arises from AgBr layer growth during cycling. While AgBr lowers Vth by introducing preoxidized Ag, the growth of it consumes Br–, thus damaging the perovskite and degrading the RS endurance. A further analysis with TOF-SIMS/XPS depth profiling, TEM-EDX, transited PL imaging, or a van der Waals transferred electrode in combination with c-AFM can be promising and bring more impact on our current explanation for the RS mechanism.

Based on the previous results, we focus on the synaptic response of volatile endurable devices to mimic biological synapses as a possible application. Biological synapses, as fundamental building blocks of neuronal networks, are the bridge transiting information between the presynaptic neurons and postsynaptic neurons. Strength of the connection is determined as the synaptic weight, and the ability of changing this strength is defined as synaptic plasticity. Synaptic plasticity, which causes variable postsynaptic currents (PSCs) in response to stimulus from presynaptic neurons, plays a crucial role in biological learning behavior. As shown in Figure a, a biological synapse consists of a presynaptic membrane, a postsynaptic membrane, and the synaptic cleft. Analogously, in our device, the Ag electrode, Cs2AgBiBr6 perovskite, and ITO electrode mimic the presynaptic membrane, synapse cleft, and postsynaptic membrane, respectively. Ionic/electronic transport of Ag+, Br–, and VBr – imitates neurotransmitter dynamics. Synaptic plasticity can be evaluated through spike-dependent plasticity (SDP), which relates to spiking features such as spike amplitude-dependent plasticity (SADP), spike number-dependent plasticity (SNDP), or through spike timing-dependent plasticity (STDP), where synaptic weight change depends on the sequence and interval of pre- and postsynaptic neural activity. To investigate the SADP behavior, we apply five consecutive pulses with period = 1 ms and pulse width = 500 μs. The amplitude ranges from +0.4 to +0.6 V with a step size of 0.1 V, in order to get excitatory postsynapse current (EPSC) as shown in Figure b. No excitation occurs at a pulse amplitude of +0.4 V. The PSC shows two sharp peaks and drops to its initial state for all the five pulses, indicating that the device behaves as a leaky capacitor. For pulses with an amplitude of +0.5 V, EPSC was first observed at the third pulse, while under +0.6 V pulse amplitude, the maximum EPSC corresponding with each pulse is 303 μA, 654 μA, 1.16 mA, 1.35 mA, and 1.43 mA. The device maintains this facilitated response at +0.6 V up to 100 kHz (Figure S10), in sharp contrast to the purely capacitive response at +0.4 V. These results demonstrate that the device successfully emulates the SADP behavior, where the postsynaptic response is gated by spike amplitude rather than timing alone.

(a) Schematic illustration comparing biological synapse and double perovskite memristive devices in the initial state; (b) spike amplitude-dependent plasticity (SADP) shown with response of postsynaptic current under different amplitudes of electrical pulses; and (c) spike number-dependent plasticity (SNDP) shown with the response of PSCs under different numbers of electrical pulses.

We have further evaluated the SNDP behavior by applying sequences of consecutive pulses. All the pulses have a fixed amplitude of +0.4 V, a width of 500 μs, and a period of 1 ms. As shown in Figure c, no excitation is detected with 5 pulses, and EPSC occurs with 10 pulses starting from the sixth pulse. EPSC increases continuously with the number of pulses without a sign of saturation. This increase in EPSC with repeated pulses indicates short-term plasticity (STP) characteristic of SNDP. While STDP further evaluates synaptic function based on stimulus timing, paired-pulse facilitation (PPF) quantifies STDP by comparing PSC magnitudes from paired stimuli. The PPF index can be described via the following eq 1 PPF⁢index=((A2−A1)/A1)×100% where A 1 and A 2 are the peak values of EPSC of the first and second pulse stimulus, respectively. As shown in Figure a, during the PPF index measurement, the pulse amplitude is set to +0.6 V for higher EPSCs. With a time interval (Δt) of 0.3 ms, a maximum PPF index of 169% was achieved. Compared with previously reported PPF indices for perovskite-based devices (Table S3), our double perovskite devices present the highest PPF index values among perovskite-based memristors, indicating good synaptic plasticity. Applying the paired presynaptic pulses with different time intervals ranging from 0.3 to 1000 ms, double-exponential function (eq ) fits well with the extracted PPF index values. 2 PPF⁢index=1+C1exp(−Δt/τ1)+C2exp(−Δt/τ2) where C 1 and C 2 are inherited from the biological synapse as the initial facilitation constants and τ1 and τ2 are considered the fast and slow characteristic relaxation time, respectively. The extracted values of τ1 and τ2 were 0.28 and 81.5 ms (Figure b), respectively, with 2 orders of magnitude difference. It is worth mentioning that due to the capacitive characteristics of our devices, instability in A1 values causes fluctuation of the PPF index with the time interval Δt > 300 ms. Therefore, the fitted PPF index function has achieved a coefficient of determination value of ∼0.87. Weight changing function is then extracted with Gaussian functions as eq 3 ΔW=W0+Aexp(−Δt/τ) where w 0 summarizes the nonassociate part for synaptic weight update, while A and τ are the fitted parameters. Short-term potentiation and depression are evaluated via repetitive pulse training (Figure S11). Owing to the volatile nature of our devices, absence of long-term potentiation (LTP) and long-term memory (LTM) is expected; however, this eliminates energy consumption associated with memory erasure. Recent studies have demonstrated that such volatile dynamics can be effectively exploited in reservoir computing for both static pattern recognition and dynamic gesture classification. Based on the fitted weight updating functions (Figure S11), we constructed a simulated ANN (artificial neural network) for evaluating accuracy by pattern recognition and as a possible application. In the MNIST (Modified National Institute of Standards and Technology) simulation, 784 input neurons were connected to 128 hidden neurons with weights adjusted from PPF-derived functions, using a simple linear activation instead of ReLu (rectified linear unit) to reduce computation costs and to simplify the hardware implementation. While 128 hidden neurons require uniform device performance, algorithm–hardware codesign can drastically reduce array size requirements, as demonstrated by a 3 × 3 synapse array enabling obstacle avoidance when integrated with an optical sensor and micro control. To assess the uncertainty effect, small fluctuations in A and τ were introduced into the weight-update function. Due to the high synaptic plasticity indicated by the PPF index of our devices, the recognition accuracy from 10 sets of training process starts from 79.10 to 90.46% by the first epoch and converges quickly. At the 35th epoch, recognition accuracy achieves a maximum value of 97.48%, while the average value is 97.07% as shown in Figure d. A peak value by 98.03% at the end of 100 epochs is achieved (Figure S12). These results verify the future potential of lead-free double perovskite memristors for neuromorphic computing, particularly in architecture leveraging dynamic, short-term plasticity.

Conclusions

In this work, we demonstrated reliable volatile memristive behavior in lead-free Cs2AgBiBr6-based perovskite memristors and established a detailed mechanism for their resistive switching and degradation under electrical cycling. The device successfully mimics key synaptic functions, which include spike amplitude-dependent plasticity (SADP) and spike number-dependent plasticity (SNDP) under different amplitudes and numbers of electrical pulses, respectively. A record high paired-pulse facilitation (PPF) index of 169% was achieved, representing strong synaptic plasticity. Through electrical transport analysis, we revealed that the RS behavior is governed by field-driven migration of Br– ions, VBr + vacancies, and injected Ag+ ions, accompanied by electrochemical reactions leading to gradual AgBr layer formation. While the presence of AgBr lowers the operating voltage, its continuous growth contributes to performance degradation by depleting halide ions and damaging the perovskite structure. Leveraging this combination of low operating voltage, forming-free operation, and strong short-term plasticity in a lead-free material, a hardware-inspired neural network simulation based on experimentally derived device parameters achieved high recognition accuracy up to 98%, providing proof-of-concept validation for the potential of this device in neuromorphic computing applications.

Experimental Section

Preparation of the Cs2AgBiBr6 Precursor Solution

To prepare 0.5 M Cs2AgBiBr6 precursor solution with a stoichiometry of CsBr, AgBr, and BiBr3 (2:1:1), we dissolved 212.81 mg of CsBr (99.99%, Sigma-Aldrich), 93.9 mg of AgBr (99.99%, Alfa Aesar), and 224.35 mg of BiBr3 (99.99%, Alfa Aesar) in 1 mL of anhydrous dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich). The mixture was continuously stirred at 80 °C until a clear, homogeneous solution was obtained. The solution was prepared inside a nitrogen-filled glovebox.

Device Fabrication

The double perovskite memristors mentioned in this paper have been fabricated on top of a laser-cut 19.8 × 19.8 mm glass substrate (15 Ω sq–1) with 150 nm ITO from Advanced Election Technology Co., Ltd., China. ITO-coated glass substrates were cleaned in an ultrasonic bath with acetone and isopropanol, 15 min in each. After the cleaning, substrates were dried with a nitrogen (N2) gas flow. Before bringing into the N2-filled glovebox (Braun, Lab Master), a UV-ozone cleaner was employed for improving the surface hydrophilicity. Prior to perovskite film deposition, both the substrate and the Cs2AgBiBr6 precursor solution were heated to 80 °C to facilitate uniform crystallization during film formation. 100 μL of the preheated perovskite solution was then dispensed onto the preheated substrate and spin-coated at 2000 rpm for 75 s. After 30 s of spin coating, a N2 gas flow at 7 bar pressure, filtered through a 5 μm PTFE filter, was directed perpendicularly onto the wet film from a distance of 10 cm and maintained until the end of the spin coating process to induce intermediate formation. Gas quenching was used to accelerate solvent evaporation and to promote uniform crystal formation. The resulting films were subsequently annealed at 250 °C for 10 min to obtain homogeneous Cs2AgBiBr6 layers. After finishing Cs2AgBiBr6 deposition, 150 nm of silver electrode is thermally evaporated on the perovskite layer via a shadow mask. The diameter of the evaporated electrodes is 300 μm, defined per shadow mask.

Perovskite Film Characterization

The resulting films were characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM), using a ZEISS Crossbeam 550 system, and the respective elemental analysis was conducted by energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) using the integrated EDX detector. Surface topography was characterized by atomic force microscopy (AFM) by using a Bruker Innova system. X-ray diffraction (XRD) was performed by using a Bruker D2 Phaser diffractometer equipped with a LynxEye detector.

Device Characterization

The cross-sectional images of the devices are captured with a ZEISS Crossbeam 550 focused ion beam scanning electron microscope (FIB-SEM) with a gallium ion source. For the IV characterization, a home-built ambient optoelectrical probe station together with a Keithley 4200A-SCS parameter analyzer is employed. Depending on the demand of measurement, probe tips are landed on the top silver electrode and connected with the 4200-MPSMU and 4200-PA for the low-current DC measurement or the 4225-PMU and 4225-remote amplifier for the pulse measurement. The optoelectrical measurement is conducted with a UV LED (Thorlabs, M385L3) as the light source. The light irradiance is adjusted via an input current controller (Thorlabs, LEDD1B) and measured with a power detector (Thorlabs, PM101A) at the sample level on the probe station.

ANN Simulation

In the simulation, the MNIST (Modified National Institute of Standards and Technology) database is selected due to its widespread application. Each handwritten digit image from the 60,000 training samples, originally in 28 × 28 grayscale pixels, is converted into vector with a size of 1 × 784. 784 input neurons corresponding to each pixel are linearly connected to the hidden layer with 128 neurons, with the weights adjusted using the function derived from the PPF index values. To minimize computational demands, we use a simple linear activation function between the hidden and output layers instead of ReLu (rectified linear unit) function. After each training epoch, we evaluate the trained model by recognizing 10,000 test images. For better evaluation of how uncertainty from the fitting curve affects the recognition accuracy brought, we add small fluctuation (10%) to the A and τ values in the weight changing function. These fluctuations are chosen from the variance interval obtained from the extracted weight update function at the start of each training process.
