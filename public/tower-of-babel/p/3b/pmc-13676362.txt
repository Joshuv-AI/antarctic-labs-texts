Occult Proximal Femur Fractures Mimicking Lumbar Spinal Stenosis
Authors: Masahiro Kawanishi, Naokado Ikeda, Hidekazu Tanaka, Kunio Yokoyama, Yutaka Ito
Published: 2026

Musculoskeletal disorders that can mimic lumbar spine disease have been systematically described in the literature1). Within this broad differential, occult proximal femur fractures (OPFFs) are particularly important because diagnostic delay is not merely academic: continued activity or weight bearing may allow an initially occult or stable fracture to progress over time to a complete or displaced fracture, resulting in irreversible functional decline and a more invasive treatment course.

A major pitfall is that lumbar magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) frequently demonstrates stenosis that may be incidental rather than causative. When clinicians anchor on such findings, an extra-spinal pain generator can be overlooked, especially in patients presenting with prolonged thigh or knee pain without a clear traumatic trigger. We report two educational cases of OPFFs initially misdiagnosed as lumbar spinal stenosis; notably, in one case, the lesion progressed to a complete fracture during the diagnostic and treatment interval.

In the first case, a patient presented with persistent thigh pain and underwent lumbar MRI at a referring hospital, which demonstrated lumbar spinal canal stenosis (Figure 1). Pelvic MRI was also obtained but was initially interpreted as not demonstrating definitive abnormal findings. Conservative management, including rehabilitation, was continued for more than one month without improvement, and the patient was referred to our institution. Neurological examination was unremarkable, and provocative lumbar maneuvers did not reproduce the pain. The Patrick (FABER) test was negative, whereas weight-bearing provocation with a single-leg stance test elicited hip-region pain on the affected side. Because the clinical pattern was not typical of neurogenic claudication or radiculopathy, we rereviewed the previously obtained pelvic MRI in detail and identified findings consistent with an OPFF. While the patient awaited hip surgery, the lesion progressed to a complete fracture. The patient was subsequently treated by a joint specialist.

In the second case, the patient presented with anterior thigh and knee pain without any inciting event. Lumbar MRI demonstrated only mild spinal canal stenosis (Figure 2), and conservative treatment was initiated. Because symptoms persisted, the patient was referred to our department. Neurological findings were minimal and did not suggest a single-root radiculopathy. As in the first case, the Patrick (FABER) test was negative, whereas the single-leg stance test reproduced hip-region pain on the affected side. Plain radiographs of the pelvis and hips showed no obvious fracture. Pelvic MRI demonstrated signal changes in the proximal femur consistent with an OPFF, and the patient was subsequently treated by a joint (hip) specialist.

These cases highlight a key diagnostic pitfall in spine practice: anchoring to degenerative findings on lumbar MRI, where stenosis may be incidental rather than causative. In such situations, a hip-origin pain generator can be overlooked—especially when routine hip load or range-of-motion tests are negative or difficult to interpret (e.g., in wheelchair users), when pain radiates to the lower leg, or when there is no clear traumatic history. In OPFFs, routine hip provocation or range-of-motion maneuvers such as the Patrick (FABER) test may be nondiagnostic, whereas weight-bearing provocation (single-leg stance) can reproduce hip-region pain, as observed in both of our cases.

In older adults with suspected occult hip or pelvic injury despite normal radiographs, occult fractures remain common2). MRI is generally the preferred modality when available; if MRI cannot be performed promptly, computed tomography (CT) is an appropriate initial alternative, with further investigation when symptoms persist despite negative CT findings3). Although classic OPFF findings include inability to lift the leg in the supine position and tenderness around the femoral triangle or greater trochanter with pain on hip rotation, functional impairment may be mild and some patients can still ambulate, increasing the risk of misdiagnosis3,4).

Importantly, pain below the knee does not exclude a hip-origin source. Hip-related referred pain may extend to the anterior tibial region5,6). Accordingly, in patients presenting with persistent thigh or knee pain with minimal neurological findings—particularly when the pain pattern does not align with lumbar imaging—early hip-focused imaging should be pursued to avoid delayed diagnosis and fracture progression2,3).

A key aspect of the originality of this report lies in clarifying why spine surgeons may misdiagnose OPFFs as lumbar spinal pathology. This diagnostic error is largely driven by cognitive bias, particularly anchoring bias toward incidental findings on lumbar MRI. Because degenerative lumbar spinal stenosis is highly prevalent in older adults, there is a tendency to prematurely attribute symptoms to these imaging findings, even when they do not fully explain the clinical presentation.

Several factors contribute to this pitfall. First, lumbar MRI abnormalities may be overinterpreted without sufficient clinical correlation. Second, neurological findings are often minimal or nonspecific in these patients, making it difficult to localize symptoms to a spinal origin. Third, commonly used hip provocation tests, such as the Patrick (FABER) test, may be negative, leading clinicians to falsely exclude hip pathology. Finally, referred pain from the hip, which may extend to the knee or even below, is frequently underrecognized in spine practice.

Therefore, careful interpretation of lumbar MRI findings is essential, with particular attention to the possibility that such findings may be incidental rather than causative. In patients with atypical symptom patterns or poor concordance between imaging and clinical findings, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for extra-spinal causes. Early use of pelvic MRI or CT should be considered to avoid delayed diagnosis and prevent progression to complete fracture. Clinicians should be particularly alert when lumbar MRI findings do not fully explain the patient's symptoms, especially in cases with persistent thigh or knee pain, minimal neurological deficits, or negative conventional hip provocation tests.
