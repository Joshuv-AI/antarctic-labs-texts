Identifying CTSF and GSTM3 as chemoresistance therapeutic targets in breast cancer through multi-omics MR analysis
Authors: Rui Liu, Ying Yao, Zhihua Lv, Jingtao Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBreast cancer is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with drug resistance remaining a major therapeutic challenge. We integrated multi-omics data—including GWAS, eQTL, and pQTL—with Mendelian randomization to identify genes associated with breast cancer risk and therapy response. Our analyses revealed CTSF and GSTM3 as key candidates, showing consistent associations at both RNA and protein levels. Functional studies indicated that CTSF expression correlates with taxane resistance but cyclophosphamide sensitivity, while GSTM3 influences response across multiple regimens. Enrichment analyses suggest roles in immune and hormone signaling pathways. These findings highlight CTSF and GSTM3 as potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for overcoming chemoresistance in breast cancer.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BRCA) remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality globally, highlighting the urgent need for diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.1 Despite advances in treatment options such as surgery, chemotherapy, radiation, and targeted therapies, a significant number of BRCA patients still face poor outcomes due to therapy resistance and disease recurrence.2,3 Genetic and molecular factors play a critical role in disease progression and treatment response, offering valuable opportunities to identify potential therapeutic targets.4,5

Chemotherapy remains a key component in systemic BRCA management, commonly integrating anthracyclines (such as doxorubicin and epirubicin), taxanes (such as paclitaxel and docetaxel), and alkylating agents (such as cyclophosphamide) to target rapidly dividing tumor cells.6 For patients with hormone receptor (HR)–positive disease, endocrine therapies—such as selective estrogen receptor modulators (tamoxifen), aromatase inhibitors (letrozole), and selective estrogen receptor degraders (fulvestrant)—have significantly improved outcomes.7 The advent of HER2 (human epidermal growth factor receptor 2)-targeted treatments (e.g., trastuzumab and pertuzumab) has similarly transformed care for HER2-positive BRCA subtypes, markedly reducing recurrence and mortality.8 Despite these advances, many patients eventually develop resistance, driven by mechanisms including drug efflux, adaptive signaling pathway activation, and metabolic changes, leading to therapy failure and disease progression. These challenges highlight the pressing need for targets and improved strategies to overcome or delay chemoresistance (i.e., therapy resistance) in BRCA, as well as resistance to endocrine or HER2-directed therapies.

Recent genome-wide association studies have uncovered multiple genetic loci linked to BRCA risk, complemented by insights from transcriptomic data.9 However, translating these findings into clinically actionable strategies remains challenging.10 For instance, although eQTL (expression quantitative trait loci) and pQTL (protein quantitative trait loci) analyses have identified genetic variants affecting gene expression and protein levels, respectively, their clinical relevance and underlying biological mechanisms still require further validation.11 Moreover, the functional roles of many identified variants in BRCA, particularly within the context of precision oncology, remain largely unexplored.

In this study, we harness a multi-omics framework that combines eQTL and pQTL data with MR (Mendelian randomization) to pinpoint clinically actionable genes associated with BRCA. By integrating large-scale genomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic datasets, we aimed to move beyond correlative associations and establish causal links to disease risk. We then investigate the biological and clinical relevance of these candidate genes, focusing on their roles in tumor progression and drug resistance. This approach highlights therapeutic targets and provides a more rigorous, mechanism-guided strategy for overcoming resistance and improving patient outcomes in BRCA.

Results

Discovery of potential cis-eQTL genes and BRCA

By integrating data from the eQTLGen consortium, UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project (UKB-PPP), and deCODE datasets, we identified a set of genes that are significant at both the RNA and protein levels. Initially, our discovery phase revealed 16,989 cis-eQTL genes from the eQTLGen consortium. These findings were subsequently validated using 1,974 cis-pQTL proteins from UKB-PPP and 1,703 cis-pQTL proteins from deCODE. Our analysis showed that 851 genes were shared between eQTLGen and UKB-PPP, 654 genes overlapped between eQTLGen and deCODE, 241 genes were common to both UKB-PPP and deCODE, and 644 genes were consistently identified across the summary data-based MR (SMR), UKB-PPP, and deCODE datasets.

We conducted SMR analysis and the heterogeneity in dependent instruments (HEIDI) test within the discovery cohort, which consisted of 20,586 BRCA cases and 201,494 controls from the FinnGen cohort. Table S1 details the genetic variants of eQTLs used in the discovery phase, where we identified 1,167 significant associations with BRCA risk. These associations were derived from cis-eQTL genes that passed both the HEIDI test (p > 0.05) and the SMR test (p < 0.05) (Table S1).

MR analysis of pQTLs in validation phase

To further assess the causal effects of candidate genes on BRCA risk at the protein level, we used cis-pQTL data from the UKB-PPP and deCODE databases. Owing to incomplete gene coverage, only 10 genes identified in the discovery phase were included in the MR analysis for validation with these cis-pQTL datasets (Figure S1).

In the validation phase using the UKB-PPP database, we confirmed that C4BPB, SPAG1, NHLRC3, and TPSD1 were significantly associated with BRCA risk. The results indicated that C4BPB, SPAG1, and TPSD1 were linked to a decreased risk of BRCA (C4BPB: OR [95% CI] = 0.86 [0.77–0.97], p = 1.57e-02; SPAG1: OR [95% CI] = 0.93 [0.88–0.99], p = 1.75e-02; TPSD1: OR [95% CI] = 0.93 [0.87–0.98], p = 1.07e-02), whereas NHLRC3 was associated with an increased risk of BRCA (OR [95% CI] = 1.14 [1.06–1.22], p = 3.97e-04) (Figure 1). These four proteins showed consistent directions of effect with the SMR results and demonstrated neither heterogeneity nor horizontal pleiotropy (Table S2). Their effect estimates were stable across the four statistical methods applied (Figure 1). Clinical expression and survival analysis

Using RNA-sequencing data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) for BRCA, we validated the previously identified candidate drug-associated genes. As illustrated in Figure S2, many of these genes showed differential expression between tumor and normal tissues. Specifically, SPAG1 (p < 2.2e-16), SPON2 (p = 2.5e-02), SNX8 (p = 6.6e-08), and CFHR5 (p = 1.9e-03) were significantly overexpressed in tumor tissue, whereas C4BPB (p = 9.0e-16), NHLRC3 (p = 9.7e-09), GSTM4 (p = 1.1e-03), GSTM3 (p = 1.7e-02), CBLN4 (p = 7.9e-15), NTRK3 (p < 2.2e-16), UROD (p = 2.3e-08), and CTSF (p = 1.5e-10) were significantly downregulated.

Paired tissue comparisons confirmed these findings, demonstrating consistent expression patterns. SPAG1 (p = 2.3e-16) remained overexpressed in tumor tissues compared to controls, while C4BPB (p = 1.3e-09), NHLRC3 (p = 2.1e-04), GSTM4 (p = 1.6e-03), CBLN4 (p = 8.2e-07), NTRK3 (p < 2.2e-16), UROD (p = 1.3e-06), and CTSF (p = 9.0e-08) were consistently underexpressed (Figure S3).

Furthermore, several genes were associated with overall survival (OS) outcomes. CTSF (HR [95% CI] = 1.74[1.18–2.55], p = 4.2e-03), PLXND1 (HR [95% CI] = 1.66[1.21–2.28], p = 1.69e-03) and CFHR5 (HR [95% CI] = 1.64[1.02–2.63], p = 3.80e-02) were correlated with poorer OS. Notably, CTSF-high tumors showed significantly predicted higher IC50s for docetaxel (p = 2.69e-13) and paclitaxel (p = 1.82e-06) and lower IC50 for cyclophosphamide (p = 8.67e-07) (Figure 6A). Clinical drug resistance validation

To validate the role of these candidate genes in mediating therapy response, we examined their expression patterns in multiple datasets drawn from the Cancer Treatment Response gene signature DataBase, including GSE194040 (paclitaxel + trastuzumab), E-TABM-43 (cyclophosphamide + Epirubicin), GSE21974 (cyclophosphamide + docetaxel + epirubicin), and GSE41998 (cyclophosphamide + doxorubicin + ixabepilone).

GSE194040 (paclitaxel + trastuzumab)

In the GSE194040 cohort, three GSTM family members (GSTM1, GSTM3, and GSTM4) were significantly elevated in responders, with p-values of 0.0029, 0.021, and 0.028, respectively (Figures 7A–7C). In contrast, CTSF and CFHR5 did not differ statistically between groups (p = 0.15 and 0.50; Figures 7D and 7E). A combined ROC (receiver operating characteristic) analysis of all five genes yielded an AUC (area under the curve) of 0.886 (p < 0.001; Figure 7F), indicating a robust ability to distinguish responders from non-responders. A corresponding heatmap underscored this distinction, with GSTM family genes prominently enriched in responders (Figure 7G). Functional enrichment analysis predicts the role of CTSF and GSTM3 in BRCA

To further explore the potential roles of CTSF and GSTM3 in BRCA, we investigated the biological functions and pathways associated with DEGs in TCGA BRCA samples with high versus low expression of these genes. Notably, DEGs linked to CTSF were predominantly enriched in immune and inflammation-related processes. The top GO Biological Process terms for CTSF-high tumors included cell chemotaxis, granulocyte migration, and neutrophil chemotaxis, indicating that CTSF upregulation in tumors is associated with genes involved in recruiting and moving immune cells. Consistent with this, KEGG pathway analysis showed that CTSF-associated DEGs were involved in inflammatory and immune signaling pathways such as the IL-17 signaling pathway, various chemokine signaling cascades, and Toll-like receptor signaling. Interestingly, the estrogen signaling pathway also emerged among the KEGG pathways for CTSF-related genes. Taken together, these results suggest that an elevated CTSF expression may exert an immunomodulatory influence within the breast tumor microenvironment (potentially affecting leukocyte infiltration and inflammation), while also participating in hormone-related signaling networks.

On the other hand, DEGs associated with GSTM3 showed strong enrichment in pathways related to development and hormone signaling. In particular, GO terms for GSTM3-high tumors included processes like mammary gland development and morphogenesis, hinting at a role in tissue development or differentiation. CTSF, GSTM3-associated genes were also enriched in the estrogen signaling pathway (a KEGG pathway) and the IL-17 signaling pathway. This indicates that GSTM3 might influence both hormonally regulated pathways and inflammatory signaling. The enrichment for mammary gland development processes suggests that GSTM3 could be involved in the differentiation state of breast cells, which might in turn affect how cells respond to therapies. In summary, GSTM3 appears to contribute to both developmental programs and hormonally regulated pathways in breast tissue, potentially impacting tumor behavior and treatment response.

Overall, the functional enrichment analyses imply distinct yet overlapping roles for CTSF and GSTM3 in BRCA biology. CTSF is linked to immune cell recruitment/activation and possibly estrogen-driven growth, aligning with its identity as a lysosomal protease that could modulate antigen processing and cell signaling. GSTM3, a detoxification enzyme, is linked to developmental pathways and hormone signaling, consistent with a gene that might help cells manage oxidative stress and the metabolic demands of growth signals. These insights provide a starting point for understanding the mechanistic underpinnings of how CTSF and GSTM3 influence tumor progression and drug response in BRCA.

Discussion

This study employed an integrative multi-omics framework to systematically identify and validate druggable genes associated with BRCA. Several candidate genes—such as GSTM3, CTSF, SPAG1, and CFHR5—emerged with significant genetic and protein-level associations. These genes displayed differential expression in tumor versus normal tissues and had prognostic relevance in survival analyses. Notably, GSTM3 and CTSF appeared to influence chemotherapy response, underscoring their potential value in personalized treatment strategies. Functional enrichment analysis further implicated both genes in pathways central to BRCA biology, including immune and hormonal signaling.

These findings align with previous reports demonstrating the utility of eQTL and pQTL analyses for uncovering cancer-associated genes.12 For example, glutathione S-transferase family members—including GSTM3 and GSTM4—participate in detoxification pathways and chemoresistance mechanisms in a variety of malignancies, including BRCA.13,14 Similarly, CTSF, a lysosomal protease, has been linked to tumor progression and immune evasion through processes such as extracellular matrix remodeling and antigen processing.15,16 However, our study uniquely integrates genetic, transcriptomic, and proteomic data to establish robust causal relationships and shared genetic determinants between protein abundance and BRCA risk.

Of particular importance are the observed links between candidate genes and chemotherapy sensitivity. For instance, high CTSF expression correlated with resistance to paclitaxel and docetaxel, but increased sensitivity to cyclophosphamide, consistent with prior reports on how lysosomal dysregulation can alter drug metabolism and cellular uptake.17,18 In addition, GSTM3 modulates taxane sensitivity, likely via its role in oxidative stress pathways that confer survival advantages under therapeutic pressure.

The identification of GSTM3 and CTSF as key modulators of BRCA progression and treatment response has significant implications for precision oncology. First, these genes could serve as predictive biomarkers to stratify patients according to their likelihood of responding to specific chemotherapeutic agents.19 For example, patients exhibiting high CTSF expression might benefit from cyclophosphamide-based regimens while potentially avoiding taxane-based therapies. Second, targeting these genes or their associated pathways may enhance treatment efficacy. Inhibiting CTSF or modulating GSTM3 activity, for instance, could sensitize resistant tumors to standard therapies, thereby offering a promising approach to overcome chemoresistance. While a subtype-specific analysis would likely provide further mechanistic insights (and potentially enhance the translational relevance of our findings), the present study primarily focused on examining the influence of GSTM3/CTSF on BRCA drug resistance—particularly paclitaxel resistance. In future studies, we plan to investigate subtype-specific differences in chemoresistance to determine whether the relationship between GSTM3/CTSF expression and chemotherapy sensitivity varies across distinct BRCA subtypes.

Moreover, functional enrichment analyses identified the involvement of GSTM3 and CTSF in immune- and hormone-related pathways, including IL-17 and estrogen receptor signaling, suggesting roles in modulating the tumor microenvironment and endocrine responses.20 Future combination strategies targeting these pathways alongside conventional therapies may produce synergistic effects and better patient outcomes.

Further exploration of GSTM3 and CTSF in regulating tumor immunity is also warranted. For instance, high CTSF expression in immune-related pathways points to its potential as a target for immunotherapeutic interventions, possibly influencing immune cell infiltration and cytokine signaling.21,22 Similarly, GSTM3 involvement in estrogen signaling indicates a role in hormone-responsive BRCA subtypes, where combining endocrine therapies with GSTM3 modulation could yield personalized treatment.

On the translational front, high-throughput screening of small-molecule inhibitors targeting CTSF or GSTM3 could expedite the discovery of drugs.23,24 In parallel, preclinical studies using patient-derived xenograft models or organoid cultures could validate these compounds’ efficacy and safety, expediting their transition to clinical trials.25

In summary, this study highlights the potential of integrative multi-omics analyses to identify and validate druggable genes associated with BRCA. By uncovering GSTM3 and CTSF as key modulators of tumor progression and treatment response, our findings provide valuable insights into BRCA pathogenesis and pave the way for personalized therapeutic strategies. Future research should focus on validating these findings in diverse populations and investigating their broader applicability to other cancers, ultimately advancing precision oncology efforts.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, the genetic data used are predominantly from European populations, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic groups. Second, while we employed rigorous statistical methods to infer causality, residual confounding cannot be entirely ruled out. Third, our functional validations were conducted primarily in cell lines; in vivo models are needed to confirm the therapeutic potential of targeting CTSF and GSTM3. Finally, the lack of subtype-specific analyses may obscure differential roles of these genes across BRCA subtypes.

Method details

Research design

In this study, we established an integrated analytical framework to systematically identify and validate druggable genes associated with BRCA. We first leveraged eQTL data from the eQTLGen Consortium and performed SMR (Summary-data-based Mendelian Randomization) analysis coupled with the HEIDI (heterogeneity in dependent instruments) teststo pinpoint genes that might causally influence BRCA risk.

Next, we further validated these causal associations at the protein level using pQTL results from both the UK Biobank Proteome Plasma Proteins (UKB-PPP) and the deCODE, performing comprehensive sensitivity analyses to support the reliability of our findings.

We also examined clinical data from TCGA (The Cancer Genome Atlas) to evaluate gene expression profiles and their prognostic significance in breast cancer. In addition, colocalization and functional enrichment analyses were conducted to explore potential mechanisms and pathways associated with the candidate genes. Finally, we employed in silico drug response prediction (using cell line data from the Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal, CTRP, together with the OncoPredict R package) and analyzed multiple published chemotherapy response datasets (from the Cancer Treatment Response gene signature Database, CTR-DB) to assess the impact of candidate genes on treatment sensitivity. These steps allowed us to identify candidate genes with potential roles in chemoresistance and to evaluate their viability as therapeutic targets in BRCA.

Exposure data

We obtained the eQTL data from the eQTLGen Consortium (https://eqtlgen.org/),26 which includes 16,989 cis-eQTL genes based on 31,684 European-ancestry blood samples. To investigate the genetic factors influencing plasma protein levels, we utilized two extensive GWAS datasets: the UKB-PPP and the deCODE Health study. The UKB-PPP, which used the Olink platform to measure protein concentration in 54,219 UK Biobank participants, identified genetic variation in 2,923 proteins.27 Similarly, the deCODE Health study utilized the high-throughput SomaScan platform to quantify 4,907 aptamer-protein pairs in 35,559 Icelandic individuals.28

As instrumental variables, we selected only index cis-pQTLs that achieved genome-wide significance (p < 5 × 10−8) and within 1 Mb of the encoding gene. To validate these variables, we employed linkage disequilibrium estimates from European samples in the 1000 Genomes Project. Consequently, we restricted our colocalization analyses to proteins with index cis-pQTLs identified in the UKB-PPP and deCODE dataset.

Outcome data

The BRCA cohort dataset was obtained from FinnGen Release 11, released in June 2024 (https://www.finngen.fi/).29 The data comprised 20,586 BRCA cases as well as 201,494 individual healthy control.

SMR analysis and HEIDI test

The SMR and HEIDI methods, by integrating GWAS and eQTL data, distinguish whether observed associations result from pleiotropy or linkage.30 The SMR approach uses summary-level GWAS and eQTL data to assess the relationship between gene expression and disease risk, with a p-value <0.05 considered a significant association. The HEIDI test further examines whether the association identified by SMR arises from pleiotropy (a single variant affecting both expression and disease) or from linkage (distinct variants in proximity). We used a HEIDI p-value cutoff of 0.05 to indicate a likely pleiotropic relationship.31 Together, these analyses provide strong evidence for causal associations between BRCA risk and gene expression identified through SMR.

Mendelian randomization analysis

Two-sample MR analysis was performed using the TwoSampleMR R package (version 0.5.7). To ensure the reliability and robustness of instrumental variables (IVs), we implemented several quality control steps. Initially, only variants with an F-statistic ≥10 were retained (F = (beta/se)2). We then prioritized variants with low linkage disequilibrium (LD; r2 < 0.1), confirmed using the European reference panel from the 1000 Genomes Project, to ensure their mutual independence.32 Finally, Steiger filtering was applied to exclude genes where the genetic instruments explained a greater fraction of outcome variance than exposure variance, mitigating reverse causation concern.33

For genes with multiple IVs in the primary analysis, several methods were used to aggregate SNP estimates: Inverse-Variance Weighted (IVW), Weighted Mode, Weighted Median, and MR-Egger. The IVW method, assuming all instruments are valid, was used for meta-analysis of SNP estimates due to its superior statistical power under the no-pleiotropy assumption.34 The Weighted Median method provides a reliable causal estimate, even if up to 50% of the IVs are invalid.35 The MR-Egger method calculates the causal effect by regressing on the slope of the Egger model, accommodating potential pleiotropy by including an intercept that reflects the average impact of pleiotropic SNPs (single nucleotide polymorphism) on the outcome.36

Additionally, statistical tests were conducted to assess horizontal pleiotropy and heterogeneity. Cochran’s Q test was used to evaluate heterogeneity among the genetic instrument estimates, as significant heterogeneity may indicate pleiotropy or varying effects of the IVs on the outcome. To address horizontal pleiotropy, we employed the MR-Egger regression intercept and the MR-PRESSO method to detect and correct for potential directional pleiotropy among the genetic instruments.36,37

Clinical expression and survival analysis

We retrieved gene expression data and corresponding clinical information for BRCA from the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov). Using R, we downloaded both count and TPM (Transcripts Per Million) format data. The TPM data were normalized with a log2(TPM +1) transformation. After filtering for samples that contained both RNA sequencing data and clinical information, we selected a total of 1231 samples for subsequent analysis.

To evaluate gene expression levels, we applied the Kruskal-Wallis (KW) non-parametric test to the count data. Survival differences between groups in the Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival analysis were assessed using the log rank test. For the KM curves, p-values and hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were derived from the log rank test and univariate Cox regression analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using R software, and results were deemed statistically significant if the p-value was below 0.05.

Colocalization analysis

COLOC analysis in genetic epidemiology examines whether associations between gene expression and a health outcome, such as BRCA, are driven by shared causal variants, using a Bayesian approach. COLOC tests five hypotheses: H0: No association between gene expression and the trait (BRCA), H1: Association with gene expression only, not with BRCA, H2: Association with BRCA only, not with gene expression, H3: Independent associations with gene expression and BRCA, and H4: A shared causal variant influences both gene expression and BRCA. A high posterior probability for H4 (PPH4 > 0.6) suggests strong colocalization, indicating potential drug targets.38

Analysis of gene expressions in BRCA

Gene expression in BRCA was assessed using both RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from the GEO database (GSE176078).39,40 For single-cell analysis, scRNA-seq data in.h5 format and associated annotation files were obtained from the TISCH database (http://tisch.comp-genomics.org/). Data preprocessing included quality control, normalization, scaling, and clustering using the R packages MAESTRO and Seurat. Cell types were re-clustered using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) and identified according to established marker genes. Gene expression patterns across different cell types, including tumor, stromal, and immune cells, were compared, and the results were visualized using violin plots and heatmaps.

RNA-seq data for various BRCA cell lines were retrieved from the CCLE database (https://depmap.org/portal/data_page/) and processed using R software. Additionally, expression levels of the genes in BRCA cell lines were calculated from the CCLE dataset. Correlating this expression dataset with drug-sensitivity profiles from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/) enabled further exploration of the relationship between candidate gene expression and drug response.

Clinical drug resistance analysis

Using breast cancer cell lines from the Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal (http://portals.broadinstitute.org/ctrp.v2.1/)41 as a training set by “oncoPredict” R package,42 we predicted variations in chemotherapy drug (Paclitaxel, Cyclophosphamide and Docetaxel) sensitivity among TCGA breast cancer patients classified by the expression levels of various candidate genes.

Clinical transcriptome analysis of cancer treatment response was performed using the Cancer Treatment Response gene signature Database (CTR-DB, http://ctrdb.ncpsb.org.cn) to validate the function of candidate genes in BRCA drug resistance.43 Four datasets were examined: GSE194040 for Paclitaxel + Trastuzumab resistance, E-TABM-43 for Cyclophosphamide + Epirubicin resistance, GSE21974 for Cyclophosphamide + Docetaxel + Epirubicin resistance, and GSE41998 for AC (Cyclophosphamide + Doxorubicin) + Ixabepilone resistance.

MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells were used for functional drug sensitivity assays. We designed and synthesized three distinct small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting CTSF and GSTM3, respectively. Each siRNA (or a negative control siRNA) was transfected into MDA-MB-231 cells, and 48 h post-transfection the knockdown efficiency was evaluated by Western blotting for CTSF or GSTM3 protein. The siRNA yielding the greatest silencing efficiency for each gene was selected for further experiments.

For apoptosis assays, cells were transfected with the selected siRNA or control siRNA. After 24 h, docetaxel (MCE, RP-56976) was added at a concentration of 0.14 μM (this dose was chosen based on an IC50 of 0.07 μM and CC50 of 0.19 μM for MDA-MB-231). Following drug exposure, apoptosis was assessed using an Annexin V–FITC/propidium iodide (PI) dual-staining kit (Beyotime, Cat# C1062L) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Stained cells were analyzed by flow cytometry to determine the percentage of early apoptotic cells (Annexin V+/PI–) and to quantify the number of apoptotic cells. All experiments were performed in triplicate and the results were used for downstream statistical analysis.

Functional enrichment analysis

We first categorized TCGA BRCA patients into high- and low-expression groups based on the expression levels of CTSF and GSTM3. Differential expression analysis was performed using the raw counts matrix and processed with the “DESeq2”and “clusterProfiler” R package according to standard procedures. This step aimed to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with varying levels of CTSF and GSTM3 expression. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG). This analysis aimed to elucidate the roles of DEGs in biological processes, cellular components, molecular functions, and their involvement in metabolic and signaling pathways.44,45

Quantification and statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using the TwoSampleMR (version 0.6.13), MendelianRandomization (version 0.9), MRPRESSO (version 1.0), DESeq2(version 1.4), clusterProfiler(version 4.1) and oncoPredict(version 1.2)packages in R Software (version 4.5.0). Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics v26.0. For the KM curves, p-values and hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were derived from the log rank test and univariate Cox regression analysis. Wilcoxon tests were applied to evaluate the expression of candidate genes in patients with clinical cancer treatment response and non-response. Unpaired t-tests were used to compare apoptosis between siRNA groups and control groups. A two-sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.
