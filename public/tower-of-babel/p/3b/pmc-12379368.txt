Calreticulin in the nasal mucus promotes neutrophil migration and pathogen clearance
Authors: Yichao Ma, Xinming Qin, Shiqi Liu, Ruoyang Lin, Baoyan Meng, Jian Lin, Qian Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The nasal cavity harbors diverse microbiota, and the nasal mucosal innate defense against microbial infiltration is crucial for respiratory infections. Both the nasal mucus covering the surface of the nasal cavity and the neutrophils beneath the nasal epithelia are the first line of innate defense against pathogens. Studying nasal mucus is challenging because of difficulties in collecting stable, high-quality samples from humans. Here, we investigated how nasal mucus cooperates with neutrophils to exert antimicrobial effects. Nasal mucus proteins, derived from nasal mucosal explants of goats, can promote neutrophil migration and increase their bactericidal activity. Calreticulin, identified from total mucus proteins, triggered ICAM-1-dependent transendothelial migration of neutrophils. Moreover, calreticulin activated the Rho GTPases of neutrophils via Toll-like receptor (TLR) 2 to promote their migration and further triggered the release of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) in a manner dependent on TLR2 and TLR4, accelerating the elimination of pathogens. In vivo studies also demonstrated that nasal inoculation with calreticulin induced neutrophil recruitment to the nasal mucosa and accelerated the clearance of Pasteurella multocida. Together, these findings highlight the synergistic interaction between nasal mucus and neutrophils as an important protective feature in the nasal mucosa.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-025-02388-5.

Introduction

The mucus layer of the nasal cavity constitutes the first line of defense against upper respiratory tract infection [1–3]. Nasal mucus is a complex viscoelastic gel synthesized and secreted by goblet cells and submucosal glands, providing a physical barrier and shielding the nasal epithelium by trapping inhaled foreign pathogens and facilitating their removal via mucociliary clearance (MCC) [4]. In recent years, significant progress has been made in identifying the antibacterial components of the intestinal mucus [5], such as zymogen granule protein 16 (ZG16), resistin-like molecule β (RELMβ), Ly6/PLAUR domain containing 8 (Lypd8), secretory immunoglobulin A (SIgA), and antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), as well as in identifying the underlying bactericidal mechanisms [6–8]. In contrast, novel anti-infective substances in airway mucus remain to be identified. However, an ongoing challenge in studying nasal mucus is the collection of high-stability and high-quality samples [9]. Since the nasal mucus layer is quite thin under resting conditions, collecting a sufficient volume from live human or animal donors will subject the epithelium to mechanical or chemical stimulation. In addition, the composition of the collected samples is complex and heterogeneous. To address this issue, our laboratory previously established a long-term in vitro goat nasal mucosa explant model that exhibited a promising mucus secretion function [10].

An increasing number of studies have shown that pathogens have developed sophisticated strategies to penetrate the mucus barrier, including mechanisms to allow efficient disruption of the mucus, enzymes that degrade the mucus, pathways that allow evasion of the barrier, the disruption of epithelial integrity and epithelial production of barrier components [11, 12]. In addition to the nasal mucus, the nasal innate immune system is a complicated system of many biological components and processes that defend the host against pathogens through the synergistic configuration of each element [13, 14]. Neutrophils, located beneath or between adjacent nasal epithelial cells, are among the first cells involved in the innate immune response to pathogens and are indispensable for host defense [15]. Neutrophils are recruited from the blood circulation and migrate through the vascular endothelium to destroy pathogens and remodel damaged tissue [16]. Furthermore, neutrophils can destroy pathogens via the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), phagocytosis, and the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) [17–19]. NETs are reticular extracellular structures released by activated neutrophils, consisting mainly of decondensed chromatin DNA decorated with antimicrobial granule proteins such as myeloperoxidase (MPO) and neutrophil elastase (NE). These structures play a critical role in the innate immune response by physically trapping and eliminating pathogens in a non-phagocytic manner [20]. Most previous studies have focused primarily on pathogen-derived environmental signals that induce the recruitment of neutrophils to the site of infection [21, 22], but the effect of nasal mucus on neutrophil migration remains unknown. Moreover, nasal mucus and neutrophils are the main gatekeepers and controllers of the interaction between bacteria and the host immune system. Hence, studies regarding the strategies for cooperation between nasal mucus and neutrophils against bacterial infection are essential.

Respiratory infections are a prevalent health issue globally. The nasal mucosa is a primary entry point and infection location for most respiratory infectious agents, such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Haemophilus influenzae, Bordetella pertussis, and Staphylococcus aureus [23, 24]. Infection with these microorganisms can result in various respiratory illnesses, ranging from mild symptoms to lung infections. Augmenting the nasal mucosal barrier to reduce bacterial interactions with nasal epithelial cells is a crucial technique for infection prevention. In this study, we used our previously developed animal model to determine how nasal mucus modulated neutrophil-mediated protection against bacteria. Calreticulin was identified from the nasal mucus, which collaborated with neutrophils to remove pathogenic agents, highlighting the cooperative interactions between nasal mucus and neutrophils that are critical for eliminating bacteria in the nasal cavity.

Materials and methods

Cells, strains, antibodies, and reagents

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were obtained and maintained in our laboratory and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C under 5% CO2 (SUNNCELL, Wuhan, China, Cat# SNL-503). The cell line was regularly tested for mycoplasma contamination.

Pasteurella multocida (serotype D) [25] and Streptococcus suis serotype 2 (ZY05719) [26] were prepared and preserved by our laboratory strain collection and cultured in tryptone soya broth (TSB) (Solarbio, Beijing, China, Cat# LA0110) and Todd Hewitt broth (THB) (Solarbio, Beijing, China, Cat# LA1860), respectively. These tube-scale cultures were incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm. To ensure bacterial viability and consistency, all strains used in this study were collected during their logarithmic growth phase. Pasteurella multocida reached the logarithmic phase after 6 h of incubation, while Streptococcus suis serotype 2 entered this phase after 3 h, with an optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) of 0.6, corresponding to approximately 5 × 10⁸ CFU/mL.

All antibodies were purchased from commercial suppliers, including Proteintech (Wuhan, China), Abcam (Cambridge, UK), Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA), and Abways (Shanghai, China).

Antibodies used in immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence assay (IFA): calreticulin (Proteintech, Cat# 27298-1-AP, 1:200 for IHC), myeloperoxidase (Abcam, Cat# ab208670, 1:200 for IF, 1:1000 for IHC).

Antibodies used in Western blot: CDC42 (Abcam, Cat# ab187643, 1:2000), Rac1 (Abcam, Cat# ab155938, 1:2000), RhoA (Abcam, Cat# ab187027, 1:2000), β-actin (Abways, Cat# AB0035, 1:5000), Phospho-PI3 Kinase p85(Tyr458)/p55(Tyr199) (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# 4228 T, 1:1000), PI3 Kinase p85 (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# 4257 T, 1:1000), Phospho-Akt (Ser473) (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# 4060 T, 1:1000), Akt (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# 9272 S, 1:1000), Phospho-GSK-3β (Ser9) (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# 5558 T, 1:1000), GSK3β (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# BS1402, 1:1000), Phospho-p38 MAPK (Thr180/Tyr182) (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# 4511 T, 1:1000), p38 MAPK (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# 8690 T, 1:1000), p44/42 MAPK (Erk1/2) (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# 4695 T, 1:1000), p44/42 MAPK (Erk1/2) (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# 4695 T, 1:1000), ICAM-1(Proteintech, Cat# 10831-1-AP, 1:2000), GAPDH (Abways, Cat# AB2000, 1:5000), His-tag (Proteintech, Cat# HRP-66005, 1:5000) and GST-tag (Proteintech, HRP-66001, 1:5000).

Antibodies used in blocking assays: TLR2 (Proteintech, Cat# 66645-1-Ig, 25 µg/mL), TLR4 (Proteintech, Cat# 19811-1-AP, 25 µg/mL), and rabbit IgG isotype control (Proteintech, Cat# 98136-1-RR, 25 µg/mL).

Chemical inhibitors used in this study were purchased from MedChemExpress (NJ, USA) and include ICAM-1 inhibitor (Cat# HY-100226), TLR2 and TLR4 inhibitor (Cat# HY-116213), TLR2 inhibitor (Cat# HY-100461), and TLR4 inhibitor (Cat# HY-107575). All chemical compounds were dissolved in DMSO and stored at − 20 °C according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Animals

The quantification of calreticulin expression and distribution was conducted in the nasal cavity of goats across various age groups (fetal, 21 days, and 60 days). A nasal mucosa explant model was developed using the nasal mucosa of 6-month-old goats for the purpose of collecting nasal mucus. 30-day-old goats were used in the calreticulin nasal administration experiment and the Pasteurella multocida infection experiment (3 goats per group). All goats used in this study were Yangtze River Delta white goats, a local breed sourced from a commercial farm in Jiangsu Province, China (Nanjing Zhushun Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). The animals were raised under strict veterinary supervision, routinely tested negative for common caprine respiratory pathogens including Pasteurella multocida, Mycoplasma ovipneumoniae, Brucella melitensis, and Streptococcus, and had no prior history of respiratory infections or antibiotic/immunosuppressive treatment before sample collection. All procedures and experiments were performed according to protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanjing Agricultural University (Nanjing, China) and followed the National Institutes of Health guidelines (Permit Number: NJAULLSC2023108).

Isolation and cultivation of the nasal mucosa explants: Six-month-old goats (n = 3) were euthanized by intravenous injection of pentobarbital sodium (100 mg/kg). The noses were sawn off the skull in front of the eyes and stripped of skin and muscle tissue. The different parts of the nasal cavity (nasal concha and septum) were transported on ice in phosphate buffered saline (PBS), supplemented with 200 units/mL penicillin and 200 µg/mL streptomycin. After the nasal tissue was washed three times in PBS, the mucosa membrane was carefully stripped from the surfaces of the ventral turbinates and septum using ophthalmic scissors and tweezers and placed on a sterile plate containing PBS. The stripped mucosa of each tissue was divided into equal explant pieces with a diameter of 6 mm using biopsy punches, and then the epithelium was placed upwards on the upper chamber of the Transwell inserts (0.4 μm pore size; 6.5 mm membrane diameter; Corning, NY, USA, Cat# 3413). To establish an air-liquid interface, serum-free medium was introduced into the lower chamber, allowing the liquid level to reach halfway up the explant thickness. After culturing the nasal mucosal explants at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 24 h, mucus was collected from the upper chamber.

Mucus preparation and total protein extraction: Nasal mucus was harvested from the apical chamber of nasal mucosa explants cultured in Transwell inserts. The inserts were carefully transferred into 6-well plates containing precooled PBS supplemented with 1% protease inhibitor cocktail. Using a sterile 1 mL pipette tip, the mucus secreted by the explants was gently aspirated and rinsed into the PBS. The collected mucus was then subjected to protein precipitation by adding ammonium sulfate to a final concentration of 80% (w/v). After overnight incubation at 4 °C, the precipitated proteins were collected by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 30 min and resuspended in PBS for subsequent analyses [27]. This extracted protein was loaded onto a Superdex-200 column connected to an ÄKTA Avant 25 instrument (GE Healthcare, Chicago, USA). Protein complexes were separated by passing PBS at 1.0 mL/min. Eluting proteins were detected by absorbance at 280 nm and collected according to the ultraviolet absorption peak of fractions. Finally, the fractions were concentrated to 1 mg/mL using Amicon Centricon filter devices and stored at −80 °C for future use.

Neutrophil isolation

Fresh samples of peripheral blood from healthy 6-month-old goats were collected in 10 mL citrate anticoagulant tubes. Red blood cells were removed by red blood cell lysis buffer (Biosharp, Anhui, China, Cat# BL503B), and the remaining cells were resuspended in 2 mL of PBS. The cells were laid on a three-layer Percoll (Solarbio, Beijing, China, Cat# 65455-52-9) gradient of 78%, 69%, and 52% Percoll. Neutrophils were generated from the interface between 69% and 78%.

Migration assay

Cell migration was determined by Transwell migration assays. Briefly, the lower sides of 5-µm-pore-size polycarbonate filters of a 24-well Transwell plate (Corning Incorporated Costar, Cambridge, MA, USA, Cat# 93421) were coated with 100 µL of 50% Matrigel (BD Biosciences, CA, USA, Cat# 354483) and allowed to solidify for 20 min at 37 °C. Subsequently, 5 × 105 freshly isolated neutrophils were labeled with carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA, Cat# 65-0850-84) and added to the upper chamber. Then recombinant calreticulin (0 µg/mL, 4 µg/mL, and 40 µg/mL, respectively) was added to the lower chambers, which contained serum-free RPMI 1640 medium, and incubated for 3 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. To accurately visualize the spatial trajectory of neutrophil migration across the Transwell membrane and to eliminate potential artifacts introduced by Z-plane positioning, Z-stack imaging was conducted using a Nikon N-STORM confocal microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). Optical sections were captured at 1 μm intervals (Z-step = 1 μm), covering the full vertical extent of the Transwell system, including the upper chamber, membrane, and lower chamber. This approach enabled high-resolution three-dimensional reconstruction of the transmigration process. To ensure consistency and minimize inter-sample variability, all imaging parameters—such as laser intensity, scan step size, detector gain, and exposure time—were maintained constant across experimental groups.

Neutrophil killing assays

Freshly isolated goat neutrophils were obtained from peripheral blood using Percoll density gradient centrifugation and resuspended in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 1% FBS. The cells were then incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂ for 30 min to allow stabilization. After stabilization, neutrophils were resuspended in PBS and independently stimulated with either total nasal mucus protein or recombinant calreticulin at the indicated concentrations for 1 h. All stimulations were conducted in 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes under the same incubation conditions. Concurrently, Pasteurella multocida and Streptococcus suis serotype 2 were harvested by centrifuging the cultures at 5000 rpm, washing twice with PBS, and resuspending the cultures in PBS, supplemented with 2% autologous serum in microcentrifuge tubes. The opsonization of bacteria in goat serum was performed by gentle inversion of the microcentrifuge tubes for 20 min at 37 °C. Then, serum-opsonized bacteria were incubated with isolated neutrophils in a 1:10 ratio for 3 h, corresponding to a final bacterial concentration of 1 × 10⁷ CFU/mL. The addition of bacteria to the assay medium without neutrophils was performed as a control for bacterial growth. Samples were further serially diluted with cold Triton X-100 (0.1%) and plated onto TSA or THA plates at 37 °C. Colony-forming units (CFUs) were calculated following overnight incubation.

Liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis

The purified peak 4 from the nasal mucus was completely dried in a SpeedVac device. The dried sample was re-dissolved in 0.1% formic acid (FA) solution. After digestion and reduction, a dissolved peptide sample was analyzed by a NanoLC-ESI-MS/MS system equipped with high-pressure liquid chromatography and an ion trap mass spectrometer. The mobile phase is liquid A: formic acid aqueous solution with a final concentration of 0.1%, and liquid B: formic acid acetonitrile aqueous solution with a final concentration of 0.1%. The flow rate was set to 300 nL/min. The sample gradient was as follows: 0–35% buffer B for 50 min, 35–100% buffer B for 5 min, and holding in 100% buffer B for 5 min. MS data was acquired using a data-dependent top10 method, dynamically choosing the most abundant precursor ions from the survey scan (300–1800 m/z) for HCD fragmentation. The automatic gain control (AGC) target was set to 3e6, and the maximum inject time was set to 10 ms. Dynamic exclusion duration was 40.0 s. Survey scans were acquired at a resolution of 70,000 at m/z 200, and the resolution for HCD spectra was set to 17,500 at m/z 200, and the isolation width was 2 m/z. Normalized collision energy was 30 eV, and the underfill ratio, which specified the minimum percentage of the target value likely to be reached at maximum fill time, was defined as 0.1%. The MS data were analyzed using MaxQuant software version 1.5.3.17 (Max Planck Institute of Biochemistry in Martinsried, Germany). MS data were searched against the UniProtKB Capra hircus database.

Expression and purification of recombinant proteins

Full-length goat calreticulin was cloned into the PpIC9K vector. For efficient integration of recombinant plasmids into the Pichia genome, PpIC9K-calreticulin plasmid DNA was linearized with Sal I. The Pichia strain was transformed with the calreticulin recombinant plasmids by electroporation as instructed by the manufacturer (MicroPulser 1652100, Bio-Rad, CA, USA). Then the recombinant strain was identified by PCR, and the PCR products from the positive transformants were sequenced to verify the insertion of the correct expression cassette. Recombinant P. pastoris strains were grown in yeast extract-peptone-dextrose medium (Sangon, Shanghai, China, Cat# B540133); buffered glycerol-complex medium (Solarbio, Beijing, China, Cat# LA5250) was used for growth, and buffered methanol-complex medium was used for induction, both at pH 6.5. The recombinant protein from the culture supernatant was purified by affinity chromatography using HisTrap HP (GE Healthcare, Chicago, USA, Cat# 17-5248-05) with the ÄKTA Avant 25 instrument (GE Healthcare, Chicago, USA). The Pichia pastoris expression system offers benefits such as proper folding and secretion of recombinant proteins into the extracellular environment. The endogenous protein secretion capacity of the Pichia pastoris expression system is limited. Importantly, the medium used in this system contains glycerol or glucose and methanol as carbon sources, supplemented with inorganic salts but devoid of proteins, thereby reducing the risk of contaminating host-derived proteins or TLR ligands. As only the culture supernatant is required for protein purification, this system minimizes endotoxin contamination and is well suited for studies involving immune cell activation.

The GST-tagged fusion expression plasmids pGEX-4T-TLR2 and pGEX-4T-TLR4 were synthesized by Beijing Tsingke Biotech Co., Ltd. The recombinant plasmids, along with the empty vector control (pGEX-4T-1), were transformed into Escherichia coli BL21 competent cells. Positive transformants were selected on LB agar plates containing 100 µg/mL ampicillin, and further verified by colony PCR. For protein expression, validated clones were inoculated into LB liquid medium supplemented with 100 µg/mL ampicillin, and cultured at 37 °C with agitation at 220 rpm until the OD₆₀₀ reached 0.6–0.8. Protein induction was initiated by adding 1 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), followed by incubation at 37 °C for 6 h. After induction, the bacterial cultures were harvested by centrifugation at 8000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the pellets were resuspended in pre-chilled PBS. Cell lysis was performed using the high-pressure homogenizer (JN-mini, Juneng Nano & Bio Technology Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China). The lysates were then centrifuged again at 8000 × g for 15 min, and the resulting supernatant was collected for subsequent purification. GST-tagged recombinant proteins were purified using a Glutathione Sepharose 4FF Gravity Column according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Smart-Lifesciences, Shanghai, China; Cat# SA008GC05). The purified proteins were either used immediately for downstream assays or stored at −80 °C for future use.

Immunohistochemical and immunofluorescent staining

Paraffin sections were dewaxed and rehydrated by incubation with xylene and descending ethanol concentrations. Antigen retrieval was performed for 30 min with citrate buffer at pH 6.0 in a decloaking chamber (DC2012, Biocare Medical, Concord, USA) at 95 °C. Sections were eliminated with endogenous peroxidase activity (0.5% H2O2), followed by blocking with 5% BSA in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. The sections were then incubated with primary antibodies (rabbit anti-calreticulin antibody, rabbit anti-MPO antibody) overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber. The SABC-POD Kit (BOSTER, Wuhan, China, Cat# SA1020) was used to amplify and visualize the signal. We used the sections without primary antibodies as negative controls. The sections were visualized under a light microscope, and different fields were counted during the statistical analysis.

For the immunofluorescent assay, tissue sections and cells were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C and then stained with the corresponding secondary antibodies, and nuclei were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA, Cat# H3569). Tissue sections and cells were observed using a Nikon confocal microscope.

Adhesion assay

HUVECs were seeded in 24-well plates and grown to confluent monolayers. After incubation of HUVECs with calreticulin for 24 h, CFSE-labeled neutrophils were cocultured with HUVECs (5 × 105 cells/well) for 1 h. The nonadherent cells were removed by washing three times with PBS. The adherent cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for analysis using fluorescence microscopy (EVOS M7000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA).

Transmigration assay

HUVECs were seeded on 24-well Transwell chambers and incubated to confluent monolayers. The cells were incubated with calreticulin for 24 h. Then, CFSE-labeled neutrophils (5 × 105 cells/well) were added to the top chamber and allowed to migrate for 3 h at 37 °C. The migrated cells were collected from the lower chamber. CFSE fluorescence (λex = 494 nm; λem = 521 nm) was quantified using a fluorometer (Infinite M200, Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland).

ROS measurement

The level of cytosolic ROS was quantified using 2’,7’-Dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) as a fluorescent probe (Beyotime, Shanghai, China; Cat# S0033M). Following treatment with calreticulin for 3 h, neutrophils were washed with PBS three times and incubated with 10 µmol/L DCFH-DA for 20 min at 37 °C in the dark. Subsequently, the cells were washed twice with PBS, and the fluorescence of DCF was recorded at 485 ± 10 nm excitation and 530 ± 12.5 nm emission wavelengths using a FACSCalibur flow cytometer (Accuri C6, BD Biosciences, San Jose, USA).

Animal design

Intranasal inoculation of calreticulin: Six 30-day-old goats with similar weights were allocated to two groups and housed in two separate rooms for 24 h prior to the experiment. The two groups were as follows: the control group and the calreticulin treatment group. Goats in the calreticulin treatment group were intranasally administered 2.5 mg/kg of calreticulin. Goats in the control group were intranasally administered the same amount of sterile PBS. These goats were euthanized to collect samples at 24 h post infection (hpi).

Pasteurella multocida challenge experiments: Nine 30-day-old goats with similar weights were allocated to three groups and housed in three separate rooms for 24 h prior to the experiment. The goats were divided into three groups: the control group, the Pasteurella multocida infection group, and the calreticulin treatment group. The goats in the calreticulin treatment group were intranasally inoculated with 2.5 mg/kg calreticulin. The control group and Pasteurella multocida infection group were treated with an equal volume of sterile PBS. After 6 h, goats in the Pasteurella multocida infection group and calreticulin treatment group were challenged with 1 mL of Pasteurella multocida (109 CFU/mL) administered by intranasal inoculation. In contrast, goats in the control group received the same volume of PBS. At 6 h post-Pasteurella multocida infection, the goats in the calreticulin treatment group were treated with calreticulin (2.5 mg/kg) again, and the goats in other groups were intranasally administered the same amount of PBS. These goats were euthanized to collect samples at 24 hpi.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.4.1. All data are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD). Two-way ANOVA or one-way ANOVA analysis was employed to determine significant differences among multiple groups, and t-tests were employed to determine differences between two groups. The significance was set as follows: ns, no significance; *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001.

Results

Total nasal mucus protein promotes neutrophil migration and collaborates with neutrophils to exert antimicrobial effects

As a general histological observation under normal physiological conditions, clustered neutrophils embedded within mucus and interspersed among nasal epithelial cells were identified in 50% (5/10) of healthy 60-day-old goats (Fig. S1). This observation indicated possible functional interactions between neutrophils and nasal mucus. Moreover, the goat nasal mucosa explants demonstrated robust viability in vitro, with active mucus secretion and ciliary movement clearly observable under the microscope (Video S1 and S2). The periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining results indicated an intact epithelial architecture with prominently dilated subepithelial glands, accompanied by strong PAS-positive signals, indicating preserved mucin retention (Fig. S2). Collectively, these findings support that nasal mucus collected via this explant model is minimally contaminated by external environmental factors, thereby ensuring a relatively stable and representative protein composition suitable for subsequent functional analyses.

Furthermore, the purity of goat neutrophils, isolated from peripheral blood, was greater than 95% as determined by Wright-Giemsa staining. After 12 h of in vitro culture, the viability of neutrophils remained greater than 95% (Fig. S3). Even at a concentration of 1 mg/mL, total mucus protein had no effect on neutrophil viability, according to the Cell Counting Kit 8 (CCK8) data (Fig. S4A). In neutrophil bactericidal assays, neutrophils were shown to independently eliminate Pasteurella multocida and Streptococcus suis serotype 2, and the bactericidal effect of neutrophils on these two bacteria increased by 40.16 ± 20.92 and 10.05 ± 1.66 times, respectively, in the presence of 100 µg/mL total mucus protein (Fig. 1A). While total mucus protein had a dose-dependent antibacterial impact on bacteria, the antibacterial effect was greatest when mucus proteins and neutrophils were co-incubated with bacteria, indicating that mucus proteins and neutrophils indeed exerted synergistic bactericidal effects. Furthermore, a concentration of 100 µg/mL total mucus protein stimulated the migration of CFSE-labeled neutrophils to the lower chamber of the Transwell chamber (Fig. 1B-C). Statistical analysis showed a significant increase in the number of cells that have migrated (P < 0.0001; Fig. S5A).

To further investigate the active components in mucus proteins that can stimulate neutrophil migration and activation, we utilized gel filtration chromatography to separate proteins of varying molecular weights, and we obtained six components, P1-P6 (Fig. 1D). The mucus proteins of different molecular weights were co-incubated with neutrophils to assess the effects of each component on neutrophil bactericidal activity. The findings indicated that the proteins in P4 could increase the bactericidal activity of neutrophils by 12.15 ± 2.65 times (Fig. 1E). Moreover, the P4 fraction demonstrated the most potent ability to stimulate neutrophil migration (P < 0.0001; Fig. 1F), while the P1 (P = 0.0005), P3 (P < 0.0001), P5 (P < 0.0001), and P6 (P = 0.0002) fractions also enhanced neutrophil migration compared to the control. The number of migrated cells was counted in different groups (Fig. S5B). Therefore, further investigation of the specific functional molecules in P4 was warranted.

The proteins in P4 were further analyzed through liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC‒MS/MS), and the top 10 proteins were identified according to the coverage of the peptides (Fig. 1G). Peptide coverage refers to the percentage of a protein’s amino acid sequence that is represented by the peptides identified in the MS/MS analysis, reflecting the extent and reliability of protein identification. Among the candidate proteins, calreticulin was selected for subsequent research based on its potential antibacterial and neutrophil-activating functions. To investigate the distribution of calreticulin in the nasal cavity, we stained the nasal cavity of fetal goats, 21-day-old goats, and 60-day-old goats via immunohistochemical staining. The nasal cavity was anatomically divided into five cross-sectional sections (CS), referred to as CSI to CSV, based on distinct spatial regions from anterior to posterior. Specifically, CSI and CSII correspond to the vestibular area, CSIII and CSIV represent the respiratory region, and CSV corresponds to the olfactory region. Figure 1H shows that calreticulin was released by glands beneath the nasal mucosa; higher levels of calreticulin were observed in the respiratory area and olfactory area than in the vestibular area, and calreticulin secretion increased with age, demonstrating age-related alterations.

Calreticulin in nasal mucus induces neutrophil migration and cooperates with neutrophils to demonstrate antimicrobial effects

We further validated the function of calreticulin in coordination with neutrophils to exert antibacterial effects; the recombinant calreticulin was obtained from the Pichia pastoris expression system (Fig. S6A-D). The impact of calreticulin on neutrophil migration was assessed in Transwell chambers coated with Matrigel. Neutrophil migration was notably increased (P < 0.0001) when 40 µg/mL calreticulin was added to the lower chamber of the Transwell plate (Fig. 2A). The statistical results of the number of migrated cells are displayed in Fig. S5C. In the neutrophil bactericidal test, the addition of 40 µg/mL calreticulin significantly increased the ability of neutrophils to kill Pasteurella multocida (Fig. 2B). In addition, to exclude potential contamination by TLR ligands, we used supernatants derived from Pichia pastoris cultures transfected with an empty vector plasmid as controls in neutrophil migration and bactericidal assays (Fig. S7).

Neutrophils undergo dynamic F-actin remodeling, an essential mechanism for migration. In the presence or absence of calreticulin stimulation, neutrophil F-actin was stained with phalloidin-FITC, and the results are shown in Fig. 2C. Following the treatment of neutrophils with 40 µg/mL calreticulin, the fluorescence intensity of the microfilament skeleton exhibited a progressive increase, reaching its peak at 30 min. Notably, calreticulin-treated neutrophils displayed an enhanced accumulation of F-actin at the cell periphery, characterized by the formation of membrane protrusions, including filopodia and lamellipodia, indicating active cytoskeletal remodeling. The activation of Rho GTPases (Cdc42, Rac1, and RhoA) is required for actin cytoskeleton rearrangement and cell polarity. Specifically, Cdc42 stimulates the production of filopodia, Rac1 induces lamellipodia formation, and RhoA induces the formation of stress fibers. After calreticulin activated neutrophils, the expression levels of Cdc42, Rac1, and RhoA steadily increased and peaked within 5 min (Fig. 2D). According to the CCK-8 data, calreticulin had no effect on neutrophil viability, confirming that the observed cytoskeletal changes were not attributable to cell damage (Fig. S4B).

ROS in neutrophils is crucial for the immune response, particularly in combating infections. ROS can oxidize and acidify microorganisms, leading to the destruction of their cell membranes, DNA, and proteins. ROS levels increased dramatically in a dose-dependent manner after neutrophils were treated with calreticulin for 3 h. Compared with those in the control group, the concentrations of 4 and 40 µg/mL calreticulin increased ROS levels in neutrophils by 11.5% ± 3.98 and 58.9% ± 3.10%, respectively. (Fig. 2E). These results indicate that calreticulin enhances neutrophil oxidative burst, thereby contributing to its antimicrobial potential.

The release of extracellular traps by neutrophils is another significant bactericidal mechanism. Activated neutrophils can release extracellular depolymerized chromatin during an infection or inflammatory response, creating an extracellular network structure that can trap pathogens and kill them with high quantities of harmful proteins. In this study, NETs were stained with SYTOX Green dye and an MPO antibody. NETs were observed following a 3 h treatment of neutrophils with 40 µg/mL calreticulin (Fig. 2F). Additionally, picogreen, a reagent for quantifying double-stranded DNA (dsDNA), was utilized to determine the quantity of extracellular dsDNA. The supernatant of neutrophils activated with calreticulin had a greater quantity of dsDNA than that of the control group (Fig. S5I), indicating enhanced NETs production upon calreticulin stimulation.

Studies have demonstrated the significance of the P38, Erk, and PI3K/AKT/GSK3β signaling pathways for neutrophil activation. These essential molecules control molecules or pathways linked to the formation of ROS, such as NADPH oxidase. Additionally, they control the activity of nuclear transcription factors such as NF-κB, which has an impact on the transcription and expression of genes linked to NETs. As shown in Fig. 2G, following calreticulin treatment of neutrophils, there was a progressive up-regulation of P38 and Erk phosphorylation, along with a gradual activation of the PI3K/AKT/GSK3β pathway. Therefore, our results indicated that calreticulin upregulated Rho GTPases and activated the PI3K/AKT/GSK3β, P38, and Erk signaling pathways, leading to neutrophil migration and synergistic antibacterial effects.

Calreticulin stimulates neutrophil transendothelial migration

Intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1), a significant adhesion molecule found on the surface of endothelial cells, interacts with ligands on the surface of white blood cells, including leukocyte function-associated antigen-1 (LFA-1), to facilitate intercellular adhesion. This adherence is necessary for immune cell movement and localization as well as the inflammatory response. Given the high conservation of calreticulin across various species (Fig. S8A) and the notable similarity in sequences and structures between goat and human calreticulin (Fig. S8B-C), we stimulated HUVECs with goat calreticulin in vitro. As shown in Fig. 3A and B, calreticulin induced a dose-dependent upregulation of ICAM-1 expression in HUVECs. To ascertain whether calreticulin induced the transendothelial migration of neutrophils, a coculture model comprising neutrophils and HUVECs was established, as illustrated in Fig. 3C. CFSE-labeled neutrophils were added to the apical surface of endothelial monolayers that had been prestimulated with different concentrations of calreticulin for 24 h. According to the CCK-8 data, calreticulin had no effect on the viability of HUVECs (Fig. S4C). As shown in Fig. 3D, the pretreatment of HUVECs with 10 µg/mL calreticulin significantly increased the transendothelial migration of neutrophils. Moreover, the number of neutrophils adhering to HUVECs increased significantly (Fig. 3E).

To investigate whether the transendothelial migration induced by calreticulin was mediated by ICAM-1, calreticulin-treated HUVECs were treated with the ICAM-1 inhibitor A205804. Calreticulin can interact with TLR2 and TLR4

To elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying calreticulin-mediated neutrophil activation, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed on neutrophils treated with calreticulin (40 µg/mL) for 0 min, 5 min, 30 min, and 60 min. Compared to those at 0 min, the number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (|log2FC|≥1 and false discovery rate [FDR] < 0.05) continuously increased over time until 60 min, with 787 genes upregulated and 228 downregulated. We performed Gene Ontology (GO) analysis on DEGs using the GOseq R package (Fig. S9A-B). As shown in Fig. 4A, compared with 0 min, the DEGs were highly enriched in the two biological processes of cell migration and leukocyte activation, and the DEGs increased in a time-dependent manner, which was consistent with our findings. Furthermore, DEGs were investigated using Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis to identify biological pathways enriched during calreticulin stimulation. The Toll-like receptor signaling pathway was identified through KEGG enrichment analysis, and the number of DEGs also gradually increased with increasing stimulation time (Fig. 4B). These findings suggest that calreticulin may activate neutrophils through engagement of TLR2 and TLR4-mediated signaling pathways, providing mechanistic insight into its immunomodulatory role.

Next, molecular docking simulations were employed to predict the possibility of interactions between calreticulin and TLR2 and between calreticulin and TLR4. As shown in Fig. 4C (a-d), blue represents TLR2, and green represents calreticulin. The red dotted line represents the salt bridge, while the blue dotted line represents the hydrogen bond. The negatively charged ASP71, GLU35, and ASP31 of calreticulin formed a possible salt bridge interaction with the positively charged ARG574, ARG447, and LYS488 of TLR2, and the positively charged ARG73 and ARG80 of calreticulin may form a salt bridge interaction with the negatively charged ASP557 and GLU369 of TLR2. The positively charged LYS196 and LYS271 of TLR4 may form a salt bridge interaction with the negatively charged ASP45 and ASP201 of calreticulin, and SER235, ARG267, and SER239 of TLR4 may form hydrogen bond interactions with LYS153, TYR150, and LEU156 of calreticulin with the predicted hydrogen bond distances of 3.1, 2.8, and 3.1 Å, respectively.

The interaction between calreticulin and both TLR2 and TLR4 was confirmed using a GST pull-down assay (Fig. 4D). We acquired the extracellular domains of goat TLR2 and TLR4 using a prokaryotic expression system (Fig. S6E-J). Goat calreticulin exhibited strong binding affinity for recombinant GST-TLR2 and GST-TLR4 fusion proteins, but not for GST alone. We additionally extracted the membrane proteins of goat neutrophils, which possess the natural full-length TLR2/TLR4 conformation, to more accurately replicate physiologically relevant conditions. Figure 4E illustrated that GST was unable to bind to TLR2 and TLR4 on the neutrophil membrane. Nonetheless, the goat GST-tagged calreticulin demonstrated the ability to bind to both TLR2 and TLR4. Together, these results provide compelling evidence that calreticulin directly interacts with TLR2 and TLR4 under both recombinant and native conditions.

Calreticulin induces neutrophil migration in a TLR2-dependent manner

In order to further investigate whether calreticulin induced neutrophil migration through TLR2 and TLR4, neutrophils were pretreated with a TLR2 and TLR4 inhibitor (sparstolonin B [SsnB], 10 µM), a TLR2 inhibitor (C29, 25 µM), and a TLR4 inhibitor (C34, 10 µM) for 30 min to assess the impact of these inhibitors on calreticulin-induced neutrophil migration. Cell viability assays confirmed that none of the inhibitors exerted cytotoxic effects on neutrophils (Fig. S4E). As shown in Fig. 5A, calreticulin-induced neutrophil migration was inhibited by SsnB (P = 0.8929) and C29 (P = 0.9970), but C34 (P < 0.0001) had no effect on this process. Quantification of migrated cells is presented in Fig. S5D, further supporting these observations. Consistently, SsnB and C29 inhibited the reorganization of the neutrophil microfilament skeleton induced by calreticulin, as illustrated in Fig. 5B. In contrast, similar to neutrophils stimulated with calreticulin, neutrophils treated with C34 accumulated F-actin at the cell edge and formed pseudopods. Additionally, we examined the effect of these inhibitors on calreticulin-induced Rho GTPases. Western blot analysis revealed that calreticulin-induced upregulation of Cdc42, Rac1, and RhoA expression was reversed by SsnB and C29, but C34 did not suppress the upregulation of Rho GTPases (Fig. 5C).

Calreticulin induces neutrophils to release ROS and NETs in a TLR2- and TLR4-dependent manner

Next, we studied how TLR2 and TLR4 impact the release of ROS and NETs in neutrophils induced by calreticulin. Figure 6A showed that only SsnB effectively blocked the increase in ROS levels in neutrophils stimulated with calreticulin. Neutrophils pre-treated with C29 and C34 showed an increase in ROS levels similar to that of neutrophils treated with calreticulin alone. The release of NETs was consistent with the amount of ROS generated. Figure 6B demonstrated that SsnB suppressed the production of calreticulin-induced NETs, the nuclei were lobed, and MPO expression was evenly distributed throughout the cytoplasm. The DNA fibers in the calreticulin group and the C29 and C34 pretreatment groups depolymerized, forming a network structure disseminated with MPO. Picogreen staining was used to quantify dsDNA released into the culture, and in accordance with the results observed by confocal microscopy above, SsnB inhibited calreticulin chromatin release in neutrophils, while C29 and C34 did not inhibit the release of calreticulin-stimulated NETs (Fig. S5J). In addition, we further carried out antibody blocking experiments, and the results were consistent with the above results, that is, the migration of neutrophils induced by calreticulin was blocked by the TLR2 antibody or the combination of the TLR2 antibody and the TLR4 antibody, while the intracellular ROS level and NETs release induced by calreticulin were blocked only when the TLR2 and TLR4 antibodies were added at the same time (Fig. S10). To exclude the possibility that these effects were due to antibody-induced cytotoxicity, CCK-8 assays confirmed that the blocking antibodies did not compromise neutrophil viability (Fig. S4F). Moreover, we investigated the effect of calreticulin on the key downstream molecules of TLR2 and TLR4. SsnB inhibited the time-dependent activation of the PI3K/AKT/GSK3β, P38, and Erk signaling pathways induced by calreticulin (Fig. 6C - E). When neutrophils were pretreated with C29 and C34, the levels of phosphorylated PI3K/AKT/GSK3β, P38, and Erk still increased gradually and peaked at 5 min after calreticulin stimulation. Collectively, these data indicate that calreticulin-induced neutrophil ROS generation and NET release are co-regulated by both TLR2 and TLR4.

Nasal administration of calreticulin can induce the recruitment of neutrophils to the nasal mucosa and accelerate the clearance of Pasteurella multocida

We further confirmed the role of calreticulin in the recruitment of neutrophils in vivo. Figure 7A presents the schematic diagram of nasal administration in goats. Figure 7B is a schematic diagram of the calreticulin nasal administration experiment, which included a control group and a calreticulin group. The respiratory area has more immune cells and blood vessels than the vestibular area; thus, we performed immunohistochemical staining of the nasal respiratory area for MPO in those two groups. The results indicated that a limited quantity of neutrophils was found in the nasal mucosa of the control group, while the number of neutrophils in the nasal mucosa increased after 24 h of nasal calreticulin administration (Fig. 7C). Quantitative analysis of MPO-positive cells confirmed this observation and is presented in Fig. S11A. Furthermore, the neutrophils that migrated to the nasal cavity were observed in three distinct forms: individual neutrophils scattered between the nasal epithelial cells (Fig. 7Cd), aggregated neutrophils covered with abundant mucus (Fig. 7Ce), and neutrophils moving to the free surface of the nasal epithelial cells (Fig. 7Cf). Moreover, we employed ELISA to quantify the neutrophil chemoattractant CXCL8 and the proinflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-1β across distinct anatomical compartments of the respiratory tract mucosa—including the vestibular, respiratory, and olfactory regions of the nasal mucosa (Fig. 7D), as well as the nasopharynx, trachea, and lung (Fig. S11B-D)—and observed that intranasal administration of calreticulin significantly upregulated the secretion of these cytokines specifically within the respiratory region of the nasal mucosa. In addition, calreticulin administration increased the expression of ICAM-1 in the nasal mucosa (Fig. 7E). Furthermore, Fig. 7F demonstrates that calreticulin also activated P38, Erk, and the PI3K/AKT/GSK3β pathway in the respiratory region of the nasal mucosa. Figure 7G is the schematic diagram of the Pasteurella multocida infection experiment. The goats were divided into a control group, a Pasteurella multocida group, and a calreticulin treatment group. At 24 hpi, we harvested the same mass of lung tissue and ground the tissue for colony counting, and the bacterial burden of the calreticulin protected group was 12.67 ± 2.33 times lower than that of the Pasteurella multocida group (Fig. 7H). Moreover, the bacterial burden in various respiratory tract regions was determined by reverse transcriptase quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) (Fig. 7I). In the calreticulin treatment group, the bacterial burden of the respiratory mucosa was much lower than that in the Pasteurella multocida group, except for that in the vestibular area.

Discussion

With daily exposure to inhaled pathogens, the nasal mucosal barrier is an important defense, including physical, chemical, microbial, and immune barriers, which could closely collaborate to increase resistance to pathogen invasion. Within respiratory mucus, lactoferrin and lysozyme have been shown to exert synergistic antimicrobial effects, whereas human β-defensins, cathelicidin LL-37, and lysozyme collectively display additive bactericidal activity against Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli [28]. Moreover, immune cells also showed an excellent synergistic bacterial killing effect. For example, NETs formation enhances the phagocytic function of macrophages and transfers biologically active neutrophil-specific antimicrobial peptides to macrophages, which are critical to combating bacterial pathogens [29]. Upon pathogen infection of the upper respiratory tract, nasal mucus contains abundant neutrophils that migrate into the nasal mucosa, and these neutrophils eliminate pathogens through phagocytosis or release NETs and strengthen the epithelial barrier [30, 31]. However, the active proteins in nasal secretions that induce neutrophil recruitment and their effects on neutrophil function remain unclear. Research on mucus composition and its protective mechanisms has long been hampered by the inherent difficulty in obtaining high-quality nasal mucus samples. Nevertheless, the anatomy, genetics, and physiology of the goat respiratory tract are closely related to those of humans, making goats ideal model animals for studying respiratory mucus [32–34]. Here, we provide novel insights demonstrating that calreticulin derived from nasal mucus promotes neutrophil recruitment and cooperatively enhances neutrophil-mediated bactericidal activity.

When pathogenic bacteria successfully overcome the physical barrier provided by nasal mucus and come into contact with airway epithelial cells, microbial components and host-derived signals at the site of interaction activate local endothelial cells, leading to the rapid recruitment of circulating neutrophils across the endothelial barrier to eliminate the invading pathogens [35]. In the nasal mucosa of goats, numerous capillaries are densely distributed beneath the epithelium, serving as a major source of neutrophils that subsequently infiltrate into the epithelial layer. Neutrophil transendothelial migration involves a multistep process, including neutrophil tethering, rolling, adhesion, crawling, and migration [36]. These steps are regulated by shear forces, chemokines, and inducible adhesion molecules expressed by endothelial cells. The interaction of ICAM-1 on the vascular endothelium and LFA-1 expressed on neutrophil surfaces is critical for regulating adhesion and transendothelial migration in neutrophils [37]. For instance, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, a significant opportunistic pathogen in cystic fibrosis patients, induces mast cell–derived IL-1α and IL-1β, which enhance ICAM-1 and E-selectin expression on endothelial cells, thereby promoting neutrophil transendothelial migration and recruitment to the site of infection [38]. In this study, calreticulin upregulated ICAM-1 expression in endothelial cells, promoted the adhesion of neutrophils to vascular endothelial cells, and facilitated neutrophil transendothelial migration. Following extravasation, calreticulin further activated neutrophils, facilitating their directed migration toward the nasal epithelium and triggering the release of ROS and NETs to eliminate invading pathogens.

The efficient recruitment of neutrophils and the execution of their antimicrobial effector functions critically depend on a series of immunoreceptors on the plasma membrane. There are more than 30 distinct receptors expressed on the surface of neutrophils, including G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), Fc receptors, adhesion receptors (e.g., integrins or selectins), cytokine receptors, and pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), which collectively sense proinflammatory mediators and regulate neutrophil migration, activation, and functional responses [39, 40]. Several membrane surface molecules, such as CD59, CD91, and the scavenger receptor, have been suggested by earlier researchers to be functional calreticulin receptors [41, 42]. Calreticulin is found on the surface of circulating neutrophils and lacks both a transmembrane domain and a glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) anchor attachment site. Therefore, calreticulin must be connected to the plasma membrane via the adapter molecule CD59. The crosslinking of antibodies and calreticulin causes a rapid increase in the intracellular free Ca2+ concentration due to the release of Ca2+ from storage, subsequently activating neutrophil oxidase activation [43]. Moreover, calreticulin expression is greater on the surface of tumor cells, apoptotic cells, and some drug-treated cells (such as anthracyclines) than on the surface of healthy cells. This calreticulin can bind to the scavenger receptor CD91, which is found on the surface of numerous innate immune cells, such as macrophages and dendritic cells, thereby promoting immune-mediated phagocytosis and clearance of these aberrant cells [44, 45]. However, the direct interaction between extracellular calreticulin and membrane receptors on neutrophils remains largely uncharacterized. In this study, we demonstrate for the first time that calreticulin directly binds to TLR2 and TLR4 on neutrophils. This finding uncovers a previously unrecognized pathway through which calreticulin may regulate neutrophil recruitment and activation, thereby contributing to the innate immune response at mucosal surfaces.

Toll-like receptors are crucial molecules that participate in the initiation of neutrophil activation [46]. Upon ligand binding, TLRs trigger intracellular signaling cascades that modulate key neutrophil functions, including transcriptional activity, phagocytosis, degranulation, ROS production, NETs release, and so on. Among the key downstream pathways, the MAPK signaling modules (Erk, JNK, and p38) and the PI3K/AKT/GSK3β axis play central roles in promoting pro-inflammatory gene expression and enhancing immune cell functionality [47, 48]. For example, during Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection, TLR2 and TLR4 mediate the activation of the kinases NF-κB, p38, ERK, and JNK in neutrophils, leading to the production of IL-6, IL-23, and IL-17. Notably, IL-17 generated by autocrine neutrophils has an important function in suppressing Mycobacterium tuberculosis growth by controlling the formation of ROS and neutrophil migration during the early stages of infection [49]. In the tumor microenvironment, tumor-associated neutrophils have been shown to produce NETs via the PI3K/AKT/ROS axis, which, through a positive feedback mechanism, enhances tumor proliferation, invasion, and metastasis [50]. In our study, we demonstrated that calreticulin activated PI3K/AKT/GSK3β, P38, and Erk in a TLR2- and TLR4-dependent manner. This activation was observed both in vitro and following nasal immunization in vivo, indicating that calreticulin serves as a potent immunomodulatory signal capable of engaging neutrophil signaling cascades. Moreover, PI3K catalyzes the intracellular conversion of phosphatidylinositol diphosphate (PIP2) to phosphatidylinositol triphosphate (PIP3), thereby initiating downstream signaling cascades involving members of the Rho family of GTPases [51, 52]. For instance, Staphylococcus aureus rapidly activates the Rho GTPases Rac1 and Cdc42 in a TLR2-dependent manner in the human monocyte line THP-1 and in TLR2-expressing 293 cells. These GTPases, along with the PI3K subunit (p85) bind to the cytoplasmic domain of TLR2 and regulate NF-кB transactivation via Akt signaling, thereby contributing critically to signal transduction pathways in innate immune cells [53]. Consistently, our studies revealed that calreticulin activated Cdc42, Rac1, and RhoA in a TLR2-dependent manner, suggesting that calreticulin facilitates neutrophil migration by promoting cytoskeletal remodeling through Rho GTPase activation.

Conclusion

In summary, our study elucidated the underlying mechanism by which calreticulin identified in nasal mucus could induce neutrophil migration and activate neutrophils to accelerate the clearance of pathogenic bacteria, which is critical for understanding the immune defense mechanism of the nasal mucosa (Fig. 8). Given the high degree of homology between human and goat calreticulin, further investigations are warranted to determine whether similar neutrophil-mediated antibacterial responses are triggered in the human nasal mucosal environment and to explore the potential of calreticulin as a target for therapeutic interventions against respiratory infections.
