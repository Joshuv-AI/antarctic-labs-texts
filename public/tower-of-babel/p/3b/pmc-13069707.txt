Logics of (dis)engagement: mapping variation in non-government norms and approaches to alcohol, ultra-processed food and related industries - a case study from Australia
Authors: Belinda Townsend, Jeff Collin, Katherine Cullerton, Kathrin Lauber, Liz Arnanz, Jane Martin, Fran Baum, Sharon Friel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA central question in contemporary health governance is how to prevent undue corporate influence. There is limited practical guidance beyond the tobacco control model. Global partnership approaches have continued to promote alcohol and ultra-processed food (UPF) industry engagement, and these industries have influenced policy and governance in ways that benefit their interests, often to the detriment of strong health regulation. In Australia industry influence has delayed the introduction of mandatory alcohol health warning labels and ensured voluntary rather than mandatory front of pack food labelling. This qualitative study is the first to our knowledge to map underlying institutional logics shaping variation in the interactions of NGOs who are not reliant on industry core funding with alcohol, UPF and related industries.ResultsDrawing on thirty interviews with senior policy and advocacy staff from nongovernment organisations active in alcohol and/or UPF advocacy in Australia, we find shared opposition to partnerships with the alcohol industry and significant variation towards approaches to interactions with the UPF industry. All interviewees spoke of challenges in delineating the interests of other industries that may work with alcohol and UPF, such as supermarkets and retailers, advertising agencies and public relation firms, and banks and investment entities. We identify four institutional logics for disengagement with alcohol and UPF industries: a logic of reputational risk, a logic of delegitimation, an FCTC logic, and a logic of competing interests. In contrast, we find two logics for limited engagement with alcohol and UPF industries: a logic of intelligence gathering, and a logic of ‘acting reasonable’. Finally, we identify two logics for engagement with a wider range of industries: a logic of partnership and a logic of revenue raising. Only a small number of organisations had formal written policies on conflicts of interest.ConclusionsWe find that many organisations have conflicting internal logics creating challenges for organisational coherence. The development of internal risk assessments by some organisations indicate efforts by staff to prevent undue corporate influence. We recommend greater transparency of policies by NGOs on preventing corporate influence, preventing conflicts of interest, and approaches to engaging a variety of corporate actors, and the establishment of a global community of practice for sharing innovative tools and approaches to preventing undue corporate influence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12992-026-01202-2.Keywords: Health policy, Advocacy, Conflict of interest, Global health, Commercial determinants of health, Civil society, NGO, Alcohol, UPF

Introduction

A central question in contemporary health governance is how to prevent undue corporate influence. Tobacco, alcohol, ultra-processed food (UPF), fossil fuel, and gambling industries use similar tactics to advance their interests, often at the expense of population health [1–6]. Shared tactics include lobbying, political party donations, direct participation in government agencies, engaging public relations (PR) firms, funding research, funding front groups and issue framing [7–12]. Government and civil society organisations engage with a diversity of commercial actors in various ways, including but not limited to; consulting, participating alongside in government committees, forming partnerships, receiving fundraising and sponsorships [13–16].

There is limited practical guidance from intergovernmental organisations for governments and civil society organisations on how to prevent corporate influence and protect health beyond the WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (FCTC) and International Code on Marketing of Breastmilk Substitutes [17]. Global initiatives such as the UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) continue to promote alcohol and UPF industry partnership approaches, enabling these industries to influence policy and governance often to the detriment of strong health regulation [18]. This is pervasive across national contexts. Ongoing industry influence in Australiadelayed the introduction of mandatory alcohol health warning labels and has ensured voluntary rather than mandatory front of pack food labelling [19, 20].

Aside from the FCTC and WHO Breastmilk Code, global initiatives to provide guidance for governments to prevent corporate influence are nascent. These include recent WHO guidance on corporate influence in noncommunicable diseases [21], and - still in draft - WHO guidance on nutrition policy [22]. There are no substantive equivalents however for civil society and nongovernment (NGO) advocacy organisations. who are increasingly active in different governance roles globally and nationally [23–25].

It is well-recognised that NGOs serve a variety of functions beyond traditional agenda-setting and issue framing, including in policy formulation and implementation, standard setting, monitoring, enforcement, and holding corporate actors and governments to account [15, 26, 27]. Yet, little is known about how NGOs are navigating the complex governance landscape, particularly relating to interactions with corporate actors whose interests collide with public health objectives. This is particularly important because without effective prevention of undue influence, NGOs themselves can be open to corporate influence and thus conflicts of interest – with ramifications for both health and equity objectives and their reputations [28–30]. A recent body of work has examined how alcohol and other harmful commodity industries are using corporate social responsibility initiatives to fund organisations who, in turn, promote industry objectives and narratives (often called Social Aspects Public Relations Organizations - SAPROs) [31–35]. This work has documented how industry funded groups behave differently to non-industry funded groups, including repeating industry narratives about alcohol harm and promoting individual responsibility [16, 36]; and misleading evidence on cancer risk [12], on alcohol harms in pregnancy [37], and alcohol consumption and heart disease [38]. This study is the first to our knowledge to interrogate variation in how NGOs active in alcohol and UPF advocacy, who are not receiving alcohol and UPF industry core funding, are navigating potential interactions with alcohol, UPF, and related industries. Focused on Australia, we ask: in what ways are NGOs active in alcohol and UPF advocacy interacting with these industries? What norms are shaping organisational approaches for or against engagement? What policies or tools are used to manage interactions, if any?

Institutional logics

We draw on the concept of institutional logics from sociology and organisation studies [39–41]. Institutional logics are the underlying organising principles which shape organisational culture and the repertoire of what is considered acceptable behavior [42]. They serve as a common frame of reference within organisations, providing individuals with a ‘shared vocabulary’ of preferences, embedded in guidelines and decision-making practices [39]. Institutional logics have been applied to understand the practices of business, state and civil society in issue areas such as climate governance [43], pesticides [44], healthcare and medicine [45, 46], and not for profit and social enterprise studies [47, 48]. A logics approach has enabled interrogation of the ‘marketization’ of the not-for-profit and charity sectors [48, 49]. Logics approaches have been used, for example, to map transformation of breast cancer advocacy in the US towards consumer corporate philanthropy [50] and competing state and charitable logics within charities in Scotland’s National Health Service [51]. We apply a logics analysis to interrogate variation in NGO positions and organisational practices towards alcohol, UPF and related industries.

Method

Interviews

30 qualitative semi-structured interviews were conducted by the first author with representatives from NGOs active in alcohol and/or UPF advocacy in Australia. A review of all submissions to government food and alcohol inquiries in Australia over 5-year period identified 134 NGOs who had made submissions [26]. This list was reviewed by a panel of academic and practitioner experts to purposively recruit organisations for interview. Organisational contacts were then identified from website and annual report searches and interview snowball recommendations led to further interviews. Of the organisations interviewed, 22 were active in both alcohol and UPF, four only alcohol and two only UPF. Organisations included charities, professional member associations (representing a group of professionals), independent statutory bodies (a semi-public administrative body outside government but receiving financial support from it) and other non-government organisations (Fig. 1). We did not include NGOs with core funding from alcohol or UPF industries (nor their campaigns, like DrinkWise) because these groups often serve as front groups for industry and have been found to advance industry objectives compared to non-industry funded groups [32, 34, 36, 52].

Analysis

Prior to interviews, organisation websites were scanned and documents including annual reports (including funding sources) and publicly available policies were collated and read. Interviewees were asked about their tactics to influence alcohol and/or UPF industries, how they navigate any interactions with these actors or commercial actors related to these industries, if their approach differed by industry, and if they had any policies or tools to manage decisions about interactions (supplementary file). Interviews were conducted between January and August 2023. Interviews were approximately one hour duration, were conducted in person or online and were recorded and transcribed. Interview transcripts were coded using NVivo software; nodes included types of NGO interactions with commercial actors; views on engaging alcohol or UPF companies; views on engaging other commercial actors; policies, norms or tools to guide interactions; and other themes that emerged from the interview date. Following established methods for qualitative analysis of institutional logics [53] we read text segments to code and group text into patterned themes of behaviour and beliefs towards commercial actors. This study is part of a wider project investigating the advocacy strategies used by organisations attempting to influence alcohol and UPF industries and their regulation. We acknowledge our positionality as a group of academic researchers on the commercial determinants of health and practitioners in policy advocacy. Ethics approval was received from the first author’s institution and was guided by Australia’s National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research (ANU, ethics number 2021/017).

Results

A spectrum of engagement

Interviews revealed that NGOs active in alcohol and UPF advocacy take a variety of approaches. We first describe organisational approaches and types of interaction with different commercial actors. We then identify different institutional logics driving these approaches.

Most interviewees reported that they did not interact directly with alcohol or UPF companies. It is important to note that interviewee responses focused on the policy and advocacy work of the organisation. Background reading of annual reports and websites prior to interviews revealed that some organisations did interact with UPF companies through limited sponsorships (e.g. sponsorship of an exhibition or conference). This discrepancy was explored during the interviews which revealed internal organisational challenges, which we explore below. There was no evidence of alcohol industry sponsorships or partnerships in the data.

A small number of interviewees reflected that their organisation did directly interact with an alcohol and/or UPF company as part of their advocacy work. These reported interactions comprised: holding a direct meeting with a company representative, sitting across from a company representative on a government taskforce or inquiry, writing a letter to company executives, or attending a company shareholder meeting.

There was a clear challenge evident across all interviews for organisations in delineating the boundaries of what constituted ‘the alcohol industry’ or the ‘UPF industry’ and which commercial actors were seen as related to but distinct from those industries. Commercial actors considered to be related to these industries included PR and advertising agencies that worked for alcohol or UPF companies, banks that invested in them, and retailers and supermarkets that supplied their products. For many interviewees, this was a challenging ‘grey area’:

Where does this stop and start? If you actually go through industry by industry by industry, and ask yourself the really difficult questions about the environment, about health in general… what you end up doing is realising that actually there are some things over here that are black, there are some things over here that are white and there’s a whole great big grey area. (charity)

The challenge is always in the grey areas. So, it’s easy to point to the tobacco industry or alcohol, or junk food industries, or you know, soft drink industry, but where do you draw the line on things like supermarkets that sell alcohol and tobacco? And where do you draw the line on sporting organisations that take sponsorship from junk food, alcohol industries? Whilst also purporting to be a healthy activity. So, that’s one of the bigger challenges. (charity)

So lobbying is the hardest, to be honest. So to find the lobbying firm who does not work with all the industries that we don’t work with is completely impossible and so what we do is the best that we can really… so tobacco is a hard no, alcohol is pretty much also a hard no, and then from a lobbying perspective, we have to work with lobbyists who also work for pharma because those who don’t just don’t exist (charity)

Within the interview cohort, alcohol-focused NGOs were more likely to see supermarkets as part of the alcohol industry (and thus a de-facto no-go zone for partnering with or receiving sponsorship or funding from). Disease-focused charities appeared to be more likely to see supermarkets as separate from these industry sectors, although this was not homogenous:

Woolworths [major supermarket chain] was an alcohol industry actor, because they owned Dan Murphy’s and they still have an interest in alcohol sales, even though they’ve got rid of the alcohol arm of their business, they’ve divested that. So Endeavour Group is an independent company now, but Woolworths has a substantial shareholding in the company, so they still have lots of influence in the company. (alcohol focused NGO)

Coles and Woolworths [major supermarket chains] is always a challenge… but we’ll say their primary purpose is not to sell cigarettes, and if their revenues from cigarettes are less than X, Y, Z %, say it’s less than 1%, and our investment as a portfolio investment is less than 1%, then you’ve got less than 1% of less than 1%, you’ve got the square root of nothing…. And so we kind of take that kind of approach that says we’re not saying nothing ever, ever, ever, because it’s actually physically impossible to do so, and this is where the grey area comes in. (charity)

we’ve got a very small pool of possible corporate partners because even the likes of like Coles and Woolies are not an option. We’ve previously had partnerships with them, but because they sell lots of things that we don’t like, we no longer do that. (charity)

Institutional logics

We identified several institutional logics informing the positions and practices of organisations (See Table 1). We identify four logics of disengagement with alcohol and UPF industries: a logic of reputation risk, a logic of delegitimation, an FCTC logic, and a logic of competing interests. In contrast, we find two logics for limited engagement with alcohol and UPF industries: a logic of intelligence gathering, and a logic of ‘acting reasonable’. Finally, we identify two logics for engagement with a wider range of industries: a logic of partnership and a logic of revenue raising. Importantly, for some organisations there were apparent internal tension between competing logics, creating challenges for organisations in developing and maintaining a clear approach. We outline the logics first, then explore challenges in the section below.

Four institutional logics appeared to inform the beliefs, normative expectations and decision-making by policy and advocacy interviewees to not engage directly with alcohol or UPF industries:

FCTC logic

Many interviewees had experiences with tobacco advocacy and viewed the alcohol or UPF industries as ‘like’ the tobacco industry. Here, interviewees cited internal norms against engagement with tobacco industry actors as informing their approach to other harmful commodity industries. Several interviewees referred to the WHO FCTC Art 5.3 which stipulates that parties protect public health tobacco control policies from vested interests of the tobacco industry as an important roadblock and guiding logic. While some organisations took this approach for both alcohol and UPF, others only spoke of this in relation to the alcohol industry, suggesting variation towards these two industries across organisations:

When it comes to the alcohol industry, we have no trust and we treat them in the same category as the tobacco industry. We don’t work with them, we don’t trust them, we don’t believe they should be sitting in the policy decision making table with regard to alcohol and health policies. (professional member association)

Our strategies are influenced or maybe tainted by our tobacco experience… So we don’t do any engagement with industry in alcohol or ultra-processed foods. They’re in fact on our list of organisations that we don’t engage with… we have a fairly straight up and down, black and white agreement that we don’t engage with harmful industries (charity)

We have rules about when we’re working with other organisations and what they do with those industries. And tobacco and alcohol are out and out deal breakers. So if we, for example, go to another organisation and if they have clients who are tobacco or alcohol we won’t work with them…So we’re very… we take a tobacco, FCTC like approach to working with the industry in that respect (charity)

Logic of reputational risk

Several interviewees expressed a logic of reputational risk, concerned with preventing reputational damage to the NGO for associating or appearing to associate with alcohol and/or UPF industries due to potential perceptions by the public or policymakers that they may be unduly influenced:

we just had a request from a manufacturer of toddler foods to partner, to fundraise on our behalf, put the logo on things, and that’s becoming more common I think as well. So there’s this reputational risk issue (NGO)

We used to have [marking associated with NGO brand]we did a deal with company X where they got the NGO symbol… Company X then slapped [the symbol] on every outlet in the country – which I think frankly we should have seen coming – and that trashed the brand (charity)

A logic of delegitimation

A third logic driving individuals and organisational approaches to not engage directly with alcohol or UPF companies was a logic of delegitimation – a desire to prevent industry actors from gaining from any engagement with the NGO in a way that benefited their market interests. This cohort of interviewees appeared to be influenced by past experiences and by well known cases in the community where companies had misrepresented interactions with NGOs, and they (or their colleagues) had been caught off guard:

It is something that we actively avoid because the industries that we are talking about, alcohol, junk food…. are known for manipulating any meeting that you might have with them into consent and approval of whatever approach it is that they’re doing because time and time the experience that we’ve had… (professional member association)

We’re cautious, I mean I’ve now got five, I think emails from the Australian Beverages Council, desperate for me to talk to them about their voluntary program to reduce the sugar in beverages, in sugary drinks…. And if they as an industry can tell the government they’ve consulted with us and we’re all best mates, that’s going to strengthen their position to run the defence against the introduction of a sugary drinks tax. I haven’t responded to any of those emails. (professional member association)

A logic of competing interests

A final logic underpinning decision making not to directly interact with alcohol and UPF companies was a view by many interviewees that these companies had competing interests that could not be reconciled with the mission of their organisation:

Well, we regularly get letters asking us to meet with industry…. And we’ve taken the view that we’re not going to meet with them and their interest is about selling their products, not about public health. So that’s why we wouldn’t meet with industry, we don’t really think that the sale of unhealthy products is a good public health agenda. (professional member association)

We tend not to engage directly with let’s say food, alcohol, tobacco industry for mainly just because we do deem that there’s an immediate conflict of interest in terms of their commercially vested interest… we wouldn’t ever partner with them. So if a wine manufacturer came and said we’ll give you ten grand a year for something, we wouldn’t do it. (charity)

The challenges and resource needs for managing and navigating the range of commercial actors meant that some smaller NGOs took a ‘no engagement’ approach to all commercial actors:

we don’t have the time and the resource to manage that success and to manage that relationship and to make sure it doesn’t come back to bite us. (NGO)

it’s such a powerful, well-resourced industry, you know it’s a bit like if you engage with Putin you need to have plenty of time to manage the relationship. (NGO)

Logics for limited engagement with alcohol and/or UPF industries

Four interviewees noted that they had directly engaged with an alcohol or UPF company as part of their advocacy in limited interactions – namely, holding a meeting, taking a phone call, and meeting with executives or shareholders. Two institutional logics appeared to inform this approach: a logic of intelligence gathering, and a logic of ‘acting reasonable’. We outline each in turn.

Logic of intelligence gathering

A logic of intelligence gathering was evident in the decisions by a few interviewees to directly interact with an alcohol or UPF company as part of their advocacy work. Here, interviewees noted that they limited their interaction to holding a meeting with a company representative, or sitting on a government taskforce or committee where an industry representative was also present, in order to attempt to glean information from them, to inform their advocacy strategy.

Interviewees sought to use these interactions to gather information on industry arguments and potential impacts of proposed regulations:

We have met with a soft drink manufacturer because if we’re pushing reformulation, we’re pushing clean water, a lot of those companies also make bottled water, a lot of them also make a non-sugar alternative. We’ll have a conversation with them. Does it influence our position? No. Does it help us better understand the impact of our proposed policy when we talk to central agencies? Yes (professional member association)

If I’m not there [at the table alongside industry], I don’t know how bad things are, or how good things are, or what’s going on. I don’t hear the intel from police on what’s going on in that environment. I don’t hear from the regulator how chummy they are with different players. And again our key role is to understand how you move these people. You need to see who they’re friends with. You need to see how they’re engaging, how they’re interacting. (NGO)

Logic of ‘acting reasonable’

A second underlying logic driving two interviewees for limited interactions was about maintaining their professional image with policymakers as ‘reasonable’ in the face of industry criticism. Notably, two interviewees reflected that they had met with an alcohol company to head off potential criticism by policymakers and industry:

In terms of meeting with the industry, we don’t routinely catch up with them. But there are different points in time where they weaponize the requests for a meeting. And where if we don’t meet with them, we’re seen as unreasonable….so they requested a meeting, and I held that meeting. When we hold the meetings, we have to have at least one other person present, so that’s really important so that it’s not communicated back… We went back and told the [government] advisor we had a meeting, we tried to talk through options, this is where it stood. But at least that was the conversation then, not you’re so unreasonable you won’t even sit down with them. So that’s the time when we engage, when it’s weaponised against us. (NGO)

Logics for engagement with a wider range of industries

Many interviewees noted that their organisation interacted in some form with other commercial industries that could be perceived to be part of the alcohol industry or UPF industry, or working closely with these industries. These included industries such as: supermarkets, grocers and retailers; advertising agencies; PR agencies; and banks and investment entities. Forms of engagement noted by interviewees here included partnerships, sponsorships, contracts for services, grant funding arrangements to other organisations, or the NGO receiving funding. Some entities noted sponsorships with UPF companies, but there was no evidence of these kinds of interactions with alcohol companies. Here, we found two logics driving this range of engagement activities: a logic of partnership and a logic of revenue raising.

A logic of partnership

The first underlying ‘logic of partnership’ reflected beliefs and preferences by interviewees for partnering with commercial entities to create shared value for both the NGO and industry. Underlying this preference for partnership were assumptions about the benefits of commercial actors:

I do think that we’re, generally speaking, not very good at understanding what the KPIs in a business are, what motivates a business. Which is why I’m very keen on saying that [our brand] on a corporate has to help them make money. Because that’s what they do. It’s got to help them sell more of what they make. Having worked out how they can make more money by associating with the charity, then you can talk to them about how they can do things which are more in line with the behaviours that the charity is trying to encourage. And if you then get both of them, you’ve got what the charity wants and you’ve got what the business wants, and then you’ve got the basis for a very long-term partnership. (charity)

Logic of revenue raising

The second logic was narrowly instrumental and also reflected core financial objectives for NGOs to raise revenue for their operations. Here, interactions with industries were viewed as a way of generating income:

what’s going to get me in the door to talk to Coles? [supermarket] Is it telling them off about their sugar-sweetened beverages aisle? Or talking to them positively about the position of vegetables in it? I mean, if I’m ringing to tell them off, I’m not going to get in the door. If I’m ringing to say, “This part of your work, I think is great…” I’ll probably get in to see the CEO [chief executive officer]… I mean, it’s just, there’s a realpolitik to it, but I think we’ll have more impact, and we’ll make more money, crudely. That’s how I, I think both of those things are true. (charity)

Competing internal logics

The interviews revealed that many organisations have competing internal logics creating organisational incoherence. This was particularly apparent for charities and professional member associations. For professional member associations, interviews revealed discrepancies between the organisational approach and the approach of individual members in several associations, both for alcohol and for UPFs. As interviewees from two different member organisations noted:

We have [members] who rely on a fundraising stream, for example, and may have had over time a relationship with a drug company, with an alcohol kind of distributor who provides kind of stuff for balls and other bits and pieces that they do. And so I just think the line for us is just sort of, is figuring out… you know, we’re very clear on the bits that we won’t accept, but the bits that we will accept are a little bit less clear. (professional member association)

As a member group and organisation, we have members who may receive funding from industry, NGOs who may receive some funding from industry for certain activities. And, you know, we have had some people say to us, “Well, you should not let them be members.” And I… and, you know, we’ve talked about that at length and that is actually… we are in the process of writing some COI [conflict of interest] disclosure kind of policy stuff now (professional member association)

One association experienced a situation where an external organisation’s conflict-of-interest policy was more stringent than that of the organisation:

It did come up recently when we took abstracts to a more rigorous journal… and they said anything that was [industry] sponsored, they would not include as abstract, so we did not include abstracts from [UPF company] in the conference proceedings, so that was interesting. So that’s the standard they made, so we just accepted that, and published the rest of our abstracts with them, but not those ones. (professional member association)

Internal discussions about tightening rules on UPF sponsorships were raised by interviewees from multiple professional member associations:

We allow companies who are in the baby formula sector… so Nestle [sponsor]… so we’re going to the next step saying, should we have them there? Should we allow them that level of access? So we’ve got that next step now, looking at whether or not we will not accept, whether we will accept any sort of sponsorship.… So, is that a conflict of interest and undue influence or is that open, transparent engagement? I’ve got policies coming out of my backside. (professional member association)

It’s come up, and there is some appetite from our members to cease any connection with that organisation…. So it is a bit of a sensitive point. It needs to go, because it doesn’t meet our guidelines when you look at vulnerable population groups… And what they’re doing around promotion of formulas…. if you look carefully at our guidelines and then you look at what the Nestle products are doing, it’s sort of in breach of our guidelines and we shouldn’t be following that (professional member association)

Interviewees from charities also reflected on competing internal logics across different parts of their organisations. Many interviewees noted that their fundraising and investment portfolios often purported a logic of partnership and/or revenue raising foremost, while advocacy and policy departments had embedded a logic of reputational risk or FCTC approach to alcohol, UPF and related industries:

It makes it really tricky when the people that you work with don’t understand the tactics of an industry wanting to make commercial profit and they’ll do whatever they can within the rules, and sometimes their own rules, they break their own rules… (charity)

One of the pressures for a charity is income. And corporates are certainly identified as potential donors… there’s significant funding available through like corporate social giving. And some corporates really need to be giving money because they need to improve their profile, and that’s usually why they do it…. So for us, that’s a problem. It’s a problem when there’s a tension between needing to get money in and choosing the right corporate. So often our marketing and business development people will say, “Well, isn’t it better to be in the tent talking to these people to try and influence their behaviour or their policies or their approach?” …. I don’t see that’s actually a legitimate or realistic reason for us to partner with them, because that’s never yet happened…. And I think we need to be able to advocate without fear of censorship or whatever. (charity)

The investment portfolios are particularly problematic because most people ignore them. In terms of, they hand it over to an investment broker and they say, go and invest that and make us 7% a year, whatever. And don’t realise that the very things they’re saying they don’t do, they’re now doing. So it’s the left hand and the right hand again, and there’s no joined up thinking around it, because it costs money to exclude things from your portfolio of course. (charity)

We were asked by a new company manufacturing babies milk powder to partner with them. And we thought, that’s good because you know, a lot of patients have had mastectomies and they have to feed their babies, we thought that was absolutely fine. We then got the lobbyists [breastfeeding advocates] having a go at us about that, because they said you are being anti-breast feeding…. we got absolutely berated for that as well. So there’s a big tension (charity)

Policies and tools to manage interactions

Most interviewees reflected that their organisation did not have formal policies on interactions with alcohol and UPF industries in place. Reasons for not having a documented policy included wanting flexibility within the organisation and wanting to prevent industry pressure on the organisation to engage:

We don’t have a written rule in the space of policy and advocacy and engaging with those, with the commercially vested interests of those industries. So, it’s probably an unwritten rule that we don’t engage. (NGO)

The black and white rules are kind of easy to write and hard to live by. And if you make them public, which you don’t need to do, because at the end of the day… so I had, a journalist he had a go and he said, how woke is that, they won’t partner with [UPF company] and had a real go. (charity)

A black letter procedural policy, whichever way you write it, you would always find an instance that wouldn’t be covered by that black letter (professional member association)

So by having a black and white policy, what will happen is, if I say to the X people, ‘you know what, what you do is really great, but it’s not for us.’ They go ‘your policy doesn’t say that. we fit with your policy’…. So there’s a very soft edged area of this, which is probably frankly the majority of it. (charity)

For one interviewee, guidance on interactions was contained in different policies and documents in the organisation:

So it’s salt and peppered across a range of different policies. So the way we deal with investment is in our investment policy… there’s no one policy on industry engagement. And then most of it is at the discretion of the CEO (NGO)

In contrast, one interviewee reflected that having a clear policy on interactions with commercial actors enabled staff within the organisation to defend their position:

I’ve already had X company approach saying they wanted to meet me and see where we can collaborate, and thankfully I found [my organisation] is very clear that we don’t have any relationships [with industry X], so that made it really easy for me to say no, I don’t think we’ll have that conversation. (NGO)

While formal policies appeared to be uncommon, a more common tool was the use of internal risk assessments guiding organisational decision-making on interactions. These tools were used by about a third of organisations - particularly disease-focused charities and statutory organisations in guiding their decisions on potential partnerships, sponsorships, or contracts for services.

When it comes to those industries and engaging with them for the purpose of fundraising, there is written rules there whereby we essentially categorise industries, as no go zones, because they’re either in direct conflict with our mission and our purpose, or the reputational risk is deemed to be too high. So we do have a fundraising and corporate partnerships decision matrix. So from policy and advocacy it’s an unwritten rule, we just don’t. When it comes to the other parts of the business doing business with those types of industries, yes we do have a framework or a matrix that helps guide that decision making, and policy. (charity)

The risk assessment’s fairly comprehensive. So we’re looking at what that support from an unhealthy partner is. So what’s the financial investment, what are the marketing strategies that are used, that are going to be used, promotional, that kind of thing? And we particularly look at it from the lens of it’s how exposed young people are going to be to that. We also look more broadly at the brand itself and the products that they promote, the overall sort of risk to health of those products. So we have an actual scoring matrix that we use to do that assessment. And essentially, I mean we’ve sort of, it comes out with a high, moderate, low risk… and then we might propose, if it is, say, a moderate risk, there may be some mitigation strategies that we can propose that would reduce that down to a lower risk for us and make it more acceptable… And then in some instances, rarely, we’re not able to reach, if it is high risk and there’s, you know, obviously significant financial gain from the other supporters, we have had to sort of pull out as well. (statutory organisation)

It was evident that organisations differed in how they conceptualised risks regarding alcohol and UPF engagement activities. Alcohol appeared to be more clearly seen as a no go risk by all types of organisations:

The matrix gets into a level of detail that calls out the range of potential industries…. we’ll determine if a particular grocery store sells alcohol and tobacco, then they’re an amber or a red, versus if they don’t sell tobacco or alcohol, chances are we can probably partner with them. (charity)

Our investment policy would say that we don’t want to invest in alcohol manufacturing. So, it’s excluded from our policy. But we haven’t necessarily said that we won’t invest in the meat industry, although I don’t think we would. But specifically, tobacco and alcohol are called out because they’re the major causative factors…I think we do see food a little differently in that space. So it’s not a clear exclusion (charity)

We have policies that say we can’t invest in alcohol production, we can’t invest in gambling providers, right. But we invest in the banks and the banks fund all those activities. So effectively, we launder our investments into gambling and alcohol by holding shares in banks, right. But the challenge we face from a practical point of view is if they were to exclude all financial entities from the investment portfolio, they probably couldn’t commercially operate and attract money to be invested with them. So there’s a kind of, you know, so the practical imperative of their need to operate overrides an absolutely pure position. And therefore what we do is we use their holdings in the banks to engage and lobby with the banks around reform. (charity)

There appeared to be more variation towards UPF depending on the type of interaction and risk mitigation:

So we do have a policy that is very much about anyone that we fund… they have to put in a declaration of any harmful industry associations that they have…they have to do a harmful industries declaration. If they do have a tobacco relationship it’s an immediate no from us. If they declare an unhealthy food one, it’s not necessarily an immediate no, it’s a conversation about if we can manage our reputational risk (statutory organisation)

We ask [a potential partner or PR agency] to declare [UPF contracts], we consider it in our decision making, and if they take on a client who works in ultra-processed foods whilst we have a contract in place, we have a clause that says we’re allowed to terminate because you take that client on…. we tend to do all of ours through contracts rather than through our policy. (charity)

It is noteworthy that many interviewees in organisations without formal policies or risk assessments spoke of the need to internally develop them to better manage engagement with commercial actors:

Ours is not explicit. It will be, I will be making it more explicit, partly because it makes my life easier if we have a simple triage process where at least I can run through a flow chart of is it on this blacklist? Then no, we’re not going there. Is it a murky space? No, we’re not going there. If I can go through a process it will be a lot easier than having a gut instinct saying no, or just saying no to everything, because that’s not actually plausible (NGO)

It is very much a case-by-case conversation. We don’t currently have a risk matrix, which we probably should have, but the group that created the policy has kept around, and so they intuitively know the risk matrix that was intended. But I don’t think we have it written down as yet. (NGO)

Discussion

This study of NGO institutional logics provides a novel contribution to literatures in global health, including the commercial determinants of health (CDoH), conflicts of interest, partnerships and the role of civil society.

First, the findings point to the importance of a nuanced understanding of what constitutes engagement practices between NGOs and corporate actors. Interviews revealed a lack of clarity and agreement amongst NGOs about what interactions constitute engagement (whether direct or indirect), and what types of engagement are considered appropriate, and with whom. We note that while many interviewees reported that their organisation did not interact directly with alcohol or UPF companies through their advocacy work, background reading of annual reports and websites identified that some organisations did have some forms of limited UPF sponsorships. These discrepancies were explored in the interviews, revealing a clash of logics within many organisations. Indeed, many interviewees viewed organisational UPF sponsorships as inconsistent with internal sponsorship policy criteria (or internal norms if no formal policy was in place). This indicates issues of policy implementation for those that did have formal policies. Several interviewees noted their organisations were amid tightening policies on sponsorship criteria in response to internal or public criticism of organisational UPF engagement. We found greater homogeneity towards alcohol, where there was no indication of partnerships, funding or sponsorships in our cohort, and greater variation towards UPF. This parallels findings of public health and nutrition researchers views of acceptability of industry funding, where there is more consensus against receiving funds from tobacco and alcohol industries and more contention and variation towards UPF [54, 55].

It is noteworthy that many interviewees did not view their sitting on government taskforces or committees alongside industry as a form of direct interaction. It is likely that the interviews did not capture the full suite of interactions between these organisations and industries linked to alcohol and UPF simply because many advocacy staff reported that they often did not know what other parts of the organisation were doing - especially fundraising and investment portfolios. As shown in the interviews, some interviewees reflected that they did not know what investments the charity had made, and whether they were investing in companies with ties to alcohol and UPF companies (or other harmful industries like fossil fuels and gambling).

Building on recent calls to examine corporate actors beyond transnational corporations [56], this study highlights the challenges for NGOs in delineating the interests of supermarket and retailers, PR firms and advertising agencies, banks and others vis-a-vis alcohol and UPF industries. We found that alcohol-focused NGOs were more likely to view commercial actors who worked with alcohol companies as ‘like’ the alcohol industry. In contrast, disease-focused organisations had greater variation in their view of acceptability for working with these industries. This may have implications for advocacy coalitions. It is well known that civil society advocacy is an important counter to corporate power and influence [23]. These coalitions may be less effective or at risk of fracturing to the extent that different organisational approaches to UPF and related industries block coalition forming or agreement on common objectives.

The findings also contribute to literature on conflicts of interest and health governance [8, 17, 30, 54, 57–62]. Recent work has demonstrated that industry-funded ‘front’ groups / SAPROs behave differently to non-industry funded groups, often touting industry objectives [12, 16, 31–34, 36, 38]. The study contributes to this work by mapping the underlying institutional logics driving the norms and approaches of NGOs who do not receive core funding from alcohol or UPF industries. It reveals how many NGOs, notably charities and member associations, are grappling with logics of the market (i.e. revenue raising and partnership) on the one hand, with logics of reputational risk and public health interests on the other.

These challenges parallel difficulties at the global level for developing and implementing guidance on managing and preventing undue influence on health policy. Dominant partnership logics at the global level through the UN SDGS and other fora have embedded norms promoting shared value for industry actors, despite their competing interest with public health policy [63–65]. Global tools have been developed to offer guidance for member states [21, 22], and toolkits for food researchers [58] - but as Daube [25] notes, such tools have not yet been adapted for use across NGOs and civil society.

Our findings on the development and use of internal risk assessments and matrices by about a third of interviewees indicate hybridisation efforts by policy and advocacy staff to navigate these competing logics. This finding contributes to not-for-profit literature regarding tensions within not-for-profit organisations between government servicing, revenue generation, and political advocacy [48, 66, 67]. We use hybridity here to refer to efforts by organisation staff to craft policies and practices in response to multiple and often conflicting logics [68, 69]. In our study, many interviewees noted that their internal risk assessments flagged certain industries as ‘no go zones’, namely tobacco and alcohol, with significant variation towards the degree of risk attributed to engaging with UPF industries, and industries related to alcohol and UPF based on the types of interaction. Here, staff within several NGOs have appeared to be able to drive conversations about conflicts of interest and reputational risk to at least sit alongside debates about revenue and partnership. This indicates that the logic of reputational risk may have greater acceptability amongst NGO boards and senior management and serve to enable greater flexibility for staff to prevent undue corporate influence, with implications for health. There remains a question as to whether a logic of reputational risk is sufficient for protecting public health and equity, yet the interviews indicate that such an approach is enabling staff to bring questions of interests and influence (via risks for the brand) on par with revenue and partnership. This finding parallels studies of social enterprise organisations which reveal how organisations have selectively coupled market and community logics to navigate tensions [67].

We found that only a small number of interviewees noted that their organisations had formal policies in place regarding navigating interactions with corporate actors. Problems of implementation were noted by interviewees from three member associations who all had some form of limited sponsorship with the UPF giant Nestle – which were viewed by interviewees as likely inconsistent with internal sponsorship criteria.

Overall, many interviewees noted their organisation had no formal policies or risk assessments but rather unwritten norms or decision making made at the discretion of CEOs or boards. Many interviewees wanted more guidance and tools for assessing the risks and benefits of commercial actor engagement, particularly for commercial actors beyond obvious alcohol and transnational UPF companies. This is only likely to increase as the changing landscape of funding puts pressure on NGOs to partner with commercial actors. These findings have real world applicability for the boards and management staff of NGOs – those charities, professional member associations, coalitions and other independent organisations active in health advocacy.

Greater understanding of these different organisational logics, including the extent to which they conflict and have implications for wider coalition building, is needed.

These qualitative findings were presented to a roundtable of NGO practitioners held at the ANU in Canberra Australia in December 2023 by the author project team. While not a part of the methods of this study, the roundtable was used to reflect on the merging findings from the study and implications for NGOs in health advocacy in Australia. What emerged from this roundtable were recommendations for greater transparency of NGO norms, policies and approaches, and the establishment of a community of practice focused on preventing undue corporate influence and the sharing of innovative tools and good practice.

Conclusion

A central question in contemporary health governance is how to prevent undue corporate influence. Tobacco, alcohol, and UPF industries use similar tactics to advance their interests, often at the expense of population health. Government and civil society organisations engage with a diversity of commercial actors in various ways, including but not limited to; consulting, participating alongside in government committees, forming partnerships, receiving funding and sponsorships. This qualitative study is the first to our knowledge to map underlying institutional logics shaping variation in the interactions of NGOs who are not reliant on industry core funding with alcohol, UPF and related industries. Drawing on thirty interviews with senior policy and advocacy staff from nongovernment organisations active in alcohol and/or UPF advocacy in Australia, we find shared opposition to partnerships with the alcohol industry and significant variation towards the UPF industry. We identify four institutional logics for disengagement with alcohol and UPF industries: a logic of reputational risk, a logic of delegitimation, an FCTC logic, and a logic of competing interests. In contrast, we find two logics for limited engagement with alcohol and UPF industries: a logic of intelligence gathering, and a logic of ‘acting reasonable’.

All interviewees spoke of challenges in delineating the interests of other industries that may work with alcohol and UPF, such as supermarkets and retailers, advertising agencies and public relation firms, and banks and investment entities. We identify two logics for engagement with a wider range of industries: a logic of partnership and a logic of revenue raising.

Only a small number of organisations had formal policies on conflicts of interest. We found that many organisations have conflicting internal logics creating challenges for organisational coherence. The development of internal risk assessments by some organisations indicate efforts by staff to prevent undue corporate influence. We recommend greater transparency of policies on preventing corporate influence and conflicts of interest, and approaches to assessing interactions with a variety of corporate actors, and the establishment of a global community of practice for sharing innovative tools and approaches to preventing undue corporate influence.
