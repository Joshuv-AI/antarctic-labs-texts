Association of preoperative frailty and delayed neurocognitive recovery in older adults undergoing major elective surgery in Thailand: a single centre prospective study
Authors: Chanya Wuthikraikun, Titima Wongviriyawong, Patumporn Suraarunsumrit, Arunotai Siriussawakul, Monai Sauejui, Rinrada Preedachitkun, Varalak Srinonprasert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundDelayed neurocognitive recovery (dNCR) is common in older adults and associated with adverse outcomes, but its relationship with frailty remains unclear. We aimed to examine the relationship between preoperative frailty and dNCR, as well as other postoperative outcomes, in older adults undergoing major elective surgery.MethodsThis prospective cohort study was conducted at a university hospital and enrolled patients aged 60 years or older who underwent elective surgery under regional or general anesthesia. Frailty was assessed using the Thai version of the FRAIL scale (T-FRAIL). dNCR was defined as a decline of at least 2 points on the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) from postoperative day 5–9. The associations between perioperative variables and dNCR were analyzed employing multivariable logistic regression models.FindingsAmong the 282 enrolled patients, 204 completed follow-up; 13.5% were identified as frail. dNCR occurred in 26.0% of non-frail and 44.4% of frail patients. Preoperative frailty was associated with an increased risk of dNCR, with an adjusted OR of 2.69, 95% CI 1.08–6.72. Frail patients had longer hospital stays (median 9.5 [7, 16] days vs 8 [6, 12]; p = 0.026), higher in-hospital mortality (7.9% vs 1.2%; p = 0.024), greater total hospitalization costs (median USD 8475 [5504–11,727] vs USD 5950 [3883–8900]; p = 0.005), and higher rate of 3-month readmissions (25.7% vs 12.8%; p = 0.042). At 3 months after surgery, they also had lower functional scores (Barthel Index 81.5 ± 23.7 vs 94.8 ± 10.7; p < 0.001) and lower health utility scores (0.79 ± 2.25 vs 0.91 ± 0.13; p < 0.001).InterpretationPreoperative frailty was associated with an increased risk of dNCR and poorer postoperative outcomes. Endorsing routine frailty screening, targeted preoperative optimization and multidisciplinary team care perioperatively might be beneficial.FundingFaculty of Medicine, Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University, Bangkok, Thailand, supported this study (grant number [IO] R016431020).Keywords: Perioperative neurocognitive disorders, Delayed neurocognitive recovery, Postoperative cognitive dysfunction, Frailty, Postoperative outcomes

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Frailty has been consistently associated with adverse postoperative outcomes in older adults, including increased complications, prolonged hospitalization, functional decline, and mortality. Meta-analyses have demonstrated a strong association between preoperative frailty and postoperative delirium (POD); however, evidence linking frailty to postoperative neurocognitive disorders beyond delirium—particularly delayed neurocognitive recovery (dNCR) as defined by contemporary perioperative neurocognitive disorder (PND) nomenclature—remains limited and inconsistent. Many prior studies focused on single surgical populations, assessed cognitive outcomes at heterogeneous or later time points, or used legacy definitions of postoperative cognitive dysfunction. Furthermore, data from low- and middle-income settings and from Asian surgical populations are scarce. Evidence on the downstream clinical and economic consequences of dNCR, including healthcare utilization, functional recovery, and health-related quality of life, is also limited.

Added value of this study

In a prospective cohort of older adults undergoing major elective surgery at a tertiary hospital in Thailand, we systematically evaluated preoperative frailty using a validated, culturally adapted frailty instrument (T-FRAIL) and assessed dNCR at a standardized postoperative time point aligned with current PND nomenclature. We demonstrate that preoperative frailty is independently associated with dNCR at 1 week, even after accounting for clinically relevant perioperative factors. Frail patients also experienced worse short-term outcomes, including longer hospital stays, higher in-hospital mortality, and greater healthcare costs, as well as poorer functional status, lower health utility, and higher readmission rates at 3 months. In addition, we identified associations between intraoperative vasopressor use and increased dNCR risk, and between gabapentinoid exposure and lower dNCR risk, generating hypotheses regarding potentially modifiable perioperative factors. By linking frailty, early postoperative neurocognitive vulnerability, and patient-centered as well as system-level outcomes, this study provides new evidence from an underrepresented setting and population.

Implications of all the available evidence

Taken together with existing literature, our findings support frailty as an integrative marker of biological vulnerability that extends beyond traditional age or comorbidity indices in identifying older surgical patients at risk of early postoperative neurocognitive decline. dNCR may serve as a clinically meaningful early cognitive signal within a broader trajectory of frailty-related vulnerability, rather than an isolated short-term phenomenon. These results underscore the importance of routine preoperative frailty screening and targeted perioperative optimization, including multidisciplinary prehabilitation and careful intraoperative hemodynamic management. Future multicenter studies with longer follow-up are needed to clarify the role of preoperative frailty in longer-term PND trajectories, and to test frailty-targeted interventions aimed at improving cognitive, functional, and healthcare outcomes in older adults undergoing surgery.

Introduction

Perioperative neurocognitive disorders (PNDs) encompass a spectrum of cognitive impairments that may occur in the preoperative, intraoperative, or postoperative periods, with significant implications for both patient outcomes and healthcare costs.1,2 According to the 2018 Nomenclature Consensus, PNDs are classified into three categories: postoperative delirium (POD), occurring within 7 days after surgery; delayed neurocognitive recovery (dNCR), identified within 30 days postoperatively; and postoperative neurocognitive disorder (pNCD), detected between 30 days and 12 months after surgery.3 The previously used term postoperative cognitive dysfunction (POCD) refers to an objectively measured decline in cognitive performance—typically involving memory, attention, processing speed, or executive function—that occurs after anesthesia and surgery.3 It is now considered to encompass both dNCR and pNCD, depending on the timing of detection.3 The reported incidence of POCD varies substantially, ranging from 10% to 40%, and is influenced by patient characteristics, procedure type, and the timing and cognitive assessment.4,5 Contemporary evidence syntheses identify several potential risk factors for POCD, including advanced age, a high burden of comorbidities, baseline cognitive impairment, and intraoperative stress.6 However, these studies demonstrate significant heterogeneity and report only a limited number of associations with high credibility, especially when distinguishing early vs later cognitive decline—highlighting the necessity for further rigorous research focused on the dNCR time-window. Recent studies have also shown that dNCR is associated with increased mortality and greater healthcare utilization.7,8 Therefore, identifying modifiable perioperative risk factors associated with dNCR is essential for developing effective prevention strategies.

Within this context, frailty—a multidimensional syndrome of reduced physiological reserve and resilience—is a compelling candidate risk marker. Its association with POD is consistent across meta-analyses.9, 10, 11 In contrast, evidence regarding dNCR remains inconsistent: frailty was identified as an independent predictor of dNCR following total hip replacement in one study, but no significant association was observed in a prospective non-cardiac surgical cohort.12,13 Biologically, frailty is characterized by chronic low-grade inflammation with microglial priming, increased blood–brain barrier vulnerability allowing peripheral cytokine entry, impaired cerebrovascular autoregulation with reduced tolerance to intraoperative and early postoperative hypoperfusion and microembolization, and decreased neuronal metabolic reserve related to sarcopenia, malnutrition, and mitochondrial dysfunction—features that plausibly heighten susceptibility to perioperative brain injury and dNCR.14

Considering these factors, this study primarily investigates whether preoperative frailty is independently associated with dNCR following major elective surgery, with a hypothesis of increased risk among frail patients. Given the limited evidence from Asian surgical populations and the availability of a validated Thai FRAIL (T-FRAIL) instrument in the local context,15 this research provides region-specific data from Thailand. Furthermore, since frailty is potentially reversible, clarifying its connection to dNCR has direct implications for perioperative pathways.16 Secondary objectives were to examine associations between preoperative frailty and healthcare utilization (hospital length of stay and direct hospital costs), in-hospital mortality, 3-month mortality, 3-month functional decline, and 3-month health-related quality of life (HRQoL).

Methods

This prospective cohort study was conducted at a large university hospital and received ethical approval from the Siriraj Institutional Review Board, Faculty of Medicine, Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University, Bangkok, Thailand (Protocol No. Si 1001/2020). From December 2020 to August 2022, Thai-speaking patients aged 60 years or older who were admitted for major elective surgery requiring regional or general anesthesia were enrolled in the study. The age threshold of 60 years was selected in accordance with national and regional definitions of older adults in Thailand and Southeast Asia and has been used in prior Thai geriatric and perioperative frailty studies.15,17 Major elective surgery was defined as a planned operative procedure and performed under general or regional anesthesia, including major abdominal, orthopedic, vascular, thoracic, urologic, gynecologic and cardiac surgeries.18 Regional anesthesia was defined as neuraxial (spinal or epidural) or peripheral nerve block techniques, with or without sedation, administered as the primary anesthetic modality.19 Patients were excluded if they had preoperative delirium, surgery cancellation, or were unable to complete cognitive assessments because of limited Thai language proficiency, significant visual or hearing impairment, psychiatric disorders, or bedridden status. Preoperative delirium was excluded to ensure that postoperative cognitive changes reflected new-onset rather than preexisting neurocognitive impairment. The remaining exclusion criteria were applied to ensure the validity and reliability of cognitive assessments and to allow meaningful interpretation of postoperative cognitive and functional outcomes without substantial floor effects. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Preoperative assessments included baseline cognitive evaluations using the Thai version of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA-Thai)20 and the Modified Informant Questionnaire on Cognitive Decline in the Elderly (IQCODE).21 Functional status was assessed using the Barthel Index for activities of daily living22 and the Lawton–Brody Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL) Scale.23 Depressive symptoms were measured with the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9),24 and HRQoL was evaluated using the EQ-5D-5L instrument, and the responses were converted into a health utility score.25

Baseline demographic and clinical information, including comorbidities, body mass index (BMI), and the number of medications, was collected through patient and caregiver interviews and a review of medical records. Details regarding comorbid conditions and surgical characteristics (e.g., type and site of surgery) were obtained from anesthetic records. Intraoperative and postoperative data, including surgical type, anesthesia technique, operative duration, and complications, were also recorded.

The Thai version of the FRAIL scale includes five components: fatigue (reduced energy compared with the past), resistance (difficulty climbing stairs), ambulation (inability to walk one block), illnesses (presence of more than five comorbid conditions), and weight loss (greater than 5% within one year).15 Each item is scored as one point, with total scores of 3–5 indicating frailty.

Cognitive function was assessed using the MoCA-Thai by a trained psychologist. Baseline assessment was performed 1–2 days before surgery. Postoperative cognitive assessment using MoCA-Thai was conducted between postoperative days 5 and 9, and a decline of ≥2 points from the preoperative MoCA score at the postoperative assessment was classified as dNCR.8 This threshold was based on the previous study8 and aligned with the 2018 International Nomenclature Consensus for PNDs.3

POD was assessed separately on daily basis for 5 days after cardiac surgery and for 3 days after non-cardiac surgery. This approach was designed considering the typical time course of delirium where the incidence after cardiac surgery could be delayed compared to other surgery.8 Assessments were performed only when patients were medically stable. The Confusion Assessment Method26 was used to screen for POD in non-ICU patients, while the CAM-ICU was employed for intubated or ICU patients.27 All delirium diagnoses were subsequently confirmed within 48 h by experienced geriatricians with more than three years of clinical practice in geriatric medicine, using DSM-5 criteria.28

We evaluated a comprehensive set of preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative variables that may contribute to the development of dNCR, based on previous studies.6,29,30 Preoperative variables included age, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification, ischemic heart disease (IHD), and cognitive status assessed by the modified IQCODE, with scores ≥3.42 indicating preexisting impairment.21 Frailty is a well-established predictor of POD; however, its association with dNCR is inconsistent across studies and remains controversial.9,12,13 We therefore prespecified frailty to examine its independent relationship with dNCR in our cohort.

Intraoperative variables included surgical type (cardiovascular and thoracic (CVT) vs non-CVT), vasopressor administration (indicator of hemodynamic instability), benzodiazepine exposure, and perioperative use of gabapentin or pregabalin. Postoperative variables included pain control and medical complications. Pain intensity was collected prospectively using a numerical rating scale (NRS) during routine postoperative assessments; where a NRS score of ≥4 was considered clinically significant. Medical complications were identified after discharge through structured review of the electronic health record, as the research team was not the primary clinical team managing postoperative care. Complications were captured using standardized diagnostic criteria and International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes, with specific focus on aspiration pneumonia, acute kidney injury (AKI), and arrhythmias.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was calculated to detect a difference in the incidence of dNCR between frail and non-frail patients. Based on previous data, the incidence of dNCR was estimated at 55% in frail patients and 30% in non-frail patients, with an expected frailty prevalence of 29% in the study population.31 Using a two-sided Z-test with a 5% significance level and 80% power, and accounting for an anticipated 10% dropout rate, the required total sample size was determined to be 172 patients.

Descriptive statistics were used to examine clinical characteristics, perioperative variables, and postoperative outcomes. Categorical variables (sex, education, ASA classification, comorbidities, Lawton–Brody IADL Scale, readmissions, and mortality) were summarized as counts and percentages. Continuous variables (age, CCI, modified IQCODE, MoCA, Barthel Index, hospital length of stay, and HRQoL) were described as mean ± SD for normally distributed data or median (interquartile ranges, IQR) for non-normally distributed data.

Postoperative outcomes were compared between the frail and non-frail groups. Binary outcomes (dNCR, POD, readmission, and mortality) were analyzed using the Chi-square or Fisher's exact test as appropriate. Continuous outcomes that approximated a normal distribution (Barthel Index and health-utility scores at 3 months) were analyzed using independent t-tests. For skewed outcomes (hospital length of stay and hospitalization cost), the Mann–Whitney U test was employed.

To evaluate factors associated with dNCR at 1 week, univariate logistic regression was initially performed for each candidate predictor. Variables with p < 0.10 in univariate analyses or prespecified clinical factors were considered for multivariable analysis. The final multivariable logistic regression model was constructed using a prespecified parsimonious approach, with the total number of variables restricted according to events-per-variable considerations to reduce overfitting.32,33 Given the limited number of participants, clinically relevant covariates were prioritized for inclusion. Among the variables deemed potentially relevant, only one representative variable with greater clinical relevance, stronger biological plausibility, and measurement robustness was retained to minimize multicollinearity. Variable selection was discussed among co-authors to reach a consensus. Accordingly, variables retained in the final multivariable model were frailty status, intraoperative vasopressor use, intraoperative benzodiazepine use, postoperative gabapentin/pregabalin use, and aspiration pneumonia. Model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and model discrimination was evaluated using pseudo-R2 statistics (Cox & Snell and Nagelkerke). Results are reported as crude and adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All analyses were two-sided, and a p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using PASW Statistics for Windows, version 18.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

A total of 305 patients were screened for eligibility (Fig. 1). Of these, 23 patients were excluded due to surgery cancellation (n = 10), incomplete frail scale data (n = 7), preoperative delirium (n = 5) or participant withdrawal (n = 1). The remaining 282 patients were enrolled and categorized based on frailty status into non-frail (n = 244) and frail (n = 38) groups. Follow-up cognitive assessments for dNCR were completed in 204 patients, including 177 non-frail and 27 frail individuals.

Table 4 shows the results of the univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses examining factors associated with dNCR at 1 week. In univariate analysis, frailty status, higher ASA stage, ischemic heart disease, CVT surgery, intraoperative vasopressor or benzodiazepine exposure, and aspiration pneumonia were significantly associated with increased risk of dNCR. In contrast, gabapentinoid use was associated with reduced risk of dNCR. In multivariable analysis, frailty (adjusted OR (aOR) 2.69, 95% CI 1.08–6.72, p = 0.034), intraoperative vasopressor use (aOR 3.75, 95% CI 1.06–13.29, p = 0.040), and gabapentinoid use (aOR 0.25, 95% CI 0.07–0.92, p = 0.036) remained independently associated with dNCR. The associations of other variables, including age, comorbidity index, preexisting cognitive impairment, and postoperative complications, were attenuated. The final model demonstrated acceptable calibration, with non-significant Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit tests across model specifications (p = 0.37–0.57), and modest discrimination, as indicated by pseudo-R2 values (Nagelkerke R2 = 0.19–0.21).

Discussion

Among older adults undergoing major elective surgery, preoperative frailty was independently associated with dNCR at 1 week. Frail patients also had poorer short-term outcomes, including longer hospital stays, higher in-hospital mortality, and higher hospital costs. Beyond the early postoperative period, frailty remained associated with medium-term outcomes. At 3 months, frail patients had poorer functional scores, lower health utility, and higher rates of readmission. These findings indicate that the occurrence of dNCR may be part of a broader trajectory of impaired recovery among frail patients. Intraoperatively, vasopressor use was linked to higher dNCR risk, whereas gabapentin or pregabalin use was associated with a lower risk. Together, these findings provide a framework for interpreting the observed prevalence patterns, mechanistic associations, and clinical implications discussed below.

The prevalence of frailty in this cohort (13.5%) falls within the lower range reported in studies of older adults undergoing surgery, where frailty prevalence has been estimated at approximately 10–30%. This may partly reflect the frailty instrument used, as studies using the FRAIL scale usually reported a lower prevalence of frailty than those using the frailty index. Furthermore, the elective surgical setting, in which preoperative selection may exclude patients with severe physiologic compromise, likely contributed to this lower prevalence.34,35 Nevertheless, the occurrence of dNCR in this study is consistent with recent studies, which estimated that dNCR affects approximately 10–40% of older adults following major surgery.3,4 The marked absolute difference in dNCR prevalence between frail and non-frail patients in our cohort underscores the clinical relevance of frailty even in an ostensibly lower-risk elective population.

Placing these findings within the broader perioperative neurocognitive literature, meta-analytic evidence consistently demonstrates that frailty is a strong predictor of POD.9 In contrast, the association between frailty and dNCR remains inconsistent across studies: a hip arthroplasty cohort reported higher dNCR rates among frail patients,13 whereas a prospective noncardiac cohort found that frailty predicted POD but not dNCR.12 These discrepancies likely reflect differences in surgical populations, anesthetic exposure, cognitive assessment timing, and definitions of early postoperative neurocognitive outcomes. Importantly, many prior studies either focused on a single surgical subtype or assessed cognitive outcomes at variable or later time points, which may obscure early postoperative cognitive trajectories.35,36 By evaluating a broad population of older adults undergoing major elective surgery and applying a standardized perioperative cognitive assessment window aligned with current PND nomenclature, our findings add clarity to the existing literature and provide evidence that frailty is independently associated with early postoperative dNCR, beyond its established association with POD.

In this context, it is notable that preoperative frailty was associated with dNCR but not POD in this cohort. This finding should be interpreted alongside emerging evidence, suggesting that POD and dNCR represent overlapping manifestations along a continuum of perioperative neurocognitive disorders rather than biologically distinct entities.7 The absence of a statistically significant association between frailty and POD in our study is therefore unlikely to reflect a true biological dissociation. Rather, it is more plausibly explained by limited statistical power, as POD was not a primary outcome and the study was not specifically designed to detect frailty–POD associations. In addition, the elective surgical setting and proactive delirium prevention practices may have reduced POD incidence, further attenuating detectable associations. In contrast, dNCR—assessed systematically at a predefined postoperative time point—may represent a more sensitive indicator of perioperative cognitive vulnerability in this context, thereby revealing the association with frailty observed in our analyses.

From a biological perspective, frailty is characterized by chronic low-grade systemic inflammation, impaired cerebrovascular reactivity, neurovascular uncoupling, and diminished metabolic and cognitive reserve.14 These vulnerabilities plausibly heighten susceptibility to perioperative physiologic stressors—including hypotension, inflammatory surges, hypoxia, and sedative exposure—thereby increasing the risk of postoperative neurocognitive decline. In this context, the observed association between dNCR and intraoperative vasopressor use in our study likely reflects underlying hemodynamic instability rather than a direct pharmacologic effect. Recent meta-analyses exploring the effect of cerebral hypoperfusion on dNCR, using intraoperative hypotension as a mediator, did not demonstrate a significant association,37,38 possibly because hypotension is typically corrected promptly by anesthesiologists, making sustained or clinically meaningful episodes difficult to ascertain. Accordingly, vasopressor use may serve as a pragmatic surrogate marker of perioperative hemodynamic instability and potential cerebral hypoperfusion, and its association with dNCR in our study suggests that dynamic blood pressure perturbations, rather than isolated hypotensive thresholds, may be more relevant to early postoperative neurocognitive outcomes. Future studies using comparable hemodynamic markers and standardized ascertainment methods are warranted to validate and extend these findings.

Interestingly, the present study demonstrated an association between gabapentinoid use and a reduced risk of dNCR. Although it has been suggested that effective analgesia may reduce the risk of PNDs, the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Gabapentinoids differ mechanistically from many other analgesics by modulating central neuronal excitability through binding to the α2δ subunit of voltage-gated calcium channels, thereby reducing presynaptic release of excitatory neurotransmitters and central sensitization.39,40 In the perioperative context, this central neuromodulatory effect, together with an opioid-sparing property, may plausibly mitigate early postoperative cognitive vulnerability,41 which may partly explain the observed association between gabapentinoid use and lower dNCR risk in our study. Nevertheless, this finding should be interpreted with caution and should not be construed as evidence of neurocognitive protection or a causal effect. Prior studies have shown that perioperative gabapentin reduces opioid requirements without reducing the incidence of POD,42 and a large cohort of older adults reported increased risks of delirium and pneumonia associated with perioperative gabapentin use, highlighting important safety concerns.43 To date, no studies have specifically evaluated the association between gabapentinoid use and dNCR, and therefore, our findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing. Gabapentinoids should continue to be used cautiously, particularly in frail older adults, with renal dose adjustment and close monitoring,44 and further prospective studies examining the relationship between gabapentinoid exposure and dNCR are warranted.

Although age and comorbidity burden are frequently reported as risk factors for POCD, neither chronological age nor CCI was associated with dNCR in our univariate analyses. This finding is not necessarily discordant with prior literature, as much of the evidence linking age and comorbidity to postoperative cognitive outcomes derives from studies evaluating POD or longer-term pNCD at later and heterogeneous time points, rather than dNCR within 30 days as defined by contemporary PND nomenclature.3,6 Given the context of our cohort—patients undergoing major elective surgery—preoperative selection and optimization likely limited the breadth of age and comorbidity severity, attenuating crude associations with early dNCR. In contrast, our outcome focused on early recovery, a period during which cognitive change may be more sensitive to acute perioperative perturbations and baseline brain reserve than to cumulative disease counts; accordingly, frailty may provide a more integrative representation of biological vulnerability that can supersede age and comorbidity indices in identifying patients at risk of dNCR.45 Consistent with this interpretation, studies that incorporate frailty measures have reported stronger and more consistent associations with perioperative neurocognitive outcomes than models relying primarily on chronological age or comorbidity burden.45

These frailty-related impairments in physiologic resilience align with broader evidence linking frailty and PNDs to prolonged hospitalization, increased healthcare utilization, and downstream functional decline. Prior studies have shown that frailty is associated with persistent postoperative functional impairment and reduced quality of life, even when short-term mortality differences are attenuated.2,34,46 In our cohort, although 3-month mortality did not differ between groups, frailty was associated with sustained functional impairment, lower health utility, and higher readmission rates, and was also associated with a higher incidence of dNCR, supporting the interpretation that frailty identifies a state of heightened vulnerability in which dNCR may emerge as a clinically meaningful cognitive signal rather than an isolated short-term cognitive phenomenon. Whether dNCR mediates these medium-term functional outcomes or represents a parallel manifestation of frailty-related vulnerability warrants further longitudinal investigation with repeated cognitive and functional assessments, particularly in relation to potentially modifiable perioperative factors.47 While frailty is consistently linked to adverse long-term postoperative outcomes, evidence defining its association with longer-term PNDs remains limited.2 Ongoing follow-up of this cohort will provide insight into outcomes at 1 year; however, interpretation of longer-term associations beyond this period may be increasingly confounded by the accrual of aging-related comorbidities and intercurrent health events.

Taken together, these findings highlight the need for comprehensive perioperative assessment and risk-reduction strategies that extend beyond hemodynamic and analgesic considerations alone. They reinforce the importance of routine preoperative frailty screening, early identification of high-risk individuals, and personalized perioperative optimization. Practically, this includes integrating structured prehabilitation—encompassing multidisciplinary medical optimization, cognitive and psychological engagement, nutritional supplementation and exercise training —into perioperative pathways, as these interventions have demonstrated benefits in functional capacity, complication reduction, and postoperative recovery among frail, high-risk surgical candidates.48

The study has several strengths. First, this prospective cohort employed prespecified assessments and a clearly defined dNCR endpoint at 1 week, consistent with the 2018 Consensus Working Group recommendations.3 Second, frailty was measured with a validated T-FRAIL instrument, providing culturally appropriate risk stratification and context-specific evidence from an Asian surgical population. Third, we captured comprehensive perioperative covariates and performed multivariable adjustments across domains, producing internally consistent signals from univariable through adjusted models. Fourth, we linked dNCR to patient-centered and system-level outcomes—function, HRQoL, length of stay, readmission, mortality, and direct hospital costs—enhancing clinical relevance. Lastly, our pragmatic cognitive assessment strategy for identifying dNCR using the MoCA supports feasibility and real-world implementation, especially where lengthy neuropsychological batteries and specialists may be limited.

However, there are several limitations. Postoperative cognitive follow-up was incomplete, with 28% of participants missing MoCA assessments due to death, medical instability, or refusal. Although this introduces potential attrition bias, it was mitigated by prescheduling assessments, recording reasons for missingness, and reporting completion rates by frailty status. The study population included a heterogeneous mix of surgical procedures and anesthetic techniques. While this heterogeneity enhances generalizability to real-world elective surgical practice, it limits the interpretability of procedure- or anesthesia-specific effects on dNCR. Differences in surgical stress, operative duration, anesthetic exposure, and perioperative management across procedures may have introduced residual confounding that was not fully captured in our analyses; therefore, our findings should be interpreted as reflecting overall associations across major elective surgery rather than effects attributable to specific surgical or anesthetic subgroups. The study was conducted at a single tertiary center, which may limit generalizability. However, consecutive enrollment and the use of a validated T-FRAIL instrument to identify a clearly defined frail population help support the applicability of our findings to similar clinical contexts, where patient profiles and frailty burdens are comparable. The primary endpoint captured the association between preoperative frailty and dNCR, though longer-term trajectories may differ; these will be assessed in planned follow-up. dNCR was prioritized because a recent meta-analysis links it to worse outcomes (e.g., longer hospital stays, higher mortality),7 and demonstrating a near-term signal supports frailty screening and targeted preoperative optimization as practical prevention strategies. The MoCA, chosen for feasibility and multi-domain coverage, offers less granularity than comprehensive neuropsychological batteries but enhances real-world implementability. In addition, penalized regression approaches such as LASSO or elastic net were not applied. The analysis was designed to examine etiological associations using clinically prespecified covariates, with the number of variables constrained by events-per-variable considerations. While penalized methods may be useful for data-driven variable selection in larger datasets or for prediction-focused models, the chosen approach was considered appropriate for the study objectives and available sample size. Residual confounding—such as factors related to anesthesia depth, detailed hemodynamics, and analgesic pathways—remains a possibility despite multivariable adjustment. Cost estimates were limited to direct in-hospital expenditures. Together, these limitations highlight the need for multicenter, procedure-specific cohorts with more granular physiologic and anesthetic monitoring to affirm the results, as well as prospective interventional trials testing frailty-targeted prehabilitation and intraoperative hemodynamic strategies using dNCR-specific endpoints. Such studies are needed to clarify mechanistic pathways and inform evidence-based perioperative care pathways for older adults undergoing major elective surgery.

In conclusion, older adults undergoing major elective surgery, preoperative frailty was independently linked to dNCR at 1 week and associated with worse short-term outcomes—such as longer hospital stays, higher in-hospital mortality and healthcare costs, and a higher rate of 3-month readmissions—as well as poorer 3-month functional status and HRQoL. These findings support the integration of routine frailty screening and targeted preoperative optimization into perioperative care pathways.
