Clinical efficacy and sagittal alignment restoration of unilateral biportal endoscopic-assisted anterior cervical discectomy and fusion for cervical spondylosis: a retrospective comparative study
Authors: Junyi He, Haiguang Long, Gejin Wei, Wanan Qin, Bei Liu, Yanping Zeng, Changzhi Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) remains the established operative approach for managing cervical spondylosis (CS). However, traditional open ACDF involves considerable damage to the surrounding soft tissues and relatively slow postoperative recovery. The emergence of Unilateral Biportal Endoscopy (UBE)-assisted ACDF (UBE-ACDF) offers a novel approach that combines effective decompression with the advantages of a minimally invasive procedure. Nevertheless, clinical evidence comparing UBE- ACDF with traditional ACDF remains scarce, and its clinical efficacy requires further evaluation.ObjectiveTo compare and evaluate the perioperative outcomes, clinical functional recovery, and imaging differences between UBE-ACDF and traditional ACDF in the treatment of CS.Methods84 cases diagnosed with CS were divided into the UBE-ACDF group (n = 32) and the ACDF group (n = 52). The perioperative efficacy (operation time, incision length, visible/hidden blood loss, hospitalization cost), clinical outcomes at multiple follow-up time points (VAS, NDI, and JOA), imaging outcome (sagittal balance index, segmental Cobb Angle and disc height) and complications were systematically compared between the two groups.ResultsThe two groups were well-matched in terms of baseline demographic and clinical characteristics. Compared with the ACDF group, the UBE-ACDF group showed significant minimally invasive advantages in the perioperative period such as shorter hospital stay (P = 0.039), and lower intraoperative visible blood loss (143.97 vs. 375.81 mL, P < 0.001). The clinical function was significantly improved in both groups. The final follow-up VAS-arm score was significantly higher in the UBE-ACDF group than in the ACDF group, indicating greater long-term arm pain relief in the conventional ACDF group (P = 0.005). Intervertebral height restoration was greater in the UBE-ACDF group. However, the interpretation of T1 slope changes was limited by significant baseline differences between groups.ConclusionUBE-ACDF may represent a feasible minimally invasive alternative to conventional ACDF for selected patients with CS. It provides comparable overall neurological and functional recovery while reducing perioperative surgical trauma. However, conventional ACDF showed better long-term arm pain relief at the final follow-up, and T1 slope findings should be interpreted cautiously because of baseline sagittal alignment imbalance. Larger prospective studies with longer follow-up are required to confirm fusion-related outcomes and long-term radiographic durability.Keywords: Cervical spondylosis, Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion, Unilateral biportal endoscopy, Sagittal alignment

Introduction

Cervical spondylosis (CS) is a degenerative change involving the cervical intervertebral discs, vertebral bodies, joints, and ligaments, which can lead to stenosis of the spinal canal and intervertebral foramen, thereby compressing the spinal cord, nerve roots, and blood vessels [1, 2]. The symptoms are complex and changeable with different compression sites, and are characterized by a long course of disease and a high risk of recurrence [3]. With the increasingly aging of the global population and the popularity of sedentary lifestyles, the incidence of CS has shown a significant increase and a trend toward affecting younger people in the past decade [4, 5]. CS ranks as the primary contributor to years lived with disability in degenerative musculoskeletal diseases, which seriously weakens the patient’s labor ability and quality of life [6]. Under the modern biopsychosocial medical model, patients are no longer satisfied with simple neural decompression, but have put forward higher requirements for early postoperative functional reconstruction, minimally invasive intervention and an enhanced perioperative rapid recovery experience.

Since the Smith-Robinson era, ACDF has traditionally been recognized as the benchmark surgical intervention for the treatment of CS due to its advantages including direct removal of anterior compression, restoration of intervertebral space height and immediate reconstruction of segmental stability [7]. Studies have shown that patients receiving classic ACDF exhibit a self-satisfaction rate of 85%-95% [8]. However, with the refinement of the clinical evaluation systems, the inherent defects of classical ACDF have gradually emerged. The surgical approach involves extensive dissection and prolonged traction of the esophagus, trachea and anterior soft tissue of the neck, and the incidence of early postoperative dysphagia is as high as 1%-71% [9]. In addition, the visual field of traditional microscope is limited to the surgeon and the first assistant, furthermore, the deep and narrow areas may remain obscured, and the posture can be easy to fatigue [10]. This visualization bottleneck not only increases the risk of intraoperative neural and vascular damage but may also lead to residual radicular pain after surgery due to incomplete decompression.

In recent years, minimally invasive spine surgery has evolved rapidly, and anterior cervical surgery has undergone a significant transition from traditional open direct visualization to endoscopic-assisted techniques [11, 12]. The UBE technology stands out with its unique “triangulation” mode of operation. UBE achieves the separation of visualization and instrument manipulation, which not only provides a high-definition, wide-angle panoramic anatomical view, effectively eliminating the visual blind spots, but also affords the operator operational flexibility similar to open surgery [13, 14]. This technique was initially applied to lumbar degenerative diseases and has shown excellent clinical efficacy [15–17]. With the accumulation of experience of surgeons, this technique was successfully extended to posterior cervical spine surgery. Clinical results have demonstrated that its improvement in VAS score in relieving radicular pain was similar to that of microscopic surgery, while showing unique advantages in preserving posterior cervical muscle attachment points and cervical spine stability [18, 19]. Based on the success achieved in the posterior lumbar and cervical spine, some scholars began to try to introduce UBE into the anterior cervical spine surgery (UBE-ACDF), and this successful integration marks a new stage of full spine coverage for this technology [20].

Although UBE technology has gained extensive clinical experience in posterior lumbar and cervical spine surgery and has been confirmed to significantly reduce tissue damage and accelerate postoperative recovery, the UBE-assisted ACDF (UBE-ACDF) is still in the early exploratory stage [20]. To our knowledge, few studies regarding ACDF assisted by UBE have been reported so far. Given the complex anterior cervical anatomy, the clinical characteristics and outcomes of this approach are not yet fully defined. Further evidence is needed to compare UBE-ACDF with conventional ACDF regarding surgical efficiency, blood loss, systemic stress response, and long-term biomechanical stability.

This retrospective study systematically evaluates the perioperative efficacy, clinical outcomes, and imaging parameters of UBE-ACDF versus standard ACDF in managing CS, aiming to clarify the safety and clinical benefits of UBE-ACDF in anterior cervical spine surgery. We hypothesize that UBE-ACDF can achieve more precise intraoperative decompression with the advantage of high-definition visualization while maintaining comparable neurological function recovery as traditional surgery, and offers significant advantages in reducing surgical stress, minimizing approach-related soft tissue complications and accelerating recovery of patients, thereby providing a high-quality evidence-based foundation for individualized decision-making in minimally invasive surgery for CS.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was a single-center, retrospective case-control study designed to compare the clinical and radiographic results of UBE-ACDF and ACDF in the surgical management of CS. The study protocol was strictly in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and was reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the 923rd Hospital of the Joint Logistics Support Force of PLA (Approval No. 923LL-KY-2026LW-037-01). The ethics committee waived the requirement for written informed consent for participation because of the retrospective nature of this study and the use of deidentified data.

Case selection criteria

The inclusion criteria were: (1) patients with radicular neuralgia, hypoesthesia, or muscle weakness; (2) the location of nerve root compression confirmed by MRI and/or CT was consistent with clinical symptoms; (3) patients treated with UBE-ACDF or ACDF after no less than 6 weeks of standard conservative treatment; (4) Age within the range of 16 to 75 years. Exclusion criteria included active infection, malignancy, traumatic fracture, prior surgery, and significant segmental instability. During the study period, 92 patients who underwent anterior cervical surgery for CS were initially screened. Among them, 8 were excluded because of incomplete perioperative or radiographic data, and lost to follow-up. Finally, the UBE-ACDF group (n = 32) and the ACDF group (n = 52) were included in the analysis. Baseline demographics, including age, gender, BMI, and preoperative functional scores, were statistically comparable across both groups (P > 0.05).

Surgical technique

All procedures were performed by the same team of senior minimally invasive spine surgeons.

UBE-ACDF procedure: Under general anesthesia, the patient was placed in the supine position with mild hyperextension of the neck (Fig. 1). Guided by C-arm fluoroscopy, a 2 cm transverse incision was made on the right side of the anterior neck. The Smith-Robinson approach was used with blunt dissection to the prevertebral fascia. The Caspar distractor was used to distract the intervertebral space. The soft tissue was retracted with an S-shaped hook to establish a stable surgical area of about 2 cm. A sheath tube and endoscope were inserted, and gravity perfusion of isotonic saline was maintained. Marginal osteophytes of the vertebral body were resected using a rongeur under a 0° or 30° endoscope. A long-handled curette was used to clear the residual disc. The osteophytes at the posterior edge of the vertebral body were carefully removed using a high-speed drill by thinning the osteophyte base into a tapered configuration. Complete decompression of the spinal cord and nerve roots was achieved. The ossified posterior longitudinal ligament was excised when necessary to fully expose the dural sac. Strict hemostasis was performed endoscopically, and a 30-degree scope was used to confirm the absence of occult compression. The upper and lower endplates were prepared until punctate bleeding was observed, with careful preservation of the bony structures. The appropriately sized cage was filled with bone graft particles and implanted into the intervertebral space. The position and depth were confirmed by C-arm fluoroscopy. Four self-locking screws were inserted to provide immediate stability. The distractor was removed, and the Kirschner pin hole was closed with bone wax. The surgical field was reconfirmed to be free of active bleeding or retained irrigating fluid. The skin was sutured layer by layer, and a drainage tube was placed as appropriate (Fig. 2).

ACDF procedure: ACDF was performed by the same senior team via the classic Smith-Robinson approach. After general anesthesia, the patient was placed in the supine position with the neck in mild extension. A transverse incision was made at the target segment, and blunt dissection was performed between the carotid sheath and the tracheoesophageal space. After the segment was confirmed by fluoroscopy, the Caspar distractor was placed. Discectomy and posterior longitudinal ligament resection were completed for adequate decompression, and the endplate was prepared until subchondral bone was exposed. PEEK cage filled with allograft or autograft bone was implanted and fixed with anterior titanium plate and screws under fluoroscopy. After irrigation and drainage, the wound was sutured layer by layer.

Perioperative management

Patients in both groups followed the same standardized rehabilitation pathways. Prophylactic antibiotics were administered to all patients preoperatively, and antibiotic administration was continued for 24–48 h postoperatively. A closed suction drain was placed and removed within 24 h once the drainage output fell below 30 ml. On postoperative day 1, patients were encouraged to assume a semi-recumbent position or begin early ambulation with cervical brace support. All cases were instructed to use a cervical collar for a duration of 4 to 6 weeks postoperatively to maintain cervical stability. All patients also followed the same multimodal analgesia protocol.

Evaluation indicators and imaging measurements

The operation time, hospital stay, and complications of the two groups were recorded in detail. Intraoperative blood loss was measured using suction canister measurement and gauze weighing. Hidden blood loss was calculated according to the method described by Gross et al. [21], based on the difference in hematocrit between preoperative values and those measured on postoperative day 3. Clinical functions were evaluated preoperatively and at 1 week, 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively. The visual analogue scale (VAS) was used to assess neck and upper limb pain. Neurological function was assessed using the Neck Disability Index (NDI) and the modified Japanese Orthopaedic Association (mJOA) score.

All imaging parameters were assessed on standardized radiographs. The measurement work was completed on PACS system. The C2-7 Cobb angle was determined as the angular measurement formed between the inferior endplates of C2 and C7. The C2-7 range of motion was measured by calculating the difference between maximal flexion and extension. Segmental stability was evaluated by measuring the horizontal displacement and angular variation at the operative level. T1 slope was recorded synchronously to observe global sequence changes. The height of intervertebral space was determined by measuring the midpoint distance between the upper and lower endplates of the surgical segment. This index was used to evaluate the maintenance of decompression space and the settlement of fusion cage. To reduce measurement bias, all data were completed independently by two senior spine surgeons in a blinded manner. Inter-observer consistency was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), with a threshold set at ICC > 0.8.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 30.0 software (version 30.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).was used to analyze the data. The independent-sample t-test was used to compare normally distributed continuous variables, including baseline data (age, BMI, and hospitalization cost) and surgery-related indicators (operation time and cumulative incision length). The Mann-Whitney U test was applied to continuous variables that failed to satisfy the normality assumption (visible blood loss and hidden blood loss). Categorical data (gender, complication rate) were analyzed by Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Clinical outcome scores (VAS, NDI, and JOA) collected at multiple postoperative time points were analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVA. Imaging parameters (C2-7 Cobb angle, T1 slope, cSVA, intervertebral space height) were measured by two researchers independently. ICC was used to evaluate inter-observer reliability of the radiographic measurements. All tests were two-sided. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline data

A total of 32 cases in the UBE group and 52 cases in the ACDF group were included in this study. There were no significant differences in age (62.72 ± 9.59 vs. 60.79 ± 8.56 years, P = 0.341), gender composition (18/14 vs. 27/25, P = 0.704), BMI (22.24 ± 2.77 vs. 23.45 ± 2.71 kg/m², P = 0.053), or follow-up time (15.25 ± 5.49 vs. 17.79 ± 9.78 months, P = 0.183) between groups, suggesting that baseline data were comparable (Table 1).

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics

In terms of hospitalization related indicators, hospital stay in the UBE group was shorter than that in the ACDF group (12.59 ± 4.96 vs. 17.17 ± 11.71 days, P = 0.039). There was no significant difference in total hospitalization cost between the two groups (31084.94 ± 7494.96 vs. 31883.18 ± 13014.35 CNY, P > 0.05), but the operation cost was slightly lower in the UBE group (6206.78 ± 1290.60 vs. 6540.18 ± 2128.82 CNY, P = 0.039) (Table 1).

The operation time of the UBE-ACDF group (204.22 ± 94.92 min) was longer than that of traditional ACDF group (174.69 ± 56.20 min, P = 0.077), though this discrepancy did not reach statistical significance (Table 2).

Perioperative parameters

In terms of perioperative trauma-related indicators, the UBE-ACDF group demonstrated significant advantages: the cumulative incision length of UBE-ACDF group was significantly shorter than that of the traditional ACDF group (4.97 ± 1.82 vs. 6.94 ± 1.58 cm, P < 0.001); The visible blood loss in the UBE- ACDF group was significantly less than that in the ACDF group [107.50 (55.00 to 133.75) vs. 237.50 (131.25 to 500.00) ml; Mann–Whitney U test, P < 0.001]. Clinical efficacy

Pain score

Preoperatively, no significant differences were observed between the two groups in VAS-neck (5.78 ± 1.21 vs. 5.65 ± 1.05, P = 0.611) or VAS-arm scores (5.66 ± 1.18 vs. 5.73 ± 1.24, P = 0.786) (Table 3). At 1 month postoperatively, both groups showed marked reductions in VAS-neck (2.88 ± 0.71 vs. 3.10 ± 0.63, P = 0.141) and VAS-arm scores (2.72 ± 0.77 vs. 2.81 ± 0.79, P = 0.615) compared to baseline, with no significant intergroup differences. This improvement continued through the 3-month and 6-month follow-ups, with no significant differences between the groups (P > 0.05). At the final follow-up, VAS-neck scores remained comparable (1.16 ± 0.85 vs. 0.98 ± 0.64, P = 0.285). However, the VAS-arm score was significantly higher in the UBE group than in the ACDF group (1.06 ± 0.91 vs. 0.60 ± 0.57, P = 0.005), indicating superior long-term upper limb pain relief in the ACDF group.

Radiological outcomes

Neurological function

No significant difference was found in preoperative JOA scores between the two groups (12.47 ± 2.45 vs. 12.62 ± 1.97, P = 0.764) (Table 3). Both groups demonstrated progressive neurological improvement compared to baseline throughout the follow-up period. There were no significant intergroup differences at 1 week (15.66 ± 1.86 vs. 13.40 ± 1.74, P = 0.531), 3 months (14.75 ± 1.50 vs. 14.69 ± 1.42, P = 0.860), or 6 months (15.69 ± 1.35 vs. 15.67 ± 1.13, P = 0.958) postoperatively. At the final follow-up, JOA scores remained comparable between the groups (15.94 ± 1.34 vs. 16.10 ± 1.01, P = 0.541).

Radiographic outcomes

Regarding global sagittal parameters, no significant differences were observed between the two groups in preoperative C2–7 Cobb angles (14.28 ± 12.16° vs. 11.21 ± 10.66°, P = 0.228) or postoperative values (15.44 ± 11.10° vs. 12.52 ± 10.67°, P = 0.234) (Table 4). The Δ C2–7 Cobb angle values (1.16 ± 4.21° vs. 1.31 ± 6.31°, P = 0.905) also showed no significant difference. However, both preoperative (22.38 ± 8.52° vs. 11.21 ± 10.66°, P = 0.001) and postoperative (25.63 ± 7.65° vs. 12.52 ± 10.67°, P = 0.001) T1 slopes were significantly higher in the UBE group than in the ACDF group. The Δ T1 slope (3.25 ± 4.73° vs. -0.23 ± 5.41°) also demonstrated a statistically significant difference between the two groups (P = 0.004).

In terms of cSVA, no significant intergroup differences were found preoperatively (16.62 ± 8.06 vs. 16.98 ± 8.29 mm, P = 0.844) or postoperatively (18.60 ± 7.10 vs. 17.44 ± 8.16 mm, P = 0.509). Δ cSVA showed no statistically significant difference between the two groups (P = 0.311). In addition, there was no significant difference in segmental Cobb angle and its Δ values between the two groups (all P > 0.05).

Complications

In the UBE-ACDF group, complications included one case of nerve root injury, one case of recurrent radicular symptoms, and one case of hematoma (Table 5). In the ACDF group, complications included one case of dural tear, three cases of nerve root injury, two cases of infection, one case of hematoma, and one case of recurrent radicular symptoms. No revision surgeries were required in either group. The overall complication rates were low in both groups, with no statistically significant difference between them (P > 0.05).

Discussion

The cervical spine is the segment with the largest range of motion and the most complex anatomical structure in the human spine, which undertakes the function of supporting the head and protecting important neural and vascular channels [22, 23]. Long-term abnormal mechanical load and chronic strain often lead to the destruction of the biomechanical stability of the cervical spine, and then cause osteophyte hyperplasia, ligament hypertrophy and intervertebral space stenosis [24]. Although most patients with CS achieve satisfactory symptom relief with conservative measures, operative intervention becomes warranted for those who fail to respond to conventional nonsurgical management [25].

ACDF was first reported by Smith and Robinson in 1958 and has since demonstrated well-established clinical efficacy. This procedure can provide immediate stability, achieve direct decompression, and restore cervical lordosis [26]. With these advantages, it has long been regarded as the “gold standard” for the treatment of CS [27, 28]. However, ACDF has its disadvantages. It changes the biomechanical stress distribution of the surgical segment and its adjacent segments, and may induce adjacent segment degeneration. In addition, some complications cannot be ignored. Common complications include postoperative dysphagia, recurrent laryngeal nerve injury, and cervical hematoma. Severe complications, such as esophageal perforation, cage migration, or pseudarthrosis, may occur in rare instances. These potential risks must be weighed in the choice of surgical protocol [29, 30].

This study systematically compared the clinical efficacy and radiographic results between UBE-ACDF and traditional ACDF in the surgical management of CS. The results showed that the two procedures could significantly reduce the pain and improve functional outcomes of patients, and the overall efficacy was similar in the short - and medium-term follow-up. Although both groups showed substantial postoperative improvement, the final follow-up VAS-arm score was significantly higher in the UBE-ACDF group than in the ACDF group. This finding suggests that the minimally invasive advantages of UBE-ACDF may not necessarily result in superior long-term radicular pain relief. The difference may be related to the learning curve of anterior cervical UBE, technical challenges in achieving consistent foraminal decompression, possible nerve root irritation during endoscopic manipulation, and baseline sagittal alignment differences. Therefore, this result should be interpreted cautiously and acknowledged as a potential limitation of UBE-ACDF in the present cohort.

To date, few studies have directly compared UBE-assisted ACDF with the traditional ACDF technique. However, the successful clinical application of endoscopic-assisted ACDF (Endo-ACDF) provided a critical rationale for the present study [31–33]. Oda et al. [34] demonstrated that endoscopic techniques significantly alleviate prevertebral soft tissue swelling at 2 and 7 days postoperatively, with safety profiles and clinical outcomes comparable to those of conventional methods. Based on these previous experiences, we hypothesized that UBE-ACDF also had good application potential. Our results confirmed that both procedures significantly improved clinical scores compared to baseline. No significant intergroup differences were observed in VAS, NDI, or JOA scores at most follow-up intervals, indicating that both surgical modalities effectively achieved neural decompression, substantial pain relief, and functional restoration. This further confirms the reliability and effectiveness of UBE-ACDF in cervical spine surgery.

UBE-ACDF showed a clear minimally invasive advantage in perioperative indicators. Our results showed that both incision length and intraoperative blood loss were significantly lower in the UBE-ACDF group than in the ACDF group. This superior hemostatic control was likely attributable to the high-definition visualization provided by the endoscopic system under continuous irrigation. Furthermore, hospital stay was also significantly shorter, reflecting the advantages of endoscopic technique in reducing muscle dissection and soft tissue injury, which helped to accelerate the postoperative recovery. These findings align with the study by Zhong et al. [20], which reported smaller skin incisions in UBE-ACDF. Although the operative time in the UBE-ACDF group was slightly longer than in the ACDF group, this difference likely reflects the inherent learning curve associated with the endoscopic technique.

Radiographic analysis further revealed the differences in the biomechanical reconstruction of the cervical spine between the two surgical procedures. In this study, no significant differences were observed in global alignment parameters such as C2-7 Cobb angle and cSVA between the two groups, indicating that UBE-ACDF could achieve adequate decompression without adversely affecting the global cervical sagittal balance. This result is consistent with the study by Zhong HZ et al. [20]. The UBE-ACDF group showed a higher T1 slope both preoperatively and postoperatively. The increase in T1 slope after UBE-ACDF may be related to restoration of intervertebral height, segmental support, Caspar distraction, and cage insertion, which can alter lower cervical orientation and cervicothoracic compensation. However, T1 slope is influenced by global sagittal posture, and the UBE-ACDF group had a significantly higher preoperative T1 slope. The finding should not be interpreted as definitive evidence that UBE-ACDF provides superior sagittal correction and this radiographic result should be considered exploratory. This phenomenon has not been previously reported, and the biomechanical significance of this finding warrants further investigation.

Our results demonstrated that UBE-ACDF had an acceptable safety profile, with no serious adverse events recorded. Complications such as nerve root injury and hematoma occurred in the UBE group, while nerve root injury, hematoma and infection were the main complications in the ACDF group, suggesting that the two procedures are associated with distinct complication profiles. It is believed that continuous irrigation reduces the risk of surgical site infection, while precise manipulation under clear endoscopic visualization may reduce the risk of neural and spinal cord injury.

Certain limitations of the present study warrant acknowledgment. First, this research was a retrospective analysis with a relatively limited sample size and potential selection bias. Second, the follow-up duration was relatively short for a cervical fusion study. Therefore, the present study could not comprehensively evaluate long-term maintenance of sagittal alignment, cage subsidence, pseudarthrosis, or adjacent segment degeneration. The current findings should be interpreted as short- to mid-term results rather than definitive long-term evidence. Third, the lack of standardized long-term imaging follow-up limited the assessment of fusion status, pseudarthrosis, cage subsidence, and adjacent segment degeneration. Therefore, future prospective studies should include longer and more consistent radiographic follow-up to evaluate fusion durability and sagittal alignment maintenance. In addition, the potential impact of the learning curve on operative time and complication rates was not accounted for, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Furthermore, the underlying biomechanical mechanisms driving the observed changes in radiographic parameters remain to be fully elucidated. Future prospective, multicenter studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up periods are warranted to validate these findings and conclusions.

Conclusions

UBE-ACDF appears to be a safe, effective, and promising minimally invasive alternative to conventional ACDF in appropriately selected patients with CS. In this cohort, UBE-ACDF achieved comparable overall neurological and functional recovery while offering clear perioperative advantages, including smaller incision length, lower visible blood loss, and shorter hospital stay. However, conventional ACDF showed greater long-term arm pain relief at the final follow-up, and the interpretation of T1 slope changes was limited by baseline sagittal alignment imbalance. Further prospective studies with standardized long-term imaging follow-up are needed to confirm fusion durability, cage subsidence, adjacent segment degeneration, and sustained sagittal alignment maintenance.
