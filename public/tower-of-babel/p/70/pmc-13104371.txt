Consider Counterforce Before More Force in Stent-Mediated Device Entrapment
Authors: Calvin Leung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Device entrapment during percutaneous coronary intervention is rare but can be life-threatening, causing obstructive ischemia, vessel dissection, perforation, retained thrombogenic foreign material, and, at times, emergency surgery.1, 2, 3, 4 Percutaneous retrieval can be challenging, and the evidence base is limited; management often relies on operator judgment under time pressure, sometimes in previously unencountered scenarios. Familiarity with retrieval tools and techniques, understanding the entrapment mechanism, and a structured stepwise approach help maximize success while minimizing collateral injury.5

In this issue of JACC: Case Reports, Jossart et al6 reported a rare but instructive scenario: a 0.014-inch coronary guidewire irreversibly trapped within a preexisting brachiocephalic trunk stent during left main percutaneous coronary intervention. Unlike anatomically mediated entrapment from tortuosity or calcification, device-related entrapment within a metallic scaffold has a distinct challenge. Uncontrolled traction risks not only wire fracture or unraveling but also stent deformation or dislodgement, with potential for major vascular injury.7

Conventional percutaneous options for device entrapment include gentle pulling, guide-catheter balloon trapping, snaring, telescoping techniques, and balloon-based modification of the entrapment site,7, 8, 9 whereas controlled rupture is a higher-risk bailout reserved for selected cases.10 In the present case, these approaches were unsuccessful or not feasible, and further traction produced visible stent motion with interaction against surrounding calcification.

The authors’ bailout—GRASP (guided retrieval with assisted stent protection)—leverages a counterforce stabilization strategy.6 Via secondary femoral access, a balloon is inflated within the brachiocephalic stent to splint and stabilize the scaffold, whereas simultaneous radial snaring applies controlled traction. Rather than simply “pulling harder,” counterforce can alter the interaction at the entrapment site, enabling bidirectional force control and minimizing stent deformation and unintended relative movement that can precipitate stent migration and vascular injury. A similar approach has been reported for pigtail catheter entrapment in a transcatheter aortic valve prosthesis, using aortic balloon inflation to stabilize the valve stent frame.11

Important pitfalls warrant emphasis. Dual access and synchronized maneuvers increase procedural complexity. Prolonged balloon inflation in a supra-aortic stent carries meaningful risks—vascular injury or dissection, thrombosis, and embolization—requiring careful balloon sizing, strict limits on inflation time and pressure, meticulous anticoagulation, and postretrieval angiography to confirm vascular and stent integrity. Notably, this technique is adjunctive and should be integrated with established retrieval methods; alone, it may not prevent wire fracture or filamentous unraveling when excessive force concentrates at hinge points, which can be difficult to manage.12

Overall, this case highlights the distinct behavior of stent-mediated entrapment and suggests that, in selected scenarios, applying counterforce to stabilize the indwelling device before further traction should be considered part of the bailout armamentarium.
