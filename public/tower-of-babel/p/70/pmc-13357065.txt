CLCA1 as an immune prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target in colorectal cancer
Authors: Zewei Cheng, Yuan Li, Xin Wang, Ben Feng, Yebo Shao, Li Yang, Jiwei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) progression is profoundly shaped by the tumor immune microenvironment. Increasing evidence suggests that dysregulated immune activation and immune evasion jointly contribute to tumor progression and therapeutic resistance. This study aims to identify novel immune-related biomarkers and explore combinatorial therapeutic strategies for overcoming immunosuppression in CRC by investigating the interplay between chloride channel accessory 1 (CLCA1)-mediated immune activation and serine proteinase inhibitor B9 (SERPINB9)-driven immune escape.MethodsIntegrated multi-omics analyses were performed to identify key immune-related prognostic biomarkers in CRC and to construct a prognostic immune signature. The predictive performance of the model was validated in independent cohorts. Functional assays were conducted in female C57BL/6J mice to evaluate the effects of CLCA1 on tumor growth and immune cell infiltration. Mechanistic analyses were used to investigate the regulatory relationship between CLCA1 and SERPINB9. In addition, computational drug screening, together with structural and biophysical assays, was applied to identify candidate small molecules targeting CLCA1.ResultsCLCA1 was identified as a central immune-related prognostic biomarker in CRC. A 14-gene immune signature effectively stratified patients into distinct risk groups and showed robust predictive value across independent cohorts. Functional studies demonstrated that CLCA1 overexpression suppressed tumor growth and promoted the infiltration of cytotoxic immune cells. Mechanistically, CLCA1 downregulated SERPINB9, a granzyme B (GZMB) inhibitor that plays a pivotal role in immune evasion. Notably, combined CLCA1/SERPINB9 expression status provided improved prognostic stratification compared with either marker alone. Computational screening further identified phloretin as a potential CLCA1-targeting compound, which was supported by structural and biophysical validation.ConclusionsThese findings identify CLCA1 as a prognostic immune modulator and a potential therapeutic target in CRC. The functional interaction between CLCA1 and SERPINB9 highlights a mechanistic axis linking immune activation and immune escape. Moreover, phloretin emerges as a candidate small molecule for modulating CLCA1, although its in vivo immunomodulatory effects and target dependency require further validation.Keywords: Colorectal cancer (CRC), immune cell infiltration, chloride channel accessory 1 (CLCA1), serine proteinase inhibitor B9 (SERPINB9), phloretin

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC), responsible for ~935,000 annual deaths globally, faces rising incidence due to lifestyle, dietary, and genetic factors (1-4). Despite advancements in surgical and targeted therapies, late-stage prognosis remains poor, compounded by the absence of reliable early diagnostic biomarkers. While immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) like anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) antibodies show promise in subsets of CRC patients, therapeutic response rates remain low (<20%), due to tumor immune evasion and inadequate T-cell infiltration (5-7). Current limitations in predicting ICI efficacy underscore the critical need to identify novel biomarkers and develop combinatorial strategies targeting CRC immunosuppression.

Chloride channel accessory 1 (CLCA1), a key regulator of mucosal immunity and ion transport, has emerged as a tumor suppressor across multiple malignancies (8-12). In CRC, CLCA1 modulates tumor-immune crosstalk by enhancing lymphocyte infiltration and suppressing immune checkpoint expression, suggesting potential synergy with ICI therapy (13,14). However, compensatory immune resistance mechanisms persist. Serine proteinase inhibitor B9 (SERPINB9), an intracellular granzyme B (GZMB) inhibitor, enables tumor evasion of cytotoxic lymphocyte-mediated apoptosis and correlates with ICI resistance (15-19). The interplay between CLCA1-mediated immune activation and SERPINB9-driven immune suppression remains unexplored, representing a critical knowledge gap in CRC immunobiology.

To address this knowledge deficit, we performed integrated transcriptomic analysis of CRC cohorts combined with immune infiltration algorithms and weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), identifying CLCA1 as a key prognostic biomarker linked to cytotoxic immune cell recruitment. Functional validation in preclinical models demonstrated CLCA1-mediated suppression of tumor growth and SERPINB9-dependent immune evasion, while computational screening via the L1000 Fireworks Display platform prioritized phloretin as a CLCA1-targeting candidate. These findings collectively establish the CLCA1-SERPINB9 axis as a determinant of CRC immunogenicity, providing both prognostic stratification tools and actionable therapeutic targets. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD and MDAR and ARRIVE reporting checklists (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-aw-2521/rc).

Methods

Data collection

RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) data and clinical information for CRC samples were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/), which included 51 normal samples and 587 tumor samples. Gene expression profiles and clinical data of GSE17536 dataset containing 117 CRC samples were derived from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gds). The data repositories and accession numbers for the TCGA datasets and the in-house mouse tumor RNA sequencing data used in this study are summarized in Table S1. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Immune cell infiltration analysis

Cell-type identification by estimating relative subsets of RNA transcripts (CIBERSORT) is a method to quantify the cell composition of complex tissues based on standardized gene expression profiles (20). The fractions of 22 immune cells for each CRC sample in the TCGA cohort were determined by employing the CIBERSORT package. The samples with P value less than 0.05 were selected for the following analysis. The differences of 22 immune cell subtypes between the normal and tumor groups were compared by using Wilcox.test.

WGCNA

WGCNA was used to identify immune-related genes (IRGs) in CRC (21). All TCGA-CRC samples were clustered to remove outliers (hclust). A soft-thresholding power was selected to achieve scale-free topology (R2≥0.9). Co-expression modules were detected via a dynamic tree-cutting algorithm (minimum module size =30, merge cut height =0.5), each represented by a distinct color. Module-trait correlations were calculated, and the module with the highest correlation to differential immune cell traits was defined as the key module. Genes within this module were designated as IRGs.

Identification of differentially expressed IRGs (DE-IRGs)

The 587 tumor samples and 51 normal samples of TCGA database were analyzed by limma package to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the CRC samples and normal samples. |Log2fold change |>1 and adjusted P value <0.05 were the screening conditions for DEGs. The expression of top 100 DEGs between the CRC samples and normal samples were displayed in a heatmap that was generated by pheatmap R package. Subsequently, the DE-IRGs were identified by overlapping the genes extracted from the key modules and DEGs using the Venny website (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/index.html).

Development and validation of the prognostic signature

DE-IRG expression profiles and corresponding clinical data from TCGA-CRC samples constituted a training set. Univariate Cox regression identified IRGs significantly associated with overall survival (P<0.05). These candidates were subjected to least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression (glmnet package) to prevent overfitting. Selected IRGs were then incorporated into a stepwise multivariate Cox model (survival package) to construct an immune-related prognostic signature. Each patient’s risk score was calculated as the sum of expression levels weighted by the multivariate Cox coefficients. Patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups using the optimal cutoff. Kaplan-Meier (K-M) analysis compared survival between groups, and time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves at 1, 3, and 5 years were generated (survival ROC package) to assess predictive performance. Finally, the signature was externally validated in the GSE17536 cohort.

Development of a nomogram incorporating risk score and clinicopathological features

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses was executed using clinicopathological features including age, gender, T stage, N stage, M stage, pathologic stage and risk score to identify independent prognostic factors for CRC. Then, a nomogram model combining independent prognostic factors was developed by rms package. The corresponding calibration plots were generated to evaluate the consistency between the predicted and actual survival.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA)

GSEA was performed based on the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) gene set (c2.cp.kegg.v7.2.symbols.gmt) and hallmark gene set (h.all.v7.2.symbols.gmt) to identify biological processes and KEGG pathways associated with prognostic gene signature. The enriched terms with a nominal (NOM) P value <0.05 were considered as statistically significant (22-24).

ROC curve analyses

Gene expression data for CLCA1, HIGD1A, and CPT2 were retrieved from the TCGA- colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) & rectum adenocarcinoma (READ) cohort and dichotomized into high and low expression groups at the median. ROC curves were generated using the pROC package (v1.18.0) in R (v4.2.1), and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to quantify each gene’s ability to discriminate CRC from normal tissue. An AUC >0.7 was considered indicative of good diagnostic performance. The optimal cutoff values were identified by the Youden index (maximizing sensitivity + specificity). Sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy at these thresholds were computed and plotted with ggplot2 (v3.4.4).

Plasmid construction and stable cell line generation

CLCA1 coding sequences were amplified from MC38 complementary DNA (cDNA) using primers in Table S2, incorporating EcoRI and BamHI sites, and cloned into the Plvx-hygr lentiviral vector (Addgene #12252) to generate Plvx-CLCA1 (ovCLCA1). An empty Plvx-hygr vector served as the negative control (Vector). Lentiviral particles were produced by co-transfecting HEK293T cells with Plvx-CLCA1 or Vector and packaging plasmids pMD2.G and psPAX2 (Addgene #12259/#12260) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) in OPTI-MEM (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Viral supernatants were collected at 48 and 72 h, filtered (0.45 µm), and stored at −80 ℃.

MC38 cells were infected with ovCLCA1 or Vector lentivirus in the presence of 5 µg/mL polybrene and selected with 50 µg/mL hygromycin B for 2 weeks to establish stable overexpression lines. CLCA1 overexpression was confirmed by Western blot and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR).

Cell culture and animals

MC38 cells were purchased from the Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Cells were cultured in DMEM (Cat# MA0212, Meilunbio, Dalian, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Cat# PWL217, Meilunbio) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 ℃ in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere.

For the in vivo studies, specific pathogen-free (SPF), female, 5- to 6-week-old C57BL/6J mice weighing 18–22 g were purchased from Charles River. Mice were housed in temperature-controlled holding rooms (20–22 ℃) with 30–34% humidity under a 12-hour light/dark cycle (lights on at 6:00 a.m.) and had ad libitum access to standard laboratory chow and water. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Shanghai Immunocan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Approval No. YMNK-AUA-01301, Approval Date: February 10, 2024), and were performed in strict accordance with institutional guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. A protocol was prepared before the study without registration.

To establish the subcutaneous tumor model and assess the impact of CLCA1, mice were randomly allocated into two groups (n=8 per group) prior to tumor inoculation. The sample size was determined based on similar preclinical tumor models to ensure adequate statistical power. MC38-Vector or MC38-ovCLCA1 cells [5×105 cells suspended in 100 µL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)] were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of each mouse. Tumor volumes were measured every 3 days using digital calipers by investigators who were blinded to the group allocation. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: V = (length × width2)/2. Throughout the study, no animals met the criteria for humane endpoints, and no animals were excluded from the final analysis. At 3–4 weeks post-inoculation, all mice were euthanized via CO2 inhalation, and the tumors were completely excised, photographed, weighed, and subsequently processed for flow cytometry analysis.

Migration and proliferation

MC38-ovCLCA1 and Vector control cells (1×105) were seeded in the upper chamber of 24-well transwell inserts (8 µm; Corning, Corning, NY, USA) with complete medium (10% FBS) in the lower chamber. After 24 h at 37 ℃/5% CO2, non-migrated cells were removed, and migrated cells on the membrane underside were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with crystal violet (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), and counted in five random fields per insert under a light microscope. Data are presented as the mean number of migrated cells per field (n=3).

For proliferation assays, MC38-ovCLCA1 and control cells were plated at 5×103 cells/well in 96 well plates. At 24, 48, and 72 h, 10 µL Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) reagent (Meilunbio) was added, and plates were incubated for 4 h at 37 ℃. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured on a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher), and proliferation rates were calculated from triplicate wells per time point.

Real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from the cells using TRIzol Reagent (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), and cDNA synthesis was performed using the HiScript III All-in-one RT SuperMix Perfect for qPCR (Vazyme) following the manufacturer’s instructions. For qPCR, cDNA was amplified using the qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen, Shanghai, China). The qPCR cycling conditions were as follows: 95 ℃ for 5 min, 40 cycles of 95 ℃ for 10 s, 55–60 °C for 20 s, and 72 ℃ for 20 s. A melting curve analysis was conducted, and the cycle threshold (Ct) values were determined during the exponential phase of amplification using QuantStudio Software v1.3 (Thermo Fisher, USA). Relative gene expression was normalized to the control group, which was set at a fold change of 1. All reactions were performed in triplicate. Primer sequences are provided in Table S2.

Immunoblotting

Protein samples (20–40 µg) were separated by 8% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) (Life-ilab) and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST) [120 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, and 0.05% Tween 20] for 2 hours at room temperature. Blots were incubated with CLCA1 (Cat# ab180851, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) antibody overnight at 4 ℃. Western blotting of α-Tubulin (Cat# ab7291, Abcam) on the same membrane was used as a loading control. The membranes were then washed with TBST 3 times and incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody. Proteins were visualized using a Pierce ECL Chemiluminescent Substrate (Tanon, Shanghai, China).

Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database analysis

Data from the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) and the HPA (https://www.proteinatlas.org/) were employed to assess the differential protein expression of CLCA1, CPT2, and HIGD1A in tumor tissues from CRC patients, relative to normal control tissues (25). Specifically, immunohistochemistry (IHC) images of normal colon and CRC tissues were retrieved from the HPA database. According to the standard HPA protocol, tissue microarrays were probed with corresponding primary antibodies and visualized using a 3,3'-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate, which yields a brown precipitate at the site of specific antigen binding. The tissue sections were further counterstained with hematoxylin to delineate cellular and histological structures. All stained slides in the HPA database are digitally scanned at a 20× magnification to generate high-resolution images.

Tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) isolation

TILs were isolated from subcutaneous MC38-ovCLCA1 and MC38-Vector tumors. Tumors were harvested and mechanically dissociated using a sterile scalpel and syringe plunger. The tissue was digested in RPMI-1640 medium containing 0.5 mg/mL Liberase TL (Roche, Indianapolis, USA) and 0.1 mg/mL DNase I (Roche) for 30 minutes at 37 ℃ with constant shaking. Following digestion, the cell suspension was filtered through a 70-µm mesh strainer (BIOFIL, Guangzhou, China) to remove large debris. The resulting single-cell suspension was washed twice with PBS containing 2% FBS.

For flow cytometry analysis, the isolated cells were stained with a cocktail of antibodies against surface markers specific to immune cell populations. The following antibodies were used: anti-CD45 (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA), anti-CD4 (BioLegend), anti-CD8α (BioLegend), anti-GZMB (BioLegend), anti-NK1.1 (BioLegend), anti-IFN-γ (BioLegend), anti-B220 (BioLegend), anti-CD11c (BioLegend), anti-Ly-6c (BioLegend), anti-Ly-6G (BioLegend), and F4-80 (BioLegend). Cell surface staining was performed in PBS containing 2% FBS for 30 minutes at 4 ℃. After staining, cells were washed with PBS and fixed in 1% paraformaldehyde (Cat# P6148, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA).

The flow cytometry analysis was performed using a BD FACS Canto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). Data were analyzed with FlowJo software (FlowJo LLC, version 10.0.8). The frequency of immune cell populations [CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, B cells, and natural killer (NK) cells] within the tumor was calculated relative to the total CD45+ cells.

Identification of candidate drugs

The L1000 FWD platform (https://maayanlab.cloud/l1000fwd/) was employed to identify small molecules that may upregulate CLCA1 expression. Using this tool, we analyzed potential drug candidates based on their correlation with CLCA1 expression in relevant gene signatures (26). Drugs with high similarity scores and strong correlation to CLCA1 were selected as potential candidates for further investigation. The drug candidates identified through this approach were assessed for their therapeutic potential in CRC.

Cellular Thermal Shift Assay (CETSA)

MC38-ovCLCA1 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 (10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin) at 37 ℃/5% CO2 to ~70% confluence. Cells were treated with 100 µM phloretin for 5 h, harvested, washed with cold PBS, and lysed in RIPA buffer with protease inhibitors on ice for 15 min. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation (14,000 ×g, 10 min, 4 ℃).

To assess thermal stability, protein samples were incubated at various temperatures ranging from 40 to 70 ℃ for 3 minutes, then cooled at room temperature for 10 min. Aggregates were pelleted (14,000 ×g, 10 min), and supernatants were analyzed by Western blot to assess CLCA1 thermal stability in the presence of phloretin.

Drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS)

DARTS assays were conducted to assess phloretin binding to CLCA1 in MC38 cells. MC38-ovCLCA1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates in complete RPMI 1640 and incubated overnight. Cells were then treated with phloretin (0, 12.5, 25, 50, 100, or 200 µM) for 1 h at 37 ℃, washed with PBS, and lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors. Lysates were adjusted to equal protein concentrations and incubated with pronase (1:100) (Roche) for 30 min at room temperature. Reactions were stopped by adding 5× SDS loading buffer, followed by SDS-PAGE and Western blotting. CLCA1 stability was determined by comparing CLCA1 band intensities in phloretin-treated versus control samples using an anti-CLCA1 antibody.

Molecular docking of phloretin to CLCA1

Molecular docking studies were performed to evaluate the interaction between phloretin and CLCA1. The three-dimensional structure of phloretin (PubChem CID: 4788) was obtained from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), and the SDF file was downloaded for docking analysis. The structure of the CLCA1 protein was obtained from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (PDB ID: AF-Q9D7Z6-F1-v4).

Molecular docking simulations were carried out using the CB-Dock2 online application (http://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock/) (27,28). The prepared CLCA1 protein and phloretin were docked using a blind docking approach, and the binding affinity was determined by the Vina scoring function. The optimal docking result with the lowest binding energy was selected for further analysis. The interaction between phloretin and CLCA1 was visualized using PyMOL (version 2.5.4), where hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions between phloretin and CLCA1 were analyzed.

Statistical analysis

All the analyses in the present study were performed using R software. The OS analyses were conducted by Kaplan-Meier analysis with the log-rank test. Wilcox.test was used to compare differences of 22 immune cell subtypes between the high-risk group and low-risk group. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 10.1. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of mean (SEM). Independent samples tests were used for pairwise comparisons, while one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for multiple comparisons. All the graphs were generated with GraphPad Prism 10.1 software. All statistical tests were considered as statistically significant with P values <0.05.

Results

Immune cell infiltration analysis in CRC

To investigate the CRC immune microenvironment, we used the CIBERSORT algorithm to quantify 22 tumor-infiltrating immune cell subsets in 51 normal and 587 CRC samples (Figure 1A). Differential analysis identified 13 immune subsets with significantly altered proportions between tumor and normal tissues, including naive B cells, plasma cells, CD4+ memory-activated T cells, γδ T cells, monocytes, M0/M1/M2 macrophages, resting/activated dendritic cells, resting/activated mast cells, eosinophils, and neutrophils (Figure 1B). These findings indicate that our prognostic signature reflects CRC immune status and underscore the pivotal role of tumor immunity in CRC progression.

Identification of key gene modules associated with differential immune cell infiltration in CRC

To identify gene co-expression modules closely associated with immune cell infiltration in CRC, we performed WGCNA using RNA-seq data from all CRC patients in the TCGA cohort. Thirteen differentially infiltrated immune cell types were incorporated as clinical traits, enabling a systematic evaluation of immune–gene interaction patterns at the network level.

To ensure the robustness and biological relevance of the constructed gene network, an appropriate soft-thresholding power (β) was selected based on the scale-free topology criterion. As shown in Figure 2A, a power of β=8 was chosen, at which the scale-free topology fitting index (R2) approached saturation while preserving sufficient mean connectivity. This indicates that the resulting network closely approximates a scale-free architecture, a fundamental property of biological networks.

Using hierarchical clustering combined with the dynamic tree cut algorithm, genes with similar expression profiles were grouped into co-expression modules. After merging highly similar modules, a total of 12 distinct gene modules were identified, each labeled with a unique color (Figure 2B). These modules represent discrete transcriptional programs potentially involved in CRC progression and immune regulation.

Subsequently, module-trait correlation analysis was conducted to assess the relationships between module eigengenes and immune cell traits. As illustrated in Figure 2C, several modules exhibited significant correlations with immune cell infiltration levels. Notably, the blue and dark-orange modules demonstrated the strongest and most consistent associations with multiple immune cell populations, including macrophage subsets and T-cell populations. These findings suggest that genes within these two modules may play critical roles in shaping the immune microenvironment of CRC. Consequently, the blue and dark-orange modules were selected for downstream analyses.

Identification of DE-IRGs in CRC

To further refine immune-relevant candidates, we performed differential expression analysis between CRC and adjacent normal tissues. Using stringent thresholds of |log2fold change| >1 and adjusted P<0.05, a total of 1,605 DEGs were identified, including 785 upregulated and 820 downregulated genes (Figure 2D). The volcano plot clearly illustrates the global transcriptional alterations distinguishing CRC from normal tissues.

A heatmap displaying the top 50 upregulated and top 50 downregulated DEGs further highlights the distinct expression patterns between tumor and normal samples (Figure 2E), confirming the reliability and biological relevance of the differential expression analysis.

To identify IRGs that are both differentially expressed and embedded within immune-associated co-expression modules, we intersected the DEGs with genes from the blue and dark-orange modules. This integrative approach yielded 509 DE-IRGs (Figure 2F). These DE-IRGs represent a focused gene set that is not only transcriptionally dysregulated in CRC but also tightly linked to immune cell infiltration, making them strong candidates for further functional characterization and biomarker development.

Development of an immune-related signature for CRC

Integrating TCGA-CRC survival data, univariate Cox regression identified 66 IRGs significantly associated with overall survival (P<0.05). Twenty-five of these genes were prioritized by LASSO Cox regression (Figure 3A). A 14 gene immune prognostic signature was then constructed via stepwise multivariate Cox regression (Figure 3B).

Based on the optimal cut-off value of risk score, the CRC patients in the TCGA database were assigned to a low-risk group (n=177) and a high-risk group (n=410) (Figure 3C). High-risk patients exhibited significantly poorer survival than low-risk patients (log-rank P<0.001; Figure 3D). Time-dependent ROC analysis yielded AUCs of 0.736 (1 year), 0.736 (3 years), and 0.800 (5 years), demonstrating robust predictive performance (Figure 3E). Expression heatmap of the 14 prognostic IRGs across risk groups is shown in Figure 3F.

Validation of the immune-related signature in an external GSE17536 dataset

To assess the robustness of our 14 gene prognostic signatures, we computed patient risk scores in the GSE17536 cohort (GEO accession: GSE17536) and stratified individuals into high-risk (n=62) and low-risk (n=115) groups. Figure S1A displays the distribution of risk scores alongside corresponding survival outcomes for CRC patients. K-M analysis indicated that high-risk patients experienced significantly poorer overall survival compared with low-risk patients (log-rank P<0.05; Figure S1B). Furthermore, time-dependent ROC analysis yielded AUCs of 0.521, 0.604, and 0.655 at 1-, 3-, and 5-year follow-up, respectively (Figure S1C), supporting the moderate discriminative performance of the signature. A heatmap of the 14 prognostic IRG expression profiles across high- and low-risk groups is provided in Figure S1D.

Risk score was an independent predictor for CRC

The risk score and clinicopathological features including age, gender, tumor (T) stage, lymph node (N) stage, metastasis (M) stage, and pathologic stage were included in univariate Cox regression analysis. Age, pathologic stage, T stage, M stage, N stage, and risk score were demonstrated to be associated with overall survival of CRC patients (Figure S1E, P<0.05), which were further incorporated into multivariate Cox regression analysis. As a result, age [hazard ratio (HR) =1.050, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.033–1.069, P<0.001], pathologic stage (HR =1.585, 95% CI: 1.129–2.227, P=0.008) and risk score (HR =2.464, 95% CI: 1.980–3.066, P<0.001) were independent prognostic factors for predicting CRC prognosis (Figure S1F). A nomogram incorporating age, pathologic stage, and risk score was constructed to predict 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival probability (Figure S1G). The calibration plots manifested that the prediction by nomogram showed an optimal consistency with actual observation, revealing that the nomogram had good performance in predicting the OS of CRC patients (Figure S1H).

Correlation analysis of the prognostic signature and immune cell infiltration

The potential molecular mechanisms associated with the prognostic gene signature was elucidated by GSEA (Figure S2). KEGG pathways and hallmark gene sets enriched in the high-risk group included PI3K AKT MTOR SIGNALING (29,30), P53 SIGNALING PATHWAY (31), PATHWAYS IN CANCER, CELL CYCLE, revealing that molecular alteration in the CRC patients with high risk score was closely related to proliferation of tumor cells. Besides, we also found that several immune-related KEGG pathways including IL2 STAT5 SIGNALING, IL6 JAK STAT3 SIGNALING, AUTOIMMUNE THYROID DISEASE, INFLAMMATORY RESPONSE, and TGF BETA SIGNALING were significantly enriched in the high-risk group.

Identification and validation of key genes in CRC and their prognostic and immunological significance

We first confirmed mRNA expression of the 14 candidate genes in MC38-Vector versus MC38-ovCLCA1 tumors by qPCR (Figure S3A). Validation in 24 human CRC specimens by qPCR demonstrated concordant differential expression patterns (Figure 4A, Figure S3B). Specifically, CHGA, CLDN23, CPT2, HPGD, ZER1, HIGD1A, SCGB2A1, GDPD3, B3GNT7, CLCA1, IGSF9, CAPN13, and MUC12 were significantly downregulated, while DUSP14 was upregulated in CRC specimens (all P<0.05). Kaplan-Meier analysis of TCGA-COAD & READ data showed that high CLCA1, HIGD1A, and CPT2 expression significantly correlated with improved overall survival (log-rank P<0.05), whereas other candidates lacked prognostic significance (Figure 4B, Figure S3C).

Protein-level data from CPTAC confirmed significant downregulation of CLCA1, HIGD1A, and CPT2 in CRC tissues (P<0.05; Figure 4C, Figure S3D). IHC from the HPA corroborated CLCA1 downregulation; HIGD1A and CPT2 protein patterns, however, did not mirror mRNA expression (Figure 4D, Figure S4A). ROC analysis confirmed that CLCA1, HIGD1A, and CPT2 each discriminated CRC from normal tissue with high accuracy (AUC >0.90; Figure 4E, Figure S4B). CIBERSORT immune-infiltration analysis revealed significant correlations between CLCA1, HIGD1A, CPT2, and various immune subsets, with CLCA1 showing the strongest associations (Figure 4F, Figure S4C). Collectively, these multi-platform data establish CLCA1 as a robust prognostic biomarker that closely associates with CRC tumor immune-microenvironment dynamics.

Biological function validation of CLCA1

To investigate CLCA1’s biological function in CRC, we generated a stable MC38-ovCLCA1 cell line via lentiviral transduction and confirmed CLCA1 overexpression by qPCR and Western blot (Figure 4G,4H). CLCA1 overexpression significantly inhibited MC38 cell proliferation (CCK-8 assay; n=3) and migration (Transwell assay; n=3) compared with vector controls (Figure S4D,S4E).

To assess CLCA1’s impact on tumor immunity, we established subcutaneous tumors in C57BL/6J mice using MC38-Vector or MC38-ovCLCA1 cells and monitored tumor growth and final weights (Figure 4I). Tumor volumes measured every 3 days revealed that CLCA1 overexpression markedly suppressed tumor growth (n=8; Figure 4J,4K).

Flow cytometry of TILs from MC38-Vector versus MC38-ovCLCA1 tumors revealed significant increases in B220+ B cells, CD4+ T cells, and CD8+ T cells in CLCA1-overexpressing tumors (Figure 4L, Figure S4F). Moreover, effector subsets-CD8+ GZMB+ IFN-γ+ T cells, CD4+ GZMB+ T cells, and CD4+ GZMB+ IFN-γ+ T cells were significantly elevated in MC38-ovCLCA1 tumors (n=4; Figure 4L, Figure S4F).

These data indicate that CLCA1 overexpression promotes immune infiltration and augments cytotoxic T lymphocyte effector function in CRC.

Mechanistic analysis of CLCA1 and tumor immunity

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which CLCA1 modulates the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), we performed RNA sequencing on subcutaneous tumors derived from MC38-Vector and MC38-ovCLCA1 mouse models. Differential expression analysis using stringent criteria (|log2fold change| ≥1, adjusted P<0.05) identified 889 significantly altered genes, including 654 downregulated and 235 upregulated genes in CLCA1-overexpressing tumors (Figure 5A). These results indicate that CLCA1 overexpression induces a broad transcriptional reprogramming within the tumor milieu.

To gain functional insight into the suppressed transcriptional programs, we conducted Gene Ontology (GO) biological process enrichment and KEGG pathway analyses on the downregulated gene set. Strikingly, immune-related processes dominated the enrichment landscape. Among these, “negative regulation of immune system process” (GO:0002683) emerged as the most significantly enriched downregulated term (Figure 5B,5C), alongside pathways associated with leukocyte activation, lymphocyte differentiation, and immune effector regulation. This enrichment pattern strongly suggests that CLCA1 overexpression alleviates intrinsic immune-suppressive mechanisms within the tumor microenvironment (TME).

Consistent with these findings, GSEA further confirmed a significant down-enrichment of immune inhibitory pathways in CLCA1-overexpressing tumors. Specifically, the gene sets corresponding to “negative regulation of immune system process”, “negative regulation of immune response”, and “negative regulation of immune effector process” exhibited robust negative normalized enrichment scores (NES <−1.5, P<0.05; Figure 5D). Together, these results indicate that CLCA1 overexpression shifts the tumor transcriptome away from immune suppression, thereby favoring immune activation.

Identification and functional significance of SERPINB9 as a key CLCA1-regulated immune modulator

To validate the RNA-seq findings, we selected the nine most significantly downregulated IRGs for qPCR verification. These included Serpinb9, Mmp12, Il7r, Ptpn22, Cd274 (Pd-l1), Pdcd1 (Pd-1), Cd55, Ccl2, and Id2. qPCR analysis confirmed significant suppression of all candidates except Pdcd1, with Serpinb9 exhibiting the most pronounced reduction in CLCA1-overexpressing tumors (Figure 5E, Figure S5A).

According to NCBI annotations, murine Serpinb9b is the ortholog of human SERPINB9 (Gene ID: 5272). Therefore, human SERPINB9 nomenclature was adopted for subsequent prognostic and pathway analyses. SERPINB9 is a well-characterized intracellular inhibitor of GZMB, enabling tumor cells to evade cytotoxic killing by CD8+ T cells and NK cells (32). Accumulating evidence indicates that elevated SERPINB9 expression confers resistance to PD-1/Cd274 immune checkpoint blockade, impairs CAR-T cell efficacy, and reduces sensitivity to cytotoxic therapies. Conversely, SERPINB9 depletion enhances GZMB-dependent apoptosis and antitumor immune responses across multiple malignancies (15,33). Moreover, recent studies demonstrate that exosomal SERPINB9 derived from tumor cells can suppress CD8+ T cell function through TP53 UFMylation (34), highlighting its critical role in shaping an immunosuppressive TME.

Prognostic relevance of combined CLCA1 and SERPINB9 expression in CRC

To explore the clinical implications of SERPINB9 dysregulation, survival analyses were performed using the TCGA-COAD and READ cohorts. While SERPINB9 expression alone did not significantly stratify overall survival (P=0.25), combined stratification based on CLCA1 and SERPINB9 expression levels markedly improved prognostic discrimination (Figure 5F, Figure S5B). Patients with high CLCA1 and low SERPINB9 expression exhibited significantly improved survival outcomes compared to other groups (HR =2.52), outperforming either marker alone (CLCA1 HR =0.61; SERPINB9 HR =0.80). These findings underscore the biological and clinical relevance of the CLCA1-SERPINB9 axis in CRC progression.

Cooperative immune activation by CLCA1 and SERPINB9 suppression

To further investigate the immune-related transcriptional programs associated with the CLCA1-SERPINB9 axis, we performed an unbiased co-expression-based GSEA using the BEST platform across CRC RNA-seq datasets. This analysis revealed that CLCA1 and SERPINB9 co-expression patterns were positively correlated with hallmark immune-activating pathways, including Interferon-Gamma Response, TNF-α Signaling via NF-κB, and Interferon-Alpha Response (Figure 5G,5H). These pathways are central to antitumor immunity, cytotoxic lymphocyte activation, and inflammatory signaling.

Collectively, these results suggest that CLCA1 overexpression suppresses SERPINB9, thereby relieving GZMB inhibition and reprogramming the TIME toward a more immune-permissive and cytotoxic state. This cooperative mechanism provides a compelling explanation for the observed enhancement of immune effector functions and improved prognostic stratification in CRC.

Screening of L1000FWD drug candidates

We used the L1000FWD platform to screen for small molecules predicted to upregulate CLCA1 expression. Candidates were ranked by connectivity score, P value, and composite score (Table S3). Phloretin (PubChem CID: 4788) emerged as the top candidate for CRC therapeutic evaluation.

Phloretin (PubChem CID: 4788) is a dihydrochalcone polyphenol with molecular formula C15H14O5 and molar mass 274.27 g/mol (Figure 6A). In conclusion, phloretin is a potential antitumor agent that targets and upregulates CLCA1 in CRC, warranting further preclinical and clinical evaluation.

Discussion

In this study, integrative multi-omics analysis and functional validation identify CLCA1 as an immune-associated tumor suppressor in CRC. Consistent with recent CRC subtype and multi-omics studies, decreased CLCA1 expression is linked to poorer prognosis (35-37). Importantly, CLCA1-high tumors showed enhanced infiltration and activity of cytotoxic immune cells, supporting the concept that CLCA1 reflects a clinically meaningful immune contexture of the TME (38,39).

Mechanistically, we uncovered an inverse association between CLCA1 and SERPINB9, a key intracellular inhibitor of GZMB that can enable tumor cells to evade cytotoxic lymphocyte killing. Prior studies indicate that SERPINB9 amplification/high expression correlates with inferior response to immune checkpoint blockade and limits engineered T-cell cytotoxicity (16,18). In our models, CLCA1 restoration suppressed SERPINB9, suggesting that the CLCA1-SERPINB9 axis may represent an intracellular layer of immune resistance operating downstream of immune-cell recruitment. Because we did not detect a direct physical interaction, the regulation is likely indirect and requires further dissection across human CRC subtypes and treatment contexts.

Therapeutically, we identified phloretin as a CLCA1-interacting compound with evidence of target engagement (CETSA/DARTS) and CLCA1 stabilization. Independent work has reported anti-CRC effects of phloretin, including induction of cell-cycle arrest/apoptosis and inhibition of oncogenic signaling such as β-catenin (40). However, clinical translation is constrained by poor solubility and bioavailability; formulation optimization (e.g., nano-/micellar delivery) may improve exposure and antitumor efficacy (41,42). Future studies should prioritize target-dependency experiments (e.g., CLCA1 loss-of-function rescue) to clarify on-target versus pleiotropic mechanisms.

From a clinical perspective, implementing CLCA1 as a biomarker will require prospective, multi-center validation with standardized detection platforms and robust cutoffs, accounting for CRC heterogeneity and immune-state variability (39). Because immune resistance in microsatellite-stable CRC is multifactorial, CLCA1 alone may be insufficient for patient stratification. Combining CLCA1 with SERPINB9 and immune infiltration metrics may better capture tumor-intrinsic and microenvironmental resistance programs (43,44). Finally, longitudinal monitoring [e.g., circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) dynamics] is increasingly used to track minimal residual disease and treatment response in CRC and may complement CLCA1-linked tissue biomarkers (45-47).

Clinical implications, challenges, and future directions

Despite the promising mechanistic and translational implications of CLCA1 in CRC, several challenges remain regarding its therapeutic targeting and clinical stratification. First, CLCA1 itself may not represent a conventional druggable target. As a secreted and membrane-associated protein primarily involved in epithelial homeostasis, direct pharmacological modulation of CLCA1 may carry risks of off-target effects on normal intestinal physiology. In addition, therapeutic strategies aimed at enhancing CLCA1 activity may be susceptible to adaptive resistance mechanisms, including compensatory activation of parallel immune-evasion pathways or reprogramming of downstream intracellular inhibitors such as SERPINB9.

Second, patient stratification based on CLCA1 expression presents additional complexity. While low CLCA1 expression is associated with immune resistance phenotypes, the optimal threshold for defining CLCA1-high versus CLCA1-low tumors remains undefined and may vary across molecular subtypes, disease stages, and treatment contexts. Intratumoral heterogeneity and temporal fluctuations in CLCA1 expression further complicate static classification based on single biopsy specimens. As a result, reliance on CLCA1 expression alone may be insufficient to accurately predict therapeutic benefit.

Future clinical implementation will likely require integrative stratification strategies combining CLCA1 with complementary biomarkers, such as SERPINB9 expression, immune infiltration metrics, or functional readouts of cytotoxic activity. Prospective studies incorporating longitudinal sampling and adaptive biomarker modeling will be essential to refine patient selection and minimize the risk of misclassification.

Finally, clinical translation of CLCA1-based tools demands careful attention to ethics and equity. Robust informed consent, data-privacy protections, and predefined policies for result disclosure are required, and trials of candidate agents (e.g., phloretin) must ensure diverse, representative enrollment and prospectively assess equity and accessibility outcomes.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study identifies the CLCA1-SERPINB9 axis as a mechanistically relevant determinant of immune resistance in CRC. CLCA1 serves as an immune-related prognostic biomarker and potential therapeutic target, while combined CLCA1/SERPINB9 assessment may improve patient stratification. Although phloretin was identified as a candidate CLCA1-modulating compound, further in vivo validation is required to confirm its target dependency and immunomodulatory activity.
