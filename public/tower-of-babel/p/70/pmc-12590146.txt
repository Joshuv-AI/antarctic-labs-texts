Isolation and characterization of chitinolytic fast-growing bacteria from marine environment in Vietnam
Authors: Thi Van Anh Tran, Thuy Hang Dam, Lan Huong Nguyen, Tuan Le, Thi Kieu Oanh Vu, Thomas Walther, Ha Thanh Le
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Chitin is the second most abundant polysaccharide in nature after cellulose. It is widely distributed among organisms across different kingdoms of life, with an annual production ranging from 1011 to 1014 tons1. Chitin comprises unbranched chains of N-acetyl-D-glucosamine (GlcNAc), existing in three crystalline structures: α, β, and γ. Alpha-chitin is the most prevalent form, present in most crustaceans (shrimps, lobsters, crabs), insects, fungi, and yeast cell walls. Beta-chitin is commonly found in cuttlefish shells, some worms’ tubes, and in the spines of the algae Thalassiosira fluviatilis. Gamma-chitin (found in some fungi and yeasts) has been poorly studied. Chitin is enzymatically degraded by chitinase, in which the α-chitin form is more stable and less susceptible to enzymatic hydrolysis than the β − form. Chitinase is of the glycosidase hydrolase family that cleaves glycoside bonds in chitin polymers. They are classified into two main groups based on their cleavage properties: endochitinase and exochitinase. Endochitinase (EC 3.2.1.14) randomly cleaves the β-1,4 glycosidic bond in the chitin molecule to form oligomers with lower molecular weight, while exo-chitinase cleaves the β-1,4 glycosides bond from the non-reducing end. Exochitinases are classified into two smaller groups based on their hydrolysis products: Chitobiosidase (EC 3.2.1.29) and β-(1, 4)-N-acetyl glucosaminidase (EC 3.2.1.30) cut the non-reducing end in chitin bond to form disaccharide (GlcNAc)2 and monosaccharide GlcNAc, respectively. However, the International Union of Biochemistry and Molecular Biology (IUBMB) classifies both exo-chitinases (EC 3.2.1.29 and EC 3.2.1.30) under the same enzyme nomenclature, β-N-acetylhexosaminidases or hexosaminidase (EC 3.2.1.52)2.

Most of the chitin formed globally is located in aquatic environments. Although this insoluble polymer is constantly generated, oceanic sediments contain only trace amounts of chitin3, indicating that efficient chitin degradation occurs concurrently with the production of this polymer in the marine environment. This massive-scale decomposition process is driven by marine chitinolytic bacteria, which play a crucial role in the nutrient cycle by generating significant amounts of carbon and nitrogen for the food web4. This highlights the potential of exploiting their degradation mechanism for chitin utilization.

The chitin degradation process in a representative V. cholerae of the Vibrio species has been proposed to proceed via several steps5, 6. Firstly, extracellular endochitinase (EC 3.2.1.14) encoded by VC1952, VCA0027 and VC0769 break insoluble chitin into soluble chitooligosaccharides. Subsequently, these chitooligosaccharides are transported into the periplasm and converted to dimers or monomers of N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) by periplasmic chitodextrinase VCA0700 and outer-membrane hexosaminidase VC2217.5 The genes VCA0027 and VC1952 (chiA1) were reported to encode extracellular chitinases, and the deletion of either of the two genes resulted in diminished extracellular chitinase activity.7 VC0769 is predicted endochitinase and was identified by sequence similarity. The VC0769 was upregulated by chitin and the ΔVC0769 strain showed some reduction in growth ability on M9 minimal medium with chitin as a sole carbon source6; its contribution in chitin degradation and extracellular chitinase was however, not yet reported. Chitodextrinase (EC 3.2.1.202) isolated from V. furnissii (homologous to VCA0700) is an endo-cleaving enzyme. Unlike endochitinase, chitodextrinase has no activity on chitin but only on oligosaccharides8. VC2217 in V. cholerae, having homologous genes in V. harvey9 and V. vulniticus10, exhibiting hexosaminidase activity (EC 3.2.1.52). Its role in chitin degradation process to monomer GlcNAc has been tested.9.

Targeting fast-growing chitinolytic bacteria offers the potential to accelerate chitin decomposition and enhance substrate utilization; therefore, the present study was undertaken to isolate and characterize fast-growing chitinolytic bacteria from Vietnamese seawater. The presence or absence of selected chitinase genes was examined by (i) detecting target genes with degenerate primers and (ii) conducting in silico analyses using complete annotations and whole-genome sequencing data from both public databases and our newly sequenced isolates. In parallel, the chitin-degrading abilities of the strains were evaluated by (i) measuring enzyme activity, (ii) monitoring growth on chitin substrates, and (iii) analyzing the composition of chitin hydrolysates. Together, these findings provides deeper insight into the relationship between the genetic potential for chitin degradation and the actual ability to decompose this substrate.

Materials and methods

Colloidal chitin preparation

Crude chitin (purchased commercially in Vietnam) was sieved through a 0.5x0.5 mm sieve after grinding. Each 1 g of chitin was added with 20 ml of chilled 36.5 % HCl and constantly stirred at 4 °C for 6 h. An equal volume of 96 % ethanol (cooled) was added to the colloidal system and refrigerated overnight. Colloidal chitin was washed with distilled water until pH of running washed water was 7, then collected by centrifugation at 6000 rpm for 20 min.

Isolation and screening of fast-growing chitinolytic bacteria

Water samples from coastal waters and crustacean-rich regions in the northern Vietnamese provinces of Nam Dinh, Hai Phong, Thai Binh, Nghe An, and Thanh Hoa were delivered in sealed containers and processed within 24 h. Fifty mililiters of samples were centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 5 min at 10 °C, then re-suspended in 5 mL sterilized 1.5 % NaCl solution before plating on chitin minimal agar plates (containing colloidal chitin 10 g, Na2HPO4 6 g, KH2PO4 3 g, NH4Cl 1 g, NaCl 20 g, yeast extract 0.05 g, MgSO4·7H2O 0.5 g, agar 20 g per litre) (chemicals used were of Samchun, Korea)11. The plates were incubated at 30 °C for 24–72 h and were examined daily. A bacterial colony with a diameter greater than 1 mm was re-streaked on a Luria Broth (LB) medium agar plate containing 1 % colloidal chitin and 1.5 % NaCl to get a pure one, which was subsequently spotted on an M9 agar plate with 1 % colloidal chitin as the sole carbon source for 3 days at 30 °C. The plate was then stained with 0.25 % Congo red working solution for 30 min before being rinsed with 0.9 % NaCl saline until the hydrolysis halos were apparent (Fig. S2). The ratio of D/d, where D is the diameter of the hydrolysis ring and d is the colony diameter, was measured to screen for potential strains semi-quantitatively.

Culture conditions for the production of chitinase

Screened strains were cultured in a chitin minimal medium with components similar to the isolation medium's. A 50 ml medium in a 250 ml flask was inoculated with young seed culture to achieve an initial OD 600 nm value of 0.1 and incubated at 30 °C, 150 rpm for 3–5 days. After incubation, the supernatant was collected by centrifuging the culture broth at 4 °C, 6000 rpm for 10 min, and was used as the crude enzyme.

Chitin hydrolysis by crude enzyme

The hydrolysis reaction consisted of 17 % colloidal chitin (17 g colloidal chitin was obtained from 1 g of crude chitin) dissolved in phosphate buffer (pH 5), and crude enzyme at a 1:1 enzyme-to-substrate ratio, incubated at 40 °C for 24 h. After centrifugation at 6000 rpm for 10 min, the total reducing sugars in the supernatant was measured using the dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) method. Oligomers (GlcNAc)i (i = 1–3) was analyzed by HPLC on the Agilent 1200 infinity system equipped with an Aminex HPX-87H column under the following conditions: 10 mM H2SO4 as mobile phase at 40 °C, flow rate of 0.6 ml/min, sample volume of 0.02 ml, and UV detection at 210 nm using (GlcNAc)i (i = 1–3) from Sigma as standard.

Chitinase assay

Total chitinase activity was measured using the modified Binod method12. The crude enzyme was added to a substrate solution containing 1 % colloidal chitin in sodium acetate buffer (50 mM, pH 5.0) with the ratio 1:1. The mixture was incubated at 40 °C for 30 min following centrifugation at 6000 rpm for 5 min to remove chitin residue. The amount of reducing sugars in the supernatant was determined by the DNS method, using N-acetyl glucosamine (Sigma) as the standard. The Lowry method was used for determining protein concentration. One unit of chitinase activity is defined as the amount of enzyme required to produce 1 μmol of reducing sugars in one minute under the above reaction conditions.

Endochitinase activity was determined using modified Yabuki method13. Incubate 2 ml of enzyme solution with 320 µl of 20 % colloidal chitin and 1680 µl of 0.05 M sodium acetate buffer, pH 5 at 35 °C for 20 min. Measure the optical absorbance of the reaction mixture at 610 nm before and after incubation. The enzyme should be diluted to ensure that the decrease in photoabsorption does not exceed 20 %. One unit of endochitinase is defined as the amount of enzyme required to reduce absorbance at 610 nm by 1 % per minute.

β-N-acetyl-D-hexosaminidase (hexosaminidase) activity was determined following the method proposed by Nguyen14. Enzyme solution (100 µL) was incubated with 100 µL 10 mM p-nitrophenyl N-acetyl-β-D glucosaminidase (pNP-NAG) and 300 µL 0.05 M citrate phosphate buffer pH 6 at 35 °C for 10 min. The reaction was then stopped by adding 500 µL of 1 M Na2CO3 and its optical absorbance of the reaction mixture was measured at 405 nm. One unit of hexosaminidase is defined as the amount of enzyme required to release 1 µmol of p-nitrophenol in one min under the reaction conditions.

Biochemical characterization and phylogenetic analysis of isolates

The genomic DNA of the isolates was extracted using CTAB protocol. The 16S rDNA sequence was amplified by PCR using universal bacterial primers 27F and 1492R with 2X Go Taq PCR Master Mix (Promega). The sequence of 16S rDNA amplified product acquired by Sanger sequencing (Apical Scientific sequencing, Singapore) was edited by BioEdit ver. 7.0 and subsequently blasted on the NCBI database to identify the closest sequences, assuring a similarity of more than 99.5 % and an E-value of zero. The Phylogenetic tree was constructed based on 16S rRNA sequences using Mega X software by neighbor-joining method. The evolutionary distance was computed with 1,000 bootstrap replicates and a cut-off value of 50 %. All representative Vibrionaceae species were selected for comparison with the isolate's 16S rRNA sequences, which had previously been reported to express chitinase activity. The 16S rRNA sequence of Shewanella inventionis was chosen as the outgroup.

The growth rate of selected isolates was assessed during cultivation in 25 mL of LB medium with 2 % NaCl, incubated in a 250 mL flask at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm. Samples were taken every hour, and OD 600 nm was measured. E. coli BL21 was used as the control. The biochemical characteristics of screened isolates were characterized using the API 20E kit following the manufacturer's instructions (Biomerieux, France).

Investigation of the chitin degradation genes

Degenerative primer design for chitinase-related gene detection

Essential chitinase-related genes were specified based on the chitinolytic pathway constructed by Hunt et al. (2008). Degenerate primers for detecting chitinase genes was designed based on sequences from different species in the family Vibrionaceae with compatible functions and similar lengths retrieved from UniProt. Clustal W was used to identify conserved regions, and primers for each gene were selected manually. The primer coverage assessment was determined using Primer Blast tools. Primer parameters such as % GC, melting temperature, self-dimer, and hetero-dimer were investigated using IDT oligo analyzer tools. PCR reaction and in silico test of chitinase gene presence

PCR reactions were carried out twice with 2X GoTaq Mastermix PCR (Promega) using the corresponding synthesized primers for each gene (Table 1). The annealing temperature of several primer pairs was optimized as needed. The PCR product of some isolates was chosen for sequencing. Bioinformatics assessment was conducted by BLAST (blastn/blastp) on the NCBI database using a nucleotide/amino acid sequence extracted from V. cholerae El Tor N16191. The target organism was set to the species corresponding to each isolated strain. Sequences with a query coverage of at least 60 % and a minimum identity of 30 % were considered15.

Genome sequencing and annotation

Genomic DNA was extracted from the overnight cultivated medium using the GeneJET Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) following the standard protocol of the kit. DNA elution was performed on 50 µL sterile TE buffer (pH 8). Complete genome analysis was conducted by Dresden-Concept Genome Center, CMCB Center for Molecular Cell Biology, Technical University Dresden (Germany) as follows: Extracted genomic DNA was quantified using the Qubit dsDNA broad-range assay (Thermo Scientific, Q33265). A 50 ng (1-2ul) aliquot of the purified sample was taken into Library prep using Illumina DNA Tagmentation chemistry (Illumina, Inc., USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The final libraries were quality-assessed using the Agilent FragmentAnalyzer NGS Kit (HS NGS Fragment Kit, DNF-475–0500) and subsequently sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 S4 flowcell with paired-end 100 bp reads to 16.5 million reads per sample. Raw reads were trimmed and filtered using Fastp v0.23.416. Genome assembly was conducted using SPAdes v3.15.517 followed by reference-based scaffolding using ragtag v2.1.018. Gene calling and annotation of strain N5.3′s genome was inferred using Bakta pipeline v1.9.319 and Rapid Annotation using Subsystem Technology20.

Growth of isolates on chitin/N-acetyl-D glucosamine substrates

After overnight preculture on LB containing 2 % NaCl, a young seed of different strains was inoculated into a tube containing mineral medium supplemented with either 1 % crude α- or β-chitin or 10 g/L GlcNAc and was cultured at 30 °C, 150 rpm. OD 600 nm was measured at 0 h, 6 h, and 24 h of cultivation. The DNS method was used to determine the amount of utilized sugar. E. coli BL21 was employed as the control strain.

Reproducibility of results and statistical tests

The presence of the chitinase gene was confirmed by PCR twice. Enzymatic assays, growing on chitin substrate ability, and chitin hydrolysis investigation were performed at least three times.

Results and discussion

Isolation and identification of chitinolytic fast-growing strains

Isolation and identification by 16S rRNA sequencing

Sixty-seven isolates were selected from M9 minimal mineral agar plates containing chitin as the sole carbon source after 24–72 h of incubation. The colonial morphology characteristics, including shape, size, color, surface, and textures, categorized these isolates into four groups (Table S1 and Fig. S1). In this study, daily screening played a crucial role in isolating fast-growing chitinolytic strains. Compared with other reports on isolating chitinolytic strains, such as Achromobacter xylosoxidans21, Stenotrophomona maltophilia22, which required incubation times of approximately 5–7 days, our approach yielded results in a significantly shorter time. Based on distinct colonial morphology and and the capacity to form a hydrolysis ring on mineral-chitin agar (Table S1 and Fig. S2), fourteen strains were selected for further analysis.

The 16S rRNA sequence identification results revealed that all selected strains belonged to the Vibrionaceae family, in which the genus Vibrio accounted for 92.8 % of seawater isolates, only strain 6.3D was of the Photobacterium (Table 2). This finding is consistent with results from previous studies which showed that chitin degradation is the core feature of this group5, and that the majority of chitinolytic microorganisms isolated from seawater belong to the family Vibrionaeceae.22, 23, 24 For instance, more than 92 % of the 88 samples obtained from various water sources in China25 and more than 90 % of the total of 64 colonies isolated from sea turtles in northwestern Mexico26 were Vibrio. Besides, Photobacterium sp. has been isolated from diverse water sources such as P. augustum, P. ganghwense, and P. halotans27. Determination of the presence of selected chitinase genes

Among the 14 isolates, ten strains showed positive PCR results when degenerated primers were used to amplify chitinase-encoding genes, indicating that all five examined chitinase genes were indeed present in the strains albeit not all at the same time. Interestingly, all V. natriegens strains in this study (7.2, 10.3, N5.3) showed negative for VC1952 (chiA1) and VC0769, whereas P. ganghwense 6.3D was negative only for VC1952 (chiA1). Additionally, most strains produced a single, correctly sized product for the examined genes. However, P. ganghwense 6.3D displayed either a differently sized product or multiple bands when primers for VCA0027 or VC2217 were used (Table 3). These results are consistent with the previous observation that these Photobacterium chitinase genes exhibit greater diversity compared to those of Vibrio5, 34. A separate primer pair was specially designed to detect genes from P. profundum, as previously reported5. VCA0700 encoding chitodextrinase was the single gene correctly amplified by PCR for P. ganghwense 6.3 D (Table 3). PCR product’s sequencing results of the representative strains of V. natriegens (strain N5.3, which showed the highest growth rate), V. alginolyticus (strain B01, which exhibited high chitinase activity), V. proteolyticus (strain B02, which had the highest chitinase activity), and P. ganghwense (strain 6.3D, the only strain of this species in the study) (supplementary material) confirmed the successful amplification of the correct target sequences.

Since only PCR products from representative strains were sequenced, an in silico analysis was carried out to support the PCR findings, following the steps described in Section 2.7.2. Species corresponding to the isolates, with complete annotations and whole-genome sequencing data available in the database, were selected for comparison (Table S3). Overall, the in silico results were consistent with the PCR data. V. natriegens ATCC 14048 does not contain any sequence homologous to VC1952 and VC0769. A BLAST search for a similar sequence of VC2217 in the genome of this ATCC 14048 revealed no similar nucleotide sequences, although homologous amino acid sequences were identified (Query cover 96 %, % ID = 43.16 %). The DNA sequence encoding this amino acid chain was more variable than VC2217 homologous found in other Vibrio isolates. (Table S3). Therefore, the reverse primer 17R2 was specifically designed to detect the presence of VC2217 in V. natriegens isolates (Table 1).

On the contrary to the PCR results, in the published genome of strain P. ganghwense C2.2, only the VC1952 did not find any homologous amino acid sequence, while the VCA0027, VC0769 and VC2217 still had one (Table S3), indicating that the genus Photobacterium was more divergent compared to Vibrio.

Strains N5.3, which exhibited the lowest chitinase activity in this study and lacked both examined chitinase genes VC1952 and VC0769, and 6.3D, the only Photobacterium in this study that lacked one examined chitinase gene VC0769, were selected for whole-genome sequencing. The whole-genome sequences of strains N5.3 has been deposited in the database under the accession number JBMOKO000000000. The whole-genome sequences of strains 6.3D has been supplied in supplementary material. Complete genome analysis confirmed previous PCR and in silico findings regarding the presence or absence of the examined genes in V. natriegens N5.3 and P. ganghwense 6.3D. In P. ganghwense 6.3D, four of the examined chitinase genes (excluding VC0027) showed greater variability in both nucleotide (data not shown) and amino acid sequences (Table S3) compared with other Vibrio species. By contrast, in V. natriegens, sequence variation was observed only in the hexosaminidase gene. Whether the absence of VC1952 and VC0769 in V. natriegens strains is associated with the non-pathogenic nature of this species remains unclear.

The association of chitinase genes’ presence with chitin degradation capacity

Chitinase activity

Extracellular endochitinase attacks insoluble chitin, generating soluble oligomers for hexosaminidase to split them further to the GlcNAc monomer. According to Hayes et al., VC0027 plays a dominant role in this process, while VC1572 and VC0769 likely play some role.6 Diminished extracellular chitinase activity by deleting each of VC1952 and VCA0027 was reported by Meibom et al.7 VC1952 and VC0769 are predicted endochitinases; their role in chitin degradation is, however, still unclear. Both genes were observed to be upregulated in the presence of crab chitin7. Deleting one of the two genes resulted in decreased growth of the mutant strain on chitin6. In our study, the endochitinase activity of V. natriegens strains (N5.3, 7.2 and 10.3) (which lacks both VC1952 and VC0769) and P. ganghwense 6.3D (which lacks VC0769) generally was 2–3 times lower than for other Vibrio strains, indicating that both VC1952 and VC0769 contribute to extracellular endochitinase activities (Fig. 2A).Fig. 2Endochitinase (A) and hexosaminidase (B) activities of isolates. In terms of hexosaminidase, P. ganghwense 6.3D showed superior activity, reaching 6.8 U/mg protein, 10–20 times higher than other isolates. The chitinase activity of species belonging to the genus Photobacterium has been mentioned only in a small number of publications.35, 36 Moreover, no study has reported the chitinolytic activity of P. ganghwense. In contrast to P. ganghwense 6.3D, V. natriegens had the lowest hexosaminidase activity, despite having VC 2717. Meanwhile, our whole genome analysis revealed that besides VC2217, the isolates 6.3D and N5.3 possessed two more hexosaminidase genes, VC0613 and VC0692, with relatively high identity compared to that of V. cholerae (Table 4). These two genes are believed to work alongside VC2217 in hydrolyzing oligomers into GlcNAc. Besides these three similar hexosaminidase genes, strain 6.3D harbors one extra hexosaminidase gene PG63DS_15815, to which no similar sequence was found in V. natriegens N5.3 and in V. cholerae (Table 4). This PG63DS_15815 gene encodes a protein with high identity (64.61 %) to VhNAG1 from V. harveyi 650. Growth on chitin as sole carbon source

OD 600 nm measurement has been applied in some studies to evaluate the bacteria’s chitin-degrading ability5, 38. All screened isolates grew on α-, β-chitin, but to varying degrees (Table 3), while the control strain E. coli BL21 (described as not growing on chitin polymer or oligomer39) remains a constant OD 600 nm value (data was not shown). This result is different from a previous report, which showed that V. natriegens ATCC 14048 could not grow on α chitin-containing media and V. parahaemolyticus could not grow on both kinds of chitin5. This difference might be explained by our screening protocol, which selects strains growing on a medium containing chitin as the sole carbon source. In general, most isolated strains could grow equally well on both chitin forms, except for V. alginolyticus MK2-1 and V. parahaemolyticus 10.2 T which grew more poorly on β-chitin. After 24 h, the OD 600 nm of V. natriegens strains (7.2, 10.3, N5.3) and P. ganghwense 6.3D on crude chitin were statistically lower on both chitin forms than other strains (the amount of residual chitin in the culture was significant when observing with the naked eye) (data not shown), which might be attributed to the absence of VC1952 and VC0769 genes (Table 3). This result correlates with the poor endochitinase activities of these strains described above (Fig. 2). V. natriegens strains' capacity to grow on chitin is compatible with previous research showing that two genes, VCA0027 (chiA/chiA2) and VCA0700 (chitodextrinase), are sufficient for V. cholerae to grow on chitin as sole carbon source6.

Growth on GlcNAc

GlcNAc is a chitin building unit. The capacity to utilise this substrate is relatively ubiquitous, even in microbes that cannot digest chitin, such as E. coli40, 41. However, several studies have shown that GlcNAc affects the expression of a wide range of genes, including those involved in chitin breakdown7, 42. Therefore, we set out to determine the strains’ GlcNAc utilisation capacity.

Differing from a previous report, which stated that not all Vibrios bearing chiA and capable of growing on chitin could grow on GlcNAc, all of our examined strains were able to grow on GlcNAc substrates (Fig. 3)43. The screening protocol of fast-growing chitinolytic bacteria could be a good explanation for this result. After 6 h, the OD 600 nm of all strains increased dramatically, including the control strain E. coli BL21 (compared with the initial OD 600 nm value of 0.05). P. ganghwense 6.3D reached the highest OD 600 nm, which was two to five times higher than the other strains, indicating its efficiency in converting GlcNAc into biomass. However, it remains unclear whether its high OD 600 nm is directly related to its elevated hexosaminidase activity. The majority of the strains consumed more than 70 % of the sugar, while the control strain E. coli only consumed roughly 50 % of the 10 g/L GlcNAc. In general, the growth capacity of diverse strains on GlcNAc-containing media (Fig. 3) and the specific growth rate on this substrate (data not shown) did not show a correlation with the presence or absence of chitinase genes. This might be because GlcNAc is a monomer, the end product of the chitin breakdown pathway, while the studied chitinase genes generally encode enzymes that actively operate at the initial stage of this process.

Hydrolysis of chitin by crude enzyme from selected strains

The presence of the chitinase genes was reflected in the hydrolysis of colloidal α-chitin by four strains V. alginolyticus B01, V. proteolyticus B02, V. natriegens N5.3, and P. ganghwense 6.3D (Fig. 4). When observed with the naked eye, the amount of chitin left in tubes containing B01, B02′s enzymes reduced significantly, while the tubes of 6.3D (lacking VC0769) and especially N5.3 (lacking VC1952 and VC0769) still had relatively high amount of substrate (Fig. 4A). The hydrolysate composition of representative strains reflected similar outcomes (Fig. 4B). The hydrolysate of different strains has a relatively consistent amount of 0.55–0.6 mg/mL acetic acid. This component was produced by the N-acetylglucosamine-6-phosphate deacetylase encoded by the nagA gene (which present in all four species through in silico testing) for effective assimilation of GlcNAc5. Thus, although VCA0027 (chiA) was employed as a marker in many studies5, 43, 44, the chiA alone cannot prove the chitin degradation character of a species. Although the contribution of specific gene needs further study, above results have convincingly demonstrated a relationship between the lack of VC1952 and/or VC0769 genes and the low competence to degrade chitin. Therefore chitin-degrading capacity might be simplified to testing for the presence of these chitinase genes.

Conclusions

All 14 isolated chitinolytic fast-growing bacterial strains from seawater in Vietnam belong to the Vibrionaceae family. These strains exhibit chitinase activity and can grow on both crude α- and β-chitin substrates. Their growth rate is significantly higher than that of E. coli which serves as a reference due to its frequent application in the industrial biotechnology.

An isolated group of V. natriegens strains (N5.3, 7.2, and 10.3) was found to lack homologs of VC1952 and VC0769, as determined by degenerate PCR and in silico analysis of the N5.3 whole-genome sequence. These strains exhibited significantly low chitinase, endochitinase, and hexosaminidase activities, grew poorly on both α- and β-chitin, and degraded chitin inefficiently. Meanwhile, P. ganghwense 6.3D strain, which lacked VC0769, displayed low endochitinase activity, poor growth on both chitin forms, and reduced chitin degradation efficiency, despite exhibiting superior hexosaminidase activity. These findings suggest that VC1952 and VC0769 play a crutial role in endochitinase activity and are essential for efficient chitin degradation.
