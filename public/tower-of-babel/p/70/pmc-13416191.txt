Effects of density and kinship on the spatial organization of an asocial ground squirrel
Authors: Jaclyn R Aliperti, Caitlin P Wells, Dirk H Van Vuren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmong mammals, spatial organization (home range size and overlap) may be influenced by kinship, and tolerance among kin is believed to play a role in the evolution of sociality. In solitary species, adults—including close kin—are presumed to have home ranges that are distinct and non-overlapping. We characterized spatial organization and behavioral interactions, including the role of kinship in a free-living population of golden-mantled ground squirrels (Callospermophilus lateralis), a presumably asocial and solitary species. We also capitalized on changes in population size over 25 years (1995 to 2019) to quantify the influence of density on that spatial organization. We found extensive overlap between female home ranges (30%) and between male and female home ranges (28%) after the breeding season. However, the core areas of adult female home ranges overlapped minimally (7%), suggesting that squirrels were territorial, but only for the inner part of their home range. Contrary to expectation for solitary ground squirrels, kinship played a role in the spatial organization: related females shared space more than unrelated females. We did not find an effect of overall population density on home range or core area size, but increased local density was associated with an increase in home range size. While we did not find an effect of density alone on spatial overlap, there was a density-mediated increase in space-sharing among kin; space-sharing by female kin was expressed primarily at high densities, suggesting that familiarity and inclusive fitness benefits may offset costs of competition and promote philopatry under certain conditions. We also found behavioral evidence to support this interaction: greater spatial overlap of home range core areas reliably increased the number of chases between squirrels, unless those squirrels were highly related. Overall, we provide evidence of a facultative transition in golden-mantled ground squirrels from asociality to the formation of single-family kin clusters at high densities, the first step toward developing sociality. Our study highlights the importance of considering behavioral interactions in asocial species, as well as the interactive effects of environmental conditions and kinship when assessing the spatial and social organization of mammals.

Understanding patterns of spatial organization in animal populations requires information on how individuals distribute themselves across a landscape (Michener 1979; Rayor and Armitage 1991; Boellstorff and Owings 1995; Jones et al. 2013). Spatial distribution is influenced by dispersal, and in mammals, dispersal is typically male-biased, with females often remaining philopatric (Greenwood 1980; Waser and Jones 1983). Natal philopatry creates the potential for related females to be clustered spatially, and tolerance among kin is believed to play a role in the evolution of sociality (Armitage 1999; Silk 2007; Innes et al. 2012).

Ground-dwelling squirrels exhibit a wide range of sociality, from solitary to highly gregarious, which Armitage (1981) hypothesized to have resulted from the effect of body size on the rate of development, hence the age of dispersal. In the short active season experienced by most ground-dwelling squirrels, large-bodied species have delayed maturity and dispersal, facilitating the formation of social groups. Small species, or those exposed to a long active season, reach maturity and disperse early in life, precluding the formation of social groups (Armitage 1981). Hence, Armitage (1981) classified ground-dwelling squirrels into 5 grades of social structure, ranging from solitary (Grade 1) to highly social (Grade 5). Michener (1983) further developed this scheme, emphasizing the importance of spatial overlap and association among kin in forming social bonds and thus shaping social structure—predicting that home ranges should be distinct and non-overlapping among individuals of asocial species, and overlap should increase as social complexity progresses to single-family kin clusters and—in the most social species—cooperative, multifamily polygynous harems. Overall, social tolerance is expected to be kin-directed (Hare and Murie 2007).

Spatial distribution for individuals that have established residency might be influenced by density (Oli et al. 2002; Innes et al. 2009). For example, an animal may either expand or contract its home range in response to changes in proximity to conspecifics or perceived availability of resources (Cooper 1978; Dahle and Swenson 2003; Kiefer and Weckerly 2005). In territorial species, for which part or all of the home range is aggressively defended, increased intruder pressure is presumed to drive an inverse relationship between density and the defended area, along with increased aggression (Temeles 1987; Keeley and McPhail 1998; Morrell and Kokko 2005). Hence, as density increases, individuals might respond in either of 2 ways: they might maintain their home range as a non-overlapping territory but reduce its size; or they might maintain the same home range size but reduce the defended area, resulting in increased spatial overlap (Stamps and Buechner 1985; Adams 2001). Optimal territory size can be considered as the outcome of costs (e.g., time, energy, and risk associated with fighting intruders) and benefits (e.g., exclusive access to resources) of territory defense within an economic framework (Schoener 1983, 1987; Morrell and Kokko 2005; Wronski and Plath 2006).

Density may affect the expression of sociality as well; there is increasing evidence that social organization varies according to circumstance (Lott 1991; Wolff 1993; Randall et al. 2005; McEachern et al. 2007). For example, the normally solitary Giant Kangaroo Rat (Dipodomys ingens) formed kin-based groups of interacting females in response to increased density (Meshriy et al. 2011), and both white-footed mice (Peromyscus leucopus) and deer mice (P. maniculatus) switched from solitary to communal nesting as a result of limited space and delayed dispersal (Wolff 1994). This pattern was also observed in ground-dwelling squirrels; California ground squirrels (Otospermophilus beecheyi), which are characterized as relatively asocial (Grade 2; Armitage 1981), form aggregations called colonies at high density (Van Vuren and Ordeñana 2012); and woodchucks (Marmota monax), considered asocial (Grade 1) transition from territoriality at low density to interacting with neighbors and forming simple familial groups when density increases (Ferron and Ouellet 1989; Maher 2006, 2009). Hence, density may influence the spatial organization of asocial mammals, and when population density is high, territoriality may break down (Wolff 1993; Morrell and Kokko 2005). Few studies have examined density-mediated space use in asocial species, perhaps due to the difficulty of measuring kinship and space use in solitary animals (Ratnayeke et al. 2002) or because investigations on less gregarious species generate relatively little interest (Michener 1983; Ferron 1985; Jesmer et al. 2011). However, understanding intraspecific variation in solitary living is essential to understanding the evolution of social complexity (Makuya et al. 2024).

Golden-mantled ground squirrels (Callospermophilus lateralis; hereafter GMGS) are a small-bodied species (150 to 300 g) that is considered asocial (Grade 1) and presumably territorial (Armitage 1981; Michener 1983). However, this classification was supported by sparse data (Michener 1983); subsequent research has shown that adult GMGS are primarily agonistic toward conspecifics (Ferron 1985)—which is expected of an asocial species—and that they are potentially territorial, although only for a portion of the home range (Jesmer et al. 2011). The role of kinship in the spatial organization of GMGS is unknown; however, related females sometimes live in close proximity, with important implications for the mating system, offspring sex ratios, age of first reproduction, and survival (Wells and Van Vuren 2017, 2018; Wells et al. 2017; Kanaziz et al. 2022).

Our objectives were to: (i) characterize the spatial organization (home range size and overlap) and behavioral interactions of a free-living population of GMGS, including the role of kinship; (ii) capitalize on changes in population size over the 25-year duration of the study to evaluate the influence of density on spatial organization; and (iii) evaluate the influence of density, spatial overlap, and relatedness on behavior. If GMGS are strictly solitary, we expected to find non-overlapping home ranges among both adult females and adult males, including close kin (Michener 1983). We hypothesized that with increased density, individual squirrels would either reduce home range size to maintain the same home range overlap, concomitant with an increase in chases resulting from home range defense, or they would maintain home range size and allow higher overlap (Stamps and Buechner 1985; Adams 2001), with a concomitant increase in social tolerance. We expected that with increased relatedness, individual squirrels would be more likely to tolerate, and less likely to chase, neighboring conspecifics (Hare and Murie 2007; Dobson et al. 2012), and that these 2 behaviors would be negatively correlated. Last, we expected that patterns would be stronger for overlap of core home ranges—areas where squirrels spend most of their time (Powell and Mitchell 2012)—than for overall home range overlap.

Methods

Study system

GMGS are found in the western United States and Canada (McKeever 1964; Bartels and Thompson 1993), where they occur in a range of mid- to high-elevation montane habitats including open conifer forest, sparse brush, and rocky slopes adjoining grasslands (McKeever 1964; Bartels and Thompson 1993; Shick et al. 2006; McEachern et al. 2011). We studied a population of GMGS for 25 years (1995 to 2019) at the Rocky Mountain Biological Laboratory (RMBL), located in the East River Valley, Gunnison County, Colorado (38°57′ N, 106°59′ W). The 16 ha study area was bordered by perennial streams on the west and south and by aspen forest on the north and east. Habitats included primarily dry meadow, interspersed with patches of wet meadow, groves of aspen (Populus tremuloides) or spruce (Picea engelmannii), and stands of willow (Salix spp.). GMGS occurred in all habitats but preferred only dry meadow habitat (Aliperti et al. 2022), which was structurally open and provided forbs such as dandelions (Taraxacum officinale) for food. Further, dry meadow habitat was interspersed with “perches,” raised prominences such as rocks that squirrels frequently used and that were associated with higher survival, presumably because perches facilitated detection of predators (Aliperti et al. 2022). Predation by red foxes (Vulpes vulpes), long-tailed weasels (Mustela frenata), and short-tailed weasels (Mustela erminea) was a significant cause of GMGS mortality at RMBL (Kneip et al. 2011).

Due to the high elevation (2900 m) of our study area, GMGS typically hibernate from late August until snow melt during late April or May (Kneip et al. 2011; Hostetler et al. 2012; Howland et al. 2024), a hibernation period up to twice as long as those exhibited by GMGS populations at lower elevations (McKeever 1964; Bronson 1979). Upon emerging from hibernation, adult (≥1 year) females breed, then give birth underground approximately 28 days later to 1 litter of pups each year (x̄ = 4.8, range = 1 to 8; Kneip et al. 2011; Wells and Van Vuren 2017). GMGS exhibit a polygynandrous mating system, and multiple paternity is common and can result in varying degrees of relatedness among pups within litters (Wells et al. 2017). Females nurse pups for approximately 30 days before juveniles emerge from their natal burrow and forage on plants, especially forbs (Phillips 1981). Juveniles usually disperse before their first overwinter hibernation, though some delay dispersal until early the following summer, and others remain philopatric (Jesmer et al. 2011; Wells and Van Vuren 2017; Nguyen et al. 2024). Immigration of GMGS from surrounding populations into our study area is common (McEachern et al. 2011; Nguyen et al. 2024).

Field methods

We studied GMGS from early June through late August each year during their summer active season. We trapped squirrels using single-door box traps (Model 201, Tomahawk Live Trap, Hazelhurst, Wisconsin) that were baited with a mixture of sunflower seeds and peanut butter. All adults were trapped during early June as part of an annual census, and pups were trapped within 2 days of emergence from their natal burrows, usually during late June or early July. We recorded sex, body mass, and reproductive status of each trapped squirrel (Wells and Van Vuren 2018). Squirrels were identified permanently using numbered tags in each ear, and they were marked for visual identification from a distance using a black, nontoxic dye mark on the dorsal pelage (Nyanzol D dye, Greenville Colorants, Greenville, South Carolina, United States). We re-trapped squirrels to renew marks as needed.

Locations of individual squirrels were determined visually using binoculars, and squirrels were identified based on unique dye marks. Squirrels in our study area were readily observable because they were diurnal, preferred open habitats with sparse vegetation, and often sat on perches (Aliperti et al. 2022). Only a part of our study area could be viewed from any given location, so we rotated through each part of the study area in a randomized order each day. We searched for each squirrel at least once each morning and afternoon to account for possible temporal variation in space use. When at least 1 squirrel was in view and identified, we employed an instantaneous scan sampling approach (Aliperti et al. 2021, 2022), in which we recorded the location of all individuals at 1-min intervals until squirrels in view left the area or disappeared underground. A given squirrel was typically in view for less than 10 min. Locations of squirrels were based on a map of the study area with 7 × 7-m gridded cells. Because some squirrels were more readily observed than others, we redirected observation efforts toward underrepresented squirrels when appropriate. We obtained 112.44 ± 5.44 (mean ± SE) locations for females (range = 31 to 361) and 94.88 ± 8.07 locations for males (range = 30 to 461).

Interactions between adult squirrels were described and recorded whenever they were observed during instantaneous scan sampling. We used the ethogram of Ferron (1985) to classify interactions. Most interactions described by Ferron (1985) were agonistic and included chases, boxing (fighting in an upright position), and wrestling (grappling in a prone position). Some interactions were recognitive and included nose-to-nose, nose-to-body, and nose-to-anogenital smelling. Cohesive behaviors included pseudocopulation, cohesive chase (in essence, a slow-motion chase), rough-and-tumble (mild disorderly fighting), social grooming, and touching (Ferron 1985). In addition, we identified 2 interactions not described by Ferron (1985). “Tolerance” occurred when 2 squirrels remained ≤2 m apart for ≥20 s, in view of each other and without expressing any other identifiable interaction. A “displacement” occurred when a squirrel vacated a perch as another squirrel approached, whereupon the approaching squirrel occupied the just-vacated perch. Displacement resembled the “avoidance” interaction described by Ferron (1985), except that in our study the interaction involved a specific resource, a perch. Displacement was considered agonistic (Owings et al. 1977; Maher 2006). Squirrels in our study area often encountered humans and seldom reacted to our presence from the observation distances we employed; if a squirrel did react, those observations were omitted. Location data were aggregated for each individual each year. All procedures met guidelines approved by the American Society of Mammalogists (Sikes et al. 2016) and followed protocols approved by the institutional animal care and use committees of the University of California, Davis, and RMBL.

Spatial organization

Squirrel locations were registered into a spatially explicit database of our study area in ArcGIS v10 (ESRI 2018), then imported into R (version 3.6.1; R Core Team 2019). We used the package “adehabitatHR” (version 0.4.16; Calenge 2006) to delineate annual home ranges for each squirrel as calculated through 95% and 50% minimum convex polygons (MCP) and kernel density estimation (KDE). For KDE home ranges, we used a fixed kernel with a reference smoothing bandwidth, href, which decreases bias when analyzing the within-home range utilization distribution by determining kernel width based on proximity among relocations (Worton 1989; Kie 2013; Maguire and Rowe 2017; Tisell et al. 2019). Because our data set included locations based on fine-scale visual observations, and because KDE outputs vary considerably based on choice of smoothing parameter and sample size (Laver and Kelly 2008; Kie 2013), we considered MCP a more conservative and appropriate method for our study; however, we characterized spatial organization using both methods (following Wauters et al. 2007). The 95% home range identifies the overall home range after outlying locations (5%) are excluded, and the 50% home range—also known as the core area—identifies areas of concentrated use within the home range (Samuel et al. 1985; Powell and Mitchell 2012).

Though squirrels are able to traverse multiple grid cells quickly, individuals sometimes remained in the same grid cell for durations of several minutes, resulting in spatial autocorrelation. To reduce spatial autocorrelation, we included only the first observation when an individual was recorded in the same location more than once within a 10-min period (Aliperti et al. 2021, 2022). We then filtered the autocorrelation-corrected dataset to include only those individuals with at least 30 locations (for MCP) or 50 locations (for KDE) and that were present for at least a 30-day period within a given year; the 30-day criterion excluded squirrels that dispersed or died early in the active season. Our minimum numbers of observations per individual were in line with those used to describe squirrel home ranges in other studies (e.g., Wauters et al. 2001; Romeo et al. 2010) and were in accordance with the recommended minimum number of observations for KDE (Seaman et al. 1999); nonetheless, we also conducted a post-hoc analysis to test whether calculations of home range size were biased by the number of locations recorded for each squirrel (Sanchez and Hudgens 2015; Maguire and Rowe 2017).

Sample sizes for home range delineation via the MCP and KDE methods were similar (Table 1). We calculated core area size for all squirrel-year pairings except for 1 female whose core area was too small to delineate by the MCP method, and we computed mean MCP core area overlap for all dyads that were used for home range overlap, other than the 3 dyads (2 female–female, 1 female–male) corresponding to the female whose core area could not be delineated (Table 1). In addition, we calculated mean core area overlap for those dyads that showed any core area overlap. We used Minta’s index (Minta 1992, 1993) to calculate mean spatial overlap of 95% home ranges and 50% core areas between all possible pairings of adult squirrels (female–female, female–male, and male–male dyads) each year:

Thus, mean overlap values were calculated as a proportion ranging from 0 (no overlap) to 1 (perfect overlap). We summarized overlap for all squirrel dyads with home range overlap >0; we excluded non-overlapping dyads because the squirrels comprising most of those dyads resided in separate parts of the study area and thus would not have had the opportunity to interact. We also counted the number of female home ranges and core areas overlapped by the average male, and the number of male home ranges and core areas overlapped by the average female. Overlaps between males and females reflected spatial relations after the breeding season, which in our study area ended by late May (Wells et al. 2017).

We calculated coefficients of relatedness for each pair of overlapping adult females based on matrilineal relationships (Wells et al. 2017). Maternity of a litter was provisionally assigned when the litter emerged from the burrow of a female who was considered reproductive based on nipple development; we searched the home ranges of all reproductive females multiple times per day during June and July (Wells and Van Vuren 2017). Maternity was verified within the first 2 days of emergence of a litter, when mothers were observed engaging in affiliative interactions such as nose-to-nose greets with their pups (Ferron 1985), and were usually trapped with members of their litter (Wells and Van Vuren 2017); maternity was confirmed with genetic data for litters born from 1995 to 2015 (Wells et al. 2017). We assumed that unmarked females that immigrated into the study area were unrelated to resident females. We calculated mean overlap of 95% home ranges for all overlapping female dyads according to 4 relatedness categories: unrelated (belonging to different matrilines); distantly related (belonging to the same matriline but not first-order relatives, e.g., aunt–niece, grandmother–granddaughter, cousins, etc.); littermate sisters (including full or half-siblings); and mother–daughter pairs. Because sharing 50% core areas might reflect a high level of tolerance, we also calculated core area overlap for those dyads that overlapped core areas, according to the 4 relatedness categories.

Effects of density on spatial organization

We used the MCP method for the calculation of home ranges and core areas when describing overlap based on kinship and when modeling the effects of density. We considered population density as the total number of adult females present in the study area for at least a 30-day period each year; as males do not defend territories and move into and out of the study area throughout the summer, they were not included in this number. However, because adult females were distributed heterogeneously across our study area, with several discrete localities of female activity centered on areas of dry meadow habitat, population density might not reflect the density realized by a given female due to local spatial clustering (Einum and Nislow 2005; Wells and Van Vuren 2017). Hence, we also calculated the number of neighboring adult female home ranges with which the home range of a given female overlapped each year, and we used this measure as a proxy for the intruder pressure, or “local density,” experienced by each female (Efford 1996; Sanchez and Hudgens 2015; Woodworth et al. 2017). During the 25 yr duration of the study, population density of adult females ranged 3 to 24, and local density ranged 0 to 15.

Dyad relatedness values were calculated as a continuous variable from matriline pedigrees and ranged from 0 (nonkin) to 0.5 (mother–daughter). Pedigrees were validated with genetic data available for most individuals from 1995 to 2015 but not for individuals born from 2016 to 2019. Because we wanted to include as many individual females as possible for this analysis from 1995 to 2019, we opted to use the estimated relatedness values from the pedigree—available for all years, versus relatedness coefficients calculated directly from genotypes in a subset of years—for consistency. Because multiple paternity occurs in 62% of GMGS litters in our study area (Wells et al. 2017), littermate sisters could be full siblings (r = 0.50) or half siblings (r = 0.25). To account for this, we averaged the two expected relatedness values and assigned r = 0.375 to all littermate sisters. Because it is rare for females to mate with the same male across years (Wells et al. 2017), we assumed that non-littermate sisters (i.e., those born to the same mother in different years) were half-sisters, with a relatedness value of 0.25. Other examples of dyad relatedness included grandmother–granddaughter dyads (r = ∼0.25), aunt–niece via littermate sister dyads (r = ∼0.1875), aunt–niece via non-littermate sister dyads (r = ∼0.125), and cousins via non-littermate sisters (r = ∼0.0625). When we instead treated kinship categories as an ordinal variable, results of the following models were qualitatively similar but more difficult to interpret beyond the first linear term; hence, we present results from models with kinship as a continuous variable.

We assessed the influence of density on spatial organization by fitting a series of generalized linear mixed effects models (GLMMs). First, we fit 4 separate log-linear GLMMs with area (95% home range or 50% core area) as our outcome variable using a Gaussian error distribution. Reproductive status (whether or not a female was reproductive each year) was included as a fixed effect because the movement patterns of reproductive females might be influenced by greater energetic requirements or limited mobility during lactation, when females tend to pups underground (Kenagy et al. 1990; Edwards et al. 2013). Because population-level density and local-level density were positively correlated (Pearson’s correlation test, r = 0.73, df = 161, P < 0.0001), we fit separate models with each density measure as a fixed effect, hence fitting 2 for 95% home range and 2 for 50% core area. To account for repeated measures or the possibility of interannual environmental differences, we included individual and year as random effects. To quantify the effects of density on home range overlap, we used only female dyads with overlap >0 and fit 4 GLMMs with a beta distribution and logit link: 2 as a function of population density (on 95% home range overlap and 50% core area overlap, respectively) and 2 as a function of local density (on 95% home range overlap and 50% core area overlap, respectively; see Methods section above). For the population density models, we included population density, kinship, and an interaction between density and kinship as fixed effects; we included the interaction because relatedness might influence overlap only during years of high density (Maher 2009; Meshriy et al. 2011). For the local density models, because local density was measured per individual rather than per dyad, we modeled spatial overlap as a function of the mean local density experienced by the 2 females in each dyad (following Sanchez and Hudgens 2015), in addition to kinship and an interaction between mean local density and kinship. To account for repeated measures of dyads and individuals across dyads or the possibility of interannual environmental differences, we included random effects of each interacting individual, dyad, and year as a random effect in all spatial overlap models. We ran models using R packages “lme4” (Bates et al. 2015) and “glmmTMB” (Brooks et al. 2017).

Effects of density, relatedness, and spatial overlap on adult female behavior

We tallied interactions between squirrels (i.e., at the dyad level) across years to characterize social interactions. We calculated interaction rates by dividing the number of interactions each year by sampling effort, which we determined by summing the hours of observations each year. Of the 201 interactions that we recorded between adult females in which both individuals were identified, we had adequate numbers of chases (n = 134) and tolerances (n = 54) for analysis, but not displacements (n = 13); hence, displacements were dropped from further analysis. We calculated the Pearson correlation coefficient to determine if interaction rates among adult females were associated with changes in density. Next, we tested the effect of spatial overlap on behavioral interactions, while controlling for kinship. Kinship was calculated as pairwise relatedness for each dyad as described above; across years, 117 dyads were related (r > 0.0) and 533 were unrelated (r = 0.0). As both the chase and tolerance data included many zeros (i.e., the majority of dyads were not observed interacting) and were overdispersed (including zeros, mean chases per dyad = 0.2; sd2 = 0.5), we constructed zero-inflated Negative Binomial models with variance = φμ in “glmmTMB” (Brooks et al. 2017). For the chase models, we fit the number of dyad chases as a function of dyad spatial overlap (e.g., 95% home range), kinship (i.e., matriline r), an interaction between spatial overlap and kinship, and random effects for the identity of member 1 of the dyad and member 2 of the dyad; the zero-inflated portion of the model included an intercept only. Ideally, we would have been able to include crossed random effects for each individual, dyad, and year (as for the spatial overlap models above), but models with this complex structure would not converge; however, estimates of fixed effects were similar in direction and magnitude across models with inclusion of different subsets of random effects. We included an offset term to account for variation in log-transformed sampling effort, which we estimated at the dyad level as the sum of the number of minutes each individual in a dyad was observed that year; with the inclusion of this time, the outcome variable is interpreted as a rate of chases. We fit tolerance models similarly, with the number of dyad tolerances predicted by spatial overlap, kinship, their interaction, and random effects for the identity of member 1 of the dyad and member 2 of the dyad. We ran each model (chase, tolerance) first with the 95% MCP spatial overlap, and then again with the 50% MCP spatial overlap to determine if patterns of dyad behavioral interactions were similar for overall home range versus core area overlap.

Results

Spatial organization

There was no significant effect of the total number of observations recorded for each squirrel on either overall MCP home range size (β ± SE = 0.002 ± 0.001, t = 1.83, P = 0.070) or MCP core area size (β ± SE = 0.001 ± 0.001, t = 0.93, P = 0.354) after accounting for the random effects of individual and year. There was an effect of the total number of observations on KDE home range size and core area size, though the effect was negative, and the regression coefficient was close to zero (home range β ± SE = −0.002 ± 0.001, t = −2.18, P = 0.031; core area β ± SE = −0.003 ± 0.001, t = −2.66, P = 0.009). While home ranges that were calculated by KDE were over twice as large as those calculated by MCP, both estimates indicated that home ranges of males were 4 times as large as those of females (Table 2). Furthermore, estimates for mean spatial overlap were similar between the 2 methods (Table 2, Fig. 1). Using MCP home ranges, we found that the average adult female GMGS had a home range that overlapped with 2.7 ± 0.18 (mean ± SE) other females (range 0 to 12) and 3.1 ± 0.14 males (range 0 to 8), while the average adult male had a home range that overlapped with 4.4 ± 0.39 females (range 0 to 15) and 3.1 ± 1.79 other males (range 1 to 8). The average adult female maintained a 50% core area that overlapped with 0.6 ± 0.06 other females (range 0 to 3) and 1.1 ± 0.08 males (range 0 to 4), while the average adult male maintained a core area that overlapped with 1.5 ± 0.15 females (range 0 to 5) and 0.8 ± 0.12 other males (range 0 to 4). These values are an underestimate because, in some cases, additional males and females were present but not considered because of inadequate sample size or less than 30-day duration.

Among female dyads that shared home ranges, a pattern of increased home range overlap with increased relatedness was evident, with close kin (sister–sister and mother–daughter dyads) showing almost twice the degree of overlap as did dyads of unrelated females (Fig. 2). For those dyads that shared core areas, a similar pattern of greater sharing among kin was evident, with 1 exception: littermate sister dyads showed less core-area sharing than any other kinship category, including non-relatives (Fig. 2).

Effects of density on spatial organization

We did not find an effect of population density on either home range size (β ± SE = −0.001 ± 0.016, t = −0.07, P = 0.946) or core area size (β ± SE = −0.017 ± 0.020, t = −0.83, P = 0.426). However, we found a significant, positive effect of local density on home range size (β ± SE = 0.108 ± 0.034, t = 3.22, P = 0.002; Fig. 3). Local density did not have a significant effect on core area size, though it had a positive effect that approached statistical significance (β ± SE = 0.086 ± 0.045, t = 1.93, P = 0.057). We did not find an effect of population density alone (β ± SE = −0.029 ± 0.019, z = −1.53, P = 0.126) on home range overlap. However, we found a significant, positive effect of an interaction between population density and kinship on home range overlap (β ± SE = 0.181 ± 0.061, z = 2.98, P = 0.003), such that more closely-related females showed greater spatial overlap as population density increased (Fig. 4A). Similarly, we did not find an effect of local density alone (β ± SE = −0.033 ± 0.051, z = −0.64, P = 0.520) on home range overlap, but we found a significant, positive effect of an interaction between local density and kinship on home range overlap (β ± SE = 0.567 ± 0.168, z = 3.37, P = 0.0007; Fig. 4B). There was no effect of population density (β ± SE = −0.004 ± 0.041, z = −0.11, P = 0.914) or local density (β ± SE = −0.078 ± 0.118, z = −0.66, P = 0.510) on core area overlap, and there were no significant interactions between either population density and kinship (β ± SE = 0.065 ± 0.138, z = 0.47, P = 0.637) or local density and kinship (β ± SE = 0.474 ± 0.430, z = 1.10, P = 0.271) on core area overlap. After accounting for interactions between density and kinship, there was no main effect of kinship on home range overlap or core area overlap in the models for either population density (home range: β ± SE = −0.222 ± 0.991, z = −0.22, P = 0.823; core area: β ± SE = 0.098 ± 1.965, z = 0.05, P = 0.960) or local density (home range: β ± SE = −0.128 ± 0.864, z = −0.15, P = 0.882; core area: β ± SE = −0.935 ± 1.913, z = −0.49, P = 0.625).

Effects of density, relatedness, and spatial overlap on adult female behavior

Behavioral interactions were infrequent, with an overall rate of 1.0 interaction per hour of observation for all adults. Most of the 893 interactions that we observed between adult squirrels were chases (73%); aggressive body contact during chases (“wrestling”; Ferron 1985) was rare, occurring in only 4% of chases. Tolerances totaled 15% of interactions, and displacements 6%. The remaining 6% of interactions included recognitive behaviors such as nose-to-nose (n = 18) and nose-to-body or to anogenital region (5); cohesive behaviors such as pseudocopulation (5), cohesive chase (5), rough and tumble (4), social grooming (3), and touching (2); and agonistic behaviors such as wrestling (3) and boxing (3). We were able to identify both actors in 652 interactions that involved chases (n = 472), displacements (n = 54), or tolerances (n = 126). Females were the initiators in 81% of chases, with 42% of recipients being other females and 58% being males; males initiated only 19% of chases (54% female recipients, 46% male recipients). Displacements were similarly sex-biased, with 61% initiated by females (64% female recipients, 36% male recipients) and 39% initiated by males (52% female recipients, 48% male recipients). Most tolerances involved female–female (54%) or female–male pairs (44%); tolerance between males was rare (2%).

The number of chases observed per adult female dyad ranged from 0 to 8 per year, and the number of tolerance interactions observed per adult female dyad ranged from 0 to 5. Correlation analysis revealed that the number of adult female dyad chases per year increased significantly with population density (r = 0.640, P = 0.001), but instances of females tolerating other females did not (r = 0.281, P = 0.173).

At the level of the entire home range (95% MCP), greater spatial overlap (β = 0.078 ± 0.013, z = 5.95, P < 0.001) and relatedness (β = 6.25 ± 2.24, z = 2.80, P = 0.005) significantly increased the rate of tolerance interactions observed. The interaction between spatial overlap and relatedness was negative (β = −0.08 ± 0.04, z = −1.83, P = 0.068), suggesting that tolerance increased with spatial overlap but less so for highly-related dyads. At the level of the core area (50% MCP), relatedness had a stronger positive effect (β = 8.30 ± 1.52, z = 5.46, P < 0.001), and spatial overlap had a slightly weaker but still positive effect (β = 0.05 ± 0.02, z = 2.41, P = 0.016) on tolerance; the interaction between spatial overlap and relatedness was still negative (β = −0.11 ± 0.060, z = −1.93, P = 0.054).

Similar to the tolerance models, at the level of the entire home range (95% MCP), greater spatial overlap significantly increased the rate of dyad chases observed (β = 0.060 ± 0.007, z = 8.31, P < 0.001). Contrary to expectation, relatedness also increased the rate of chases observed, but only marginally (β = 2.49 ± 1.38, z = 1.80, P = 0.072). The interaction between spatial overlap and relatedness was negative—chases increased with overlap, though less so for highly-related dyads—but not significantly so (β = −0.044 ± 0.030, z = −1.50, P = 0.133). However, at the level of the core area (50% MCP), this interaction was reliably negative (β = −0.090 ± 0.034, z = −2.66, P = 0.008; Fig. 5).

Discussion

GMGSs are a relatively small-bodied species, which enables individuals to reach adulthood upon emerging from their first hibernation bout, and ostensibly precludes the formation of long-term social bonds among conspecifics (Armitage 1981). Hence, GMGS are presumed to be asocial and solitary, characterized by nonoverlapping adult home ranges (Armitage 1981; Michener 1983). Contrary to this expectation, we found extensive overlap between female home ranges and between male and female home ranges (Table 2; Fig. 1). Moreover, the home range of a given squirrel overlapped with several members of the same and opposite sex. However, our home range overlap (30%) was consistent with overlap values reported for solitary mammals, although it was toward the upper edge of the range (5% to 37%; Oli et al. 2002; Burton and Krebs 2003; Kitchen et al. 2005; Støen et al. 2005; Innes et al. 2009; Jones et al. 2013; Rodgers et al. 2015; Tisell et al. 2019; Aronsson et al. 2020). Our results are also consistent with those for other asocial (Grade 1) squirrels. Woodchucks (M. monax) showed home range overlap within and between sexes that ranged ca. 5% to 20%, along with some degree of territoriality (Meier 1992; Swihart 1992; Maher 2009), and Franklin’s Ground Squirrel (Poliocitellus franklinii) also showed home range overlap both within and between sexes (Choromanski-Norris et al. 1989). Interestingly, some species classified as Grade 2 show a similar pattern as we found for GMGS. For Richardson’s ground squirrels (Urocitellus richardsonii; Michener 1979), adult females showed extensive home range overlap (35%), but minimal core area overlap (3%), and a similar pattern was evident for California ground squirrels (O. beecheyi; Boellstorff and Owings 1995). Perhaps the distinction between Grades 1 and 2 lies more with interactions and kinship than with spatial dispersion. We found that home range sizes of males were much larger than for females; a similar difference was found for Franklin’s ground squirrels (Choromanski-Norris et al. 1989) and woodchucks (Swihart 1992; Maher 2004). Males and females in our study were generally similar in body size (Howland et al. 2024), so the difference in home range size probably is not due to higher metabolic requirements by males. The explanation may be associated with the mating system, which is polygynandrous (Wells et al. 2017); we measured home ranges after the mating season ended, and males may have roamed over enlarged home ranges to acquire information on potential mates for the next year.

Jesmer et al. (2011) reported home range sizes for female GMGS (3.57 ha 95% MCP, 3.75 ha 95% KDE) that were more than twice as large as those we report; their study site was in a conifer forest that may have supported a lower density of food resources than the forb-rich meadows of our study area. Despite the difference in home range sizes, overlap values for the 95% home range (0.31) and 50% core area (0.03) of adult females reported by Jesmer et al. (2011) were remarkably similar to our values. This similarity suggests that extensive overlap of home ranges but minimal overlap of core areas are characteristic of GMGS and supports the hypothesis that GMGS are territorial, but only for the inner part of their home range (Ferron 1985; Jesmer et al. 2011).

Contrary to expectation, kinship played a role in the spatial organization of GMGS: related females shared space more than unrelated females. At the level of the 95% home range, littermate sister and mother–daughter dyads showed differing levels of relatedness but similar levels of overlap (0.43 for sister–sister, 0.45 for mother–daughter; Fig. 2), suggesting that kin identification in GMGS is based primarily on familiarity (Michener 1983; Armitage 1989). Woodchucks, another asocial species, showed a similar pattern of increased home range overlap with increased relatedness (Maher 2009). In yellow-bellied marmots (Marmota flaviventer), a highly social species (Grade 4), home range overlap increased with kinship, but the relationship was not proportional; space sharing was disproportionately directed to close kin (r = 0.50; Armitage 1996).

The core area of GMGS included 50% of locations but averaged only 22% of home range size, indicating an area of intensive use and, presumably, importance. For those dyads that overlapped core areas, space sharing (0.26) was nearly as great as for those that overlapped home ranges (0.30), suggesting that most females did not share core areas, but for those that did, sharing was extensive. As was the case with home ranges, kinship played a positive role in core area overlap, with the exception of littermate sisters (Fig. 2). The explanation may be reproductive competition between sisters; yearling females with a littermate sister nearby realized a 78% reduction in probability of breeding yet experienced no effect from proximity of their mother or more distantly-related females (Wells and Van Vuren 2018).

Behavioral interactions were dominated by agonistic encounters (80% of all interactions), most of which were chases. Ferron (1985) also observed a high rate of agonistic interactions (72%, n = 60) for this species, which is expected for an asocial ground squirrel (Michener 1983). Woodchucks, also considered asocial, likewise show a preponderance of agonistic interactions (Maher 2006). In our study, females initiated most agonistic encounters, with both males and females as the targets of the aggression, suggesting that female behavior largely determines the spatial organization. However, our observations were conducted after mating occurs, and the role of males in behavioral interactions might have been different during the mating season. Tolerance was a common interaction for female–female and female–male dyads, but rare for male–male dyads. A lack of male–male tolerance might be expected during mating, when males presumably are competing for access to mates, and perhaps that competition continued beyond the mating season.

Despite considerable variation in density, both at the population level and at the local level, neither of the expected outcomes of increased density was realized: we did not see reduced home range size coupled with unchanged overlap (e.g., Priotto et al. 2002; Drake et al. 2015), nor did we see unchanged size coupled with increased overlap (e.g., Wolff 1985; Hoset et al. 2007). Instead, we found a positive effect of density on home range size (Fig. 3) and a corresponding near-significant effect on core area size, and these effects were realized only at the local level. This result was unexpected, and the cause is uncertain. One explanation is that females range farther when surrounded by neighbors in order to gain familiarity with territorial borders and reduce time spent on aggressive interactions in the long-term (“dear enemy effect”; Fisher 1954; Temeles 1994; Siracusa et al. 2017). Another explanation is that females expand their home ranges into less crowded, lower-quality areas, where they need to roam farther to access patchily-distributed resources (McShea and Schwede 1993; McLoughlin and Ferguson 2000; Kilpatrick et al. 2001; Harris and Leitner 2004). For example, male Bushbuck (Tragelaphus scriptus) responded to increased intruder pressure by expanding their territories to access potential mates (Wronski and Plath 2006). GMGS in our study area prefer dry meadow habitat with more perches, and availability of those perches increases the probability of survival (Aliperti et al. 2022). Possibly, increased local density prompts females to move into dry meadow habitat with fewer perches. Also, reproductive females might move into less a crowded habitat to give birth, away from the threat of harassment or infanticide by neighboring squirrels (Michener 1982; Ebensperger and Blumstein 2007), and then move the litter to a better habitat as pups emerge; litter relocation is common in this species (Aliperti et al. 2020). Alternatively, perhaps the causality between local density and home range size is reversed; when home range sizes increase for reasons other than density, a given home range might overlap with more neighboring home ranges, thereby increasing the number of overlapping neighbors.

We did not find an effect of density on overlap of home ranges or core areas, but we did find an interaction between density and kinship on home range overlap: related females showed greater home range overlap as either population density or local density increased (Fig. 4). Hence, kinship influenced spatial overlap, but only at high densities. Moreover, we found a similar density-mediated interaction in adult female behavior, in which chases increased with spatial overlap unless dyads were highly related (Fig. 5). Large increases in our population are driven primarily by increased survival and reproduction of residents, rather than by immigration (Hostetler et al. 2012). Evidently, as density increases, females are more tolerant of neighboring kin than of non-kin. Note that this finding is based on comparisons between unrelated squirrels and mother–daughter pairs, the highest-related dyads. We lacked sufficient data to analyze behavioral interactions between known sister pairs, which could offer further insights.

Density-mediated philopatry and spatial overlap among kin have been reported in other solitary mammals (e.g., Kitchen et al. 2005; Støen et al. 2005; Maher 2009; Meshriy et al. 2011; Innes et al. 2012), and the formation of kin clusters at high density is an important step in the evolution of sociality (Waser and Waser 1985; Maher 2009; Meshriy et al. 2011). In GMGS, the creation of kin groups can have important demographic implications; density of kin, but not non-kin, influenced litter sex ratios for female GMGS—presumably due to competition for resources among philopatric females (Wells and Van Vuren 2017)—and survival is lower for females surrounded by more kin (Kanaziz et al. 2022). Nonetheless, GMGS may build a facultative tolerance to kin during high-density years because the inclusive fitness benefits of sharing resources with close relatives can outweigh the costs of competition (Hamilton 1964; Kitchen et al. 2005; Maher 2009; Banks et al. 2011). At high density or when resources are limited, surrounding habitat may be saturated with aggressive neighbors; hence, close relatives may tolerate the retention of female kin when the probability of successful dispersal is low (Waser and Jones 1983; Wolff 1993; Van Vuren and Armitage 1994; Solomon 2003).

Our results suggest that the spatial organization of GMGS resembles that of other solitary mammals, in that females maintain areas of exclusive use (Armitage 1981; Michener 1983). However, spatial overlap among adults suggests that this species defends only the core area of its home range and that females share more space with kin than non-kin (McEachern et al. 2007; Maher 2009; Innes et al. 2012; Rodgers et al. 2015; le Roex et al. 2022; Makuya et al. 2024), but only at high densities. Further, this density-mediated increase in space-sharing and tolerance among kin suggests that familiarity and inclusive fitness benefits may offset costs of competition and promote philopatry under certain conditions. Overall, we provide evidence of a facultative transition in GMGS from asociality to the formation of single-family kin clusters (Grade 2), the first step toward developing sociality (Armitage 1981; Michener 1983; Waser and Waser 1985; Maher 2009). Our study highlights the importance of considering behavioral interactions in asocial species, as well as the interactive effects of environmental conditions and kinship when assessing the spatial and social organization of mammals.
