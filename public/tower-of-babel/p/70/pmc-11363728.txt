Frontal alpha asymmetry predicts subsequent social decision-making: A dynamic multilevel, neural, and developmental perspective
Authors: Rebecca Revilla, Cailee M Nelson, Nicole R Friedman, Summer S Braun, Caitlin M Hudac
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSocial motivation, the human desire to engage with others, is likely to underlie higher levels of social cognition and the formation of interpersonal relationships. Yet, this topic has been understudied in adolescents despite the critical developmental and maturational changes that occur during this period and the relevance of social motivation to clinical and neurodevelopmental disorders. Using electroencephalography (EEG) and an implicit-association paradigm (Choose-A-Movie Task; Dubey et al., 2015), we examined how brain responses underlying socially motivated decisions informed future decisions in 54 youth (aged 10–14 years) and 50 young adults (aged 18–33 years). As the first study to use this task during EEG recording, we implemented time-frequency analyses and a trial-by-trial dynamic statistical approach. Results suggested that both age groups preferred low-effort choices and increasingly preferred nonsocial choices over time. P3 amplitude also increased over time and was sensitive to effortful decisions, particularly for adults, but not social content. Both groups showed larger leftward frontal alpha asymmetry (FAA) during nonsocial feedback, and FAA predicted future decisions differently for adults than youth. The current study highlights FAA and trial-by-trial analyses as useful tools in understanding the neural mechanisms underlying socially motivated decisions, which differ across development, time, and individuals.Keywords: Social decision-making, Electroencephalography (EEG), Frontal alpha asymmetry, Adults and adolescents, Trial-by-trial dynamics

Introduction

An innate desire to connect with others, often described as social motivation, pushes people to seek and build interpersonal relationships (Chevallier et al., 2012). Social motivation varies across individuals and shifts across development; however, it is unclear how neural differences across individuals and developmental periods may influence socially motivated behaviors and decision-making both broadly and dynamically, from moment to moment (Altikulaç et al., 2019, Pluymen et al., 2021, Tamir and Hughes, 2018). This is particularly relevant during adolescence, as it is a developmental period characterized by critical social and reward brain changes connected with increased sensitivity to social rewards (Casey et al., 2008, Foulkes and Blakemore, 2016). Adolescents’ increasing sensitivity to social rewards corresponds with a greater desire to seek social interaction and form relationships with peers (Dziura et al., 2023, Flores et al., 2018). These socially-motivated behaviors are developmentally critical and evolutionarily adaptive, as adolescence requires individuals to become steadily more independent and able to navigate new, complex social environments (Eccles et al., 2003, Ellis et al., 2012). While sensitivity to social rewards reduces in young adulthood (Jones et al., 2014, Wang et al., 2020), it is still unclear how this is reflected in social motivation and related behaviors. Additionally, few studies have examined how social motivation influences social decision-making across these periods.

Dubey et al. (2015) developed the computer-based Choose-A-Movie (CAM) task to capture ongoing socially motivated behaviors as participants make decisions about “unlocking” one of two patterned boxes. The two box patterns are each implicitly associated with social or nonsocial content that is presented after a box is fully unlocked. Importantly, to create a proxy for social effort, each patterned box requires participants to click to unlock one to three locks. Some trials require equal effort to unlock either box, while other trials involve more effort to unlock either the social-associative or nonsocial-associative box. Adults were more likely to choose social-associative boxes than nonsocial-associative boxes when equal or minimal effort (i.e., one-lock differential) was required but not when maximum effort (i.e., two-lock differential) was required (Dubey et al., 2015, Williams and Cross, 2018).

In contrast, adolescents were equally likely to choose social-associative and nonsocial-associative boxes when the effort required was equal but tended to choose low-effort nonsocial options when the required effort was unequal (Dubey et al., 2017a). When examined across 4-to-20-year-olds, social preference appeared to reduce at 11 years of age and increase into late adolescence (Dubey et al., 2017b). Together, these findings suggest that there are developmental differences in behavioral inclination to choose social options; however, the dip in early adolescent social preferences was unexpected. Authors of these original studies (Dubey et al., 2017, Dubey et al., 2017) identified other studies that have also shown a shift towards non-social stimuli during adolescence (Ewing et al., 2013, Sasson et al., 2008) and hypothesized these findings could be due to a true reduction in social motivation in early adolescence, the authors’ use of adults as social stimuli versus youth, or more cautious social seeking in the sample proposedly related to a higher sensitivity to social rejection. To better characterize and explain potential differences, the current study examined trial-level decision-making and associated neural mechanisms using the CAM task.

Results from fMRI studies affirm that the associative context of the box (social vs nonsocial) is modulating decision-making (Dubey et al., 2020, Williams et al., 2020). However, fMRI cannot capture rapid and potentially nuanced shifts in motivational behavior in the same way as temporally precise electroencephalography (EEG; Hajcak et al., 2019; Hudac et al., 2021), which is amenable to a dynamic approach using single-trial analysis (Blankertz et al., 2011, Hudac et al., 2022, Si et al., 2020). The frontal P3 component is present approximately from 250 ms to 500 ms after a choice has been made and is related to attentional resource allocation, motivational salience, and sensitivity to novelty (Polich, 2007, Yeung and Sanfey, 2004). Previous studies have found an increased positive-going amplitude in anticipation of attentionally and motivationally salient conditions including socially rewarding stimuli (Cox et al., 2015; A. Flores et al., 2015). Accordingly, we examined the P3 component during the CAM task to examine differences in the attentional and motivational salience of social versus nonsocial stimuli.

Social motivation may influence social decision-making based upon trait-level differences in social motivation measured via self-report (Neuhaus et al., 2021). Yet, the extent to which social feedback is perceived as enjoyable or motivating in the moment may also influence subsequent decisions. Although enjoyment itself is difficult to capture, frontal alpha asymmetry (FAA) has been studied in connection with emotional and motivational states (Coan et al., 2006) and is often conceptualized as reflecting approach/withdrawal processes (Harmon-Jones et al., 2010, Smith et al., 2017). FAA is determined by comparing alpha band (e.g., 8–12 Hz; Galang and Obhi, 2019) power between left- and right-hemispheres (Harmon-Jones et al., 2018). While FAA can be calculated in several different ways, a common approach in developmental literature is to subtract the natural log of absolute alpha power in the front left hemisphere from the front right hemisphere (i.e., FAA = ln(right)-ln(left); Vincent et al., 2021). Additionally, alpha power is thought to be inversely related to regional cortical activity (Harmon-Jones et al., 2018) such that more positive and negative FAA values indicate greater left and right hemispheric cortical activity respectively. Studies examining FAA in the context of approach/withdrawal states consistently show that approach processes relate to relatively greater rightward FAA (i.e., left frontal cortical activation) and withdrawal/avoidance processes are related to relatively greater leftward FAA (i.e., right frontal cortical activation; Deng et al., 2023; Sobotka et al., 1992). Additionally, links between self-reported liking of stimuli and approach-oriented FAA, even when stimuli were not social, suggest that FAA is a useful tool for better understanding motivational states (Gable and Harmon-Jones, 2008, Kline et al., 2000). However, very little work to date has examined FAA in the context of social motivation.

Present study

Our goal was to study social decision-making in a dynamic, single-trial context to better understand how neural mechanisms of social motivation on the previous trial may inform subsequent decisions.

First, building from prior work using the CAM task (Dubey et al., 2015, Dubey et al., 2018, Dubey et al., 2020), we evaluated behavior (likelihood of selecting the social-associative box) and neural responses (P3 amplitude) for each decision made that varied by effort (i.e., current lock distribution). Increasingly across the experiment, we anticipated that participants would strengthen implicit associations between the box pattern and feedback content (i.e., social versus nonsocial) and subsequently make more effortful decisions towards a social preference. Since adolescent decision-making in previous CAM studies showed no social preference for equal-effort options but a preference for low-effort nonsocial options (Dubey et al., 2017, Dubey et al., 2017), we predicted that youth would be equally likely to choose social and nonsocial options when equal effort was required but would choose more nonsocial options when low-effort was required, while adults would choose more social options. However, when maximum effort was required, we anticipated an overall preference for low effort options across age groups. As box-content associations grow stronger, we predicted an increased P3 amplitude for social decisions reflecting motivational salience for socially rewarding stimuli (Cox et al., 2015, Flores et al., 2015).

Second, we evaluated the feedback phase by examining social motion perception (Knight et al., 2022, Pavlova, 2012, Perry et al., 2010), particularly within the alpha band (8–12 Hz; Fitzpatrick et al., 2019). We predicted that social feedback relative to nonsocial feedback would elicit increased rightward FAA (i.e., more positive values or more leftward cortical asymmetry), aligned with work indicating that leftward cortical asymmetry) is implicated in approach-oriented situations (Harmon-Jones and Gable, 2018, Rodrigues et al., 2018). In this way, FAA during feedback will reflect the current state of motivation. We expected both adults and youth would demonstrate increased rightward FAA to social relative to nonsocial motion perception feedback, though this response would be stronger in the youth group, reflecting heightened neural reactivity to social stimuli during adolescence (Nelson et al., 2005, Ruff and Fehr, 2014).

Lastly, we evaluated how neural mechanisms of social motivation predict subsequent social decision-making on the next trial. Within our single-trial approach, we predicted that increased rightward FAA during feedback would subsequently influence social decision-making on the next trial. As a new approach, there is a limited basis to make a specific hypothesis about the developmental differences between youth and adults, especially considering contradictory behavioral evidence demonstrating a slight tendency for youth to choose nonsocial options on the CAM task despite increased social reward seeking during adolescence. Accordingly, we hypothesized neural mechanisms of approach processes (i.e., more rightward FAA and greater left cortical activity) would predict subsequent social decision-making for adults only.

Methods

Participants

Adult participants were recruited from the local psychology subject pool (compensated with course credit) or advertisements posted around the community (compensated with $10/hour). Youth participants were recruited from the community; a subset (n = 18) elected to participate in one of our summer camps where participants complete the research study one day and enjoy group activities with their peers on the other days. All participants were screened via a custom screener for neurological and psychiatric disorders but were not excluded based on this criterion: seven adults and one youth indicated anxiety symptoms, seven youth indicated attention symptoms, and one youth indicated depressive symptoms. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision. Following EEG, two adults and one youth were excluded due to technical errors in EEG data collection.

Table 1 reports the final sample that consisted of 50 adults (aged 18–33 years; 46 % non-White and/or Hispanic or Latine) and 54 youth (aged 10–14 years; 40.7 % non-White and/or Hispanic or Latine). Of note, this community sample of adults were characterized with elevated social motivation scores, indicating less social motivation compared to the youth sample who scored within normal limits (Constantino and Gruber, 2012). Based upon self-report via the Pubertal Development Scale (Petersen et al., 1988), most of the youth sample were prepubertal (n = 28, 51.9 %) or early pubertal (n = 17, 31.5 %), with six mid-pubertal (11.1 %), two late pubertal (3.7 %), and one postpubertal (1.9 %).2 The local ethical review board approved this project and procedures. All adult participants and legal guardians of youth participants gave written informed consent and assent was obtained from youth participants prior to beginning the study.

Trait-level social motivation

The Social Responsiveness Scale, Second Edition (SRS-2) is a parent-report and self-report measure designed to quantify social abilities and related characteristics typically seen in individuals with autism spectrum disorder across five subareas: social motivation, social cognition, social awareness, social communication, and restricted interests and repetitive behaviors (Constantino and Gruber, 2012). The social motivation subscale was used in the current study to assess an individual’s motivation to socially interact with others. Although the SRS-2 was developed for autism spectrum disorder populations, the social motivation subdomain varies across neurotypical individuals, shows partial overlap with autistic samples (O’Connor et al., 2019, Sedgewick et al., 2016), and corresponds to other neural social processes, such as face perception (Key and Corbett, 2014). The social motivation subscale consists of 11 items (e.g., “My child/I would rather be alone than with others”) that are rated on a four-point Likert scale (1 = Not True, 4 = Almost Always True). Items were summed together, and t-scores were used. Cronbach’s alpha for this subscale within the current sample was α =.85. T-scores ranged from 51 to 104 with a mean t-score of 73 and a standard deviation of 12. Higher scores indicate more difficulties with social motivation and lower scores indicate less difficulties with social motivation.

Stimulus design and procedures

The Choose-A-Movie task (CAM) was adapted for use in the current study from the original version (Dubey et al., 2015; see Fig. 1). The task was displayed on a 24” widescreen EIZO FlexScan EV2451 monitor using E-Prime 3.0 (Psychology Software Tools, Pittsburgh, PA). Participants were told that the purpose of the task was to unlock boxes to play a short video. Participants learned how to engage with the task during a brief practice period, which is a simplified version of the main task depicted in Fig. 1. Participants used a 5-button box held on their lap to make their selections using the furthest left and furthest right buttons. To become familiar with the unlocking procedures and start building implicit associations, participants were shown a practice set of 6 trials with a single patterned box in the center of the screen. One button press unlocks one lock, and multiple button presses are required to unlock multiple locks. Each lock takes 1 s to unlock (i.e., change from yellow-closed to grey-open), which is a proxy for effort. Once all locks on the single patterned box were unlocked, a video clip played for 3 s. Each patterned box is always associated with a social video or a nonsocial video in both the practice period and the main task (e.g., unlocking the box with the broken glass pattern always plays a social video). Participants had unlimited time to make their selections and completed 60 trials. Box pattern location was balanced so that the broken glass pattern appeared on the left side the same number of times as the circular geometric pattern. On average, it took participants approximately 10–15 min to complete all 60 task trials.

Social Motion Choose-A-Movie Task. (a) Example of a practice trial in which a patterned box is associated with a social feedback video. (b) Example of a trial with a 2:1 lock ratio in which the participant chooses to open the box associated with a nonsocial feedback video. (c) Depiction of possible lock ratios at the beginning of the trial including equal effort, social effort, and nonsocial effort.

Trials began as one of three conditions that were randomly presented to participants: (1) Equal Effort Trials (n=12) had an equal number of locks on the social box and nonsocial box (e.g., equal effort required to unlock either social or nonsocial; 4 trials with 1:1 locks, 4 trials with 2:2 locks, 4 trials with 3:3 locks); (2) Social Effort Trials (n=24) had more locks on the social box than nonsocial box (e.g., more effort required to unlock social vs. nonsocial; 12 trials with 3:1 locks, 6 trials with 2:1 locks, 6 trials with 3:2 locks); and (3) Nonsocial Effort Trials (n=24) had more locks on the nonsocial box than the social box (e.g., more effort required to unlock nonsocial vs. social; 12 trials with 3:1 locks, 6 trials with 2:1 locks, 6 trials with 3:2 locks). In this way, participants unlocked a minimum of 84 locks across the 60 task trials. Adults unlocked a mean of 103 trials (SD=15.2; range 84–147) and youth unlocked a mean of 117 trials (SD=23.4; range 87–180).

To increase the salience of the reward videos, we utilized videos from a prior social attention discrimination study that pilot testing demonstrated to be interesting and dynamic (Pierce et al., 2011). Three second clips were taken from multiple videos with a varying start time to generate 60 social videos of children moving on a yoga mat and 60 nonsocial videos of colored dynamic geometric patterns. In this way, videos did not repeat but had similar content.

EEG acquisition and processing

Continuous EEG was recorded from a high-density 128-channel geodesic net using Net Station 5.3 software integrated with a 400-series high-impedance amplifier (MagStim-EGI). Electrode impedances were below 50 kohms at the start to maximize signal-to-noise ratio, within the standard range for high-impedance amplifiers. During acquisition, EEG signals were referenced to the vertex electrode, analog filtered (0.1 Hz high-pass, 100 Hz elliptical low-pass), amplified, and digitized with a sampling rate of 1000 Hz; additionally, researchers observed and marked periods of off-task behavior or behaviors interfering with the EEG signal. The continuous EEG data was then downsampled to 250 Hz and filtered using a bandpass filter at 0.1 Hz - 40 Hz. Automated artifact rejection was completed using the clean_rawdata plugin in EEGLAB (Delorme and Makeig, 2004). Channels were rejected if they were poorly correlated with one another using a rejection threshold of 0.9 (Delorme, 2023). Artifacts were then corrected using the Artifact Subspace Reconstruction (ASR) algorithm (Mullen et al., 2015) and removed channels were interpolated. Data was then re-referenced to average and epoched depending on the phase of the experiment we were interested in.

For the decision phase, EEG data was epoched from –200–700 ms time-locked to the button response at the moment of unlocking. Data was baseline corrected from –200–0 ms and peak P3 amplitude values were extracted from 250 to 500 ms for each unlock trial across frontal left, medial, and right electrodes. 3 Trials with P3 amplitudes ± 50 µV were deemed artificial and excluded. P3 latency findings were not the focus of the current study but are available in Supplemental Information (SI 2.1).

For the feedback phase (i.e., when participants saw either the social or nonsocial videos), EEG data was epoched from 0 to 3000 ms time-locked to the start of the video. Data was decomposed using the default time-frequency decomposition settings in EEGLAB. Alpha absolute power was computed by extracting power spectral density values from the time-frequency decomposed EEG data for the alpha frequency band commonly associated with social motion perception (e.g., 8–12 Hz; Fitzpatrick et al., 2019; Perry et al., 2010) across frontal electrodes for each trial. Absolute alpha power was then extracted from frontal left and right channels for each trial. Preliminary models confirmed that absolute alpha values were larger for left hemisphere, F(1, 12005) = 465.87, p <.0001, and this hemisphere effect was increased for nonsocial feedback, F(1, 12005) = 3.90, p =.05, and increased for Youth, F(1, 12005) = 79.97, p <.0001. In other words, there were asymmetry effects. Thus, analysis focused on frontal asymmetry that was computed on natural log transformed absolute alpha power for left relative to right [i.e., ln(right) – ln(left); Vincent et al., (2021)].

Analytic plan

All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.3.1; model syntax available in Supplemental Information [SI 2.2]). For all analyses requiring posthoc testing, false-discovery rate correction (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995) was applied for multiple comparisons. As a base, all models were fit with a random intercept for each person to account for repeated measures and a fixed effect of trait social motivation (SRS-2 subdomain; Level 2).

Behavioral responses were analyzed using mixed-effects binary logistic regression using the ‘glmer’ function of the lme4() statistical package (Bates, 2010; see SI 1.1). To characterize social decision-making, models predicted likelihood of selecting the social box pattern with fixed effects for social effort (Level 1), group (adult, youth), and the interaction between social effort and group. Social effort was calculated as number of social locks minus number of nonsocial locks and was updated each epoch (i.e., decisions within trial) based upon the current distribution of locks. In this way, positive values indicated social effort, zero indicated equal effort for social and nonsocial, and negative values indicated nonsocial effort. Effort could be minimal (±1) or moderate (±2). To test whether behaviors changed over time, a fixed effect of trial order (Level 1) was included; this did not interact with other predictors due to power constraints.

P3 amplitude was predicted using the mixed-effects model using the ‘lme’ function with fixed effects for social effort, group (2: Adult, Youth), trial order, and a full-factorial with interactions between all fixed effects except trait-level social motivation (see SI 1.2).

FAA was predicted using the base mixed-effects model using the ‘lme’ function with additional fixed effects for feedback condition (2: Nonsocial, Social), group (2: Adult, Youth), trial order, and a full-factorial with interactions between all fixed effects except trait-level social motivation (see SI 1.3).

Lastly, we tested whether FAA predicted likelihood of making a subsequent social decision. Excluding the first trial, decision phase analyses were repeated for each trial (t) with the same effects as described above with an additional fixed effect for the previous trial FAA value (see SI 1.4).

Of note, across all statistical models, two sets of sensitivity analyses indicated consistent results with the main analyses: first, excluding participants with less than 10 feedback trials of either condition (4 youth, 3 adults); second, excluding participants with psychiatric considerations: ADHD (6 youth), anxiety (6 adults, 1 youth), depression (1 youth), intermittent explosive disorder (1 youth), and Tourette’s syndrome (1 youth).

Chi-square analyses indicated that adult (M = 28.22, SD = 8.64, range = 4–56) and youth (M = 27.85, SD = 7.85, range = 0–57) participants completed a similar number of social feedback trials, p = 1.

Neural predictors of social decisions

Lastly, we examined how FAA predicts social decisions. The number of points per axis for evaluating the adaptive Gauss-Hermite approximation to the log-likelihood was set to 0 per recommendations when nAGQ > 1 is determined to be nearly unidentifiable (Breslow, 2004). All omnibus effects are reported in Table 2 and the correlation of fixed effects is available in SI Table 4.

Regarding behavioral decisions, increased leftward FAA (i.e., associated with rightward cortical activity and withdrawal) in the previous trial predicted a decreased likelihood of selecting the social box, p =.02 (see Fig. 5A; alternative orientation in SI Fig. 3). A similar effect of trial was observed as in the earlier model, where there was a decreased likelihood of selecting the social box across trials, p =.01. Importantly, there was a three-way interaction between previous trial FAA, group, and social effort, F(1, 104) = 26.67, p =.001. There were no group differences when prior FAA was zero. At more negative levels of prior FAA (indicative of withdrawal), adults were more likely to choose the low-effort option than youth. In other words, youth were more likely to choose the social box even when there were more locks on the social box (e.g., social effort greater than 0) relative to adults. In contrast, as levels of prior FAA moved toward zero (i.e., no asymmetry), youth and adults exhibited similar patterns, such that both were more likely to choose the low-effort option, particularly when the social box had more locks.

First, pubertal development score was included in the full factorial; however, there were no effects related to puberty, p >.34. Second, a model with age centered at 12 years indicated a significant four-way interaction between social effort, FAA, trial, and age, p =.003. To interpret this interaction, model estimates were generated for ages 10, 12, and 14 years with 95 % confidence intervals. Within-youth age differences were more apparent when there were more locks on the social box (e.g., social effort greater than 0; see Fig. 5B). Regarding P3 amplitude, the group by trial interactions observed previously were further modulated by FAA and social effort in a four-way interaction, p =.02. To clarify these effects, posthoc marginal means were estimated and compared for social effort levels (-2, 0, 2) and prior FAA levels (-4, 0, 4) across trials (Fig. 6). For adults, during choices requiring social effort (i.e., 2 more locks on the social box), P3 amplitude was predicted to increase across the experiment when prior FAA levels were zero (slope =.05, se =.03, uncorrected) or four (slope =.14, se =.07, uncorrected). During choices requiring nonsocial effort (i.e., 2 more locks on the nonsocial box), the P3 amplitude was predicted to increase across the experiment, when prior FAA was negative (slope =.091, se =.03, p =.03 FDR-corrected, at FAA = −4) but P3 amplitude was predicted as a trend to decrease when prior FAA was positive (slope = −.18, se =.07, p =.07 FDR-corrected at FAA = 4). For youth, trial slope was not significant, p >.71 for any of the marginal means. In sum, prior FAA was observed to modulate P3 amplitude but only for adults when the boxes had different number of locks (i.e., a choice regarding effort was required).

For social effort, positive values indicate more locks on the social box (+2 = purple long dashed line), zero indicates equal number of locks on each box (0 = black dot-dashed line), and negative values indicate more locks on the nonsocial box (-2 = green solid line).

Discussion

This study evaluated social decision-making in a dynamic context for youth (aged 10–14 years) and adults using an established box-selection task with implicit social associations (Dubey et al., 2015). This was the first time this task had been used with EEG and a single-trial approach. Although we expected adults to make more selections towards a social preference than youth, we discovered neither group showed a social preference, and rather, adults’ selections were guided more strongly by overall effort than youth. However, over time, the likelihood of making a social selection diminished for both groups, which could indicate a decrease in social preference or an increase in nonsocial preference throughout the experiment. P3 amplitude during decision-making and FAA during task feedback provided insight into the mechanisms potentially underlying these shifts in participants’ behavior, which generally conflicted with adult studies using this task (Dubey et al., 2015, Dubey et al., 2017, Dubey et al., 2020, Williams and Cross, 2018). However, previous studies collapsed behavioral data across the experiment while the current study modeled social decisions moment-to-moment across time. Importantly, results demonstrated how ongoing brain states influence and predict social decision-making.

P3 modulated by effort demands during decision-making

When choosing between the lowest and highest effort options (e.g., 3:3 lock ratio relative to 3:1 lock ratio), P3 amplitude increased over time for both groups but particularly for adults. Since P3 amplitude is sensitive to both positive and negative valence stimuli (Pinheiro et al., 2016, Rossi et al., 2017, Yeung and Sanfey, 2004), indicating increased attentional resources, adults may have become especially attentive to the effort ratio the longer they performed the task. Similarly, P3 has been associated with orienting and evaluation of novel stimuli (Friedman et al., 2001). This interpretation of P3 amplitude suggests that as participants in the current study were presented with new and differing effort ratios, particularly ones where the effort differential was greatest, more evaluative processing was required to decide. Perhaps as fatigue set in, adults were increasingly motivated to avoid high effort options when they could choose the lowest effort option. The stronger effect observed in adults could be related to developmental differences in the ability to evaluate and encode the motivational salience of varying content and to use this information to make behavioral adjustments (Riggins and Scott, 2020). Although overall P3 amplitude was larger for youth, this is likely due to developmental differences in P3 associated with other cognitive (e.g., information processing capacity) and maturational developmental differences (e.g., skull thickness; Frodl et al., 2001; van Dinteren et al., 2014).

As it was found that P3 amplitude was not modulated by social effort (i.e., extended effort to engage with social feedback) but rather global effort, this may indicate that P3 was not sensitive to the valence of the feedback stimuli but instead the magnitude of effort. This is in line with previous research which suggests P3 amplitude is more sensitive to reward magnitude rather than valence (Yeung and Sanfey, 2004). In the current study, lock differences (i.e., effort magnitude) were clearly visible to participants during each decision whereas social and nonsocial feedback were implicitly associated with boxes over time. As such, participants may have found effort more immediately salient than the feedback they were expecting to receive. These results may also be driven by the overall behavioral preference for low-effort options and the increasing preference for nonsocial videos over time. As this study was one of the first to examine social preference over time and the current findings are somewhat inconsistent with previous social decision-making research that suggests there is preference for social videos at minimal or equal effort (e.g., Dubey et al., 2015; Williams and Cross, 2018), future research would benefit from continued examination of neural responses to effort and preference for social stimuli on trial-by-trial basis.

Frontal alpha asymmetry developmental differences and decision predictions

Average leftward FAA (i.e., rightward cortical activity), a proxy for withdrawal processes, was larger (i.e., more negative FAA values) for both groups during nonsocial feedback which was in line with our expectations. These results may indicate that participants experienced greater levels of approach (Briesemeister et al., 2013, Harmon-Jones et al., 2010, Harmon-Jones and Gable, 2018), enthusiasm (Davidson et al., 2000), or positive valence (Keller et al., 2000) when viewing social feedback. Given that mean FAA values for social feedback were also negative (i.e., indicating leftward FAA and withdrawal processes) and participants were more likely to choose the nonsocial feedback as time went on, there remains a need to further explore how FAA varies across different social contexts and whether specific brain regions or networks are implicated per context. Similarly, we expected youth to demonstrate greater rightward FAA (i.e., leftward cortical activity) during social feedback compared to adults, as adolescents demonstrate increased neural reactivity to social stimuli (Nelson et al., 2005, Ruff and Fehr, 2014); yet, we found only a group-level effect, such that youth had increased leftward- FAA than adults (averaged across social and nonsocial conditions). Like the developmental differences found in P3, developmental differences in average FAA may be due to maturational differences in the brain. For example, hemispheric lateralization of the language and auditory systems, which has been linked to brain asymmetry (Wang et al., 2023), is still relatively underdeveloped in youth compared to adults (Paquette et al., 2015, Yamazaki et al., 2018). The extent by which FAA shifts in conjunction with developmental changes in social motivation will require deeper, ongoing study of youth across longitudinal time.

Neural approach/withdrawal processes modulate subsequent decisions

Critically, FAA during feedback predicted behavioral selections on the next trial, such that as prior FAA values became more negative (indicating more withdrawal-like processes), during feedback adults were more likely to choose the low-effort option. In contrast, greater indication of withdrawal processes during feedback increased the likelihood for youth to choose the high-effort option. Interestingly, when effort was equal for both social and nonsocial boxes, adults with more positive prior FAA (i.e., indicative of approach) during the previous trial were more likely to select the social box; whereas youth, regardless of prior FAA, were selecting boxes at near chance levels. Across all effort trials, there were no group differences when prior FAA was zero, such that both youth and adults were predicted to make similar decisions. Although pubertal development did not impact how prior FAA predicted subsequent decisions, chronological age was shown to gradually become more adult-like from 10 to 14 years. Of note, 10-year-olds were predicted to be the least influenced by effort and were most likely to select the social box. Developmental differences between model-predicted 10-, 12-, and 14-year-olds were most apparent when the social box had more social locks, which may suggest that although the social association was implicit, there may be a developmental shift occurring between 10 and 12 years.

Taken together, these results suggest a link between approach motivation and social decision-making that differs developmentally. For example, while effort was a driving factor, when feedback from the previous trial elicited greater withdrawal neural markers, youth were more likely than adults to choose the social box, even when greater effort was required. Given that approach systems are associated with reward and emotions like enthusiasm (Davidson et al., 2000), the current findings might suggest that youth’s preference for social contexts are more sensitive to how rewarding they found previous feedback. In other words, the findings suggest that youth withdrawal or avoidance may lead to seeking the social box, which may indicate youth subsequently work to reestablish feelings of enthusiasm or reward by choosing social feedback, even if it requires greater effort. However, the current study did not find a behavioral preference for social feedback in youth or adults, even when effort was equal. As the first study to examine how neural markers of approach processes predict social decision-making from trial-to-trial, we encourage studies to continue trial-level approaches to better understand dynamic patterns of social decision-making.

Lastly, the P3 amplitude was modulated by neural processes during feedback but only in adults when considering dynamic shifts. Specifically, following greater indication of approach processes (i.e., positive prior FAA), the adult P3 amplitude was predicted to increase across the experiment when more social effort required (i.e., there were more locks on the social box). An opposite effect was observed following indication of withdrawal processes, such that the adult P3 amplitude was predicted to increase across the experiment when nonsocial effort was required. Although there is limited work on the dynamic processes of social decision making, these findings suggest that motivational/affective processes during feedback modulate subsequent behavioral decisions and decision-making neural processes, which is consistent with research showcasing that the P3 is implicated in motivationally salient, socially rewarding stimuli (Cox et al., 2015; Flores et al., 2015). This evidence warrants further consideration of how underlying brain states influence and inform decision-making, broadly, and particularly for social decision making (see Pedroni et al., 2017 as an example in risk taking). In addition, the lack of P3 modulation across trial in the youth sample is stark. Investigation of the developmental trajectories of social decision making will be critical for a deeper understanding of how these processes develop across later adolescence and emerging adulthood.

Limitations

First, the stimuli used in the current study differed from stimuli used in previous CAM studies (Dubey et al., 2015; Dubey et al., 2017, Dubey et al., 2017; Williams and Cross, 2018), which may have contributed to differences observed between previous study findings and the current study findings. Although we chose our feedback stimuli based on long-standing differentiation of social and nonsocial motion perception (Pierce et al., 2011), much of the prior work was conducted in toddlers and young children. Given that the social content of these videos depicted young children, these videos may have been less motivationally salient for adults and youth. Videos of social actors also are limited in that they may not evoke social motivation as strongly as real social interactions. For example, in trying to control the saliency of each video we used clips that only varied in a few ways (e.g., children performing different arm movements). Thus, participants may have found the stimuli boring and not as rewarding as true social interactions. Accordingly, future studies could further examine how characteristics of social stimuli, such as age of actors, and more ecologically valid social situations affect social motivation and its neural correlates across development. Second, in this study, we did not record quantitative or qualitative measurements of why people made their selection, which may be helpful to include in future studies to differentiate between explicit and implicit social preferences. Third, when extracting P3 amplitude we baseline corrected from –200–0 ms prior to stimulus onset where there was a large amplitude change that occurred almost immediately before stimulus onset (see Fig. 3). As this was likely due to anticipation of the button press or button pressing that may have occurred early and it is known that button pressing diminishes P3 amplitude (Salisbury et al., 2001). Future research may benefit from replication and changing task instructions so that participants are aware that they cannot press a button until the stimulus appears. Fourth, we did not include FAA differences during social and nonsocial feedback in our predictive model as we did not have enough power to include this factor and make sound conclusions. We opted to utilize a simpler analytic approach to best characterize existing contiguous, sequential data. Alternative analytic strategies were considered: one model with an inclusion of additional random effects per prior feedback condition supported the current results, whereas other models separately evaluating neural predictions following social and nonsocial feedback did not replicate the three-way finding indicating that youth were more likely to choose the social box even when there were more locks on it. We caution that interpretations would benefit from continued work and potential computational approaches (e.g., using existing data to simulate to a large enough sample to address the influence of the prior trial condition). Future research should aim to increase the number of trials and sample size to account for this as it may better explain the relationship between approach processes and socially motivated decisions. Finally, our youth cohort only included 10-to-14-year-olds, which does not allow us to generalize our findings across the entire span of adolescence. Considering that adolescence starts with puberty and ends around 25 years old depending on individual development (Sawyer et al., 2018), future developmental work should aim to collect data representative of the entire span of adolescence to accurately capture how social motivation may naturally shift across adolescence and into adulthood.

Implications

In addition to providing further support that social motivation differs across development, our results suggest that social motivation is dynamic, changing moment-to-moment, and the behavioral manifestation of social motivation can be significantly influenced by other factors, like required effort and possibly social content. These findings highlight the importance of considering context when examining how social motivation may drive social behaviors. For example, if an individual is exhibiting behavioral indicators of low social motivation, it is important to consider the role of external factors. Relying solely on behavioral indicators may inaccurately reflect an individual’s true desire for social connection, which can lead to inappropriate or unhelpful interventions (Jaswal and Akhtar, 2018). This is particularly relevant for understanding potential social motivation differences in populations with clinical and neurodevelopmental disorders (e.g., depression, social anxiety, schizophrenia, autism spectrum disorder; Bagg et al., 2023; Catalano and Green, 2023; Rum et al., 2023).

Conclusions

The current study was the first to examine how individual and developmental differences in social motivation at a neural level influence decision-making over time. Our results suggest that differences in the effort required to achieve various outcomes impact socially motivated decision-making and that over time individuals become more responsive to effortful decisions, as indicated by neural measures. We also found that brain correlates of approach processes were greater for youth and nonsocial feedback. Finally, neural responses to feedback, as characterized by FAA, predicted subsequent socially motivated decisions and did so differently between youth and adults. These findings are relevant for characterizing developmental changes in social motivation that drive important social behaviors, such as relationship building. These findings may also inform future studies that aim to understand the mechanisms underlying observed social motivation differences in populations with clinical and neurodevelopmental disorders. As more work continues to implement single-trial, dynamic approaches and utilize neural biomarkers like FAA to measure momentary experiences, the current study offers an important starting point for future research to better understand the relation between social effort and FAA.
