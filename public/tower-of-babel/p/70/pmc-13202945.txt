Region of residence, exposure to the media, and awareness of sexually transmitted infections among women in Ghana
Authors: Anthony Edward Boakye, Rita Tekpertey, Peter Mensah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSexually transmitted infections have a direct impact on sexual and reproductive health, causing stigma, infertility, malignancies, and pregnancy difficulties, and can significantly raise the risk of acquiring and transmitting human immunodeficiency syndrome .ObjectiveBased on this, the study aimed to investigate how region of residence and exposure to the media influence women’s awareness of sexually transmitted infections in Ghana.MethodsThe study made use of the 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey datasets which was provided by Monitoring and Evaluation to Assess and Use Results Demographic and Health Survey program. Using these datasets, the study examined 2894 women aged 15–49 years to ascertain if region of residence, and exposure to the media are key predictors of women’s sexually transmitted infections awareness. Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 27 was used to process the data and were analyzed with frequency distribution, chi-square test, and binary logistic regression. The frequency distribution was used to aggregate participants’ responses into proportions. The Pearson’s chi-squared test of independence was used to test the hypotheses postulated in the study, either to confirm or reject the null hypotheses while the binary logistic regression was used to determine the effect of the independent variables on the dependent variable .ResultsOverall, 98.5% of women are aware of sexually transmitted infections. Women in the Western region (p < 0.001; aOR = 1. 14; 95%CI: 3.44–3.81), those in Central (p < 0.001; aOR = 1. 03; 95%CI: 3.27–3.28), Volta (p < 0.001; aOR = 1.15; 95%CI: 3.58–3.74), Eastern (p < 0.001; aOR = 8.70; 95%CI: 2.72–2.77), Ashanti (p < 0.001; aOR = 1.03; 95%CI: 3.10–3.46), Oti (p < 0.001; aOR = 6.79; 95%CI: 2.14–2.15), and Savannah (p = 0.008; aOR = 5.64; 95%CI: 1.57–20.15) regions had a higher likelihood of sexually transmitted infections awareness compared with those in the Upper West. Women who had not used internet at all last month prior to the study (p = 0.004; aOR = 19.87; 95%CI: 2.61–150.90), and those who had used internet at least once a week last month prior to the study (p < 0.001; aOR = 1.62; 95%CI: 3.10–8.46) had greater odds of sexually transmitted infections awareness.ConclusionOur study shows that region of residence, listening to the radio, watching television, and using the internet were associated with women’s sexually transmitted infections awareness. These findings highlight the need to expand media access nationwide to improve sexually transmitted infections awareness and prevention efforts in Ghana.Keywords: Awareness, Media exposure, Region, Residence, Sexually transmitted infection

Introduction

Bacterial sexually transmitted infections (STIs), including gonorrhea, chlamydia, syphilis, and trichomoniasis, continue to occur at an alarming rate [1], with an estimated 374 million annually worldwide [2]. Among the four major STIs, the one with dominant prevalence in 2020 was chlamydia (129 million) followed by gonorrhea (82 million), syphilis (7.1 million), and the least was trichomoniasis (156 million) [2], indicating that globally, every day, more than 1 million treatable STIs are acquired in people aged 15 to 49 years, with the majority of them asymptomatic [3]. Besides, STIs have a direct impact on sexual and reproductive health, causing stigma, infertility, malignancies, and pregnancy difficulties, and can significantly raise the risk of acquiring and transmitting HIV [3]. Hence, they cause inflammation or open sores in the genital area, which often create easy entry points for the virus and can increase HIV viral load in bodily fluids, making transmission more likely [3].

While few STIs are immediately life-threatening, the consequences of not treating them can be severe [4]. STIs, left untreated, the chances of contracting HIV rise almost tenfold [4]. Populations in low-and middle-income countries are particularly vulnerable, with a higher prevalence of STIs due to factors such as lack of awareness and access to diagnostics and treatment [4].

In 2022, the WHO estimated the prevalence of STIs to be higher in Africa than in other regions [4]. In 2019, approximately 50 million women aged 15–49 years become infected annually [5]. These women were infected with one of the four major curable STIs, which include chlamydia, gonorrhea, syphilis and trichomoniasis [5]. Among the STIs cases, the dominant was Trichomoniasis (24 million), followed by Chlamydia (13 million), then Gonorrhea (12 million), and the least was Syphilis 1 million [5]. In sub-Saharan Africa, the largest estimates of STIs worldwide are reported in this region [6]. This underscores that STIs affect people of all socioeconomic classes in every country. Therefore, without proper precautions, no one is immune from these infections [6].

In Ghana, evidence suggests that approximately 3.4% of the population is infected with STIs, of whom about 28% are asymptomatic [7, 8]. STIs rate among females aged 15 to 19 years rose from 2.2% in 2003 to 7.1% in 2022. Further, adolescents aged 10 to 19 years made up 6% of the country’s new HIV infections in 2022. Furthermore, a total of 1,791 new HIV infections, representing 10.8% of all new cases, occurred within this age group [9]. Most previous studies on this phenomenon have predominantly focused on knowledge and prevalence of common STIs [7]; knowledge of STIs [10]; and knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding STIs [11–16]. Despite ongoing efforts to improve STIs awareness globally, there is limited research on the role of region-specific barriers and media exposure in shaping women’s knowledge of STIs in Ghana. This study aims to fill that gap.

The study further hypothesized that there is no statistically significant relationship between region of residence, exposure to the media, and women’s awareness of STIs in Ghana. This hypothesis was premised on Diffusion of Innovations Theory by Rogers in 2003 [17]. This theory expounds that once the STIs health-related message reaches a critical mass of adopters, diffusion accelerates and awareness tends to equalize across populations, thereby reducing the influence of geographic location or differential media exposure [17]. Hence, STIs information has been disseminated for decades through National HIV and STIs campaigns, antenatal and reproductive health services clinics, and school curricula in many sub-Saharan African countries, including Ghana [18–20]. Therefore, the basic awareness of STIs may have reached saturation thereby minimizing regional or media-based differences [17, 21]. Consequently, women across regions and media exposure levels may demonstrate comparable awareness, yielding no statistically significant relationship.

Methods

Data source and study design

The study used the 2022 GDHS datasets. The datasets were provided by Measure DHS Initiative or Program through the link: https://dhsprogram.com/data. The 2022 GDHS survey employed a cross-sectional descriptive design and drew much on a nationally stratified representative sample [17]. Further, the survey relied much on an updated sampling frame prepared by the Ghana Statistical Service (GSS) based on the 2021 Population and Housing Census. This current study echoed on the design of the DHS to investigate how region of residence, and exposure to the media influence women’s awareness of STIs in Ghana.

Sample and sampling procedure

A sample of 18,450 households was selected for the survey, which consisted of 15,014 women aged 15–49 years and 7,044 men aged 15–59 years [16]. The survey used a stratified two-stage cluster sampling design to recruit participants across all 16 regions of Ghana [17]. Data collected revolved around socio-demographic characteristics and reproductive health topics, which included (fertility, family planning, and maternal and child health), with standardized, face-to-face interviewer-administered questionnaires administered in households [17]. The study included 2894 (19.27%) women aged 15–49 years; hence, they had complete information on STIs awareness and the explanatory variable (exposure to the media), and excluded those without complete information. For the DHS program, no missing data are imputed. Even when there are missing data or “don’t know” responses, they are coded as missing in the datasets. Although, in this current study, the analytic datasets were devoid of missing data.

Measures

Independent variables

The Independent Variables (IVs) in the study were region of residence and exposure to the media (internet, radio, TV, & newspaper or magazine). These factors were carefully chosen to assess their influence on women’s awareness of STIs in Ghana. Region of residence was an indicator for measurement, while exposure to the media had the following (frequency of using the internet last month, frequency of reading newspapers or magazines, frequency of listening to the radio, and frequency of watching television) as indicators for measurements. Exposure to the media was categorized based on frequency of use, such as ‘not at all, ‘less than once a week,’ at least once a week,’ and ‘almost every day’ for each media form (internet, radio, TV, & newspaper or magazine). The forms of the media exposure were analyzed independently to examine their individual impacts on women’s STIs awareness in Ghana.

Dependent variable

The Dependent Variable (DV) was STIs awareness. STIs awareness was itself an indicator for measurement. The STIs awareness was measured on a dichotomous scale: yes “1” / no “0”. Hence, the outcome variable was coded as 1 (aware of STIs), and 0 (not aware of STIs).

Fieldwork

The survey’s data collection started on 17 October 2022 and ended on 14 January 2023. In all, data collection lasted for three (3) months. Field assistants were assisted with computer tablets to collect the data.

Ethical consideration

Although ethical clearance was not required for secondary data use, however, the GDHS data collection process adhered to strict ethical guidelines set by the Ghana Health Service and ICF.

Data processing and analysis

Data was processed with SPSS version 27 and incorporated the survey design, which includes stratification, clustering, and weighting. To produce population-level estimates, we applied the GDHS sampling weights, cluster number, and sampling stratum to account for the survey’s complex design. The data were summarized with frequency, percentages, chi-square and binary logistic regression. Further, the frequency and percentages were used to summarize participants’ responses into proportions, and weighted percentages and their corresponding 95% CIs were reported. Pearson’s chi-square analysis was conducted to examine the relationships between each independent variable and women’s STI awareness to decide whether to accept or reject the null hypotheses postulated in the study. Furthermore, the results were reported with an adjusted F statistic and its degrees of freedom. However, binary logistic regression was also used to test the influence of the IVs on the DV. Both unadjusted and adjusted binary logistic regression models were fitted. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs), P-values, and their corresponding 95% CIs were used to present the results of the binary logistic regression. Multicollinearity among the explanatory variables was evaluated using Variance Inflation Factors (VIF) and tolerance values, and no serious multicollinearity issues were found. Hence, all VIF values were below 2 and tolerance values below 1. Significance level was pegged at 0.05 (5%). This standard serves as a balance between minimizing false positives and detecting real effects [22–25]. Besides, Model fit was justified with multiple complementary indices, including Pseudo R Squares, χ² tests, calibration statistics, and discrimination indices, consistent with recommended practice for binary logistic regression modeling [26–28].

Results

Descriptive results

Out of 2894 women aged 15–49 years who responded to the 2022 GDHS STIs awareness question. The majority, 98.5% (95% CI [97.8, 99.0]), were aware of STIs, while 1.5% (95% CI [1.0, 2.2]) were not aware (See Table 1). Regarding region of residence, the dominant category was Greater Accra, constituting 17.1% (95% CI [14.8, 19.7]), while the least was Western North, 2.4% (95% CI [2.0, 2.9) (See Table 1). Whereas 84.4% (95% CI [82.2, 86.3]) had not read a newspaper or magazine at all prior to the study, 7.6% (95% CI [6.1, 9.4]) had read a newspaper or magazine at least once a week. With respect to frequency of listening to the radio, 57.2% (95% CI [54.1, 60.3]) had listened to the radio at least once a week, while 18.7% (95% CI [16.2, 21.4]) had not listened to the radio at all before the study. Concerning the frequency of watching television, 60.6% (95% CI [57.3, 63.8]) had watched television at least once a week, while 19.3% (95% CI [17.0, 21.8]) had watched television less than once a week before the study. Whereas 49.2% (95% CI [46.3, 52.0]) had not used the internet at all in the last month prior to the study, 2.7% (95% CI [2.0, 3.6]) had used the internet less than once a week (See Table 1).

Discussion

The study attempted to unravel the influences of region of residence, and exposure to the media on Ghanaian women’s awareness of STIs. The findings brought to the fore that women from Western, Central, Volta, Eastern, Ashanti, Oti, and Savannah regions had greater odds of STIs awareness compared with women in the Upper West region. Consistent with previous studies, which found that women’s likelihood of HIV testing varied significantly with rural versus urban residence and region, with women in certain regions (e.g., Western region) more likely to be tested than those in others [29, 30]. The significant p-values (all < 0.001) found indicate that these regional differences are unlikely due to chance. It might not be wrong for one to conclude that the relatively lower awareness of STIs among women from the Upper West region is linked to lower media reach, fewer health campaigns, or access barriers. The finding suggests that regions with better access to schooling, health services, and public media campaigns often show higher health awareness. The finding prioritizes targeted STIs education in the Upper West region, using culturally appropriate and language-specific approaches. Further, there is a call to allocate additional public health resources (community outreach, CHPS compounds, NGO partnerships) to regions with low awareness. This finding supports the idea that geographic context—region, province, rural versus urban—can influence women’s STIs awareness and related health behaviors because place often captures differences in: access to healthcare and education, source and reach of health information, cultural norms and social networks, and infrastructure and media penetration.

It is noted that exposure to the media enables users to come across information they might not be aware of. Surprisingly, our test statistic reveals that women who had not used the internet at all last month, prior to the study, had greater odds of STIs awareness. This outcome refuted a previous study, which found that non-internet users in the 2007 HINTS sample had significantly lower odds of being aware of the HPV vaccine — and of knowing that HPV causes cervical cancer and that HPV is sexually transmissible — compared with internet users [31]. The finding highlights the continued importance of offline communication channels. It is possible to conclude that women who do not use internet possibly rely on traditional media (radio, community education) that could emphasize STIs awareness more heavily. The finding suggests that internet access in Ghana does not automatically increase health awareness uniformly. The plausible explanation for this finding could be that non-internet users may overlap with demographic groups (such as older, married, and more health visits) that receive STIs messages through other channels.

Unexpectedly, the findings indicate that women’s awareness of STIs is significantly associated with both region of residence and exposure to the media. More importantly, the test statistic of region of residence and women’s awareness of STIs in Ghana indicates that a relationship exists between region of residence and women’s awareness of STIs; therefore, the null hypothesis was rejected. This outcome shows that women’s levels of STIs awareness differed significantly across regions. A p-value of < 0.001 indicates a stronger relationship, which means that region of residence is a stronger predictor of women’s awareness of STIs. This outcome agrees with a previous study, which found that 77% of young women in urban areas sought treatment for STIs, compared to 65% in rural areas [32]. The finding was also consistent with a prior study showing that residency is associated with STIs-related care-seeking behavior [33].

Moreover, significant relationships were revealed between women’s STIs awareness and frequency of media use; due to this, the null hypothesis was not confirmed. The observed relationship meant that the more women are exposed to the media, the more they become aware of STIs, which inevitably leads to awareness. This outcome agrees with previous studies, which also found that women exposed to mass media were more likely to test for HIV compared to those with no exposure (69.0 vs. 31.0%) [29, 34, 35]. Overall, these findings highlight the combined effect of regional context and media exposure on women’s STIs awareness. While regional disparities point to structural and contextual differences, the significant associations with internet, radio, and television use underscore the significance of mass media as effective channels for sexual health education. These findings advocate that region-specific and media-based interventions may be crucial for improving women’s STIs awareness, particularly in regions with lower media access.

The study found that 98.5% of women were aware of STIs. This finding refuted previous studies, which found a limited awareness of some STIs in sub-Saharan Africa, where HIV and AIDS and other STIs are highly prevalent [36, 37]. This outcome suggests that most women in Ghana are aware of STIs. The plausible explanation for this finding could be that reproductive health education is well disseminated in Ghana, and women pay particular attention to the information. On the other hand, the 1.5% who intimated that they were not aware of STIs reasons could be that where they live in Ghana, it is out of the coverage area of reproductive health education, and also do not have easy access to messages relating to STIs. This outcome implies that a significant percentage of women in Ghana are naïve about STIs, and that to close the awareness gap, health communication strategies should prioritize radio and TV campaigns in rural regions where media exposure is limited. Additionally, health education should be integrated into community health worker outreach programs. This outcome corroborated with a previous study, which found that a significant minority of young women in Ghana did not seek treatment for STIs [32].

It appeared women residing in the Greater Accra region of Ghana were the dominant category (17.1%) in the study, indicating that women from the capital city of Ghana were interested in the study and took part in their numbers. It might not be wrong to emphasize that Greater Accra is the most urbanized and densely populated region in Ghana, with greater access to households, infrastructure, and survey coverage. On the other hand, 2.4% were residents in the Western North region, indicating that they were the least represented category in the study. This underscores a targeted intervention to encourage all women across Ghana to get involved in all national studies in their numbers. It is possible to mention that the lower representation from the Western North region may be related to its smaller population size, more rural settlement patterns, and potential logistical challenges in data collection.

It emerged that 49.2% of participants had not used the internet at all in the last month prior to the study. This finding refuted a previous study, which found that nearly three-quarters of their study participants (74.6%) have internet addiction (36.6% mild; 35.1% moderate; 2.9% severe) [38]. This finding implies that a significant proportion of Ghanaian women do not have an interest in browsing, which hypothetically translates into a majority not using the internet in the month prior to the survey. A plausible reason for this outcome could be that the majority of Ghanaian women do not have time to browse due to their packed daily activities. However, the 2.7% who reported using the internet less than once a week prior to the study may have used it in their daily activities. Further, these women are well-resourced or in the richest wealth quintile, which invariably enables them to purchase data for browsing. This implies that only few Ghanaian women have interest in browsing, hence, internet use. This outcome disagrees with previous studies, which found that approximately half (53.9%) reported spending less than 2 h per day online, and more than two-thirds (75.2%) went online 5–7 days a week [39, 40]. This finding underscores an immediate action (policy) that combines affordable access (subsidized data, digital skills training (literacy), promoting relevant content, and tackling socio-cultural barriers with supportive laws like the National Gender Policy and targeted programs.

The study found that the overwhelming majority (84.4%) of women had not read a newspaper or magazine prior to the study. This finding disagrees with a previous study, which found that almost two-thirds (73.7%) of the participants have not read any newspaper or magazine [41]. This outcome implies that most Ghanaian women either do not have an interest in reading, do not have easy access to newspapers, or are unable to purchase newspapers. The plausible reason for this outcome could be that these women are not educated, and that they cannot read. It could also be that their daily activities do not leave them time to read the newspapers. On the other hand, the 7.6% who intimated they had read a newspaper or magazine at least once a week, the reason could be that they have the time to read, or they have the money to buy, or they are well educated and are able to read. The implication of this finding is that the overwhelming majority of Ghanaian women do not show interest in newspapers or magazines. This finding agrees with previous studies, which found that less educated women read a newspaper or magazine at least once a week, suggesting older women watch more TV [42]. On the contrary, this outcome refuted a previous finding that 12.3% of women reported reading newspapers or magazines almost every day and 41.1% at least once a week [43]. This finding emphasizes an urgent intervention that focuses on easy accessibility, relevance (content reflecting women’s lives), and empowerment (literacy/training), including subsidies for print, and promoting digital access incentives.

The analysis of women’s radio listening found that 57.2% had listened to the radio at least once a week prior to the study. This finding refuted a previous study, which found that radio was the most frequently accessed among both women and men (17% and 29%, respectively), followed by television (16% and 21%, respectively [44]. The implication of this outcome is that a significant proportion of Ghanaian women do not have access to the radio or are non-news listening citizens. It is possible to explain that these women, instead of listening to the radio they either watch television or read newspapers. On the other hand, the 18.7% of individuals who reported not having listened to the radio at all prior to the study may have a reason for not wanting to be abreast of trending news in Ghana. This finding pinpoints a gap in exposure to this traditional mass media channel. It is possible to explain that a substantial percentage of the population may not be reached by radio-based health information on STIs. A plausible explanation for this finding is that the lack of radio use may be linked to younger age groups preferring digital media, limited access to radio devices, urban lifestyle patterns, or time constraints. It could also reflect a gradual shift away from radio toward alternative information sources such as the internet and television. Consequently, this outcome emphasizes the need for diversified communication strategies that combine traditional and digital media to ensure inclusive and effective dissemination of health messages. This outcome differs from a previous study, which found that 71.5% listen to the radio, 52.7% listen occasionally, and 37% listen frequently [45].

More importantly, it was revealed that more than half (60.6%) had watched television for at least once a week. This outcome refuted a previous study that found that 9.9% of participants had watched television at least once a week [46]. This finding suggests that most Ghanaian women do not all the time watch television. The plausible explanation for this finding could be that these women perhaps enjoy watching television, but do not always get the time to watch it. However, 19.3% indicated they had watched television less than once a week, which suggests they do find time among their daily packed activities to watch television. It could also be that they watch television all the time, but their television was spoiled prior to the survey. One could say that a significant proportion of Ghanaian women are interested in watching television. This finding underscores the need for policies to focus on creating content tailored to women’s needs (health, finance, empowerment), promoting gender-sensitive broadcasting, and potentially subsidizing access for low-income women. This finding refuted a previous study that found that 6.2% of participants had watched television less than once a week [46].

Implications

The study findings emphasize the following key policy implications to strengthen reproductive health communication in Ghana. First, given that women who did not use the internet at all still had significantly greater odds of STIs awareness, it is important to expand the use of radio, community durbars, churches, mosques, and women’s groups for STIs education, especially in rural and peri-urban areas. Further, it is crucial to integrate STIs messaging into existing maternal and child health programs to reach women who may not be digitally connected. Furthermore, it is important to collaborate with local opinion leaders, queen mothers, and community health volunteers to disseminate accurate STIs information. Second, the strong regional effects across most regions suggest that geographic and contextual factors such as access to health services, education, and media play a critical role in STIs awareness. Therefore, Policymakers should adopt a regionally differentiated STIs strategy rather than a one-size-fits-all approach.

Limitations

Although the current study advanced on how the region of residence and exposure to the media influence women’s STIs awareness. However, there are a few limitations that are worth mentioning. The lack of longitudinal data made the current study rely heavily on cross-sectional data, which could not establish causal effects. Moreover, reliance on self-reported data on media exposure and STIs awareness from the 2022 GDHS is subject to social desirability or recall bias, and the likelihood of this bias affecting the current study’s findings is high. Therefore, future studies should conduct qualitative studies in low-awareness regions to understand structural, cultural, and health system barriers.

Conclusion

Our study shows that region of residence, listening to the radio, watching television, and using the internet were associated with women’s STIs awareness. These findings highlight the need to expand media access nationwide to improve STIs awareness and prevention efforts in Ghana. Also, social media platforms like Facebook and WhatsApp have grown in popularity, and their role in disseminating STIs-related health messages are essential to improving women’s STIs awareness. Future research should explore why non-internet users show high awareness, examining the role of radio, health workers, and social networks.
