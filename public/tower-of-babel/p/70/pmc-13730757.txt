Association Between Ratio of Cholesterols and Coronary Artery Stenosis According to Low-Density Lipoprotein-Cholesterol Levels and Statin Use
Authors: So Ree Kim, Mi-Na Kim, Hee-Dong Kim, Dong-Hyuk Cho, Hack-Lyoung Kim, Yong Hyun Kim, Jin Oh Na, Hyun-Ju Yoon, Mi Seung Shin, Myung-A Kim, Kyung-Soon Hong, Wan Joo Shim, Seong-Mi Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Author's summaryThis study investigated the association between cholesterol ratios and coronary artery disease (CAD) in 2,410 patients with suspected angina, considering statin use and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels. CAD was present in 36.5%, and 52% were on statins. Cholesterol ratios—including total cholesterol/high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), non-HDL-C/HDL-C, and LDL-C/HDL-C—showed significant associations with CAD. These associations remained consistent regardless of statin use or LDL-C levels. Incorporation of cholesterol ratios provided modest improvement in model discrimination, supporting their role as supplementary indicators in assessing CAD risk.

INTRODUCTION

Dyslipidemia is one of the main risk factors for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD).1)2) Small dense low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) particles mainly carry cholesterol in plasma and cause plaque formation by penetrating through endothelial cell membrane when vascular endothelium is injured.3) As high levels of LDL-C were proven to be closely related to ASCVD in the vast epidemiological studies4) and the clinical benefits of lowering LDL-C were solidly identified,5) guidelines for the management of cardiovascular disease risk have defined LDL-C levels as the principal target for lipid-lowering therapy6)7) and LDL-C has been the main trigger to start lipid-lowering therapy.8)

However, several scoring systems that predict future risk of developing ASCVD do not consider statin usage, while their total cholesterol level can be largely affected by statin.9)10)11)12) In addition, other lipid parameters, such as non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (non-HDL-C), total cholesterol/HDL-C ratio, and LDL-C/HDL-C ratio, have gained attention as alternative treatment targets of ASCVD.13)14) In addition, on-treatment levels of non-HDL-C, apolipoprotein (apo) B, total cholesterol/HDL-C ratio, LDL-C/HDL-C ratio, and apo B/A-I ratio were associated with future ASCVD occurrence.15) Despite achieving their primary LDL-C target goal, patients still have a residual risk of developing coronary artery disease (CAD). However, the additive roles of the cholesterol ratio in relation to the presence of CAD are not well understood. Using a contemporary Korean multicenter registry, we aimed to evaluate the association between lipid parameters and CAD in patients with suspected angina, considering LDL-C levels and statin use.

METHODS

Ethical statement

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of each center (Korea University Anam Hospital [Seoul, Korea]: 2021AN0011). Written informed consent was obtained from each subject for registry participation.

Materials and methods

The data of this study were collected from the database of the KoRean wOmen’S chest pain rEgistry (KoROSE). The KoROSE is a prospective nationwide registry from 21 cardiovascular centers that aims to investigate the characteristics and outcomes of South Korean women with or without CAD.16) At participating sites, consecutive patients with chest pain aged 20 years or over, who were referred for elective invasive coronary angiography, were enrolled. Patients with the acute coronary syndrome were not eligible for study participation. To identify sex differences, men has been also enrolled in this registry since October 2013. Patients with end-stage renal disease, chronic obstructive lung disease, primary pulmonary hypertension, malignancy, and autoimmune disease were excluded. For a decade, from April 2012 to May 2022, a total of 3,397 patients were registered on the database. After excluding patients without data on coronary angiography (n=319), patients without data on anti-lipid agents (n=126), and patients enrolled before October 2013 (n=533, during when only women were enrolled), total 2,410 patients were analyzed.

Clinical data on demographics, including age and body mass index (kg/m2), history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and smoking status, were collected. Waist circumference, blood pressure, and heart rate were measured by a trained nurse. Hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure of 140 mmHg or more, or diastolic blood pressure of 90 mmHg or more, a previous history of hypertension, or taking anti-hypertensive medications. Diabetes mellitus was defined as a prior history of diabetes mellitus or taking anti-glycemic agents. Dyslipidemia was defined as a previous history of dyslipidemia or taking anti-lipid medications, at least one of following criteria: total cholesterol ≥240 mg/dL; LDL-C ≥160 mg/dL; triglycerides ≥200 mg/dL; HDL-C <40 mg/dL in men, <50 mg/dL in women. Obesity was defined as body mass index ≥25 kg/m2, and central obesity was defined as waist circumference ≥85 cm in women and ≥90 cm in men. Concomitant cardiovascular medications, including aspirin, renin-angiotensin system blockers, beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, and lipid lowering therapy including statins, were also reviewed.

Blood samples were collected after overnight fasting to assess major laboratory parameters, including total cholesterol, triglycerides, HDL-C, and LDL-C. LDL-C levels were directly measured when available. For patients with missing LDL-C data, LDL-C was calculated using Friedewald equation: Calculated LDL-C = Total Cholesterol − (HDL-C + Triglycerides/5), provided that triglyceride levels were <400 mg/dL.17) Non-HDL-C was calculated as total minus HDL-C. Total cholesterol/HDL-C ratio, non-HDL-C/HDL-C ratio, triglycerides/HDL-C ratio, and LDL-C/HDL-C ratio were also calculated.

Individuals of the study population were evaluated for the primary LDL-C treatment target goal, as defined in Korean guidelines for the management of dyslipidemia.6)18) The LDL-C target goals were defined according to risk categories as follows: LDL-C <70 mg/dL for patients with atherosclerotic ischemic stroke, transient ischemic attack, carotid artery disease, peripheral artery disease, abdominal aortic aneurysm, or diabetes with major cardiovascular risk factors, target organ damage, or a disease duration of ≥10 years; LDL-C <100 mg/dL for those with diabetes without major cardiovascular risk factors and with a disease duration of <10 years; LDL-C <130 mg/dL for individuals at moderate risk (defined as having ≥2 major risk factors); and LDL-C <160 mg/dL for those at low risk (0–1 major risk factor). Major cardiovascular risk factors were defined as: age ≥45 years in men, ≥55 years in women; family history of premature cardiovascular disease; hypertension; smoking; low HDL-C (<40 mg/dL in men, <50 mg/dL in women). As all patients enrolled in this registry had no previously diagnosed cardiovascular disease, patients with a target LDL-C <70 mg/dL were those with diabetes and additional cardiovascular risk factors. All data were entered into an electronic web-based computerized database.

Invasive coronary angiography was performed using the standard technique, and all management strategies for CAD, including coronary revascularization, were chosen at the attending physician’s discretion. An CAD was defined as any ≥50% stenosis of epicardial coronary arteries. The extent of CAD was classified as 1-, 2-, or 3-vessel disease. Left main disease was defined by ≥50% stenosis of left main coronary artery.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed variables, median (interquartile range) for non-normally distributed variables, and categorical variables were summarized as frequencies with corresponding percentages. Comparisons between categorical variables were performed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test, depending on the distribution.

To evaluate the association with the presence of CAD in patients with suspected angina and to account for potential multicollinearity among lipid parameters, we performed a multivariable logistic regression analysis using principal component analysis (PCA)-derived components. We selected covariates that were either statistically significant in univariable analysis or deemed clinically relevant to CAD. Specifically, age, sex, obesity, hypertension, diabetes, smoking status, statin use, and lipid parameters were consistently included.19) Principal components, which are uncorrelated linear combinations of the original lipid variables, were extracted after adjusting for relevant covariates (PCA-augmented model). Variance inflation factors were calculated to evaluate multicollinearity, and all variance inflation factors in the final model were below 5, indicating acceptable collinearity levels.

The discriminatory performance of the logistic regression models for identifying CAD was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic curve analysis, and the area under the curve (AUC) was compared using DeLong’s test. To assess the incremental discriminative ability of models including a single principal lipid component compared to models with individual lipid variables, we compared PCA-only model with PCA-augmented model, by calculating the net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI). Additionally, subgroup analyses were conducted to determine whether the associations between lipid-related variables and CAD persisted among patients with LDL-C within target ranges. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 18.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.0.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A 2-sided p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Among a total of 2,410 patients with suspected angina, 1,448 (60.1%) were women (Table 1). The mean age was 63 years old, and 61.0%, 75.5%, and 25.4% had hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes, respectively. Half of the total patients were taking statins (1,253 [52.0%]) for primary prevention of ASCVD. Statin users were older, and had more co-morbidities including hypertension and diabetes than non-users. In statin non-users, only 30 (2.6%) patients were taking lipid lowering medications other than statin.

Table 2 shows laboratory data according to statin use. Statin users showed lower total cholesterol, LDL-C, and non-HDL-C compared to non-users. The ratio of cholesterols was also lower in statin users than non-users. As statin-users had more cardiovascular risk factors, they were more likely to have a lower target level for LDL-C compared to non-users (target <70 mg/dL: 32.6% vs. 15.7%). To address clinical relevance, we analyzed lipid parameters in 1,440 (59.8%) patients with LDL-C within target ranges. A high total cholesterol/HDL-C ratio and LDL-C/HDL-C ratio were significantly associated with the presence of CAD (Table 5). Patients with LDL-C within target ranges but with persistently elevated cholesterol ratios were predominantly younger men, often without hypertension or diabetes, but with other metabolic syndrome–related risk factors (Supplementary Tables 5 and 6). This subgroup demonstrated lower rates of statin use compared with their counterparts. Similar associations between cholesterol ratios and CAD were observed across various subgroups (Supplementary Figure 2).

DISCUSSION

In this large, real-world Korean registry of patients with suspected angina, approximately one-third were diagnosed with CAD, with a higher frequency among statin users. The proportion of patients with LDL-C within target ranges was similar between statin users and non-users, despite lower LDL-C levels in those receiving therapy. Several cholesterol ratios, including total cholesterol/HDL-C, non-HDL-C/HDL-C, and LDL-C/HDL-C, were associated with the presence of CAD. These associations remained consistent regardless of statin use or LDL-C levels. The total cholesterol/HDL-C and non-HDL-C/HDL-C ratios moderately improved model performance, while the LDL-C/HDL-C ratio mildly improved model performance.

LDL-C remains the strongest validated therapeutic target in current guidelines. However, discriminative values of other lipid indexes have been demonstrated in previous studies. In post-hoc analyses of major lipid-lowering trials, individuals with elevated non-HDL-C15) or triglyceride levels20) had an increased risk of coronary heart disease, independent of LDL-C levels. In a large prospective study of over 20,000 healthy participants, even among those with low LDL-C (<100 mg/dL), a higher total cholesterol/HDL-C ratio was associated with a 2-fold greater risk of developing CAD during 11 year of follow-up.13) These findings are supported by pathophysiological insights: under normolipidemic conditions, LDL-C is closely correlated with non-HDL-C, but in the presence of triglyceride-rich lipoproteins, apoB and non-HDL-C may better reflect cardiovascular risk. This imbalance becomes particularly relevant when triglyceride-rich lipoproteins (remnants) are elevated, as in metabolic syndrome or diabetes, where LDL-C levels may appear at target while apoB, non-HDL-C, and remnants remain high—a hallmark of residual risk.

Statin has consistently been proven effective in reducing cardiovascular events in large, randomized trials and are widely prescribed for the prevention of ASCVD.21)22)23) In our registry, statin users were older, had more hypertension and diabetes, and were more frequently diagnosed with CAD despite lower on-treatment LDL-C levels compared with non-users. This likely reflects treatment indications bias, whereby patients at higher baseline risk are more often prescribed statins. These findings highlight that, even under statin therapy, residual risk of CAD remains and should be further investigated in prospective studies.

Lipid parameters in statin users should be interpreted in the context of on-treatment LDL-C levels. Prior post hoc analyses have reported that non-HDL-C and apoB may be more closely related to cardiovascular outcomes than LDL-C alone, and that lipid ratios incorporating HDL-C and apoA-I can provide additional prognostic information.15) In our registry, direct measurements of apoB and apoA-I were unavailable, which limits direct comparison with these prior findings. Nonetheless, statin users showed lower levels of apoB-containing lipoprotein cholesterol (e.g., LDL-C, non-HDL-C) compared with non-users, while HDL-C levels remained relatively stable. As a result, cholesterol ratios with HDL-C in the denominator may be reduced disproportionately in statin users compared to non-users. This differential effect can partly explain why the association between cholesterol ratios and CAD may appear attenuated or altered across statin strata.

In our study, patients with LDL-C within target ranges but with persistently elevated cholesterol ratios were more often younger men, with fewer cases of hypertension and diabetes but features of metabolic risk and lower rates of statin use. A ratio of apoB-containing lipoprotein cholesterol to HDL-C approximates the balance between atherogenic burden and anti-atherogenic capacity. A higher total/HDL-C or LDL-C/HDL-C ratio reflects a greater circulating burden of cholesterol carried by atherogenic lipoproteins, which can promote endothelial dysfunction and initiate atherogenesis. Conversely, lower ratios indicate reduced vascular exposure to plasma cholesterol and more efficient reverse cholesterol transport, thereby contributing to a lower risk of cardiovascular disease.24)

This study has several limitations. First, the analysis was based on cross-sectional registry data, which precludes causal inference between lipid parameters, medication use, and the development of CAD. The findings should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, rather than providing prognostic or therapeutic implications, and require validation in prospective cohorts. Nevertheless, a key strength of this study is the use of a large, real-world Korean multicenter registry of patients with suspected angina with confirmed angiographic findings. Second, the long data collection period (2012–2022) may have introduced variability, as treatment guidelines and statin prescription patterns changed over time. Third, baseline LDL-C levels before statin initiation were unavailable, making it impossible to evaluate the relative reduction in LDL-C, a key therapeutic target in current guidelines.25) Information on statin dosage and duration was also lacking, limiting assessment of dose-response relationships. Fourth, direct comparison of OR magnitudes across differently scaled variables should be interpreted with caution. Our results should be considered in the context of clinical relevance and the associations observed, rather than as definitive comparisons of predictive strength between parameters. Fifth, although LDL-C was directly measured in most cases, it was estimated by the Friedewald equation when triglycerides were <400 mg/dL, which may have underestimated LDL-C values in some patients. Finally, advanced lipid markers such as apoB, apoA-I, and lipoprotein(a) were not available in this registry, restricting comparison with cholesterol ratios.

In this large, real-world Korean registry of patients with suspected angina, cholesterol ratios showed associations with the presence of CAD. These associations remained consistent regardless of statin use or LDL-C levels. This registry highlights the importance of real-world data in understanding contemporary lipid management and CAD presentation in Korea, and may inform future prospective research.
