Trends in Vascular Risk Factor Control Among US Adults With Prior Stroke: 1999 to 2023
Authors: Wells Andres, Aaron Rothstein, Sabrina Abbruzzese, Eric Stulberg, Connor A. Law, Santosh Murthy, Hooman Kamel, Kelly L. Sloane, Andrea L.C. Schneider
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundManagement of modifiable risk factors is key in the prevention of recurrent stroke. We investigated trends in vascular risk factors (hyperglycemia, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, and smoking) control among US adults with stroke.MethodsWe performed analyses of data from 1999 through 2023 National Health and Nutrition Examination Surveys participants with self‐reported stroke. Meeting guideline‐recommended targets for risk factor control was defined as hemoglobin A1c <6.5% without diabetes or hemoglobin A1c <7.0% with diabetes, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol <70 mg/dL, blood pressure <130/80 mm Hg, and nonsmoking. Age‐standardized mean hemoglobin A1c, low‐density lipoprotein, and blood pressure and prevalence of meeting each recommended target were calculated for each epoch (1999 through 2002, 2003 through 2006, 2007 through 2010, 2011 through 2014, 2015 through 2020, and 2021 through 2023).ResultsMean hemoglobin A1c increased from 5.7% (95% CI, 5.5–5.9) in 1999 through 2002 to 6.0% (95% CI, 5.7–6.3) in 2021 through 2023 (P‐trend=0.04) and LDL decreased from 125.9 mg/dL (95% CI, 119.4–132.4) in 1999 through 2002 to 115.6 mg/dL (95% CI, 107.8–123.5) in 2021 through 2023 (P‐trend<0.001), whereas blood pressure was largely stable. The prevalence of meeting glycemia guidelines decreased from 90.7% (95% CI, 85.9%–95.6%) in 1999 through 2002 to 83.9% (95% CI, 70.2%–97.5%) in 2021–2023 (P‐trend=0.03), whereas the prevalence of meeting lipid guidelines increased from 1999 through 2002 (5.1% [95% CI, 0%–11.0%]) to 2021 through 2023 (13.6% [95% CI, 6.9%–20.4%; P‐trend=0.002). The prevalence of meeting smoking and blood pressure guidelines was stable. Fewer than 10% of individuals met guidelines for all 4 risk factors over the study.ConclusionsIn this nationally representative sample of US adults with stroke, <10% met guideline recommendations for all risk factors with an overall stable trend. These results suggest that efforts aimed at glycemia, blood pressure, and lipid control and smoking cessation could be improved to reduce the burden of recurrent stroke.

The prevention of recurrent stroke relies on effective management of key modifiable risk factors, including hyperglycemia, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, and smoking tobacco. However, control of vascular risk factors and attainment of guideline‐recommended targets among stroke survivors and among US adults overall have been poor despite advances in the treatment of hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 Prior studies have investigated risk factors individually or over broader epochs, but there is a need to understand nationally representative temporal trends in the cumulative control of these risk factors among individuals with a prior stroke. Among young adults with stroke, the prevalence of hyperlipidemia and hypertension increased from 2003 through 2012 and achievement of recommended glycemic targets stagnated from 2001 through 2018 in the overall US population. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Among stroke survivors, smoking prevalence remained unchanged from 1999 through 2018, even as smoking rates in the general population declined, 6 and in a prior analysis based on the American Heart Association's Life's Simple 7 guidelines, few individuals with stroke met all metrics between 1988 and 2014. 3 Here, using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), we investigated contemporary trends in vascular risk factor control among US adults with prior stroke from 1999 through 2023.

Methods

Data Availability

The NHANES data are publicly available at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/.

Study Population and Stroke Definition

The NHANES is a series of cross‐sectional surveys administered by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. From 1999 through 2018, data were collected biennially. The 2019 to 2020 survey cycle was suspended early (in March 2020) due to the COVID‐19 pandemic, with data collection resuming in August 2021 for the most recently available 2‐year cycle (2021–2023). 9 Participants are selected from the civilian, noninstitutionalized population of the US using a complex, stratified, multistage, probability‐clustered sampling design. The NCHS institutional review board approved the study protocol, and all participants provided written informed consent. 10

The present analysis includes all 2778 participants in NHANES from the 1999 through 2023 survey cycles aged ≥20 years who self‐reported a history of (all‐cause) stroke. Stroke was ascertained using the question, “Has a doctor or other health professional ever told you that you had a stroke?” 11

Stroke Risk Factors

Participants were asked to self‐report a history of medical conditions, including hypertension (yes, no; ascertained from the question, “Have you ever been told by a doctor or other health professional that you had hypertension, also called high blood pressure?”), 12 hyperlipidemia (yes, no; ascertained from the question, “Have you ever been told by a doctor or other health professional that your blood cholesterol level was high?”), 12 diabetes (yes, no; ascertained from the question, “[Other than during pregnancy], have you ever been told by a doctor or health professional that you have diabetes or sugar diabetes?”), 7 and smoking (categorized as current, former, never; based on questions inquiring about having smoked 100+ cigarettes in lifetime). 6

Risk factor control data included systolic (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP), fasting low‐density lipoprotein (LDL), and hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c). Blood pressure was measured 3 consecutive times after resting quietly for 5 minutes; if a measurement was incomplete or interrupted, a fourth measurement was performed (auscultation protocol using a mercury sphygmomanometer in 1999–2018 and oscillometric protocol using an Omron HEM‐907 XL device in 2017–2023). 13 The mean of the first 3 SBP and DBP measurements was used in the analyses. Serum total cholesterol, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, and triglycerides were measured enzymatically. Triglycerides and LDL cholesterol measurements were available only in the fasting subsample (fasting >8 hours; n=1295 participants with stroke). LDL cholesterol was calculated according to the Friedewald calculation. 14 HbA1c was measured from whole‐blood samples using high‐performance liquid chromatography.

Guideline‐Recommended Targets

Guideline‐recommended targets for poststroke risk factor control were drawn from the 2021 American Heart Association Guidelines for the Prevention of Stroke in Patients With Stroke and Transient Ischemic Attack. 1 Meeting blood pressure guidelines was defined as SBP <130 mm Hg and DBP <80 mm Hg. Meeting lipid guidelines was defined as fasting LDL <70 mg/dL. Glycemic control was defined as HbA1c <6.5% for individuals without history of diabetes or HbA1c <7.0% for individuals with a history of diabetes. Never smoking or former smoking were considered to meet smoking guidelines. To account for the 2021 guidelines 1 being more aggressive in risk factor recommendations than were recommendations during the majority of the years included in the analyses, 15 we performed sensitivity analyses using the following definitions for meeting guideline‐recommended targets: SBP <140 mm Hg and DBP <90 mm Hg for blood pressure, 16 HbA1c <6.5% for individuals without history of diabetes or HbA1c <8.0% for individuals with a history of diabetes for glycemic control, 17 and LDL <100 mg/dL for lipids. 18

Other Variables

Self‐reported sociodemographic data included age (years; continuous and categorized as 20–39, 40–59, and ≥60 years), sex (male, female), race and ethnicity (non‐Hispanic White, non‐Hispanic Black, Mexican American, Other [Other includes all other races and ethnicities not otherwise specified], Asian American), education (less than high school, high school diploma or equivalent, some college or above), and family income‐to‐poverty ratio (<130%, 130%–350%, >350%). Body mass index (continuous; kg/m2) was calculated as measured weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed incorporating appropriate NCHS‐derived survey weights to account for NHANES sampling design and to make estimates reported here nationally representative of the civilian, noninstitutionalized population of US adults aged >20 years with self‐reported stroke from 1999 through 2023. 19 Standard errors were obtained using the Taylor series (linearization) method in accordance with analysis recommendations from the NCHS. All analyses were conducted using Stata SE version 19.5 (StataCorp, College Station, TX).

To increase the precision of estimates and minimize the effects of the small sample size, data from surveys conducted between 1999 and 2023 were grouped into the following epochs (in accordance with NHANES analytic and reporting guidelines) 9 , 19 : 1999 through 2002, 2003 through 2006, 2007 through 2010, 2011 through 2014, 2015 through 2020, and 2021 through 2023. Multiple imputation by chained equations with 100 sets of imputations was used to account for missing data under the missing‐at‐random assumption. The following variables contained missing data that were imputed: hypertension (n=9), hyperlipidemia (n=214), diabetes (n=102), smoking (n=5), HbA1c (n=450), SBP (n=463), DBP (n=463), fasting LDL cholesterol (n=314), education (n=9), family income‐to‐poverty ratio (n=306), and body mass index (n=476). The following variables contained no missing data but were included in the imputation models: stroke, epoch, survey weight, age, sex, and race and ethnicity. Complete case analyses were performed as sensitivity analyses.

Participant characteristics were calculated for each epoch as weighted means±SEs for continuous variables and as weighted proportions and SEs for categorical variables. Weighted age‐standardized mean values (95% CIs) for HbA1c, LDL cholesterol, SBP, and DBP were calculated for each epoch overall and stratified by diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and hypertension diagnoses, respectively. Weighted age‐standardized prevalence (95% CI) of meeting guideline‐recommended targets was calculated for glycemic, lipid, and blood pressure control and smoking for each epoch. The age‐standardized distribution of the number of guideline‐recommended targets met was also calculated for each epoch. In accordance with NCHS analytic recommendations, estimates were age‐standardized to the 2000 US population using census data and the following age categories: 20 to 39, 40 to 59, and ≥60 years. 20 Weighted linear regression models adjusted for age group were used to evaluate age‐adjusted differences in HbA1c, LDL cholesterol, SBP, and DBP between epochs and to evaluate age‐adjusted linear trends over time across the midpoint of each epoch. Similarly, weighted logistic and ordinal logistic regression adjusted for age group were used to evaluate age‐adjusted differences in the proportion of participants meeting guideline‐recommended targets between epochs and to evaluate age‐adjusted linear over time trends across the midpoint of each epoch.

Results

Between 1999 and 2023, the mean age of NHANES participants aged 20 years and older with a history of stroke was 64.8 years, and 56.0% were women. The proportion of stroke survivors with comorbid diabetes and hyperlipidemia increased from 1999 through 2002 to 2021 through 2023 (P‐linear trend=0.005 and <0.001, respectively), while hypertension and current smoking were stable (P‐linear trend=0.91 and 0.65, respectively; Table 1).

The age‐standardized prevalence of individuals meeting current guideline recommendations for glycemic control decreased from 90.7% (95% CI, 85.9%–95.6%) in 1999 through 2002 to 83.9% (95% CI, 70.2%–97.5%) in 2021 through 2023 (P‐linear trend=0.03; Figure 2). The age‐standardized prevalence of individuals meeting current guideline recommendations for smoking was stable at >60% from 1999 through 2002 to 2021 through 2023 (P‐linear trend=0.56). The age‐standardized prevalence of individuals meeting current guidelines for lipid control increased over time (P‐linear trend=0.002) from 1999 through 2002 (5.1% [95% CI, 0%–11.0%]) to 2021 through 2023 (13.6% [95% CI, 6.9%–20.4%]). Although the age‐standardized prevalence of individuals meeting current guideline recommendations for blood pressure control was similar in 1999 through 2002 compared with 2021 through 2023 (P=0.14), there was evidence of an increase in the age‐standardized prevalence from 1999 through 2002 (46.0% [95% CI, 33.2%–58.8%]) to 2011 through 2014 (68.2% [95% CI, 59.2%–77.1%]; P<0.001 compared with 1999 through 2002) that was followed by a decrease to 2021 through 2023 (37.1% [95% CI, 17.2%–56.9%]; P=0.003 compared with 2011 through 2014). In contrast with the primary definition for meeting guideline recommendations for glycemic control, the age‐standardized prevalence for the secondary definition was stable at >90% over time (P‐linear trend=0.99). The age‐standardized prevalence over time for the secondary definitions for meeting guideline recommendations for lipid and blood pressure control was similar to the primary definitions (Figure 2). Discussion

In a nationally representative sample of US adults with a history of self‐reported stroke between 1999 and 2023, our results show that the overall prevalence of meeting current guideline recommendations for major stroke risk factors was largely stable, with <10% of stroke survivors meeting current guideline recommendations for all 4 risk factors (hyperglycemia, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, and smoking).

Over the study period, the prevalence of meeting current glycemic control guideline recommendations was the highest among the 4 risk factors evaluated at >80%, although the prevalence did decrease over time. Mean HbA1c levels increased overall and among individuals without diabetes. In contrast, among individuals with a history of diabetes, the mean HbA1c value was stable over time. These results among stroke survivors are consistent with findings from a prior paper reporting a lower prevalence of glycemic control in the general population, but are in contrast with this prior paper's findings of a decline in glycemic control among individuals with diabetes over time. 21 This difference may be attributable to differential monitoring or treatment of hyperglycemia in survivors of stroke with diabetes compared with the broader population of all individuals with diabetes.

The prevalence of meeting current guideline recommendations for lipid control was consistently the lowest among the 4 risk factors evaluated, regardless of the definition used (LDL <70 versus <100 mg/dL). However, consistent with prior work, 12 the mean measured LDL significantly decreased from 1999 through 2002, to 2015 through 2023 among survivors of stroke, although not to current guideline‐recommended levels. In 2006, the SPARCL (Stroke Prevention by Aggressive Reduction in Cholesterol Levels) trial 22 was published, demonstrating decreased rates of recurrent stroke and cardiovascular events with high‐intensity statin therapy, and both ezetimibe (2004) and the first PCSK9 (proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9) inhibitor, alirocumab (2015), were approved for the treatment of hyperlipidemia. 22 , 23 However, limited early uptake and accessibility barriers to PCSK9 inhibitors may have constrained their impact during 2015 through 2023. There was also a change in the guidelines in 2014 recommending more aggressive hyperlipidemia management than was done previously. 15 The trend in lipid control we show by measured LDL suggests that the widespread adoption of statin use and the advent of new classes of lipid‐lowering medication have played a significant role in improving lipid management, although further work remains to be done.

Before the 2017 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guidelines for the prevention, detection, evaluation, and management of hypertension, blood pressure targets for primary and secondary prevention of stroke were <140/90 mm Hg. 15 , 24 We found similar patterns over time in the prevalence of meeting guideline recommendations for hypertension regardless of the definition used (<130/80 versus <140/90 mm Hg). Our findings extend upon prior work in the NHANES that found improved blood pressure control between 1999 and 2016 in the overall US population. 21 , 25 , 26 Among stroke survivors, we found an increase in prevalence of meeting blood pressure control guidelines in 2011 through 2014, followed by a decrease in 2021 through 2023 to levels similar to 1990 through 2002; a similar nonlinear trend was also shown in prior work among individuals with diagnosed diabetes. 21 Mansoor et al also found that proportions of survivors of stroke achieving blood pressure control goals decreased in NHANES over their study period from 53.3% in 2009 through 2010 to 38.6% in 2017 through 2020. 27 However, their estimates and methods differed from ours in several ways. They performed a complete case analysis, while our primary analyses used multiple imputation by chained equations to account for missing data. Moreover, our analysis extends the years evaluated to 1999 through 2023 and applies stricter, current guideline‐based thresholds (ie, blood pressure <130/80 versus <140/80 mm Hg). Under these contemporary definitions, <10% met all 4 targets across 1999 through 2023, compared with ≈11% reported by Mansoor et al over 2009 through 2020 using less stringent definitions. 27

The age‐standardized prevalence of meeting current guideline recommendations for smoking was stable in our population over time. These stable smoking results are consistent with prior work that showed stable prevalence of smoking among survivors of stroke in the NHANES, despite well‐documented decreases in the general population. 6 , 28

Our study has limitations that should be taken into consideration. Although this sample is nationally representative, it draws only from the civilian, noninstitutionalized population; thus, survivors of stroke in rehabilitation centers or nursing facilities are excluded, limiting the generalizability of our findings. However, secondary stroke prevention in the noninstitutionalized population is of particular importance as this population is at particular risk of severe disability from recurrent stroke events. Second, the self‐report nature of medical history, including ascertainment of stroke history, in the NHANES may result in an underestimation of stroke. In prior work, self‐reported history of stroke has been shown to have a sensitivity of 75% and a specificity of 98%. 29 Self‐reported data did not include information about stroke type (ischemic versus hemorrhagic), so investigation by stroke type could not be undertaken. It is important to consider our all‐cause stroke results in the context of prior work using US data from the Global Burden of Disease study, which suggested that the age‐standardized prevalence of ischemic stroke decreased while the age‐standardized prevalence of hemorrhagic stroke increased from 1999 through 2021, 30 though ischemic stroke remained the majority (≈87% of all strokes in 2020). 31 While current recommendations for smoking, glycemia, and blood pressure management are comparable between ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke, 1 , 32 lipid recommendations are not a formal part of hemorrhagic stroke recommendations. 32 Third, the number of years included in each epoch was not the same over the entire study period due to COVID‐19–related disruptions, and we acknowledge that the precision of estimates in the 2‐year epoch is lower than the precision of estimates in the other epochs. Finally, while we used multiple imputation by chained equations to reduce bias due to missing data (and results were similar to our complete case sensitivity analyses), residual bias remains possible.

Conclusions

Our results suggest that there is a high (>80%) but declining prevalence of meeting current guideline recommendations for glycemic control among survivors of stroke but lower prevalence of meeting current guideline recommendations for blood pressure control, smoking, and, in particular, lipid control. There is ample opportunity to target elevated glycemia, blood pressure, and hyperlipidemia, as well as smoking, as a means to reduce recurrent stroke burden in the United States. In addition to the development of innovative treatments for secondary stroke prevention, future stroke research and practice should also include pragmatic trials and community‐based studies as a means of testing interventions to improve secondary stroke prevention guideline adherence.
