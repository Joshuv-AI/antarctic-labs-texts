Comprehensive Benchmark Study of Diffusion-Based 3D Molecular Generation Models
Authors: Yifei Qin, Xuexin Wei, Mingyuan Xu, Jiaqiang Wu, Miru Tang, Ting Ran, Hongming Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Deep diffusion models have emerged as powerful tools for de novo molecular generation in three-dimensional (3D) conformational space, leveraging forward noising and reverse denoising processes to learn the probability distributions of molecular geometries and their physicochemical properties. However, systematic comparisons of their performance remain limited. Here, we present a comprehensive benchmark of nine state-of-the-art diffusion-based 3D molecular generative models trained on two representative data sets, QM9 and GEOM-Drugs. Evaluation was performed using four types of metrics, encompassing both two-dimensional (2D) structural features and 3D geometric characteristics. Our results demonstrate that nearly all models perform worse on 3D metrics compared to 2D metrics. Most generated 3D structures exhibit significant deviations from the energy-minimized reference, highlighting the persistent challenges in achieving accurate 3D spatial modeling. Furthermore, their effectiveness declines when producing 3D structures of larger and more complex molecules. Among the models, MiDi and EQGAT-diff consistently outperform the other models, with MiDi showing particularly robust performance. In summary, this study highlights recent advances in deep diffusion models for 3D molecular generation while delineating the key challenges that need to be addressed by next-generation approaches.

Introduction

Deep diffusion models constitute a class of generative models that approximate complex data distributions by defining a forward Markovian diffusion process, which progressively perturbs data with noise, and by learning its corresponding reverse denoising process to reconstruct samples. Compared with approaches such as variational autoencoder (VAE), generative adversarial networks (GANs), recurrent neural networks (RNNs), convolutional neural networks (CNNs), or plain Transformers, diffusion models produce more detailed and diverse outputs, avoid issues like mode collapse common in GANs, and provide more stable training. , Over the past few years, deep diffusion models have garnered significant attention in drug design. By simulating the evolution of molecules in physical and chemical spaces, these models can efficiently explore the vast drug-like molecular space. However, the absence of systematic and objective evaluation benchmarks poses a major challenge in comparing the performance of different models. Addressing this gap is crucial for advancing the field and ensuring the reliability of these innovative approaches.

Deep diffusion models have demonstrated significant potential in addressing challenges related to two-dimensional (2D) and three-dimensional 3D molecular representations and complex interactions during molecular generation. Among these, the equivariant diffusion model (EDM) stands out as a leading approach for de novo 3D molecule generation. As its name suggests, EDM utilizes an equivariant diffusion mechanism to preserve the geometric symmetry of generated 3D molecules. However, it faces limitations in molecular uniqueness, often producing structurally redundant molecules, and in structural novelty, with generated structures closely resembling those in the training set. Additionally, EDM struggles to generate the complete molecular structure. To address the issue of structural completeness, the Geometry-Complete Diffusion Model (GCDM) introduces reinforced geometric constraints. However, GCDM shares similar shortcomings with EDM in terms of structural uniqueness and novelty. Both models also exhibit low structural validity, frequently generating molecules that violate fundamental chemical rules, a drawback that is not often seen in general 2D molecular generative models. In response to these challenges, Peng et al. proposed MolDiff, a model that outperforms EDM and GCDM in terms of chemical validity by explicitly constraining the interrelationships between atoms and bonds during the generation process. While MolDiff improves the rationality of generated molecules in both 2D and 3D aspects, it still falls short of achieving structural diversity and faces challenges with the overabundance of sampled molecules. These limitations highlight the need for further advancements in diffusion-based molecular generation to enhance both diversity and validity.

The geometric latent diffusion model (GEOLDM) introduces an innovative approach by mapping molecular representations to a latent space, significantly enhancing the structural diversity of sampled molecules. Additionally, it improves sampling efficiency, although the quality of 3D conformations, particularly in terms of molecular stability, requires further refinement, especially for complex molecular structures. Another notable model, the molecular diffusion model (MDM), diverges from the 3D point cloud representations used in earlier models by incorporating interatomic relationships. MDM introduces a distributional controlling variable at each diffusion/reverse step to ensure the thorough exploration of the chemical space, leading to a marked improvement in the structural diversity of generated molecules. In contrast to the aforementioned models, which rely on predefined rules to derive molecular bonds from conformations, the MiDi model was proposed to simplify the generation process through an end-to-end differentiable framework. This approach simultaneously generates a molecular graph and its corresponding conformation, offering a more integrated and efficient solution for molecular generation. These advancements collectively highlight the ongoing progress in addressing key challenges in molecular generation, such as diversity, stability, and efficiency. MolFM (Equivariant Flow Matching), a groundbreaking molecular generation model introduced by Song et al., represents a significant advancement over traditional generative approaches. MolFM incorporates a novel and principled technology known as Equivariant Flow Matching (EquiFM). This innovation not only enhances the flexibility of selecting diverse probability paths tailored to different modalities but also significantly improves sampling speed. By leveraging EquiFM, MolFM offers a more efficient and adaptable framework for molecular generation, setting a new standard in the field. In addition, a model named JODO incorporates a diffusion graph transformer architecture to simulate the relationship between molecular graphs and geometries during the diffusion process. This model introduces a relational attention mechanism that enables explicit interactions between node and edge features while simultaneously propagating and updating scalar features and geometric vectors.

Despite the proliferation of diffusion-based molecular generation models, a significant gap persists in the development of unified and comprehensive evaluation metrics to objectively assess the quality of generated molecules. Early models, such as EDM, MDM, and GCDM, primarily rely on conventional 2D/3D molecular evaluation metrics, including molecular validity, uniqueness, novelty, and stability, to evaluate the quality of generated 3D molecules. GEOLDM adopts a similar approach, while MolDiff advances its evaluation framework by integrating Jensen–Shannon divergence calculations between generated and training molecules, with a focus on structural parameters such as bond length, bond angle, and dihedral angle. MiDi further refines this by employing the Wasserstein distance to measure distributional differences, using metrics like BondLengthW1 to quantify discrepancies in bond length distributions. Although EquiFM was released more recently, it continues to rely on the same molecular evaluation metrics introduced in EDM. Additionally, JODO incorporates the MOSES benchmark, leveraging metrics such as Fréchet ChemNet Distance (FCD), Similarity to the Nearest Neighbor (SNN), Fragment Similarity (Frag), and Scaffold Similarity (Scaf). GuacaMol and MOSES are widely adopted evaluation frameworks in the field, serving as benchmark standards for molecular generation. However, these frameworks tend to place excessive emphasis on property optimization, potentially overlooking other critical aspects of 3D structural properties. Benchmarking studies on pocket-based 3D generative models have been reported recently, but they did not focus on ligand-based generative models. These underscore the urgent need for updated and more comprehensive evaluation frameworks that align with the rapid advancements in diffusion-based 3D molecular generation.

In this study, we systematically evaluated the latest diffusion-based molecular generation models trained on both small-molecule and large-molecule data sets. Our analysis employed a comprehensive set of 2D and 3D evaluation metrics, encompassing traditional measures such as molecular validity, uniqueness, novelty, and stability, as well as advanced Jensen–Shannon divergence to assess the distribution differences in bond lengths, bond angles, and dihedral angles. By comparing the performance of different models, we explored their strengths and limitations in 3D molecular generation, with a particular focus on structural accuracy and chemical feasibility.

Materials and Methods

Model Configuration

Nine diffusion-based 3D molecular generative models from three different categories, published between 2021 and 2024, were selected for this study (Table ). The main category is diffusion-based probabilistic models (DDPMs), which include EDM, GCDM, MolDiff, EQGAT-diff, GEOLDM, etc. Distinctly, MolFM incorporates equivariant flow matching in the diffusion process, and the JODO model employs the Stochastic Differential Equations to make the noise addition and removal processes more continuous. QM9 and GEOM-Drugs molecular sets were selected as the training data set. QM9 contains approximately 130,000 organic small molecules composed of only five elements: C, H, O, N, and F. Especially, the atomic number of molecules was smaller than 10, which makes QM9 an ideal benchmark for evaluating models generating basic small molecules. GEOM-Drugs includes larger and more complex molecules than QM9, often with heteroaromatic rings, multiple rotatable bonds, and diverse stereochemistry, which is suitable for benchmarking molecular generative models on realistic and high-complexity structures. Among the selected models, MDM and MolFM were trained only on the QM9 data set, while the remaining models were trained on both the QM9 and GEOM-Drugs data sets. Since official pretrained models were not provided for MDM, it was retrained following the methodology described in the original publication. The other models were directly obtained from their respective GitHub repositories and could be used without additional training.

To ensure the fairness of the evaluation, we kept all models in their default settings during the sampling process, with the exception of the same sampling size. Specifically, each model was required to generate 10,000 molecules to ensure that the data was sufficient to evaluate the generalization ability of all the models over chemical spaces.

Molecules extracted from the FDA-approved drugs in the DrugCentral-2023 database, named the FDA-Drug set, were used as an external reference set to calibrate the general drug-likeness of sampling sets of various generative models. Molecule filtering was conducted for this data set using stringent criteria to retain only organic small molecules. First, molecules with a heavy atom count between 5 and 60 were selected to ensure consistency with the molecular size range present in the QM9 and GEOM-Drugs data sets. Second, compounds containing inorganic or metallic elements were excluded.

2D Molecular Features

The generated molecules were characterized using a set of 2D topological descriptors, including the number of heavy atoms, chiral center, ring, aromatic ring, rotatable bond, and the proportion of sp3-hybridized carbon atoms (Fsp3). The distribution similarity of these structural features was compared between the generated molecules of the models and the reference set randomly extracted from their training set. The FDA-Drug set was also employed as an external reference set.

2D Molecular Similarity

2D molecular similarity between the generated compounds and those in the training set was subsequently evaluated using two established metrics: FCD.score and KL.score. Fréchet ChemNet distance (FCD) is calculated based on the latent representations of ChemNet, following a principle similar to the Fréchet Inception Distance commonly used in image generation models. Specifically, this method calculates the mean and covariance of the activations from the penultimate layer of ChemNet for both the reference molecule set and the generated molecule set. Then, FCD is computed using the Fréchet distance between these two sets of values. The FCD.score is derived from FCD, as shown in eq , where a larger FCD.score value indicates a higher similarity between the two sets of molecules. 6 FCD.score=e−0.2×FCD KL divergence was employed to compare the distribution of molecular descriptors between two molecular sets. The considered descriptors include BertzCT, MolLogP, MolWt, TPSA, NumHAcceptors, NumHDonors, NumRotatableBonds, NumAliphaticRings, and NumAromaticRings, as defined in Guacamol. Additionally, the maximum nearest-neighbor similarity (MNNS) of Morgan fingerprints for a molecule within a data set was used as a descriptor.

To model the distribution of continuous descriptors, kernel density estimation (KDE) was applied, while histograms were used to compute the probability distributions of discrete descriptors. These distributions were used to calculate the KL divergence for each descriptor. Finally, the KL.score was determined as the average KL divergence across all descriptors. A smaller KL.score value indicates a higher divergence of two data sets across all descriptors. 3D Structural Features

3D structural features include the statistical distribution of bond length, bond angle, and dihedral angle of the generated 3D conformations, whose quality is assessed by comparing them with those in the minimized 3D conformations for all generated molecules using the Jensen–Shannon divergence (JSD). Detailed information on bond types, bond angles, and dihedral angles is shown in Figure S1. The minimized conformations were generated using the LigPrep module in the Schrödinger software package (version 2020), with energy minimization performed under the OPLS3 force field. The JSD was calculated using the following equations, which are used to quantify the distance between two probability distributions. 8 M=(P+Q)2 9 JSD(P,Q)=12(Dkl(P||M)+Dkl(Q||M)) where P represents the probability distribution of 3D properties in the conformations generated by the model for a certain 3D property, while Q represents the distribution of the same properties in the minimized conformations. M denotes the average of P and Q. The Kullback–Leibler (KL) , divergence (D kl) is used to quantify the distribution differences of P and Q relative to M. Then, the average of the KL divergences of P vs M and Q vs M is used to represent the Jensen–Shannon divergence (JSD). A JSD value of 0 indicates that the distributions of P and Q are identical, while a value of 1 indicates the distributions are completely dissimilar.

Molecular stability was proposed by the EDM algorithm. First, atomic stability (Atom.stab) is defined as the ratio of stable atoms to the total number of atoms present in all generated molecules. A stable atom is one that adheres to valence-bond theory based on its bonding interactions with neighboring atoms in 3D space. The theoretic criteria for determining whether two atoms are bonded are detailed in Tables S1–S3. A molecule is considered stable if all its constituent atoms are stable. Molecular stability is then calculated as the percentage of stable molecules among the unique molecules in the generated set. Detailed definitions can be found in eqs and . 10 Atom.stab=numberofstableatomsinuniquemoleculestotalnumberofatomsinuniquemolecules 11 Mol.stab=stablemoleculesuniquemolecules In addition, we introduced two metrics to evaluate the coordinate RMS deviations (RMSDs) between each generated conformation and its corresponding low-energy conformation for every chemically valid molecule. The low-energy conformations were obtained in two ways: (1) by directly optimizing the generated conformations using the MMFF force field to generate a local minimum conformation and (2) by independently generating the global minimum conformation using the LigPrep method as mentioned before. The energy difference (ΔE) between generated and local minimum conformations was also evaluated, and molecules with ΔE < 10 kcal/mol were defined as energy-compliant. Furthermore, the torsional geometry of the molecules was evaluated using two metrics. The first metric is the torsion fingerprint deviation (TFD), which measures the torsional divergence between the generated and global minimum conformations. The second metric evaluates the aromatic planarity compliance rate, defined as the proportion of generated molecules whose aromatic rings fully satisfy the criteria of planarity. In this study, an aromatic ring was considered planar if all torsional angles defined by four atoms within the ring were smaller than 10°, and all bond angles defined by three atoms within the ring were smaller than 8°.

Results and Discussion

Performance on Metrics of General Molecular Quality

The general molecular quality was first evaluated for the models trained on the QM9 data set using six metrics: molecular validity, connectivity, uniqueness, novelty ratio, overall molecular diversity, and scaffold diversity (Table ). All models in general revealed extremely high molecular validity, with JODO, EQGAT-diff, and MDM reaching 99% validity. This suggests that all of these models can yield chemically plausible and viable structures. MolDiff exhibits the lowest validity and even lower than EDM, somehow in contrast to what was produced by their own evaluation. The MDM achieved 100% connectivity, which means that all generated molecules are formed by only a single molecular fragment. Other models including EDM and GCDM also did exceedingly well, all with connectivity reaching 98%. As for uniqueness, the EDM, GEOLDM, and MolFM are superior to other models unexpectedly. Relatively, the uniqueness values of MolDiff and MDM are smaller than 85%. All models had high novelty ratios over 90% except MolDiff and JODO, and the EQGAT-diff model outperformed the others at 94.86%. This indicates that most molecules generated by these models have distinct structures from those in the QM9 data set. The molecular diversity of generated molecules is higher than 0.9 across all models, with the leading model JODO, EDM, and MiDi scoring 0.92. These models also had extremely high scaffold diversity over 0.95. Overall, the diffusion-based 3D generative models have the ability to generate chemically plausible, novel, and diverse structures.

In addition to examining the overall structural quality of the generated molecules, it is critical to inspect the generation set on the topological structural aspects in order to properly evaluate their physical plausibility. Here, we examined the distribution of several 2D topological properties including the number of heavy atoms, chiral center, ring structure, aromatic ring and rotatable bond, and the ratio of sp3-hybridized carbon atom (Fsp3) (Figure ).

Figure A shows the distribution of heavy atom count. Overall, there are no significant differences in the distribution of heavy atoms among all of the models. Relatively, the GCDM shows the largest similarity with the reference set. The other models generate a subset of molecules with over 9 heavy atoms. Some molecules generated by the MolDiff model even have a fraction of molecules with more than 10 heavy atoms, which is outside the training set. Notably, the MDM generated molecules with fewer than 8 heavy atoms, which is not observed for other models, indicating that this model tends to generate smaller molecules compared to the reference set.

In terms of the number of chiral centers, no significant distributional discrepancies were observed across all models (Figure B). This indicates that the majority of models generate molecules with consistent chiral complexity. Notably, the JODO model has the highest similarity to that of the reference set. The number of rings also serves as a critical metric for assessing chemical structural complexity, given that most pharmacological compounds contain at least one ring structure. As illustrated in Figure C, the overall distributions are highly consistent, indicating comparable structural complexity for all models in terms of ring count. However, it is noteworthy that the GCDM and MDM exhibit a slightly higher ring count, suggesting their capability to generate molecules with a larger number of rings. As for the aromatic ring count, most models exhibit similar distributions to the reference set (Figure D). The majority of the generated molecules do not contain an aromatic ring, which is similar to the QM9 training set. It is important to emphasize that all models are trained on the QM9 data set, which primarily consists of small molecules with simple chemical structures and low aromaticity. The GCDM, EQGAT-diff, and MDM even failed to generate molecules with an aromatic ring. The analysis of rotatable bond count, a measure of molecular flexibility, demonstrates that the MolDiff and MDM models are two exceptions, as their generated molecules exhibit different distributions from that of the reference set (Figure E). Some MolDiff molecules have more than four rotatable bonds, while the MDM molecules in general have fewer rotatable bonds, likely attributable to their smaller molecular size. The ratio of sp3-hybridized carbon atoms (Fsp3) serves as an indicator of molecular planarity. The Fsp3 distribution of the JODO model closely mirrors that of the reference set (Figure F), while, in contrast, the MDM exhibits Fsp3 values approaching 1, indicating that the majority of carbon atoms in its generated molecules are sp3 hybridized. This distribution suggests that MDM-generated molecules are saturated and lack aromatic rings, consistent with the earlier findings regarding aromatic ring count.

Performance on Metrics of 2D Molecular Similarity

In this study, we introduced two metrics, named FCD.score and KL.score, to quantify the overall molecular similarity between the generated molecules and the molecules in the training set. The FCD.score measures the distance between two distributions in a high-dimensional chemical space characterized by the learned molecular embeddings derived from the ChemNet model, while the KL.score assesses the average distribution divergence of multiple properties between two data sets. Here, the distribution of both physicochemical descriptors and molecular fingerprint were integrated into this metrics.

For the models trained on the QM9 data set, the FCD.score and KL.score exhibit similar distribution patterns across the evaluated models (Figure A,B). MolDiff and MDM yield lower scores on both metrics, suggesting a greater discrepancy between the molecules they generate and those in the training set. In the case of GEOM-Drugs, all models achieve notably low FCD scores (Figure C), indicating a substantial divergence in the chemical space, characterized by the ChemNet model, between the generated and training sets. Moreover, these FCD scores are significantly lower than those observed for QM9, highlighting the models’ failure to capture the ChemNet-defined chemical distribution of the GEOM-Drugs data set. In contrast, KL scores for GEOM-Drugs generally exceed 0.4 (Figure D), reflecting a stronger capacity to learn molecular feature distributions that integrate physicochemical descriptors and molecular fingerprints. Notably, EQGAT-diff, MolDiff, and MiDi demonstrate comparable performance on the KL.score across the two data sets. By comparison, the KL.score of EDM, JODO, GELDM, and GCDM are substantially lower than those in the QM9 case, indicating a reduced ability for them to model the chemical space of large-sized molecules defined by physicochemical properties and molecular fingerprints.

Performance on Metrics of 3D Geometric Properties

In addition to assessing 2D-related metrics, the evaluation of 3D conformational quality is equally critical, as these 3D generation models are required to produce both 3D conformations and 2D graph structures during the generation process. To evaluate model performance on 3D geometric generation, we analyzed the differences in the distributions of bond lengths, bond angles, and dihedral angles between the generated 3D conformations and those optimized using the OPLS3 force field. The divergence was quantified using the Jensen–Shannon Divergence (JSD) index. A higher JSD value indicates a greater deviation between the generated and optimized conformations, reflecting the model’s limitations in capturing molecular 3D geometry.

For the models trained on the QM9 data set, most models achieved average JSD values for bond lengths ranging from 0.4 to 0.5, suggesting moderate deviations between the generated and optimized conformations (Table ). However, the MolDiff and MDM models exhibited significantly higher average JSD values (0.612 and 0.552, respectively), indicating their substantial discrepancies in bond length distributions. For the models trained on the GEOM-Drugs data set, the average JSD values generally increased across most models (Table ), with the exception of EQGAT-diff and MolDiff, highlighting the challenges in generating accurate 3D conformations for larger and more complex molecules. Notably, MolDiff’s JSD decreased while being trained on this data set compared to QM9, suggesting improved accuracy in bond length generation for more complex structures. In contrast, the EDM and GEOLDM showed significant increases in JSD values (above 0.6), underscoring their difficulties in learning complex 3D conformations.

Performance on Metrics of 3D Conformation Plausibility

To evaluation the conformation plausibility of generated molecules, we compared the RMSD of atom coordinates between the generated conformation and one low-energy conformation of each molecule. On one hand, the generated conformation was compared with the low-energy conformation obtained by energy minimization of itself. On the other hand, an independently generated low-energy conformation utilizing the conformation generation program was used as an additional reference. These two approaches were employed to assess how much the generated conformation diverges from the local minimum and global minimum conformations, respectively.

In comparison with the local minimum conformations, all models achieve a lower RMSD distribution for the molecules generated by QM9-trained models than those generated by GEOM-Drugs-trained models (Figure A,B). The main reason can be attributed to the much less structural complexity of the molecules in the QM9 data set compared to those in the GEOM-Drugs data set. In addition, molecular flexibility may also contribute to the discrepancy, as there are many more rotatable bonds in the molecules of the GEOM-Drugs data set (Figure ). The average RMSD values for the molecules generated by the QM9-trained models were larger than 2 Å, indicating that it is very hard for the models to generate low-energy conformations even for low-complexity molecules. Similar results can be observed in the comparison with the global minimum conformation (Figure C,D). In addition, we evaluated the energy compliance (ΔE < 10 kcal/mol) between the generated conformations and their optimized counterparts. The results, summarized in Tables S4 and S5, confirm that the ability of the models to generate low-energy conformations largely decreases for more complex molecules. Among these models, EQGAT-diff performs best in generating complex molecules, but yields only 12.85% low-energy conformations in the generated set. JODO, which directly generates a molecular graph as EQGAT-diff, achieves the best performance on the QM9 data set.

Regarding atomic stability, most models generate molecules with a high degree of chemical plausibility at the atomic level. The JODO, EDM, GCDM, GEOLDM, MolFM, and MDM all achieve atomic stability rates exceeding 99%, indicating their ability to effectively capture the right chemical connectedness of individual atoms and ensure that the generated atoms adhere to appropriate chemical bonding states. Notably, most of these models, such as EDM, GEOLDM, MolFM, and GCDM, rely on third-party rules to generate bond types based on distances between atom pairs and their respective atom types. This explains that they excel at reproducing correct atomic connectivity patterns in chemical structures.

In terms of molecular stability, the differences between models become more pronounced. GEOLDM (95.66%) exhibits the best performance, as it does in atomic stability, followed by MolFM (94.54%) and JODO (93.52%), among which only JODO belongs to the class of models that directly generate a molecular graph. These models not only ensure chemical reasonability at the atomic level but also effectively maintain the stability of molecular structures. In contrast, MolDiff (74.23%) shows the lowest molecular stability, although it has extremely high atomic stability. This observation aligns with the earlier findings regarding its molecular validity on QM9.

The results obtained from the GEOM-Drugs data set are presented in the Supporting Information (see Table S6). All models, except MolDiff, achieve atom stability above 80%. MolDiff consistently shows lower atom stability, a trend also observed with the QM9 data set. However, all models exhibit extremely low molecular stability, highlighting their inability to generate theoretically accurate 3D molecular structures for larger molecules, which aligns with the observation about bond length distribution in the JSD analysis.

Overall Performance

This study evaluated the effectiveness of 3D diffusion models in generating 2D chemical structures and further examined the reasonableness of the generated 3D conformations from multiple perspectives. Overall, the models exhibited broadly comparable performance on 2D metrics, and for most models, the distributions of 2D features in the generated molecules were close to those of the training set molecules. However, in the 3D metric evaluations, the models often showed substantial variation, with performance noticeably worse than in the 2D metrics. In addition, we assessed the generative capability of these models across different molecular sizes. The results showed that models trained on the GEOM-Drugs data set with more complex molecules had significantly weaker learning ability in both 2D and 3D features compared to those trained on the QM9 data set with small-sized molecules, indicating that the ability of current diffusion models to learn complex molecular chemical spaces still falls short of expectations.

In the 2D metrics, certain models trained on the GEOM-Drugs data set showed poor performance in molecular connectivity, an indicator of general molecular quality. All of these were models that rely on third-party software to construct molecular graphs. Such models also underperformed in drug-likeness and synthetic accessibility evaluations. In the analysis of 2D topological features, these models exhibited significantly lower performance than direct molecular-graph generative models on complexity-related descriptors such as the number of chiral centers, the number of aromatic rings, and the proportion of sp3-hybridized atoms. This difference was more pronounced for models trained on the GEOM-Drugs data set. These findings suggest that diffusion models capable of directly generating molecular graphs can better learn the distribution of chemical space and more effectively capture the structures of complex molecules. Furthermore, when evaluating the similarity of the generated molecules to the training set in chemical space using molecular latent representations and molecular fingerprints, the models relying on third-party software again performed poorly on the GEOM-Drugs data set. Among the models that directly generate the molecular graph, MiDi achieved relatively balanced performance on both QM9 and GEOM-Drugs, with only a modest shortcoming in the chemical validity of generated complex molecules, consistent with previous literature reports.

Then, we placed particular emphasis on evaluating the capability of diffusion models in 3D structure generation. First, we assessed the ability of the models to learn local 3D chemical structures at the levels of bond lengths, bond angles, and torsion angles. In bond length evaluations, all models performed unsatisfactorily; however, performance improved notably in bond angle and torsion angle evaluations, particularly for torsion angles in low-complexity molecules. Compared to bond lengths, the superior performance in torsion angles indicates that these models are more effective at learning high-dimensional conformational patterns than at precisely reproducing tightly constrained local geometries, highlighting the need to incorporate explicit chemical priors to improve accuracy in bond modeling. Second, we evaluated the differences between generated conformations and low-energy conformations from the perspectives of overall molecular conformation and torsional geometry. In terms of RMSD and energy differences between conformations, all models struggled to generate 3D structures close to low-energy conformations, with no significant differences among the models, especially for complex molecules. However, in torsional geometry, the models that directly generate molecular graphs outperformed other models, and notably, these models also showed better capability in preserving aromatic planarity. Finally, we examined the chemical validity of bonding from the perspectives of atomic and molecular stability in theory. Since the models that directly generate molecular graphs predict bond types through a neural network, their performance on this metric was poorer. Models relying on third-party software for molecular graph construction adhered more strictly to chemical bonding rules for atomic connectivity, resulting in fewer atoms with incorrect bonds. For simple molecules, all models generated a large proportion of molecules fully compliant with chemical bonding rules, but this proportion dropped sharply for complex molecules.

In summary, diffusion models underperformed on low-dimensional geometric metrics and low-energy conformation reproduction, which are direct indicators of 3D structural accuracy. Direct molecular-graph generative models generally performed better in capturing 3D structural features, but worse in theoretical atomic connectivity compared to models using third-party graph generation. Among the direct molecular-graph generative models, EQGAT and MiDi showed relatively stable performance across all 3D evaluations.

Conclusions

In this work, we conducted a systematic evaluation of diffusion-based molecular generative models with a particular focus on their capability to generate chemically valid 3D structures. Across 2D metrics, most models performed comparably, and their generated molecular feature distributions closely matched those of the training sets. Models that rely on third-party software to reconstruct molecular graphs showed weaker performance than the models capable of directly generating molecular graphs, among which the MiDi model maintained balanced performance across data sets, although they still exhibited some limitations in generating chemically valid complex molecules.

In 3D evaluations, performance differences between models became more pronounced, with EQGAT and MiDi achieving relatively stable performance. All models struggled to precisely reproduce bond lengths, while showing better accuracy in bond angles and especially torsion angles, indicating a stronger ability to learn high-dimensional conformational patterns than to replicate tightly constrained local geometries. Furthermore, all models had difficulty generating conformations close to low-energy structures in terms of RMSD and energy difference, particularly for complex molecules. Direct molecular-graph generative models achieved superior torsional geometry and aromatic planarity, but models using third-party graph construction produced fewer chemically invalid bonds.

Taken together, these findings indicate that while recent diffusion models have made notable progress, the current state of de novo 3D molecular generation is not yet sufficient for seamless integration into automated molecular design engines. The main limitations include (1) insufficient fidelity in local 3D geometry, particularly bond lengths; (2) reduced accuracy for large, complex molecules; and (3) trade-offs between chemical validity and accurate topology in the process of molecular graph generation. Future research should focus on optimizing model architectures to enhance the overall quality of generated molecules, in particular 3D geometry, thereby advancing their practical applications in drug design and related fields.
