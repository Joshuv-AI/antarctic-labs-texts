Cochlear Implantation for Sensorineural Hearing Loss Related to Cochlear Aqueduct Obstruction by a Jugular Foramen Tumor
Authors: Nicole Ewer, Mana Espahbodi, Kathryn Johnson, Richard H Wiggins, Richard K Gurgel, Neil S Patel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Tumors within the jugular foramen are typically benign and include jugular paragangliomas (JP), meningiomas, and lower cranial nerve schwannomas [1]. These can present with symptoms including pulsatile tinnitus, hearing loss, otalgia, and cranial nerve (CN) deficits leading to facial weakness, hoarseness, dysphagia, and dysarthria. Hearing loss can be conductive or sensorineural depending on the site of tumor involvement. In cases of sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL) observed in more advanced JP tumors, the hearing loss may be attributed to a tumor that has developed a cochlear or labyrinthine fistula, compression of the cochleovestibular nerve, involvement of the inferior cochlear vein, and/or the involvement of the cochlear aqueduct [2, 3, 4].

The cochlear aqueduct is a small channel inside the temporal bone that connects the subarachnoid space with the scala tympani and is thought to provide maintenance of pressure between the inner ear and cerebrospinal fluid. It is the conduit that causes sensorineural (i.e., cochlear) hearing loss in the setting of bacterial meningitis [5]. Given its close anatomical relationship with the inferior cochlear vein, involvement of the cochlear aqueduct by a tumor is considered a surrogate marker for inferior cochlear vein involvement as the cochlear aqueduct is consistently paralleled by an accessory canal containing the inferior cochlear vein [1, 2].

Treatment options for benign jugular foramen tumors include observation, stereotactic radiation (SRS), or microsurgery. The treatment decision involves a comprehensive assessment of factors including pathology, tumor location, growth rate, age of the patient, morbidity of treatment, and tumor progression. Surgical resection has the potential for irreversible lower cranial neuropathy. SRS has been used to achieve excellent long‐term tumor control without a heightened risk of lower cranial neuropathy; however, long‐term follow‐up is necessary [6]. In patients with serviceable hearing pre‐treatment, SRS has a low risk for short‐ to intermediate‐term risk of progression to non‐serviceable hearing [7].

Patients with asymmetric SNHL or single‐sided deafness have undergone CI even in the presence of skull base tumors, including vestibular schwannoma [8], meningioma, and others. However, certain tumor‐related factors may preclude CI candidacy, particularly when the tumor causes significant compression or damage to the cochleovestibular nerve, leads to cochlear fistula formation, or results in erosion of the otic capsule, all of which can compromise cochlear integrity and neural conduction. In contrast, when SNHL is attributed to tumor involvement of the cochlear aqueduct rather than direct destruction of the cochlea or auditory nerve, CI may remain a viable option, potentially offering meaningful auditory benefits and hearing preservation.

Currently, the literature is lacking with regard to treatment strategies to restore hearing of individuals in these cases. The objective of this case report is to present a specific case of SNHL due to tumor involvement of the cochlear aqueduct and demonstrate the feasibility of staged CI after SRS to achieve tumor control and auditory rehabilitation.

Materials and Methods

This is a case review of a patient, a 42‐year‐old female, presented with sudden‐onset progressive left‐sided SNHL with pulsatile tinnitus and otalgia. Her medical history was notable for otic capsule‐sparing left mastoid temporal bone fracture following a TBI at the age of 38 resulting in mild left‐sided conductive hearing loss. Prior to her current presentation, she had developed dysphagia, dysphonia, and left tongue deviation. Audiogram results confirmed new‐onset left‐sided SNHL with an increase in pure tone average (PTA) at 0.5, 1, 2, and 3 kHz to 119 dB HL (hearing level) from 50 dB HL 2 years prior. MRI findings revealed a mass within the left jugular foramen and hypoglossal canal, with a differential diagnosis of hypoglossal schwannoma or jugular paraganglioma without evidence of intracochlear obstruction. The mass extended in the petrous bone to involve the cochlear aqueduct (Figures 1 and 2). In‐office flexible laryngoscopy revealed mild paresis of the left vocal cord and tongue hemiparesis.

Axial contrast‐enhanced T1 MRI. Red arrow: Presence of enhancement of left cochlear aqueduct and adjacent dura consistent with tumor involvement.

Following comprehensive evaluation and discussion of potential treatments of observation, SRS, and microsurgery, it was recommended to pursue SRS treatment given her laryngeal function and the location of the tumor. Specifically, microsurgical excision via transjugular approach or far lateral approach would place her lower CNs at significant risk.

The patient underwent SRS 3 months after the lesion was found on brain MRI. The tumor received 15 Gy in a single fraction with isolation of the brain stem and optic nerves. The patient tolerated SRS treatment well; however, she reported increased numbness and weakness of the left tongue without objective changes on examination.

Two weeks after SRS treatment, she underwent CI with a lateral wall electrode (MED‐EL Synchrony 2 FlexSOFT). Electrode insertion was uncomplicated, and intraoperative x‐ray showed expected intracochlear device positioning. One week later, she underwent CI activation and mapping. Speech perception testing and mapping were conducted at 1 month and 6 months post‐CI activation. She proceeded to undergo anatomy‐based fitting using OTOPLAN On Demand (CAScination AG licensed by MED‐EL Corp., Durham, NC, USA) (Figure 3).

This study was deemed IRB‐exempt as it is a retrospective review of a single case, and no identifiable patient information is included. Main outcome measures included audiometric outcomes pre‐ and post‐CI, including PTA and AzBio sentence scores in quiet, and imaging of tumor pre‐ and post‐SRS.

Results

At presentation, audiometric testing revealed a left PTA of 119 dB HL and a WRS score of 0% in the affected ear, and a right PTA of 22 dB HL and a WRS score of 100% in the unaffected ear. One month after CI activation following SRS treatment, audiometric testing showed a PTA of 42 dB and an AzBio sentence score of 50% in quiet. At the six‐month follow‐up, PTA improved to 35 dB, though the AzBio sentence score declined to 23% in quiet. By the one‐year follow‐up, PTA was 38 dB, with an AzBio sentence score of 29% in quiet (Table 1). Datalogging showed CI use of 11.4 h per day at 1 month, decreasing to 7.4 h per day at 6 months and 5.5 h per day at 1 year post‐activation. The patient also reported discontinuing aural rehabilitation exercises during this period.

Discussion

This report summarizes the use of CI following SRS for a jugular foramen tumor causing SNHL. While the literature on CI in jugular foramen tumors in patients is scarce, there is a documented case in a patient with bilateral SNHL due to multiple JP's. After microsurgical removal of tumors, the patient was implanted unilaterally and had an appropriate audiometric response; however, specific audiometric results were not provided. Interestingly, 3 years after implantation, the CI was removed due to acute, progressive hearing impairment and pain to acoustic stimuli. At that time, a biopsy of the cochlea was performed revealing fibrosis without tumor recurrence. Selivanova and colleagues concluded that CI can restore hearing in deafness due to bilateral glomus jugulare tumors; however, cochlear fibrosis may occur as a late complication [9].

Medina and colleagues describe CI in a patient with JP treated with radiation and subsequent radiation‐induced SNHL. There were no reported complications, and the patient had stable 100% sentence recognition postoperatively [10]. Therefore, CI is a successful means of hearing rehabilitation in both radiation‐induced SNHL and SNHL related to tumors of the jugular foramen.

SNHL in JP tumors has multiple proposed etiologies: cochlear or labyrinthine fistula, compression of the cochleovestibular nerve, involvement of the inferior cochlear vein as a cause of hearing loss, and/or involvement of the cochlear aqueduct [2, 3, 4]. Interestingly, early animal studies on the occlusion of the cochlear aqueduct showed no evidence of morphological or functional change of the cochlea [11, 12]. Additionally, a post‐mortem analysis of a 74‐year‐old with bilateral down‐sloping SNHL revealed idiopathic, unilateral obliteration of the cochlear one‐third of the aqueduct with a patent cochlear aqueduct in the contralateral ear, suggesting that occlusion of the cochlear aqueduct may not always be associated with SNHL [5].

However, specifically, a more recent investigation by Patel and colleagues revealed that cochlear aqueduct involvement by JP was significantly associated with SNHL (increase in bone conduction PTA of 19 dB HL) when loss of fluid signal within the cochlear aqueduct was observed on T2‐weighted MRI, enhancement was observed in the cochlear aqueduct on post‐contrast MRI, or when there was bony erosion/expansion of the cochlear aqueduct on CT [4]. Ongoing research with larger cohorts may enhance our understanding of mechanisms of SNHL in jugular foramen tumors.

Unilateral SNHL in cases that result in compression or damage of the cochleovestibular nerve or cochlear fistulas may result in scenarios unsuitable for CI due to obliteration of the cochlea or true retrocochlear hearing loss. However, if cochlear aqueduct involvement can be identified as the cause of unilateral hearing loss in the setting of a jugular foramen tumor, then patients could potentially benefit from a CI for the preservation of hearing as reported in this study.

Comparison between 1‐month and 1‐year follow‐up revealed a reduction in speech perception. This decline may be attributed to decreased implant usage and cessation of aural rehabilitation exercises from 1 to 12 months. The exact reasoning behind the decline in device use and rehab exercises remains unclear; however, it was likely driven by the patient's excellent contralateral hearing, which may have reduced reliance on the implant and motivation to continue aural rehabilitation exercises. Furthermore, there was an observed marginal increase in tumor volume, which could potentially be associated with the observed decrement.

Limitations of this study include a small sample size as a case report, retrospective nature, lack of control group, and short period of follow‐up. However, these results assist in filling a knowledge gap regarding CI in patients with SNHL due to jugular foramen tumors.

Conclusion

Hearing rehabilitation with CI for hearing loss caused by cochlear aqueduct involvement by a jugular foramen tumor resulted in improved audiometric outcomes in this case report. Patients with similar areas of tumor involvement with ipsilateral advanced SNHL should be considered candidates for CI.
