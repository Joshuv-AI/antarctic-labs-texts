An integrated peripheral immune circuit of inflammation and dysfunction underlies drug resistance in pediatric drug-resistant epilepsy
Authors: Ting Zhao, Hong-jian Li, Hui-lan Zhang, Yan Sun, Lu-hai Yu, Jing Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo systematically investigate the dynamic changes in peripheral immune cell composition and function under different clinical phenotypes, detect important immune subpopulations and regulatory networks that cause drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE), and offer new theoretical and translational insights into immune classification, early intervention, and targeted therapy in pediatric DRE.MethodsPeripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from three DRE cases, two medication-effective cases (ME), and two healthy control cases (HC) were subjected to single-cell RNA sequencing (ScRNA-seq) analysis. The results showed variations in the proportions and gene expression profiles of immune cell types among the three groups. Differential gene trend analysis was conducted to screen signaling pathways and core genes associated with DRE. Monocytes, B cells, and T cells underwent further analysis, which identified cell subpopulations strongly related to DRE and changes in DRE-associated pathways. Finally, intercellular communication analysis was conducted.ResultsMBNL1, FOS, VPS13B, Rap1, and cAMP pathways related to DRE, as well as SKAP1, RAP1B, BRAF, and FOS genes were identified. Further in-depth analysis identified the VCAN+Mono subpopulation, as well as changes in DRE related pathways in T cells and B cells. Compared with the ME group, CD4+Tcm increased, and CD8+Tem decreased in patients with DRE. Finally, the DRE group exhibited enhanced pro-inflammatory properties of VCAN+Mono, increased exhaustion signaling in CD8+Tem, and reduced ability to adhere to and locate target cells. The small sample size may have affected statistical power, rendering some findings unreliable.ConclusionsThis is the first study to use scRNA-seq analysis on PBMCs from children with DRE, ME, and HC. The findings identified important genes and pathways that may be associated with DRE. It identified a VCAN+Mono subpopulation that is strongly associated with DRE and has pro-inflammatory properties. The results provide novel insights into the immune regulatory mechanisms of DRE and offer theoretical justification for the development of novel biomarkers and potential targets.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08344-2.Keywords: Drug-resistant epilepsy, Single-cell RNA sequencing, VCAN+Mono, Differential genes, Therapeutic targets

Introduction

Epilepsy is one of the most prevalent severe brain disorders; it is characterized by recurrent spontaneous seizures, frequently accompanied by neuropsychiatric and cognitive problems and a high mortality [1]. According to a 2019 study published in the Lancet, epilepsy affects more than 70 million individuals worldwide, with a higher incidence in children and adolescents [2]. There are over 6 million individuals with active epilepsy in China, where the prevalence ranges from 0.4% to 0.7% [3].

Long-term usage of antiseizure medicines (ASMs) is the primary treatment for epilepsy [1]. However, no drug can completely control epileptic seizures, and patients still experience the neurological complications of epilepsy and the adverse effects of medication therapy [4]. Approximately one-third of individuals with epilepsy have drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE), a condition in which over 60% of patients can be treated with medication [5]. Between 30% and 40% of individuals with epilepsy develop drug resistance after ASM use, which leads to unsuccessful treatment [6–8]. DRE-induced repeated seizures provide a considerable risk of unexpected death and can have varied effects on cognitive behavior, brain function development, and quality of life in patients. However, the pathophysiology of DRE is unclear. This warrants investigating DRE pathophysiology and developing novel targeted drugs.

The peripheral immune system is central to the onset and progression of epilepsy, and systemic immune responses may influence neuroinflammatory processes through the blood-brain barrier. However, current research has principally focused on local changes in brain tissue, and there are a few systematic immune markers that can differentiate between treatment-sensitive and treatment-resistant patients. Moreover, the systemic immune characteristics of epilepsy are poorly understood. The rapid advancement of single-cell RNA sequencing (ScRNA-seq) and its derived technologies (such as CITE-seq and spatial transcriptomics) has allowed for an in-depth analysis of gene expression states at the single-cell level. They elucidate intercellular heterogeneity and the complexity of disease microenvironments [9, 10]. ScRNA-seq is becoming an important tool for developing cell atlases, analyzing tissue cell heterogeneity, and identifying pathogenic gene markers [11].

The regulatory mechanisms of peripheral blood PBMCs in drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE) are still unclear, and few studies have investigated the transcriptomic heterogeneity of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) in patients with epilepsy. This study compares the transcriptomic features and regulatory networks of PBMCs in children with DRE and those in the drug-responsive group using scRNA-seq technology and bioinformatics. The research aimed to identify key feature genes and regulatory relationships between them to offer new insights into DRE diagnosis and treatment.

To this end, ScRNA-seq was conducted on PBMCs from healthy children, patients sensitive to epilepsy treatment, and patients tolerant to treatment. The primary goals were to systematically elucidate dynamic changes in the composition and function of peripheral immune cells under different clinical phenotypes, identify key immune subsets and regulatory networks driving treatment tolerance, and offer novel theoretical and translational insights into immune classification, early intervention, and targeted therapy of DRE.

Methods

Research participants

Blood samples and clinical data were collected from three pediatric patients with DRE, two pediatric patients sensitive to ASM treatment with controlled seizures, and two healthy children, who were hospitalized in the Department of Neurology at Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region People’s Hospital and Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region Children’s Hospital between January and August 2025. Four to five milliliters of fasting, ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)-anticoagulated blood samples were collected. The seven samples were divided into the drug-resistant epilepsy group (DRE), medication-effective group (ME), and healthy control group (HC) groups. This Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region People’s Hospital Ethics Committee approved this study, which strictly adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki (Approval No.: KY2025082928). Sample collection, PBMC preparation, and sequencing

Freshly collected EDTA-anticoagulated blood was used to isolate PBMCs using the Ficoll-Paque density gradient separation technique. Successful PBMC isolation was indicated by a viability rate > 90%. Single-cell libraries were constructed, and they underwent quality control analysis. The 10×Genomics single-cell sequencing platform was used for sequencing once the libraries passed quality check. The sequencing depth was at least 50,000 reads per cell, and the sequencing strategy was 150 bp paired-end reads.

Retrieval and process of bulk RNA-seq data

The National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) provided public expression matrix data files of bulk RNA-seq (GSE143272, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE143272). For analysis, 10 healthy samples, 16 VA drug-sensitive samples, and 9 VA drug-resistant samples were selected.

Gene set scoring

To evaluate the activity of predefined gene sets in separate cells, the AddModuleScore function from the Seurat package was used.

Raw data processing

A total of 114,347 cells from three groups of human samples were analyzed. SeekSoulTools (http://seeksoul.seekgene.com/zh/v1.2.1/) was used to create digital gene expression matrices. The R package Seurat (version 4.3.0) [12] was used to transform the UMI count matrices into Seurat objects. Cells with UMI counts < 1,000, detected genes < 500, or mitochondrial UMI counts > 20% were considered low-quality and removed; finally, 94,385 cells were retained. Genes detected in fewer than five cells were removed in subsequent analyses.

After quality control, Seurat was used to integrate the matrices of several samples and facilitate cross-sample comparisons in subsequent analyses. The “Normalize Data” function was used to log-normalize the combined count matrix, and the “Scale Data” function was used to scale it. The “Find Variable Features” function was used to identify the 2,000 most variable genes, and the “Run PCA” function was used to calculate principal components (PCs). Subsequently, data integration was conducted using the Harmony algorithm [13]. The first 30 principal components were used to determine the nearest neighbors, and the “Find Clusters” function (resolution = 0.5) was used to cluster the data. Next, tSNE or UMAP plots were used for visualization. The “wilcoxauc” function from the Presto (version 1.0.0) package was used to identify the cell type of each cluster by detecting marker genes for each cell cluster. The ScType tool was used to annotate the cell types [14].

Pseudotime analysis

Monocle2 (version 2.26.0) was used for pseudotime analysis, trajectory construction, and calculation of pseudotime-dependent gene expression [15]. The DDRTree (version 0.1.5) method was used for dimension reduction. Cell subtypes and cell states were used to illustrate the cell trajectory. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) based on pseudotime function were identified using the Basic Differential Analysis algorithm. Branched Expression Analysis Modeling was used to identify genes with branching-dependent regulation.

Cell–cell communication

CellChat (v2.1.1) was used to predict cell–cell interactions based on the expression of recognized ligand–receptor pairs in different cell types [16]. Following the official procedure, normalized counts were loaded into CellChat, and preprocessing functions were used to identify Over Expressed Genes, Over Expressed Interactions, and project Data with predetermined parameters to explore possible cell–cell communication networks at various stages. The Secreted Signalling pathways were chosen as the database, and the precompiled human Protein–protein-Interactions served as a priori network information. Standard parameters and fixed randomization seeds were used to apply the core functions computeCommunProb, computeCommunProbPathway, and aggregateNet for primary analyses. Finally, the “netAnalysis_signallingRole” function was applied on the netP data slot to identify the network senders and receivers.

Functional enrichment analysis

Using KOBAS 2.0 [17], Gene Ontology (GO, http://geneontology.org/) enrichment analysis was used to classify the functions of genes and identify GO concepts that were significantly enriched among the DEGs. Simultaneously, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG, http://www.kegg.jp/) enrichment analysis of the DEGs was conducted to explore their functions and identify significantly enriched KEGG pathways. Each term’s enrichment was assessed using hypergeometric tests and the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR control technique.

Statistical analysis

The pheatmap package (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/pheatmap/index.html) in R was used for clustering based on Euclidean distance. Either the unpaired t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test (for a sample size < 30) was used for comparisons that involved two groups. Multivariate techniques, such as analysis of covariance, were used for comparisons that involved several groups.

Results

Single-cell profiling depicts altered immune cell composition and dysregulated pathways in pediatric epilepsy

Peripheral blood was collected from HC, ME, and DRE groups, and PBMCs were isolated for scRNA-seq analysis to investigate possible biomarkers for pediatric drug-resistant epilepsy (Fig. 1A). A total of 94,385 single-cell profiles were obtained for subsequent analysis after preprocessing and quality verification of the sequencing data. Based on classic markers, the following 13 cell types were identified: B cells, CD14-positive monocytes (CD14+ Mono), CD16-positive monocytes (CD16+ Mono), CD4-positive T cells (CD4+ T), CD8-positive T cells (CD8+ T), hematopoietic stem cells (HSC), megakaryocytes (Megakaryocyte), miT cells, natural killer cells (NK), plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDC), plasma cells (Plasma), proliferative T cells (Proliferative T), and γδ T cells. Particularly, B cells, CD14+ Mono, CD4+ T, CD8+ T, and NK cells were the major cell populations (Fig. 1B-C).

Changes in cell proportions showed that B cells and γδ T cells were upregulated in the DRE group and downregulated in the ME group, compared with the HC group. CD4+ T cells were downregulated in the DRE group and upregulated in the ME group. CD8+ T cells were upregulated in both the DRE and ME groups, with the ME group exhibiting a more noticeable increase. Both the DRE and ME groups showed a decline in CD14+ monocytes, CD16+ monocytes, megakaryocytes, miT cells, plasma cells, and proliferative T cells; however, the ME group showed a particularly marked decrease in CD14+ monocytes (Fig. 1D). The differential gene expression profile showed several DEGs among the groups (Fig. 1E).

GO enrichment analysis showed that upregulated DEGs in the ME group were enriched in transcription and translation regulation, mitochondrial ATP synthesis, aerobic respiration, and T cell signaling pathways, compared with those in the HC group (Fig. 1F). In contrast, upregulated DEGs in the DRE group were enriched in transcription and translation regulation, mitochondrial ATP synthesis, aerobic respiration, guanosine triphosphate (GTP) enzyme activity, and calcium ion concentration pathways (Fig. 1G).

According to KEGG enrichment analysis, both the ME and DRE groups were enriched in T cell signaling, NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity, Th1/Th2/Th17 cell differentiation, tumor necrosis factor (TNF) signaling, mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling, and neurotrophic factor signaling pathways, compared with the HC group. Additionally, the DRE group was enriched in the cell cycle, chemokine signaling, B cell signaling, and autophagy pathways (Figure S1A-B). In addition to being enriched in phagocytosis, extracellular matrix (ECM)-receptor interaction, and mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathways, the DRE group showed further enhancement of T cell signaling and Th17 cell differentiation signaling than the ME group (Figure S1C). Therefore, DRE may be associated with variations in GTPase activity, T cells, Th17 cell differentiation, and mTOR-related pathways.

Discussion

DRE diagnosis primarily depends on the outcomes of drug treatment, which frequently takes years. In addition to worsening the patient’s brain dysfunction, recurrent seizures lead to related psychological disorders (such as depression and anxiety), intellectual disabilities (particularly in children), social stigma, reduced quality of life, increased risk of mortality, and shortened life expectancy. These events severely impact daily life and social functioning and place a substantial economic burden on society and families [18].

Maximizing seizure control, decreasing seizure severity and frequency, improving cognitive function, promoting social function recovery, and enhancing quality of life are the goals of DRE treatment. Children are in a distinct stage of growth and development, and several drugs pose risks and uncertainties. Therefore, one of the challenges in treating DRE is delivering precise treatment strategies based on the patient’s actual situation. A more effective treatment strategy can be developed if early detection and prediction of DRE onset are achievable. The pathogenesis of DRE remains unclear. Numerous hypotheses have been proposed by both domestic and international studies, such as the drug transporter, pharmacokinetic, drug target, gene mutation, and neural network hypotheses [19]. This necessitates actively exploring DRE pathogenesis and identifying biological markers for the condition.

This study systematically analyzed the cellular composition and functional states of the peripheral immune microenvironment in DRE. Thus, PBMCs from three children with DRE, two children who responded effectively to ASM treatment, and two healthy controls were subjected to high-precision ScRNA-seq analysis. This study focused on key immune subsets, such as B cells, CD14+ monocytes, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and NK cells. Evaluation of their immune characteristics, gene regulatory networks, and correlation between key genes and DRE suggested that differences in GTPase activity, T cells, Th17 cell differentiation, and mTOR pathways may be associated with DRE.

However, statistical power may have been impacted by the limited by the small sample size, rendering some findings unstable. Furthermore, this study was not intended to differentiate medication effects from illness effects, although ASMs have immunomodulatory properties (for example, valproic acid alters T cell activity as an HDAC inhibitor) [20].

Furthermore, clustering analysis of the expression trends of key differential genes affecting DRE, as well as GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were conducted. The expression of neurodevelopment-related genes is strongly associated with epilepsy. This network includes MBNL1, FOS, VPS13B, Rap1, and the cAMP pathway, as well as SKAP1, RAP1B, BRAF, and FOS genes, which may serve as biomarkers for DRE. Their expression patterns provide a novel insight into understanding and predicting the response of patients with DRE to immunotherapy. Moreover, these genes may serve as biomarkers to optimize the responsiveness of combination therapy. However, as potential biomarkers, these signaling pathways and genes must be verified in separate patient cohorts owing to the sample size limitations.

The FOS gene (also termed c-fos) is a common immediate early gene. Growth factors, cAMP analogs, neurotransmitters, or KCI-induced membrane depolarization can cause fast c-fos transcription in primary neuron cultures. Additionally, its induced expression has been observed in the intact brain in a response to several physiological and pathological stimuli, including seizures, traumatic injury, somatosensory stimulation, and long-term potentiation. In certain situations, c-fos expression is highly correlated with neuronal activity, which reflects neuronal synaptic plasticity responses [21]. During seizures, the c-fos gene is activated, and Fos protein accumulates in the nucleus owing to extreme excitation [22, 23]. The BRAF gene (V-raf murine sarcoma viral oncogene homolog B1) encodes the B-Raf protein, a key kinase in the MAPK pathway. cAMP signaling regulates BRAF expression through PKA. Koh et al. [24] reported that the BRAF V600E mutation in the neuronal lineage leads to intrinsic epileptogenicity, and the ventricular administration of the BRAF inhibitor Vemurafenib (PLX4032) significantly reduces seizure frequency and decreases REST and MYC expression. Mertz et al. [25] reported that cAMP levels in the brain rise during seizures; however, enhancement of the cAMP pathway appears to lower the seizure induction threshold. Therefore, the cAMP pathway may confer pro-epileptic effects.

Furthermore, monocytes that showed significant changes in both the DRE and ME groups were subjected to reclustering analysis. ASM resistance may be associated with enhanced monocyte inflammatory responses, whereas treatment sensitivity may be associated with monocyte-mediated T cell responses. VCAN+Mono decreased in the ME group but increased in the DRE group. Compared with the ME group, upregulated DEGs of VCAN+Mono in the DRE group were enriched in inflammatory response, chemotaxis, apoptosis, signal transduction, and cell adhesion pathways.

VCAN, a large extracellular matrix proteoglycan, is widely present in humans. It is central to maintaining tissue structural integrity, regulating interactions between cells and the extracellular matrix, and controlling physiological processes, such as inflammatory responses and angiogenesis [26]. High VCAN expression in monocytes can form complexes with CCL2 and be secreted into tissues, which sustains a high local concentration gradient of chemokines. Thus, more circulating monocytes will infiltrate the lesion site. The infiltrated monocytes produce VCAN and CCL2, creating a positive feedback loop that continuously intensifies the fibrotic response and promotes tissue damage [27]. In multiple sclerosis, VCAN can increase the toxicity of Th17 cells and promote their polarization, resulting in an inflammatory microenvironment that is unfavorable for myelin regeneration. By promoting myelin repair, regulating the local immune response, and directly relieving the inhibition on OPCs, its inhibitors can mitigate Th17-mediated neurotoxicity [28].

Taken together, monocytes with high VCAN expression may promote inflammatory responses and mediate tissue damage. VCAN can mediate neurotoxicity through Th17 cells in the nervous system. The identification of the VCAN Mono subpopulation that is specifically amplified in the DRE group was combined. Notably, this subpopulation showed a dual “pro-inflammatory and pro-oxidative” pathogenic potential and was enriched in classical inflammatory pathways. Moreover, it exhibited an activated oxidative stress state. Differentiation trajectory analysis showed an enriched subpopulation at the pseudotime endpoint, which reflected a highly activated terminal effector cell state. Thus, VCAN+Mono is a key peripheral immunological pathogenic subpopulation in pediatric drug-resistant epilepsy. Its dual features provide a novel molecular perspective for understanding the mechanisms underlying epilepsy drug resistance and may serve as a target for immune intervention.

Furthermore, this study involved an exhaustive investigation of T cells and B cells. Children with DRE may exhibit impaired differentiation of CD4+ T cells. However, they have fewer CD8+ Tem cells than children with ME, which reduces their ability to eliminate diseased cells and finally contributes to the development of drug resistance. Furthermore, pediatric DRE exhibits more severe inflammatory responses and stronger electrical signal transmission, with cAMP signaling and neurotrophic factor signaling being central to DRE.

Intercellular interactions are central to the onset, development, and therapeutic response of diseases. With an emphasis on DRE-related VCAN+Mono and the highly upregulated communication between CD8+ Tem cells and other cells, this study explored cell-cell interactions. Among these, CD45 (PTPRC, protein tyrosine phosphatase receptor type C) is the primary glycoprotein receptor for LGALS9 (Galectin-9) on the surface of B cells and T cells [29]. Galectin-9 is extensively expressed by tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs); it interacts with CD45 on CD8+ T cells to induce T cell exhaustion. In this study, the DRE group showed stronger LGALS9-CD45 signaling than the ME group. This interaction may be related to reduced CD8+ Tem cells in the DRE group, weakening T cell phagocytosis of damaged cells and mediating drug-resistant epilepsy.

TNFSF13B (BAFF), a member of the TNF superfamily, is primarily produced by myeloid cells (macrophages, dendritic cells), astrocytes, and activated T cells. TNFRSF13B/C are BAFF-specific receptors [30]. Through the NLRP3 inflammasome and NF-κB, BAFF can trigger inflammatory reactions [31], and it can promote meningitis progression in the nervous system through B cells, T cells, and myeloid cells [32]. NAMPT can directly bind to ITGA5_ITGB1 (integrin α5β1), a typical fibronectic receptor, to promote inflammatory responses [33]. In this study, the DRE group showed stronger LGALS9-CD45, TNFSF13B-TNFRSF13B, TNFSF13B-TNFRSF13C, and NAMPT-(ITGA5 ITGB1) signaling than the ME group. Therefore, the reduction of CD8+Tem cells in the DRE group may be related to these signaling interactions, and activation of inflammatory responses mediates drug-resistant epilepsy.

PTPRM is a receptor-like protein tyrosine phosphatase that belongs to the R2B subfamily and possesses both cell adhesion and signal transduction functions. Its extracellular region consists of a MAM domain, immunoglobulin-like domains, and fibronectin type III repeats; these elements mediate intercellular adhesion through homophilic binding [34]. PVR (CD155) and NECTIN2 (CD112, PVRL2) are important CD226 (DNAM-1) ligands, which act together to control the CD226-mediated signaling network. As an activating receptor for T cells, CD226 is constitutively expressed on CD8+ T cells and naïve CD4+ T cells [35, 36]. CD55 (decay-accelerating factor, DAF) is a complement regulatory protein, and ADGRE5 (CD97) belongs to the adhesion G protein-coupled receptor family. Their interaction has unique immunoregulatory functions [37]. CD55-ADGRE5 interaction generates effective co-stimulatory signals for T cells, which promotes T cell proliferation, activation, and IFN-γ and GM-CSF secretion [38]. In this study, intercellular communication involving CD8+ Tem cells showed that PTPRM-PTPRM, PVR-CD226, NECTIN2-CD226, (ITGAV ITGB1)-ADGRE5, and CD55-ADGRE5 signals were weaker in the DRE group than in the ME group. CD8+ Tem cells showed downregulated PTPRM, CD226, and ADGRE5, and changes in ligand-receptor expression were consistent with alterations in signaling strength. Thus, reduced CD8+ Tem cells in the DRE group may be related to these interactions, which would impair T cell-mediated phagocytosis of damaged cells and contribute to epilepsy drug resistance.

However, there are certain drawbacks to using CellChat’s ligand-receptor interaction analysis to predict immunological communication networks in DRE. This is because it is based on transcriptomic data and may not accurately reflect functional signaling interactions. These include: (i) the lack of correlation between mRNA and protein abundance for ligands/receptors; (ii) inability to detect post-translational modifications, shedding, or ectodomain cleavage required for ligand activation; (iii) the possibility of false positives owing to co-expressed but non-interacting receptor-ligand pairs. By predicting neutralizing antibodies of ligands, receptor-blocking peptides, or co-culture systems with receptor-deficient cells, this study will confirm the aforementioned findings in the future.

Collectively, children with DRE have elevated VCAN+Mono, which can enhance inflammatory responses through cell-cell interactions, inhibit the function of effector T cells, and weaken interactions between various cells and effector T cells related to T cell adhesion and activation. In summary, certain monocytes and decreased effector T cell function may promote DRE in children by boosting the inflammatory response. Nevertheless, these results need to be confirmed in larger cohorts because the functional role of VCAN+Mono in epilepsy is still speculative.

In conclusion, these results indicated that the pathophysiology of seizure disorders is influenced by the immune system and the cerebrovascular system. However, there is currently inadequate information in the literature to determine how peripheral immune alterations may relate to neuroinflammatory processes within the brain. According to Librizzi et al. [39], seizures are caused by activated peripheral immune cells, such as PBMCs, and the extravasation of serum proteins when the blood-brain barrier (BBB) is compromised. Günbey et al. [40] reported that proinflammatory cytokines and elevated p21/p16 expression in the drug-resistant group may suggest that continuous seizures cause cellular stress, which may make drug-resistant pediatric patients with epilepsy more susceptible to senescence.

Impairment of the BBB, which serves as the initial defense of the central nervous system, has been related to seizure onset and epilepsy progression [41]. Pro-inflammatory pathways are activated, neuronal dysfunction occurs, and seizures occur during BBB disruption, allowing both plasma proteins and blood-borne immune cells to extravasate into the brain parenchyma [42]. In animal models of acquired epilepsies and in brain tissues from patients with temporal lobe epilepsy or cortical development abnormalities undergoing epilepsy surgery, rapid activation of pro-inflammatory cytokines and danger signals are observed after both acute and chronic seizures [43]. Patients with various types of focal epilepsies have altered cells of both adaptive and innate immunity in their serum and cerebrospinal fluid [41, 44]. Moreover, tuberous sclerosis complex, type IIB focal cortical dysplasia, and encephalitis with frequent seizures appear to have a high level of peripheral immune cell infiltration [44]. Additionally, inflammatory processes may contribute to the development and recurrence of epileptic seizures [45]. Furthermore, by boosting the inflammatory response, a decrease in the activity of monocytes and effector T cells may cause pediatric DRE.

Furthermore, oxidative stress contributes to the development of DRE [46–54]. Oxidative stress is a critical factor in the pathophysiology of epilepsy, and is central to epileptogenesis and seizure-induced neuronal pathology. NADPH oxidase 2 (NOX2) serves as a major source of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the brain [46, 47]. In addition to increasing levels in epilepsy models and mediating seizure-induced neuronal damage, ROS contribute to inflammation in several diseases [48]. ROS has been associated with seizure-induced neuronal damage and eventually cell death [49].

Singh et al. [50] demonstrated that GSK2795039 (a strong NOX2 inhibitor) mitigated oxidative DNA damage, maintained hippocampal neuronal integrity, and differentially modulated the production of pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines. Singh et al. [51] reported that the thioredoxin-mimetic tripeptide TXM-CB3 (CB3) alters redox and inflammatory pathways while preserving neuronal integrity. According to Sandouka et al. [52], the expression of nuclear factor erythroid 2-like 2 (Nrf2) and its downstream genes is transiently increased and peaks at early timepoints after a seizure, predominantly in the hippocampus. Singh et al. [53] reported that Nrf2 may serve as a target for epilepsy management. Furthermore, Sandouka et al. [54] demonstrated that dimethyl fumarate (DMF) administration after status epilepticus increased Nrf2 activity, attenuated status epilepticus- induced neuronal cell death, and decreased the frequency and total number of seizures, compared with vehicle-treated animals.

The functional condition of patients with DRE is not directly informed by the initial finding of altered CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cell subsets. Exhaustion markers (e.g., PD-1, TIM-3, and LAG-3), cytokine signaling (e.g., IFNG, IL10), or functional immune states (e.g., proliferation, cytotoxicity) were not directly measured in the current dataset. This is an obvious limitation of the study. Chronic inflammation and recurrent seizures may move T cells away from classical activation and toward exhaustion (marked by overexpression of PD-1, TIM-3, and LAG-3, with decreased cytokine production) in chronic epilepsy and other persistent neurological conditions [55, 56]. The decreased CD4⁺: CD8⁺ ratio in some patients with DRE may reflect selective loss of CD4⁺ T cells or the relative expansion of CD8⁺ T cells, both with distinct functional implications. Throughout the course of RE at a specific location in the central nervous system, Mair et al. [55] observed a dynamic and one-way T cell response: the development of T cell residence after CNS entry, neuronal death, and T cell exhaustion.

It was impossible to distinguish among these possibilities with the current data. Therefore, rather than being proof of a particular functional condition, T cell changes should be interpreted as correlative evidence of immune dysregulation. To ascertain whether the changed T cell composition in DRE reflects activation, exhaustion, or compensation, flow cytometry will be used to detect exhaustion markers, conduct intracellular cytokine staining after stimulation, and conduct functional T cell assays (such as proliferation and cytotoxicity).

In addition to the changes in immune cells in DRE detected through peripheral single-cell transcriptomics, related studies have reported other biomarker fields in epilepsy, including immune-mediated neuronal injury, network-level electrophysiological biomarkers (such as pathological high-frequency oscillations), and ScRNA-seq studies on disease-associated cellular states [57–59]. Taiwo et al. [57] reported that both patients had increased IgG antibodies and three types of glutamate receptor antibodies to the AMPA-GluR3B, NMDA-NR1, and NMDA-NR2 peptides. Pfisterer et al. [58] demonstrated that different neuronal subtypes from multiple families of main neurons (L5-6_Fezf2 and L2-3_Cux2) and GABAergic interneurons (Sst and Pvalb) exhibit the largest transcriptomic alterations. The subpopulations related to cognitive impairment (ExN1, 3, 8, and InN1, 6) were initially detected by Ma et al. [59] using snRNA-seq to characterize transcriptomic changes in hippocampal neurons of DRE cognitively impaired mice. Furthermore, Vaughan et al. [60] developed a streamlined ex vivo technique for examining seizure-induced transcriptional changes.

This study has certain limitations: (1) The small sample size may have affected statistical power, rendering some findings relatively unstable. (2) DRE is highly heterogeneous, and only three patients were included in this study. Therefore, gene expression patterns should be viewed as preliminary findings specific to these cases. Larger-scale studies are necessary to confirm their generalizability. (3) The patients received various ASMs, many of which confer immunomodulatory effects and may affect the transcriptional features of peripheral blood mononuclear cells. Although ASM regimens were recorded and considered as a covariate, disease-associated signatures were the key focus of this exploratory transcriptome analysis. (4) The findings were based on an exploratory analysis of a single transcriptome in conjunction with a literature review and have not yet been validated independently and experimentally. Overfitting and analytical bias might occur if this stage is skipped.

Nonetheless, this preliminary study has several implications. Through ScRNA-seq, this study explored the regulatory characteristics of key genes in peripheral blood immune cells of DRE samples and identified potential biomarkers for DRE. This research provides important insights into the network regulating molecular communication of DRE immune and offers crucial support for the development of novel diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. Large-scale, multicenter cohorts are required for additional validation to advance the clinical translation into precise DRE treatment.

Conclusion

To the best of knowledge, this is the first study to use scRNA-seq analysis on PBMCs from children with DRE, ME, and HC. MBNL1, FOS, VPS13B, Rap1, and cAMP pathways may be associated with DRE, as well as SKAP1, RAP1B, BRAF, and FOS genes. These signaling pathways and genes must be confirmed in separate patient cohorts as candidate biomarkers because of sample size limitations.

Furthermore, DRE, which has potent pro-inflammatory actions, may involve the VCAN+Mono subpopulation. Additionally, patients with DRE exhibit impaired and deteriorated T cell functions. These findings provide both novel insights into the immune regulatory mechanisms underlying DRE and theoretical support for the development of novel biomarkers, and could be a potential targets. However, these results need to be confirmed in larger cohorts because the functional role of VCAN+Mono in epilepsy remains elusive.
