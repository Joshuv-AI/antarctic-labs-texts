A Novel DRD2 Antagonist, SD2-2305, Exerts Anticancer Effects in Colorectal Cancer Cells through G1 Arrest and Caspase-Dependent Apoptosis
Authors: Na Hyun Kim, Geun Young Cho, In-Tae Kim, You-Jin Choi, Keunwan Park, Kyungsu Kang, Joon-Seok Choi, Sujin Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains difficult to treat due to challenges in early detection and the development of therapeutic resistance. Dopamine receptor D2 (DRD2) plays a critical role in regulating cell proliferation and apoptosis in various solid tumors. Given the relatively high levels of dopamine in the gastrointestinal tract, colorectal tumors may be particularly susceptible to DRD2-mediated signaling. In this study, we evaluated the anticancer activity of a newly synthesized DRD2 antagonist, SD2-2305, and investigated its underlying mechanisms in human CRC cell lines HCT116 and HT-29. SD2-2305 reduced cell viability in a time- and dose-dependent manner. Induction of apoptosis was confirmed by Annexin V/PI staining and immunoblot analysis, with activation of caspase-3, caspase-7, and caspase-9, although no significant changes were observed in the expression of Bcl-2 family proteins. Furthermore, SD2-2305 induced G1 phase cell cycle arrest, accompanied by downregulation of Cyclin A, Cyclin B, CDK1, and CDK2, and upregulation of p21. While key survival pathways (JAK2/STAT3, PI3K/Akt, and MAPK) remained relatively unaffected, SD2-2305 modulated growth factor receptors post-transcriptionally, decreasing HER2/ErbB2 and increasing TGF-beta receptor 1 expression. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the DRD2 antagonist SD2-2305 exerts potent anticancer effects through the induction of cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, and suggest that the anticancer activity of SD2-2305 is associated with coordinated modulation of growth factor receptor signaling and cell-cycle regulators in CRC cells.

INTRODUCTION

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most commonly diagnosed malignancy worldwide and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality (Baidoun et al., 2021). According to the World Health Organization, approximately 1.9 million new cases were diagnosed and nearly 900,000 deaths were attributed to CRC in 2022 (Bray et al., 2024). CRC is a multifactorial disease driven by aberrant activation and dysregulation of diverse signaling pathways. Among these, the JAK/STAT, PI3K/Akt/mTOR, MAPK/ERK, and Wnt/β-catenin pathways are recognized as central axes underlying colorectal tumorigenesis and malignant progression (Li et al., 2024). Current standard treatment strategies for CRC include surgical resection combined with chemotherapy and targeted therapies; however, the prognosis for patients with metastatic or recurrent disease remains poor (Benson et al., 2024). Moreover, resistance to epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) inhibitors is not solely attributable to EGFR itself but may arise from sustained activation of downstream signaling cascades or bypass mechanisms involving alternative pathways such as HER2/ErbB2 (HER2), MET, and G protein–coupled receptors (GPCRs) (Li et al., 2024). Therefore, therapeutic strategies focused exclusively on EGFR are insufficient, highlighting the urgent need to identify novel anticancer targets in CRC (Dorsam and Gutkind, 2007).

Dopamine has long been recognized as a neurotransmitter in the central nervous system; however, accumulating evidence indicates that it is also synthesized and functions in various peripheral organs (Klein et al., 2019). While dopamine receptor D2 (DRD2) signaling is best known for its inhibitory role in the nervous system, recent studies have demonstrated that DRD2 also regulates cell survival, stress responses, cell cycle progression, and metabolic processes in non-neuronal tissues, including the gastrointestinal tract, endocrine system, and tumor microenvironment (Shakya et al., 2023; Sim et al., 2024; Tan et al., 2021). Notably, the colon is among the organs with the highest dopamine concentrations in the human body, where dopamine is synthesized by both colonic epithelial cells and the enteric nervous system. Intestinal dopamine exerts diverse physiological functions by binding to dopamine receptors, including regulation of gut motility, fluid and electrolyte secretion, and immune responses (Li et al., 2006). Intriguingly, recent studies have reported increased expression of dopamine receptors in CRC, suggesting that dopamine may influence tumor cell proliferation and survival through receptor-mediated signaling (Kline et al., 2018). In particular, DRD2 overexpression has been observed across multiple cancer types, including lung, brain, breast, and gastric cancers (Rosas-Cruz et al., 2021), and emerging evidence supports the hypothesis that the dopamine–DRD2 axis may intersect with key survival signaling pathways to modulate tumor growth and apoptosis in CRC (Arang and Gutkind, 2020; Weissenrieder et al., 2019).

In this study, we investigated whether the DRD2 antagonist SD2-2305 (Fig. 1A), a newly synthesized DRD2 antagonist, exerts anticancer activity in human CRC cell lines HCT116 and HT-29, and further sought to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying its anticancer activity, with a particular focus on apoptosis, cell cycle regulation, and major survival and growth factor receptor signaling pathways.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cell lines and cell culture

Human CRC cell lines HCT116 and HT-29 were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA). HCT116 and HT-29 cells were cultured in DMEM and RPMI-1640 medium (Hyclone, Logan, UT, USA), respectively, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Hyclone) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Hyclone). Cells were incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. For cell viability assays, both cell lines were seeded at a density of 4×104 cells/well in 48-well plates, whereas for all other analyses, cells were seeded at a density of 3×106 cells/dish in 100-mm culture dishes.

Synthesis of 5-chloro-N-(3-(4-(5-chloro-2-oxo-2,3-dihydro-1H-benzo[d]imidazol-1-yl)piperidin-1-yl)propyl)-1H-indole-2-carboxamide (SD2-2305)

5-Chloro-1-(piperidin-4-yl)-1,3-dihydro-2H-benzo[d]imidazol-2-one was alkylated with N-Boc-3-iodopropylamine in the presence of K2CO3 in DMF, thereby introducing a propylamine tether at the piperidine nitrogen. Subsequent removal of the Boc protecting group using HCl afforded the corresponding free amine. The resulting amine was then subjected to amide coupling with 5-chloroindole-2-carbonyl chloride in the presence of TEA, yielding the target compound SD2-2305 (5-chloro-N-(3-(4-(5-chloro-2-oxo-2,3-dihydro-1H-benzo[d]imidazol-1-yl)piperidin-1-yl)propyl)-1H-indole-2-carboxamide).

Analytic data: 1H NMR (600 MHz, MeOD): δ 7.50 (d, 1H, J=1.9 Hz), 7.31 (d, 1H, J=8.8 Hz), 7.16 (d, 1H, J=8.5 Hz), 7.07 (dd, 1H, J=8.8, 1.1 Hz), 6.95 (d, 1H, J=2.0 Hz), 6.93 (s, 1H), 6.89 (dd, 1H, J=8.5, 2.0 Hz), 4.19 (m,1H), 3.37 (t, 2H, J=6.8 Hz), 3.08 (d, 2H, J=11.9 Hz), 2.48 (t, 2H, J=7.3 Hz), 2.39 (qd, 2H, J=12.7, 3.7 Hz), 2.13 (t, 2H, J=11.6 Hz), 1.80 (qui, 2H, J=6.9 Hz), 1.67 (d, 2H, J=12.2 Hz).

13C NMR (150 MHz, MeOD): δ 162.3, 154.9, 135.1, 132.5, 129.3, 128.5, 127.7, 126.5, 125.3, 123.8, 120.6, 120.4, 113.0, 110.0, 109.2, 102.0, 55.8, 52.8, 50.6, 37.7, 28.1, 26.3.

HRMS (EI): m/z calcd for C24H25Cl2N5O2 [M+] 485.1385, found 485.1380.

WST (water-soluble tetrazolium) cell viability assay

QuantiMax WST assay reagent (BioMax, Seoul, Korea) was used to assess cell viability following the manufacturer’s manual. First, HCT116 and HT-29 cells were seeded in quadruplicate into a 48 well plate and grown for 24 h at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. The cells were then treated with SD2-2305 at 0, 5, 10, 25 and 50 µM. After 24 or 48 h treatment with SD2-2305, the cells were incubated with WST reagent, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm with a microplate reader (BMG Labtech, Ortenberg, Germany).

Annexin V/propidium iodide staining assay and cell cycle analysis

To detect the apoptosis of HCT116 and HT-29 cells treated with SD2-2305 for 24 h, Annexin V-fluorescein isothiocyanate apoptosis detection kit (BD Biosciences, San Diego, CA, USA) was used. Briefly, harvested cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 0.1% bovine serum albumin (BSA), and resuspended in 1× binding buffer containing Annexin V and propidium iodide (PI). Fluorescence intensity was measured by flow cytometry using a FACS Verse flow cytometer (BD Biosciences).

For cell cycle analysis, the cells treated with SD2-2305 for 24 h were washed with PBS containing 0.1% BSA and fixed in ice-cold ethanol (100%) overnight. The fixed cells were washed again with PBS containing 0.1% BSA and stained with 50 µg/mL of PI solution containing 100 μg/mL of RNase A (Biosesang, Daejeon, Korea) by incubating for 30 min at room temperature. The DNA content was assessed by flow cytometry.

Immunoblot assay

The cells treated with SD2-2305 were harvested, washed with PBS, and lysed with radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer containing 1 mM NaF, 1 mM sodium orthovanadate, 0.1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, and a protease inhibitor cocktail. The protein yield was measured using Pierce Coomassie (Bradford) protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Protein samples were resolved by sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, and the resolved proteins were transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (EMD Millipore Corporation, Billerica, MA, USA). The membrane was blocked in Tris-buffered saline–Tween 20 detergent (TBST) containing 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated with primary antibodies (1:1,000 dilution in 2% skim milk in TBST) overnight at 4°C. The membrane was incubated with the primary antibodies, washed with TBST, and then incubated with the corresponding secondary antibodies (1:5,000 dilution) for 4 h at room temperature. The proteins on the membrane were visualized by chemiluminescence using an Amersham enhanced chemiluminescence reagent (GE Lifesciences, Piscataway, NJ, USA).

Antibodies

Antibodies detecting Bcl-2, Bcl-xL, cyclins (A, and E), p16, p21, p27, p53 were obtained from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Dallas, TX, USA). Anti-cyclin B antibodies were obtained from Abcam (Cambridge, UK), and anti-beta-actin antibody was from Sigma Aldrich (Burlington, MA, USA). Anti-CDK1 antibody and all secondary antibodies conjugated with the horse-radish peroxidase (HRP) were purchased from Thermo Fisher (Waltham, MA, USA). Primary antibodies against all other proteins were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Denver, CO, USA). The details of the antibodies used are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using Easy-Blue (Intron Biotechnology, Seongnam, Korea), and cDNA was synthesized with the QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). For qRT- PCR analysis, iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) was used, following the manufacturer’s instructions. The gene-specific primers are listed in Table 1. Data were analyzed by the 2-ΔΔCt method with normalization to ACTB mRNA levels.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Single comparisons were performed using the Student’s t-test. Data analysis was performed using SigmaStat 3.5 (Systat Software, Inc., San Jose, CA, USA). A probability (p) value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

SD2-2305 induces apoptosis in human CRC cells HCT116 and HT-29

To investigate the effect of SD2-2305 on the viability of human CRC cells HCT116 and HT-29, we performed a WST assay. WST assay revealed that SD2-2305 treatment reduced cell viability in a dose- and time-dependent manner (Fig. 1B). Morphological observations revealed that SD2-2305 induced cell rounding typical apoptotic morphological features such as shrunken morphology compared with control cells (Fig. 1C). Flow cytometry analysis following annexin V/PI staining revealed that the percentage of annexin V-positive cells increased from 11.69% to 17.61%, and 18.14% as the concentration of SD2-2305 increased from 0 to 7.5, and 15 µM, respectively, for HCT116 cells. Similarly, the ratio of cells undergoing apoptosis increased from 8.13% to 12.63%, and 28.38% as the concentration of SD2-2305 increased (Fig. 1D). In addition, we observed the proteolytic activation of caspase-3, caspase-7, and caspase-9 in HCT116 cells by immunoblot analysis, together with changes in pro-apoptotic or anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 family proteins (Fig. 1E). These results suggest that SD2-2305 induces caspase-dependent apoptosis in CRC cells.

SD2-2305 arrests the cell cycle at the G1 phase and decreases the levels of cellular cyclin/CDK proteins

To determine whether SD2-2305-induced cell death was associated with cell cycle arrest, we analyzed the percentage of cells in each cell cycle phase of CRC cells using flow cytometry. Following treatment with increasing concentrations of SD2-2305, the proportion of cells in the sub-G1 phase increased from 1.1% to 6.79% and 8.51% in HCT116 cells, and from 0.76% to 0.92% and 2.11% in HT-29 cells, further confirming the induction of apoptosis. In addition, the proportion of cells in the G1 phase increased from 37.8% to 48.6% and 50.6% in HCT116 cells, and from 63.1% to 65.3% and 79.6% in HT-29 cells, indicating that SD2-2305 induced G1 phase cell cycle arrest in both CRC cell lines (Fig. 2A, 2B). Immunoblot analysis of cell cycle regulators, including cyclins, Cdks, and cell cycle inhibitor proteins, demonstrated that the expression levels of Cyclin A, Cyclin B, Cdk1, and Cdk2 were dramatically decreased by SD2-2305 treatment in both cell lines, whereas p21 expression was markedly increased (Fig. 2C, 2D). These results indicate that, during SD2-2305-induced apoptosis, the cell cycle is arrested at the G1 phase through the downregulation of cyclin/CDK expression and the upregulation of p21 expression.

SD2-2305-induced apoptosis is not associated with marked changes in JAK/STAT or PI3K/Akt signaling

Next, we investigated the expression and phosphorylation status of key signaling proteins involved in major signaling pathways, including the JAK/STAT and PI3K/Akt pathway, which regulate cancer cell survival, proliferation, and metastasis. Immunoblot analysis performed following SD2-2305 treatment revealed no significant changes in either the protein expression levels or phosphorylation status of JAK2 and STAT3 (Fig. 3A). In addition, analysis of key protein kinases involved in the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, including PI3K, Akt, mTOR, and p70S6K, as well as those associated with the MAPK pathway, showed no marked alterations except for a dramatic decrease in p38 phosphorylation (Fig. 3B, 3C). These results suggest that SD2-2305-induced apoptosis is not accompanied by apparent activation in JAK/STAT or PI3K/Akt signaling pathways, unlike previously reported DRD2 antagonists such as domperidone.

SD2-2305 downregulates HER2 protein levels while upregulating TGF-β receptor 1 in CRC cells

To investigate the upstream signaling pathways involved in SD2-2305-induced apoptosis, the expression levels of major growth factor receptors were examined by immunoblot analysis following SD2-2305 treatment. After treating HCT116 and HT-29 cells with SD2-2305 for 24 h, we observed a dose-dependent decrease in HER2 protein levels and a gradual increase in the expression levels of FGFR3, FGFR4, and TGF-β receptor 1 (TGFBR1) in both cell lines. However, no significant changes in protein expression were observed for EGFR, MET, IGF1R, or TGFBR2, and no consistent responses were detected across both cell lines (Fig. 4A). To determine whether SD2-2305-induced changes in growth factor receptor protein levels were regulated at the transcriptional level, we measured the mRNA expression of HER2, TGFBR1, and TGFBR2 by qRT-PCR. qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated that SD2-2305 treatment regulated the mRNA expression of HER2, TGFBR1, and TGFBR2 in a manner markedly different from their corresponding protein expression patterns, showing no apparent correlation with the observed changes in protein levels (Fig. 4B). These findings suggest that SD2-2305-induced alterations in HER2, TGFBR1, and TGFBR2 protein expression are unlikely to be driven primarily by transcriptional regulation and may instead involve post-transcriptional or post-translational mechanisms.

DISCUSSION

CRC remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide, and patients with advanced or metastatic disease still experience poor long-term survival despite recent therapeutic advances (Bray et al., 2024; Morgan et al., 2023). Although combination chemotherapy and molecularly targeted therapies have improved clinical outcomes, the emergence of drug resistance frequently limits durable treatment responses in CRC patients (Martinelli et al., 2020; Misale et al., 2014; Tang et al., 2023). Immune checkpoint inhibitors have shown significant clinical benefits primarily in mismatch repair-deficient or microsatellite instability-high CRCs, whereas the majority of microsatellite-stable tumors exhibit limited responsiveness to current immunotherapies (Guven et al., 2024). In addition, the marked molecular heterogeneity of CRC and the lack of reliable predictive biomarkers continue to hinder the development of effective personalized therapeutic strategies (Dienstmann et al., 2017; Malla et al., 2022). Therefore, the identification of novel therapeutic targets and the development of effective anticancer agents targeting these molecules are essential for improving therapeutic efficacy and clinical outcomes in patients with CRC (Hwang et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2024).

The WST assay revealed that the viability of CRC cells was reduced by SD2-2305 in a dose- and time-dependent manner (Fig. 1B). Cancer cell proliferation can be suppressed either through the activation of apoptotic pathways or the inhibition of survival signaling pathways (Moyer et al., 2025), and the induction of apoptosis has emerged as a promising strategy for anticancer therapy (Kang et al., 2023). Morphological observations showed that SD2-2305 induced cell rounding and typical apoptotic morphological features in HCT116 cells, whereas it suppressed cell proliferation and caused the cells to adopt a shrunken morphology in HT-29 cells. Since we observed that SD2-2305 reduced the viability of the CRC cells HCT116 and HT-29, we investigated whether SD2-2305 induces apoptosis in these two cell lines. The annexin V/PI apoptosis assay demonstrated that SD2-2305 significantly increased the proportion of apoptotic cells (Fig. 1D). Since activation of the caspase cascade is a hallmark event during apoptosis progression (Budihardjo et al., 1999), we further investigated the molecular mechanisms underlying SD2-2305-induced apoptosis and observed the activation of caspase-3, -7, and -9, together with pro- or antiapoptotic Bcl-2 family proteins (Fig. 1E).

Since cell cycle regulation plays a critical role in the progression of apoptosis (Vermeulen et al., 2003), we analyzed the cell cycle distribution of the CRC cells HCT116 and HT-29. SD2-2305 treatment increased the proportion of cells in the sub-G1 phase in both cell lines, consistent with the induction of apoptosis. In addition, SD2-2305 treatment increased the proportion of cells in the G1 phase in both cell lines (Fig. 2A, 2B). Moreover, analysis of the intracellular expression levels of cell cycle inhibitors and p53 revealed a marked increase in p21 expression in both cell lines, while p27 expression was additionally increased in HT-29 cells. The expression level of p53, a transcription factor involved in the regulation of cell cycle inhibitor expression, remained unchanged or was slightly decreased. In contrast, p16 was not detected in either cell line (Fig. 2C, 2D). These results suggest that the SD2-2305-induced downregulation of cell cycle regulators and upregulation of cell cycle inhibitors contribute to the induction of apoptosis in CRC cell lines.

The survival of cancer cells is regulated by multiple signaling pathways, including the PI3K/Akt, JAK2/STAT3, and mTOR pathways (Mengie Ayele et al., 2022; Porta et al., 2014; Rascio et al., 2021). Aberrant activation of the JAK2/STAT3 signaling axis has been implicated in cancer cell proliferation and tumor progression (Park et al., 2022). STAT3 is a well-established transcription factor that regulates diverse biological processes, such as cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis (Li et al., 2023). Upon phosphorylation by JAK kinases, STAT3 undergoes dimerization and subsequently translocates to the nucleus, where it promotes the transcription of various oncogenes. In this study, we examined whether SD2-2305 interfered with the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway in CRC cells. We found that the protein and phosphorylation levels of JAK2 and STAT3 were not altered by SD2-2305 treatment (Fig. 3A), with the exception of an increase in STAT3 Tyr 701 phosphorylation that occurred in the absence of consistent changes in other pathway components and in parallel with apoptotic cell death. Therefore, this phenomenon is more likely to represent a compensatory or stress response than a primary pro-survival signal. We also analyzed the protein and phosphorylation levels of protein kinases related to cell survival signaling, such as PI3K/Akt, mTOR-S6K, and MAPK pathways. However, we did not observe significant changes in these kinases in either protein expression or phosphorylation (Fig. 3B, 3C). Previous studies have reported that DRD2 antagonism induces apoptosis through suppression of STAT signaling, as well as through reductions in Akt phosphorylation, mTOR activity, and ERK signaling antagonists (Shakya et al., 2023; Tegowski et al., 2018). However, these effects were not observed in apoptosis induced by the newly synthesized DRD2 antagonist, SD2-2305. These findings indicate that SD2-2305-induced apoptosis is not associated with major inhibitory or activating effects on canonical JAK2–STAT3, PI3K–Akt, mTOR–S6K, or MAPK signaling under the conditions tested, and suggest that other mechanisms are primarily responsible for its pro-apoptotic activity.

Although DRD2 is a G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR), it is capable of cross-talk with receptor tyrosine kinases such as EGFR, and DRD2 expression has been reported to be associated with prognosis and therapeutic response in HER2-positive breast cancer (Tan et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2005). Therefore, we investigated the effects of SD2-2305 on the expression levels of receptor proteins closely associated with cancer development and progression, including EGFR, MET, HER2, FGFR, and TGFBR. Immunoblot analysis of growth factor receptors revealed that SD2-2305 treatment markedly decreased HER2 expression and increased the expression of TGFBR1, a key mediator involved in apoptosis-inducing signaling pathways (Fig. 4A). Further investigation is needed to determine how increased TGFBR1 expression affects CRC cell viability. Although FGFR3 expression was elevated in both cell lines, this response is considered to represent a compensatory mechanism against apoptosis or cellular stress: Co-treatment with a STAT3 inhibitor may synergistically enhance SD2-2305–induced apoptosis. TGFBR2 expression was decreased in HCT116 cells but increased in HT-29 cells, suggesting that the two cell lines exhibit distinct responses to SD2-2305 treatment.

Our results suggest that SD2-2305 induces apoptosis in CRC cells through a mechanism distinct from the domperidone-induced apoptosis (Shakya et al., 2023; Sim et al., 2024). Importantly, the lack of concordance between mRNA and protein changes for HER2, TGFBR1, and TGFBR2 suggests that SD2-2305 influences these receptors predominantly through post-transcriptional mechanisms. Further experiments measuring protein stability, such as cycloheximide chase experiments, would help verify the role of SD2-2305 in this regard. Taken together with the observed G1 arrest and caspase-dependent apoptosis, these data support a model in which SD2-2305 exerts its anticancer effects primarily through coordinated modulation of growth factor receptor signaling and cell-cycle regulators, rather than through direct inhibition of classical survival kinase cascades (Fig. 5). This approach could pave the way for better treatment strategies, either by targeting DRD2 alone or by combining SD2-2305 with standard chemotherapeutics.

This study also has limitations. Our mechanistic analysis was confined to in vitro experiments in two CRC cell lines, and the downstream events linking receptor changes to apoptosis remain unclear. SD2-2305 was initially designed based on structure-activity relationships, and the HitHunter system (Eurofins DiscoverX, Fremont, CA, USA) was employed to evaluate its DRD2 antagonism prior to assessing its anticancer activity. We have observed that DRD2 knockdown induces apoptosis in CRC cells, and a detailed mechanistic study is currently ongoing (unpublished data). In addition, our cell-based findings would require in vivo validation, such as tumor xenograft models. Despite these limitations, our findings identify SD2-2305 as a promising DRD2 antagonist candidate that induces G1 arrest and caspase-dependent apoptosis in CRC cells and highlight growth factor receptor remodeling as a potential mechanism to overcome resistance to existing treatment modalities.
