Post‐diagnosis smoking habit change and incident dementia in cancer survivors
Authors: Hyeok‐Hee Lee, Jaeun Ahn, Changchuan Jiang, Young‐gun Lee, Hyeon Chang Kim, Hokyou Lee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONMany individuals change their smoking habits after cancer diagnosis. We aimed to evaluate the association of post‐diagnosis smoking habit change with incident dementia in cancer survivors.METHODSWe identified 558,127 individuals who were diagnosed with cancer at age ≥ 20 and survived for ≥ 3 years. Participants were classified into four groups: (1) sustained non‐smokers, (2) initiators/relapsers, (3) quitters, and (4) continuing smokers. Dementia risk in each group was assessed using a cause‐specific Cox model.RESULTSAfter cancer diagnosis, 2.3% of pre‐diagnosis non‐smokers initiated/relapsed into smoking, while 51.7% of pre‐diagnosis smokers quit smoking. Compared to sustained non‐smokers, multivariable‐adjusted risk of dementia was 29% higher among initiators/relapsers, 11% higher among quitters, and 31% higher among continuing smokers. Compared to continuing smokers, the risk was 15% lower among quitters.DISCUSSIONIn cancer survivors, smoking initiation/relapse was associated with increased risk of dementia, whereas smoking cessation was associated with decreased risk of dementia.Highlights Approximately half of pre‐diagnosis smokers quit smoking after a cancer diagnosis.Smoking cessation was associated with a 15% reduced risk of dementia.More than 2% of pre‐diagnosis non‐smokers initiated or relapsed into smoking after a cancer diagnosis.Smoking initiation/relapse was associated with a 29% elevated risk of dementia. Keywords: cancer, dementia, smoking habit change, survivor, tobacco

BACKGROUND

Cancer and dementia are among the major causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide. 1 , 2 Their incidence continues to grow with population aging, thereby posing substantial economic and health‐care burden on society. 2 , 3 Notably, a high incidence of dementia has been reported among individuals with cancer, 4 , 5 presumably due to their extended lifespan, 6 shared risk factors between cancer and dementia, and chemotherapy‐induced neurocognitive sequelae. 7 , 8 The prevention of dementia is thus a high public health priority for this vulnerable population.

Despite the well‐established detrimental effects of tobacco consumption on health outcomes—including dementia—in the general population, 9 , 10 , 11 many cancer survivors still fail to quit smoking. 12 Continued smoking after a cancer diagnosis has been associated with increased risks of poor treatment response, treatment‐related toxicities, and cancer recurrence. 13 However, the association of smoking habit, especially post‐diagnosis changes, with incident dementia in cancer survivors remains uncertain. Given the particular significance of adverse health consequences and socioeconomic burden associated with dementia in this high‐risk population, understanding the link between changes in smoking habits and dementia risk would be crucial to improve the prognosis and well‐being of cancer survivors.

Using a Korean nationwide health claims database, we investigated the association of post‐diagnosis smoking habit change with the risk of dementia in cancer survivors.

METHODS

Data source

The National Health Insurance Service (NHIS), a single provider of universal health‐care coverage in Korea, provides a nationwide database encompassing sociodemographic details; reimbursement claims based on the International Classification of Disease, 10th revision (ICD‐10) coding; general health examination results; and death information of the entire Korean population. 14 General health examinations are provided biennially by the NHIS to all Korean adults, during which health‐related lifestyles, clinical metrics, and biochemical parameters are assessed at designated health screening institutions. The quality of the examination is overseen and controlled in accordance with relevant national laws and regulations. 15 Further details of the NHIS database and health examination are described elsewhere. 14 , 15 , 16

This study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki; the study protocol was approved by the institutional review board of Severance Hospital, Seoul, Korea (4‐2022‐0605). Written informed consent was waived as this study was based on de‐identified administrative data.

Systematic review: We reviewed the literature using traditional sources (e.g., PubMed). Study population

Individuals with cancer were identified by the ICD‐10 codes (Table S1 in supporting information) and the rare and intractable disease code (V193) for cancer. 17 , 18 Between January 1, 2005, and December 31, 2014, a total of 2,095,550 adults aged ≥ 20 years were newly diagnosed with cancer. Among 1,509,973 (72.1%) 3‐year survivors, we excluded participants who did not undergo either pre‐ or post‐diagnosis health examination within 3 years or those who had dementia or mild cognitive disorder before the index date (i.e., date of 3‐year survival; Figure S1 in supporting information). This resulted in a final study cohort of 558,127 participants (Figure S2 in supporting information). The period of 3 years was chosen a priori based on previous literature as well as anticipated follow‐up duration. 19 , 20 , 21

Post‐diagnosis smoking habit change

Pre‐diagnosis smoking habit was evaluated during the last examination within 3 years before a cancer diagnosis (Exam 1); post‐diagnosis smoking habit was evaluated during the last examination within 3 years after a cancer diagnosis (Exam 2; Figure S1). In both exams, participants’ smoking habits were self‐reported based on standardized questionnaires and classified into non‐smoking (never or past smokers) and smoking (current smokers).

We assessed post‐diagnosis changes in smoking habits by comparing participants’ smoking habits in Exams 1 and 2: those who were not smoking in Exam 1 were categorized as (1) “sustained non‐smokers” if they were still not smoking in Exam 2, or as (2) “initiators or relapsers” if they were smoking in Exam 2. Participants who were smoking in Exam 1 were categorized as (3) “quitters” if they were no longer smoking in Exam 2, or as (4) “continuing smokers” if they were still smoking in Exam 2. The median (interquartile range) time interval between Exam 1 and a cancer diagnosis was 0.6 (0.1–1.3) years, and that between a cancer diagnosis and Exam 2 was 2.0 (1.5–2.5) years. The observed changes in smoking habits were hence regarded to have occurred after a cancer diagnosis.

Covariables

Data on other health‐related lifestyles (e.g., alcohol consumption and physical activity), clinical metrics (e.g., body mass index [BMI] and blood pressure [BP]), and biochemical parameters (e.g., fasting glucose and total cholesterol) were collected during Exam 2. Health‐related lifestyles were self‐reported by participants using standardized questionnaires. Clinical metrics were assessed by trained health‐care personnel in line with the protocols endorsed by the NHIS. Biochemical parameters were measured by each health screening institution, as overseen by the Korean Association of Laboratory Quality Control. 15 Less than 3% of the health examination results had missing data on covariables; only complete examinations were used for the analysis. BP‐, glucose‐, or lipid‐lowering drug use, neuropsychiatric comorbidities (cerebrovascular disease, psychotic disorder, mood disorder, anxiety disorder, and sleep disorder; Table S2 in supporting information), and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) 22 were determined from insurance claims during a 3‐year look‐back period from the index date. Details on cancer, including the type (location), presence of distant metastasis at initial diagnosis, and chemotherapy and external beam radiotherapy use, were also collected from the claims data.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was all‐cause dementia, defined as ≥ 1 hospitalization record or ≥ 2 outpatient records with a diagnosis code for dementia (ICD‐10: F00‐F03, G30). 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 The secondary outcomes included each subtype of dementia, namely Alzheimer's disease (AD), vascular dementia, and other dementia (Table S2). Participants were followed up from the index date (i.e., date of 3‐year survival) until the occurrence of an outcome event, death, or December 31, 2021, whichever came earliest (Figure S1).

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were presented as median (interquartile range) or number (%) as appropriate. We estimated the cumulative incidence of dementia while accounting for the competing risk of all‐cause death using the Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard model, adjusting for age, sex, household income quartile, and residential area. 27 Incidence rates were calculated as the number of outcome events per 1000 person‐years of follow‐up. We calculated hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of dementia and its subtypes for each smoking habit change group using the cause‐specific Cox proportional hazard model, accounting for a competing risk of all‐cause death. 27 , 28 The models were adjusted for age, sex, household income quartile, residential area, alcohol consumption, physical activity, BMI category (kg/m2; < 18.5, 18.5–22.9, 23–24.9, and ≥ 25), systolic BP, fasting glucose, total cholesterol, BP‐lowering drug use, glucose‐lowering drug use, lipid‐lowering drug use, cerebrovascular disease, psychotic disorder, mood disorder, anxiety disorder, sleep disorder, CCI category (0, 1, 2, and ≥ 3), cancer type, presence of distant metastasis at initial diagnosis, year of cancer diagnosis, and time‐varying chemotherapy and external beam radiotherapy use. Covariables were selected a priori based on their possible associations with smoking habit and dementia. 29 The proportionality of hazards was confirmed via Schoenfeld residuals and visual inspection of log‐minus‐log plots.

In cancer‐specific analyses, we investigated the association of post‐diagnosis smoking habit change with dementia risk among seven major cancer types/groups in Korea (i.e., upper gastrointestinal [GI], lower GI, hepatobiliary/pancreatic/other GI, lung, breast/female genital organs, male genital organs, and hematologic cancers). In subgroup analyses, participants were stratified by age, sex, overweight/obesity, hypertension, diabetes, hypercholesterolemia, CCI, presence of distant metastasis, year of cancer diagnosis, and receipt of chemotherapy prior to index date. In sensitivity analyses, we: (1) excluded the first 2 years of follow‐up, as an incident dementia during this early period may not have been affected by changes in smoking habits; (2) further included a quadratic term for age in our multivariable‐adjusted model, given the considerable effect of age on the risk of dementia; (3) additionally adjusted for smoking pack‐years when comparing quitters with continuing smokers; and (4) repeated the main analysis in a 5‐year cancer survivor cohort.

We conducted the following secondary analyses to identify any potential heterogeneity in dementia risk within the study groups. First, we subcategorized initiators/relapsers into initiators (N = 6154) and relapsers (N = 4267). Second, we subcategorized continuing smokers into non‐reducers (< 50% reduction, maintenance, or increase in daily tobacco use; N = 22,315) and reducers (≥ 50% reduction in daily tobacco use; N = 5219). 21 , 30 All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc.) and R version 4.0.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

A total of 558,127 cancer survivors (median age, 59 years; 51.2% female) were included in the study. Among 456,433 pre‐diagnosis non‐smokers, 446,012 (97.7%) sustained non‐smoking and 10,421 (2.3%) initiated or relapsed into smoking after a cancer diagnosis. Among 101,694 pre‐diagnosis smokers, 52,600 (51.7%) quit smoking and 49,094 (48.3%) continued smoking after a cancer diagnosis. Females comprised 62% of sustained non‐smokers, while males accounted for > 80% of the other groups. Compared to sustained non‐smokers or quitters, initiators/relapsers and continuing smokers had lower household income, lived more commonly in rural areas, drank more often, exercised less often, were more frequently on glucose‐lowering drugs, showed higher prevalence of cerebrovascular disease and psychotic disorder and lower prevalence of sleep disorder, and had higher CCI (Table 1). The use of chemotherapy, broken down by anticancer drug classes, and external beam radiotherapy is summarized in Table S3 in supporting information. Figure S3 in supporting information describes the patterns of post‐diagnosis smoking habit change according to cancer type.

Primary analyses

During a median follow‐up of 7.4 years, there were 51,904 incident cases of all‐cause dementia. Of these events, 47,615 (91.7%) occurred at age ≥ 65 years. The cumulative incidence of dementia was the highest among continuing smokers, followed by initiators/relapsers, quitters, and sustained non‐smokers (Figure 1). Compared to sustained non‐smokers, multivariable‐adjusted risk of dementia was 29% higher among initiators/relapsers (HR, 1.29; 95% CI, 1.21–1.37), 11% higher among quitters (HR, 1.11; 95% CI, 1.07–1.15), and 31% higher among continuing smokers (HR, 1.31; 95% CI, 1.26–1.35; Figure 2; Table S4 in supporting information). Compared to continuing smokers, multivariable‐adjusted risk of dementia was 15% lower among quitters (HR, 0.85; 95% CI, 0.81–0.89; Table S4). The findings were consistent for all subtypes of dementia, including AD, vascular dementia, and other dementia (Figure 3, Table S5 in supporting information).

In cancer‐specific analyses, the risk of dementia was higher in initiators/relapsers than in sustained non‐smokers among upper GI, lower GI, hepatobiliary/pancreatic/other GI, lung, breast/female genital organ, male genital organ, and hematologic cancer survivors (Table 2). Quitters showed a lower risk of dementia than continuing smokers among upper GI, lower GI, hepatobiliary/pancreatic/other GI, lung, male genital organ, and hematologic cancer survivors (Table S6 in supporting information). In subgroup analyses, the associations of post‐diagnosis smoking habit change with dementia risk were generally consistent regardless of age, sex, overweight/obesity, hypertension, diabetes, hypercholesterolemia, CCI, presence of distant metastasis, year of cancer diagnosis, and receipt of chemotherapy prior to index date (Table S7 in supporting information). In sensitivity analyses, (1) excluding the first 2 years of follow‐up or (2) further including a quadratic term for age in the multivariable‐adjusted model minimally attenuated our main findings (Figures S4, S5 in supporting information); (3) additional adjustment for smoking pack‐years did not materially alter the association of quitters versus continuing smokers with the risk of dementia (HR, 0.77; 95% CI, 0.73–0.82); and (4) the results of the main analysis were replicated in a 5‐year cancer survivor cohort (Figure S6 in supporting information).

Secondary analyses

First, smoking relapsers were comparable to initiators in terms of dementia risk (HR, 0.95; 95% CI, 0.84–1.07). Second, smoking reducers did not show a lower risk of dementia than non‐reducers (HR, 0.97; 95% CI, 0.86–1.10; Table S8 in supporting information).

DISCUSSION

In this nationwide cohort study of all adult cancer survivors in Korea, 51.7% of pre‐diagnosis smokers quit smoking after a cancer diagnosis. Smoking cessation was associated with a 15% reduction in the risk of all‐cause dementia, as well as with decreased risks of AD, vascular dementia, and other dementia, compared to continued smoking. In contrast, 2.3% of pre‐diagnosis non‐smokers initiated or relapsed into smoking after a cancer diagnosis. These individuals faced a 29% elevated risk of all‐cause dementia and a higher risk of each dementia subtype compared to sustained non‐smokers. The associations remained robust across diverse subgroups and sensitivity analyses. Notably, reducing smoking without completely quitting after a cancer diagnosis did not confer a reduced risk of dementia.

Cancer and dementia share common risk factors and pathogenic processes, such as aging, inflammation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and aberrant cell‐cycle activation. 31 Patients with cancer also frequently report cognitive impairment during or after chemotherapy, which has been reported to share similarities with the pathological processes and clinical manifestations of age‐related neurodegenerative disorders. 7 , 8 These mechanisms may explain, at least in part, the high incidence of dementia among individuals with cancer. 4 , 5 In our study, 1.3% of cancer survivors developed dementia each year, with the annual incidence rate rising to 3.3% among those aged ≥ 65 years. In light of rapid population aging and increasing cancer survival rates, our findings underscore the importance of dementia as a target for prevention to improve the long‐term health and well‐being of cancer survivors.

Tobacco smoking is a widely recognized risk factor for both AD and vascular dementia. 9 , 10 Previous meta‐analyses of prospective studies reported that smoking was associated with ≈ 30% higher risk of all‐cause dementia, with similar relative risks observed between AD and vascular dementia. 9 , 10 Smoking‐induced cerebral oxidative stress triggers the deposition of amyloid and tau proteins, potentially leading to neurodegeneration and ultimately elevating the risk of AD. 32 Smoking is also a well‐established risk factor of cerebral infarction, 33 a key pathophysiological mechanism of vascular dementia. Smoking cessation is expected to slow or halt the aforementioned processes and has been reported to be associated with a lower risk of dementia. 34 , 35 However, previous studies investigating the associations of tobacco smoking and its cessation with dementia have largely been conducted among generally healthy populations. Our study extends the prior knowledge to cancer survivorship care by demonstrating that smoking cessation after a cancer diagnosis is associated with a reduced risk of all‐cause dementia, as well as AD, vascular dementia, and other dementia, while smoking initiation or relapse is associated with an elevated risk of dementia and all of its subtypes.

Dementia is associated with diminished health‐related quality of life, loss of self‐care, and elevated risks of complications. 36 In the context of cancer survival, these adverse consequences of dementia may hinder adherence to physician advice, treatment compliance, and surveillance for recurrence, potentially leading to a worsened prognosis. Moreover, the development of dementia during the survivorship phase could further exacerbate the socioeconomic burden faced by caregivers. In this regard, strategies focusing on dementia prevention hold substantial significance for the rapidly growing community of cancer survivors and their families.

The National Comprehensive Cancer Network Survivorship guidelines strongly advocate for smoking cessation as part of routine care for all cancer survivors. 37 Yet, our study found that about half of smokers continued to smoke after a cancer diagnosis. Additionally, > 2% of non‐smokers either initiated or relapsed into smoking after the diagnosis. These post‐diagnosis changes in smoking habits, combined with their observed association with incident dementia, highlight the urgent need for efforts to discourage smoking as a measure for dementia prevention in cancer survivors. Notably, while complete cessation of smoking was associated with a lower risk of dementia, merely reducing smoking post‐diagnosis did not show a similar association. This is in line with previous findings observed in the general population 38 and suggests that smoking cessation initiatives should aim at complete abstinence, rather than at reduction, to alleviate the burden of dementia and improve the prognosis in cancer survivors.

This study has several notable features. First, to our knowledge, it represents the first and most comprehensive investigation to date exploring the association between post‐diagnosis changes in smoking habits and the risk of dementia in adult cancer survivors. Second, large sample size and number of events allowed us to perform granular analyses across diverse dementia subtypes, subgroups, and cancer types. Third, we could identify post‐diagnosis smoking initiators or relapsers, who represent a small proportion (< 2%) of cancer survivors, and explore their risk of dementia.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, owing to the observational nature of the study, the possibility of residual confounding cannot be excluded. Nonetheless, we did adjust for a variety of covariables, including neuropsychiatric comorbidities and cancer characteristics. Second, subclinical cognitive impairment or undiagnosed dementia may have affected an individual's smoking habit change (i.e., reverse causation). To account for this, we excluded participants with mild cognitive disorder from our main study population. Moreover, the findings were consistent in our sensitivity analysis that excluded the first 2 years of follow‐up. Third, smoking status was determined through self‐reported questionnaires rather than through biochemical tests. Yet, self‐reports of tobacco consumption have been shown to be reasonably accurate, 39 especially among patients with newly diagnosed cancer. 40 Fourth, the follow‐up duration was relatively short (median 7.4 years). However, the consistency of our results in an earlier‐diagnosed subgroup (median follow‐up, 10.6 years) and in a sensitivity analysis that excluded the first 2 years of follow‐up provides some reassurance. Fifth, we used hospital diagnosis codes (i.e., ICD‐10) to define incident dementia. Although the ICD‐10 codes for dementia have been validated in other administrative data and widely used in prior population‐based studies, 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 their accuracy warrants further validation in the NHIS database. Last, our findings may not be generalizable to those who were excluded from the study due to non‐participation in health examinations. Nevertheless, the baseline characteristics of the survivors who participated in pre‐ and post‐diagnosis health examinations were in broad agreement with those of the survivors who did not.

In conclusion, cancer survivors who quit smoking after their cancer diagnosis experienced a decreased risk of dementia, whereas those who initiated or relapsed into smoking faced an increased risk of dementia. These findings emphasize the importance of directing attention toward smoking prevention initiatives as a measure to reduce the burden of dementia among cancer survivors.
