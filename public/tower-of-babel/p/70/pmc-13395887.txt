The human placenta development and anatomy
Authors: Dorcas Quaye, Peter Ofori Appiah, Dorotheah Obiri, John Ahenkorah, Kwame Adu-Bonsaffoh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The human placenta is a specialized fetomaternal organ whose architecture evolves throughout pregnancy. Its development begins with implantation, followed by the differentiation of trophoblast lineages that establish the early villous structures and mediate subsequent exchange between the maternal and fetal circulations. By the end of the second trimester, coordinated remodeling of villous stroma, vasculature, and syncytium transforms the placenta into a highly specialized interface capable of meeting the growing demands of pregnancy. Despite extensive research in this area, most developmental accounts emphasize early morphogenesis, leaving the structural transitions of mid- and late gestation underexplored These later phases are critical for understanding how architectural specialization supports placental function and how deviations in these processes underpin pregnancy complications. This review synthesizes current understanding of placental morphogenesis and histomorphology across gestation. It integrates classical placental histology findings with recent advances in three-dimensional imaging, lineage tracing, and vascular modeling. By combining developmental and structural perspectives, this review offers an updated understanding of how the architecture of the placenta evolves to sustain pregnancy.

Introduction

The placenta is a transient fetomaternal organ that plays a central role in eutherian reproduction. Human placental development begins with implantation, when the trophectoderm differentiates into distinct trophoblast lineages that invade the maternal endometrium and establish the foundations of the villous tree. With continued branching and vascular remodeling, these early structures expand into a highly perfused organ capable of meeting the increasing metabolic and physiological demands of gestation [1, 2]. By week 34–36, distinct histological changes occur, including thinning of the trophoblastic layers, increased capillarization of the villous core, and a reduction in stromal density. These microstructural changes shorten diffusion distance and enhance exchange efficiency as pregnancy approaches term [3–5]. Each stage of this process, from early implantation events to the emergence of distinct villous subtypes, represents a carefully timed adaptation that ensures the placenta is able to meet the growing metabolic and physiological demands of pregnancy.

At full term, the human placenta typically weighs 500–600 g, measures 15–25 cm in diameter, and has a central thickness of 2–3 cm [5, 6]. The mature placenta assumes a typically discoid or oval shape, with a smooth fetal surface covered by the amnion and a lobulated maternal surface divided by decidual septa [7]. These features define the final anatomical configuration of the human placenta.

While implantation and early trophoblast differentiation have been extensively characterized, the histomorphological transformations that occur during the second and third trimesters have received comparatively less attention. This maybe due to ethical and technical constraints associated with studying the human placenta in vivo. During this later period, the placenta undergoes profound histological and structural remodeling, which determines the placenta’s final efficiency and functional capacity. This review provides an integrative synthesis of human placental development and histomorphology, tracing its progression from implantation through late gestational maturation. The review emphasizes how sequential structural adaptations culminate in the formation of the mature placenta.

Development of the human placenta

Implantation and early placental morphogenesis

Fertilization results in the formation of a zygote, which undergoes successive mitotic divisions (cleavage) to form a morula and subsequently a blastocyst [8] (Fig. 1). The blastocyst consists of an inner cell mass (embryoblast), which forms the embryo proper, and an outer trophoblast layer, which contributes to placental development [2]. Upon reaching the uterine cavity at approximately days 4–5 post-fertilization, the blastocyst sheds the zona pellucida, a process termed hatching, and aligns its embryonic pole with the receptive endometrial surface [9]. Implantation occurs in distinct but overlapping phases: apposition, where the blastocyst loosely aligns with the endometrial epithelium; adhesion, which involves receptor-mediated attachment between trophoblast cells and the epithelial surface; and invasion, wherein trophoblasts breach the basement membrane and penetrate the underlying stroma [10]. This coordinated sequence is tightly regulated by cytokines, growth factors, and proteases that modulate tissue receptivity and trophoblast invasiveness and establish the foundation for placental morphogenesis and fetal–maternal interface formation [1]. By days 6–7, the trophoblast undergoes lineage differentiation into cytotrophoblasts (CTBs) and syncytiotrophoblasts (STBs). By day 7 post-fertilization, implantation is underway, and the syncytiotrophoblast begins to invade the superficial layers of the endometrial lining of the uterus. This invasion triggers profound changes in the maternal endometrium. This transforms the maternal endometrium into specialized tissue known as the decidua, a hormonally modulated and immunologically tolerant environment supportive of embryonic development [13]. At this stage, CTB proliferation, particularly at the embryonic pole, results in the expansion of the syncytiotrophoblast, which gradually envelops the developing conceptus and forms the external covering of the gestational sac [14]. At approximately 8–9 days post-fertilization, the syncytiotrophoblast develops multiple small vacuoles [15]. These vacuoles enlarge and coalesce to form a network of interconnected spaces, eventually giving rise to the lacunar system, an early precursor to the intervillous space [1, 16]. Simultaneously, the syncytiotrophoblast continues to project into the maternal decidua, where the syncytiotrophoblast forms finger-like trabeculae that initially erode and tap into the maternal endometrial glands, which release secretory products rich in glycogen, lipids, and growth factors, collectively referred to as histotrophs [16, 17]. Histotrophic nutrition sustains the developing embryo during the avascular phase of early development [17].

By days 11 to 12 post-fertilization, the invasiveness of the syncytiotrophoblast intensifies as it reaches and breaches the walls of maternal capillaries. This penetration allows maternal blood to flow directly into the expanding lacunar system, establishing primitive uteroplacental circulation [9]. Chorionic villous formation

The underlying cytotrophoblasts proliferate rapidly, forming solid cellular protrusions that extend into the overlying syncytial trabeculae, creating the primary villi [15] (Fig. 2). These structures appear around day 12 post-fertilization and represent the first morphological signs of villous placental architecture [20]. By day 14, mesenchymal cells invade the core of these primary villi, converting them into secondary villi [2]. The mesenchymal core subsequently undergoes vasculogenesis and angiogenesis to form a primitive capillary network by day 21, giving rise to tertiary or definitive villi, which are the essential structural and functional units of the placenta [2, 16]. Villus vessels gradually expand and begin to form connections with larger vessels developing in the chorionic plate, extending into the connecting stalk, a primitive structure that contains the developing umbilical arteries and vein [12]. The formation of these connections establishes the extraembryonic vascular system, bridging the placenta with the embryonic body [21].

Spiral artery remodeling and placental circulation

Around the eighth week of gestation, remodeling of the maternal spiral arteries begins. This process is closely associated with the formation of the cytotrophoblastic shell, which arises from cytotrophoblast cells extending through the overlying syncytiotrophoblast layer [22] (Fig. 3). These extensions anchor the developing placenta to the maternal decidua basalis [23]. From the distal ends of these anchoring villi, a specialized population of cytotrophoblasts, extravillous trophoblasts (EVTs), differentiate and invade the maternal uterine tissues [24]. These cells migrate along two main pathways: interstitial EVTs, which invade the decidual stroma, and endovascular EVTs, which specifically target the lumens of the spiral arteries [12]. Interstitial EVTs play a role in modifying the extracellular matrix of the decidua and preparing the stromal environment for vessel remodeling [19]. Moreover, endovascular EVTs enter the spiral arteries, where they plug the lumen temporarily and progressively replace the maternal endothelial lining through epithelial‒endothelial transition (EET) [19, 25]. During this transformation, EVTs contribute to the destruction of the tunica media, including the smooth muscle and elastic components of the vessel wall [22, 26]. This restructuring leads to dilation of the vessel lumen and the loss of vasoreactivity, effectively converting the spiral arteries into high-capacity, low-resistance channels that deliver large volumes of maternal blood directly into the intervillous space at lower pressures [21, 23, 26]. The change in blood flow velocity from an initial velocity of 2–3 m/s in unremodeled arteries to approximately 0.2 m/s after remodeling prevents shear stress damage to the delicate villous architecture and ensures optimal uteroplacental perfusion [12, 27]. Importantly, this process is not solely mediated by fetal trophoblasts. Mesenchymal villi

Mesenchymal villi represent the earliest form of definitive/tertiary villi, are predominantly found during the first trimester, and function as progenitors for all subsequent villous types [2, 31]. Structurally, they are small (approximately 40–80 µm in diameter) and exhibit a loose connective tissue matrix enriched with collagen type I, scattered fibroblasts, and Hofbauer cells; fetal macrophages are believed to play a role in immune tolerance and vasculogenesis [32]. Mesenchymal villi are also characterized by rudimentary fetal capillaries, which lack sinusoidal dilatation with avascular regions at the tips, known as villous sprouts [31]. These villi are enveloped by two distinct trophoblastic layers: an inner cytotrophoblast layer encasing the stromal core and an outer syncytiotrophoblast layer lining the villous surface [33]. Early endothelial cells and small vascular precursors may be present, but their functional contribution to exchange is absent at this stage. By term, mesenchymal villi represent only approximately 1% of the total villous volume, having differentiated into other more specialized types [29].

Immature intermediate villi

From week 8 of gestation, mesenchymal villi progressively develop into immature intermediate villi [29]. These villi serve as transitional structures with increased cellular complexity and proliferative capacity. With diameters ranging from 100 to 300 µm, the stroma is composed of loose reticular tissue, a modest extracellular matrix, and a relatively high concentration of Hofbauer cells and nascent vascular channels [2, 34]. A defining histological feature is the presence of stromal channels—fluid-filled cavities along the roll axis of the villi and surrounded by fibroblasts. The physiological role remains speculative but may be related to stromal expansion [29]. Although still underdeveloped in terms of vascularization, these villi perform exchange functions in the absence of terminal villi and serve as the major structural units for branching during the early and middle-second trimesters [35].

Stem villi

Stem villi arise from further differentiation of mesenchymal or immature intermediate villi and function primarily as mechanical support scaffolds of the villous tree [32, 36]. With diameters ranging from 100 µm to several millimeters, they contain denser fibrous stroma and are traversed by larger-calibrated fetal vessels, including arteries and veins that constitute the fetal vascular network [1, 30, 34]. These vessels are directly connected to the chorionic plate and umbilical circulation via the villous trunk system [2]. The stem villi are also responsible for anchoring the villous structure to the decidua. As pregnancy progresses, the trophoblastic covering of the stem villi is partially replaced by fibrin-type fibrinoid material [15]. Owing to the sparse presence of fetal capillaries and degenerative alterations in the trophoblastic layer, stem villi contribute minimally to fetomaternal exchange and endocrine function. At term, they account for approximately 20–25% of the total villous volume [32].

Mature intermediate villi

During the second trimester, mesenchymal cells within the immature intermediate villi undergo further maturation into mature intermediate villi, which act as transitional units between the stem and terminal villi [2, 9]. These villi measure between 80–120 µm in diameter and show increased vascularization, decreased stromal bulk, and a denser endothelial network suitable for facilitating the initial stages of fetomaternal exchange [32, 33]. By term, they contribute approximately 25% to the total villous volume. Importantly, mature intermediate villi serve as the origin points for the terminal villi, which are considered the primary exchange units of the mature placenta [36].

Terminal villi

The terminal villi represent the final stage of villous development, typically emerging around the 27th week of gestation [34, 36]. Rather than forming de novo, terminal villi arise as capillary-rich protrusions from the surface of mature intermediate villi in response to increasing fetal metabolic demands [2]. These structures are characterized by their small size (50–100 µm in diameter; ~ 200 µm in length), high surface-area-to-volume ratio, and extensive capillarization, features that maximize the efficiency of transplacental exchange [4, 29]. The stromal core is markedly attenuated, and dilated sinusoids facilitate close apposition between fetal and maternal blood supplies [35]. At term, terminal villi constitute approximately 40% of the placental villous volume, reflecting their dominant role in fetomaternal exchange [32]. Their formation is essential for adequate placental function, and impairments in their development are strongly associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes, including intrauterine growth restriction and preeclampsia [11].

The stem villi serve as the primary structural components of placental cotyledons. From each stem villus, approximately three to five intermediate villi emerge, which subsequently give rise to approximately ten to twelve terminal villi [38].

Structural and functional adaptations of the placenta in the second and third trimesters

Hematotrophic nutrition and functional maturation

During the transition from the first to the second trimester, the human placenta shifts from histotrophic to hematotrophic nutrition, marking a pivotal phase in its functional maturation [39] (Fig. 5). At approximately 11–13 weeks of gestation, the maternal spiral arteries remodel sufficiently to allow controlled maternal blood flow into the intervillous space [40]. By the beginning of the second trimester (week 14), the placenta is considered structurally and functionally mature. At this stage, the placenta is capable of executing all its primary roles: nutrient and gas exchange, hormone production, barrier protection, and immune modulation [16]. Trophoblast cells, particularly syncytiotrophoblasts, begin secreting high levels of placental hormones such as human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), human placental lactogen (hPL), progesterone, and estrogens [41–43]. These hormones are critical for sustaining pregnancy and modulating maternal physiology.

Formation of the vasculosyncytial membrane

As gestation progresses through the second trimester, the placenta undergoes continuous remodeling to optimize maternofetal exchange and support the escalating metabolic demands of the fetus [44]. A key transformation during this period is the progressive thinning of the placental barrier. Initially, composed of multiple layers, including the syncytiotrophoblast, cytotrophoblast, basal lamina, stromal connective tissue, and fetal capillary endothelium, this ‘barrier’ becomes markedly attenuated as pregnancy progresses [45, 46]. By the end of the second trimester, the pace of cytotrophoblast proliferation does not completely keep up with the extensive branching and expansion of the villous surface. Consequently, the cytotrophoblast layer becomes flattened and, in some regions, discontinuous. This thinning permits areas of the syncytiotrophoblast to lie in close proximity to the underlying fetal capillaries, leading to the formation of specialized structures known as vasculosyncytial membranes [35, 46]. The development of the vasculosyncytial membrane markedly improves the efficiency of oxygen, nutrient, and waste exchange by reducing the diffusion distance from approximately 50 μm to just 1–2 μm [7, 32]. Concurrently, terminal villi undergo further structural refinements that make them ideally suited for exchange. Their stromal cores become thinner and more vascularized, with dilated capillaries closely apposed to the syncytial surface, and numerous microvilli develop on the apical syncytial membrane, vastly increasing the surface area available for diffusion and further augmenting transport efficiency [12, 32, 47]. Interestingly, a recent placental microvascular imaging study using MV-Flow demonstrated a gestational-age-dependent increase in microvascular flow velocity and a corresponding decline in vascular resistance, which is consistent with progressive vascular remodeling and perfusion optimization [44].

Formation of the decidual septa

At approximately 20–26 pf, the maternal decidua contributes structurally to placental efficiency through the formation of decidual septa [7]. These septa develop from decidual cell proliferation and extend into the intervillous space, dividing the placenta into approximately 15–20 functional compartments or cotyledons, each housing one or more villous trees [7, 48]. Although the septa do not reach the chorionic plate, they serve to organize blood flow within the intervillous space, ensuring that oxygenated maternal blood, delivered via ~ 30–40 spiral arteries, enters at regular intervals and perfuses the villi uniformly [9, 23, 49]. The intervillous space can hold approximately 600–700 mL of blood at a time and is replenished multiple times per minute, creating a dynamic and richly vascularized environment across a villous surface area of 4–14 m2 [5].

Placental activity reaches its functional peak between 34 and 36 weeks of gestation, reflecting the maximal villous surface area, optimized thinning of the placental barrier, and peak vascularization of terminal villi [5, 50]. Nutrient transport systems (e.g., amino acid and glucose transporters) and hormone secretion intensify to support rapid fetal growth in the third trimester. At this point, the placenta operates at a high metabolic capacity, matching the increasing demands of the fetus [1].

Placental senescence

Beyond 37 weeks of gestation, signs of placental senescence begin to emerge [5]. Syncytial knot formation, calcification, and fibrinoid deposition increase, whereas nutrient transport efficiency gradually decreases. Apoptotic processes become more prominent in syncytiotrophoblasts, and the placental barrier may thicken in localized regions due to aging-related changes [11, 49]. While the placenta continues to function adequately until term, these histological changes suggest a functional decline in preparation for parturition, aligning with the physiological limits of placental support.

Morphology of a typical human placenta at full term

Structurally, the human placenta comprises both fetal and maternal components that work synergistically to support fetal development throughout gestation [51] (Fig. 6). The fetal surface or chorionic plate, oriented toward the amniotic cavity, is smooth, bluish-gray, and glistening due to its coverage by the amniotic membrane [6]. Beneath the amnion lies the chorionic plate, which houses large chorionic vessels that radiate from the point of umbilical cord insertion [52, 53]. These vessels converge into two arteries and one vein in the umbilical cord, which inserts most commonly at the center or slightly eccentrically, although marginal or velamentous insertions may also occur [7].

The placental parenchyma, consisting of extensively branched chorionic villous trees suspended in the maternal intervillous space, is sandwiched between the chorionic and basal plates [54]. The intervillous spaces arise from early lacunae in the syncytiotrophoblast and become filled with maternal blood delivered via ~ 30–40 spiral arteries piercing the decidua basalis [23].

The maternal surface of the placenta, which is in contact with the uterine wall, appears rough and spongy due to the presence of 15–28 lobules or cotyledons [2]. Each cotyledon houses one or more complete villous trees separated from adjacent units by decidual septa, which originate from the decidua basalis [7, 32]. The basal plate, which lies just above the myometrium, contains maternal decidual cells, extravillous trophoblasts, and maternal blood vessels. At term, the human placenta typically weighs 500–600 g, has a diameter of 15–25 cm, and has a thickness of approximately 2–3 cm at the center [5, 55]. The placenta occupies approximately 15–30% of the internal uterine surface. The placental shape is generally discoid, although variations exist [6, 55].

Anatomical organization of the human placenta: maternal–fetal interface

At the end of the first trimester (week 13), the placenta is clearly divided into two main regions: (i) the fetal portion, derived from the chorion frondosum, and (ii) the maternal portion, formed by the decidua basalis of the uterine endometrium. There is no point during gestation when fetal and maternal tissues truly “fuse” or become indistinguishable at the histological level [7, 11]. However, from week 16 onwards, their structural and functional integration becomes very intimate, especially at the junctional and intervillous zones. Although histologically distinct, the fetal and maternal tissues become functionally and morphologically interdependent to such an extent that they appear macroscopically inseparable [6]. Importantly, a syncytiotrophoblast layer of fetal origin forms a selective barrier that separates maternal blood from fetal tissue. While this barrier generally prevents direct mixing of the two circulations, limited cellular trafficking does occur. This allows fetal cells to enter maternal circulation and vice versa, a phenomenon relevant to isoimmunization and non-invasive prenatal testing [7, 56]. Thus, while gross anatomical boundaries blur, the two components remain histologically distinct, reflecting the specialized, compartmentalized architecture of the human hemochorial placenta.

Conclusion and future directions

The human placenta represents a continuum of structural and developmental adaptation, evolving from early trophoblast invasion to the formation of a mature, typically discoid organ capable of sustaining pregnancy to term. Historically, studying the human placenta across gestation has been constrained by ethical and technical limitations, restricting most observations to post-delivery specimens or early in vitro models. However, recent advances have begun to bridge this gap. The emergence of trophoblast and placental organoid cultures, stem-cell-based models, and single-cell lineage tracing now allows early developmental processes to be studied with unprecedented precision. In parallel, non-invasive imaging methods, including high-resolution Doppler ultrasound, MRI, and other placental perfusion mapping techniques, enables the examination of structural and vascular adaptations during the second and third trimesters in vivo.

Harnessing these innovations enables researchers to move beyond static histomorphology toward a dynamic view of placental biology that captures how structure and function continuously evolve throughout pregnancy. Integrating these approaches promises a deeper understanding of normal placental morphogenesis and provides new insights into how deviations in this developmental trajectory contribute to adverse pregnancy outcomes.
