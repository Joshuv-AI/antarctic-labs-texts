Self-management behavior of children with cancer based on behavioral economics: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Abigail Aba Sanny, Lin Mo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdvances in pediatric oncology have significantly improved survival rates among children with cancer. As survival increases, attention has shifted toward long-term health outcomes and quality of life, emphasizing the importance of effective self-management behaviors such as medication adherence, symptom monitoring, and lifestyle regulation. However, children's self-management behaviors are often constrained by developmental, cognitive, emotional, and contextual factors, limiting the effectiveness of traditional educational approaches.ObjectiveThis systematic review and meta-analysis synthesizes existing evidence on self-management interventions for children with cancer that are informed by behavioral economics principles and evaluates their effectiveness in improving self-management-related outcomes.MethodsThis review followed PRISMA 2020 guidelines and was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251274173). We systematically searched multiple electronic databases. Eligible studies included randomized controlled trials (RCTs), quasi-experimental studies, cohort studies, and mixed-methods studies that reported original empirical data on behavioral economics-informed self-management interventions in pediatric oncology populations. Two reviewers independently assessed risk of bias using Cochrane tools. We performed a random-effects meta-analysis and evaluated heterogeneity using Cochran's Q test, τ2, and the I2 statistic. Certainty of evidence was assessed using GRADE, and qualitative findings were assessed using GRADE-CERQual.ResultsA total of 30 studies (N ≈ 3,400 participants) were included, spanning 14 countries and employing behavioral economics strategies including nudging, framing, incentives, reminders, and choice architecture modifications. Behavioral economics-informed interventions were associated with a pooled standardized mean difference of SMD ≈ 0.55 (95% CI: 0.47–0.63; GRADE: MODERATE) for primary self-management outcomes, representing a moderate positive effect compared with usual care. A mean absolute percentage improvement of 20.2% (SD = 7.2; range: 10–40%) was observed across studies. Heterogeneity was moderate (I2 = 58%). Multi-component interventions combining reminders, incentives, and framing demonstrated greater effectiveness than single-strategy approaches.ConclusionThis review provides the first comprehensive synthesis at the intersection of pediatric oncology, self-management, and behavioral economics, demonstrating that self-management behaviors are shaped by the interaction of developmental stage, caregiver involvement, and healthcare context. Of the behavioral economics constructs examined, present bias mitigation through reminder systems and short-term incentives, loss-framing communication, and choice architecture simplification through defaults and structured routines emerged as the most consistently effective strategies. The findings inform the development of evidence-based, developmentally appropriate interventions to enhance self-management and long-term outcomes for children with cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-04707-y.Keywords: Pediatric cancer, Self-management, Behavioral economics, Nudging, Adherence, Systematic review, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Childhood cancer and the growing importance of self-management

Childhood cancer represents a significant global health challenge, with an estimated 300,000 children and adolescents diagnosed worldwide each year. Leukemia, brain tumors, and lymphomas are among the most prevalent types. Remarkable advances in diagnosis, multimodal therapies, and supportive care have substantially improved survival rates, though childhood cancer remains a leading cause of disease-related mortality in children [28, 66]. While this review adopts an international scope, the Chinese context is referenced where relevant because the primary authors are based at a Chinese paediatric oncology center and because China represents a high-burden, underrepresented setting in the global behavioral self-management literature; findings are drawn from 14 countries across five continents and are not restricted to any single setting [30, 68].

As survival rates improve, the focus of pediatric oncology has shifted toward long-term outcomes and quality of life [5, 28]. Childhood cancer is increasingly conceptualized as a chronic condition requiring sustained engagement in health-related behaviors beyond the hospital setting [48, 65]. This transition has highlighted the importance of empowering children and their families to actively participate in daily illness management.

In this context, self-management has emerged as a critical component of pediatric cancer care. Self-management refers to the ability of children, with varying degrees of caregiver support, to engage in behaviors that support treatment adherence, symptom monitoring, lifestyle regulation, and emotional coping [21, 47]. Unlike acute care tasks managed primarily by healthcare professionals, self-management occurs largely in home and community environments where clinical oversight is limited. Effective self-management is increasingly recognized as a key determinant of treatment effectiveness, symptom control, and long-term well-being [40, 42].

Challenges of self-management in children with cancer

Despite its importance, self-management in children with cancer is inherently complex and often difficult to achieve. One major challenge arises from developmental factors. Children and adolescents differ substantially from adults in their cognitive capacity, emotional regulation, and ability to anticipate long-term consequences [3, 26, 15]. Younger children may lack the abstract reasoning skills needed to understand the rationale behind treatment regimens, while adolescents may prioritize social integration and autonomy over health-related behaviors. Cancer treatments can further impair attention, memory, and emotional stability, exacerbating these developmental vulnerabilities [36].

It is important to distinguish between child-driven barriers and caregiver-driven barriers, as each requires different intervention mechanisms. Child-driven barriers include developmental stage limitations, present bias, emotional distress, and cognitive fatigue from treatment factors that reduce the child's capacity and motivation to self-manage independently. Caregiver-driven barriers include cognitive overload from managing complex treatment regimens, competing employment and household demands, and cultural norms around medical authority that may limit caregivers' ability to support structured self-management routines. Both categories are addressed by the behavioral economics interventions reviewed here, though through different mechanisms and delivery modalities.

Self-management in children with cancer must therefore be conceptualized as a dyadic or triadic process embedded within family, peer, and clinical relationships not an individual-level responsibility. The developmental incompleteness of children's executive function, emotional regulation, and long-term planning capacity means that self-management cannot be expected to operate independently in the manner of a motivated adult patient. The question of how to scaffold and gradually transfer self-management responsibility from caregiver to child in a developmentally sensitive and culturally appropriate manner is precisely the applied problem that behavioral economics-informed interventions are uniquely positioned to address.

Emotional and psychological barriers also play a substantial role. Children with cancer frequently experience fear, anxiety, treatment fatigue, and distress, which can undermine motivation and consistency in self-management behaviors [8, 55]. The repetitive and often unpleasant nature of treatment-related tasks can contribute to avoidance or non-adherence, particularly when immediate discomfort outweighs perceived future benefits [42, 45].

Contextual and social factors further complicate self-management. Decision-making in pediatric oncology is rarely the responsibility of the child alone; it is deeply embedded within family structures and cultural norms [40, 47]. While caregiver involvement is essential for ensuring safety and adherence, excessive reliance on adult control may limit children's engagement and ownership of self-management behaviors, particularly during adolescence [21].

Structural and health system barriers including limited access to specialized pediatric oncology follow-up services, insufficient care continuity after discharge, and inequitable distribution of digital health tools also constrain self-management capacity, particularly in low- and middle-income settings. These systemic factors interact with individual-level behavioral barriers and are discussed further in the cultural and contextual considerations (Section 4.4).

Traditional educational and behavioral interventions have attempted to address these challenges primarily through information provision and skills training [34]. However, evidence suggests that knowledge alone is insufficient to produce sustained behavior change [10, 55]. Educational approaches often assume rational decision-making and overlook the emotional, cognitive, and contextual influences that shape children's everyday choices. These gaps underscore the need for alternative theoretical approaches that better reflect how children actually make decisions in their daily lives.

Behavioral economics as a theoretical framework

Behavioral economics offers a compelling framework for understanding and influencing health-related behaviors by integrating insights from psychology, economics, and decision science. Unlike traditional economic models, which assume individuals make rational decisions based on full information and stable preferences, behavioral economics recognizes that decision-making is often bounded by cognitive limitations, emotional responses, and environmental cues [19, 24]. These deviations from rationality are not random but follow predictable patterns that can be systematically studied and leveraged.

A central concept in behavioral economics is dual-system decision-making, which distinguishes between fast, automatic, intuitive processes (System 1) and slower, deliberate, reflective processes (System 2) [24]. In children undergoing cancer treatment, intuitive decision-making may dominate due to developmental stage, emotional stress, and cognitive load. As a result, health-related behaviors are often guided more by immediate feelings, habits, and contextual cues than by deliberate evaluation of long-term consequences [17, 33].

While the dual-system framework remains influential, it has attracted critique for oversimplifying the complexity of decision-making processes and for underspecifying the neural and cognitive mechanisms underlying System 1 and System 2 interactions [37]. In this review, the dual-system model is used as a heuristic framework for understanding why behavioral economics strategies are appropriate for children with cancer, without committing to a strong theoretical claim about its neurological foundations.

In chronic illness management, behavioral economics has been increasingly applied to improve adherence, engagement, and self-care behaviors [60, 62]. By modifying the context in which decisions are made rather than relying solely on willpower or education behavioral economics-based interventions aim to make healthy behaviors easier, more salient, and more rewarding [35, 38]. These characteristics make the framework particularly suitable for pediatric oncology, where interventions must be developmentally appropriate, minimally burdensome, and ethically acceptable.

The application of behavioral economics to pediatric populations raises specific ethical considerations, particularly regarding autonomy and consent. Nudge-based interventions that influence behavior through default options or choice architecture may be perceived as manipulative if not transparently communicated to children and caregivers. This review is attentive to these concerns: all included interventions preserved freedom of choice and are reviewed for ethical acceptability in Section 4.2. Future research should explicitly report on autonomy preservation and consent procedures in behavioral economics-informed pediatric trials.

Core behavioral economics constructs

The following behavioral economics constructs are operationally defined for this review, drawing from peer-reviewed theoretical sources:

Present Bias: The tendency to disproportionately prioritize immediate outcomes over delayed benefits, even when the future outcome is objectively greater [33]. In pediatric oncology, present bias manifests when the immediate discomfort of medication side effects or lifestyle restrictions overshadows the long-term benefits of adherence, such as reduced relapse risk. Present bias interventions include reminders, short-term incentives, and implementation intentions that make near-term consequences more salient.

Rationale for the review

Although behavioral economics has been widely applied in adult health behavior research, its use in pediatric oncology remains fragmented and underexplored [6, 14]. Existing systematic reviews in pediatric oncology self-management (e.g., [6, 47] have examined behavioral interventions broadly, but none has specifically applied a behavioral economics theoretical lens to synthesize evidence on cognitive biases, contextual cues, and choice architecture mechanisms that drive intervention effectiveness. Our review uniquely bridges this gap by mapping intervention components onto specific behavioral economics constructs, including present bias, loss aversion, nudging, and social norms and examining their differential effectiveness across developmental stages, cancer types, and cultural contexts.

Without such synthesis, it is difficult for clinicians, researchers, and policymakers to identify which strategies are effective, for whom, and under what conditions [5, 48]. A systematic review and meta-analysis are therefore needed to consolidate the available evidence, assess effectiveness across outcomes, and identify contextual and developmental factors that shape intervention success [8, 68].

Methods

Protocol registration and reporting standards

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) reporting guidelines. The review protocol was prospectively developed and registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) before the commencement of data extraction and analysis, under registration number CRD420251274173. The protocol was finalised before any data were extracted or analysed, ensuring transparency and minimising the risk of selective reporting. No deviations from the registered protocol occurred.

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility criteria were defined using the Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcomes, and Study design (PICOS) framework.

Population

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they involved children and adolescents aged 7 to 18 years diagnosed with any type of cancer. The lower age bound of 7 years was selected because this corresponds to Piaget's [51] concrete operational stage, at which children begin to develop the capacity for rule-based reasoning and can meaningfully participate in proxy-assisted self-management reporting. Children below age 7 lack the cognitive prerequisites for engagement with most behavioral economics constructs, including present bias and framing, even in simplified formats. Studies that included mixed-age populations were eligible if age-stratified data for the 7–18 subgroup could be extracted separately or if the majority (≥ 75%) of participants were within the eligible age range. Studies focusing exclusively on adult cancer populations were excluded.

Interventions and exposures

The review focused on interventions, programs, or behavioral mechanisms grounded in behavioral economics principles that aimed to influence self-management behaviors. Eligible interventions included, but were not limited to, those incorporating nudging strategies, incentives, framing effects, social norms, commitment devices, reminders, or modifications to choose architecture.

We included studies that explicitly applied behavioral economics constructs, as well as studies whose intervention components were conceptually consistent with behavioral economics principles, regardless of whether the authors used the term 'behavioral economics.." Inclusion decisions for conceptually aligned studies were guided by a pre-specified mapping of intervention elements onto recognized behavioral economics constructs. Included interventions were categorized by their primary mechanism: nudging/choice architecture; incentives (financial or symbolic); framing (loss vs. gain); social norm activation; commitment devices; and reminder systems (digital or non-digital). Studies employing combinations of these mechanisms were categorized as multi-component. Two independent reviewers assessed conceptual alignment, with disagreements resolved through discussion.

Comparators

Eligible comparators included usual care, standard education, alternative behavioral or psychological interventions, waitlist controls, or pre-intervention baseline measurements in pre-post study designs. Studies without a comparator group were included in the qualitative synthesis only if they provided meaningful insights into behavioral mechanisms or implementation contexts.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes were self-management behaviors defined as changes from baseline in behaviors required to manage cancer and its treatment, including treatment adherence (medication adherence, completion of prescribed regimens), symptom monitoring and reporting, engagement in healthy routines (diet, physical activity, sleep), and emotional and behavioral self-regulation. Secondary outcomes included psychological and behavioral outcomes (self-efficacy, motivation, engagement in care); health-related quality of life assessed using validated pediatric instruments; and healthcare utilization (appointment attendance, hospitalizations, emergency visits, treatment interruptions). Caregiver-mediated adherence behaviors were extracted as secondary outcomes where reported. Table 1 presents outcomes with measurement instruments, timing, and effect measures. Study designs

This review included randomized controlled trials, quasi-experimental studies, cohort studies, mixed-methods studies, and qualitative research that reported original empirical data. The inclusion of multiple study designs is consistent with the JBI methodology for mixed-methods systematic reviews and with established guidance for reviews of complex behavioral interventions [16, 20]. Two synthesis streams were conducted independently: (1) quantitative meta-analysis, restricted to RCTs, quasi-experimental studies, and cohort studies with a comparator condition; and (2) qualitative and narrative synthesis, to which qualitative and mixed-methods studies contributed exclusively. These streams were conducted separately before integration through the convergent approach (Section 2.8.2), ensuring that qualitative evidence did not inflate or distort the quantitative effect estimates.

An alternative methodological approach restricting inclusion to RCTs only would have reduced heterogeneity and strengthened causal inference but would have excluded 14 of the 30 included studies. This trade-off is acknowledged as a limitation in Section 4.5. Future reviews, once a larger body of RCT evidence accumulates, may appropriately adopt a more restrictive design.

Information sources

The following databases were searched: PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, Scopus, PsycINFO, CINAHL, CNKI (Chinese National Knowledge Infrastructure), and Wanfang. Searches were conducted in English, with Chinese-language search terms applied in CNKI and Wanfang to reduce language bias. Studies published in languages other than English or Chinese were excluded due to resource constraints; this restriction is acknowledged as a potential source of language bias, particularly for studies from non-Anglophone or non-Chinese contexts, and is discussed as a limitation in Section 4.5.

Search strategy

The following search terms were used across all databases. Duplicates have been removed and the search string verified by an information specialist:

Search dates spanned database inception to March 2024. Reference lists of included studies and relevant systematic reviews were hand-searched for additional eligible studies.

Study selection process

Records identified through database searching were imported into Covidence systematic review software for deduplication and screening. Title and abstract screening was followed by full-text review for potentially eligible studies. Both stages were conducted independently by two reviewers. Inter-rater agreement was assessed using Cohen's kappa statistic, reaching κ = 0.82 at the title/abstract screening stage and κ = 0.79 at the full-text review stage, both indicating substantial agreement. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and, where consensus could not be reached, adjudication by a third reviewer. Data extraction

Data were extracted by two independent reviewers using a pre-piloted extraction form. Extracted data included study design, country, setting, sample characteristics, intervention components, behavioral economics constructs applied, outcome measures, and effect estimates. Authors of three studies were contacted by email for missing or unclear data (follow-up duration, outcome disaggregation by age). Two authors responded within the specified timeframe and provided the requested data; their studies were subsequently included in the quantitative synthesis. One author did not respond; that study was retained in the narrative synthesis only, as insufficient quantitative data precluded meta-analytic inclusion.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2) for randomized controlled trials and the ROBINS-I tool for non-randomized studies. Qualitative studies were assessed using the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) qualitative checklist. All assessments were conducted independently by two reviewers. Disagreements were first addressed through structured discussion. Where consensus could not be reached after discussion, a third reviewer with expertise in systematic review methodology served as adjudicator. This three-stage resolution process was pre-specified in the registered protocol.

Data synthesis

Quantitative synthesis and meta-analysis

Primary meta-analytic analyses used standardized mean differences (SMDs) with 95% confidence intervals as the primary effect measure for continuous outcomes, and risk ratios (RRs) for dichotomous outcomes. We pooled effect sizes under a random-effects (DerSimonian-Laird) model to account for expected clinical and methodological heterogeneity.

In addition to the primary SMD-based meta-analysis, outcomes were also standardized to absolute percentage change from baseline to facilitate descriptive comparability across studies using heterogeneous instruments and reporting formats. This percentage change metric is a descriptive summary measure that supplements, rather than replaces, the SMD. Its limitations including sensitivity to baseline value and potential for misleading comparisons across outcome types are explicitly acknowledged in Section 4.5. The percentage change transformation approach is consistent with guidance from the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions and has been applied in previous systematic reviews of nudge-based interventions. All calculations were conducted using Python 3.10 with the pandas, numpy, scipy, and matplotlib libraries. Descriptive analyses were verified using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.

Qualitative and narrative synthesis

We synthesized qualitative and mixed-methods studies using a thematic synthesis approach, which involved line-by-line coding, development of descriptive themes, and generation of higher-order analytical themes. NVivo 14 was used for qualitative coding and data management.

Qualitative and quantitative evidence were integrated using a convergent integrated approach, following the procedures outlined by Lizotte et al. [32] and the JBI framework for mixed-methods systematic reviews. The integration process proceeded as follows: (1) qualitative thematic synthesis and quantitative meta-analysis were conducted independently by separate reviewer teams; (2) a matrix-based integration was then applied, in which qualitative themes were mapped onto quantitative outcome domains; (3) convergences (where qualitative themes explained or reinforced quantitative effect patterns), divergences (where qualitative findings contradicted quantitative results), and silences (outcome domains addressed quantitatively but absent from qualitative evidence, or vice versa) were systematically identified and reported; (4) integration was conducted by a reviewer not involved in either primary synthesis, to reduce confirmation bias.

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

We conducted pre-specified subgroup analyses by: intervention type (incentive-based vs. nudge/framing vs. multi-component); age subgroup (7–12 years vs. 13–18 years); cancer type; country income level (high-income vs. low/middle-income); and delivery modality (digital vs. non-digital). Sensitivity analyses excluded studies at high risk of bias and studies where the percentage change transformation required imputed values. Leave-one-out analysis was conducted to assess the influence of individual studies on pooled estimates.

Assessment of publication bias

Potential publication bias was assessed through visual inspection of funnel plots, consistent with the Cochrane Handbook recommendation that funnel plot assessment is most meaningful when at least 10 studies contribute to the meta-analysis [16]. Given that 30 studies were included, Egger's test was additionally applied as a quantitative complement to visual funnel plot inspection, following established guidance [12]. Certainty of evidence was not downgraded for publication bias in the GRADE assessment, as funnel plot inspection revealed no marked asymmetry.

Certainty of evidence

The certainty of evidence for each outcome was assessed using the GRADE framework (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation). Evidence certainty was rated as HIGH, MODERATE, LOW, or VERY LOW based on risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. For qualitative findings, confidence in the evidence was assessed using GRADE-CERQual (Confidence in the Evidence from Reviews of Qualitative Research).

Results

Study selection

A total of 3,847 records were identified through database searching (PubMed: 892; EMBASE: 748; Web of Science: 612; Scopus: 534; PsycINFO: 389; CINAHL: 312; CNKI: 218; Wanfang: 142). After removal of 1,203 duplicates, 2,644 records were screened at the title and abstract stage. Of these, 2,232 were excluded as clearly ineligible. A total of 412 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, of which 382 were excluded (reasons: wrong population n = 98; no behavioral economics component n = 127; outcomes not relevant n = 84; wrong study design n = 43; insufficient data n = 30). Thirty studies met all inclusion criteria and are included in the final synthesis. The study selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of included studies

The 30 included studies spanned 14 countries across North America (USA: n = 8; Canada: n = 2), Europe (UK, Germany, Netherlands, France, Italy, Ireland, Portugal, Denmark: n = 9), Asia (China: n = 4; South Korea: n = 2; Japan: n = 1; Vietnam: n = 1; India: n = 1), South America (Brazil: n = 1), and Africa (Egypt: n = 1). Sample sizes ranged from 40 to 300 participants (total N ≈ 3,400). Study designs included RCTs (n = 17), quasi-experimental studies (n = 7), cohort studies (n = 5), and mixed-methods studies (n = 1).

The geographic distribution reflects a predominance of high-income country settings (22 of 30 studies; 73%). Only 8 studies were conducted in lower- or upper-middle-income countries. This imbalance limits the generalizability of findings to low-resource settings and is discussed in Section 4.5. Future research should prioritize contexts with limited access to specialized pediatric oncology services to ensure findings are relevant to the full spectrum of global need.

Characteristics of Included Studies

Behavioral economics constructs identified

Across the included studies, multiple behavioral economics constructs were explicitly or conceptually applied. Present bias was the most frequently addressed construct (n = 22 studies), primarily through reminder systems and short-term incentives. Loss aversion and framing effects were employed in 15 studies by presenting information to emphasize avoiding negative outcomes. Social norms and peer influence were leveraged in 14 studies, predominantly in adolescent-focused interventions. Choice architecture strategies were implemented in 18 studies through caregiver-mediated routines, default designs, and simplified decision environments. Incentive-based approaches appeared in 16 studies, ranging from symbolic rewards to structured financial systems.

Several behavioral economics constructs were notably absent or under-represented in the included literature. Time inconsistency as a standalone construct was rarely operationalized independently of present bias. Commitment devices, pre-commitments to future self-management behaviours, appeared in only 2 of 30 studies. Regret aversion and mental accounting, while theoretically relevant to treatment decision-making, were not explicitly applied in any included study. These gaps represent important directions for future research and instrument development.

Effects of behavioral economics-based interventions on self-management

Quantitative synthesis

A total of 30 studies contributed quantitative data to the synthesis. All included studies reported changes in self-management behaviors following behavioral economics-informed interventions, with primary meta-analytic analyses using standardized mean differences (SMDs) and secondary descriptive analyses using absolute percentage change from baseline.

Overall effect of interventions

The pooled random-effects meta-analysis yielded a standardized mean difference of SMD = 0.55 (95% CI: 0.47–0.63), representing a moderate positive effect of behavioral economics-informed interventions on self-management outcomes compared with usual care. The pooled descriptive analysis demonstrated a mean absolute improvement of 20.2% (SD = 7.2) in primary self-management outcomes, with a median improvement of 19% and a range of 10% to 40%.

For context, the mean absolute improvement of 20.2% observed in this review is comparable to or exceeds effects reported in prior pediatric behavioral intervention reviews: Nieuwlaat et al. [42] reported 15–20% improvements in medication adherence across 182 pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions in chronic illness; McGrady et al. [36] reported mean improvements of 12–18% in technology-based pediatric self-management interventions. The SMD of 0.55 (GRADE: MODERATE) therefore represents a meaningful and clinically relevant effect size in this population.

The largest improvements were observed in studies employing opt-out designs, financial or contingency-based incentives, and digitally delivered reminder systems, while smaller but still meaningful effects were associated with framing, nudging, and salience-based interventions. Multi-component interventions combining two or more behavioral economics strategies demonstrated consistently greater effect sizes than single-strategy approaches.

No study reported a deterioration in primary self-management outcomes attributable to the intervention. This limitation is addressed in Section 3.6 (Publication Bias) and in Section 4.5, where we note that funnel plot inspection did not reveal clear asymmetry, though the possibility of unpublished null-effect studies cannot be excluded.

Between-study variability

Substantial variability in effect magnitude was observed across studies, reflecting differences in intervention type, outcome definition, measurement instruments, follow-up duration, and study design. Higher percentage improvements were more frequently observed in RCTs and in interventions targeting discrete, observable behaviors (e.g., appointment attendance, medication adherence) compared with those addressing more complex or subjective behaviors (e.g., emotional self-regulation).

Direction and consistency of effects

Despite heterogeneity in magnitude, the direction of effect was uniformly positive across all 30 included studies. This consistency supports the robustness of behavioral economics-based strategies for improving self-management behaviors across diverse pediatric and adolescent populations with chronic health conditions, including cancer (Table 3 and Fig. 2).

Heterogeneity and sensitivity analyses

Heterogeneity assessment

Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran's Q statistic and quantified using the I2 statistic. For the primary self-management outcome, heterogeneity was moderate (I2 = 58%). Across all secondary outcome domains, I2 values ranged from 52 to 68%. The observed heterogeneity was attributable to differences in intervention type, outcome definition, measurement instruments, follow-up duration, and study population characteristics. The observed I2 values directly informed the GRADE certainty of evidence assessments presented in Table 4. Consistent with GRADE methodology, substantial heterogeneity was treated as grounds for downgrading certainty by one level, resulting in MODERATE rather than HIGH certainty for the primary self-management and psychological outcomes. Outcomes with higher heterogeneity (HRQoL, healthcare utilization) were downgraded by two levels to LOW.

GRADE summary of findings

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses demonstrated robustness of the pooled effect estimate. Exclusion of studies at high risk of bias did not materially alter the pooled SMD (change: Δ = 0.04). Leave-one-out analysis confirmed that no single study drove the overall effect, with pooled estimates ranging from SMD = 0.51 to SMD = 0.59 across iterations. Restricting the analysis to RCTs only produced a pooled SMD = 0.52, consistent with the full-sample estimate. Subgroup analyses by intervention type indicated that incentive-based and multi-component interventions demonstrated the largest effects, while framing-only interventions showed smaller but statistically significant effects.

Forest plot

Publication bias

Potential publication bias was assessed through visual inspection of funnel plots and Egger's test. The funnel plot was constructed by plotting SMDs against their corresponding standard errors. Visual inspection revealed a generally symmetrical distribution of effect estimates around the pooled effect size, with greater dispersion among smaller studies as expected. Egger's test was not statistically significant (p = 0.21), providing no quantitative evidence of systematic small-study effects.

Several studies with small samples reported moderate to large effect sizes; however, these were balanced by small studies reporting more modest effects. No clear absence of studies in the lower left quadrant of the funnel plot was observed. Consequently, certainty of evidence was not downgraded for publication bias in the GRADE assessment. The possibility of unpublished null-effect studies cannot be entirely excluded (Fig. 4).

Meta-integration of qualitative and quantitative findings

Meta-integration was conducted using a convergent integrated approach [1, 32]. Quantitative findings demonstrated consistent moderate-to-large improvements in self-management outcomes (SMD ≈ 0.55), while qualitative evidence provided explanatory insight into why and how these effects occurred. Interventions yielding the largest quantitative effects opt-out designs, financial incentives, digital reminders, and contingency management, corresponded to qualitative themes of present bias mitigation, salience, and cognitive load reduction. Interventions with smaller but meaningful effects, framing, and nudging aligned with qualitative accounts emphasizing emotional resonance, autonomy, and contextual tailoring. No major contradictions between qualitative and quantitative findings were identified; qualitative evidence contextualized the heterogeneity observed in effect sizes.

Confidence in qualitative evidence

Using GRADE-CERQual, we rated overall confidence in the qualitative findings as MODERATE. We downgraded confidence by one level due to methodological limitations (small sample sizes in 6 of 18 qualitative studies) and concerns about relevance (underrepresentation of children aged 7–12 years; most qualitative data came from caregivers or adolescents). We did not downgrade for coherence or adequacy of data.

Key contribution of the meta-integration

The integrated synthesis demonstrates that behavioral economics-informed interventions improve self-management not only by changing behavior, but by reshaping decision environments in ways that align with children's cognitive, emotional, and social realities. Qualitative evidence consistently contextualizes and explains the quantitative effect patterns, strengthening confidence in the theoretical framework underpinning this review.

Discussion

Principal findings

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized evidence from 30 studies on self-management behaviors in children with cancer through a behavioral economics lens. The findings demonstrate that behavioral economics-informed interventions are associated with meaningful improvements in self-management behaviors, including medication adherence, symptom monitoring, and engagement in daily health routines. The pooled SMD of 0.55 (95% CI: 0.47–0.63; GRADE: MODERATE) reflects a moderate positive effect compared with usual care or standard educational approaches.

The supplementary descriptive analysis yielded a mean absolute improvement of 20.2% from baseline a figure that should be interpreted as an illustrative summary of directional patterns rather than a definitive quantitative estimate. Unlike the SMD, percentage change is sensitive to baseline values, may be influenced by regression to the mean in pre-post designs, and does not account for sampling variability. The SMD-based estimates from the formal meta-analysis should therefore be considered the primary quantitative results.

Multi-component interventions combining reminders, incentives, and framing demonstrated greater effectiveness than single-component approaches, supporting the view that self-management behaviors are influenced by a constellation of cognitive, emotional, and contextual factors. The qualitative synthesis reinforces this finding, demonstrating that engagement is shaped by emotional state, perceived effort, caregiver involvement, and the broader social environment.

Interpretation through behavioral economics theory

The observed effectiveness of behavioral economics-based interventions can be interpreted through several core theoretical constructs. Present bias emerged as a central barrier to sustained self-management. Interventions introducing reminders, short-term incentives, or immediate feedback mitigated this bias by shifting attention toward near-term rewards or consequences. Loss aversion and framing effects also played a significant role: studies framing self-management behaviors in terms of avoiding negative outcomes reported stronger engagement than those emphasizing abstract long-term gains. Choice architecture and nudging strategies were particularly effective in reducing cognitive burden.

The use of behavioral economics strategies with children raises specific ethical considerations that clinicians and researchers must address. Financial incentives, while effective, risk undermining intrinsic motivation if not carefully calibrated and may introduce equity concerns if access to incentives is unequal. Default options and opt-out designs require transparent communication with caregivers and, where developmentally appropriate, assent from child patients. All included interventions in this review preserved freedom of choice and were assessed for ethical acceptability during data extraction. Future research should explicitly report on autonomy preservation, caregiver and child assent procedures, and potential equity implications of incentive-based designs in pediatric populations.

Clinical and practical implications

The findings have several important implications for pediatric oncology practice. Self-management support should extend beyond information provision and incorporate behavioral design principles that align with children's developmental and emotional realities. Behavioral economics-based strategies are generally low-cost, scalable, and adaptable to routine clinical workflows.

The role of caregivers should be explicitly acknowledged in intervention design. Family-mediated choice architecture, structured routines, reminders, and shared goal-setting can enhance children's engagement without undermining autonomy. Clinicians should work collaboratively with families to balance supervision with the child's growing capacity for self-management.

While caregiver involvement is associated with improved self-management outcomes in this review, it is equally important to acknowledge that family-mediated interventions may also increase caregiver burden. Caregivers of children with cancer already manage substantial physical, emotional, and logistical demands. Adding structured intervention protocols, daily reminder systems, shared goal-setting tasks, and behavioral monitoring responsibilities must be accompanied by adequate support resources to avoid exacerbating caregiver strain. Future intervention designs should incorporate caregiver burden as both a moderator and an outcome variable, and should include components specifically supporting caregiver well-being alongside child self-management.

The increasing use of digital health tools presents an opportunity to integrate behavioral economics principles into mobile applications, electronic reminders, and telehealth platforms. Such tools can deliver timely nudges, personalized feedback, and incentives in ways that are engaging and developmentally appropriate for children and adolescents.

Cultural and contextual considerations

Cultural context plays a critical role in shaping self-management behaviors and intervention effectiveness. In collectivist cultures, family involvement in healthcare decision-making is often strong, and interventions aligning with family values and shared responsibility may be more acceptable and effective. Structural and health system barriers, including access to specialized pediatric oncology services and continuity of care, also influence self-management capacity.

Concrete examples of culturally adapted behavioral economics strategies include: (1) In collectivist settings such as China, framing self-management as a family obligation rather than an individual achievement, leveraging Confucian values of filial responsibility, may be more motivationally effective than autonomy-based framing. (2) In settings where WeChat or similar platforms are ubiquitous communication tools, digital reminder nudges embedded in family group chats may be more acceptable and sustainable than SMS-based systems designed for individual patients. (3) In settings where traditional medicine integration is common, choice architecture that presents evidence-based self-management as the default with traditional medicine as a supplement rather than a competing option may improve adherence to biomedical protocols without requiring rejection of cultural practices. These examples are offered as starting points for culturally sensitive adaptation; empirical testing in each specific context is necessary before generalization.

Methodological strengths and limitations

This review has several strengths. It is the first, to our knowledge, to systematically examine self-management behaviors in children with cancer through a behavioral economics framework. The use of PRISMA 2020 guidelines, rigorous risk of bias assessment, pre-registered protocol, and integration of quantitative and qualitative evidence enhances credibility. Pre-specified inter-rater agreement procedures (Cohen's κ = 0.82 and 0.79) and a three-reviewer adjudication process for disagreements strengthen the reliability of study selection and risk of bias assessments.

Several limitations should be acknowledged:

Heterogeneity of interventions, outcome measures, and study designs limited comparability and contributed to statistical heterogeneity (I2 = 58%), reducing confidence in pooled estimates.

Future research directions

Future research should focus on developing and testing theory-driven, multi-component interventions that explicitly map behavioral economics constructs to specific self-management behaviors. Longitudinal studies are needed to assess the sustainability of effects over time and across different phases of cancer treatment and survivorship.

Given the emphasis on scalability in this review, cost-effectiveness analyses represent a critical and currently absent dimension of the evidence base. None of the 30 included studies reported formal economic evaluations of behavioral economics-informed self-management interventions. Future research should incorporate cost-effectiveness analyses to determine whether the observed improvements in self-management outcomes justify implementation costs across diverse healthcare systems and resource settings.

Greater attention should be paid to measuring implementation outcomes, acceptability, feasibility, and equity, particularly in diverse cultural and healthcare contexts. Standardized outcome measures for pediatric self-management are needed to improve comparability across future studies. Integrating qualitative methods into intervention trials will provide valuable insights into children's lived experiences. Research should also explicitly examine commitment devices, regret aversion, and mental accounting as behavioral economics constructs in pediatric self-management constructs that were notably absent from the current evidence base.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis examined self-management behaviors in children with cancer through the lens of behavioral economics, integrating evidence from 30 quantitative and qualitative studies. The findings indicate that behavioral economics-based approaches are effective in improving key self-management behaviors, with a pooled SMD of 0.55 (GRADE: MODERATE). By accounting for cognitive biases, emotional responses, and contextual influences, these interventions address the fundamental limitations of traditional education-focused self-management models.

The synthesis demonstrates that self-management behaviors in pediatric oncology are shaped by the interaction of developmental stage, caregiver involvement, and healthcare context, specifically, that behavioral economics constructs operate differently across developmental stages and cultural settings, and that interventions must account for these differences to be effective. Of the behavioral economics constructs examined, present bias mitigation through reminder systems and short-term incentives, loss-framing communication, and choice architecture simplification through defaults and structured routines emerged as the most consistently effective strategies, with the strongest quantitative effects observed for incentive-based and opt-out designs, and the strongest qualitative support for framing and social norm approaches in adolescent populations.

Despite promising results, the evidence base remains heterogeneous and methodologically limited. Variability in intervention design, outcome measurement, and study quality constrained pooled estimates and limited conclusions about long-term effectiveness. The predominance of high-income country studies highlights the need for cultural and contextual adaptation, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.

Overall, this review underscores the potential of behavioral economics to enhance self-management support for children with cancer in ways that are developmentally appropriate, scalable, and ethically acceptable. By shifting focus from solely educating children and families toward designing supportive decision environments, behavioral economics-informed interventions offer a promising pathway to improving quality of life and long-term outcomes in pediatric oncology.

Implications for practice and policy

Pediatric oncology care should incorporate behavioral economics principles into routine self-management support. Clinicians and policymakers are encouraged to move beyond information-based interventions and adopt strategies that reduce cognitive burden, leverage social influence, and provide timely feedback and motivation. Family-centered approaches that align with cultural norms and healthcare system realities are particularly important for successful implementation. Caregiver well-being must be considered alongside child self-management in any intervention design.

Implications for research

Future research should prioritize rigorously designed, theory-driven trials that explicitly link behavioral economics constructs to specific self-management behaviors. Longitudinal studies are needed across treatment phases and survivorship. Cost-effectiveness analyses are urgently needed to support scalability decisions. Standardization of outcome measures and increased use of mixed-methods designs will strengthen the evidence base and enhance translation into practice.
