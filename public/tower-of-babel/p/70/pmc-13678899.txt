Cross-sectional association among passive social media use, peer attachment security, and restrained eating among Chinese college women: incorporating the sociocultural and attachment perspectives
Authors: Yunyan Zhao, Jenny Padilla, Liangshuang Yao, Xiaojun Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRestrained eating has often been studied from either sociocultural or attachment perspectives, but relatively few studies have examined how attachment-related factors may operate alongside sociocultural influences. Additionally, most studies have focused on Western populations, despite evidence suggesting that sociocultural influences on eating behaviors vary across cultures. Guided by both sociocultural and attachment theories, the present study examined how passive social media use and peer attachment security were related to restrained eating among Chinese college women, and whether thin-ideal internalization and body satisfaction mediated these associations.MethodsData were collected from 404 Chinese college women aged 18–30 through Wenjuanxing, a widely used online survey platform in China. Participants completed the Chinese version of the Surveillance Use Scale, the Ideal-Body Internalization Scale, the Body Image State Scale, and the Inventory of Parent and Peer Attachment.ResultsA moderated mediation model revealed that passive social media use, peer attachment security, and their interaction were significantly associated with restrained eating, with different mediating mechanisms. First, passive social media use was directly associated with restrained eating. Second, peer attachment security was indirectly associated with restrained eating through body satisfaction. Importantly, the interactive effect of passive social media use and attachment was directly associated with restrained eating. Specifically, this effect was significant only for individuals with low levels of peer attachment security.ConclusionsBy integrating selected sociocultural pathways and attachment perspectives within a non-Western context, this study offers a nuanced understanding of restrained eating among Chinese college women. Findings suggest that passive social media use and peer attachment security function through distinct mechanisms. Moreover, the effect of passive social media use is contingent on peer attachment security, suggesting that interpersonal resources may buffer vulnerability to digital environmental pressures.The findings highlight the importance of considering both digital experiences and interpersonal relationships when examining eating-related behaviors. Practically, interventions may benefit from promoting awareness of passive social media use while also fostering supportive peer relationships and positive body image.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05197-8.Keywords: Passive social media use, Peer attachment, Restrained eating, Thin-ideal internalization, Body satisfaction

Background

Restrained eating refers to the deliberate restriction of food intake for the purpose of controlling body weight [1]. This behavior is particularly common among college women, a group especially vulnerable to body image concerns during a life stage characterized by social transitions, identity exploration, and heightened exposure to cultural beauty ideals [2, 3]. In China, up to 52.8% of college students reported engagement in restrained eating, with higher prevalence among females than males [4]. Although often adopted as a weight management strategy, it has been linked to a range of negative outcomes, including loss-of-control eating [5] and poorer mental health outcomes such as depression, anxiety, and nonsuicidal self-injury [1]. These risks underscore the importance of identifying the factors that contribute to restrained eating.

Two theoretical perspectives may be particularly relevant to understanding restrained eating. From a sociocultural perspective, appearance-related pressures from the sociocultural context may contribute to body image concerns and eating behaviors. From an attachment perspective, the quality of close relationships may shape self-perceptions and influence individuals’ vulnerability to such pressures. Together, these perspectives suggest that restrained eating may be associated with both external sociocultural factors and individual differences in interpersonal functioning. Accordingly, the present study integrates selected pathways from the Tripartite Influence Model (TIM) and attachment theory to examine how passive social media use and peer attachment security are associated with restrained eating among Chinese college women.

Passive social media use from a sociocultural perspective

The sociocultural perspective emphasizes the role of cultural norms and social environments in shaping individuals’ body image and eating behaviors. A key framework within this perspective is the TIM, which posits that appearance-related pressures from the media, peers, and family contribute to body satisfaction and, in turn, disordered eating through two primary mechanisms: thin-ideal internalization and appearance comparisons [6, 7]. In recent years, social media has become a pervasive part of college students’ lives, offering continuous exposure to idealized and often digitally altered body images. This heightened appearance-related pressure may intensify appearance-related pressures and contribute to restrained eating behaviors [8, 9]. As a result, researchers have extended the TIM to include social media as an additional and particularly salient source of appearance-related influence in contemporary sociocultural environments [10].

Passive social media use

Findings on the association between social media use and well-being have been inconsistent, with meta-analytic evidence showing substantial heterogeneity in effect sizes across studies, partially depending on the type of social media use examined [11, 12]. To address this, researchers have distinguished between active and passive social media use. Active social media use involves direct interaction with others, such as posting content, commenting, and messaging, whereas passive social media use refers to browsing or viewing content without actively engaging [11, 13].

Passive social media use is one of the most common forms of social media engagement among college students and has been consistently associated with greater body dissatisfaction and disordered eating [10, 14]. In contrast, evidence suggests that active social media use may be linked to more positive body-related outcomes, such as higher body esteem [15]. One explanation is that passive use often involves prolonged exposure to idealized appearance-related content and greater opportunities for appearance-based social comparison without the benefits of direct social interaction. Supporting this distinction, a study of physically active men found that although neither active nor passive social media use predicted appearance valence, only passive use was associated with appearance salience, suggesting a stronger link with body-related cognitive processes [16]. Together, these findings indicate that passive social media use may be particularly relevant for understanding body image concerns and restrained eating.

Role of thin-ideal internalization

According to the TIM, thin-ideal internalization is a central pathway between sociocultural factors and restrained eating [6]. Sociocultural exposure to thinness may influence individuals to varying degrees, and thin-ideal internalization reflects the extent to which individuals adopt culturally promoted standards of thinness as personal ideals. Once internalized, these ideals serve as standards for evaluating one’s own and others’ bodies and guide body-related attitudes and behaviors. From the perspective of objectification theory, repeated exposure to appearance-focused sociocultural messages also encourages individuals to evaluate themselves from an observer’s perspective, increasing the salience of internalized appearance ideals and motivating efforts to attain them through body management behaviors such as restrained eating [17, 18].

On one hand, passive social media use may be associated with thin-ideal internalization. For Chinese young women, prevailing cultural norms emphasize thinness as a key standard of feminine beauty and a symbol of self-discipline [19]. These ideals are reinforced through social interactions and expectations, where appearance often dictates perceptions of personal and social value, thereby intensifying restrained eating behaviors [3, 19]. For example, Wang et al. [10] found that viewing body images on social media increased thin-ideal internalization, in turn predicted higher levels of restrained eating among Chinese adolescents aged 12–19.

On the other hand, internalized thinness standards may extend beyond cognition to behaviors of body surveillance and body management practices, including restrained eating, in an effort to align one’s body with one’s ideal standard. Consistent with this view, research has found that thin internalization mediated the relationship between traditional media exposure, family and peer pressures and college girls’ restrained eating across different cultures [20]. Thus, thin internalization might also mediate the relationship between passive social media use and restrained eating.

Role of body satisfaction

Body satisfaction represents individuals’ positive evaluations of their physical appearance and body shape. Within the TIM, body dissatisfaction is considered a proximal predictor of disordered eating behaviors [6]. Passive social media use may increase exposure to idealized and often digitally altered body images, which can promote biased and unfavorable evaluations of one’s own appearance, thus may be associated with biased body self-perception and less body satisfaction [21]. For example, Xiang & Kong [14] found that passive social media use was associated with lower body satisfaction, which was further linked to disordered eating behaviors in Chinese middle-school students. Thus, body satisfaction may mediate the relationship between passive social media use and restrained eating.

The TIM further proposes that the sociocultural context contributes to body dissatisfaction through thin-ideal internalization [6]. Because body ideals portrayed on social media are often unrealistic and difficult to attain, individuals who internalize these ideals may perceive a greater discrepancy between their actual and ideal body, resulting in lower body satisfaction [22]. Therefore, thin-ideal internalization and body satisfaction may operate sequentially in linking passive social media use to restrained eating. Although these studies independently support the mediating roles of thin-ideal internalization and body satisfaction in adolescents, few studies have examined whether these factors function as sequential mediators in the relationship between passive social media use and restrained eating among college women. Examining this serial mediation would offer more comprehensive evidence for applying the TIM to the context of passive social media use, helping clarify its psychological processes and better inform prevention efforts for college women.

Peer attachment from an attachment perspective

Attachment is another key factor in body image perception and eating behaviors [19–21]. According to attachment theory, early relational experiences shape individuals’ self-concept and expectations of others through internal working models [23]. Attachment theory has been conceptualized both in terms of the dimensions of attachment anxiety and attachment avoidance and in terms of the degree of security within specific relationships [24]. Consistent with the latter perspective, the present study conceptualizes peer attachment as the overall quality and security of peer relationships, reflected in trust, communication, and low alienation, rather than as separate dimensions of attachment anxiety and avoidance. This conceptualization is consistent with our aim of examining how supportive peer relationships during emerging adulthood may be associated with responses to appearance-related experiences.

Individuals with secure attachment typically develop positive self-perception and greater body appreciation, whereas those with insecure attachment are more likely to experience negative self-perception and heightened perfectionism, traits constantly linked to disordered eating behaviors [25, 26]. Supporting this, a meta-analysis found that attachment insecurity, across relationships with parents, peers, and romantic partners, was associated with dieting behaviors (e.g., sugar avoidance) and restrictive eating rules [27]. Moreover, a review by Chan et al. of 19 studies found that higher peer attachment insecurity was linked to more disordered eating symptoms [28]. However, most prior research has primarily focused on parent-child attachment, with less attention given to peer relationships [27, 28]. This is notable given that peer relationships become especially salient during adolescence and emerging adulthood, serving as key sources of body image feedback and shaping eating behaviors [28, 29]. Consistent with this perspective, insecure peer attachment has been linked to disordered eating behaviors in both Spanish and American youth samples [30, 31]. Despite these associations, the mechanisms through which peer attachment is associated with restrained eating remain underexplored.

Role of thin-ideal internalization

Thin-ideal internalization may be one pathway linking peer attachment security to restrained eating. From a self-presentation perspective, individuals with low attachment security may rely more heavily on external validation for self-worth [32], and place more importance on appearance in their self-concept [33]. As a result, they may be more likely to adopt sociocultural standards of attractiveness, including the thin ideal. Relatedly, previous research found that higher peer attachment anxiety was associated with greater internalization of appearance ideals among American female samples [34, 35]. Therefore, thin-ideal internalization may help explain the association between peer attachment security and restrained eating.

Role of body satisfaction

Body satisfaction may also play a mediating role between peer attachment security and restrained eating. Attachment theory suggests that attachment relationships shape individuals’ self-perceptions and sense of self-worth [23]. Consistent with this perspective, higher peer attachment security was associated with greater body appreciation among Portuguese women [36]. Supporting the mediating role of body satisfaction, Koskina and Giovazolias [37] found that body dissatisfaction mediated the association between insecure romantic attachment and disordered eating among Greek college students.

Furthermore, research suggests that peer attachment may be associated with body dissatisfaction through greater thin-ideal internalization. For instance, Patton et al. [34] demonstrated that peer attachment anxiety predicted body dissatisfaction, with thin-ideal internalization mediating their association among American adolescent and college women. Taken together, the findings suggest that lower peer attachment security may be associated with restrained eating through greater thin-ideal internalization and lower body satisfaction. However, most of this research has been conducted in Western contexts, limiting our understanding of how these processes operate in non-Western cultural settings.

Integrating sociocultural and attachment pathways

Attachment may shape how individuals perceive their body image and regulate eating behaviors in the face of media pressure [38]. Securely attached individuals are more likely to have developed adaptive emotion regulation strategies, such as seeking support and engaging in co-regulation, which may offer resilience when navigating appearance-related pressures [39]. In contrast, individuals with insecure attachment often adopt maladaptive regulation strategies due to inconsistent or unavailable attachment figures. These strategies may include hyperactivation (e.g., rumination) or deactivation (e.g., emotional cutoff) in response to stressful situations [39, 40]. As a result, attachment insecurity may amplify the association between media exposure and restrained eating behaviors. Supporting this idea, Goossens et al. [29] found that insecure parental attachment and thin-ideal internalization were jointly associated with eating concerns in American adolescent girls. Notably, the association between thin-ideal internalization and eating concerns was stronger among those with lower attachment security. However, it remains unclear whether similar interactive effects extend to peer attachment security and passive social media use, and how this joint effect might relate to restrained eating.

Role of thin-ideal internalization and body satisfaction

Peer attachment may shape how individuals respond to media exposure, particularly through its association with thin-ideal internalization and body satisfaction. Individuals with insecure peer attachment may be especially vulnerable to internalizing idealized body standards promoted on social media. This vulnerability may arise from a greater dependence on external validation to maintain self-esteem and a tendency to engage in ruminative thought patterns [32, 40]. Rumination, in turn, may intensify the internalization of thin-ideal contents and lead to reduced body satisfaction [41]. Consequently, individuals with insecure peer attachment may be particularly susceptible to the harmful effects of social media on body image. Supporting this, de Vries et al. [42] found that a positive maternal relationship buffered the negative association of general social media use on body dissatisfaction among Dutch adolescents aged 12–19. However, their study did not examine specific types of social media engagement, such as passive social media use, nor did it explore links to restrained eating.

Chinese cultural and social media context

As of June 2024, China had approximately 1.10 billion mobile internet users, with individuals aged 20 to 40 accounting for 32.8% of the total population [43]. Among Chinese college students, platforms such as WeChat Moments are primary social network sites. Similar to Facebook and Instagram, these platforms enable users to share photographs, videos, and text-based updates and receive feedback through likes and comments. Although not exclusively image-based, visual content is a prominent feature, particularly on WeChat Moments, where posting is typically initiated through photo or video sharing with text added as captions. Unlike Facebook and Instagram, WeChat Moments and Qzone primarily operate primarily through existing social networks rather than anonymous public, providing frequent opportunities for appearance-related self-presentation and amplifying exposure to appearance norms and social feedback [44].

The association between passive social media use and restrained eating may be particularly pronounced in the Chinese context, where values of social harmony and relational interdependence are deeply embedded. In Chinese society, one dimension of identity, the “other-oriented self”, emphasizes maintaining harmony with “generalized others”, that is, distant members of broader society [45]. This cultural orientation resonates with the nature of social media, where users frequently engage in self-presentation for broad audiences. As a result, the perceived importance of social evaluation and feedback may be heightened, making Chinese college women especially vulnerable to social media contents and the thin-ideal internalization encountered on these social media platforms.

The present study

Drawing on both the Tripartite Influence Model and attachment theory, the present study examines associations among passive social media use, peer attachment security, and restrained eating, and tests the serial mediating roles of thin-ideal internalization and body satisfaction in a sample of Chinese college women (see Fig. 1).

The conceptual model. Note. Joint effect = the interaction (product term) between passive social media use and peer attachment

We hypothesized that, (1) passive social media use would be positively associated with restrained eating, both directly and indirectly through greater thin-ideal internalization and lower body satisfaction; (2) peer attachment security would be negatively associated with restrained eating, also both directly and indirectly through greater thin-ideal internalization and lower body satisfaction, and (3) passive social media use and peer attachment security would be jointly associated with restrained eating. Specifically, we hypothesized that high peer attachment security could buffer the negative association between high passive social media use and restrained eating, both directly and indirectly by reducing thin-ideal internalization and enhancing body satisfaction.

By doing so, this study aims to provide a more comprehensive understanding of how and when social media use is associated with body image concerns and disordered eating, highlighting the complex interplay between sociocultural and interpersonal factors. Practically, the findings can guide the development of culturally sensitive interventions that target both digital media literacy and relational dynamics, such as peer attachment, to more effectively cultivate resilience in the contemporary digital context.

Methods

Procedures

Data came from a cross-sectional project conducted in spring 2019, which aimed to examine the relationships among attachment, social media use, and self-perception. Participants were recruited through flyers distributed on two popular social media platforms, WeChat Moments and Qzone. The flyers included a brief description of the study’s purpose (exploring self-perception, attachment, and social media use), along with eligibility criteria (female university students), the estimated time for completion (about seven minutes, based on pilot testing), compensation (࿥2.5), and a Quick Response (QR) code that linked to the online survey hosted on Wenjuanxing, a platform for survey data collection in China.

The survey opened with an introductory information page identifying the research team from Central China Normal University and explaining the study’s purpose, voluntary nature, expected time commitment, and confidentiality measures. Participants were required to provide electronic informed consent by checking an agreement box to confirm that they had read and understood the study information and agreed to participate. The survey collected information on participants’ demographics (gender, age, marital status, height, and weight), social media use (platforms and frequency), and body image measures (body satisfaction, thin-ideal internalization, self-objectification, restrained eating, self-compassion, rumination, and attachment). To ensure anonymity, no personally identifiable information, such as names or student IDs, was collected.

At the time of data collection, the formal ethics committee for psychological research at Central China Normal University had not yet been established; therefore, formal institutional approval was not available. All procedures were reviewed and supervised by Professor Xiaojun Sun and followed the ethical standards of the American Psychological Association and relevant Chinese guidelines for human subject research. Given that the dataset was anonymous and the present study involved no interaction with human participants, this secondary analysis was determined to be not human subjects research by the University of Notre Dame Institutional Review Board (Protocol No. 26-02-9901).

Participants

A total of 442 individuals completed a survey on Wenjuanxing using either a mobile phone or laptop. The median completion time was approximately 8 min. Each participant was compensated ࿥2.5 (approximately $0.40) for their participation. Of the 442 respondents, 408 participants met the eligibility criteria: (1) identifying as female and unmarried, (2) currently enrolled in a college/university, (3) having an account on at least one social platform, and (4) actively using that account. Four participants who were younger than 18 years of age (all aged 17 years) were excluded. The eligible participants were between 18 and 30 years old (M = 21.69, SD = 2.49), including 50 (12.38%) freshman, 68 (16.83%) sophomores, 54 (13.37%) juniors, 77 (19.06%) seniors, and 155 (38.37%) graduate students. They reported using social media platforms, including WeChat Moments, Qzone, and Weibo, for an average duration of 6.37 years (SD = 2.18). Their body mass index (BMI) values ranged from 14.98 to 27.89 kg/m² (M = 19.90, SD = 2.01). According to the Working Group on Obesity in China, 14 participants (3.47%) were classified as overweight (BMI = 24.0–27.9 kg/m²), and 99 participants (24.50%) were classified as underweight (BMI < 18.5 kg/m²). Before analysis, the data were screened for univariate and multivariate outliers, and cases identified as outliers were excluded (see Analysis plan).

Measurement

Passive social media use was assessed using the Chinese version of the Surveillance Use Scale [46], adapted from a measure on Facebook surveillance use [47]. It consists of four items (e.g., “Read the newsfeed,” “Read a friend’s status update,” “View a friend’s photo,” and “Browse a friend’s personal page”. Participants rated each item on a 5-point (1 = never to 5 = frequently) scale, with higher scores indicating more frequent passive social media use. This scale has been widely used and demonstrated good psychometric properties [14, 48]. Cronbach’s α was 0.74 in this study.

Thin-ideal internalization was measured using the Chinese version of the nine-item Ideal-Body Internalization Scale [49, 50]. Sample items included “Women with toned (lean) bodies are more attractive”, and “Slender women are more attractive”. Items were rated on a 5-point (1 = completely disagree to 5 = completely agree) scale, with higher scores reflecting greater thin-ideal internalization. This scale has been used in previous studies on body image [51]. Cronbach’s α was 0.90 in this study.

Body satisfaction was measured using the Chinese version of the Body Image State Scale which includes six items on body shape, height, weight, and general appearance [52, 53]. Sample items included “Right now I feel … with my body size and shape” and “Right now I feel … physically attractive”. Items were rated on a 9-point (1 = extremely dissatisfied to 9 = extremely satisfied) scale, with higher scores indicating greater body satisfaction. This scale has been commonly used in previous studies [44, 54]. Cronbach’s α was 0.91.

Peer attachment security was assessed using the Chinese version of the Inventory of Parent and Peer Attachment [24, 55]. The peer attachment subscale comprises 3 dimensions: communication (three items, e.g., “If my friend knows something is bothering me, she asks me about it.”), trust (three items, e.g., “My friend respects my feeling.”) and detachment (four items, e.g., “Talking over my problems with my friend makes me feel ashamed or foolish.”). Items were rated on a 5-point (1 = almost never or never true to 5 = almost always or always true) scale. Detachment items were reverse scored, and all items were summed, with higher scores indicating more attachment security. This measure demonstrated good psychometric properties in Chinese college samples [56, 57]. In this study, Cronbach’s α were 0.86, 0.87, and 0.79 for communication, trust, and detachment, respectively.

Restrained eating was measured using the Chinese version of the Revised Restrained Scale (RRS) [58, 59]. The RRS was selected because it is a commonly used measure of restrained eating and has been validated among Chinese college students, the population targeted in this study [58]. The concern dieting subscale includes six items, four of which were rated on a 4-point scale from 0 = never to 3 = always, e.g. “Do you give too much time and thought to food?”), while the remaining two items were rated on a 5-point scale from 0 = never to 4 = always, e.g., “How often do you diet”. The weight fluctuation subscale consists of four items, such as “In a typical week, how much does your weight fluctuate (in kilos)?” rated on a 4-point scale from 0 = 0–0.5 to 4 = 2.5 + and “What is the maximum amount of weight (in kilos) within a week” rated on a 4-point scale from 0 = 0–0.5 to 4 = 2.5+. Scores for all items were summed, with higher scores indicating greater levels of restrained eating. The validity of this measure among Chinese college students has been supported in prior research [60, 61]. Cronbach’s α was 0.67 for concern dieting and 0.68 for weight fluctuation in this study.

Analysis plan

Prior to analysis, data were screened for univariate and multivariate outliers. Univariate outliers were defined as values more than 3 standard deviations above or below the sample mean. Three participants with extreme BMI values (26.04–27.89), five participants with extreme thin-ideal internalization scores (1.00–2.22), and two participants with extreme restrained eating scores (3.80) were excluded. One additional participant with a BMI of 14.98 was removed because the value indicated severe underweight status and was considered outside the intended population. Multivariate outliers were identified using Mahalanobis distance based on all study variables. Three cases exceeded the critical χ² value at p < .001 and were excluded. In total, 14 participants were removed, resulting in a final sample of N = 390.

Preliminary analyses were conducted in SPSS version 28.0 to compute descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, and range) and bivariate correlations, providing an overview of data distribution and assessing associations between variables. Next, a moderated mediation model was tested in Mplus version 7.0 considering its robust capabilities for estimating complex moderated mediation models and bootstrapped indirect effects. The model examined (a) the individual and combined effects of passive social media use and peer attachment security on restrained eating, and (b) the mediating roles of thin-ideal internalization and body satisfaction in the relationships between predictors and outcomes, as depicted in Fig. 1.

All variables were standardized prior to analysis. Indirect effects were estimated using bootstrapping (5,000 resamples) following Hayes’s recommendations [62]. This resampling technique generates bias-corrected confidence intervals, providing robust assessments of significance regardless of normality, and hence enhances the reliability and validity of mediation analyses, especially in models with complex pathways. For significant interaction effects, simple slopes and the Johnson-Neyman technique were applied to illustrate the nature of the moderation. While simple effects reveal how the effect’s strength and direction vary at different levels of peer attachment security, the Johnson-Neyman technique identifies specific values of peer attachment security where the effect shifts from nonsignificant to significant. Additionally, BMI was included as a covariate considering its potential link to body satisfaction and restrained eating [28].

A posterior power analysis was conducted to examine the power of detecting the mediated moderation effect with the current sample size using power4mome [63]. The analysis included 2,000 Monte Carlo replications per sample across 40 simulated datasets (each with n = 400), with population parameters derived from the SEM results. The simulations indicated that a sample of approximately n = 370 would achieve 80% power. The test yielded an estimated power of 0.82 (95% CI [0.78, 0.86]) at a sample size of 400. Therefore, the final analyzed sample of 390 participants provided adequate power and was sufficient for reliable results.

Results

Descriptive statistics and bivariate correlations

Descriptive statistics and correlations for the included participants are shown in Table 1. Participants reported moderate-to-high levels of thin-ideal internalization (M = 4.09, SD = 0.48; scale range: 1–6) and neutral levels of body satisfaction (M = 5.22, SD = 1.34; scale range: 1–9). These results indicate a general tendency to endorse thin-ideal norms while holding a neutral perception of their body image. Regarding correlations, passive social media use was significantly associated with thin-ideal internalization (r = .16*, p < .01) and restrained eating (r = .17**, p < .01), but was not associated with body satisfaction (r = .08, p = .12). In contrast, peer attachment security was positively correlated with body satisfaction (r = .17**, p < .01), but was not related to thin-ideal internalization (r = .06, p = .28) or to restrained eating (r = − .02, p = .55). In addition, thin-ideal internalization was positively correlated with restrained eating (r = .13, p < .05), whereas body satisfaction was negatively correlated with restrained eating (r = − .29, p < .001).

Effect of peer attachment security

More peer attachment security was positively linked to more body satisfaction (β = 0.20, p < .001), but was not significantly associated with thin-ideal internalization (β = 0.06, p = .29) or restrained eating (β = 0.02, p = .69). Regarding indirect effect, peer attachment security was related to restrained eating through body satisfaction (β = -0.04, SE = 0.01, 95% CI [-0.07, -0.02]), such that greater peer attachment security was associated with higher body satisfaction, which in turn was associated with lower restrained eating. In contrast, the indirect effect through thin-ideal internalization was not significant (β = 0.01, SE = 0.01, 95% CI [0.00, 0.02]), as shown in Table 2. In addition, the chain mediating effect through thin-ideal internalization and body satisfaction was also not significant (β = 0.00, SE = 0.00, 95% CI [0.00, 0.01]).

Joint effect of passive social media use and peer attachment security

After accounting for the mediators, the joint effect between passive social media use and peer attachment security remained significantly associated with restrained eating directly (β = -0.16, p < .01). Simple slope analysis (as in Fig. 3) indicated that passive social media use was positively linked to restrained eating at low levels of peer attachment security (-1 SD, β = 0.38, p < .001), but not among those with high levels of peer attachment security (+ 1 SD, β = 0.06, p = .34). As shown in Fig. 4, Johnson-Neyman analysis further showed that passive social media use was positively related to restrained eating only for individuals with low peer attachment security (below 6.55).

Sensitivity analysis

Considering the wide age range in the sample, a sensitivity analysis was conducted that included age as an additional covariate. The results were consistent with the original model that did not control for age. The revised model fit was a saturated model, and explained 24.70% of the variance in restrained eating, i.e., 0.60% more than the original model. Age was associated with restrained eating (β = -0.03, p < .05), but not thin-ideal internalization (β = -0.01, p = .56) or body satisfaction (β = 0.03, p = .14). The path coefficients were nearly identical to those in the original model, with all differences smaller than 0.01.

Discussion

Drawing on selected constructs from the TIM and attachment perspectives [27, 34], the present study examined the association among passive social media use, peer attachment, and restrained eating, as well as the mediating roles of thin-ideal internalization and body satisfaction among Chinese college women. The results show that both passive social media use and peer attachment security were related to restrained eating. However, only peer attachment security was indirectly related to restrained eating both through body satisfaction, whereas passive social media use was not indirectly related to restrained eating. Moreover, their joint effect was significantly associated with restrained eating.

These findings demonstrate the importance of considering both digital and interpersonal factors when understanding restrained eating [10, 19]. While passive social media use was only directly associated with restrained eating, body satisfaction emerged as a salient pathway linking peer attachment security to restrained eating. The significant interaction effect further suggests that the effect of social media use may depend on the quality of individuals’ peer relationships. These results suggest the protective role of secure peer relationships, which may promote healthier body perceptions and reduce vulnerability to restrained eating behaviors.

More broadly, the findings contribute to the growing literature examining disordered eating in non-Western contexts and emphasize the importance of culturally contextualizing sociocultural and attachment-based models of eating behaviors [64]. Practically, the results support interventions that strengthen peer support and promote positive body image, while also addressing appearance-related pressures encountered through social media. Such efforts may be particularly important in the Chinese context, where appearance ideals and social evaluation are highly salient.

Passive social media use from the sociocultural perspective

The results support the direct association between passive social media use and thin-ideal internalization, and between passive social media use and restrained eating. This shows social media’s importance as a sociocultural force of body image and eating behaviors.

Role of thin-ideal internalization

Contrary to our expectations, passive social media use was not indirectly associated with restrained eating through thin-ideal internalization, despite significant associations between passive social media use and thin-ideal internalization, and between thin-ideal internalization and restrained eating. One possible explanation is that the role of appearance-ideal internalization may be more complex than originally conceptualized in the TIM [6].

Although the TIM identifies thin-ideal internalization as a key mechanism linking sociocultural pressures to body dissatisfaction and disordered eating, subsequent developments in sociocultural theory and measurement suggest that appearance ideals are multidimensional rather than limited to thinness alone [65]. For example, the Sociocultural Attitudes Toward Appearance Questionnaire 4 (SATAQ-4) expanded the assessment of appearance-ideal internalization to include both thinness and muscularity ideals, reflecting broader societal changes in beauty standards and recognition that multiple appearance ideals may be linked to body image and eating-related behaviors [65]. However, the present study examined only thin-ideal internalization. Although thinness remains a prominent beauty ideal among Chinese women, contemporary social media platforms frequently promote a broader range of appearance ideals, including fitness, body tone, attractiveness, and lifestyle-related markers of self-discipline and success [66]. Consequently, the measure used in the present study may not have fully captured the appearance standards most relevant to passive social media use, potentially weakening the indirect effect through thin-ideal internalization.

Role of body satisfaction

Passive social media use was not directly related to body satisfaction in the current model, thus mediating role is not supported. This finding stands in contrast to predictions of the TIM [6]. However, it is consistent with prior research among Korean college women [67]. A potential explanation relates to the multidimensional nature of body image in contemporary social media environments. The measure used in the present study primarily assessed satisfaction with overall body shape, weight, and appearance. However, social media platforms increasingly promote and evaluate a broader range of appearance attributes, including facial attractiveness, skin quality, body tone, and other highly specific physical features [68]. Through image editing tools, filters, and appearance-focused feedback such as likes and comments, social media may encourage users to attend to and evaluate detailed aspects of their appearance that are not fully captured by traditional measures of body satisfaction.

This issue may be particularly relevant among Chinese and other Asian women, for whom appearance ideals often extend beyond thinness to include characteristics such as fair skin and delicate facial features [69]. For example, Jackson et al. [70] found that Chinese women experienced heightened media pressure related to both body weight and facial appearance, particularly from culturally congruent (Asian) sources. Hence, passive social media use may be more strongly related to dissatisfaction with specific appearance attributes than to global evaluations of body satisfaction. Future research may benefit from adopting more comprehensive and culturally sensitive assessments of body image that capture the broader range of appearance concerns salient in contemporary social media contexts.

Another possibility is that passive social media use may be associated with restrained eating through more complex processes. For example, the Elaborated Sociocultural Model identified social comparison and body surveillance as important pathways linking appearance ideals to body dissatisfaction [71], whereas recent social media adaptations of the TIM have included body shame and psychological distress [72]. Because social media is highly visual, interactive, and embedded in everyday life, users are repeatedly exposed to appearance-related content and appearance-focused feedback throughout the day. Such experiences may elicit more immediate processes, such as body shame or psychological distress, which may in turn encourage restrained eating as a strategy to improve appearance or reduce appearance-related concerns.

Peer attachment from the attachment perspective

Extending previous research on attachment and disordered eating, this study showed that peer attachment security and restrained eating were not directly associated, but were indirectly connected through body satisfaction.

Role of thin-ideal internalization

Contrary to our hypothesis, peer attachment security was not associated with thin-ideal internalization and therefore showed no indirect association with restrained eating through this pathway. This finding differs from previous research conducted in Western samples, which found associations between attachment anxiety and media ideal internalization [34, 35]. One possibility is that thin-ideal internalization was already highly endorsed in the current sample (M = 4.09, SD = 0.48, on a 5-point scale with 3 as neutral). In a cultural context where thinness is widely valued, attachment security may have less effect on whether individuals endorse the thin ideal. Additionally, as mentioned above, the present study focused specifically on thin-ideal internalization, which may not fully capture the broader appearance ideals relevant to college women. Future research should examine a wider range of appearance-related beliefs and ideals.

Another possible explanation is that peer attachment security may be associated with eating behaviors primarily through self-evaluative processes, such as body satisfaction, rather than through the endorsement of sociocultural appearance ideals.

Role of body satisfaction

As expected, body satisfaction mediated the association between peer attachment security and restrained eating. Higher peer attachment security was associated with greater body satisfaction, which in turn was linked to lower levels of restrained eating. This aligns with attachment theory and previous findings, showing that secure peer relationships may be linked to more positive self-perceptions, thereby acting as a protective factor against disordered eating behaviors [28]. Individuals with more secure attachment may hold more positive self-perceptions and greater feelings of acceptance of their physical appearance [37]. In contrast, lower attachment security may be associated with more negative body evaluations, increasing vulnerability to restrained eating behaviors.

Integrating the sociocultural and attachment perspective

Importantly, the findings partially support the proposed model that combines sociocultural and attachment perspectives, demonstrating that the peer attachment security and passive social media use had a joint effect on restrained eating directly. Specifically, passive social media use was not significantly associated with restrained eating among individuals with high peer attachment security. One interpretation is that women with less peer attachment anxiety tend to exhibit higher levels of self-compassion [26], which may buffer against the pressures of thin-ideal body image on social media. In contrast, those with low attachment security may be more prone to maladaptive responses such as rumination, which was closely related to disordered eating [40].

Overall, this study incorporated elements of sociocultural and attachment perspectives to offer a more comprehensive understanding of disordered eating in the digital context. The findings indicate that both sociocultural force and interpersonal relationships are relevant, but they may be associated through distinct pathways. Passive social media use was directly associated with restrained eating, whereas peer attachment security was indirectly associated with restrained eating through body satisfaction. These findings underscore the protective role of secure peer relationships in fostering positive body evaluations and reducing vulnerability to restrained eating.

By illustrating how these pathways interact, this study contributes to more nuanced and context-sensitive theoretical frameworks. These findings suggest that effective interventions should not only enhance digital literacy and media awareness but also strengthen secure peer relationships and promote healthier body perceptions to help reduce restrained eating. At the same time, efforts to improve media literacy should be accompanied by broader initiatives that challenge the cultural valorization of thinness and promote more diverse and inclusive appearance ideals. Such approaches may be particularly important in contexts where thinness is closely linked to perceptions of attractiveness and social value.

Limitations and future directions

Drawing on both the sociocultural and attachment perspectives, this study proposed an integrative model to investigate the association among passive social media use, peer attachment security, and restrained eating, and examined the mediating role of thin-ideal internalization and body satisfaction. It contributes to a more comprehensive understanding of disordered eating in the digital age, providing valuable insights for designing more effective interventions that promote healthier relationships with food and body image. Nevertheless, several limitations should be noted. First, the study extended TIM to the social media context and focused on a selected set of constructs within the TIM, including thin-ideal internalization, body satisfaction, and restrained eating. As such, the present study should be viewed as examining selected sociocultural pathways derived from the TIM rather than providing a comprehensive test of the model. Notably, we did not include the SATAQ, which assesses multiple dimensions of appearance-ideal internalization and appearance-related pressures and may provide a more comprehensive assessment of sociocultural influences [65]. Future research could incorporate both SATAQ dimensions alongside social media influences to examine whether different appearance ideals and sources of pressure are associated with body image and restrained eating [72].

Second, the present study focused on body satisfaction, which reflects positive evaluations of one’s body. Although body satisfaction is closely related to body dissatisfaction, they are conceptually distinct constructs and may demonstrate different associations with sociocultural and interpersonal factors. Therefore, the observed pathways involving body satisfaction should be interpreted within the context of this specific operationalization. Future research could examine whether similar patterns emerge when body dissatisfaction or other dimensions of body image are assessed directly.

Moreover, the present study conceptualized peer attachment as attachment security in peer relationships without distinguishing between attachment dimensions or attachment figures. Given that attachment anxiety and attachment avoidance may have distinct associations with emotion regulation, body image, and disordered eating behaviors [25, 39]. Future research is needed to examine these attachment dimensions separately and investigate their roles across different attachment figures (e.g., peers, parents, romantic partners) [31].

In addition, this study used a convenience sample of Chinese college women. Since the TIM has been primarily developed and tested in Western populations, it may not fully apply to other populations [64]. For example, Chinese culture places a strong emphasis on social harmony and sensitivity to others’ opinions, which may heighten the effect of social media compared to other cultural settings [45]. Moreover, participants generally reported high levels of thin-ideal internalization and relatively low BMI, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to populations with different body characteristics or appearance-related beliefs. Additionally, this relatively restricted variability in thin-ideal internalization may have attenuated effect sizes, especially for moderation effects, by limiting the ability to detect conditional relationships. Future studies should examine these associations in more heterogeneous samples with greater variation in BMI and appearance-related beliefs.

Married college women were also excluded from the study. Future research should recruit more diverse and representative samples to enhance external validity. Additionally, male participants were not included. Given evidence that the relationships among social media use, attachment, and body image concerns may differ by gender [9, 14], future studies should include male participants to explore potential gender differences and examine the applicability of the findings.

Lastly, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to infer causal relationships. Longitudinal or experimental designs in future research would strengthen the evidence for directional pathways.

Conclusion

The present study contributes to the growing literature on body image and eating behaviors by drawing on selected pathways derived from sociocultural and attachment perspectives within contemporary social media context. The findings suggest that restrained eating among Chinese college women may not be understood solely as a consequence of individual choices or media exposure, but rather as the product of broader cultural ideals operating within both digital and interpersonal environments. In particular, the findings indicate that the association between passive social media use and restrained eating depends on peer attachment security, with high peer attachment security buffering the association between passive social media use and restrained eating.

From an applied perspective, interventions may benefit from increasing awareness of the potential influence of passive social media exposure on eating-related behaviors. At the same time, fostering secure peer relationships and supporting positive body image may help reduce vulnerability to restrained eating. Overall, the findings highlight the importance of addressing both digital and interpersonal contexts when promoting healthy eating behaviors and body image among Chinese college women.
