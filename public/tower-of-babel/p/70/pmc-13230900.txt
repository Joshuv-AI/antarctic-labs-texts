Tree structural modeling from leaf-on point clouds for biomass carbon stock estimation
Authors: Yuto Nakamura, Taiga Yamazaki, Hiroaki Shirakawa, Hiroki Tanikawa, Masahiro Nagao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Urban trees represent valuable renewable biomass sources, but traditional allometric equations inadequately capture structural variability in urban environments. Therefore, considering tree structure is crucial for accurate biomass measurement. While LiDAR-based 3D modeling can reflect tree architecture, generating reliable models from dense foliage point clouds has remained difficult. To address this challenge, we developed the Skeleton Generative Method (SkeletonGM), which reconstructs tree trunks and primary branches from mobile laser scanning data even under heavy foliation. SkeletonGM produces a clarified skeletal point cloud that is subsequently converted into 3D tree models using AdTree; aboveground woody biomass is then calculated by combining the estimated volume with species-specific wood density. To validate the proposed method, we applied SkeletonGM to 33 hinoki cypress trees in a forest stand and 25 dawn redwood trees in a park, and compared the resulting biomass estimates with values derived from allometric equations. The results showed strong agreement with the reference equations (R² = 0.95 and 0.94; mean absolute percentage error = 14.1% and 14.6%). These findings indicate that the proposed method has strong potential to improve the accuracy of biomass assessment for urban trees.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47736-8.

Introduction

Accurate assessment and management of tree resources are essential for achieving the sustainable utilization of woody biomass1,2. Urban green spaces and street trees represent typical environments in which tree surveys are conducted regularly by municipal authorities, and basic information such as tree species, diameter at breast height (DBH), and tree height is relatively well documented.

Allometric equations using DBH and tree height have long been widely employed for biomass estimation and have demonstrated their effectiveness in natural forests3–12. However, it has been pointed out that the estimation accuracy of these methods decreases for urban trees whose structure is substantially influenced by urban environmental conditions13–17.

Trees grown in urban environments tend to have less biomass than those grown in forests, and it has been conventional practice to multiply allometrically derived values by a correction factor of 0.813,14. However, a study examining the validity of this correction factor found that biomass estimation errors ranged from − 63% to 163% across different DBH classes, indicating that a single uniform correction factor is insufficient to address the uncertainty inherent in applying forest-derived allometric equations to urban trees15. Such discrepancies are a consequence of the fundamentally different growing conditions between urban and forest environments, suggesting that the predictive accuracy of allometric equations has inherent limitations16,17. Although developing urban-specific allometric equations through the felling of urban trees represents one potential solution, destructive sampling of this kind is not readily feasible due to restrictions on tree removal in urban areas16.

Recent advances in three-dimensional (3D) measurement technologies, particularly light detection and ranging (LiDAR), have enabled the non-destructive acquisition of detailed tree structures[18,19,20,21]. Particularly, mobile laser scanning (MLS) has emerged as a powerful tool, offering rapid collection of high-density point cloud data over large areas using vehicle-mounted sensors. Compared to airborne laser scanning (ALS), MLS provides higher resolution through closer-range measurements, and greater efficiency than terrestrial laser scanning (TLS), making it well-suited for evaluating urban vegetation such as street trees22. To estimate biomass from point clouds, numerous methods based on quantitative structure models (QSMs) have been developed. These approaches extract individual tree structures and reconstruct 3D models using geometric fitting23–37. For instance, AdTree24 reconstructs skeletal structures using minimum spanning trees and cylinder fitting. AdQSM extends this approach for biomass estimation by calculating volumes[25,26]. Aboveground biomass is calculated by estimating volumes and applying species-specific wood densities[32,33,35,36]. However, dense foliage often hides internal structures during MLS data collection, limiting the accuracy of 3D models38–44. Measurement constraints in urban environments further exacerbate this issue45–47, and leaf-off data acquisition is equally impractical in subtropical and tropical environments dominated by evergreen species.

To address these limitations, recent studies have explored the reconstruction of missing internal structures. For example, Wang et al. proposed applying α-shape algorithms to point cloud outer surfaces and regenerating internal structures using L-system approaches27. However, L-system methods depend heavily on species-specific growth parameters and cannot accommodate individual tree variations. Bornand et al. reported success in complementing point clouds of deciduous tree trunks and primary branches using transformer-based PointTr models[29], though they noted significant limitations when applying PointTr to conifers due to strong laser scattering by needle structures [29, 47]. Similarly, SK-TreePCN30 requires leaf-off point clouds, leaving reconstruction under dense foliation conditions as an unresolved challenge. Thus, accurately reconstructing tree structure and estimating biomass from incomplete point clouds under leaf-on conditions remains a major challenge.

This study addressed these challenges by developing a skeleton generative method, called SkeletonGM, a new reconstruction method designed to infer trunk and primary branch structures from point clouds with limited internal visibility. Rather than aiming at highly detailed 3D tree reconstruction, SkeletonGM is primarily intended to support the estimation of biomass and carbon sequestration by incorporating tree structural information. To demonstrate the versatility of the method, we applied SkeletonGM to two contrasting species: Chamaecyparis obtusa (commonly known as hinoki cypress), which has relatively sparse foliage, and Metasequoia glyptostroboides (commonly known as dawn redwood), a species with dense foliage for which existing methods typically fail. SkeletonGM addresses the challenge of estimating biomass for individual trees using 3D models under leaf-on conditions.

Methods

Point cloud data acquisition

A hand-held LiDAR, LiGrip O1 Lite or LiGrip GS-1, manufactured by Green Valley, was used for the measurements. The former LiDAR has a scan accuracy of 2 cm, a scan range of 70 m, and a scan rate of 200,000 pts/s. The latter LiDAR has a scan accuracy of 2 cm, a scan range of 120 m, and a scan rate of 320,000 pts/s. It is also equipped with a high-resolution camera and is capable of generating colored point clouds through post-processing. Simultaneous localization and mapping was performed using LiDAR360-MLS for post-processing.

In this study, measurements were taken from coniferous trees whose trunks were assumed to be straight. As examples of coniferous trees, we measured the dimensions of hinoki cypress and down redwood. For hinoki cypress, a forest site was selected: the Akatsu Research Forest of the University of Tokyo in Aichi Prefecture, Japan. Hinoki cypress trees in the Akatsu Research Forest were measured using O1-Lite in November 2024. As hinoki cypress is an evergreen species, foliage was fully retained at the time of measurement. Dawn redwood trees were measured in the Metasequoia Plaza of Heiwa Park, located in Aichi Prefecture, Japan. Measurements were conducted in June 2024, during the fully leaf-on season when the trees exhibited dense foliage. Although situated within an urban park, this site was intentionally selected because the trees are not subject to regular pruning or intensive management. Biomass estimates derived from established allometric equations were used as benchmark reference values for validation. Since the accuracy of allometric equations is known to decline in heavily pruned or structurally modified urban trees, sites with minimal anthropogenic alteration were chosen to ensure the validity of the comparison. Measurements at both sites were conducted with formal permission obtained from the respective managing authorities.

The diameter at breast height (DBH), defined as the trunk diameter at 1.2 m above the ground, was manually measured for the tree selected for point cloud analysis. Because direct measurement of the tree height was difficult, it was estimated from the vertical distance between the lowest and highest points in the LiDAR data.

A total of 33 hinoki cypress trees with diameters at breast height (DBH) ranging from 12.0 to 33.9 cm and heights between 14.4 and 18.4 m were measured. In addition, 25 dawn redwood trees were surveyed, with DBH values ranging from 16.6 to 54.7 cm and heights between 10.3 and 24.6 m (Table S1, Fig. S1).

Point cloud data processing

Single trees were extracted from the measured point cloud data. The segment tool of CloudCompare was used for the cropping. Trees were clipped from the base to the top of the tree so that the entire tree canopy was included. Obstructions around the trunk were carefully avoided during this process.

Technical Description of SkeletonGM

To create a 3D tree model from a point cloud, the trunk and branch structures had to be clearly measured. In addition, when leaves were present at the time of measurement, they blocked the LiDAR sensor signal, and the structure within the tree canopy could not be adequately measured. Therefore, leaves were removed from the obtained point cloud, and the branches and trunk were regenerated.

This section describes the skeleton generative method (SkeletonGM), which was developed to reconstruct trunk and branch structures from tree point clouds. SkeletonGM was implemented in Python. The method consisted of two main processes: trunk generation and branch generation. The process of trunk reconstruction consisted of the following five steps, as shown in Fig. 1.

Step 1. Slice the point cloud

First, the tree point cloud was sliced horizontally to obtain a point cloud for each section. The slice width was a parameter, which in this study was set to 0.50 m.

Step 2. Classify the trunk point cloud by convex hull area

The sliced point cloud was sorted from the bottom to the top of the tree, and each slice was evaluated to determine whether it contained only the trunk. This classification was based on a comparison of convex hull areas. A slice was classified as trunk if its convex hull area was smaller than that of the lowest slice. Otherwise, it was classified as non-trunk (i.e., containing branches or leaves).

Step 3. Perform circle fitting for trunk point groups

For slices determined to be a trunk point group, circle fitting was performed to calculate the center and diameter of the trunk.

Step 4. Perform voxelization and calculate the centroid of non-trunk points

To capture the overall structural trend, non-trunk slices—typically from the upper parts of the tree—were first grouped into four integrated sections. Voxel grid sampling (voxel size = 0.30 m) was then applied to mitigate point density bias caused by LiDAR measurements. The centroid of each voxelized slice was then computed. This process was based on the hypothesis that the trunk structure lies near the centroid of the surrounding branch point cloud. To stabilize the estimated trunk axis, the centroid of each current slice was averaged with the centroid obtained from the immediately preceding slice. This smoothing step assumed that the trunk structure extends approximately linearly along the vertical axis, with no significant lateral deviation in centroid positions. For the initial non-trunk slice, where no preceding centroid was available, the average was calculated using the centroid of the lowest trunk slice identified in Step 3.

Step 5. Calculate diameters by assuming a cone for non-trunk point groups

For upper slices identified as non-trunk, a conical trunk model was assumed, under the hypothesis that the trunk gradually tapers from the highest slice identified as trunk to the top of the tree. Based on this assumption, the trunk diameter for each slice was estimated using the centroid as the center. The trunk point cloud was then reconstructed using the procedures described in Steps 1 through 5. Although a perfectly straight conical trunk model could have been assumed from the base to the apex, this study aimed to represent leaning or curved trees by incorporating centroid-based alignment for each slice.

Step 1. Slice the point cloud

First, the tree point cloud was sliced horizontally to obtain a point cloud for each section. The slice width was a parameter, which in this study was set to 1.0 m.

Step 2. Calculate the convex hull of the slice

The convex hull was computed for each slice of the point cloud. This step was based on the assumption that the outermost points of a slice correspond to the endpoints of well-developed branches.

Step 3. Obtain neighboring points

For each vertex of the extracted convex hull, neighboring points were identified. The optimal number of neighbors was assumed to depend on the point density of the original point cloud. In this study, the number of nearest neighbors was set to 1,000.

Step 4. Calculate principal component vectors to obtain the branch direction

Principal component analysis was applied to the neighboring points of each vertex of the convex hull to compute the principal component vectors. The first principal component vector was used to estimate the local branch growth direction at each vertex.

Step 5. Generate a reference point for each branch

A sequence of points was generated along the direction of the first principal component vector. This sequence connected each trunk vertex with its corresponding convex hull vertex and served as a reference for branch reconstruction. In this study, each sequence consisted of 11 points.

Step 6. Regenerate the branches

The neighboring points around each branch reference point were extracted to reconstruct the branch point cloud. In practice, when a branch existed, its internal structure was often captured as a sparse and incomplete point cloud due to occlusion and limited visibility. Generating a series of reference points along the estimated branch direction allowed aggregation of these sparse points to form a more coherent representation of the branch structure.

3D Model-based volume estimation

AdTree was used as the 3D modeling method in this study. The implementation was based on the main branch of the GitHub repository published by Du et al. (2019) and accessed by us on December 19, 2025. AdTree was primarily designed for visual modeling and did not inherently generate closed meshes. Therefore, in this study, the code was partially customized to enable volume calculation by adding caps to both ends of each generated cylinder, thereby creating closed mesh structures. Biomass was estimated by multiplying the 3D model volume by the species-specific density. A density of 0.45 g/cm³ was used for hinoki cypress48 and 0.28 g/cm³ for dawn redwood49.

Leaves were not included in the generated 3D models. Therefore, the estimated biomass represented not the total AGB but rather the Aboveground Woody Biomass (AGWB). Biomass was generally estimated by multiplying the volume derived from a 3D model by species-specific wood density. In this study, this conventional method was adopted to compare the accuracy between models.

Allometric biomass calculation methods

The allometric equation-based method was used as a benchmark for comparison with the volume-based approach using the 3D model. This method estimated biomass based on DBH, tree height, and species-specific wood density. These equations were derived from destructive measurements, that is, by actually felling trees and measuring their biomass.

In this study, we used a generalized allometric formula developed by Ishihara et al. (2015), which was based on data from 1,203 trees (covering 102 species) in natural forests ranging from Hokkaido to subtropical regions in Japan5. This formula could estimate not only total AGB but also the biomass of separate parts such as the trunk, branches, leaves, and roots. The coefficient of determination for AGB was reported to be 0.988, with a relative error of 11.7%. For leaf biomass, a relative error of 38.5% and an R² value of 0.892 were reported.

For comparison purposes in this study, the aboveground woody biomass (AGWB) of hinoki cypress was calculated by subtracting the leaf biomass from the total AGB. For dawn redwood, only the total AGB was used, because the allometric formula by Ishihara et al. did not include a leaf biomass equation for deciduous conifers. Ideally, biomass values obtained through destructive sampling would have been used as ground truth. However, due to the difficulty of conducting such measurements, we treated the values estimated by the allometric formula as reference values in this study.

Results

3D Modeling using leaf-on point clouds of hinoki cypress with distinct trunk

Figure 3 shows 3D models of 33 leaf-on hinoki cypress point clouds generated by three methods. The existing methods (AdTree and AdQSM) produce more complex and voluminous structures in the upper crown regions with dense foliage, whereas the SkeletonGM-AdTree method.

results in a reduced upper crown volume. Trunk geometry appears visually similar across all methods.

Measured hinoki cypress point clouds, SkeletonGM skeleton point clouds, and reconstructed 3D models.

The AdTree and AdQSM 3D models were directly reconstructed from the leaf-on point clouds. The SkeletonGM-AdTree models were reconstructed via skeleton point clouds generated from the leaf-on point clouds using SkeletonGM.

To quantitatively evaluate the reconstructed 3D models, diameter at breast height (DBH), tree height, and aboveground woody biomass were calculated for the three modeling methods. The results are shown in Fig. 4. For DBH, both existing methods exhibited a tendency to overestimate the reference values, with mean absolute percentage errors (MAPE) of 24.6% for AdTree and 24.5% for AdQSM. In contrast, SkeletonGM-AdTree closely matched the field measurements, achieving a substantially lower MAPE of 2.91%. Tree height estimates showed excellent agreement with the point cloud data for all methods. Aboveground woody biomass, calculated from model volume and wood density and evaluated against reference values derived from allometric equations based on field measurements, was consistently overestimated by the existing methods. The MAPE was 76.4% for AdTree and 74.2% for AdQSM. In contrast, SkeletonGM-AdTree showed markedly improved accuracy, with a MAPE of 14.1%.

Discussion

Accuracy differences in 3D modeling

It is widely recognized that point cloud quality (noise, density heterogeneity, and occlusion by foliage) significantly affects modeling accuracy in TLS-based tree 3D modeling50–52. Similar challenges were also observed in this study; however, preprocessing with skeleton point clouds generated by SkeletonGM proved to be an effective strategy for improving reconstruction accuracy. Each parameter is discussed in detail below.

Estimation accuracy of DBH

For diameter at breast height (DBH), the existing methods failed to accurately reconstruct the trunk of dawn redwood, a species in which the trunk is largely obscured by the crown. Due to the insufficient presence of trunk points within the point cloud, DBH estimates deviated substantially from field measurements, and overestimation of trunk diameter was frequently observed.

The difficulty of trunk estimation in species with dense crowns has been reported in multiple previous studies. Calders et al. (2015) demonstrated that occlusion by foliage reduces trunk visibility and affects QSM construction accuracy52, and Terryn et al. (2022) also reported that TLS sampling of trunks becomes incomplete in high crown density environments53. The dawn redwood in this study faced a similar situation. SkeletonGM generates trunk point clouds using measurable near-ground trunk information, tree height, and crown center points. Furthermore, by removing surrounding crown point clouds, the trunk portion becomes relatively clarified. These skeleton point clouds are considered to have led to substantial improvement in DBH accuracy.

Estimation accuracy of tree height

For tree height, no significant differences were observed among methods, as all algorithms estimate based on the difference between the lowest and highest points in the point cloud. This result indicates that tree height estimation depends more strongly on the vertical acquisition quality of point cloud data than on geometric modeling of trunks and branches.

Estimation accuracy of aboveground woody biomass

For aboveground woody biomass, since direct measurement was difficult, values calculated using allometric equations were used as ground truth. For hinoki cypress, the existing methods showed a high MAPE of approximately 75% with a tendency toward overestimation. Multiple studies have attributed the tendency of TLS-derived QSMs to overestimate biomass to excessive generation of small branches caused by leaf points. Raumonen et al. (2013) reported that TLS scanning under leaf-on conditions causes overestimation of model volume23, and Calders et al. (2015) noted similar issues52. Furthermore, Demol et al. (2022) quantitatively demonstrated volume overestimation of small branches by TreeQSM54.

The approximately 75% overestimation observed in this study, although using different methods, falls within the range reported in previous studies (30–100%; Raumonen et al., 2013; Calders et al., 2015; Demol et al., 2022). In contrast, models generated via SkeletonGM showed substantial accuracy improvement with a MAPE of 14.1%. Since the obtained 3D models clearly show reduced crown volume, this suggests the combined effect of improved trunk thickness estimation and crown structure regeneration.

For dawn redwood, estimation errors in aboveground biomass were extremely large at 386% for AdTree and 8320% for AdQSM. This is likely attributed to the absence of trunk points and the erroneous use of crown points as trunk points, as well as excessive branch generation caused by leaf points. The 3D models obtained by these methods failed to adequately reproduce the internal crown structure and exhibited shapes conforming to the crown exterior. SkeletonGM acquires characteristic points on the crown exterior through convex hull analysis and estimates primary branches extending from the trunk by determining direction vectors from crown points surrounding these characteristic points. This approach enabled estimation of the internal crown skeleton, leading to substantial accuracy improvement with a MAPE of 14.5%.

It should be noted that the present study is limited to comparing biomass estimates between allometric equations and 3D models. Accurate evaluation of aboveground woody biomass calculated from 3D models requires comparison with directly measured values. As a reference, we conducted destructive sampling of one hinoki cypress tree and confirmed that the proposed method outperformed existing methods. This result is presented in Supplementary Fig. 8.

Parameter tuning of SkeletonGM

SkeletonGM involves five parameters: for trunk point cloud generation, the slice width and voxel size (Steps 1 and 4 in trunk generation); for branch point cloud generation, the slice width, the number of nearest neighbors, and the number of points connecting the trunk to each branch (Steps 1, 3, and 5 in branch generation). We conducted a preliminary analysis to observe output trends and determine suitable values for these parameters. The following sections outlines our strategy for parameter tuning.

The slice width used for trunk point cloud generation is a parameter that determines the resolution at which trunk shape information is utilized. When the slice width is large, the trunk geometry becomes excessively averaged, whereas an overly small slice width increases sensitivity to variations in point density and measurement noise. The slice width was gradually reduced from 3.0 m, and the parameter value at which the results became stable (slice width = 0.50 m) was adopted in this study.

The voxel size should be adjusted so that terminal branches constituting the crown are represented by a single point, thereby reducing biases in point density caused by measurement conditions. In this study, the voxel size was set to 0.3 m based on the scale of terminal branches observed in the field.

The slice width used for branch point cloud generation influences the level of detail captured in the crown outline and is dependent on tree height. In this study, a value of 1.0 m was adopted, corresponding to the unit scale of tree height. However, for trees whose height falls outside the range considered in this study (10.3–24.6 m), parameter adjustment is desirable. For such adjustments, it is recommended to examine several sample trees in the field, confirm the actual number of primary branches, and calibrate the parameter so as to minimize the discrepancy between the observed number of primary branches and those generated by SkeletonGM.

The number of neighboring points used to determine branch direction should be sufficient to capture a single branch–foliage cluster extending from the trunk, as the objective is to estimate the direction of primary branches. If too few points are selected, the branch direction cannot be adequately characterized; conversely, if too many points are included, points belonging to adjacent branch–foliage clusters may be incorporated. Based on preliminary analysis, 1,000 neighboring points were found to appropriately represent a single branch–foliage cluster for both hinoki cypress (Fig. 2, S3) and dawn redwood.

The number of points used to connect the trunk to each branch is a parameter that should be adjusted according to the horizontal distance from the trunk to the outer edge of the crown. If the number of points is too small, the branch structure cannot be sufficiently represented; if too large, computational cost increases unnecessarily. In this study, even for dawn redwood, which exhibited relatively large crowns, the distance from the trunk to the crown edge was approximately 5 m. Considering that a resolution of 0.5 m was sufficient to represent the trunk structure, we adopted the same interval for branch representation, resulting in a sequence of 11 points.

Limitations and applicable conditions of the proposed method

SkeletonGM assumes that individual tree point clouds are pre-segmented; the development of a fully automated segmentation-to-modeling pipeline was beyond the scope of this study.

In addition, the current implementation has limitations when applied to morphologically complex tree structures. For trunk reconstruction, measurable lower sections are modeled using circle fitting, whereas upper sections obscured by the canopy are approximated using a conical assumption. This simplification may introduce increasing discrepancies as tree size increases. Consistent with this tendency, the fitted regression for dawn redwood (y = 1.2x − 59) yielded a slope slightly greater than unity, suggesting a tendency toward overestimation for larger individuals, particularly those exceeding approximately 800 kg AGB (Fig. 7). Furthermore, the method assumes a single, vertically extending trunk; therefore, accuracy may decrease for trees with basal bifurcation, pronounced inclination, or curvature. Because the trunk accounts for approximately 70% of total aboveground woody biomass, further improvement in trunk modeling accuracy is particularly important for enhancing the reliability of total AGB estimation.

Regarding branch reconstruction, the convex hull–based extraction of the outer crown boundary may produce structurally different representations for trees with markedly different crown architectures, such as horizontally spreading or pendulous forms or during leaf-off conditions, compared with trees having conical crown shapes. However, branch-related limitations are expected to have a smaller impact on total AGB estimation than trunk-related errors, given the lower proportional contribution of branches to total aboveground biomass.

Because dawn redwood is a deciduous species, it also provides an opportunity to evaluate the seasonal robustness of the proposed approach. Future studies could acquire both leaf-on and leaf-off datasets for the same individuals to directly assess how defoliation and refoliation influence trunk and branch reconstruction accuracy and total AGB estimation. Such comparative validation would help clarify the seasonal sensitivity and broader applicability of SkeletonGM.

Further validation for morphologically complex tree forms and extension of the algorithm to account for bifurcating trunk structures remain important directions for future work.

Application to biomass carbon stock estimation incorporating tree structure

The most significant advantage of SkeletonGM lies in its ability to estimate trunk structure from point cloud data acquired under leaf-on conditions. In urban LiDAR observations, data acquisition under leaf-off conditions is often difficult due to seasonal and species-specific constraints, limiting the applicability of conventional structural models and detailed tree architecture reconstruction methods.

Since SkeletonGM generates skeletal structures based on geometric information from the trunk base, it can utilize trunk shape information even in situations where occlusion by foliage is present. This characteristic enables consideration of individual growth patterns and structural differences that cannot be captured by average estimates based on limited metrics such as DBH and tree height, as in allometric equations. This provides a new option for evaluating urban tree biomass and carbon stock.

Conclusion

This study proposed SkeletonGM, a skeleton generative method designed to reconstruct trunk and primary branch structures from leaf-on LiDAR point clouds with limited internal visibility. Skeletal extraction prior to 3D modeling yielded biomass estimates closer to allometric reference values than those obtained without this preprocessing step.

The method was validated using two contrasting conifer species, hinoki cypress and dawn redwood, demonstrating robust performance even under dense foliage conditions where conventional methods failed. While biomass estimates were evaluated against allometric equations due to the difficulty of destructive sampling, the results indicate that incorporating structural information through skeleton-based reconstruction can mitigate the overestimation commonly observed in leaf-on QSM approaches.

SkeletonGM is particularly suited for conditions where leaf-off data acquisition is impractical or impossible, including urban and mobile LiDAR applications as well as subtropical and tropical environments dominated by evergreen species. Future work will focus on extending the method to trees with more complex trunk morphologies and on validation using destructive measurements or alternative reference datasets.
