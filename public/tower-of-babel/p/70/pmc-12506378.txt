Nocturnal hypoxia moderates the relationship between rapid eye movement sleep and anxiety
Authors: Fei Jiang, Tianbai Li, Jinsong Huang, Lijun Fan, Wenzhu Zhou, Weidong Le, Gang Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveSleep problems, especially rapid eye movement sleep disturbances, are associated with various neurological and psychological disorders. Given the potential of nocturnal hypoxia to induce psychological symptoms, we investigated its moderating effect on the relationship between sleep variables and psychological symptoms among individuals with sleep disturbances.MethodsA total of 160 participants who complained of sleep problems were enrolled, and their hypoxic and sleep indices were recorded using overnight polysomnography. The psychological status of all participants was further assessed using Symptom Checklist-90-R (SCL-90-R, Chinese version). Hierarchical regression analyses were employed to examine the influence of hypoxic indices and sleep indices, and the interaction of these variables on psychological symptoms. Simple slope analyses were used to probe significant interactions.ResultsHierarchical regression analyses indicated that the cumulative duration of nocturnal hypoxia (SpO2 < 95%) moderated the relationship between the percentage of rapid eye movement sleep (RT%) and psychological symptoms (somatization, obsessive compulsive, interpersonal sensitivity, depression, anxiety, hostility, phobic anxiety, and psychoticism). To account for the increased risk of Type I errors due to multiple statistical tests, we applied False Discovery Rate correction. After applying the FDR correction, the p-value for the cumulative duration of nocturnal hypoxia (SpO2 < 95%) in moderating the relationship between RT% and anxiety remained significant (β = 0.312, △ F = 16.586, p < 0.01, 95% confidence interval [0.009, 0.025]).ConclusionOur study demonstrates that nocturnal hypoxia, particularly the cumulative duration of hypoxia (SpO2 < 95%), moderates the association between RT% and anxiety symptoms. These findings indicate a potential interaction between rapid eye movement sleep and nocturnal hypoxia in relation to psychological symptoms. Further research is warranted to clarify the underlying mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07440-9.Keywords: Nocturnal hypoxia, Rapid eye movement sleep, Polysomnography, Mental health, SCL-90-R, Moderating effect

Background

Sleep disturbances have long been recognized as both a symptom and a risk factor for various psychological disorders [1–3]. Poor sleep quality, insufficient sleep, and sleep disorders such as insomnia and sleep apnea are consistently linked to the onset and exacerbation of psychiatric conditions, including depression, anxiety, and mood disorders [4, 5]. Sleep deprivation has been shown to impair emotional regulation and increase stress reactivity, potentially triggering or worsening symptoms of anxiety and depression [6, 7]. Additionally, disturbed sleep patterns may contribute to altered brain function, particularly in regions involved in emotional processing, such as the prefrontal cortex and limbic system [8–10]. These alterations may heighten the risk of emotional dysregulation, rumination, and maladaptive coping mechanisms, which are commonly observed in individuals with psychiatric disorders [11, 12].

Rapid Eye Movement (REM) sleep plays a critical role in emotional and psychological health. Disturbances in REM sleep have been closely linked to various psychiatric disorders, including depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder [13–15]. REM sleep dysregulation has been proposed as a potential endophenotype of depression [13]. For example, in adolescent samples, higher levels of depressive symptoms have been associated with increased REM fragmentation and higher REM density [14]. Individuals with major depressive disorder often experience alterations in REM sleep states, such as shortened REM latency and increased REM density, reflecting impaired emotional processing and regulation [16, 17]. Furthermore, chronic REM sleep restriction has been shown to induce anxiety-like behavior and alter monoamine systems in the amygdala and hippocampus in animal models, particularly in young male rats [18]. This disruption may lead to rumination, negative emotional bias, and a heightened vulnerability to mood disturbances. Studies have also demonstrated that patients with obstructive sleep apnea syndrome (OSAS) have a higher incidence of depression and anxiety, especially those with REM-related sleep disturbances [19]. Both REM sleep percentage and REM sleep latency have been significantly correlated with difficulties in emotional regulation in individuals with insomnia disorder [20]. During REM sleep, the brain shows heightened activity in regions critical for emotional regulation, such as the amygdala and prefrontal cortex [15]. Impaired REM sleep, characterized by reduced duration or disrupted cycles, has been shown to contribute to emotional dysregulation and increased stress sensitivity [20]. In the context of sleep disorders, such as sleep apnea, REM sleep is particularly vulnerable to disruption due to intermittent hypoxia and fragmented sleep cycles [21]. These disturbances can exacerbate psychiatric symptoms, particularly in individuals with pre-existing mood disorders.

On the other hand, the effects of hypoxia on psychological symptoms have been increasingly recognized. Previous studies have shown that long-term exposure to anoxic environments can lead to neuropsychiatric disorders and increase the risk of depression, anxiety, and other psychological problems [22, 23]. It has also been reported that living at high altitudes can increase the risk of depression and suicide [24, 25]. Acute high-altitude hypoxia exposure has been shown to cause adverse neurological consequences [26]. Furthermore, growing evidence suggests that hypoxia may play a role in the pathophysiology of schizophrenia [27–30]. Interestingly, depression and anxiety are common comorbidities in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and associated hypoxia [31]. Long-term hypoxia has been shown to affect dopaminergic and serotonergic systems, increasing the risk of psychiatric disorders [32–34]. Additionally, hypoxic conditions can modify signaling pathways, such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor and Ras homolog gene family member A, contributing to increased inflammatory mediators and ultimately leading to cerebral hypoxic damage [22, 35–38].

Accumulating evidence suggests that nocturnal hypoxia plays a significant role in modulating both sleep patterns and psychological symptoms. Conditions such as OSAS, intermittent hypoxia, and high-altitude exposure are commonly associated with nocturnal hypoxia, which disrupts normal sleep architecture, particularly impacting the duration and quality of REM and deep sleep stages [21, 39, 40]. Studies have shown that hypoxic episodes during sleep lead to sleep fragmentation, decreased sleep efficiency, and increased sleep latency, which in turn contribute to heightened daytime sleepiness and cognitive dysfunction [41, 42]. Moreover, animal studies have demonstrated that both sleep fragmentation and intermittent hypoxia can alter cognitive function while exerting opposing effects on anxiety behaviors [43]. These findings suggest that nocturnal hypoxia not only affects sleep but may also modulate the relationship between sleep disturbances and psychological symptoms.

Given the growing evidence, it is plausible that nocturnal hypoxia may moderate the relationship between sleep disturbances and psychological distress. However, There is limited literature exploring the relationship between nocturnal hypoxia, sleep, and psychological symptoms. Therefore, we hypothesize that nocturnal hypoxia moderates the association between sleep disruptions, especially REM sleep state, and psychological symptoms. Our study aims to examine this moderating effect in individuals with sleep disturbances.

Methods

Participants

We enrolled 160 participants over the age of 18 with sleep complaints (40 males, mean age 58.78 years) from Dalian Seventh People’s Hospital between 2019.9 and 2023.11. All participants were examined by polysomnography (PSG) because of sleep complaints, including sleep disorders, snoring, abnormal behavior during sleep, and observed apnea and other complaints related to sleep. Participants were recruited from the outpatient or inpatient department of Dalian Seventh People’s Hospital. General information collected for each case includes gender, age, marital status, family history of mental disorders, smoking history, drinking history, major illness, medication, duration of sleep disorders, body mass index, and so on. All procedures in this study involving human participants were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. The study was approved by the ethics committee of Dalian Seventh People’s Hospital (No.2023-04). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardians.

PSG measures

Nocturnal PSG was recorded by 64-channel PSG (Compumedics Grael, Australia) and scored according to the American Academy of Sleep Medicine guidelines. Sleep architecture and related variables were recorded, including total sleep time (TST), sleep efficiency (SE%), awakening time (AT), awakening number (AN), sleep latency (SL), percentage of REM state (RT%), I phase (N1%), II phase (N2%), III phase (N3%). Nocturnal hypoxia was assessed using T95 and T90, defined as the cumulative time (in hours) spent with oxygen saturation (SpO2) below 95% or 90% during TST. Additional hypoxia metrics, including mean SpO2, were analyzed for consistency (see Table 1). All nocturnal hypoxic values are reported in hours unless otherwise specified. To examine ventilation and airflow, we evaluated the apnea-hypopnea index (AHI). Apnea was defined as a complete or near-complete (≥ 90%) cessation of airflow for more than 10 s. Hypopnea was defined as a reduced respiratory signal, by > 30%, lasting for at least 10 s and associated with oxygen saturation ≥ 3% from baseline or an arousal. AHI was defined as the average sum of the number of apnea and hypopnea events per hour. Measurements were performed from 22:00 to 06:00 of the next day in a dark and quiet room. Sleep staging was determined by two well-trained technicians to ensure accuracy and consistency, following the standard criteria outlined in the American Academy of Sleep Medicine scoring manual. Prior to analysis, rigorous quality checks were applied to the polysomnographic data. Segments contaminated by artifacts, such as movement, muscle activity, or electrode malfunction, were identified and excluded from analysis through visual inspection. Only clean, artifact-free segments were included in the final dataset, ensuring the accuracy and reliability of the sleep staging and subsequent analyses.

Mental health status measurements

Symptom Checklist-90-Revised (SCL-90-R, Chinese version), a self-report questionnaire, was used in the present study to assess mental health status. This instrument assesses various dimensions of psychological symptoms, including somatization, obsessive compulsive, interpersonal sensitivity, depression, anxiety, hostility, phobic anxiety, paranoid ideation, and psychoticism. Participants were asked to rate their behaviors, feelings, and thoughts over the past week using a 5-point Likert scale. The time interval between mental testing and overnight PSG administration was restricted to within three days to minimize potential variations in mental health status.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using the statistical package for the social sciences (SPSS INC, Chicago, IL, USA, version 25). Descriptive statistics were calculated for gender, age, marital status, family history of mental disorders, smoking history, drinking history, major illness, medication, duration of sleep disorders, body mass index (BMI), sleep indices and hypoxic indices. Absolute and relative frequencies were calculated for qualitative variables. Hierarchical regression analyses examined the influence of hypoxic variables and sleep variables, and the interaction of these variables on psychological symptoms and self-esteem. We conducted a hierarchical regression analysis in several steps. Four regression models were run. Initially, a model was constructed to examine the relationship between relevant demographic characteristics and psychological symptoms. Control variables, including age, gender, benzodiazepine use, smoking history, BMI, and AHI, were included in this model. Subsequently, RT% was added as an independent variable. Next, the moderator variable T95 was introduced into the model to assess its impact on the relationship between RT% and psychological symptoms. Finally, the interaction term between RT% and T95 was added to the model to explore potential moderating effects. Simple slope analyses were then performed to investigate significant interactions.

To control for the increased risk of Type I errors due to multiple statistical tests, we applied the False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction to adjust for multiple comparisons. Specifically, the p-values from all statistical tests were ranked, and the FDR was calculated using the formula: . where Pi represents the original p-value for the i-th test, m is the total number of tests performed, and i is the rank of the p-value. The FDR threshold for significance was set at 0.05. Tests with adjusted p-values below this threshold were considered statistically significant, indicating that the corresponding findings were unlikely to have arisen by chance.

Results

Characteristics of participants

A total of 160 participants (40 male, with an average age of 58.78 ± 10.15) were included in the present study. The majority of participants were married (94.4%), while only 3.8% were widows or widowers. Notably, 11.9% of the participants reported a family history of mental disorders, 23.8% had a history of hypertension, 9.4% had a history of diabetes, 17.5% had a history of fatty liver, 13.1% had a history of cerebral infarction or lacunar infarction, 10.0% had a smoking history, and 6.2% had a drinking history. Additionally, 70.0% of the participants had used one benzodiazepine while 16.9% had used more than one benzodiazepine within a week. It is worth noting that among all participants, 80% had an AHI < 5, 15% had an AHI between 5 and 14, 4.4% had an AHI between 14 and 30, and 0.6% had an AHI > 30 (Table 1).

Hypoxia-related variables included an average SpO2 of 94.01 ± 4.92%, and the cumulative duration of hypoxia, as indicated by T95 and T90, reflected the time spent at different levels of oxygen saturation during sleep. AHI with a mean value of 3.30 ± 5.49 indicated ventilation status (Table 1). In order to analyze sleep architecture and related variables, we examined TST (354.59 ± 85.86), SE% (77.37 ± 11.69), AT (99.04 ± 44.60), SL (43.69 ± 28.73), RT% (16.29 ± 6.79), N1% (5.02 ± 5.15), N2% (72.29 ± 14.42), N3% (6.39 ± 10.65) (Table 1).

To assess the psychological status of patients with sleep disturbances, we employed the Symptom Checklist-90-R (SCL-90-R), measuring nine dimensions of psychometric properties. The highest average score among these dimensions was observed for obsessive-compulsive tendencies (2.27 ± 0.80), followed by depression (2.19 ± 0.82), anxiety (2.15 ± 0.85), somatization (2.06 ± 0.71), interpersonal sensitivity (1.92 ± 0.72), psychoticism (1.79 ± 0.66), hostility (1.76 ± 0.67), phobic anxiety (1.75 ± 0.80), with the lowest score recorded for paranoid ideation (1.66 ± 0.68) (Fig. 1).

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the moderating effect of nocturnal hypoxia on the relationship between sleep indices and psychological symptoms in individuals with sleep disturbances. The results support the hypothesis that nocturnal hypoxia moderates the relationship between sleep disturbances and anxiety. Specifically, we found significant interactive effects between RT% and T95 on anxiety. Anxiety scores decreased as RT% increased at low levels of hypoxia, whereas they increased as RT% rose at high levels of hypoxia. These findings suggest that the moderating effects of hypoxia on anxiety vary depending on the severity of nocturnal hypoxia, as reflected by T95 levels.

There is a wealth of evidence linking REM sleep state to mental and neurodegenerative disorders [50–53]. For instance, Depression is characterized by a decrease in REM sleep latency and an increase in rapid eye movement density. Schizophrenia, mental retardation, and dementia are all associated with REM sleep disturbances [56]. Aditionally, hypoxic exposure, rather than arousal, has been shown to induce overall deficits in the electroencephalogram delta power of non-rapid eye movement (NREM) and marked deficits in the total amount of REMs [54]. Research on lucid dreaming has suggested that the cortico-subcortical networks involved in voluntary respiratory movements are preserved during REM sleep state [55]. The above studies indicate that hypoxia interacts with REM sleep state, potentially influencing the occurrence of psychiatric symptoms. This finding is consistent with our results, which suggest that hypoxia, through its impact on sleep patterns, may modulate the development of psychological symptoms.

Possible mechanisms underlying the moderating effect of nocturnal hypoxia

The moderating effect of nocturnal hypoxia on the relationship between REM sleep and anxiety symptoms may be explained by several interconnected mechanisms. First, hypoxia during sleep disrupts the normal restorative functions of REM sleep [21, 44], which plays a critical role in memory consolidation, emotional regulation, and overall cognitive functioning [15, 20, 45, 46]. Intermittent hypoxia has been shown to alter the activity of brain regions involved in emotional processing, such as the amygdala, prefrontal cortex, and hippocampus, potentially amplifying the emotional dysregulation associated with REM sleep disturbances [47, 48]. Previous studies have consistently reported that reduced or fragmented REM sleep is linked to elevated anxiety and other mood disturbances [18, 49]., with one study demonstrating significantly higher anxiety and depression scores in patients with REM-related OSAS [19].

Second, hypoxia-induced sleep fragmentation may impair the normal processing and consolidation of emotional memories during REM sleep, further exacerbating anxiety symptoms. Repeated nocturnal hypoxia episodes trigger frequent arousals, disrupt sleep continuity, and lead to alterations in sleep architecture, including reduced proportions of REM and slow wave sleep, increased awakenings, and overall fragmented sleep [47, 50]. In addition, nocturnal hypoxia can activate inflammatory pathways and oxidative stress, both of which are implicated in the pathophysiology of anxiety disorders and are known to be modulated by sleep patterns [51, 52].

Moreover, the cumulative duration of hypoxia (T95) may reflect a physiological threshold beyond which the brain’s capacity to compensate for disrupted sleep becomes overwhelmed. At lower levels of hypoxia, compensatory mechanisms may preserve sleep quality and mitigate psychological effects [47, 53–55]. However, as hypoxia severity increases, these adaptive responses diminish, leading to more pronounced anxiety symptoms [55]. These mechanisms collectively may explain the moderating role of nocturnal hypoxia on the relationship between REM sleep and anxiety, and warrant further investigation in future studies.

Acute and chronic hypoxia

Our results showed that the moderating effects of hypoxia on psychological symptoms differ between low and high T95 levels, which may reflect fundamentally different neurophysiological responses to varying durations of hypoxic exposure. In fact, during acute hypoxia, the deployment of multiple oxygen sensors enables neurons to adapt to physiological changes. These adaptive responses include synaptic signaling, which is often influenced by changes in anaerobic metabolism [56–58]. However, chronic hypoxia can lead to more severe disruptions in synaptic transmission and activate transcription factors involved in regulating oxygen homeostasis [56, 59]. Over time, the brain’s compensatory mechanisms for prolonged oxygen deficiency become compromised, potentially contributing to the emergence of psychological symptoms [23]. Chronic hypoxia triggers a cascade of cellular events, including oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neuroinflammation, all of which can result in neuronal damage and subsequent brain dysfunction [60–64]. For example, prolonged activation of microglia towards an M1 phenotype promotes the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, exacerbating neuronal injury [65]. Furthermore, chronic hypoxia impairs neurovascular coupling and disrupts the blood-brain barrier, further promoting neuroinflammation and neuronal loss [36].

Recent studies have provided compelling evidence linking chronic hypoxia to structural brain changes, such as neuronal loss, alterations in gray matter volume, and disruption of white matter integrity, all of which may contribute to the development of depression and anxiety [66–71]. Research has also shown that chronic hypoxia significantly reduces neuronal numbers in the hippocampal CA1 and CA3 regions of rats, potentially inducing anxiety-like behavior [72–74]. Additionally, chronic hypoxic stress may exacerbate serotonin imbalances in the brain, increasing susceptibility to depression, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor resistance, and potentially heightening the risk of suicide [75]. Hypoxia-induced changes in vascular function and blood-brain barrier integrity further contribute to brain damage, leading to the accumulation of toxic metabolites and inflammation, all of which may play a role in the development of psychological symptoms such as depression and anxiety [76, 77]. These mechanisms, initially adaptive, can become maladaptive over time, leading to irreversible brain damage and the manifestation of psychological symptoms. This existing evidence base suggests a potential transition point where initial physiological adaptations to hypoxia give way to more persistent neural system reorganization, a phenomenon that may account for the distinct patterns of psychological symptom manifestation observed at varying degrees of hypoxic exposure.

Hypoxia, sleep and anxiety

In our result, the moderating effects on symptom of anxiety are prominent, indicating the importance of considering the relationship between anxiety-related issues and hypoxic conditions. This observation aligns with the correlation between hypoxia and anxiety behavior, as supported by numerous animal experiments [74, 78, 79] and clinical articles [80, 81]. It is worth noting that anxiety is commonly observed in patients with respiratory problems, with respiratory symptoms often serving as the primary triggers for anxiety attacks. Regarding treatment approaches, respiratory therapy has been explored for managing anxiety [82]. Additionally, yoga respiratory practice has demonstrated efficacy in modulating the activity of brain regions involved in emotional processing, notably including the amygdala, anterior cingulate, anterior insula, and prefrontal cortex [83].

In summary, our study fills a critical gap in previous research by emphasizing the significance of cumulative time of mild hypoxia in the development of psychological symptoms. Furthermore, we determine the moderating effects of T95 on the relationship between RT% and psychological symptoms. These findings underscore the importance of considering targeted therapies aimed at reducing the duration of hypoxia and hypoventilation. For instance, continuous positive airway pressure treatment during sleep may be a potential therapy for mental disorders in the future targeted therapy for reducing the duration of hypoxia and hypoventilation should be concerned [84–86]. Such therapies aim to mitigate the impact of sleep-related hypoxia and hypoventilation on psychological well-being. Further research is needed to explore the interaction effects of nocturnal hypoxia and sleep disruption, and their potential to enhance mental health outcomes in individuals with sleep disturbances.

Limitations

Although this study yielded some clinically relevant evidence on the psychological impact of nocturnal hypoxia and sleep disruption, it has some limitations. First, the sample sizes of this research are relatively small, which may result in possible bias. We recruited participants who complained of sleep problems, leading to potential selection bias. This selection bias means that the present results may not necessarily be representative of a randomly chosen sample. Second, the overnight PSG results in our study do not indicate an individual’s chronic hypoxia degree. However, repeated PSG can yield more accurate results but increase the pressure on funding and reduce the patients’ compliance. Therefore, future research should explore alternative detection methods and indicators to better characterize long-term chronic hypoxia. Third, the SCL-90-R questionnaire used in our study, while comprehensive in assessing various dimensions of psychological disorders, is a self-rating scale. Self-report measures may introduce subjectivity and potential response bias. In further research, the introduction of other assessment tools may be more objective and quantitative. Nonetheless, this study serves as an initial exploration of the relationships between sleep-related hypoxia and mental disorders. The presented data underscore the importance of delving deeper into the intricate interactional mechanisms linking hypoxia and sleep disturbances, particularly focusing on the role of sleep-related hypoxia. Moreover, several alternative explanations for the observed associations should be acknowledged. It is possible that factors other than REM sleep disturbances, such as NREM sleep disruptions, comorbid depression, medication use, or undiagnosed psychiatric conditions, may also contribute to the relationship between nocturnal hypoxia and anxiety symptoms. Additionally, the possibility of bidirectional relationships cannot be excluded—anxiety symptoms themselves may impair sleep architecture and increase the likelihood of nocturnal hypoxia through hyperventilation or altered respiratory control during sleep. These factors were not assessed in this study and should be addressed in future investigations. Further research should investigate how the interaction between hypoxia and hypoxia manifests, including potential neurobiological, physiological, and behavioral mechanisms. Investigating these mechanisms holds promise for developing targeted interventions aimed at mitigating the adverse effects of hypoxia on mental health in individuals with sleep disturbances.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that nocturnal hypoxia, particularly the cumulative duration of hypoxia (SpO2 < 95%), moderates the association between RT% and anxiety symptoms. These findings indicate a potential interaction between REM sleep and nocturnal hypoxia in relation to psychological symptoms. Further research is warranted to clarify the underlying mechanisms.
