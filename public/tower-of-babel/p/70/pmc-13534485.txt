Anthropogenic accessibility and observer inequality dictate spatial biodiversity patterns in Malaysia
Authors: Saman Ghasemian Sorboni, Sharareh Pourebrahim, Jit Ern Chen, Mehrdad Hadipour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Global biodiversity monitoring increasingly relies on open-access community science data, but these opportunistic records harbor complex biases that can severely distort macroecological inference. Here, we disentangle how human behavior, infrastructure, and taxonomy interact to shape perceived biodiversity patterns across the two distinct biogeographic regions of Malaysia. Analyzing 336,042 research-grade iNaturalist records, we quantified observer inequality and taxonomic disproportionality. We estimated true species richness (Chao2) to map spatial inventory completeness (median = 33.3%) and employed Zero-Inflated Negative Binomial GLMMs and effort-corrected Generalized Additive Models (GAMs) to test the effects of topography, accessibility, and observer classification. We demonstrate extreme observer inequality (Gini = 0.854), with data collection heavily anchored to urban centers. Crucially, a significant three-way interaction revealed that dedicated “Power Users” successfully penetrate roadless interiors in Peninsular Malaysia, whereas casual observers remain strictly road-bound. Taxonomically, the data exhibits a severe charismatic skew, massively over-representing Aves and Reptilia while under-sampling foundational hyper-diverse clades like Insecta and Fungi. Furthermore, explicitly modeling non-linear sampling effort (user-days) rendered the effects of elevation and terrain ruggedness statistically non-significant. This demonstrates that perceived biodiversity deficits in rugged, high-elevation terrains are anthropogenic artifacts of human inaccessibility rather than true ecological absences. To meet global conservation targets, state funding and structured monitoring should complement opportunistic data by actively targeting these remote, under-sampled geographic and taxonomic shortfalls.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58296-2.

Introduction

Mitigating the global biodiversity crisis requires high-resolution, spatially explicit data on species distributions1,2. To track these conservation targets and guide protected area expansion, international policy frameworks emphasize the need for robust monitoring systems3,4. However, because traditional biodiversity surveys remain limited by logistical constraints and restricted coverage, macroecology has increasingly turned toward open-access data to fill these critical gaps5–7. Consequently, community-contributed observations now represent a substantial proportion of global occurrence records, forming a critical component of databases like the Global Biodiversity Information Facility8,9.

Platforms such as iNaturalist greatly expand data accessibility, yet their opportunistic nature introduces well-documented biases that complicate ecological inference. Spatial bias typically concentrates observations near accessible infrastructure, resulting in uneven landscape sampling10,11. Taxonomic bias leads to the over-representation of charismatic taxa relative to more diverse but less detectable groups12–14. Additionally, differences in observer expertise, effort, and mobility generate substantial heterogeneity in data contribution15. Together, these biases can fundamentally distort estimates of species distributions and community composition if not explicitly addressed14.

Although citizen science bias is widely recognized, existing studies primarily focus on accessibility correlates like proximity to roads, implicitly treating occurrence records as independent observations16,17. This approach insufficiently integrates the highly uneven distribution of sampling effort among users into spatial modeling frameworks. Furthermore, the extent to which observed spatial patterns in megadiverse tropical systems reflect true ecological processes versus human sampling behavior remains poorly resolved11,18.

Malaysia provides an ideal system for addressing these questions. As a tropical biodiversity hotspot, it supports high species richness across complex environmental gradients, including variations in moisture availability that drive forest ecology19. Crucially, the country comprises two distinct biogeographic regions. Peninsular Malaysia features dense road networks and urbanization, whereas Malaysian Borneo contains extensive forest cover, rugged terrain, and relies heavily on riverine transport due to limited road access. This contrast offers an opportunity to examine how fundamentally different modes of transport infrastructure influences citizen science observations20,21.

Here, we integrate observer-level inequality, spatial accessibility, and effort-corrected richness estimation within a unified analytical framework. We hypothesize that apparent spatial gaps in biodiversity data within remote or topographically complex regions are primarily driven by sampling effort limitations rather than underlying ecological constraints. By explicitly incorporating non-linear effort corrections and accounting for spatial autocorrelation, we aim to disentangle patterns arising from human behavior from those reflecting true environmental gradients. Ultimately, the motivation of this study is to identify which of these under-sampled regions represent the most pressing spatial data shortfalls for future surveys.

Analyzing a dataset of research-grade community observations across Malaysia, our specific objectives are to: (1) quantify inequality in observer contributions and its effect on spatial sampling patterns; (2) estimate inventory completeness to map substantial data deficiencies; (3) evaluate representation across major taxonomic groups; (4) test whether relationships between topography and species richness persist after controlling for sampling effort using Generalized Additive Models (GAM); and (5) organize these findings to provide actionable insights for prioritizing future biodiversity monitoring.

Materials and methods

Spatial framework, and data acquisition

The study was conducted across the terrestrial extent of Malaysia, a tropical country characterized by two distinct biogeographic regions comprising Peninsular Malaysia and East Malaysia (Malaysian Borneo). Peninsular Malaysia is characterized by dense road networks and extensive urbanization, whereas Malaysian Borneo is defined by highly rugged terrain, extensive primary forest cover, and a heavy reliance on riverine transport. These regions differ substantially in terms of infrastructure, topography, and accessibility, providing a suitable system to examine how landscape structure influences spatial sampling patterns.

To standardize spatial analyses and mitigate, though not completely eliminate, the influence of the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem (MAUP), we implemented a grid-based framework22. A continuous hexagonal grid was generated across the national extent using a cylindrical equal-area projection (EPSG:6933). Hexagonal cells were selected to ensure uniform adjacency and minimize directional bias23,24. The grid resolution was set to 20 km (cell area ≈ 346 km²), representing a balance between spatial detail and sampling density25. While we acknowledge that applying a uniform scale aggregates diverse taxonomic groups with varying dispersal capacities, this resolution serves as a stable macroecological baseline; it is sufficiently coarse to mitigate the spatial uncertainty and commission errors inherent in unstructured data, yet fine enough to prevent the artificial inflation of zero-counts across localized grids26.

Biodiversity occurrence data were obtained from the iNaturalist platform for the period 2015–2025. Only “Research Grade” observations were retained. This strict criterion inherently restricts the dataset to occurrences where community consensus identifies the organism to the species level, and automatically resolves taxonomic synonyms against the platform’s standardized backbone. Spatial filtering was applied to maintain positional accuracy suitable for grid-level analyses. Records lacking geographic coordinates or with reported positional uncertainty exceeding 2,000 m were excluded. The final dataset comprised 336,042 occurrence records. All spatial operations were conducted in R using the sf package27.

Observer dynamics and taxonomic bias

To quantify inequality in observer contributions, we calculated the Gini coefficient based on the cumulative number of observations across all unique observers ranked by their total contribution volume, rather than cumulatively over time. Crucially, these metrics reflect the current digital state and user dynamics of the citizen science platform, rather than an absolute representation of baseline biological reality. The Gini coefficient ranges from 0 (equal contribution) to 1 (maximum inequality) and was computed using standard formulations implemented in R28,29.

To account for heterogeneity in observer behavior, users were classified into three contribution tiers based on total observation counts: Transient (1–5 observations), Regular (6–100 observations), and Power Users (> 100 observations). These categories were used to evaluate how observer effort influences spatial sampling patterns.

In parallel to spatial and behavioral metrics, we quantified taxonomic bias for the platform as a whole (rather than at the individual observer level) to determine the extent to which community science effort deviates from actual species representation. Observations were aggregated into major taxonomic clades (e.g., Aves, Insecta, Plantae). Spatial modeling of accessibility and observer behavior

Environmental and anthropogenic predictors were derived for each 20 km grid cell to quantify factors associated with spatial variation in sampling intensity. Topographic variables were obtained from a 30 arc-second (~ 1 km resolution) Digital Elevation Model. Mean elevation and Terrain Ruggedness Index (TRI;30 was calculated at its native 1 km resolution. Subsequently, we extracted the spatial mean of both elevation and TRI pixels falling within each 20 km hexagonal grid cell.

Accessibility was quantified using Euclidean distance from each grid cell centroid to (i) the nearest major road and (ii) the nearest urban center, defined using the global populated places dataset from Natural Earth. While calculating true path distance or setting distances to zero for road-intersecting cells provides higher local precision, using grid-based distance proxies remains a standard approach for capturing macro-scale accessibility gradients, even across coarse 20 km resolutions31,32. All continuous predictors were standardized (mean = 0, standard deviation = 1) prior to modeling to facilitate model convergence and comparison of effect sizes.

To separate the human observation process from the underlying ecology, we first modeled human sampling behavior. To analyze variation in observation density, we fitted a Zero-Inflated Negative Binomial Generalized Linear Mixed Model (GLMM) using the glmmTMB package in R33,34. The response variable was the total observation count aggregated for each observer class within a given grid cell. Observer class (Transient, Regular, or Power User) was therefore explicitly structured as a categorical predictor variable to evaluate behavioral differences. The fixed-effects structure included a three-way interaction between standardized distance to roads, biogeographic region (Peninsular Malaysia vs. Borneo), and observer class, as well as a two-way interaction between distance to cities and region. Standardized elevation and TRI were included as additive main effects.

Spatial autocorrelation was accounted for by incorporating an exponential spatial covariance structure based on the geographic coordinates of grid cell centroids35. To accommodate excess zeros in the data, a zero-inflation component with a constant intercept (~ 1) was specified, allowing the probability of structural zeros to be modeled independently of the count process.

Model fit, assumptions, and residual spatial autocorrelation were evaluated simultaneously using simulation-based residual diagnostics implemented in the DHARMa package36. To facilitate interpretation of interaction effects, estimated marginal means were computed from the fitted model.

Effort-corrected robustness and richness estimation

While the GLMM quantified human spatial bias, to evaluate the robustness of observed ecological patterns, we fitted a Generalized Additive Model (GAM) with a negative binomial error distribution37,38. This secondary model was specifically designed to test whether topographic variables independently drive observed species richness after mathematically controlling for the non-linear accumulation of human sampling effort. Sampling effort was quantified as the number of unique user-day combinations per grid cell and was log-transformed to stabilize variance.

The response variable was species richness per grid cell, calculated as the number of unique taxa. The model included penalized regression splines to estimate non-linear effects of sampling effort, elevation, and TRI. Sampling effort was log-transformed not only to stabilize variance but to account for the non-linear, asymptotic accumulation of species typical of ecological rarefaction. Distance to the nearest road was included as a parametric linear term with an interaction by region.

To account for spatial structure, a two-dimensional thin-plate regression spline was fitted using the geographic coordinates of grid cell centroids. The model was estimated using Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) to optimize smoothing parameters.

Model complexity was evaluated using the effective degrees of freedom (EDF) of each smooth term. Model performance was assessed using the proportion of deviance explained and adjusted 2.

Incidence-based richness estimation and inventory completeness

To quantify spatial variation in sampling completeness, we estimated species richness within each grid cell using an incidence-based approach39,40. Because raw occurrence counts are strongly influenced by sampling effort, we defined discrete sampling units to construct presence–absence incidence data41,42. A sampling event was defined as a unique combination of observer and date. This definition converts occurrence records into incidence frequencies and reduces the influence of repeated observations of the same species by a single observer within a given day.

Expected species richness was estimated using the Chao2 estimator, which is suitable for incidence-based data and performs well in communities with many infrequently detected species. This approach aligns with the analytical framework of the iNEXT package43. Importantly, Chao estimators are mathematically sensitive to the presence of singletons, thus, cells containing high numbers of uniques will project a steep expected richness. Results

Observer inequality and data attrition

The initial data retrieval yielded 510,637 research-grade occurrences across Malaysia. Following spatial data cleaning, the analytical dataset was finalized at 336,042 observations (Fig. 1). Analysis of the observer metadata identified a high degree of inequality in data contribution, quantified by a Gini coefficient of 0.854 (Fig. 2A). Stratification of the contributor pool demonstrated that “Power Users” (> 100 observations) accounted for the majority of the dataset, contributing 226,468 records. “Regular” observers (6–100 observations) contributed 91,533 records, while “Transient” observers (1–5 observations) accounted for the remaining 18,041 records (Fig. 2B).

Taxonomic disproportionality

Evaluation of taxonomic representation revealed severe disproportionality between raw observation volume and expected species richness. Plotting the actual sampling effort against the expected representation on a logarithmic scale (Fig. 3) demonstrated that community science data deviates substantially from a 1:1 proportionality ratio.

Vertebrate clades, specifically Aves and Reptilia, positioned substantially above the 1:1 reference line, exhibiting high positive Log2 bias ratios. This indicates these groups constitute a fraction of the dataset’s total volume that is disproportionately large relative to their proportional species richness in the region. Mammalia and Amphibia also clustered above the reference line, though with lower overall observation volumes. In contrast, foundational and hyper-diverse clades, notably Insecta, Plantae, and Fungi, plotted below the 1:1 line. While Insecta and Plantae accounted for high absolute observation counts, their sampling effort remains disproportionately low relative to their high taxonomic diversity. Micro-organisms (Protozoa, Chromista) recorded the most severe negative bias ratios in the dataset.

Interacting anthropogenic drivers of spatial bias

The zero-inflated negative binomial spatial GLMM successfully converged, with the zero-inflation parameter indicating a highly significant baseline probability of structural zeros across the grid (β = -4.46, p < 0.001). Biologically, this implies that large tracts of the landscape, regardless of their underlying environmental suitability, remain fundamentally unsampled, acting as complete informational voids due to an absolute lack of human visitation. Assessment of the simulated residuals confirmed adequate model fit, with no significant deviation, overdispersion, or outlier inflation detected (Fig. S1).

Analysis of the conditional model revealed that anthropogenic infrastructure significantly dictates observation density, though these effects are highly modulated by geographic region and observer classification (Table S1). Proximity to urban centers demonstrated a strong, universal positive effect on sampling density; as distance to cities increased, expected observations significantly decreased β = -1.32, p < 0.001). Topographic variables exhibited mixed main effects; standardized elevation was not a significant predictor of observation counts (β = 0.13, p = 0.398), while terrain ruggedness (TRI) showed a positive association (β = 0.37, p = 0.009).

A highly significant interaction was detected between road distance and biogeographic region. In Malaysian Borneo (the baseline factor), standardized distance to roads did not significantly influence observation counts (β = 0.02, p = 0.929). Conversely, in Peninsular Malaysia, increasing distance from road networks resulted in a severe and significant reduction in data collection (β = -2.00, p < 0.001).

Crucially, the model identified a significant three-way interaction between distance to roads, region, and observer class. In Peninsular Malaysia, Power Users exhibited a significantly attenuated negative response to increasing road distance compared to “Transient” observers (β = 0.99, p = 0.002). Bias-corrected estimated marginal means (Fig. 4) visually confirm this divergence: the predicted observation counts for Transient and Regular users approach zero rapidly as distance from roads increases, whereas the predicted counts for Power Users remain elevated at greater distances into the roadless interior. Furthermore, while the interaction was not statistically significant in Malaysian Borneo, visual inspection of the marginal effects (Fig. 4) indicates that Power User observations also exhibit a decreasing trend in this region as distance from roads increases, albeit less precipitously than casual observers.

Effort-corrected species richness

To test whether spatial patterns were artifacts of uneven sampling, the effort-corrected Generalized Additive Model (GAM) accounted for 97.1% of the total deviance (adjusted R2 = 0.874) in observed species richness (n = 955). The two-dimensional thin-plate spatial spline successfully captured residual geographic autocorrelation across the national extent (edf = 19.80, p < 0.001). Crucially, diagnostic checks confirmed that spatial concurvity between the topographic variables and the spatial spline was sufficiently low to permit reliable inference.

Sampling effort (log-transformed user-days) exhibited a highly significant non-linear relationship with observed species richness (edf = 9.91, p < 0.001). demonstrating a steep initial accumulation of species that plateaus at higher effort thresholds (Fig. S2). In contrast to the GLMM predicting human behavior, the parametric effects of standardized distance to roads (β = 0.10, p = 0.086) and its interaction with region (β = -0.12, p = 0.248) were not statistically significant predictors of species richness in the GAM.

Notably, when explicitly controlling for spatial clustering and sampling effort, the non-linear smooth terms for both topographic variables, standardized elevation (edf. = 1.01, p = 0.192) and terrain ruggedness (edf = 2.90, p = 0.106), were not statistically significant. This indicates that topographic gradients do not exert an independent negative effect on actual species richness; rather, the perceived absences in rugged and high-elevation terrains are artifacts of the severe drop in human sampling effort in those regions.

Spatial data shortfalls and inventory completeness

Projecting the curated observations onto the analytical framework generated 955 terrestrial cells at the 20 km resolution. Utilizing this 20 km grid, calculation of the Chao2 expected species richness demonstrated uneven spatial coverage across the national extent (Fig. 5A). Among the 955 sampled terrestrial cells, the median inventory completeness was calculated at 33.3% (mean = 39.2%). The interquartile range for inventory completeness spanned from 19.6% to 50.0%, with a minimum of 3.2% and a maximum of 100.0%. Grid cells classified as informational dark spots, having an inventory completeness of less than 20%, were prevalent, particularly distributed across the interior landscape of Malaysian Borneo compared to coastal regions (Fig. 5B).

Discussion

The human geography of biodiversity data

The extreme inequality in observer contribution documented in this study indicates that open-access biodiversity data in Malaysia is not generated by a diffuse, decentralized crowd. Rather, sampling effort is highly asymmetrical, overwhelmingly driven by a dedicated minority of Power Users, who produce the vast majority of research-grade records. This concentration of effort fundamentally dictates the spatial footprint of the resulting dataset. Our models confirm that this footprint is strongly anchored to human infrastructure; proximity to urban centers universally increased expected observation density across all regions and observer classes. This urban aggregation likely reflects the convenience of opportunistic sampling near high population densities and established academic institutions, yielding a dataset that maps human settlement patterns as strongly as it maps ecological communities47,48.

The influence of road networks further stratifies this spatial bias, though this effect is highly contingent on both biogeography and observer behavior. In Peninsular Malaysia, moving away from road infrastructure results in a steep overall decline in data collection. However, the three-way interaction in our model reveals a significant behavioral divergence: Transient observers are largely restricted to the immediate vicinity of roads, whereas Power Users are significantly less deterred by the lack of infrastructure, acting as the primary source of biological data in the roadless interior. Conversely, in Malaysian Borneo, terrestrial distance to roads did not exhibit the same restrictive effect. This regional contrast likely reflects Borneo’s distinct infrastructural reliance on riverine transport and the profound inaccessibility of its primary forests, rendering standard road buffers less predictive of human movement. This biogeographical contrast represents a critical dynamic in tropical macroecology: spatial data gaps cannot be treated uniformly across large scales. Instead, they are the complex product of distinct regional infrastructure interacting with the specific mobility thresholds of different observer tiers49.

Topography as an accessibility constraint

A prevailing assumption in broad-scale macroecology is that species richness attenuates along steep elevational gradients and highly rugged terrain50,51. However, our effort-corrected Generalized Additive Model demonstrates that for unstructured community science, this perceived decline is an artifact of sampling constraints. When explicitly controlling for the non-linear accumulation of human effort, neither elevation nor topographic complexity remained statistically significant predictors of richness. This indicates that topography primarily restricts human access, although we acknowledge a potential confounding effect: because human effort is inherently constrained by topography, the effort variable likely absorbs statistical variation that might also reflect true ecological declines along these gradients.

Consequently, the vast spatial data shortfalls identified across the mountainous interior of Borneo and the Titiwangsa Range are severe informational deficits. Rather than interpreting these rugged regions as ecologically empty, they should be viewed as the ‘low-hanging fruit’ for future targeted biodiversity surveys. As established in broader distribution modeling literature, relying on uncorrected occurrence data to generate species distribution models risks formalizing human accessibility limits as false ecological boundaries52,53.

The functional consequences of taxonomic bias

Consistent with global patterns of charismatic taxonomic bias13,54,55, our analysis shows that community science in Malaysia functions disproportionately as an avian and reptilian monitoring network. While the high volume of vertebrate data is valuable for targeted conservation initiatives, it structurally overshadows hyper-diverse clades that are foundational to ecosystem stability. The under-representation of groups such as Insecta, Plantae, and Fungi indicates that opportunistic sampling routinely ignores the taxa primarily responsible for critical ecosystem services, including pollination, decomposition, and carbon sequestration.

This taxonomic blind spot significantly limits the utility of unstructured, open-access platforms as standalone proxies for total ecosystem health56,57. If state conservation strategies rely exclusively on the aggregated volume of unstructured data, funding and policy attention will inevitably skew toward vertebrate conservation. Recognizing this functional bias is essential for evaluating the limits of community-contributed data and ensuring comprehensive biodiversity assessments.

Actionable insights for conservation policy

To meet the targets of the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (GBF), national conservation policies must transition from passively accumulating opportunistic data to more actively guiding community science efforts58,59. The grid cells exhibiting < 20% inventory completeness, highlight immediate priorities for resource reallocation. For the future of citizen science, platform administrators and conservation NGOs should actively guide community efforts toward these inaccessible high-elevation zones, rather than relying solely on state-funded surveys. Furthermore, park managers could help mitigate spatial aggregation by implementing targeted gamification or localized “BioBlitz” events60. Such initiatives could be designed to incentivize the highly mobile “Power User” demographic to explore under-sampled geographic coordinates, complementing their ongoing surveys in urban or peri-urban reserves61,62.

Simultaneously, policy frameworks must address the profound functional gaps caused by taxonomic bias63. Because unstructured platforms consistently skew toward charismatic and conspicuous vertebrates, these platforms cannot be solely relied upon to assess total ecosystem health55,64,65. To ensure that the taxa responsible for foundational ecosystem services are not neglected in national conservation planning, the citizen science community must strategically cultivate expertise in underrepresented clades. Specifically, community-led botanical, entomological, and mycological expeditions should be prioritized by platform organizers to counterbalance the disproportionate volume of citizen-driven avian and reptilian data.

Limitations and future directions

Although our spatial framework accounts for non-linear sampling effort, several methodological limitations warrant consideration. First, our accessibility models relied exclusively on terrestrial road networks. We did not incorporate riverine transport infrastructure, which is a primary mode of access in the interior of Malaysian Borneo and likely explains some of the residual spatial patterns observed in that region. Second, our analysis focused explicitly on spatial, behavioral, and taxonomic biases, thereby holding temporal dynamics constant. Future studies should partition sampling data by wet and dry seasons to determine if wilderness penetration by Power Users is restricted to dry seasons. Third, while the Chao2 estimator is highly effective for incidence-based data with many rare taxa, it fundamentally assumes a closed population structure. Applying such estimators across 20 km grids is useful for assessing comparative completeness, but the Chao2 estimates should be treated as statistical baselines rather than exact censuses.

Moving forward, the field of biodiversity informatics must evolve beyond merely quantifying citizen science bias toward actively calibrating it66,67. To explicitly resolve the anthropogenic artifacts identified in this study, specifically the extreme aggregation of data near human infrastructure, future research should prioritize Integrated Species Distribution Models (ISDMs). By mathematically fusing massive volumes of spatially biased data evaluated here with highly controlled datasets from standardized professional surveys, ISDMs can leverage the geographic breadth of platforms like iNaturalist while anchoring the models against the illusion of topographic emptiness68.

Conclusion

As the global conservation community increasingly relies on open-access informatics to monitor accelerating biodiversity loss, recognizing the human architecture embedded within this data is paramount. This study demonstrates that unstructured occurrence records in megadiverse tropical landscapes do not map biodiversity directly; rather, they map the intersection of human infrastructure, severe observer inequality, and taxonomic preference. By explicitly correcting for non-linear sampling effort and spatial clustering, we demonstrate that the apparent absence of species in rugged, high-elevation terrains is primarily a human accessibility artifact rather than an ecological reality. Furthermore, by contrasting the distinct infrastructural reliance of Peninsular Malaysia and Malaysian Borneo, we highlight that spatial data shortfalls are fundamentally shaped by regional geography. Ultimately, to support global conservation policy, researchers and state agencies must transition from passively accepting these accessibility artifacts to actively targeting these remote, under-sampled regions in future structured surveys.
