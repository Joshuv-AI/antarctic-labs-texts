Depth-augmented diffusion policy with pseudo-depth for robust robotic manipulation
Authors: Lesia Hentosh, Yaroslav Hladun, Ivan Tsmots, Oleh Bisikalo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiffusion Policy has emerged as a powerful approach for imitation learning in robotic manipulation. However, policies trained solely on RGB observations often fail to capture the spatial structure of a scene, which limits performance on tasks requiring precise distance-aware motions, such as approaching an object along the camera axis. A natural remedy is to provide depth as an additional input, but two practical challenges remain: designing a conditioning mechanism that encourages the policy to use depth effectively and obtaining reliable depth when hardware measurements are unavailable or noisy. This paper presents Depth-Augmented Diffusion Policy, a framework that addresses both issues. The method employs separate visual encoders for RGB images and depth maps and fuses their representations through stochastic feature-level addition with modality dropout, reducing modality dominance and promoting the use of geometric cues during action generation. In addition, the results demonstrate that pseudo-depth generated by the pre-trained Depth Anything V2 estimator can replace sensor depth, removing the dependence on an RGB-D camera. The proposed approach is evaluated on a real-world robotic manipulation task performed using the Somatic Jinx robot, in which the robot must close a toilet-seat lid. The proposed policy achieves a 95% success rate with sensor depth and 90% with pseudo-depth, compared with 50% for the RGB-only baseline and 75% for 3D Diffusion Policy. These results show that explicit depth conditioning substantially improves performance on this distance-sensitive manipulation task and that pseudo-depth can serve as an effective alternative to hardware depth in this setting. Additional evaluation is conducted on the LIBERO simulation benchmark. Pseudo-depth augmentation yields a modest but consistent improvement across three task suites. The results indicate that the benefit extends beyond the single real-world task but remains most pronounced in distance-sensitive manipulation. Introduction

Imitation learning from expert demonstrations has become a major paradigm for teaching robots complex manipulation skills, particularly in settings where manual reward engineering is difficult and task execution requires stable visuomotor coordination. Recent surveys emphasize that demonstration-based learning remains one of the most practical approaches for robotic manipulation, especially when transferring human expertise into real-world control policies1–3.

Among recent approaches, Diffusion Policy4 has emerged as a particularly effective framework, leveraging denoising diffusion models to represent multimodal action distributions and generate smooth long-horizon action trajectories. In its journal version, the method was shown to outperform prior baselines across multiple robotic manipulation benchmarks, highlighting diffusion-based visuomotor learning as a strong direction for imitation learning.

However, policies trained solely on RGB observations suffer from a fundamental limitation: they lack explicit access to the three-dimensional geometry of the scene. While RGB images provide rich appearance and texture cues, they do not directly encode metric distance, relative depth ordering, or surface geometry in a form that can be reliably exploited by the controller5. This deficiency becomes critical in tasks that require precise distance-aware motions, such as approaching an object along the camera optical axis or executing contact-rich interactions at a specific depth. The absence of explicit metric depth in RGB-only policies, and the resulting difficulty with distance-aware motions, is consistent with surveys of embodied robotic manipulation and of monocular depth estimation, which emphasize geometric perception as a prerequisite for reliable spatial reasoning2,6.

A natural remedy is to augment visual observations with depth information. Recent work on 3D Diffusion Policy (DP3)7 follows this direction by incorporating 3D visual representations into diffusion-based imitation learning and demonstrates strong performance in simulated and real-world tasks. However, such approaches increase the complexity of the perception pipeline and typically depend on depth sensing or explicit 3D reconstruction at deployment time. An alternative is to concatenate RGB and depth maps directly, yet naïve fusion does not necessarily ensure that the policy will actually exploit the depth modality during learning.

In parallel, monocular depth estimation has advanced rapidly, and recent foundation-style models have shown that high-quality pseudo-depth can be inferred from RGB images alone. In particular, Depth Anything V28 reports finer and more robust depth predictions than its earlier version and offers a practical basis for replacing hardware depth in downstream vision tasks. This creates an important opportunity for robotic learning: if pseudo-depth can provide sufficiently stable geometric cues, depth-aware manipulation policies may be deployed without requiring RGB-D sensors.

This paper presents Depth-Augmented Diffusion Policy, a framework that addresses two key challenges in integrating depth into diffusion-based imitation learning. First, a dual-encoder architecture with stochastic modality fusion is introduced, where separate Vision Transformers process RGB and depth independently and their latent representations are combined through randomized element-wise addition with modality dropout during training. This design is intended to reduce modality dominance and encourage systematic use of geometric information during action generation. Second, pseudo-depth maps generated by Depth Anything V28 are investigated as an alternative to hardware depth sensing.

The proposed approach is evaluated on a real-world toilet-seat-closing task performed using the Somatic Jinx robot and demonstrates substantial improvements over the baselines. Beyond its technical role as a distance-sensitive manipulation benchmark, the toilet-seat-closing task is also representative of sanitation-related manipulation in institutional environments. Such tasks are directly relevant to healthcare facility management, hospital service robotics, and hygiene automation, where robotic systems must be integrated into existing organizational routines and human workflows. Prior studies have examined innovative work behavior, job crafting, leadership, job satisfaction, and dynamic capabilities in hospital settings9–11. Their findings indicate that successful technology adoption depends not only on technical reliability but also on the integration of new tools into staff roles, work processes, and institutional practices.

The RGB-only Diffusion Policy baseline achieves only a 50% success rate, whereas DP37 reaches 75%. In contrast, the proposed depth-augmented policy achieves 95% with sensor depth and 90% with pseudo-depth, representing a 45 and 40% point improvement over the RGB-only baseline, respectively.

State-of-the-art

This section reviews the scientific approaches that form the theoretical and methodological foundation of the proposed study. First, it considers imitation learning as one of the key directions for enabling robots to acquire complex manipulation skills from expert demonstrations. Special attention is given to monocular depth estimation, as this line of research creates the basis for generating pseudo-depth without the use of specialized RGB-D sensors. The section also examines diffusion models in control and decision-making tasks, where they have demonstrated high effectiveness in modeling complex multimodal action distributions. Finally, the use of depth information in robotic manipulation is analyzed, since it provides geometric cues that are critical for the accurate execution of spatially sensitive motions. Despite substantial progress in diffusion-based policy learning, an unresolved need remains for a simple and effective depth-integration strategy. Such a strategy should avoid unnecessary perception-pipeline complexity and reduce the risk that the depth modality is ignored during training.

Imitation learning and behavior cloning

Imitation learning enables robots to acquire manipulation skills by observing expert demonstrations without explicitly designing a reward function1–3. This approach is particularly important for robotic manipulation, where formalizing the objective in the form of a reward function is often difficult, and task success depends strongly on the consistency between perception and action. Behavior cloning (BC)12, as the simplest form of imitation learning, formulates the problem as supervised learning over state–action pairs. Despite its conceptual simplicity, BC is sensitive to distribution shift between training and deployment, which leads to the accumulation of errors during multi-step execution13. The DAgger method13 partially addresses this problem through iterative dataset aggregation. However, it requires repeated access to the expert, which reduces its practical applicability in real robotic systems.

In recent years, diffusion-based approaches have attracted particular attention due to their high effectiveness in policy learning. In particular, Diffusion Policy4 models the action distribution as a denoising process and naturally captures the multimodality of control actions, thereby enabling the generation of smooth and temporally consistent control sequences. The approach proposed in this paper is built directly on this architectural idea. Unlike the standard Diffusion Policy, the proposed approach extends observation encoding through the explicit introduction of the depth modality, with the aim of improving the accuracy of distance-sensitive motions in robotic manipulation tasks.

Monocular depth estimation

Monocular depth estimation aims to construct dense depth maps from a single RGB image. Early methods in this area were mostly trained on individual domain-specific datasets, which limited their generalization ability14. Subsequent progress was associated with the transition to training on mixed datasets and to zero-shot transfer across different domains. In this direction, MiDaS15,16 became one of the key approaches demonstrating the feasibility of robust monocular relative depth estimation on heterogeneous data, whereas ZoeDepth17 extended this idea toward the integration of relative and metric depth.

Further substantial progress was achieved in the works Depth Anything18 and Depth Anything V28. The former showed that large-scale use of unlabeled and weakly labeled data makes it possible to build a foundation-style model for robust depth estimation. In Version V2, the authors improved fine-grained detail and estimation robustness by training a strong teacher model on synthetic data and subsequently distilling knowledge to real images through pseudo-labeling. An alternative line of research is represented by Marigold19, in which a diffusion-based image generator was adapted for monocular depth estimation. Although this approach provides high detail, its computational complexity is higher, which limits its suitability for real-time robotic applications. Considering the balance between accuracy, efficiency, and generalization capability, the proposed framework employs Depth Anything V2 for pseudo-depth generation. More recent estimators continue to improve this trade-off, including Metric3D for zero-shot metric depth20, ZoeDepth17 for combining relative and metric depth, and Depth Anything 3 for spatially consistent geometry recovery from an arbitrary number of views21. Related stereo, dense-correspondence, and occupancy-based approaches include DCPI-Depth22, TiCoSS23, D3Stereo24, and ViPOcc25.

Diffusion models in decision-making tasks

Beyond image generation, diffusion models have been increasingly applied to sequential decision-making, planning, and control tasks26. In Diffuser, trajectory optimization is formulated as a denoising process over state–action sequences, thereby extending diffusion modeling to the domain of behavior synthesis. This idea was further developed in Diffusion Policy4, where a 1D U-Net is conditioned on visual observations to generate sequences of control actions. Such a formulation has proven particularly effective for robotic manipulation, where actions are inherently multimodal and temporally coherent trajectory generation is critically important.

In DP37, this idea is further extended by replacing 2D visual observations with compact 3D representations constructed from point clouds. The reported results indicate that geometric information can indeed improve policy performance in tasks that require spatial reasoning. However, this approach requires the construction, filtering, and encoding of 3D representations, which increases the complexity of the perception pipeline. In contrast, the proposed approach introduces geometric information through depth maps within a simpler architecture that remains close to the standard Diffusion Policy framework4. This makes it possible to preserve the simplicity of a 2D-based observation pipeline while augmenting it with depth-aware encoding and stochastic multimodal fusion.

Depth in robotic manipulation

Depth information has long been regarded as an important source of features for robotic manipulation, since it provides access to scene geometry, spatial relationships between objects, and the distance to the target27,28. In traditional approaches, RGB-D sensors are used to construct point-cloud or voxel-based representations for grasp planning, object pose estimation, and local environment modeling tasks27. In learning-based approaches, depth is often added to RGB as an additional input channel28. However, such naïve modality integration is not always effective, as the model typically tends to rely on the modality with stronger pre-trained features, while the other is used only partially or is effectively ignored. In related robot-interaction tasks, RGB-D-based perception has already demonstrated practical value for localizing door handles and supporting real-time robotic operation in visually diverse environments29. More broadly, the importance of geometric sensing for autonomous robot operation has also been emphasized in prior work on LiDAR-based perception for mobile robots30. In related areas of multimodal learning, this problem has been addressed through the use of separate encoders, attention mechanisms, and various forms of feature-level conditioning31. Task-specific fusion in robot learning has increasingly relied on attention. For example, See, Hear, and Feel fuses vision, audio, and touch for manipulation32, whereas VIMA uses alternating cross-attention and self-attention over multimodal prompts33. Such mechanisms are expressive but introduce additional parameters and still assume that the policy can learn, from limited demonstrations, which modality to emphasize. In the proposed method, this issue is addressed through stochastic modality fusion with modality dropout. Conceptually, this approach is based on the idea of dropout regularization34, but transfers it from the level of individual neurons to the level of observation modalities. This makes it possible to reduce the risk of RGB-channel dominance, encourage the systematic use of depth features, and improve policy effectiveness in tasks where correct execution directly depends on the accuracy of geometric target localization. The section "Stochastic modality fusion" formalizes why this modality-level dropout is preferable to learned fusion in the low-data regime: it preserves the expected fused representation, guarantees gradient flow to the non-dominant encoder, and introduces no additional trainable fusion parameters.

Compared with prior depth-aware policies, the novelty of the proposed approach is threefold. First, unlike DP37, which constructs an explicit 3D point-cloud representation, the standard 2D Diffusion Policy pipeline is retained, and depth is introduced only as a second image-like modality, avoiding point-cloud construction and filtering at deployment. Second, in contrast to naïve RGB-D fusion by channel concatenation, the proposed stochastic modality fusion applies dropout at the level of whole modalities rather than individual neurons, which prevents the strongly pre-trained RGB stream from dominating and encourages each encoder to learn a self-sufficient representation. Third, the experimental results demonstrate that monocular pseudo-depth can replace hardware depth within this design at only a small cost in success rate.

Materials and methods

System overview

Depth-Augmented Diffusion Policy is proposed as an extension of the standard Diffusion Policy4 with explicit depth conditioning for distance-aware robotic manipulation. The pipeline consists of three modules. The pseudo-depth synthesis module generates dense depth maps from a single RGB camera using Depth Anything V28. The dual-encoder observation module contains separate Vision Transformers for RGB and depth and combines their representations through stochastic element-wise addition. The conditional 1D U-Net then generates action trajectories through iterative denoising using the fused visual features and the robot’s proprioceptive state as conditioning inputs. The complete architecture and information flow are illustrated in Fig. 1.

Component-level architecture of the proposed Depth-Augmented Diffusion Policy. Separate CLIP-initialized ViT-B/16 encoders process RGB and sensor or pseudo-depth observations. Their 768-dimensional features are combined through stochastic modality fusion and concatenated with the 6D end-effector state to condition a FiLM-modulated 1D U-Net, which generates an action trajectory through DDIM denoising for receding-horizon control.

During training, both modalities are retained with probability 0.50, whereas either RGB or depth is dropped with probability 0.25. The retained single modality is scaled by a factor of 2, which preserves the expected fused representation. At inference, both modalities are used with equal weights. The fused 768-dimensional feature is concatenated with the 6D end-effector state and used to condition the FiLM-modulated 1D U-Net. The network predicts a trajectory comprising 3D position and 6D rotation commands through -step DDIM denoising, after which the first actions are executed before replanning.

Pseudo-depth synthesis module

Standard Diffusion Policy operates on RGB observations, which provide rich appearance information but lack explicit geometric cues about the three-dimensional structure of the scene. Tasks that require precise distance-aware motions, such as approaching an object along the camera axis, are particularly challenging without depth information.

One solution is to use a depth sensor, such as an RGB-D camera. However, hardware depth measurements are not always available, can be noisy, and introduce sensor-specific artifacts. Instead, pseudo-depth maps are generated using Depth Anything V28, a state-of-the-art monocular depth estimation model. Given an RGB image , the model produces a dense depth prediction via frozen inference, with the estimator’s weights kept fixed during policy training. The resulting pseudo-depth map is visualized as a three-channel image using a colormap and resized to to match the input resolution of the vision encoder.

Depth Anything V2 produces relative, non-metric depth and does not recover absolute scale. Because the depth map is consumed only as a colormap-encoded conditioning image rather than as metric distance, relative depth ordering is sufficient for the distance-aware motions considered here. During the approach phase, the policy must determine how close the end-effector is to the lid relative to the remaining scene. This is an ordinal within-frame judgment. It is invariant to the global scale and shift that distinguish relative depth from metric depth. Relative depth cannot directly support objectives specified in absolute metric units. Examples include movement to a commanded stand-off distance expressed in meters and estimation of the physical size of an unseen object. Such tasks would require a metric estimator, such as ZoeDepth or Metric3D, or explicit metric calibration. A key advantage of pseudo-depth over raw sensor depth is its spatial smoothness and consistency. Training on large-scale diverse data enables the estimator to produce geometrically coherent depth maps. These maps are often less affected by sensor-specific noise, missing values, and measurement artifacts.

Dual-encoder observation module

Vision encoders

Two separate Vision Transformer (ViT-Base/16)35 encoders are employed. Both encoders are initialized from CLIP pre-trained weights (vit_base_patch16_clip_224.openai) via the TIMM library36,37. Each encoder takes a input and produces a 768-dimensional feature vector by extracting the CLS token from the final transformer layer. ImageNet normalization is applied to the inputs, and GroupNorm is used in place of BatchNorm to ensure stable training with small batch sizes. The RGB encoder processes the original color image I, while the depth encoder receives the pseudo-depth map (or sensor depth D in ablation experiments) as a three-channel colormap image produced by applying a colormap to the raw depth values, which converts the single-channel depth into an RGB-like representation compatible with the ViT patch embedding layer. ImageNet normalization is applied to both RGB and depth inputs. This choice is preferred over CLIP-specific normalization because it produced more stable training and higher task success for the colormap-encoded depth images. Both encoders are unfrozen during training but receive a reduced learning rate ( the base learning rate) for stable fine-tuning.

At each control step, the policy is conditioned on a single RGB frame, the corresponding depth map, and the current proprioceptive state.

Stochastic modality fusion

A straightforward way to combine two modality encoders, such as simple concatenation or fixed-weight addition, can cause the policy to rely mainly on one modality and disregard the other. To address this issue, a stochastic modality fusion scheme is introduced that encourages each encoder to learn representations that are useful on their own.

Three properties make this scheme preferable, under limited demonstrations, to learned fusion operators such as the cross-attention mechanisms discussed in the section "Depth in robotic manipulation". First, modality dropout trains a shared policy across the RGB-only, depth-only, and joint-modality configurations, thereby discouraging co-adapted solutions that operate correctly only when both streams are present. Second, it guarantees gradient flow to the non-dominant branch: in depth-only steps, the depth encoder is the sole source of visual conditioning and therefore receives informative gradients even when the RGB stream would suffice on its own. This is precisely the failure mode of fixed-weight fusion, in which a strongly pre-trained RGB encoder can minimize the training loss alone while the depth branch atrophies. Third, the fusion operator is parameter-free. Unlike cross-attention, it introduces no additional weights that must be estimated from the same 46 demonstrations. The price is reduced expressiveness because attention can learn state-dependent modality weighting. However, in the low-data imitation regime considered here, modality use is enforced by construction rather than expected to emerge from learned attention.

At inference, a single forward pass is performed with both modalities active and with the expected training-time fusion coefficients. Because the downstream denoising network is nonlinear, this deterministic prediction is not generally identical to a Monte Carlo average over sampled modality masks. It is therefore used as a computationally efficient approximation rather than as an exact ensemble average, avoiding the additional latency associated with multiple forward passes. A Monte Carlo variant may nevertheless be useful when an explicit uncertainty estimate for the fused action is required, for example, to reduce the contribution of the depth stream when pseudo-depth is unreliable. The fused feature is concatenated with the robot’s proprioceptive state (a 6D rotation vector representing the end-effector orientation relative to the episode start) and passed as global conditioning to the denoising network.

Inference

At inference time, the DDIM scheduler generates an action trajectory in denoising steps (compared to T=50 training steps), providing substantial acceleration. The policy outputs a trajectory of actions, of which only the first n=8 are executed before re-planning with a new observation (receding horizon control).

Pose representation

All observations and actions are expressed in a relative coordinate frame defined by the robot’s pose at the beginning of each episode. Specifically, positions are transformed via the SE(3) inverse of the initial end-effector pose, and rotations are represented using the 6D continuous parameterization38, which maps the first two columns of a rotation matrix to a 6-dimensional vector with Gram-Schmidt orthonormalization during decoding. This relative representation improves generalization across different initial configurations.

Data augmentation strategy

Training from a limited set of 46 real-world demonstrations poses a significant risk of overfitting. To improve generalization without collecting additional data, a multi-level augmentation strategy is employed that operates on both the observation images and the action-space targets. The augmentation strategy is based on mild and diverse perturbations. Rather than applying a single aggressive transform, multiple augmentations are composed at low individual activation probabilities, so that the policy encounters a wide variety of small visual variations during training.

Image-level augmentations

Image augmentations are implemented using the Albumentations library42 and applied independently to each frame through a temporal augmentation wrapper, so that frames within the same observation window are augmented independently rather than sharing the same random seed. Because the policy is conditioned on a single observation frame (The section "Vision encoders") rather than on a stacked sequence of frames, this per-frame augmentation does not introduce temporal inconsistencies at the policy input. A global gate probability determines whether augmentation is applied to a given frame, and, when activated, each transform is sampled according to its own application probability. Per-transform probabilities are deliberately kept low to maintain a diverse augmentation distribution. Each individual perturbation remains sufficiently mild to preserve the spatial correspondence between an image and its recorded end-effector pose. Stronger settings could disrupt this correspondence.

The RGB pipeline composes four groups of transforms: mild geometric perturbations, photometric color jitter, motion blur, and sensor-like noise. The depth pipeline reuses only the geometric group, ensuring that physically implausible photometric distortions are not applied to the geometric signal. The complete list of transforms, parameter ranges, and application probabilities is provided in Appendix A.

Experiments

Experimental setup

Task and dataset

The proposed approach is evaluated on a real-world robotic manipulation task involving toilet-seat closing. This task requires the robot to approach the seat, make contact, and push it down to the closed position, a motion that demands precise distance estimation along the camera axis and thus naturally exposes the limitations of depth-unaware policies. Because the end-effector can secure the lid only along a narrow contact region, the policy must reach the correct stand-off distance along the camera axis within a small tolerance, which makes accurate depth perception decisive for task success.

Robot demonstrations were collected by teleoperating the robot using a SpaceMouse interface, following the protocol of the original Diffusion Policy work4. The robot used is the Jinx platform developed by Somatic (https://getsomatic.com/), equipped with a single Intel RealSense D435 RGB-D camera. The dataset comprises 46 demonstration episodes, all of which are used for training without a held-out validation split. A separate validation set is not employed because frame-level or episode-level validation loss is an imperfect proxy for closed-loop task performance in diffusion-based imitation learning. Evaluation rollouts were not used for checkpoint selection. All reported results were obtained using the fixed final checkpoint after 60,000 training steps. The absence of a held-out validation split remains a limitation because the final checkpoint may not correspond to the model with the best generalization performance4,7. The corpus of 46 episodes creates a risk of overfitting. Three factors partially mitigate this risk. These are the multi-level augmentation strategy described in the section "Data augmentation strategy", the relative pose representation introduced in the section "Pose representation", and evaluation on new physical executions of the task rather than on training data, as described in the section "Evaluation protocol". The RealSense D435 provides both RGB images and hardware depth maps, enabling a direct comparison between sensor depth and pseudo-depth generated by Depth Anything V28.

Evaluation protocol

Each policy variant is evaluated over 40 independent rollouts on the physical robot. The success rate (%) is used as the primary metric. A trial is considered successful if the toilet-seat lid is fully closed at the end of the episode.

Baselines

Four experimental configurations are evaluated. The RGB-only baseline uses the standard Diffusion Policy with a single CLIP visual encoder based on ViT-Base with a 16 by 16 patch size applied to RGB images, without any depth input. 3D Diffusion Policy is included as the 3D baseline, which conditions the diffusion model on point-cloud representations constructed from depth measurements rather than on 2D images. For this baseline, point clouds were generated from the same RealSense D435 depth images using the camera intrinsics. The point clouds were cropped to the workspace bounding box to remove background regions, including the floor. Farthest-point sampling was then used to retain 1,024 points. The resulting point clouds were encoded by the lightweight three-layer MLP with max pooling introduced in DP37. DP3 was trained on the same 46 demonstrations and uses the same action representation and control rate as the proposed method. To avoid weakening the baseline through mis-tuning, its hyperparameters were not re-optimized for the present setup. Instead, the original DP3 implementation was used with its default training configuration as released by the authors (AdamW, learning rate 1 × 10⁻⁴, batch size 128, 3,000 training epochs, and a DDIM scheduler with 100 training and 10 inference steps). The comparison therefore reflects DP3 under its recommended settings rather than under a schedule matched to the proposed method. The depth-augmented model is tested in two variants. One uses sensor depth maps acquired from an Intel RealSense D435 sensor as input to the depth encoder, and the other replaces the hardware depth with pseudo-depth maps generated by Depth Anything V28 while keeping the remaining architecture unchanged.

Implementation details

All models are trained on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU. The complete training configuration—optimizer and learning-rate schedule, batch size, numerical precision, checkpointing, and action-horizon settings—is given in Appendix B.

Simulation benchmark

To assess whether the benefit of depth augmentation extends beyond the single real-world task, additional evaluation is conducted on LIBERO, a standard simulation benchmark for language-conditioned robotic manipulation. Three task suites are used: LIBERO-Spatial, LIBERO-Object, and LIBERO-Goal. They evaluate generalization over spatial arrangements, objects, and goals, respectively. Each suite comprises ten tasks with fifty human demonstrations per task. Two configurations are compared under the same training and evaluation protocol: an RGB-only Diffusion Policy with a single RGB encoder and a pseudo-depth-augmented variant with separate RGB and depth encoders. As in the real-world experiments, the pseudo-depth is produced by Depth Anything V2 from the agentview RGB image, so no simulator depth is used. In the LIBERO study, RGB and pseudo-depth features are combined by simple concatenation rather than by the stochastic fusion mechanism described in the section "Stochastic modality fusion". This comparison evaluates the combined effect of introducing pseudo-depth and a dedicated depth encoder, whereas the influence of the fusion operator is examined separately in the section "Ablation study of stochastic modality fusion". Following the standard LIBERO43 protocol, each configuration is evaluated over fifty rollouts per task and averaged across three random seeds (1,500 rollouts per suite), and the mean success rate for each suite is reported.

The RGB-only baseline achieves a success rate of only 50%, confirming that the standard Diffusion Policy struggles in tasks that require precise depth perception. 3D Diffusion Policy, denoted as DP3 in this study7, improves performance to 75%, which indicates that explicit geometric information is beneficial for the task. However, the proposed depth-augmented approach substantially outperforms the RGB-only baseline and performs on par with or above DP3. The variant using sensor depth achieves a success rate of 95%, corresponding to an improvement of 45% points over the RGB-only baseline and 20% points over DP3. The pseudo-depth variant reaches 90%, which corresponds to an improvement of 40% points over the RGB-only baseline and 15% points over DP3. Wilson 95% confidence intervals were calculated over the 40 trials per method. The intervals are 35.2–64.8% for RGB-only Diffusion Policy, 59.8–85.8% for DP3, 83.5–98.6% for the proposed sensor-depth variant, and 76.9–96.0% for the proposed pseudo-depth variant. Two-sided Fisher exact tests show that both depth-augmented variants significantly outperform the RGB-only baseline (p < 0.001). The 5-percentage-point difference between sensor depth and pseudo-depth is not statistically significant (p = 0.68). This result supports the use of pseudo-depth as a practical alternative to hardware depth in the considered task. With only 40 trials per method, the comparison with DP3 is borderline significant for sensor depth (p ≈ 0.03) and not significant for pseudo-depth (p ≈ 0.14). Accordingly, the proposed method is described as performing on par with or exceeding DP3 rather than as a definitive improvement.

Several observations follow from these results. First, the large gap between the RGB-only baseline and the depth-augmented variants supports the conclusion that explicit depth conditioning is substantially beneficial for the considered distance-sensitive manipulation task. Second, the proposed method matches or exceeds DP3 despite relying on a simpler 2D representation rather than 3D point clouds. The observed performance may reflect two factors. The first is the stochastic modality fusion scheme described in the section "Stochastic modality fusion", which encourages robust representation learning from both modalities. The second is the strong visual representation provided by the CLIP-pretrained ViT encoders. However, the encoders used in the proposed method benefit from CLIP pretraining, whereas the DP3 point-cloud encoder does not. The comparison is therefore partly confounded by the pretraining source. A controlled ablation using, for example, a depth encoder trained from scratch is left for future work.

Pseudo-depth and sensor depth

A particularly noteworthy finding is that the pseudo-depth variant achieved a success rate of 90%, whereas the sensor depth variant reached 95%, resulting in a gap of only 5% points. This result has important practical implications, as it shows that a policy can achieve strong depth-aware performance without relying on a physical depth sensor and can instead operate entirely on monocular depth estimation derived from a standard RGB camera.

This small performance gap may be explained by the inherent trade-off between the two depth sources. Sensor depth obtained from the RealSense D435 provides hardware-based depth measurements but may contain noise, missing values on reflective surfaces, and sensor-specific depth artifacts. In contrast, pseudo-depth generated by Depth Anything V28 produces smoother and more spatially consistent maps, although it does not preserve metric accuracy and may contain estimation errors. In practice, the smoothness and consistency of pseudo-depth may, at least partially, compensate for the lack of metric precision. This explanation remains a hypothesis because confirming the mechanism would require a controlled experiment that isolates map smoothness from other model components. The direct comparison presented below nevertheless demonstrates close structural agreement between pseudo-depth and sensor depth in the task-relevant region. In the considered setting, pseudo-depth policy succeeds in 90% of trials, indicating that the estimated depth provides useful geometric information in the task-relevant region. This interpretation is also consistent with the lower success rate of the RGB-only baseline, which lacks explicit depth conditioning and is therefore more susceptible to errors in approach-distance estimation.

To quantify the agreement between pseudo-depth and sensor depth directly, the two depth sources were compared on 2,920 frames sampled across all 46 episodes. Because Depth Anything V2 produces relative (affine-invariant) depth, each prediction was aligned to the RealSense reference by a per-frame least-squares scale-and-shift fit before computing error metrics. In the task region the two sources agree closely in structure: SSIM = 0.90, boundary F-score = 0.69, and threshold accuracy δ₁ = 0.93, with a mean absolute relative error of 0.20. The scale-invariant metrics, including SSIM and boundary F-score, remain essentially unchanged across alignment protocols. In contrast, absolute metrics such as δ₁ and RMSE depend strongly on the selected alignment. They are therefore reported only for the fixed alignment protocol because pseudo-depth is non-metric. The residual disagreement is concentrated at the boundaries of the seat, gripper, and grab bar. Sensor depth exhibits quantization banding and noise in these regions, whereas the monocular estimate remains smoother and more complete. Part of the observed discrepancy may therefore arise from limitations of the sensor-based reference. The released frames are 8-bit video-encoded, which limits precision and fills some raw-sensor dropouts. Therefore, emphasis is placed on structural agreement rather than pixel-exact error. The high structural fidelity in the task region is consistent with the near-equal success rates of the two depth variants (Section "Quantitative results") and supports pseudo-depth as a practical substitute for hardware depth in this setting.

To quantify this smoothness advantage directly, four noise statistics were computed for both depth sources over the same 2,920-frame sample and the same released 8-bit data, after per-frame robust normalization of each map to [0,1] (2nd–98th percentile). Sensor depth has a 2.9-fold higher mean spatial gradient magnitude than pseudo-depth, with values of 0.0170 and 0.00581, respectively. Its variance of the 3 × 3 Laplacian response is 17.5 times higher, with values of 0.0271 and 0.00155. Sensor depth also has a 20.6-fold higher fraction of speckle outliers, defined as pixels differing from the 11 × 11 local median by more than 0.1. The corresponding fractions are 3.01% and 0.146%. All three differences are observed in each of the 46 episodes. Temporal variation is measured as the mean absolute difference between consecutive normalized frames. It is comparable for the two sources, with values of 0.0140 for sensor depth and 0.0171 for pseudo-depth. The per-episode ratios range from 0.4 to 1.3. This result is consistent with the frame-independent operation of the monocular estimator and the quantization plateaus present in the sensor stream. These measurements quantitatively substantiate the smoothness advantage anticipated in the section "Pseudo-depth synthesis module" and illustrated qualitatively in Fig. 2. The benefit of pseudo-depth is concentrated in reducing spatial artifacts, including speckle, missing values, and banding, rather than in improving temporal stability.

The proposed stochastic fusion yielded the highest observed success rate. However, with 40 trials per variant, the pairwise differences relative to concatenation and fixed addition were not statistically significant. The ablation therefore indicates a favorable empirical trend rather than definitive evidence of superiority. Simple concatenation of the two 768-dimensional feature vectors produces a 1536-dimensional conditioning vector, which increases the parameter count of the FiLM layers and does not encourage the encoders to learn complementary representations. Fixed element-wise addition with constant weights avoids this issue but allows the RGB encoder, initialized from strong CLIP features, to dominate learning, effectively reducing the depth encoder to a negligible contribution. The proposed stochastic fusion scheme addresses both problems by randomly dropping one modality during training with equal probability for the RGB and depth branches, thereby forcing each encoder to learn self-sufficient representations. The factor of 2 applied to the surviving modality preserves the expected value of the fused vector (its expectation over the random coefficients equals the inference-time sum), giving consistent inputs in expectation to the FiLM conditioning layers. During inference, both modalities are combined with equal weights, yielding complementary features that jointly encode appearance and geometry.

The total inference time of 62.9 ms corresponds to a control frequency of approximately 15.9 Hz. The policy executes eight actions per inference cycle at a robot control rate of 15 Hz. These actions span 8/15 = 0.53 s. The resulting replanning frequency is approximately 1.9 Hz, which provides sufficient time for a complete forward pass. The toilet-seat-closing task is quasi-static, and an effective replanning rate near 2 Hz is adequate. Executing a short action chunk before replanning follows the standard receding-horizon procedure used in Diffusion Policy4. When using sensor depth from the RealSense D435, the Depth Anything V2 forward pass is skipped, reducing inference time to 42.9 ms. These results confirm that the framework operates comfortably within real-time constraints for the considered manipulation task.

Failure-mode analysis

Every failed trial reported in Table 1 was inspected to analyze the residual errors of the depth-augmented policy. Failures occurred in four of the 40 pseudo-depth trials and two of the 40 sensor-depth trials. The six failures fall into two categories, both of which occurred with both depth sources. The first and more frequent category was premature grasping, observed in three pseudo-depth trials and one sensor-depth trial. In these cases, the policy initiated the closing motion before the end-effector reached the correct contact depth, causing the gripper to actuate short of the lid. The second category was excessive contact force, observed once for each depth source. In these trials, the end-effector pressed too firmly while engaging the lid, and the robot’s torque-limit safeguard stopped the motion. This resulted in a safe termination rather than a collision or hardware damage.

These failures are attributed primarily to limitations in policy-level precision during the approach and contact phases rather than to depth-estimation errors. Both failure modes were also observed when sensor depth was used, indicating that they were not specific to pseudo-depth. None of the four pseudo-depth failures was associated with an artifact caused by the reflective porcelain surface. Although reflections degraded the RealSense depth maps and produced the missing regions and noise visible in Fig. 2, the corresponding pseudo-depth maps remained dense and spatially smooth. The observed failures were instead related to the timing of grasp initiation and the regulation of contact force, both of which depend mainly on the learned control policy. This interpretation is consistent with the absence of a statistically significant difference between the pseudo-depth and sensor-depth variants reported in the section "Quantitative results". The remaining performance gap therefore appears to arise from fine-grained control at the moment of contact rather than from the quality of the depth input. Improving contact-phase control through force or tactile feedback and additional contact-rich training is consequently identified as a promising direction for future work. The task also provides evidence under a reflective-surface condition because the porcelain lid represents a challenging surface for depth sensing. No failure was directly associated with reflection-induced depth artifacts. Transparent objects, which are harder still for both sensor and monocular depth, do not appear in this task and remain untested (Section "Conclusions").

The improvement is modest but consistent across all three suites, indicating that the benefit of explicit depth conditioning observed in the real-world experiment also extends to a standard simulation benchmark and to tasks beyond the main study. However, the gains are substantially smaller than the 40-percentage-point improvement obtained on the real toilet-seat-closing task in the section "Quantitative results". This difference is expected because the LIBERO suites mainly contain table-top pick-and-place and goal-reaching tasks, which depend less on precise axial-distance estimation than the contact-critical lid-closing task and therefore provide less opportunity for depth information to improve performance. The pseudo-depth-augmented variant yielded small positive mean differences across all three evaluated suites, although the observed improvements remain modest. The strongest effect is observed in manipulation tasks that are more sensitive to object distance and spatial arrangement. The success rate on LIBERO-Spatial increased by 1.7% points, which is consistent with the spatial focus of this suite. LIBERO-Object showed an improvement of 1.3% points, whereas LIBERO-Goal exhibited the smallest increase of 0.9% points because its tasks depend more strongly on goal interpretation than on geometric precision. Together with the real-robot results, this pattern suggests that depth conditioning may be more useful for tasks with stronger spatial and contact-related demands. Because the LIBERO experiments use feature concatenation rather than stochastic modality fusion, these results support the cross-task value of pseudo-depth conditioning but do not independently validate the proposed fusion mechanism.

Conclusions

Depth-Augmented Diffusion Policy was developed to integrate depth information into diffusion-based imitation learning for robotic manipulation. The framework addresses two practical challenges: effective conditioning on depth without suppressing geometric cues and reliable depth acquisition when a hardware sensor is unavailable. A dual-encoder architecture with stochastic modality fusion enables the RGB and depth encoders to learn independently useful representations, thereby reducing modality collapse. Depth Anything V2 pseudo-depth provides an alternative to sensor depth and removes the dependence on an RGB-D camera while preserving strong manipulation performance.

Real-world experiments on the toilet-seat-closing task demonstrated substantial improvements. The depth-augmented policy achieved a 95% success rate with sensor depth and 90% with pseudo-depth, compared with 50% for the RGB-only baseline and 75% for 3D Diffusion Policy. The results indicate that explicit depth conditioning improves performance in the considered distance-sensitive task and that pseudo-depth can serve as a practical alternative to hardware depth in this setting.

A limitation of the present study is that the real-world evaluation was conducted on a single manipulation task using a relatively small dataset of 46 demonstrations and a single robotic platform. Therefore, the present results should be interpreted as evidence for this task setting rather than as a claim of broad generalization across tasks, robots, or environments. The LIBERO simulation suites (Section "Generalization to the LIBERO benchmark") broaden the evaluation to simulated table-top tasks, but the real-world study still rests on a single task and platform. Because the depth-augmented model contains a second visual encoder, the comparison with the single-encoder RGB-only baseline does not fully isolate the effect of depth information from the effect of additional encoder capacity. Performance may be sensitive to inaccurate pseudo-depth on reflective or transparent surfaces. Domain shifts, changes in camera viewpoint, and highly dynamic scenes may also reduce performance. In addition, pseudo-depth is relative rather than metric and may therefore be unsuitable for tasks that require absolute scale. In such cases, a metric monocular estimator such as ZoeDepth or Metric3D could be incorporated into the same dual-encoder architecture. Two further methodological limitations follow from the small demonstration corpus. First, all 46 demonstrations are used for training without a held-out validation split, as described in the section "Task and dataset". A fixed final checkpoint is therefore used instead of validation-based checkpoint selection. This avoids selection based on evaluation rollouts but does not guarantee that the selected checkpoint has the best generalization performance. Second, each of the 40 evaluation rollouts per method is an independent physical execution rather than a replay of a demonstration. Nevertheless, the trial count remains modest. A larger evaluation campaign would produce narrower confidence intervals and stronger estimates of performance variability. Further evaluation is required for the proposed approach on more diverse manipulation scenarios, larger datasets, and transfer across robot embodiments. The framework can be extended to multi-task and multi-object learning, contact-rich manipulation, dynamic scene understanding, tactile-depth multimodal fusion, and integration with reinforcement learning for adaptive control. Evaluation on additional simulation benchmarks and standardized comparison with a broader range of prior methods would further strengthen the generalization analysis. A controlled robustness study under injected sensor noise and partial occlusion, complementing the noise and blur augmentations described in the section "Image-level augmentations", is also required to quantify the degradation of pseudo-depth and sensor depth under such perturbations. Another important direction is a systematic study of multimodal conditioning design. This includes attention-based cross-modal fusion as an alternative to feature-level addition, different modality-dropout probabilities, raw single-channel depth input versus colormap representation, and alternative encoder pretraining sources. These studies are left for future work because each variant requires retraining and separate real-robot evaluation. They are also independent of the central question examined in this study, namely whether depth conditioning and pseudo-depth improve manipulation performance.

Appendix A: Image augmentation parameters

This appendix lists the complete set of image-level augmentation transforms, parameter ranges, and application probabilities summarized in the section “Image-level augmentations”.

The RGB pipeline is composed of four groups of transforms. The first group contains geometric augmentations. Mild spatial perturbations are applied to simulate small viewpoint variations. Specifically, SafeRotate with a rotation range of ± 5° and probability p=0.1 introduces slight rotational variation with black border fill, whereas RandomResizedCrop with output size , scale range , aspect-ratio range , and probability p=0.15 introduces subtle zoom and aspect-ratio jitter. These transforms are deliberately kept conservative in order to preserve the spatial correspondence between the image and the recorded end-effector pose.

The second group contains color augmentations. Seven photometric transforms are used to diversify the appearance distribution. ColorJitter modifies brightness, contrast, saturation, and hue within the specified ranges. RandomBrightnessContrast and HueSaturationValue introduce additional intensity and color variation. RGBShift changes each color channel within the specified range. CLAHE is applied with a clip limit of 4.0, whereas RandomGamma and RandomToneCurve modify nonlinear intensity characteristics. All parameter ranges and application probabilities are reported below.

The third group consists of blur-based augmentation. MotionBlur with a kernel size of up to 5 and probability p=0.1 is used to simulate the slight blur that may arise from camera motion or robot motion during task execution.

The fourth group comprises noise-based augmentations. Three noise models are used to inject sensor-like perturbations: GaussNoise with standard deviation in the range and probability p=0.1, ISONoise with color shift in the range , intensity in the range , and probability p=0.05, and MultiplicativeNoise with a multiplier in the range and probability p=0.05.

The depth pipeline includes only the geometric transforms used in the RGB pipeline, namely SafeRotate and RandomResizedCrop with identical parameters, in order to preserve spatial consistency between the two modalities under geometric perturbations. Color, blur, and noise augmentations are intentionally excluded from the depth branch because they would introduce physically implausible distortions into the geometric signal.

Appendix B: Training configuration

This appendix specifies the complete training configuration summarized in the section “Implementation details”.

All models are trained on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU. The AdamW optimizer is used with a base learning rate of and a linear warmup over 2,000 steps. The vision encoders receive a reduced learning rate of (). Training runs for a maximum of 60,000 gradient steps with a batch size of 64.
