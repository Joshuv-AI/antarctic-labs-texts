CO2 leakage alters bacterial community structure and metabolic function of soybean rhizosphere in the Ordos Basin
Authors: Lu Xue, Junjie Ma, Haofan Wang, Lin Li, Jinfeng Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCO2 capture and storage (CCS) is a pivotal technology for mitigating global climate change, but CO2 leakage from geological storage sites risks disrupting soil rhizosphere bacterial communities critical to soil functionality. To clarify their response mechanisms, this study focused on CO2-sensitive soybean (Shanning 17) in the Ordos Basin, establishing a simulated CO2 leakage platform with four treatments: CK (control, ambient CO2), 10%, 30%, and 50%. Illumina NovaSeq 16 S rRNA sequencing combined with PICRUSt functional prediction was used to analyze rhizosphere bacteria. Results showed CO2 leakage altered the bacterial community: Chao1 index decreased by up to 4.27%, Pielou’s evenness index increased, and Beta diversity changed notably. Bacteroidetes relative abundance increased significantly, while Proteobacteria, Acidobacteria, and Nitrospirae decreased; the rare phylum Deferribacteres was only detected under 30% and 50% CO2; Five key genera (RB41, MND1, Nitrospira, Solirubrobacter, Gaiella) dominated community shifts, with abundance trends matching their phyla. Functionally, The dominant Level 1 metabolism pathway (average relative abundance 81.02%), as well as the Level 2 pathways of amino acid metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, and energy metabolism, were significantly affected, and these functional changes were closely linked to shifts in bacterial community structure. This study supports environmental impact and risk assessment for CCS projects in the Ordos Basin and similar geological storage areas.Keywords: Microbial structure and function, NovaSeq sequencing, PICRUSt, CO2 leakage, CO2 capture and storageSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Microbiology

Introduction

CO2 capture and storage (CCS) is widely recognized as a pivotal technology for mitigating global climate change, primarily by reducing anthropogenic CO2 emissions into the atmosphere1–3. CCS achieves this by capturing CO2 from large industrial sources, such as coal-fired power plants, and injecting it through dedicated pipelines into stable geological formations (e.g., depleted oil and gas reservoirs, deep saline aquifers, and unmineable coal seams) for long-term sequestration and atmospheric isolation4–6. Despite its critical role in achieving global net-zero emissions, the risk of CO2 leakage from geological storage remains a major environmental concern and a key barrier to large-scale deployment7–9.

Such leakage, which can occur through injection wells, faults, or other subsurface pathways, and once it migrates upward to the surface, can substantially alter the physicochemical properties of the overlying soil (e.g., pH reduction via CO2 dissolution to form carbonic acid, decreased oxygen partial pressure due to CO2 accumulation, and changes in soil moisture and nutrient availability)10–12. These soil environmental changes further act as direct driving forces to disrupt soil microbial communities, among which soil bacteria—being the most abundant and active component—play pivotal roles in sustaining soil ecosystem functionality, including soil organic matter decomposition, nitrogen fixation and transformation, rhizosphere nutrient cycling, and pollutant degradation13,14. Specifically, the alteration of soil physicochemical properties induced by CO2 leakage can directly select for bacterial taxa with adaptive traits (e.g., acid tolerance, anaerobic metabolism) while inhibiting taxa sensitive to environmental stress, thereby leading to shifts in bacterial community structure and functional potential. Therefore, investigating the response mechanisms of soil bacterial community structure and metabolic functions to CO2 leakage is not only essential for revealing the ecological impact pathway of CCS-related leakage but also critical for improving the scientificity of CCS environmental risk assessment.

Existing studies have confirmed that elevated soil CO2 concentrations induced by leakage can significantly disrupt microbial activity and community structure in rhizosphere ecosystems, with clear dose-effect and temporal dynamic characteristics15,16. Mechanistically, CO2 diffusion into the soil reduces microbial respiration rates and total biomass by altering intracellular pH balance and energy metabolism pathways17,18, a phenomenon supported by reduced microbial abundance observed in controlled leakage simulation experiments. High-throughput sequencing (HTS) analyses have revealed pronounced shifts in bacterial diversity: Chao and Shannon indices consistently decline in CO2-affected soils (e.g., 60-day leakage reduced the Chao index by 17–27.8% in aquic soil ) [19, while the ACE (Abundance-based Coverage Estimator) index increases transiently, indicating notable altered community composition19. Notably, temporal dynamics studies have shown an initial slight increase in the Chao index (possibly due to the temporary activation of some copiotrophic taxa) followed by sharp declines, which reflects the progressive intensification of microbial stress as CO2 accumulation persists and soil acidification worsens20.

Cumulative evidence from multiple analytical approaches has clarified the shifts in soil microbial community structure induced by CO2 leakage, with consistent trends and notable nuances. In particular, CO2 leakage generally enriches anaerobic, acid-resistant, and metal-resistant bacterial taxa in the soil, which reflects microbial adaptation to the altered physicochemical environment (e.g., acidification, hypoxia) caused by elevated soil CO217,21. Using quantitative real-time PCR (q-PCR), Ziogou and Šibanc et al. further observed that total bacterial and archaeal abundances decrease with increasing soil CO2 concentrations in natural leakage systems22,23—a pattern that has been validated in simulation experiments, though short-term leakage effects are reversible24. Denaturing gradient gel electrophoresis (DGGE) analyses have complemented these findings: Oppermann (2010) detected high abundances of anaerobic taxa (e.g., methanogenic Archaea, sulfate-reducing Bacteria [SRB]) at natural high-CO2 leakage sites25, while Ma et al. identified halophiles (Thiohalocapsa halophila) and anaerobes (Porphyromonas gingivicanis) in the sorghum rhizosphere under simulated high CO2 conditions26, highlighting taxon-specific adaptive responses. High-throughput sequencing (HTS) studies by Tian and Chen et al. have further specified that Acidobacteria and Bacteroidetes the relative abundances increase under high CO2 conditions, underscoring phylum-level shifts linked to CO2-induced environmental changes20,27.

In summary, CO2 leakage-induced soil environmental changes (primarily acidification and hypoxia) drive the enrichment of anaerobic and acid-tolerant bacterial taxa while inhibiting nitrifying bacteria and other aerobic taxa, which is hypothesized to cause differential alterations in bacterial functional pathways related to carbon and nitrogen metabolism in the rhizosphere soil—key processes maintaining soil ecosystem stability. To date, modern biological techniques (including q-PCR, DGGE, and HTS) have been widely applied to systematically reveal the dynamic characteristics and core patterns of soil bacterial diversity and community structure under CO2 leakage scenarios, providing substantial basic data for understanding community-level responses. However, a critical knowledge gap remains: compared with the relatively mature research on community structure, there is still a lack of systematic exploration into the response pathways and regulatory mechanisms of bacterial metabolic functions under CO2 leakage conditions. Specifically, how do shifts in bacterial community structure translate into changes in metabolic functions? What are the key functional genes and pathways involved in microbial adaptation to CO2 leakage? Clarifying these inherent logical links through in-depth experiments and theoretical discussions is urgent to improve the comprehensive cognitive system from bacterial community structure to metabolic functions under CO2 leakage.

PICRUSt functional prediction analysis, which can infer corresponding bacterial metabolic functions from 16 S rRNA gene sequences, provides an effective technical approach to address this knowledge gap. This method has been successfully applied in various habitats (e.g., plant rhizosphere soil, lake sediments) to predict microbial functional potential, and its application in CO2 leakage research can help reveal the response rules of soil bacterial metabolic functions to leakage stress28,29. As a large-scale cratonic sedimentary system in northern China, the Ordos Basin has been identified as a core potential area for CO2 geological storage due to its excellent tectonic stability, thick cap rocks, and favorable stratigraphic structure30. Building on previous findings of our research group—where soybeans were identified as a crop sensitive to CO2 leakage in the Ordos Basin31—this study selected soybean rhizosphere soil as the research object, given that the rhizosphere is a hot spot of microbial activity and nutrient cycling, making it more sensitive to environmental changes induced by CO2 leakage.

By establishing a CO2 simulated leakage platform, this study used 16 S rRNA Illumina NovaSeq high-throughput sequencing technology to characterize the composition and shift patterns of the bacterial community in soybean rhizosphere soil, and combined it with PICRUSt functional prediction analysis to explore the response characteristics of bacterial metabolic functions (especially carbon and nitrogen metabolism pathways) to CO2 leakage. The core objectives of this study are to: (1) clarify the dynamic response of soybean rhizosphere bacterial community structure to CO2 leakage; (2) reveal the corresponding changes in bacterial metabolic functional potential and key functional pathways; (3) improve the complete cognitive system from rhizosphere soil bacterial community structure to metabolic functions under CO2 leakage conditions. This study not only deepens the scientific understanding of the evolution mechanism of the surface soil ecosystem under the scenario of CO2 leakage from carbon storage projects but also provides a critical scientific support for assessing the environmental impacts and risks associated with large-scale CCS projects in the Ordos Basin and other similar geological storage areas.

Materials and methods

Experiment design

In the experiment, soybean (Shanning 17), a crop sensitive to CO2 leakage screened by our research group in previous studies, was used as the research object31. The experimental soil was a typical silty clay loam (sand 11.80%, silty 68.47%, clay 19.73%). A CO2 leakage simulation platform was constructed, and four soil CO2 concentration treatments were established, including the control group (CK), representing natural soil conditions with a CO2 concentration of 0.5%-4%, as well as 10%, 30% and 50% soil CO2 concentration. Each treatment was performed in triplicate with three biological replicates to investigate the structure and function of bacterial community in the soybean rhizosphere soil under CO2 leakage conditions.

The CO2 leakage simulation platform mainly consisted of four parts: a CO2 sensor, a CO2 controller, a solenoid valve, and an online monitoring system (Fig. 1). Specifically, the CO2 sensor (SKA-203) was a gas detection module based on Non-Dispersive Infra-Red (NDIR) absorption, which could real-time monitor the CO2 concentration at a specific position; the CO2 controller adopted an SKA/NE-301 intelligent gas transmitter to convert the real-time CO2 concentration signal into an electrical signal, thereby realizing precise control of CO2 concentration. Both the CO2 sensor and the controller had a measurement range of 0 ~ 100%, a response time of 30 ~ 60 s, and a linear error of ± 1.0%. Air inlet pipes were arranged at the bottom of the plant growth chamber to continuously introduce CO2 gas, and the CO2 sensor was vertically embedded at a depth 20 ~ 30 cm in the box to real-time detect the CO2 concentration at 20 ~ 30 cm in the soil layer. For the detailed design of the CO2 leakage simulation platform, growth chamber and gas distribution mode, please refer to the article by Xue et al.32. The simulation experiment was initiated at the five-leaf stage of soybean with continuous CO2 gas supply, and the cultivation lasted for 80 days until the soybean matured.

The CO2 leakage simulation platform and experimental site.

Soil sample preparation and analysis

Soil sample collection

After the experimental period, soil samples were collected from each CO2 concentration treatment group. For each growth chamber, fresh soil samples were collected from the 10–30 cm soil layer using a three-point sampling method and thoroughly mixed (each mixed sample was approximately 100 g). After removing large impurities such as plant roots and gravel, the collected soil samples were mixed again, and 2–3 g of each sample was placed into a 5 mL sterile centrifuge tube. The CK (control group), 10%, 30%, and 50% soil CO2 concentration treatments were labeled as Dw, Dx, Dy, and Dz, respectively, resulting in a total of 12 soil samples (4 treatment groups × 3 biological replicates). All samples were sealed and stored at -80℃ for subsequent DNA extraction.

DNA extraction and 16 S rRNA gene sequencing DNA

Total genomic DNA samples were extracted using the OMEGA Soil DNA Kit (D5625-01) (Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions, and stored at -20 °C prior to further analysis. The quantity and quality of extracted DNAs were measured using a NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and agarose gel electrophoresis, respectively.

The V3–V4 hypervariable region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using the forward primer 338F (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3’) and the reverse primer 806R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’). PCR amplicons were purified with the Agencourt AMPure Beads (Beckman Coulter, Indianapolis, IN) and quantified using the PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA).

After the individual quantification step, amplicons were pooled in equal amounts, and paired-end sequencing (2 × 250 bp) was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq platform using the NovaSeq 6000 SP Reagent Kit by Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China).

Sequence analysis

Microbiome bioinformatics analysis was performed using QIIME2 2019.4 with minor modification according to the official tutorials (https://docs.qiime2.org/2019.4/tutorials/)33. Primer removal, quality filtering, denoise, splicing and chimaeras removal were conducted using the Divisive Amplicon Denoising Algorithm 2 (DADA2)34. Each high-quality denoised sequences was defined as Amplicon Sequence Variant (ASV). Taxonomic annotation of ASVs was using the classify-sklearn naive Bayes taxonomy classifier in feature-classifier plugin against the SILVA Release 132 Database35,36.

Bioinformatics and statistical analysis

Bioinformatics data analyses were mainly conducted using QIIME2 and R software (v. 3.5.1). Alpha diversity indices (Chao1, Simpson, Pielou’s evenness, and Good’s coverage) of the bacterial community were calculated using QIIME2. Beta diversity of the bacterial community was characterized by nonmetric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) analysis and unweighted pair-group method with arithmetic means (UPGMA) clustering analysis, both based on the Bray-Curtis distance matrix. Principal components analysis (PCA) was performed to explore differences in genus-level species among groups, which was based on the species abundance matrix and employed Euclidean distance matrices (EDM) to quantify the dissimilarities in genus-level species composition across different treatment groups.

The functions of bacteria community were predicted using PICRUSt2 (Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States) based on KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) databases (https://www.kegg.jp/)28. The KEGG Pathway Database is the main part of KEGG, which is divided into four levels: level 1 includes 6 major metabolic pathways, level 2 includes 45 metabolic pathway sub-functions, level 3 is the specific metabolic pathway diagram of each sub-function, and level 4 corresponds to the specific annotation information of the functional units (KEGG orthologous groups, KO) involved in the metabolic pathway28,37,38. Using the normalized KO abundance table, the principal co-ordinates analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis distance was performed to identify functional differences among samples in low-dimensional space.

All alpha diversity and microbial relative abundance data were analyzed using the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test, and pairwise comparisons between groups were conducted with Dunn’s post-hoc test. The significance of differences in Beta diversity was tested using Adonis analysis in QIIME2.

Data availability

The datasets generated and/or analysed during the current study are available in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) repository under Bioproject accession number PRJNA1367906.

Results

Diversity of soil bacterial community

Variation of Alpha diversity

Chao1, Simpson, Pielou’s evenness, and Good’s coverage represent typical alpha diversity indices, quantifying community richness, diversity, evenness and species coverage degree, respectively. Under elevated soil CO2 concentration, the Chao1 index of bacterial community in the soybean rhizosphere soil showed a statistically significant decrease (P < 0.05). Compared with the CK, Chao1 values decreased by 0.56%, 1.93%, and 4.27% under soil CO2 concentrations of 10%, 30% and 50%, respectively (Fig. 2-a). The Pielou’s evenness index displayed an increasing trend along with the elevated soil CO2 concentration (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2-c). Conversely, no significant differences were found in the Simpson and Good’s coverage indices among different CO2 treatments (P > 0.05) (Fig. 2-b, d).

Changes of Alpha diversity in soybean rhizosphere soil under CO2 leakage conditions.

Changes of Beta diversity

Beta diversity reflects compositional variation among microbial communities in response to environments changes and evaluates inter-sample dissimilarity. In this study, non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) analysis demonstrated that bacterial communities from different CO2 treatments were clearly separated along Axis 1, while variations between Dy and Dz were alsoaffected by Axis 2 (Fig. 3-a). Unweighted pair-group method with arithmetic mean (UPGMA) clustering analysis revealed that community dissimilarity among 12 soil samples ranged from 15.9% to 18.8%. These samples were clustered into two distinct classes: Dw and Dx fell into one cluster, and Dy and Dz formed the other. Dx slightly differed from Dw, but was distinctly separated from Dy and Dz (Fig. 3-b). Additionally, Adonis analysis verified that soil bacterial communities differed significantly under gradient CO2 concentrations (R2 = 0.3646, P < 0.01) (Table 1).

Characteristics of soil bacterial community structure

Composition of dominant and rare bacteria at the phylum level

High-throughput sequencing generated a total of 1,121,907 high-quality sequences from 12 soil samples. The number of high-quality sequences per sample ranged from 77,495 to 111,204, with an average of 93,492. Taxonomic classification identified 42 phyla, 149 classes, 452 orders, 804 families and 1380 genera. According to the classification standard proposed by Galand (2009)39, bacteria with a relative abundance of > 1% were defined as dominant phyla, while those < 0.01% were regarded as rare phyla. Under CO2 leakage conditions, nine dominant phyla were observed in soybean rhizosphere soil, including Proteobacteria, Acidobacteria, Actinobacteria, Chloroflexi, Gemmatimonadetes, Rokubacteria, Bacteroidetes, Latescibacteria, and Nitrospirae. Total relative abundance of these phyla accounted for 93.25%. Among them, Proteobacteria (30.39%), Acidobacteria (22.65%), and Actinobacteria (13.53%) were the most dominant (Fig. 4-a). A total of 11 rare phyla were detected, among which five phyla (Calditrichaeota, Zixibacteria, WS2, Dadabacteria, and Deferribacteres) showed measurable abundance (Fig. 4-b).

Relative abundance of dominant and rare bacteria at the phylum level

Community composition of dominant phyla remained stable in soybean rhizosphere soil under different CO2 concentrations, whereas relative abundance of certain phyla changed markedly. In detail, the relative abundance of Proteobacteria, Acidobacteria, Bacteroidete, and Nitrospirae showed significant differences under CO2 leakage conditions (P<0.05) (Fig. 5-a). Compared with CK, Proteobacteria abundance decreased by 1.16%, 1.91%, and 2.05% at 10%, 30%, and 50% CO2, respectively. Similarly, Acidobacteria decreased by 0.01%, 1.69%, and 2.40%, and Nitrospirae decreased by 0.16%, 0.29% and 0.59%. In contrast, Bacteroidetes increased by 0.46%, 0.67% and 0.99%, respectively.

No significant differences were observed in rare phylum including Calditrichaeota, Zixibacteria, WS2, and Dadabacteria among treatments (P > 0.05). Importantly, the rare phylum Deferribacteres only appeared at 30% and 50% CO2, and its relative abundance was highly significantly different (P < 0.01) (Fig. 5-b).

To identify key discriminatory taxa under various CO2 leakage levels, principal component analysis (PCA) based on Euclidean distance matrix (EDM) was conducted to compare the community abundance profiles among the groups. At the genus level, PCA indicated that PC1 and PC2 contributed 57.10% and 16.70% of total variation, respectively, with a combined contribution of 73.80%, which constituted the main source of sample differentiation under different soil CO2 concentrations (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Changes of microbial community structure in soybean rhizosphere soil under CO2 leakage conditions

Environmental stress and plant root exudates are key drivers of soil microbial diversity of soil microbial diversity40, but the hierarchical and interactive mechanisms between anthropogenically induced CO2 leakage and rhizosphere bacterial diversity remain incompletely understood. Our findings revealed that CO2 leakage significantly decreased the Chao1 index of Alpha diversity (Fig. 2-a), increased the Pielou’s evenness index (Fig. 2-c), and altered the Beta diversity (Fig. 3) ,indicating a decline in species richness but an increase in community evenness. These patterns align with previous studies in maize and ryegrass rhizospheres under CO2 leakage, suggesting conserved responses across plant species19,41.

Notably, the marked reduction in Proteobacteria and Acidobacteria—the two most dominant phyla—was likely the primary driver of these diversity shifts. The core mechanism underlying the decline of these two phyla is the synergistic effect of CO2 leakage-induced soil acidification and hypoxia, which disrupts their metabolic homeostasis and competitive fitness: exogenous CO2 dissolves in soil pore water to form carbonic acid (H2CO3), which dissociates into H+ and HCO3− ions, thereby lowering soil pH19; concurrently, CO2 accumulation in soil pores displaces O2, creating a hypoxic microenvironment (O2 concentration < 5%) that inhibits aerobic metabolism. Proteobacteria and Acidobacteria are predominantly aerobic taxa42, with their central carbon metabolism and energy production tightly linked to oxidative phosphorylation—hypoxia directly blocks this pathway by limiting electron transport chain activity, leading to ATP depletion and reduced cellular proliferation. Additionally, these phyla lack efficient pH buffering systems (e.g., proton-translocating ATPases) compared to acid-tolerant taxa (e.g., Bacteroidetes), so acidification disrupts the tertiary structure of key metabolic enzymes (e.g., dehydrogenases, phosphatases) and impairs membrane permeability, further suppressing their survival. Indirectly, CO2 leakage indirectly impacts these phyla by inhibiting plant photosynthesis43,44—reducing photosynthate allocation to roots—and inducing plant senescence11,45, which alters the quantity (reduced total exudation) and composition (shift from labile sugars to defensive metabolites) of root exudates46. Since Proteobacteria and Acidobacteria rely heavily on labile root exudates as carbon and energy sources47, these shifts further reduce their competitive advantage relative to taxa that utilize more recalcitrant carbon sources.

In contrast, Bacteroidetes abundance significantly increased under CO2 leakage (Figs. 4-a and 5-a). Its enrichment is driven by unique adaptive traits for hypoxic/acidic environments and its role in enhanced denitrification: Bacteroidetes include facultative anaerobes that switch to fermentative/anaerobic respiration under low O2, gaining a competitive edge over obligate aerobes48. They also possess acid-tolerance mechanisms (proton-translocating proteins, cytoplasmic buffers like glutamate and trehalose) to maintain intracellular pH homeostasis49. Critically, many Bacteroidetes encode denitrification genes (nirK, nirS, nosZ)50,51, and CO2-induced lower O2 and altered redox potential (+ 200 mV to -100 mV) enhance denitrification, expanding their niches52,53. The concurrent decline of Nitrospirae (obligate aerobic nitrifiers) reinforces this mechanism: hypoxia inhibits their nitrite oxidoreductase activity54, while denitrification (favored by Bacteroidetes) depletes nitrates, creating a reciprocal shift that suppresses Nitrospirae and promotes Bacteroidetes. Acidobacteria reduction differed from Tian et al. but aligned with Zhang et al.20,55, driven by stress intensity, baseline soil pH, and hypoxia: Tian et al. used higher CO2 rates (extreme acidification selecting acidophilic subgroups) and acidic soil, while our alkaline soil (pH 7.8) harbors neutral/alkaline-tolerant Acidobacteria sensitive to mild acidification and hypoxia42. This highlights context-dependent microbial responses to CO2.

Our results show that while CO2 leakage altered the relative abundance of some bacteria, the composition of dominant populations remained similar at the phylum level (Fig. 4-a). This stability in dominant phylum composition reflects functional compensation in rhizosphere microbial communities—a key adaptive feature that maintains ecosystem functionality under environmental stress: while specific taxa within dominant phyla are replaced (e.g., aerobic Proteobacteria taxa replaced by anaerobic Bacteroidetes taxa), the core ecological functions (e.g., nutrient cycling, organic matter decomposition) mediated by these phyla are partially retained, ensuring the basic stability of the rhizosphere ecosystem. Notably, we detected the presence of rare bacteria Deferribacteres at high soil CO2 concentrations (30% and 50%) (Figs. 4-b and 5-b). Deferribacteres are obligate or facultative anaerobes that utilize various alternative electron acceptors (e.g., Fe3+, nitrate, Mn4+) for energy metabolism56,57, and their emergence under high CO2 conditions directly supports the hypothesis of Feitz et al. that CO2 leakage induces a shift of the microbial community toward anaerobic microorganisms21. Mechanistically, this shift is driven by CO2-induced hypoxia and the subsequent increase in available alternative electron acceptors (e.g., Fe3+ released from soil minerals under acidic conditions), which create a unique ecological niche for Deferribacteres—these taxa can outcompete other anaerobes for alternative electron acceptors, representing late-stage stress adaptation.

PCA identified five responsive genera (RB41, MND1, Nitrospira, Solirubrobacter, Gaiella; Table 2; Fig. 6), among which RB41 (Acidobacteria), MND1 (Proteobacteria), and Nitrospira (Nitrospirae) declined with increasing CO2 (Fig. 7)—consistent with the response trends of their respective dominant phyla. This consistency reflects phylogenetic conservation of stress response traits within these phyla, where core physiological characteristics (aerobic metabolism, pH sensitivity) are conserved across taxonomic levels (from phylum to genus). Notably, RB41 (an obligate aerobe in Acidobacteria dependent on neutral pH), MND1 (an obligate aerobe in Proteobacteria class Alphaproteobacteria requiring neutral pH), and Nitrospira (an obligate aerobic nitrifier) all decline under CO2-induced hypoxia/acidification58, mirroring the responses of their parent phyla. In contrast, Solirubrobacter and Gaiella (Actinobacteria) increase (Fig. 7): their optimum pH ranges (6.0–9.0 and 6.5–7.5, respectively) and pH homeostasis mechanisms (P-type ATPases, proline) enable survival in moderate acidification (pH 6.2-7.0), and their facultative anaerobiosis enhances competitiveness under low O259,60. Their ecological roles remain poorly characterized despite preliminary evidence suggesting involvement in recalcitrant organic matter degradation. Further studies (e.g., pure culture experiments, metatranscriptomics) are needed to elucidate the full mechanistic basis of their increased abundance, including their potential roles in nutrient cycling and plant-microbe interactions under CO2 leakage.

Changes of microbial community function in soybean rhizosphere soil under CO2 leakage conditions

Using Bray-Curtis PCoA, we demonstrated that CO2 leakage altered the functions of the bacteria community, as well as its structure (Fig. 8). According to PICRUSt function predictions, metabolism (Level 1) was the most dominant pathway (average 81.03%, Table 3), indicating it is key for rhizosphere bacteria to cope with CO2 stress and support soybean growth. We detected 30 Level 2 pathways, reflecting high functional richness. Among these, amino acid and carbohydrate metabolism increased, while energy metabolism fluctuated (Fig. 9)—all shifts are directly driven by taxonomic changes at both phylum and genus levels, which possess distinct functional traits that modulate specific metabolic processes—this taxon-function linkage is critical for understanding how CO2 leakage alters rhizosphere ecosystem function:

The pathway of amino acid metabolism is closely related to soil nitrogen (N) transformation, and its increased abundance showed a negative association with Nitrospira (genus, Nitrospirae phylum) and positive association with Solirubrobacter (genus, Actinobacteria phylum). The mechanistic driver of this shift is CO2-induced suppression of nitrification and enhanced organic N mineralization: CO2-induced hypoxia/acidification reduces Nitrospira, inhibiting nitrification and decreasing plant-available nitrate54. In response, bacteria upregulate organic N recycling via amino acid pathways, enhanced by Solirubrobacter’s protease/urease activity, mediating rhizosphere N cycling.

Carbohydrate metabolism, a central pathway in microbial energy acquisition, involves the degradation of complex organic compounds (e.g., cellulose, hemicellulose) and is integral to soil carbon (C) cycling61. Our analysis revealed a positive relationship between carbohydrate metabolism and Bacteroidetes genera, as well as a negative relationship with RB41. The mechanistic basis for this shift is the change in the ability of rhizosphere bacteria to utilize different C sources, driven by taxonomic turnover: Bacteroidetes (which increase under CO2leakage) possess a diverse array of glycoside hydrolases that enable them to efficiently degrade complex carbohydrates (e.g., cellulose, hemicellulose) from senescent plant tissues—this is critical because CO2 leakage reduces labile root exudate C (e.g., glucose, sucrose) availability62,63. Conversely, RB41 (an Acidobacteria genus that declines) is specialized in the degradation of recalcitrant carbohydrates (e.g., lignin), and its reduction shifts carbohydrate metabolism toward the utilization of labile-to-semi-labile C sources. This taxon-driven shift in carbohydrate metabolism alters the rhizosphere C cycle, potentially increasing soil organic matter decomposition under CO2 leakage.

The first increase and subsequent decrease of energy metabolism pathway reflects a two-stage adaptive response of the microbial community to increasing CO2 stress. In the early stage (low CO2 concentration, 5–10%), mild hypoxia and acidification induce bacteria to upregulate energy metabolism (e.g., anaerobic respiration) to maintain cellular activity—MND1 (a Proteobacteria genus), which is an aerobic energy-metabolizing taxon that can switch to facultative anaerobic respiration, drives this increase by upregulating genes encoding anaerobic respiratory enzymes (e.g., nitrate reductase). In the late stage (high CO2 concentration, 30–50%), severe hypoxia (O2 < 2%) and acidification (pH < 6.5) inhibit key energy-metabolizing enzymes (e.g., cytochrome c oxidase), leading to a decline in pathway abundance. This two-stage response highlights the dynamic nature of phylum-taxon-function links under increasing environmental stress, where different keystone taxa drive functional shifts at different stress intensities.

At Level 3, methane metabolism was enriched (Fig. 10), proposed as a testable hypothesis: CO2 may enhance its potential via Bacteroidetes (phylum) and Deferribacteres (genus), both associated with anaerobic metabolism. Bacteroidetes contribute to acetoclastic methanogenesis51, while Deferribacteres produce H2 for hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis —consistent with natural CH4 emissions17,22,23. Validation requires mcrA qPCR and CH4 flux measurements. Links between soil properties and other Level 3 pathways need further investigation with integrated taxonomic and physicochemical data.

It is worth noting that this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged when interpreting the results, as they identify key directions for future research to strengthen the mechanistic understanding of CO2 leakage effects on rhizosphere microbial communities. PICRUSt analysis only predicts microbial functional potential based on 16 S rRNA gene sequences, rather than measuring actual metabolic activity—the observed shifts in predicted functional pathways do not directly reflect changes in in situ bacterial metabolism, as they do not account for post-transcriptional (e.g., mRNA degradation) or post-translational (e.g., enzyme phosphorylation) regulation. Future studies should use functional genomics (e.g., metagenomics to identify functional genes, metatranscriptomics to quantify gene expression) and biochemical assays (e.g., enzyme activity measurements for key metabolic enzymes, such as protease, urease, and mcrA) to validate these predictions and quantify actual metabolic activity.

Conclusion

(1) CO2 leakage significantly reduced the bacterial Chao1 index (Alpha diversity), increased the Pielou’s evenness index, and altered the Beta diversity of soil bacteria, as evidenced by clear separation of bacterial communities among different CO2 treatments.

(2) CO2 leakage induced significant shifts in the relative abundance of specific bacterial phyla, without altering the overall structure of the dominant phylum community. Among the dominant taxa, the relative abundance of Bacteroidetes increased significantly, whereas that of Proteobacteria, Acidobacteria, and Nitrospirae decreased notably. Additionally, the rare phylum Deferribacteres was exclusively detected under high CO2 leakage conditions.

(3) Five taxa at the genus level, including RB41, MND1, Nitrospira, Solirubrobacter, and Gaiella, were identified as the key drivers that caused the difference in soil sample composition induced by CO2 leakage, with their relative abundances showing consistent trends with their respective parent phyla.

(4) CO2 leakage profoundly altered the functional potential of the rhizosphere bacterial community, with significant impacts on metabolic pathways across KEGG Level 1, 2, and 3; specifically, the primary Level 1 metabolism pathway, along with the Level 2 pathways of amino acid metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, and energy metabolism, were all markedly affected by CO2 leakage, and these functional changes were closely linked to the observed shifts in bacterial community structure.

This study provides critical insights into the ecological impact pathway of CO2 leakage from geological storage projects on surface soil ecosystems, deepening our understanding of the evolution mechanism of rhizosphere microbial communities under CO2 stress. The findings also offer important scientific support for environmental risk assessment and management of large-scale CCS projects in the Ordos Basin and other similar geological storage areas. However, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of this study: PICRUSt2 only predicts functional potential based on 16 S rRNA gene sequences, rather than measuring actual metabolic activity. Future research should integrate metagenomics, metatranscriptomics, and biochemical assays to validate these functional predictions and clarify the regulatory mechanisms linking bacterial community structure to metabolic activity. Additionally, long-term field experiments are needed to verify the short-term simulation results and explore the cumulative effects of CO2 leakage on rhizosphere microbial ecosystems and crop growth.
