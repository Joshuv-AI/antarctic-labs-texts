Versatile metal-phenolic network structure (MPNs) as advanced delivery platforms for bioactive compounds: The design principles, foundations, applications, and future perspectives
Authors: Yafei Zhang, Zenghui Xia, Yi Xue, Kai Zhang, Yujia Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Despite possessing many important functions, the applications of bioactive compounds (BCs) are significantly limited due to various inherent shortcomings. In recent years, a novel class of materials known as metal-phenolic network structures (MPNs) has emerged. As the application scope of MPNs expands, it has become evident that this network structure can protect encapsulated active substances from adverse external environmental influences. This review first introduces the types and properties of polyphenols and metal ions, as well as the basis of their interactions. Subsequently, the construction methods of MPNs and their advantages as delivery platforms for BCs are discussed, including stimuli-responsive properties, adsorption properties, UV resistance, antioxidant activity, and antimicrobial effects. The mechanisms involved in the loading of BCs into MPNs and their novel applications in nanoparticles, films, coatings, capsules, emulsions, and hydrogels are elucidated. Additionally, the safety of MPNs and their fate within the human body are addressed. The role of artificial intelligence in advancing the development of MPNs as delivery platforms for BCs is also described in detail. Finally, the advantages, limitations, and obstacles to the clinical transformation of MPNs as delivery platforms are discussed. This review aims to uncover the significant role of MPNs in delivering BCs, thereby promoting the development of MPNs and the broader application of BCs.

Introduction

Bioactive compounds (BCs) are derived from a range of natural sources, including animals, plants, microorganisms, and marine organisms [1]. These compounds can be classified into various categories, including active proteins, polysaccharides, alkaloids, terpenoids, phenolics, nitrogen-containing compounds, and organosulfur compounds [2]. Besides, probiotics are often mentioned simultaneously as a special type of BC, although they are not extracted chemical substances [3,4]. Given the significance of probiotics, they are also included in the scope of discussion in this review. BCs confer numerous health benefits, including anti-diabetic, anti-inflammatory, and anti-cancerous properties, as well as improvements in digestion and blood circulation. Common examples of BCs encompass resveratrol, paclitaxel, curcumin, carotenoids, quercetin, naringin, fat-soluble vitamins, and polyphenolic flavonoids, among others [5]. Many BCs are not only incorporated into food as functional components but also utilized as pharmaceuticals for disease treatment.

However, certain BCs exhibit characteristics of chemical instability and undesirable sensory properties. These compounds are susceptible to degradation by light, heat, oxygen, pH changes, metal ions, enzymes, salts, and other factors, ultimately leading to their deterioration [6]. Additionally, lipophilic BCs, such as fat-soluble vitamins, carotenoids, and omega-3 fatty acids, present challenges related to poor water solubility and low intestinal absorption efficiency [7]. The harsh conditions within the digestive system further impede the effective utilization of BCs. The stomach's acidic environment, with a pH of 2, contrasts sharply with the alkaline environment of the large intestine, where pH ranges from 7 to 8. Moreover, various digestive enzymes are present in the stomach, including lipase and pepsin, along with pancreatic enzymes and those secreted by microorganisms in the intestinal tract. Higher concentrations of salt ions and the presence of various microorganisms in the digestive system may also contribute to the inactivation of BCs [5]. Collectively, these factors diminish the efficient utilization of BCs.

In recent years, self-assembling coatings formed through the coordination of polyphenols and metal ions have garnered extensive attention [8,9]. These coatings, referred to as metal-phenolic network structures (MPNs), can be assembled on a variety of matrix materials with differing sizes and surface topologies [8]. As the application scope of MPNs has expanded, it has become evident that this network structure can protect encapsulated active substances from adverse external environmental influences, including oxidative damage, thermal denaturation, enzyme lysis, and ultraviolet (UV) radiation, while also exhibiting targeted properties [10]. For instance, a recent study by Z. Wang et al. demonstrated the formation of MPNs using EGCG, manganese ions (Mn2+), and aluminum ions (Al3+) for loading the antigen ovalbumin [11]. This approach not only safeguarded the antigenic ovalbumin but also enabled targeting of lymph nodes, resulting in effective therapeutic effects in inhibiting the development of B16F10 melanoma in mice. This technique facilitated a fivefold reduction in the volume of subcutaneous tumors when assisted by MPNs. The advanced properties of MPNs make them highly suitable for the delivery of BCs [[12], [13], [14]]. Key advantages include: 1) MPNs exhibit good biocompatibility and biosafety, as polyphenols are common functional components in food known for their health benefits, while metal ions (such as Se4+, Ca2+, Fe3+, Zn2+, etc.) are essential minerals for human health. 2) MPNs can respond to external stimuli such as changes in pH, ionic strength, REDOX environment, and light, allowing for controlled regulation of the decomposition of their structure. 3) The synthesis conditions for MPNs are mild, permitting synthesis at room temperature and in aqueous phases, which minimizes the reduction in the activity of BCs and facilitates their transport. 4) Various BCs can be readily loaded into MPNs through different supramolecular interactions (such as hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions), presenting significant advantages over complex chemical conjugation and assembly involving organic reagents. 5) Functional polyphenols and metal ions can be easily incorporated into MPNs, providing new functions or enhancing the performance of other functional components.

While several excellent reviews have summarized the applications of MPNs in food preservation technologies [15], the food industry [16], delivery and removal [17], antimicrobial applications [18], and cancer theranostics [19], this review specifically focuses on the application of MPNs in the delivery of BCs (Fig. 1). Emphasis is placed on the advanced features of MPNs as a delivery platform, elaborating on the entire axis of BC delivery, including the loading of BCs, the formation of carriers with varying structures, responsive release, as well as the advantages, disadvantages, and clinical transformations. Initially, the types, properties, interaction modes among the building blocks in MPNs, and the construction methods of MPNs are introduced. Importantly, this review details the advantages of MPNs as a BC delivery platform and the mechanisms involved in loading BCs into MPNs. Following this, novel applications of MPNs in conventional BC delivery systems—such as nanoparticles, films, coatings, capsules, emulsions, and hydrogels—are presented, addressing challenges such as poor structural adaptability, low load capacity, and insufficient interface robustness. The safety of MPNs and their final fate within the human body are also discussed. Finally, the application and future prospects of artificial intelligence in enhancing the delivery of BCs via MPNs are examined in detail.Fig. 1The types, interaction modes between building blocks in MPNs, and the construction methods of MPNs are introduced. Types and properties of MPN molecular building blocks

Phenolic ligands

Phenolic ligands are compounds characterized by one or more hydroxyl groups attached to a benzene ring. As natural bioactive substances, they primarily exist in plants as secondary metabolites. Due to their unique chemical properties, plant polyphenols function as a natural defense barrier, enabling plants to resist pathogen and pest invasions while also inhibiting the reproduction of these pathogens [20]. Polyphenols play pivotal roles in various plant processes, including preventing DNA damage, regulating metabolism, and facilitating signal transduction [21]. To date, researchers have successfully extracted and identified over 8000 polyphenolic compounds [22]. These compounds exhibit complex structures, typically comprising aromatic rings bonded to hydroxyl groups. The ability of phenolic compounds to interact with metal ions is significantly influenced by the type and structure of the compounds, highlighting the importance of understanding the relationship between structure and function [23]. Thus, a comprehensive understanding of the classification and structural characteristics of phenolic ligands is necessary.

Phenolic compounds can be classified into five categories: phenolic acids, flavonoids, stilbenes, tannic acids, and other compounds, as illustrated in Fig. 2A–F [23]. Based on their carbon skeleton structure, phenolic acids can be further divided into hydroxybenzoic acids (HBAs) (Fig. 2A) and hydroxycinnamic acids (HCAs) (Fig. 2B). HBAs possess a common C6-C1 structure, with examples including syringic acid, salicylic acid, protocatechuic acid, gentianic acid, and gallic acid. In contrast, HCAs display a C6-C3 structure, with different phenolic acids formed depending on substituents R1, R2, and R3 on the benzene ring, such as ferulic acid, erucic acid, coumaric acid, chlorogenic acid, and caffeic acid [24]. Flavonoids exhibit a characteristic C6-C3-C6 structure, where one pyran ring (the C ring) connects two benzene rings (the A and B rings) (Fig. 2C) [25]. Flavones represent the simplest structural type among flavonoids and are known for their strong anti-inflammatory properties, potentially related to the position and number of -OH groups on the A and B rings [25]. Stilbene polyphenols, primarily synthesized through stilbene and phenylpropanoid metabolic pathways in plants, mainly include resveratrol and its derivatives (Fig. 2D) [26]. Tannic acids represent highly polymerized polyphenols with diverse applications, with their abundant -OH groups conferring excellent physicochemical properties. They can be categorized into hydrolyzed tannic acids and condensed tannic acids (also known as proanthocyanidins) according to their constitutive units (Fig. 2E) [26]. Other types of polyphenols, such as lignin and coumarin (Fig. 2F), are noted, but these compounds exhibit minimal ability to interact with metal ions. The influence of polyphenol structure on complexing metal ions

An important structural feature that enables polyphenols to bind to metal ions is the presence of ortho-dihydroxyl groups, such as the 7-8 and 3′-4′ dihydroxy groups found in flavonoids or in gallic acid (GA). Typically, the interaction between polyphenols and metal ions occurs when one or both of the dihydroxyl groups dissociate. In coordination chemistry, polyphenol ligands are classified as hard bases. According to the hard and soft acid and base (HSAB) theory, these ligands can stably bind to metal ions characterized as hard acids (e.g., Fe3+) and medium acids (e.g., Fe2+) [27]. Moreover, the structure formed by polyphenols and metal ions is pH-dependent. Under acidic conditions, the ortho-phenolic hydroxyl groups of polyphenols can enter the inner coordination sphere of hydrated metal ions via single or double dissociation, replacing water molecules to form a single ligand complex. As the pH increases, di-ligands and tri-ligands may be formed. For instance, the complexes of tannic acid (TA) with Fe3+ are formed as follows: a single complex at pH < 2, a double complex in the range of 3 < pH < 6, and a triple complex at pH > 7, as illustrated in Fig. 2G [23]. These complexes exhibit characteristic ligand-metal charge transfer bands in their respective UV-visible spectra, with maxima located at >600 nm, ∼565 nm, and ∼510 nm, correspondingly [28]. Consequently, MPNs demonstrate a strong pH-dependent characteristic, which is a significant reason for their ability to dynamically respond and release BCs.

Additionally, the 5-(and/or 3-)-hydroxyl groups in conjunction with the ketone group at the C4 position in polyphenols exhibit a strong binding affinity for metal ions. Polyphenols that encompass this structure are primarily flavonols and flavonoids. For example, quercetin features two chelating sites characterized by this structure (the 5 and 3-hydroxyl groups with the ketone group at C4) and possesses a robust chelating capability for metal ions (Fig. 2H) [29,30]. Some polyphenols, such as naringenin, possess only a single chelating site (Fig. 2H) [29]. When the available binding sites are reduced, the MPNs formed from polyphenols and metal ions are smaller in size, resulting in improved bioaccessibility and solubility of the MPN complex. Therefore, selecting the appropriate types of polyphenols and metal ions based on the experimental purpose is critical for the design of an effective delivery platform utilizing MPNs.

Another notable structural characteristic is observed in polyphenols that contain multiple phenolic hydroxyl groups, such as tannic acids. These compounds are typically composed of several ester groups covalently bonded to a core glucose unit and catechol [31]. Notably, the concentration of metal ions can influence the structure and stability of MPNs. For instance, at low concentrations, iron ions interact with the ortho-phenolic hydroxyl groups in tannic acids. As the concentration of iron ions increases, they serve as coordination sites to cross-link polyphenols, resulting in the formation of larger MPNs [32]. However, if the concentration of iron ions is further elevated, excessive coordination with tannic acid may occur, leading to the dominance of single complexes and subsequent dissociation of the MPNs, as depicted in Fig. 2I.

In addition to the aforementioned structural considerations, the complexation ability of phenolic compounds is influenced by their reduction potential. The reduction potential of several common phenolic compounds is ranked as follows: propyl gallic acid > gallic acid (GA) > caffeic acid (CA) > pyrogallic acid > protocatechuic acid > butylated hydroxyanisole (BHA) > vanillyl alcohol > ferulic acid > guaiacol > coumaric acid [33]. However, the binding affinity of flavonoids or phenolic acids for metal ions does not strictly adhere to this ranking, as it is closely related to the specific structure of the phenolic acids [34]. For example, flavonoids that contain a catechol moiety on the B ring exhibit a stronger coordination ability with metal ions [33]. Conversely, phenolic acids lacking di-hydroxyphenyl or tri-hydroxyphenyl groups, such as syringic acid and vanillic acid, show minimal chelating ability for metal ions, likely due to the relatively low negative charge density at the interaction sites. Moreover, the presence of methoxy groups can hinder polyphenol binding with metal ions, which paradoxically opposes their capacity for free radical scavenging [33].

Metal ion

The periodic table comprises six metalloids, 18 nonmetals, and 87 metals, extending from element 1 (hydrogen) to element 111 (roentgenium). Both metals and metalloids possess empty orbitals in their electronic configurations, enabling them to function as electron acceptors, while phenolic ligands serve as electron donors. The interaction between phenolic ligands and metal ions encompasses a wide range of metal ions, from alkali metals to metalloids [22]. Metal ions are critical structural components of MPNs. By adjusting the valence state of these metal ions and their molar ratios to phenolic ligands, the coordination state can be precisely regulated, thereby influencing the structure and function of MPNs. This adjustable characteristic bestows unique properties upon MPNs [17]. Notably, some metal ions serve as essential trace elements in the human body, playing vital roles in maintaining electrical charges and osmotic pressure, facilitating intercellular communication, regulating DNA transcription, enabling electron transfer, and supporting various fundamental physiological activities [35]. Specifically, MPNs constructed with Sr2+ and Mg2+ have demonstrated potential in promoting osteogenic differentiation and hold promise in tissue engineering applications. Transition metal ions like Cu2+, Zn2+, Mn2+, Co2+, Fe3+, and Ti4+ are frequently utilized in MPN preparation, imparting antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and anti-fouling properties [36]. Iron, as an essential trace element, is critical for maintaining normal physiological functions and contributes to many enzymatic processes involved in cellular activities such as proliferation, respiration, electron transport, and protein synthesis [37,38]. In contrast to MPNs developed with transition metal ions, those formed from main group metal ions (such as Al3+, Ga3+, and In3+) typically exhibit lighter colors, enhancing optical transparency [39]. Rare earth metal ions (Eu3+, La3+, Gd3+, and Sm3+) are advantageous for developing functional MPNs with anti-cancer, antibacterial, and anti-inflammatory properties [40]. Furthermore, MPNs synthesized from multiple metal ions can synergistically enhance the functional characteristics of the delivery platform. By adjusting the coordination and proportion of different metal ions, MPNs with superior antibacterial properties, high biocompatibility, and enhanced catalytic efficiency can be tailored, thus demonstrating superior performance compared to single-metal MPNs in various aspects [41].

The interaction foundations among MPN molecular building blocks

In MPNs, polyphenols interact with metal ions through several mechanisms, including coordination bonding, cation-π interaction, redox reactions, and dynamic covalent bonding [22]. Among these, coordination bonding represents a significant interaction between polyphenols and metal ions. The di-hydroxyphenyl (catechol) or tri-hydroxyphenyl groups in phenolic compounds act as electron donors, while transition metal ions and main group metal ions provide empty orbitals. Each hydroxyl oxygen atom can donate a pair of electrons to the vacant orbitals of the metal ion, resulting in the formation of reversible coordination bonds and supramolecular structures of MPNs (Fig. 3A) [13,35]. The electronic configuration of the metal ions often influences the color of the MPNs. Strongly colored MPNs are formed by multivalent metal ions with partially filled d orbitals (e.g., Mn2+, Fe3+, and Ti4+). In contrast, light-colored MPNs tend to be created by main group metal ions (e.g., In3+, Ga3+, and Al3+) with filled d/f-shells or empty orbitals [44]. Furthermore, the presence of numerous di-hydroxyphenyl or tri-hydroxyphenyl groups in phenolic compounds provides effective negative binding sites for cations, facilitating the formation of cation-π interactions [22,35]. These cations typically include alkaline earth or alkali metal ions, which possess ns1 or ns2 electron configurations, existing predominantly in monovalent or divalent forms [45]. Cation-π bonding is a form of electrostatic interaction, where the π electron cloud of an aromatic ring is distributed above and below the ring plane, resulting in two “electron cloud discs” that exhibit a negative electric potential. When a cation approaches the π plane of the aromatic ring, an electrostatic attraction occurs between the cation and the negative π electron cloud [46,47]. Consequently, the types of aromatic side groups, characterized by varying electron densities (such as catechol and hydroxyl groups), influence the strength of the cation-π interaction. For instance, the presence of a single electronegative hydroxyl group in a phenolic ligand can significantly diminish the cohesion strength attributed to cation-π interactions [48]. Additionally, the strength of cation-π interactions is affected by the specific type of metal ion involved; interactions with alkali (e.g., K+, Na+, and Li+) and alkaline-earth metal ions (e.g., Ca2+ and Mg2+) tend to be stronger than those involving transition metal ions [45]. Redox interactions typically occur when noble metals interact with polyphenols. Given that the reduction potentials of many noble metal ions exceed those of polyphenols, o-diphenol groups within phenolic molecules can readily be oxidized to corresponding quinones or semiquinones. Furthermore, upon accepting electrons from phenolic compounds, noble metal ions are reduced to their metallic forms (metal nanoparticles) (Fig. 3B) [22,49]. Dynamic covalent bonds also facilitate interactions between metal ions and polyphenols. Metalloid elements, which exhibit both metallic and non-metallic properties, such as boron (B), germanium (Ge), and silicon (Si), tend to form covalent bonds with phenolic ligands, leading to the formation of covalent cross-linked networks [50]. The mechanism underlying the reversible formation of dynamic covalent bonds involves a transformation in the electronic configuration and hybridization mode of metalloid elements under varying pH conditions. For example, a boronic acid-phenol network structure is formed through interactions between vicinal diol groups in phenolic ligands and boronic acid, exhibiting cis-diol responsiveness and pH dependency. When the pH exceeds the pKa value of boronic acid motifs (4.5-10), boron can transition from sp2 hybridization to tetrahedral sp3 hybridization, allowing it to react with cis-diols and form cyclic boronate esters, as illustrated in Fig. 3C [22,51]. The fabrication method of MPNs

Compared to other nanomaterials, the fabrication of MPNs offers advantages in terms of rapidity and simplicity [52]. The fabrication process can be described as the coordination of metal ions with phenolic hydroxyl groups through physical mixing. Following this coordination, metal ions and polyphenols spontaneously aggregate to form particles ranging from nanometers to micrometers in size [53]. Three primary fabrication methods for MPNs are identified: direct self-assembly, templating assembly, and emulsion-based self-assembly.

The direct self-assembly method encompasses both one-step and multi-step synthesis techniques (Fig. 3D) [35]. The one-step method is the most commonly used and straightforward approach for fabricating MPNs. In 2013, Ejima et al. successfully detailed the assembly of MPNs using a one-pot method [28]. This process involves adsorbing some TA molecules onto the surface of a polystyrene template, while the remaining TA reacts with Fe3+ to form the TA-Fe3+ complex. Subsequently, these complexes deposit onto the template, resulting in an MPN layer approximately 10 nm thick. This one-step synthesis method requires minimal specialized equipment or specific solvents, utilizing readily available and cost-effective materials [54]. In contrast, the multi-step synthesis method is another widely utilized direct self-assembly approach. The core principle involves polyphenolic ligands repeatedly and alternately combining with metal ions to produce multilayer MPN films [55]. For example, TA may first be adsorbed onto the surface of a substrate, followed by washing away unbound TA, after which an excess of Fe3+ is added. The process continues with repeated rinsing of unabsorbed polyphenols and metal ions and the addition of new metal ions and polyphenols in each cycle, allowing for control over the MPN thickness compared to the one-step method [19].

Template-mediated MPN self-assembly employs template substrates to guide or confine the deposition and structural growth of MPNs, enabling fine control over their morphology, structure, and size [56]. This method typically consists of two steps: synthesizing MPNs on the template's surface and subsequently removing the template substrate. Depending on the type of template used—such as nanoparticles, two-dimensional materials, or vesicles—template-mediated self-assembly can yield MPNs with varying morphologies and functions [57]. Calcium carbonate is among the frequently utilized templates due to its controllable size, shape, simple synthesis route, and ease of removal. For instance, Ping et al. employed calcium carbonate as a template to in-situ prepare TA-Al3+ MPN films at pH 8 [58]. Upon removal of the substrate template, pH-responsive MPN capsules were successfully fabricated for the delivery of BCs. Other template options include mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSN) and poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) [59]. Drawing inspiration from the complex self-assembly observed in natural systems, Lin et al. synthesized polymer cubosomes (PCs) utilizing linear polystyrene-block-poly(ethylene oxide) (PS-b-PEO) [42]. Subsequently, the PCs served as the synthetic template for MPNs, as illustrated in Fig. 3E [42]. After the in situ formation of MPNs, the PC template was dissolved using tetrahydrofuran, resulting in ordered mesoporous MPN particles with a large-pore single cubic network (∼40 nm). These MPNs have been demonstrated to effectively load various proteins of differing shapes, sizes, and charges, such as glucose oxidase (GOx), horseradish peroxidase, immunoglobulin G, and bovine hemoglobin [42].

Compared to other methods, emulsion-based self-assembly offers excellent biocompatibility and effective loading capacity for BCs. An emulsion consists of a mixture of two immiscible liquids, such as water and oil, allowing for the dissolution of amphiphilic BCs. The immiscible liquids create a physical barrier within the emulsion, preventing droplet aggregation and ensuring stability [60]. Emulsion-based MPNs self-assembly can be categorized into two approaches: self-assembly occurring within the emulsion and self-assembly at the emulsion interface [61]. For instance, Besford et al. generated oleic acid emulsions with diameters ranging from 100 to 250 nm through ultrasonic treatment (Fig. 3F) [43]. Subsequently, poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG)-based polyphenolic ligands and metal ions were simultaneously cross-linked and assembled on the emulsion surface, forming interface MPNs. Despite maintaining similar size and dispersibility to the emulsion without MPNs, its stability was significantly enhanced. Notably, doxorubicin (DOX) could be effectively loaded into these emulsions. Due to the MPN coating, the emulsion exhibited pH-responsive characteristics, facilitating the release of DOX at intracellular pH levels, thereby generating substantial cytotoxicity [43]. This approach holds potential for applications in BC delivery.

Each of these fabrication methods presents distinct impact on the structure of the formed MPNs. One-step synthesis methods are characterized by simplicity and the MPNs structure formed is relatively simple. In contrast, multi-step synthesis allows for precise control over MPN thickness. In addition, apart from the difference in thickness, the MPN assembled by multi-step synthesis has a higher content of metal ions and a denser structure [62]. This is because during the multi-step synthesis process, the number of polyphenols on the substrate surface is less than that of metal ions in the solution, and most of the chelating sites of polyphenols are occupied by metal ions. In the one-step synthesis methods, polyphenols and metal ions are mixed in the solution to form small complexes, which are then adsorbed onto the template. Therefore, the chelating sites of some polyphenols are not occupied. For example, the metal ion content of Fe3+-TA MPN synthesized by the multi-step method is 50 mol%, while that of the one-step method is only 25 mol% [63]. The templating assembly precisely controls the size, arrangement and structure of MPN by regulating the template shape, featuring high flexibility and controllability [64]. Therefore, templating assembly can enhance scalability and ensure reproducibility due to the controllability of template preparation. Emulsion-based self-assembly is advantageous for its biocompatibility and the expandability of MPNs, though some specialized emulsions may incur additional costs [61]. Compared with the templating assembly, the MPN synthesized by emulsion-based self-assembly has lower structural diversity.

The advanced properties of MPNs as the delivery platforms

The diversity of MPNs structures promote MPNs to possess advanced properties. For instance, the MPN assembled by the multi-step method has a thicker shell, which can load more BCs. Moreover, MPN with a higher content of metal ions has stronger antibacterial properties. MPN assembled by the one-step synthesis methods is more sensitive to the external environment. The MPN capsules assembled by the template method can protect the internal BCs from damage caused by harsh environments. Moreover, this core-shell structure can also carry more BCs [64]. The MPN assembled by emulsion-based self-assembly can adhere to the surface of the emulsion, enhancing the stability of hydrophobic substances. Ultimately, it not only protects hydrophobic BCs but also improves their bioavailability [61]. In addition, MPNs exhibit a range of advanced properties pertinent to BC delivery. Notably, the dynamic structure governed by coordination endows MPNs with stimulus-responsive behavior, allowing for controlled disassembly and assembly under specific conditions, such as variations in pH [65]. Furthermore, MPNs possess the capability to continuously eliminate free radicals, as their network structure stabilizes polyphenols and extends their antioxidant effects. Additionally, the strong absorption of UV rays by polyphenols enables MPNs to shield sensitive BCs from UV exposure. The antibacterial properties of metal ions and certain polyphenols can also enhance the antibacterial efficacy of BCs in specific applications, creating a synergistic effect. Moreover, the favorable biocompatibility of polyphenols and specific metal ions promotes the application of MPNs in BC delivery [66]. These advanced characteristics empower MPNs to provide responsive, protective, and active preservation functions within complex external or physiological environments. Consequently, this section focuses on the advanced properties of MPNs in BC delivery.

Stimuli-responsive property

A significant feature of MPNs is their ability to respond to various external environmental factors, including changes in pH, glutathione (GSH) reduction, adenosine triphosphate (ATP) reduction, reactive oxygen species (ROS), enzymes, temperature, and others [[67], [68], [69]]. Table 1 summarizes and compares the release efficiency and environmental sensitivity of MPNs across different stimuli-responsive categories. Numerous experiments have confirmed that these MPNs exhibit high release efficiency and robust responsiveness. The stimulus-responsive characteristics of MPNs are attributed to the sensitive coordination interactions between ortho-phenolic hydroxyl groups and metal ions. The effectiveness of this responsiveness is influenced by both the type of polyphenols and the type, valence state, and composition ratio of the metal ions involved [75]. For example, metal ions in higher valence states, such as Fe3+ and Zr4+, typically display stronger chelating capabilities compared to those in lower valence states, such as Cu2+ and Fe2+. Under acidic conditions, MPNs dissociate to form single complexes due to the protonation of catechol groups. As pH increases, double and triple complexes are formed, which enhances the bonding strength between polyphenols and metal ions [61]. Notably, the specific pH range that controls the coordination transition of MPNs varies according to the structure of the polyphenols, the specific metal ions, and changing environmental conditions. For instance, the acid sensitivity of MPNs diminishes with increasing metal ion content, as a higher degree of coordination enhances the stability of the MPN system, thereby reducing sensitivity to acidic conditions. This adjustable pH-response characteristic enables the assembly and disassembly of the delivery system, facilitating the targeted release of BCs. The pH range within the tumor microenvironment typically falls between 5.7 and 6.8, while lysosomal pH ranges from 4.5 to 5.0 [76]. Additionally, ATP serves as a stimulus-response condition for MPNs. In anticancer treatment, the multidrug resistance exhibited by cancer cells can lead to the extrusion of drug molecules, thereby diminishing therapeutic efficacy [5]. P-glycoprotein (P-gp), an ATP-dependent-binding cassette (ABC) transporter, requires significant energy generated from ATP hydrolysis to maintain its function effectively. Consequently, reducing ATP levels can effectively inhibit the expression of P-gp, thereby counteracting the multidrug resistance of tumor cells [79]. Zhu et al. synthesized hollow iron-tannate MPN capsules capable of degrading through reactions with ATP, which were employed to deliver DOX and GOx for tumor treatment (Fig. 4B) [72]. This degradation of MPNs, coupled with GOx-mediated glucose consumption, led to a significant decrease in intracellular ATP levels. As ATP levels diminished, the expression of P-gp in tumor cells was notably down-regulated, facilitating the accumulation of DOX within the cells. Ultimately, this MPN capsule effectively inhibited the multidrug resistance of tumors, demonstrating a significant inhibitory effect on tumor growth [72].

ROS represent a class of free radicals or highly reactive molecules/ions derived from O2. Common forms include singlet oxygen, superoxide anion radicals, hydrogen peroxide, and hydroxyl radicals [38]. The concentration of ROS in the human body markedly increases during chronic inflammation, bacterial infections, and within tumor tissues, which presents an opportunity for the development of ROS-responsive MPNs [80]. The mechanism by which MPNs respond to ROS involves the oxidation of phenolic hydroxyl groups in polyphenols to quinone structures, which diminishes their coordination ability with metal ions. Once metal ions are released, the structural integrity of the MPNs is compromised, resulting in the release of the encapsulated BCs. Based on this principle, Mao et al. utilized MPNs to encapsulate fucoxanthin for the treatment of ulcerative colitis (UC) (Fig. 4C) [73]. UC is characterized as a recurrent and chronic intestinal inflammatory disease marked by significant ROS production and destruction of epithelial barriers. The study indicated that as the concentration of H2O2 increased (ranging from 0.1 to 10 mmol/L), the release rate of fucoxanthin significantly escalated. At an H2O2 concentration of 10 mmol/L over 40 h, the responsive release of fucoxanthin reached 90%. MPN-based nanoparticles were found to down-regulate oxidative stress levels and inflammatory factors, promote the repair of intestinal damage, and ultimately lead to significant amelioration of UC conditions [73].

Enzymes offer unique advantages as factors for stimulus response. Most enzymatic reactions are characterized by rapidity, efficiency, and mild conditions for reaction. Additionally, enzymes display specificity and selectivity for substrates, allowing for controllable biological and chemical reactions. Common enzymes include matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), cysteine caspases, cathepsins, hyaluronidases, and phospholipases [81]. MMPs, which are zinc-dependent endopeptidases, are involved in the degradation of the extracellular matrix and the regulation of bioactive molecules on cell surfaces. Beyond their regulatory functions under physiological conditions, MMPs are implicated in biological diseases such as tumor invasion, cardiovascular diseases, and bacterial infections [81]. For example, periodontitis, a chronic disease resulting from dental plaque accumulation, is marked by excessive MMP activity, leading to the destruction of periodontal tissues [80]. Xu et al. synthesized nanoparticles by coordinating TA with cupric ions, subsequently integrating them into a triglycerol monostearate/2,6-di-tert-butyl-4-methylphenol hydrogel (Fig. 4D) [74]. These hydrogels were designed to hydrolyze in response to elevated MMP levels. Post-treatment, macrophages transitioned from the M1 to M2 phenotype, resulting in reduced expression of pro-inflammatory factors and increased osteogenic gene expression, which alleviated inflammation and facilitated tissue regeneration in periodontitis. Additionally, Jing et al. constructed MPN nanocapsules using TA and trivalent iron ions [82]. The cellulase secreted by bacteria could bind to TA in the MPNs, serving as a biochemical signal that accelerated the reduction of trivalent iron ions. Analysis of experimental results confirmed the generation of divalent iron ions, triggering rapid shell disintegration and ensuring the localized release of the bacteriostatic agent at the site of infection.

Furthermore, temperature is frequently employed as a triggering condition for stimulus responses [83,84]. At defined critical temperatures, thermoresponsive polymers can undergo aggregation or disintegration through structural changes. However, MPNs typically exhibit a relatively limited response to temperature. Therefore, poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAM) is often utilized to aid in constructing temperature-responsive MPNs. PNIPAM is a well-studied and widely applied temperature-sensitive polymer [77]. Reversible and rapid phase transitions occur at approximately 32 °C. Below this phase transition temperature, PNIPAM exists in a fully hydrated state due to hydrogen bonding with surrounding water molecules. Upon heating, the intermolecular or intramolecular hydrogen bonds between amide repeat units become more favorable than those with water molecules, leading to the transformation of PNIPAM into hydrophobic or dehydrated spheres [75]. Additionally, the phase transition temperature of PNIPAM can be modulated by altering its hydrophilic and hydrophobic components. Numerous temperature-responsive MPNs have been developed based on this principle [75,85]. Kim et al. fabricated temperature-sensitive MPN capsules using bis-catechol-PNIPAM and trivalent iron ions [75]. The MPN capsules exhibited reversible changes in size and shell thickness in response to temperature fluctuations. At 25 °C, the dextran release efficiency reached 70% within 90 min. Guo et al. incorporated benzotriazole and PNIPAM into MPNs to produce temperature-responsive nanoparticles with antibacterial properties, achieving an inhibition rate of 80% against both Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) [85]. Moreover, temperature-responsive MPNs have been utilized to address infectious bone defects. For instance, S. Yang et al. developed a temperature-responsive shape-memory scaffold (Fig. 4E) [77]. This scaffold could adaptively bond with the defect interface at the physiological temperature of 37 °C. The MPN coating not only demonstrated capability in clearing pathogens but also regulated the immune microenvironment while promoting bone regeneration.

Adsorption properties

The adhesion properties of MPNs primarily arise from the polyphenols incorporated within them. Many polyphenols possess catechol chemical structures, which typically confer excellent interfacial adhesion capabilities to MPNs, enabling them to effectively adsorb onto various substrates [86]. The adsorption capacity can be categorized into three main aspects. First, there is adsorption on inorganic materials, where MPNs form coatings on inorganic substrates such as silicon wafers through hydrogen bonding (H-bonds) and metal coordination bond interactions. Second, MPNs demonstrate strong adsorption affinity for organic polymers, with catechol exhibiting a notable affinity for organic surfaces. Third, MPNs are capable of adsorbing dyes and heavy metals, effectively removing pollutants from the environment. Additionally, MPNs exhibit high adhesion properties to bacteria. Li et al. utilized this characteristic to create microcapsules of ionic liquid choline and geranic acid, encapsulated by adhesive MPNs (Fig. 4F) [15]. The interface of MPNs stabilized the emulsion while effectively adhering to bacterial surfaces. The presence of MPNs can compromise bacterial cell walls, promote the release of intracellular contents, and disrupt biological membranes, thus exerting a potent bactericidal effect (Fig. 4G) [15].

Ultraviolet (UV) resistance

Due to the strong oxidizing nature of UV radiation, it can induce lipid peroxidation, DNA damage, protein denaturation, and cytochrome degradation, significantly impairing the activity of sensitive BCs and potentially leading to their degradation. MPNs exhibit excellent UV shielding properties, offering a considerable advantage in protecting and delivering sensitive BCs [87]. The UV resistance of MPNs is attributed to the strong absorption of UV radiation by the aromatic structures of phenolic ligands. Furthermore, charge transfer complexes formed between phenolic ligands and metal ions can synergistically diminish UV effects within the spectral range of 200-400 nm. Park et al. developed a novel UV-resistant and antioxidant Pickering emulsion system by integrating MPNs formed from TA and zinc ions into functionalized cellulose nanofibers (Fig. 5A) [88]. In reconstructed human skin models, this system significantly reduced UV-induced oxidative stress, inhibited the expression of MMP-1, and preserved the integrity of the stratum corneum. Moreover, the system not only demonstrated exceptional interface stability but also exhibited outstanding antioxidant properties, retaining 94% of α-tocopherol over 50 days and reducing cellular ROS levels by 80% [88]. During the delivery of BCs, MPNs can serve as an outer coating, directly encapsulating sensitive BCs to prevent photo-oxidation caused by UV exposure while mitigating the migration issues associated with traditional additives. Antioxidant

The antioxidant properties of MPNs are primarily attributed to the phenolic ligands contained within them. These phenolic ligands eliminate free radicals through several mechanisms, including single electron transfer (SET), sequential proton loss electron transfer (SPLET), hydrogen atom transfer (HAT), and transition metal chelation (TMC) (Fig. 5B) [89]. SET involves phenolic ligands, not only providing hydrogen atoms but also donating electrons to exhibit antioxidant activity. During this process, free radicals accept electrons from the phenolic ligands, transforming into energy-stable anions with an even number of electrons, while the phenolic ligands themselves form stable cations [91]. In addition to donating electrons in neutral solutions, polyphenols can ionize hydrogen ions in alkaline solutions or polar solvents, converting into phenolic anions. Through these anions, oxygen free radicals can be directly converted into anions via the SET mechanism [91]. SPLET, on the other hand, refers to the process where phenolic ligands first donate protons, leading to the formation of anions, followed by the donation of electrons. When the free radical acquires electrons, its originally unpaired electrons pair up to form a complete electron pair, resulting in the radical being transformed into a negatively charged anion. This mechanism is particularly effective in polar solutions and is believed to occur for nearly all phenolic ligands in such environments due to their inherent polarity, which facilitates proton donation [89]. In the HAT mechanism, the hydroxyl group of phenolic ligands can dissociate to release hydrogen atoms in non-polar solvents. Each hydrogen atom carries one electron, which can pair with an unpaired electron in a free radical, forming a new covalent bond. Consequently, free radicals are converted into stable molecules, leading to a significant decrease in their reactivity [92]. In addition, phenolic ligands can also exert antioxidant activity through the TMC mechanism. The Fenton reaction involving some low oxidation state (such as divalent iron ions) is one of the main sources of free radicals [38]. This reaction produces highly reactive alkoxyl and/or hydroxyl radicals. When metal ions are not bound to proteins or chelating agents, this reaction can be catalyzed by transition metal ions such as copper, manganese, and cobalt. Metal chelating agents can block metal ions to reduce the occurrence of the Fenton reaction. Polyphenols act as preventive antioxidants through the TMC mechanism. In this mechanism, polyphenols capture metal ions and form stable chelates. Metal ions participate less in the Fenton reaction and reduce the generation of free radicals [93]. Interestingly, it had been reported that increasing the content of Fe3+ could enhance the antioxidant efficiency of MPNs [90]. At relatively high concentrations of Fe3+, the scavenging activity against DPPH approached 98%. Owing to this remarkable antioxidant activity, TA-iron MPNs have been employed to enhance the performance of corn starch/soy protein isolate films (Fig. 5C and D) [90]. This active food packaging material effectively protects strawberries during storage. The mechanism by which increasing pro-oxidative metal ions enhances the antioxidant activity of polyphenols may be related to the high or low ionic potential of the metal. It is speculated that high ionic potential metal enhances antioxidant performance by stabilizing electron charge, while low ionic potential metals will disrupt the electron distribution to reduce efficacy [94]. The exact mechanism should be explored in depth in the future.

Antimicrobial

The building blocks of MPNs, which include phenolic ligands and metal ions, both demonstrate antibacterial activity. Most polyphenols possess intrinsic antibacterial properties that are closely associated with their structural characteristics and the quantity of phenolic hydroxyl groups present. For instance, the inhibitory effect of TA on Escherichia coli (E. coli) and S. aureus is dependent on the concentration of phenolic hydroxyl groups; higher concentrations of TA result in stronger antibacterial effects and longer durations of antibacterial activity [95]. The antibacterial mechanisms employed by polyphenols encompass various strategies, including the inhibition of biofilm formation (Fig. 6A), interaction with bacterial cell walls or membranes leading to their destruction, inhibition of multiple bacterial enzymes, deprivation of substrates to reduce the expression of specific genes (which hinders the production of proteins necessary for quorum sensing), direct effects on DNA, RNA, and key proteins, as well as chelation of metal ions resulting in their loss (Fig. 6B) [96]. Furthermore, metal ions also serve as antibacterial agents in various material forms. Certain metal ions effectively inhibit the growth of microorganisms. For instance, noble metals such as silver (Ag) and gold (Au) have been extensively utilized in various antibacterial applications due to their high toxicity to most bacteria, exhibiting notable antibacterial and bactericidal activity even at very low concentrations [98]. Transition metal ions such as Cu2+, Fe3+, Zn2+, Ti4+, and Co2+ also demonstrate broad-spectrum antibacterial effects. The antibacterial mechanisms of these metal ions typically involve interactions with the bacterial cell membrane, causing rupturing and leakage of cellular contents; stimulation of ROS, leading to bacterial damage; and binding with proteins, DNA, peptides, and other biomolecules, resulting in functional impairment (Fig. 6C) [18]. Phenolic ligands can exert a synergistic antibacterial effect when combined with metal ions. Numerous studies have indicated that MPNs display superior antibacterial activity compared to free polyphenols [99]. This enhanced efficacy is likely due to increased lipophilicity of the complexes, facilitating better penetration into the cell membrane, thus improving antibacterial properties. For example, Yu et al. designed antibacterial MPN nanoparticles utilizing Cu2+ and natural polyphenols (Fig. 6D) [97]. These nanoparticles exhibited lower antibacterial concentrations than most reported nanomaterials containing Cu2+, with antibacterial concentrations that could be 10 to 1000 times lower than comparable materials. Additionally, these MPN nanoparticles demonstrated negligible toxicity across various models. The mechanism of loading BCs into MPNs

In addition to polyphenols, many other BCs, such as proteins, polysaccharides, lipids and probiotics, can also be loaded into MPNs. During the loading process of BCs into the MPNs system, non-covalent interaction forces play a significant role, including H-bonds, multi-point H-bonds, hydrophobic interaction forces, and electrostatic interaction forces [47]. These interactions mainly occur between the phenolic ligands and BCs. Multi-point H-bond binding is the most important binding mode between phenolic ligands and active proteins. In this interaction, the phenolic ligands bonds with peptide groups in active protein molecular skeleton through double H-bonds at the ortho-phenolic hydroxyl group. The carbonyl oxygen in active proteins acts as the H acceptor, while the H in the phenolic hydroxyl group serves as the H donor. Another H-bonding mode between the phenolic ligands and active protein is that the H atom on the N atom in active protein serves as the H donor, and the other phenolic hydroxyl O acts as the H acceptor, as shown in Fig. 7A [23]. Consequently, the loading of active proteins into MPNs is driven by multi-point H-bonds. It should be noted that the active protein can only be loaded in a proton donor solvent such as acetic acid or water. When solution contains alkali, making the solution a proton complex, the H-bond weakens, which is manifested as a weakened binding with active proteins [103]. Additionally, hydrophobic combination is another driving force. In aqueous solutions, a repulsive force exists between hydrophobic groups and water molecules. When these two hydrophobic groups approach each other, they will combine, and the water molecules will displace from the non-polar interface between the interacting groups, which results in a decrease in the free enthalpy of the system and an increase in its entropy. This contact between the two hydrophobic groups stabilizes the interaction, as shown in Fig. 7B [23]. For instance, GA groups are hydrophobic groups that can well form hydrophobic associations with the hydrophobic positions in active proteins, thereby stably loading the active proteins within the interior of MPNs. In addition, proline in active proteins is a kind of amino acid with the special structure. The side chain of proline forms a pyrrole ring structure with peptide bonds (Fig. 7C). This structure endows the side chains of active proteins with strong hydrophobicity, while also restricting the free rotation of peptide bonds. When forming polypeptides, only cis-structures can be formed, and there is no H on the N atom. This is conducive to H-bonding with phenolic ligands, which provides a stronger force for the loading of active proteins [104]. The loading mode of polysaccharides into MPNs is similar to that of proteins. However, due to the complexity of the polysaccharide spatial structure, there are also differences in their binding with phenolic ligands. Polysaccharides are mainly loaded through forming H-bonds between phenolic ligands, water molecules and glucose residues [105]. Although polysaccharides contain almost no hydrophobic groups, some polysaccharides usually have helical or hydrophobic hole structures, thus possessing a strong complexing ability with phenolic ligands and ultimately being able to load polysaccharides easily [106]. For fatty BCs, MPNs typically do not interact directly with them. For the loading of these BCs, MPNs is utilized to form a stable network at the oil-water interface. These MPNs-stabilized emulsions can effectively prevent phase separation and droplet aggregation. Moreover, MPNs also act as a barrier against free radicals and oxygen, effectively protecting lipids from oxidation and prolonging their shelf life [107]. In addition, for other hydrophobic BCs (such as vitamins), they can also be loaded into MPNs through hydrophobic interactions and π-π stacking. The long phytyl tail chain of vitamin E and the conjugated isoprene chain of vitamin A will spontaneously approach the hydrophobic benzene ring skeleton of polyphenols in the aqueous phase environment. By resisting contact with water molecules, a hydrophobic association structure is ultimately formed [108,109]. This loading can anchor the fat-soluble vitamins in the hydrophobic microenvironment of MPN, reducing their contact with the harsh external environment. The Chroman ring of vitamin E and the conjugated polyene structure of vitamin A carry a delocalized π-electron system. The aromatic/conjugated plane will stack with the benzene ring in a nearly parallel and misaligned arrangement. Non-covalent weak interactions are formed through the dispersive force between π electrons [108,109]. This effect further strengthens the combination of vitamins and polyphenols, allowing the phenolic hydroxyl groups to come into closer contact with vitamin E and vitamin A. Therefore, polyphenol quenches the surrounding free radicals to prevent fat-soluble vitamins from being oxidized and consumed. There are many factors that affect the encapsulation efficiency of MPNs, such as the type and valence state of metal ions, the type of polyphenols, the amount of MPNs used, the type of solvent, and the external environment for encapsulation. Compared with divalent metals (Cu2+, Zn2+), trivalent Fe3+ and tetravalent Zr4+ have multidentate coordination capabilities, which can form bidentate/tridentate stable coordination structures with polyphenols, enhancing the density of the MPNs network and reducing the leakage of BCs with small molecular weight [13,22]. In addition, polyphenols with a large number of phenolic hydroxyl groups can form a more stable network structure with metal ions, which also reduces the leakage of BCs in complex environments. Kou et al. built MPNs to load catechins [111]. The encapsulation efficiency of the gel based on MPNs for catechins was 62.7%. However, the encapsulation rate of catechins by gels without iron ions was less than 50%. In addition, gels based on MPNs also had good stability in gastric juice. Its release of catechins was only 13.5% in the gastric phase, effectively protecting catechins from degradation. The release of catechins in the intestinal phase was 64.1%, demonstrating the protective and targeted release capabilities of MPNs for BCs [111]. In addition, the usage of MPNs also affects the loading efficiency of BCs. When the dosage of EGCG and Fe3+ was 2 mL, the encapsulation rate of cinnamaldehyde by MPNs was approximately 85% [112]. As the dosage of EGCG and Fe3+ decreases, the encapsulation rate of cinnamaldehyde would be lower than 65%. In addition, the bioaccessibility of cinnamaldehyde loaded by MPNs could reach 28.9%, while as the dosage of EGCG and Fe3+ decreases, the accessibility of cinnamaldehyde was only 15.5%. This demonstrated good stability during gastrointestinal transportation.

MPNs as advanced delivery platforms for BCs

Although BCs have many health benefits, their low bioavailability and instability significantly hinder their effective utilization in food and medicine systems [113]. BCs are usually susceptible to environmental degradation and metabolic decomposition and have unfavorable physicochemical properties such as astringency and bitterness. MPNs has many advantages and can effectively solve those problems in the application process of BCs. For instance, the synthesis conditions of MPNs are mild, which can minimize the denaturation of BCs. Various BCs can be easily incorporated into the interior of MPNs through supramolecular interaction [[12], [13], [14]]. Therefore, MPNs is an effective delivery platform for various BCs, including polyphenols, active proteins, polysaccharides, and probiotics. The conventional systems currently used for BCs delivery include nanoparticles, films/coatings/capsules, emulsions, and hydrogels. These systems offer solutions through chemical and physical protection, facilitating storing in harsh environments, crossing biological barriers, targeted delivery, and controlled release [5]. Herein, the application of MPNs in these different systems is highlighted to better promote the development of BCs delivery. Nanoparticles

The delivery of BCs in the form of nanoparticles by MPNs can be categorized into two cases, pure MPNs and the incorporation of other BCs onto MPNs. The BCs delivered by the pure MPNs nanoparticles are mainly polyphenols and metal ions that constitute MPNs. Although polyphenols are an important type of BCs, they have the characteristics of poor stability, low permeability and low bioavailability [5]. Pure MPNs nanoparticles assembled from polyphenols and metal ions not only address these challenges but also may exert a synergistic effect with metal ions, further enhancing the therapeutic effect. In the research conducted by Q. Zhang et al., single ultrasmall MPNs were prepared using Fe3+ and GA for the treatment of calcium oxalate (CaOx) nephropathies (Fig. 7D) [100]. These MPNs could specifically adsorb onto the high-energy crystal face to inhibit the growth of CaOx monohydrate, which promoted the transformation of highly toxic CaOx monohydrate into low-risk CaOx dihydrate. Furthermore, MPNs possessed the capacity to scavenge free radicals, mitigate oxidative damage and inhibit cell apoptosis. MPNs also showed sensitivity to kidney injury, aggregating in damaged renal tissue, improving tubular function, reducing tubular damage and inflammation, and inhibiting crystal formation. These effects cannot be achieved by using GA or Fe3+ alone [100]. Additionally, pure MPNs nanoparticles are also used to treat osteoporosis. Chen et al. developed multifunctional MPNs nanoparticles by mixed stirring using bioactive metals (strontium ions) and functionalized phenols (EGCG) (Fig. 7E) [101]. In the microenvironment of osteoporosis, MPNs nanoparticles underwent degradation due to the acidic environment. The release of strontium ions could up-regulate the expression of osteogenic genes, thereby enhancing the differentiation and mineralization of pre-osteoblasts. The release of EGCG could down-regulate the expression of osteoclast genes, significantly inhibiting the activation and formation of pre-osteoclasts. Compared with the use of polyphenols and metal ions alone, MPNs retained more of the trabecular pattern. Multi-component MPNs had synergistic effects in inhibiting osteoclastogenesis and promoting osteogenesis to regulate unbalanced bone remodeling [101].

In addition to pure MPNs nanoparticles, MPNs can also serve as a delivery platform to deliver other BCs. For instance, CpG-oligodeoxynucleotide (CpG-ODN) can activate NF-κB signaling and induce the release of pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-6, IL-12, and TNF-α [102]. However, CpG-ODN carries a negative charge and has a weak ability to be internalized into macrophages. In order to address the limitations of CpG-ODN applications, Han et al. prepared MPNs by coordinating manganese ions with TA, and then encapsulated CpG-ODN internally through H-bonds to fabricate nanoparticles, as shown in Fig. 7F [102]. When combined with irreversible electroporation, nanoparticles could be effectively internalized into macrophages and successfully triggered M1 polarization to release pro-inflammatory factors.

Films/coatings/capsules

The films/coatings/capsules share a common characteristic that they form a thin coating on the surface of BCs to encapsulate them. The mechanism is that MPNs are applied in the form of coatings. Therefore, Films/coatings/capsules will be introduced together. Phenolic ligands tend to exhibit high adhesion to various substrates, whether hydrophilic or hydrophobic, and coordinate with metal ions. Therefore, MPNs have been utilized for BCs delivery in the form of coatings [133]. Due to the heterogeneous structures of polyphenols and the random complexation, most MPNs coatings are amorphous. MPNs can be coated on cargoes of almost all sizes, from nano to macro. For instance, the size of antibodies is several nanometers. By binding to specific antigens on the cell surface, they can induce or inhibit signaling pathways, activate the immune system to suppress cell proliferation, and play a crucial role in the treatment of some diseases [11]. However, the limited cellular uptake and inefficient endosomal escape of antibodies have hindered their broader application. Honda et al. prepared MPNs and encapsulated antibodies to fabricate core-shell structured complexes (Fig. 8A) [134]. This complex had a diameter of approximately 30 nm and exhibited excellent pharmacokinetics and aggregation in tumors after intravenous injection. After being taken up by cells through endocytosis, MPNs facilitated endocytic escape and antibody release. With the assistance of MPNs, the complex could enhance the therapeutic effect of antibodies against orthotopic breast tumors in mice [134]. Besides, Q. Probiotics are a special type of BCs that have the functions of regulating blood sugar metabolism, improving lactose intolerance, and maintaining the balance of intestinal flora [137]. However, oral administration of probiotics can lead to a decline in their quantity and activity, and ultimately only a small portion of the probiotics can colonize the intestines [137,138]. Emulsion

The emulsion is a kind of colloid delivery system characterized by the dispersion of one phase in the form of small droplets within another immiscible phase. These droplets are widely used as carriers to enhance the stability, dispersibility and absorption of BCs [7]. However, traditional emulsions are prone to physical instabilities (such as coalescence, phase separation, and flocculation), particularly when exposed to processing and storage environments such as heating, freezing, and drying, as well as during the digestion and absorption process in the gastrointestinal tract [113]. These limitations hinder the application of emulsion delivery BCs. MPNs has amphiphilic properties and can effectively enhance the properties of emulsions. Therefore, Lan et al. used EGCG, Fe3+, and soy protein to synthesize nanoparticles to stabilize the emulsion (Fig. 10A) [112]. This emulsion delivery system had smaller droplet sizes, higher moduli, and a higher encapsulation rate for cinnamaldehyde. Furthermore, these nanoparticles containing MPNs had a narrower particle size distribution, stronger free radical scavenging ability, and better thermal stability [112]. In addition, it is worth mentioning that some MPNs themselves have good emulsifying properties and can be directly employed as emulsifiers. Hu et al. adopted the stepwise interfacial assembly strategy to construct MPNs-based emulsifiers (Fig. 10B) [107]. Specifically, TA was first used to form an emulsion, and then metal ions were added in situ to form MPNs. Experimental results indicated that higher concentrations of TA were conducive to form larger MPNs, and the corresponding emulsions exhibited the best stability, showing excellent stability within a 21-day storage period. In comparison to TA alone, the adsorption layer of MPNs at the interface was denser. These findings provided novel insights into the design of multifunctional emulsions and the delivery of BCs [107]. Pickering emulsion is defined as an emulsion stabilized by colloidal particles as emulsifiers. MPNs can also enhance the stability of Pickering emulsions and develop multifunctional Pickering emulsions. For example, the surface of silica was functionalized by using MPNs coatings (Fig. 10C) [139]. This could regulate the surface charge and wettability of silica nanoparticles and could serve as emulsifier for Pickering emulsions. Hydrogels

Hydrogel is a kind of gel with a three-dimensional network structure. It has many advantages in delivering BCs, such as biocompatibility, hydrophilicity, and simple construction methods [5]. More importantly, its three-dimensional network structure can easily load BCs, basically not restricted by the molecular structure, molecular weight and type of BCs [140]. The combined design of MPNs and hydrogel can enhance the mechanical strength, responsiveness and adhesion of the gel, protect BCs from harsh environments, and promote the controlled release of BCs [141]. These features jointly establish multiple protection mechanisms, ensuring the stable and efficient delivery of sensitive BCs. Liu et al. prepared microgels based on cellulose nanofibers by the synergistic effect of MPNs and Fe3+ coordination cross-linking (Fig. 11A) [142]. This microgel could effectively encapsulate unstable BCs folic acid, and its encapsulation rate could reach 88%. Furthermore, this microgel could control the release of folic acid and increase its bioavailability to 91%. The incorporation of MPNs also endowed this microgel with remarkable antioxidant and antibacterial properties [142]. In addition, MPNs can also enhance the mechanical properties of the gel. After EGCG and metal ions were prepared into MPNs, they could be introduced into soy protein isolate solution to prepare enhanced composite hydrogels. The H-bond between MPNs and soy protein isolate caused the smaller soy protein isolate molecules to aggregate into larger protein clusters. Low-field magnetic resonance imaging indicated that the composite hydrogels retained more water. The addition of MPNs significantly enhanced the mechanical properties of the hydrogels, with G′ being 2.1 times higher than that of soy protein hydrogels [144]. The safety of MPNs and their final fate in the human body

In the practical application of MPNs, a very important issue is their toxicity, including biocompatibility and biosafety. For instance, although metal ions are essential for the human body, they may still have long-term toxicity. The biocompatibility of MPNs can be evaluated through both in vivo and in vitro assessments. At present, many experiments have confirmed that MPNs have good biocompatibility and minimal cytotoxicity [145]. MPNs self-assembled from natural polyphenols and Cu2+ possessed excellent antibacterial properties and had a lower Cu2+ antibacterial concentration than most reported nanomaterials (Fig. 12A) [97]. These different concentrations of MPNs were incubated together with mouse NIH/3T3 cells and human umbilical vein endothelial cells. Even at high concentrations, the toxicity of the two cell lines could be ignored. The hemolysis test could evaluate the biocompatibility of MPNs. If MPNs have a destructive effect on the red blood cells membrane in mice, red blood cells would absorb water, expand and rupture, and release hemoglobin. Ultimately, a clear red solution will be formed. The results showed that MPNs did not cause any obvious hemolysis even at high concentrations (20-60 μM), indicating its high blood biocompatibility. In the zebrafish embryo acute toxicity test, the morphology of zebrafish did not change significantly. MPNs had no effect on the movement behavior of zebrafish either. It indicated that the cytotoxicity of MPNs could be ignored (Fig. 12B–G) [97]. In addition to pure MPNs, the complexes of MPNs with other substances also have good biocompatibility and biosafety. To alleviate the drug resistance and the side effects of chemotherapy drugs in patients with non-small cell lung cancer, multifunctional nanocomposites were prepared [146]. During the treatment period, HE staining images showed that the nanocomposites did not cause significant damage to heart, liver, spleen, lungs, or kidneys. Hemolysis experiments had shown that the nanocomposites were compatible with red blood cells even at high concentrations (Fig. 12I). Aspartate transaminase and alanine transaminase levels indicated that the liver function of mice remained normal during continuous treatment. The final fate of MPNs in the human body is also an important aspect of their safety. Their trajectory can be divided into four different processes, namely administration, distribution, metabolism and excretion, collectively referred to as ADME (Fig. 12J) [35]. The main administration method of MPNs is oral administration, while the use of injection, inhalation and dermal administration is still relatively rare. After oral administration, the biological distribution of the delivery system is a key process that directly affects the efficacy of BCs. Oral administration usually targets organs such as the stomach, small intestine, and colon, and can treat many gastrointestinal related diseases. After entering the bloodstream, the MPNs-based delivery platform can be distributed in the body through passive targeting, active targeting, and cellular targeting [147]. The optimal distribution of MPNs in the human body should prioritize their targeting in damaged tissues, thereby minimizing toxic effects on normal tissues and organs. Cells can internalize MPNs through two independent endocytic pathways, pinocytosis and phagocytosis [148]. Pinocytosis is a process typically involving the uptake of particles by small vesicles with a diameter of less than 0.15 μm. Phagocytosis refers to the internalization process of larger particles with a diameter greater than 0.25 μm through phagosomes [149]. After entering the human body, the organism will eliminate nanoparticles through various pathways, including gastrointestinal tract clearance, mucociliary clearance, renal clearance, hepatic clearance and clearance by the reticuloendothelial system [150]. MPNs are sensitive to external stimuli, and they are prone to degradation during metabolism and then excreted from the body. Overall, MPNs exhibit good biocompatibility and biosafety, are not easy to accumulate in the human body, and represent a relatively safe delivery platform.

The application of artificial intelligence in MPNs

The building blocks of MPNs molecules are both extensive and modular. The phenolic ligands alone contain over 8000 different molecules found in nature and an almost unlimited number of theoretically synthesized phenolic ligands [151]. In the periodic table, 87 metal ions and 6 metalloids all have empty orbitals in their electronic configurations, which can interact with phenolic ligands in various ways [13]. Theoretically, there are a great variety of MPNs. Different types and characteristics of BCs often require different MPNs for protection and delivery. In this case, manual experimental screening of polyphenol bank and metal ions is usually time-consuming and laborious. Therefore, there are many challenges in effectively and quickly screening MPNs with specific functions from a broad combination and using them as delivery platforms to deliver BCs.

With the rapid development of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning, this problem is expected to be effectively addressed. In recent years, AI has developed rapidly, including image recognition, computer vision, and natural language processing [152]. Especially the recently emerged large language models and generative AI have already surpassed the capabilities of human intelligence in some aspects. The structure of polyphenols is complex, featuring a variety of different basic carbon skeletons and an irregular distribution of hydroxyl groups. In addition, the degree of polymerization of monomers into polyphenols varies and chiral stereoisomerism also exists. These variations may have led to different binding capabilities with metal ions. The identification of polyphenol structures by AI often requires the combination of multiple spectral techniques. The information involved in these spectral techniques includes frequency range, peak area, peak spacing, number of peaks, and maximum absorbance peak [153]. The application of AI in the identification of polyphenol structures mainly involves three methods [154]. Firstly, from molecules to spectra, this involves spectral prediction and then identifying candidate polyphenols in the database. Secondly, from spectrum to structure. Investigate and predict the structure based on spectral data. Thirdly, the de novo spectral generation of polyphenol molecules. R. Li et al. developed an AI-assisted polyphenol structure identification system [155]. This system relied on phenylboronic acid-functionalized nanopore to recognize the single-molecule structural response of polyphenols. In this system, there was a high correlation between gallic acid acylation, stereochemical changes, hydroxylation patterns and current fingerprint patterns, thus enabling the clear distinction of structurally similar polyphenols. Ultimately, it was found that with the help of AI classification, the recognition efficiency of catechins could reach 96.9%. In addition, in this system, the binding information of polyphenols and metal ions could also be identified. For instance, in this system, it was found that the order of EGCG's binding ability with metal ions was Cu2+ > Pb2+ > Mg2+ > Ca2+. Moreover, EGCG preferentially coordinated with Pb2+ through B-ring diol [155]. This AI-assisted system has established a universal strategy for the chelation recognition of polyphenols and multiple metal ions based on current fingerprint patterns.

AI has significant value in analyzing complex biological systems, screening the key ligands, identifying disease biomarkers and potential targets, simulating the interaction between delivery systems and cargoes, and predicting the safety and effectiveness of delivery systems [156]. However, there are two key challenges in the application of AI in the MPNs field. 1) The diversity of MPNs materials reported in the current literature is limited, resulting in insufficient database resources. 2) the inadequacy of conventional molecular feature representations to capture the unique characteristics of MPNs. To address these challenges, D. Fan et al. constructed a comprehensive database of MPN photothermal properties (Fig. 13A) [157]. The constructed database was used for training and evaluating multiple machine learning models. The XGBoost model demonstrated superior performance across F1-score metrics, recall, and macro precision. The trained XGBoost model was applied to the virtual database, and 1654 MPNs with high photothermal potential were identified. The experimental results verified that the accuracy of the high-photothermal MPNs identified by this model is 70%. In addition, several previously unreported high-photothermal MPNs were discovered, demonstrating their advantages in photothermal antibacterial applications [157]. GPT-4 excels at integrating interdisciplinary information and providing an intuitive interface, making the information more accessible. Furthermore, the adaptability and capability of GPT-4 for precise customization for therapeutic screening tasks enhance the operational efficiency and scalability. Q. Fan et al. developed a large language model-assisted metal–phenolic network (LLMPN) nanoplatform using GPT-4 (Fig. 13B) [158]. The LLMPN was used for targeted screening of phenolic building blocks that could inhibit the development of osteosarcoma. Multiple correlations revealed that among over 60 polyphenol candidates, gossypol might exhibit excellent anti-osteosarcoma activity. In vitro experiments further confirmed the highly efficient inhibitory ability of gossypol on osteosarcoma cells. Subsequently, Cu2+ coordinated with the hydroxyl groups in gossypol to form MPNs. Hyaluronic acid was used to modify the surface of MPNs to enhance the stability and targeting of the system. Experiments had found that MPNs-based nanoparticles were preferentially internalized by osteosarcoma cells. Due to the acidic environment in lysosomes, MPNs dissociated and released gossypol and Cu2+, effectively disrupting genetic mechanisms of the osteosarcoma cells and leading to the genotoxic cell death [158]. In addition, MPNs had excellent adhesion properties and could adhere to the surfaces of organic, inorganic and biological entities in a short period of time. Ye et al. integrated the MPNs-mediated separation and machine learning-assisted surface-enhanced Raman scattering method for the accurate classification and high-precision quantification of nano-plastics, as shown in Fig. 13C [159]. The key issues that AI may need to focus on solving in the future include optimizing the coordination structure of MPNs, predicting assembly outcomes, and customizing functional attributes. For instance, Y. Chen et al. recently customized MPNs with highly efficient antioxidant properties to treat atherosclerosis [160]. In this study, an AI-assisted strategy was developed. Four kinds of anti-atherosclerotic polyphenols were identified by combining network pharmacology and quantitative activity relationship modeling, including protocatechuic acid, salvianic acid A, salvianolic acid B, and hydroxysafflor yellow A. Subsequently, quaternary MPNs was designed. The optimized MPNs demonstrated good efficiency in inhibiting lipid uptake and accumulation in RAW 264.7 cells and mediating cholesterol effluence. Besides, MPNs could also improve lipid metabolism, reduce oxidative stress and inflammation, and effectively treat atherosclerosis [160]. In addition, Cheng et al. also customized a colorimetric sensor array based on MPNs that could respond to the freshness of meat [161]. The multi-level competitive coordination between MPNs and amine substances endowed this system with color variations far exceeding those of traditional dyes. After combining with the convolutional neural network, the overall detection accuracy rate reached 99.83%. However, perhaps because the application of AI in MPNs is still in its infancy, there are currently few reports on its application in other aspects of MPNs. This also indicates that more scientists from different fields should collaborate in the future to promote the application of AI in MPNs.

It is worth mentioning that, in addition to screening specific functions of MPNs, AI may also effectively analyze the combination and release dynamics of BCs and MPNs. The combination and release mechanism of BCs is the basis for applying the MPNs delivery platform. Multi-scale simulations and AI-assisted modeling can quantify binding free energies, diffusion, and kinetic barriers, linking macroscopic release behaviors to molecular interactions [162]. Furthermore, the computational model not only clarifies these processes but also provides predictive criteria that directly guide the design of experiments and the optimization of formulas. The binding strength, conformational dynamics and stimulus-response processes can be quantified at the molecular level through molecular dynamics simulations, quantum-chemical approaches and free-energy calculations [156]. This enables theoretical descriptions to be transformed into experimentally measurable parameters, such as the binding constant, release rate, and pharmacokinetic half-life of BCs [163]. MPNs and BCs often interact through non-covalent bonds. In non-covalent systems, hydrophobic interactions, H-bonds, and electrostatic interactions work together to stabilize BCs, while allowing for reversible release under appropriate stimulation, such as acidic pH. The simulation results provide quantitative thresholds, which can offer reliable information for the selection of building block types, size control and surface chemical properties of the MPNs platform in practice [164]. Overall, insights led by AI are gradually evolving from post-mechanical interpretation into the active design of MPNs-based delivery platforms. The calculated binding free energy and barriers depict the feasible range for BCs loading and release. The activation energy and rate constant provide pre-synthetic criteria for selecting linker types and substituents [152]. Researchers are establishing a predictive and quantitative workflow that links energy landscapes, macroscopic performance, and molecular structure. This is achieved by mapping the computational load to the experimentally observable values. This AI simulation-driven framework may drive a leapfrog development of the MPNs delivery platform.

The advantages and limitations of MPNs as a delivery platform

In comparison to conventional delivery systems like liposomes, poly(lactide-co-glycolide) (PLGA) nanoparticles, chitosan-based carriers, and zein nanoparticles [165], MPNs present unique advantages and certain limitations as a delivery platform. MPNs confer several distinct advantages. Firstly, the multifunctionality of both metal ions and polyphenols is notable. Polyphenols exhibit properties such as antioxidation, anti-inflammation, antibacterial effects, and the ability to eliminate ROS [23]. Certain metal ions also possess antibacterial properties and can engage in Fenton/Fenton-like catalytic reactions [38,166]. These functionalities are not found in liposomes, zein, or PLGA. Secondly, MPNs display superior adhesive properties, allowing them to easily adhere to a variety of substrates at both microscopic and macroscopic scales. The phenolic hydroxyl groups of polyphenols facilitate hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, coordination interactions, and π-π interactions with diverse matrices [23]. In contrast, the interaction modes between chitosan, liposomes, or zein and other substances tend to be simpler and weaker [165]. Thirdly, MPNs exhibit high loading efficiency and can encapsulate a wide array of BCs, ranging from nanometer to micrometer sizes. In contrast, liposomes typically require BCs to be smaller, such as polypeptides, small molecule proteins, or nucleic acids, which increases the risk of leakage [5]. Fourthly, the preparation conditions for MPNs are characterized as green, mild, simple, and devoid of residual organic solvents. The synthesis of PLGA, on the other hand, often involves toxic solvents such as dichloromethane and acetone. Zein frequently necessitates crosslinking agents to enhance encapsulation efficiency. Lastly, MPNs can respond to a broader range of external environmental stimuli, including pH, GSH, ATP, ROS, enzymes, and temperature. Conversely, the range of environmental changes that other delivery systems can accommodate is typically more limited [167].

Despite their advantages, MPNs also present several drawbacks. Firstly, the most critical concern is the need for comprehensive and long-term evaluation of the safety of MPNs. Prolonged intake of MPNs may result in the accumulation of metal ions within the human body [168]. Metals such as aluminum, rare earth elements, and heavy metals (e.g., Cd, Pb) are excreted slowly, leading to potential continuous deposition in the brain, liver, kidneys, and bones. Additionally, when essential metals like Fe, Cu, or Zn exceed their metabolic thresholds, they may induce overload poisoning. Transition metals (Fe3+, Cu2+, Mn2+) can trigger the Fenton reaction, resulting in oxidative stress [34]. Furthermore, current research on MPNs is primarily limited to in vitro and small animal studies, with a lack of long-term in vivo data regarding metabolism, accumulation, chronic toxicity, and genotoxicity, which hampers their clinical application [169]. Secondly, the circulation stability of MPNs in vivo is inferior to that of PLGA. PLGA can circulate in the body for several days to months, providing an advantage in achieving sustained release [170]. The phenolic hydroxyl groups present in MPNs are susceptible to adsorption with plasma proteins, leading to clearance by the mononuclear phagocytic system. Thirdly, the stability of MPNs during storage requires further enhancement. Given their sensitivity to various external environments, MPNs may degrade or aggregate during storage and transportation, impacting their functional efficacy. Fourthly, the metal ions and polyphenols used in the currently research are mostly high-purity reagents. Reagents with impurities may affect the synthesis efficiency of MPN. Therefore, from the perspective of techno-economic analysis (TEA), higher costs may be incurred in large-scale production in the future.

Fifthly, from the perspective of life cycle assessment (LCA), the extensive use of polyphenols and metal ions in industry may impose additional environmental burdens. Polyphenols are commonly obtained by liquid-liquid and solid-liquid extraction methods [171]. The solvents used in these extraction methods include acetone, alcohols (ethanol and methanol), ethyl acetate, and diethyl ether. In addition, pure organic solvents cannot be used to extract phenolic acids with polarity. In this case, ethanol-water or acetone-water mixtures are commonly used. For highly non-polar polyphenols, chloroform, dichloromethane, hexane and benzene are commonly used [172]. The extraction, separation and purification processes of metal ions often also require a large number of chemical reagents. During the flotation process of metal ions, the reagents commonly used include foaming agents (phenylethyl ester oil, terpineol oil, and methyl isobutyl methanol), collectors (xanthate, ethylthiourethane, and black medicine), activators (sodium sulfate and copper sulfate), inhibitors (zinc sulfate and sodium silicate), pH regulators (soda, lime, sodium hydroxide and sulfuric acid), flocculants and dispersants [173]. For platinum group metals, the strong oxidant hydrochloric acid is used for dissolution due to the stable property of their anionic chloride complexes. Then it was finally separated through cementation, precipitation, ion exchange, and solvent extraction [174]. The use of these organic solvents or chemical reagents not only may impose an additional burden on the environment, but also may remain in the final products and pose a threat to human health. This point requires additional attention in large-scale industrial production of MPNs.

Finally, reproducibility between batches and the controllability of large-scale production are significant challenges. The coordination assembly of MPNs is sensitive to variations in pH, metal/polyphenol ratios, and temperature, which limits production primarily to laboratory settings [169]. Industrial production may face difficulties in controlling particle size and coating thickness. In contrast, the industrial processes for liposomes, PLGA, and chitosan-based carriers are well-established, with relatively complete quality control standards [170]. In addition, the main construction methods (direct self-assembly, templating assembly, and emulsion-based self-assembly) reported in most current studies are at the laboratory level [64,86]. The output of these methods is often only a few micrograms or a few milligrams each time [175]. Many steps in these methods are difficult to apply in industrial production, including ultrasound, vortex, high-speed centrifugation, oscillation, and re-dissolution of precipitated substances. Perhaps for this reason, MPNs has not yet been industrialized and put on the market for sale. In the future industrial-scale amplification process, the following several standards may be given priority consideration. 1) The method must be robust enough to ensure high reproducibility and high yield for each batch of synthesis. 2) The protocol for manufacturing should be clear, detailed and easy to follow. Researchers with basic laboratory skills in chemistry can easily understand and make reasonable adjustments. 3) The equipment of the large-scale production line used is easy to set up and replicate. 4) It is best to be capable of continuous automated production operations. From single production in the laboratory to large-scale production in factory, manual operation can lead to time-consuming and labor-intensive work, which may hinder the wider application of MPNs. Recently, the microfluidic production of MPNs explored by Shen et al. provided a very good solution [176]. When the span of microfluidic mixing time was greater than the nucleation time of MPNs, nano-sized MPNs capsules could be continuously constructed. Experiments found that the size of MPNs synthesized by microfluidics was 100-200 nm, and the loading efficient of curcumin, vitamin D, and paclitaxel, was as high as 40-70%. The throughput of each channel was as high as 70 mg/h [176]. This method increased the synthetic flux of MPNs and provided a feasible solution for continuous synthesis with amplification potential.

Thus, significant progress is necessary for MPNs to transition from laboratory settings to clinical applications. Governmental oversight and approval processes for MPNs require strengthening. In 2021, the European Food Safety Authority introduced a structured risk assessment methodology for various matrix materials in food and nutritional products, emphasizing the need for thorough evaluations of physical and chemical properties, transparent regulatory communications, and stakeholder engagement [177]. In 2022, the FDA released guidelines encompassing key regulatory considerations such as biomaterial quality assurance, safety assessments, characterization techniques, analytical validation, manufacturing protocols, risk regulation, and ethical and environmental issue considerations. Adherence to these policies is essential for the regulation of MPNs [177]. Given that MPNs are still in their infancy, their development lags significantly behind that of more established delivery systems, such as certain liposomes, PLGA microspheres, and chitosan mucosal preparations that have already received FDA approval [167]. In addition, public acceptance of MPNs poses another barrier to commercialization. During the process of clinical translation and commercialization, it is crucial to promote the advantages of MPNs and alleviate public concerns regarding their use. Nevertheless, given the numerous advanced features inherent to MPNs, their application in clinical practice may soon contribute to human health.

Conclusions and future prospects

Utilizing MPNs for the delivery of BCs can significantly enhance their protection and utilization efficiency. The types and structures of polyphenols, as well as the types and valence states of metal ions, influence the properties of MPNs. Polyphenols and metal ions can interact through coordination bonding, cation-π interactions, redox reactions, and dynamic covalent bonding. Methods for constructing MPNs include direct self-assembly, templating assembly, and emulsion-based self-assembly. As advanced delivery platforms for BCs, MPNs offer various notable advantages, such as stimuli-responsive properties, adsorption capabilities, UV protection, antioxidant effects, and antimicrobial properties. During the loading of BCs into the MPN system, non-covalent interaction forces, including hydrogen bonds, multi-point hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, and electrostatic interactions, play a critical role. MPNs can be incorporated into nanoparticles, films, coatings, capsules, emulsions, and hydrogels to enhance BC delivery efficiency. Furthermore, MPNs exhibit favorable biocompatibility and biosafety. The integration of AI in the MPN delivery platform may facilitate substantial advancements in BC delivery. However, several issues warrant attention. In practical applications, MPNs may degrade due to structural instability during prolonged storage. Additionally, the current range of MPN building blocks is limited, presenting an opportunity for future expansion of the construction toolbox. Research on MPNs is predominantly focused at the laboratory level, with large-scale industrial application remaining a significant challenge. Interdisciplinary technological integration is currently insufficient, and the implementation of a data-driven approach to MPN design is still in its nascent stages. The stability of MPNs in the gastrointestinal tract is relatively poor, which somewhat restricts their use in BC delivery. Finally, while existing experimental data have confirmed the safety of MPNs, comprehensive investigations into metabolic fate, long-term toxicity, and human assessments are essential for further validation.
