Drug Reaction with Eosinophilia and Systemic Symptom Syndrome Due to Everolimus: A Case Report
Authors: Mehmet Erdem Çakmak
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractDrug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptom (DRESS) is a life-threatening drug hypersensitivity reaction that is characterized by skin rash, hematological abnormalities (eosinophilia, atypical lymphocytosis), lymphadenopathy, and internal organ involvement (liver, kidneys, and lung). Many drugs may cause DRESS syndrome, the most frequently reported of which are antiepileptics and allopurinol. In this case report, a patient who developed DRESS syndrome due to everolimus was presented herein.Keywords: Drug hypersensitivity, Eosinophilia, Everolimus, Hypersensitivity

INTRODUCTION

Drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptom (DRESS) is a life-threatening drug hypersensitivity reaction that is characterized by skin rash, hematological abnormalities (eosinophilia, atypical lymphocytosis), lymphadenopathy, and internal organ involvement (liver, kidneys, and lung)1. Although the incidence of DRESS syndrome is unknown, a prospective study in Western India2 estimated the annual incidence to be 0.9/100,000. Many drugs may cause DRESS syndrome, the most frequently reported of which are antiepileptic agents (carbamazepine, lamotrigine, phenytoin, phenobarbital) and allopurinol3. DRESS syndrome has a long latent period (two to eight weeks) between drug exposure and disease onset. There is no pathognomonic symptom or diagnostic test for DRESS. Appropriate clinical findings and laboratory data are required for the diagnosis of DRESS syndrome4. The most important step in the treatment of DRESS syndrome is to discontinue the use of the culprit drug. The main medication in the treatment is systemic steroids5.

In this case report, a patient with metastatic renal cell carcinoma was presented who developed DRESS syndrome due to everolimus.

CASE REPORT

A 54-year-old female patient was admitted to our allergy outpatient clinic with a pre-diagnosis of drug allergy due to complaints of itching, swelling, and shortness of breath. The patient had a history of left nephrectomy due to renal cell carcinoma five years ago. During the follow-up period, everolimus was initiated at a dose of 10 mg/day for the patient by the oncologist due to peritoneal metastasis. Skin rashes and shortness of breath developed on the 15th day of the everolimus treatment, so the treatment was discontinued by the oncologist. The patient was admitted to our allergy outpatient clinic 10 days after discontinuing the everolimus treatment. The patient had not taken any other medication for two months before or during the everolimus treatment. On physical examination, her temperature was 38.5℃, heart rate was 90 beats/min, blood pressure was 135/85 mmHg, and oxygen saturation was 95%. The patient had maculopapular rashes that had resolved on her back (Fig. 1). Examination of the intraoral and genital areas and mucous membranes were normal. In the respiratory system examination, rhonchi were present in both lung fields. Other physical examination findings were normal. The laboratory tests showed an eosinophil count of 0.8×103/µl (normal: 0~0.5×103/µl), eosinophil percentage of 13.8%, glucose level of 106 mg/dl (normal: 70~100 mg/dl), creatinine level of 1.04 md/dl (normal: 0.5~1 md/dl), alanine aminotransferase level of 91 U/L (normal: <35 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase level of 48 U/L (normal: <35 U/L), and sedimentation rate of 64 mm/h (normal: 0~30 mm/h). Right lung middle lobe and lower lobe peripheral infiltrations (Fig. 2) on thorax computed tomography were evaluated as drug-induced pneumonitis. The patient’s RegiSCAR score was five. The patient was diagnosed with DRESS syndrome and methylprednisolone was initiated at a dose of 32 mg/day. The dose of methylprednisolone was gradually reduced over three weeks, after which it was discontinued. The patient’s rashes and shortness of breath completely resolved.

The patient was admitted to our allergy clinic 10 days after everolimus treatment was discontinued, and the patient’s maculopapular skin rashes had partially resolved.

DISCUSSION

DRESS syndrome is a drug hypersensitivity reaction with eosinophilia and life-threatening systemic symptoms, which can be caused by many drugs, including antiepileptics (phenytoin, carbamazepine, phenobarbital, valproate, lamotrigine), antidepressants, sulfonamides (dapsone, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole), non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (naproxen, diclofenac, ibuprofen), antibiotics (metronidazole, spiramycin, piperacillin-tazobactam), angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors (captopril, enalapril), beta blockers (atenolol), allopurinol, dobutamine, and propylthiouracil6. In the literature, the most common drugs causing DRESS syndrome have been reported to be antiepileptics and allopurinol3. There is only one case report of DRESS syndrome due to everolimus, in which DRESS syndrome developed on the eighth day after an everolimus-eluting coronary stent was implanted7. Therefore, to the best of our knowledge, this current patient is the second case in the literature to have developed DRESS syndrome after everolimus treatment. In this patient, DRESS syndrome developed on the 15th day of everolimus treatment. In the first case in the literature7, symptoms resolved in four months after treatment, but in the case presented herein, the symptoms resolved in three weeks.

Everolimus is an oral mTOR (mammalian target of rapamycin) inhibitor. Antitumor activity has been demonstrated in many malignancies, including breast cancer, renal cell carcinoma, angiolipoma, neuroendocrine cancer, and central nervous system tumors. Serious side effects related to everolimus, such as stomatitis, anemia, pulmonary toxicity, hyperglycemia, and myelosuppression (thrombocytopenia, neutropenia), are commonly reported8. Pulmonary toxicity and sepsis are the most potentially fatal side effects due to everolimus and usually require drug discontinuation. However, serious side effects with everolimus are rare, and the drug is generally well tolerated9,10. In the current case, the life-threatening side effects of pneumonitis developed and these pulmonary complaints resolved after systemic steroid treatment.

The main medication in the treatment of DRESS syndrome is systemic steroids, especially if there is internal organ involvement. Although there is no consensus in terms of the optimal corticosteroid dose, duration of treatment, and rate of dose reduction, an initial dose of 0.5~1.0 mg/kg/day of prednisolone or equivalent is recommended. The corticosteroid dose should be reduced gradually according to the patient’s response to the treatment5. In the presence of severe internal organ damage, pulsed intravenous corticosteroid therapy can be applied11. Another treatment option for the treatment of DRESS syndrome is intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). IVIG administered at a dose of 1~2 g/kg may be performed as an additional treatment in patients who do not respond to systemic steroid therapy and in the presence of proven viral reactivations. IVIG may be effective through immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory mechanisms12. The current case was successfully treated with systemic steroids and IVIG treatment was not performed.

DRESS syndrome is a life-threatening hypersensitivity reaction. Although DRESS syndrome may develop with many drugs, the development of DRESS syndrome after the intake of everolimus is rare. This case report included a patient who developed DRESS syndrome after the intake of everolimus and was successfully treated.
