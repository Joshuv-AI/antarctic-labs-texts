Major urinary protein 1 modulates neuronal excitability in the medial prefrontal cortex in a male mouse model of affective empathy
Authors: Kun Zhang, Dake Song, Yingying Zhang, Le Yang, Ban Feng, Ruixia Liu, Hengxin Gong, Longbiao Cui, Shanbo Ma, Yanwei Chen, Min Wang, Xinshang Wang, Yumei Wu, Ting Bai, Kun Xia, Minggao Zhao, Shuibing Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Affective empathy describes the capacity to share others’ emotions, yet the molecular mechanisms governing individual differences in empathic sensitivity remain poorly understood. Using a male mouse model of social transmission of depression (STD), we demonstrated that STD‑sensitive observer mice exhibit heightened neuronal excitability and upregulated major urinary protein 1 (MUP1) expression in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) compared with STD‑insensitive observer mice. Mechanistically, MUP1 drives the STD‑sensitive phenotype by modulating astrocytic glutamate transporter 1 (GLT1) to augment mPFC neuronal excitability. Via virtual screening, we identified two small‑molecule compounds (C25 and C11) that bidirectionally modulate STD‑related behaviors. In summary, our work establishes MUP1 as a master modulator of STD vulnerability, deciphers mechanisms driving heterogeneous affective empathy across individuals, and unveils actionable therapeutic candidates for empathy-related psychiatric illnesses.

Introduction

Empathy is a multifaceted psychological and neurocognitive construct that refers to the ability to perceive, understand, and emotionally resonate with the mental states, feelings, and perspectives of others. As a fascinating yet poorly characterized phenomenon, it is commonly categorized by researchers into two distinct types: cognitive empathy and affective empathy1. Specifically, affective empathy refers to the social transmission of emotional states between conspecifics, and serves as the foundation for empathy-related affective responses2. Beyond humans, social transmission of emotions plays a pivotal role in facilitating effective communication, fostering social bonds, and promoting prosocial behaviors across many social species3. Previous studies have observed the phenomenon of social transmission of emotions in rodents (including mice), which can perceive and exhibit behavioral responses to the negative or distressed states of conspecifics4.

Regarding factors regulating social transmission of emotions, although some psychological factors (e.g., social affinity and personality traits) have been reported to modulate its intensity5, the underlying neuronal circuits and molecular mechanisms remain largely unelucidated. Heightened social interactions with stressed conspecifics is not only the basis of social transmission of emotion but also underpins behaviors such as providing assistance to distressed individuals and offering social support to stressed partners4,6,7. However, the decision to engage in social interaction with stressed conspecifics and share their stress may entail inherent risks of potential harm, including inflammation and emotional deterioration8,9, which may be regulated by higher-order brain regions.

The medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) is critical for both social behaviors and social decision-making in conflict situations10,11, and its function is modulated by multiple neuromodulatory systems (e.g., oxytocin4, vasopressin12, dopamine13, testosterone, and opioid14). Additionally, the mPFC not only plays a pivotal role in emotional processing but also serves as a key neural substrate for empathy15. Activation of the mPFC has been shown to be necessary for social interaction and empathy16,17; however, the mechanisms underlying its activation in these contexts, as well as individual differences therein, remain to be further elucidated.

Glutamate serves as the primary excitatory neurotransmitter in the central nervous system (CNS), and its extracellular concentration is tightly regulated by the efficient clearance of synaptic glutamate via excitatory amino acid transporters (EAATs). Astrocytic glutamate transporter 1 (GLT1) is a Na⁺-dependent transmembrane symporter18 and represents the predominant astrocytic glutamate transporter in the adult brain, with an expression level 4–6-fold higher than that of excitatory amino acid transporter 1 (GLAST) in astrocytes19. However, the role of GLT1-mediated potentiation of mPFC excitatory transmission in affective empathy remains to be fully elucidated.

In the present study, we investigated the molecular mechanisms underlying the degree of social transmission of depression (STD) when a mouse is exposed to a depressed conspecific. Building on previous evidence4,20, we quantified the intensity of STD in observer mice using stress-related behavioral assays. Observer mice were categorized into STD-sensitive (STDs) and STD-insensitive (STDi) groups based on their behavioral performance. We found enhanced mPFC activation was necessary for STDs-phenotype. Further research revealed that major urinary protein 1 (MUP1), a secreted lipocalin (LCN) previously reported as a pheromone in male urine21, was upregulated in the mPFC of STDs versus STDi mice. Increased MUP1 enhances mPFC neuronal excitability through direct binding to GLT1, exhibiting a binding affinity of 23.6 nM. From the ChemDiv Library, we screened small-molecule compounds: Compound 25 (C25) antagonized MUP1 function and ablated STD, while Compound 11 (C11) potentiated MUP1 activity and exacerbated STD. These findings suggest that modulation of the MUP1/GLT1 axis may represent a potential therapeutic target for neuropsychological disorders characterized by either empathy fatigue or empathy deficits22.

Results

Elevated MUP1 expression in the mPFC of STD-sensitive mice

In our STD model, pair-housed C57BL/6 J mice (with ≥ 2 weeks of cohabitation) were randomly assigned to either the observer or defeated group. The experimental paradigm consisted of two consecutive phases: a social defeat phase and a cohabitation interaction phase. Specifically, during the 20-min social defeat phase, CD-1 aggressor mice and defeated C57BL/6 J mice were housed in a single cage separated by a perforated Plexiglas divider, while observer mice were placed in an adjacent empty compartment of the same apparatus. For the remainder of the day (cohabitation interaction phase), defeated mice were returned to their home cages to cohabitate overnight with observer cage-mates. This entire protocol was repeated daily for 10 consecutive days, with a novel CD-1 aggressor used each day to avoid habituation effects. As for the control (Con) group, mice were daily exposed to CD-1 mice via perforated barriers but not subjected to aggressive encounters (Suppl. Fig. 1a). STD scores were quantified based on six independent behavioral assays (e.g., open field test, elevated plus maze test), with values normalized relative to the Con mean ± 1 standard deviation. Our model successfully recapitulated the STD-like phenotypes among cage-mate conspecifics (Suppl. Fig. 1b–g), which is consistent with findings from previous studies23,24.

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying mPFC activation in mediating STD, we employed isobaric tags for relative and absolute quantification (iTRAQ)—a high-throughput protein quantification technique—to identify differentially expressed proteins between STDs and STDi mice. This analysis identified several proteins, including major urinary protein 1 (MUP1), sodium-dependent dopamine transporter (DAT), and phosphodiesterase 10 A (PDE10A) (Fig. 1g and Suppl. Data 1). Among these, MUP1 exhibited the most significant difference, with higher expression in the mPFC of STDs mice compared to STDi mice—a finding validated by Western blot (Fig. 1h). MUP1, a member of the LCN protein family, is classified as a pheromone excreted in urine to mark conspecific territoriality. Beyond its urinary excretion and pheromonal properties, MUPs have also been reported to be expressed in neurons, satellite glial cells, and Schwann cells, where they regulate neurite outgrowth and diabetic neuropathy27. Immunofluorescence staining and Western blot analyses confirmed MUP1 protein expression in the mPFC, but with the highest expression detected in the liver (Fig. 1i–k).

To further identify the cell types expressing Mups (which share ~99% sequence identity)28, we reanalyzed single-cell mRNA sequencing data from the Allen Brain Atlas29 and found that Mups were predominantly expressed in neurons (Fig. 1l, 91.5% in Glutamatergic, 8.5% in GABAergic neurons). Complementarily, RNAscope in situ hybridization staining further validated that Mup1 mRNA is primarily expressed in glutamatergic neurons (Fig. 1m). Together, these results conclusively confirm MUP1 expression within the mPFC and imply a plausible role of MUP1 in the regulation of STD.

Disruption of MUP1 expression in the mPFC impairs social preference for defeated mice and attenuates STD

To investigate the necessity of MUP1 for STD, viruses expressing Mup1-targeting short hairpin RNA (shRNA) were injected into the mPFC to downregulate MUP1 protein levels (Fig. 2a, Suppl. Fig. 2a). Mup1 shRNA treatment did not affect social interest or social novelty responses to naive mice but significantly reduced social preference for defeated mice (Fig. 2b). Additionally, Mup1 shRNA-mediated knockdown (KD) in observer mice attenuated depression-like behaviors following STD exposure (Fig. 2c), indicating that MUP1 is a key regulator of STD. MUP1 belongs to the MUPs protein family, which is encoded by a cluster of 21 highly homologous Mup genes and 21 pseudogenes spanning approximately 2 megabase pairs (Mbp) on chromosome 430. Given the difficulty of specifically deleting Mup1 without affecting other Mup family members, the full-length 2.2 Mbp Mups gene cluster were deleted using the CRISPR/Cas9 system (Fig. 2d) as reported previously31. Mups knockout (Mups−/− or KO) mice also exhibited impaired social preference for defeated mice and attenuated STD phenotypes (Fig. 2e, f). Similar results were observed in conditional Mups KO (cKO) mice with excitatory neuron-specific deletion generated by crossing calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II alpha (Camk2a)-Cre mice (expressing Cre in excitatory neurons) with Mupsfl/fl mice (Camk2aCre; Mupsfl/fl) (Fig. 2g–i). We further knocked out Mups specifically in the mPFC by locally injecting AAV-hSyn-Cre-EGFP into Mupsfl/fl mice (Fig. 2j). The results showed that mPFC-specific Mups deletion reduced social preference for defeated mice (Fig. 2k) and alleviated STD (Fig. 2l). To rule out olfactory or visual deficits as the cause of impaired social preference for defeated mice in MUP1 KD/KO mice, we conducted the buried food and novel object recognition tests. No significant differences were detected between MUP1 KD/KO and WT mice (Suppl. Fig. 2b, c), confirming intact olfactory and visual functions in the former. Increasing MUP1 expression in the mPFC promotes social preference for defeated mice and STD

Furthermore, we examined the effect of MUP1 overexpression in WT mice. After local injection of Mup1-mCherry into the mPFC of WT mice (Fig. 3d), we found that MUP1 upregulation significantly increased the social preference for defeated conspecifics and facilitated STD (Fig. 3e, f).

Given that mPFC activation is essential for STD, we next tested whether MUP1 overexpression is sufficient to activate the mPFC. Immunofluorescence staining showed that stereotactic injection of the Mup1-mCherry significantly increased the number of c-FOS⁺ cells in the mPFC (Fig. 3g), indicating that MUP1 overexpression enhances mPFC neuronal activity. To confirm that MUP1-mediated facilitation of STD is dependent on mPFC activation, we employed the inhibitory chemogenetic tool hM4Di to suppress mPFC neuronal activity. Notably, chemogenetic inhibition of the mPFC completely abolished the facilitatory effect of MUP1 on STD (Fig. 3h).

To further validate the critical regulatory role of MUP1 in STD transmission, we performed a two-round STD behavioral assay. Observer mice were initially stratified into STDs and STDi subgroups according to their behavioral performance following the first round of STD exposure. Subsequently, MUP1 was knocked down in STDs mice and overexpressed in STDi mice, respectively, after which all mice underwent the second round of the STD assay. We found that the behavioral phenotypes of both subgroups were effectively reversed by these targeted manipulations during the second round of STD exposure (Fig. 3i). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that MUP1 serves as a key regulator of mPFC function, thereby mediating the development of STD by facilitating social preference for defeated conspecifics.

Exogenous MUP1 treatment significantly increased both the frequency and amplitude of spontaneous excitatory postsynaptic currents (sEPSCs) in mPFC pyramidal neurons (Fig. 4b, c), indicating that MUP1 facilitates excitatory synaptic transmission in these neurons. In contrast, MUP1 had no significant effects on action potential properties, resting membrane potentials (RMPs), miniature excitatory postsynaptic currents (mEPSCs), spontaneous inhibitory postsynaptic currents (sIPSCs), miniature inhibitory postsynaptic currents (mIPSCs), or evoked inhibitory postsynaptic current paired pulse ratio (eIPSCs PPR) (Fig. 4d, e and Suppl. Fig. 3).

Notably, MUP1 perfusion significantly reduced the evoked excitatory postsynaptic current paired pulse ratio (eEPSCs PPR) of pyramidal neurons in the mPFC across multiple interstimulus intervals, suggesting that the underlying mechanisms of MUP1 action involve a presynaptic component (Fig. 4f). Critically, pretreatment with L-α-aminoadipic acid (L-AAA), a specific astrocyte toxin, abolished MUP1-induced facilitation of sEPSCs (Fig. 4g). In contrast, pretreatment with UCPH-101 (a GLAST blocker), SCH-23390 (a dopamine D1-like receptor antagonist), CGS 15943 (an adenosine A2 receptor antagonist), or MPEP (a metabotropic glutamate receptor 5 antagonist) had no such inhibitory effect (Suppl. Fig. 4a).

Furthermore, pretreatment with dihydrokainic acid (DHK), a selective inhibitor of GLT1, also abolished the MUP1-induced facilitation of sEPSCs (Suppl. Fig. 4b). Consistent with these findings, mPFC neurons from STDs mice exhibited significantly increased frequency and amplitude of sEPSCs compared to those from STDi mice (Fig. 4h), which is in line with the higher MUP1 expression levels observed in STDs mice. Collectively, these results demonstrate that MUP1 enhances mPFC excitability by promoting excitatory synaptic transmission, and this effect is dependent on astrocyte function, likely via GLT1.

To further elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms, recombinant His-tagged MUP1 was incubated with mPFC tissue lysates to capture MUP1-interacting proteins. These complexes were then co-immunoprecipitated (co-IP) using anti-His-Tag magnetic beads and subsequently identified via liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) (Fig. 4i). Among the identified MUP1-binding proteins, several molecules involved in regulating synaptic transmission were detected, including syntaxin-binding protein 1 (STXBP1), GLT1, sodium- and chloride-dependent glycine transporter 1 (GLYT1), GLAST, synaptotagmin-1 (SYT1), sodium- and chloride-dependent GABA transporter 1 (GAT1), synaptotagmin-7 (SYT7), and CaM kinase-like vesicle-associated protein (CAMKV) (Supplementary Data 2). Co-IP combined with Western blot analysis confirmed the interaction of MUP1 with STXBP1 and GLT1, whereas no binding was observed between MUP1 and SYT1 or GLAST (Fig. 4i). Immunoelectron microscopy further detected MUP1-positive particles in both neurons and the plasma membrane of astrocytes (Fig. 4j). Virtual molecular docking was employed to predict the binding interfaces between MUP1 and its interacting partners (Fig. 4k and Suppl. Fig. 5). Additionally, biolayer interferometry (BLI) assays were performed to measure the binding affinities of MUP1 with STXBP1 and the GLT1 fragment (aa143-234), yielding dissociation constants (Kd) of 20.2 nM and 23.6 nM, respectively (Fig. 4l and Suppl. Fig. 6).

Given that GLT1 mediates the clearance of over 90% of synaptic glutamate in astrocytes33, we hypothesized that MUP1 may enhance neuronal excitability by regulating astrocytic glutamate reuptake. To test this, we measured intracellular glutamate concentrations in primary cultured astrocytes following exogenous MUP1 administration. MUP1 treatment significantly reduced intracellular glutamate levels in astrocytes, suggesting that MUP1 impairs GLT1-dependent glutamate reuptake (Fig. 4m). These findings are consistent with the role of MUP1 in regulating synaptic transmission. Collectively, our results validate that MUP1 plays a critical role in regulating mPFC excitability through an astrocytic GLT1-dependent pathway.

To elucidate how MUP1 regulates GLT1 function, we performed molecular dynamics (MD) simulations—consistent with established methodologies—to investigate the effects of MUP1 binding on GLT1 conformation and activity in the presence of glutamate. For the glutamate/GLT1 complex, the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) rapidly increased to approximately 1.5 Å in the initial phase and remained between 1.0 and 2.0 Å for the remainder of the simulation. In contrast, for the MUP1/glutamate/GLT1 complex, the RMSD of the ligand (glutamate) remained stable at approximately 0.5 Å throughout the simulation with minimal fluctuations (Fig. 5b). This indicates a relatively stable binding state of glutamate within the MUP1/GLT1 complex, with negligible conformational changes over time.

Additionally, the radius of gyration (RoG) of the glutamate/GLT1 complex remained at a relatively low level throughout the simulation, indicating a compact conformational state (Fig. 5b). During MD simulations, MUP1 binding did not alter the number of hydrogen bonds in the glutamate/GLT1 complex (Fig. 5c). Notably, in the MUP1/glutamate/GLT1 complex, the root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) of most GLT1 residues was maintained at a low level; specifically, the RMSF peaks at residues 250 and 550 were significantly reduced compared to those in the glutamate/GLT1 complex. This suggests that GLT1 exhibits greater conformational stability and reduced flexibility in the presence of MUP1 (Fig. 5c).

Furthermore, the binding free energy (ΔG bind) of the MUP1/glutamate/GLT1 complex was significantly higher than that of the glutamate/GLT1 complex (−52.35 ± 2.37 vs. −28.34 ± 4.04 kcal/mol, respectively; Fig. 5d). Collectively, these results support the hypothesis that MUP1 binding induces conformational changes in the catalytic domain of GLT1 when GLT1 is bound to glutamate. This MUP1-induced conformational alteration is likely the key mechanism by which MUP1 regulates GLT1-mediated glutamate reuptake.

hPAEP, a MUP1 analogue, binds to hGLT1 in humans and predicts the severity of autism spectrum disorder children

MUPs-encoding genes belong to the LCN family in mice but are absent in humans34. Phylogenetic divergence analysis of the mouse Mups gene cluster has revealed its closest homology to human LCN9 (hLCN9) and human progestagen-associated endometrial protein (hPAEP)35. Based on this evolutionary relationship, we hypothesized that hLCN9 or hPAEP may exert a GLT1-regulating function in humans, analogous to that of MUP1 in mice.

To experimentally validate this hypothesis, we employed co-IP combined with LC-MS to screen for binding proteins of hLCN9 and hPAEP in surgically resected human brain tissues (Suppl. Data 3 and 4, Suppl. Fig. 7a). The reliability of LC-MS detection was ensured by monitoring protein coverage and molecular weight distribution (Suppl. Fig. 7b, c). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis of the identified binding proteins revealed two significantly enriched pathways: amino acid transport and metabolism, and intracellular trafficking, secretion, and vesicular transport (Suppl. Fig. 7d, e).

Notably, human GLT1 (hGLT1) was identified as a potential binding partner of hPAEP (Suppl. Fig. 8a). Western blot analysis further confirmed that hPAEP, but not hLCN9, interacts with hGLT1 in human brain tissues (Suppl. Fig. 8b). Functional validation showed that exogenous addition of recombinant hPAEP reduced intracellular glutamate concentrations in the human glioma cell line U251 (Suppl. Fig. 8c), indicating that hPAEP may regulate hGLT1 function—mirroring the role of MUP1 in mice.

A core clinical feature of some autism spectrum disorder (ASD) children is deficits in recognizing and responding to others’ emotional signals36,37, which manifests as impaired affective empathy. To explore the association between hPAEP levels and ASD clinical severity, we collected clinical behavioral data and blood samples from children with ASD. Correlation analysis demonstrated a negative association between plasma hPAEP levels and behavioral severity in these ASD children (Suppl. Fig. 8d), consistent with our previous findings regarding MUP1-mediated regulation of STD.

Together, our data support evolutionary conservation of the MUP1-GLT1 (mouse) and hPAEP-hGLT1 (human) axes as actionable targets to regulate empathy, with important mechanistic implications for empathy deficits linked to ASD.

The top 30 compounds (ranked by docking scores, designated Compound 1 to Compound 30) were subsequently assessed for their ability to modulate MUP1-mediated reduction of intracellular glutamate levels in astrocytes. Treatment with Compound 25 (C25, 1 μM) antagonized the MUP1-induced decrease in glutamate concentrations in primary cultured astrocytes, whereas Compound 11 (C11, 1 μM) potentiated this MUP1-mediated effect (Fig. 7b). Moreover, C25 and C11 exerted distinct regulatory effects on MUP1-perfusion-induced enhancement of sEPSCs (Suppl. Fig. 9).

In the absence of exogenous MUP1 in the perfusion buffer, C11 and C25 regulated the frequency and amplitude of sEPSCs in opposing directions (Fig. 7c). In vivo experiments showed that intraperitoneal (i.p.) administration of C25 or C11 (1 mg/kg) exerted opposing effects on social preference toward defeated mice and STD progression: C25 reduced social interaction with defeated mice and attenuated STD, whereas C11 enhanced social interaction with defeated mice and exacerbated STD (Fig. 7d, e).

Collectively, these findings identify two small molecules (C25 and C11) that target the MUP1/GLT1 binding pocket and bidirectionally regulate STD, highlighting their potential as tool compounds for dissecting the MUP1/GLT1 signaling axis and as candidate therapeutic agents for empathy-related disorders.

Discussion

Empathy enables individuals to understand and share the emotions of others39,40, and its dysregulation is implicated in a broad spectrum of psychiatric and neurological disorders41–43. Investigating the neural and molecular mechanisms of empathy is therefore not only of fundamental scientific importance but also holds practical implications for fostering prosocial behaviors and developing interventions for disorders characterized by social dysfunction. The development of empathy relies on conserved social mechanisms including behavioral mimicry and affective contagion, which serve as the core underpinnings of empathic responses44. Notably, evidence indicates that social orientation toward a depressed conspecific—an essential basis for empathy emergence—necessitates mPFC engagement45,46. In this study, we observed heightened mPFC activation in mice with STDs phenotypes compared to their STDi counterparts. This finding aligns with reports of impaired empathy-related phenotypes following mPFC inactivation in a frontotemporal dementia (FTD) mouse model15, as well as with studies demonstrating that only gregarious mouse strains exhibit increased activation in the mPFC47.

Regarding the molecular mechanism underlying mPFC hyperactivation in STDs mice, we detected elevated levels of MUP1 in the mPFC. Traditionally, MUPs are classified as pheromone-associated molecules that mediate urinary chemical communication, individual recognition, and fear induction in rodents48–50. Beyond these canonical functions, peripheral studies have also implicated MUPs in the regulation of glucose and lipid metabolism, though the underlying mechanisms remain poorly characterized51,52. For instance, MUP1 enhances insulin sensitivity in the skeletal muscle by facilitating the translocation of glucose transporter 4 (GLUT4)53. Previous studies have shown that MUPs are also expressed in dorsal root ganglia (DRG) neurons of mice, and MUPs KD attenuates experimental diabetic neuropathy27. However, the physiological function of MUP1 within the CNS remains largely unexplored. A key finding of the present study is the identification of MUP1 as a critical non-olfactory mediator in the CNS. Specifically, MUP1, after being secreted from neurons, mediates the activation of the mPFC in STD-related behavioral phenotypes by binding to astrocytic GLT1. However, given that only male mice were used in the present study, we cannot definitively conclude whether MUPs exert sexually dimorphic functions between males and females. To address this uncertainty, we reanalyzed single-cell RNA sequencing data from the Allen Brain Atlas and detected no differential expression of Mups genes between male and female mice in the mPFC (Suppl. Data 5). Nevertheless, whether MUP1 exhibits gender-specific functional differences or exerts sexually dimorphic effects remains to be further elucidated.

While Mup genes are absent in humans, we demonstrated that their functional homolog, hPAEP, binds to hGLT1 in brain tissue and similarly modulates glutamate reuptake. We found that MUP1 increased neuronal excitability (Fig. 4c), an effect that could be abolished by a GLT1‑selective inhibitor (Suppl. Fig. 4b). MUP1 also directly bound to GLT1 (Fig. 4i, k, l). Based on these findings, we hypothesized that MUP1 may exert its effects by inhibiting GLT1 function. However, an inconsistency in the behavioral data remains unresolved: GLT1 KO and MUP1 overexpression elicited opposite behavioral phenotypes (Fig. 6i, j). Although our SPR and co‑IP assays verified direct binding between MUP1 and GLT1, behavioral outcomes were considerably more complex than those observed at the molecular and cellular levels. One possibility is that GLT1 mediates extrasynaptic glutamate reuptake and modulates cognition, memory, and emotion54,55. We therefore cannot rule out that the observed changes in social preference or STD following GLT1 KD may reflect indirect effects stemming from alterations in these behaviors. Another possibility is that MUP1 facilitates STD via partial inhibition of GLT1, whereas shRNA-mediated excessive suppression of GLT1 may also impair STD. A similar paradox has been documented in studies of the serotonin transporter (SERT): SERT inhibitors exert potent antidepressant effects, whereas SERT‑KO mice exhibit exacerbated depressive‑like behaviors56. Furthermore, GLT1 KD led to a significantly greater number of c-Fos+ cells and enhanced sEPSCs relative to MUP1 overexpression (Fig. 6g, h). Notably, excessive neuronal excitation may disrupt STD via inducing neuronal dysfunction, and such overexcitation may partially account for this phenomenon. Nevertheless, this behavioral discrepancy warrants further investigation.

Clinically, we identified a negative correlation between plasma hPAEP levels and behavioral symptom severity in children with ASD. Although reports on affective empathy in ASD have been inconsistent22, our data suggest that the MUP1/GLT1 axis in mice and its human ortholog, the hPAEP/hGLT1 pathway, may represent an evolutionarily conserved signaling module with considerable potential for regulating empathy-related processes.

Dysregulation of GLT1 has been strongly implicated in the pathogenesis of diverse neurological disorders, including cerebral ischemia, schizophrenia, epilepsy, and ASD18,57. The transcription of GLT-1 is tightly governed by a panel of transcription factors, including specificity protein 1 (Sp1), cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB), and estrogen-responsive elements. Beyond transcriptional regulation, post-translational modifications—such as phosphorylation, glycosylation, and ubiquitination—also play critical roles in modulating GLT1 activity18,58. Accordingly, a growing body of research has compared the efficacy of diverse pharmacological agents targeting GLT1 expression and function based on their distinct mechanisms of action18. In the present study, we uncovered a previously unrecognized role of GLT1 in regulating empathy and identified two chemicals that modulate GLT1 function by interfering with the binding pocket of the GLT1/MUP1 complex.

Although the STD observed in our mice model cannot be simplistically equated with the full complexity of human empathy, it provides valuable mechanistic insights into the neurobiological basis of high- versus low-empathy traits. STD encompasses both the transmission and central processing of distress signals. MUP1 deficiency impairs social preference toward defeated mice, suggesting that MUP1 may not directly contribute to central processing, but rather acts by modulating basic social transmission with stressed conspecifics. However, whether MUP1 directly regulates STD via modulating emotional processing remains to be further elucidated. Variability in MUP1 expression—and consequently, empathy-related behaviors—may be influenced by multiple factors including early-life environment, nutrition, prior experience, social familiarity, genetic background, sex, stress exposure, and stimulus presentation frequency. For instance, rodent observers exhibit fear contagion only if they have shared prior aversive experiences with demonstrators59,60, and genetic/epigenetic factors shape individual susceptibility to affective contagion61.

Besides getting depressed, we also observed that some STDi mice with higher MUP1 expression displayed attenuated depressive-like behaviors (Fig. 1b, h). We speculate that this heterogeneity may arise from individual biological differences. Although the precise mechanisms underlying their resistance to MUP1-mediated regulation remain to be fully clarified, a recent study offers a potential mechanistic explanation: negative emotion contagion can enhance stress resilience by promoting serotonin release from the habenula62. We hypothesize that this neurobiological pathway may underlie the antidepressant-like effects observed in a subset of STD-exposed mice in our study. Therefore, robust assessment of affective empathy should integrate multi-component behavioral assays and physiological recordings63.

In summary, the present study advances our mechanistic understanding of central nervous system MUP functions and identifies synaptic pathways as well as key molecular targets governing affective empathy. These observations provide promising translational insights for developing therapeutics against empathy-associated neuropsychiatric disorders.

Methods

Animals

Male 10–16 weeks-old C57BL/6 J and CD-1 breeders (Vital River Laboratories, Beijing, China) were purchased and used to set up the chronic social defeat stress paradigm. Mice were housed under standard laboratory conditions with a 12‑h light‑dark cycle (lights on at 07:00), ambient temperature maintained at 22–24 °C, and relative humidity controlled at 50–60%. Food and water were provided ad libitum. All experiments performed were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Fourth Military Medical University (FMMU). Mice were randomly assigned to either experimental or control group.

The Mups KO mice were kindly provided by Chen Liang, and their generation was previously described in the literature31. The genotyping primers were detailed below. MupdelF1 (5′-CATAAAGCACTAAAGCAGG-3′) anneals to sequence upstream of Mup gene cluster. MupwtF1 (5′-ATACTTAGCATACTGTCCCTG-3′) anneals to sequence within Mup gene cluster. MupR1 (5′-GAACAGTCCTTGGCCTATC-3′) anneals to sequence downstream of Mup gene cluster. MupdelF1 and MupR1 amplify a fragment of ~200 bp corresponding to the knockout mutant. MupwtF1 and MupR1 amplify a fragment of ~485 bp corresponding to the WT allele.

Mups Camk2a conditional knockout mice were generated by crossing the floxed Mups allele (Mupsfl/fl) mice with Camk2a-Cre transgenic mice, which expresses Cre recombinase in excitatory neurons under the control of the Camk2a promoter and conditional knockout genes in excitatory neurons. Mupsfl/fl mice were obtained by crossing between Mupsfl/w mice, which were generated by GemPharmatech Co., Ltd (Nanjing, China). Briefly, transformed embryonic stem cells colonies were screened by long-template PCR with the following primer sets: GJS0219111712-01-Mup-5wt-tF1 (5’-AAATCCTACAAGCCCTCAGCAG-3’) and GJS0219111712-01-Mup-5wt-tR1 (5’-TAGCTCCAGGTCTGCATCATTC-3’) to generate a fragment of 255 bp corresponding to the wild-type alle and a fragment of 347 bp corresponding to the floxed alle; 10884 (5’-GTTCTCCGTTTGCACTCAGG-3’) and oIMR8990 (5’-CAGGTTCTTGCGAACCTCAT-3’) to generate a 485 bp band for Camk2a-cre positive clones.

Social transmission of depression

Naive C57BL/6 J mice were pair-housed as cagemates for at least 2 weeks before being assigned to either observer or defeated mice. The social transmission of depression (STD) consisted of two distinct phases: the social defeat phase and the interaction phase. During the social defeat phase, the social defeat procedure was performed with minor modifications as previously described in ref. 64. Briefly, CD-1 mice with consistent attack latencies (≤ 30 s in three consecutive screening tests) were housed in cages equipped with perforated Plexiglas dividers, allowing sensory but not physical contact between mice. Observer mice were placed in the empty compartment adjacent to the CD-1 aggressor, while defeated mice were positioned in the compartment containing the aggressor. In this setup, defeated mice were subjected to attacks by the CD-1 aggressor and exhibited defensive postures. After 20 min, defeated mice were returned to the observer mice for overnight social interaction during the interaction phase. This protocol was repeated for 10 consecutive days, with each observer mouse “witnessing” its cagemate being defeated by a new CD-1 aggressor daily. As a control, mice underwent 10 days of daily transfers to either side of a perforated Plexiglas partition with a CD-1 mouse on the opposite side.

Behavioral tests

Behavioral tests were performed during the light cycle with dim light illumination. All mice were transferred to the behavior testing room at least 30 min before the experiments to acclimatize to the environment.

Open field test

The open field test (OFT) was conducted on day 11 after depression contagion as described in a previous report65. It was carried out in the open fixed, a square arena with clear Plexiglas walls and floor, and placed inside an isolation chamber (44 × 44 cm) with dim illumination and a fan. Mice were placed in the center of the box and allowed to freely explore for a 15 min period. In all behavioral test, mice were videotaped using cameras and analyzed with a DigBehv software (Jiliang, Shanghai, China).

Elevated plus maze test

The elevated plus maze test (EPMT) was conducted after OFT on the same day as described in the report66. The apparatus comprised of two open arms (25 × 8 × 0.5 cm) and two closed arms (25 × 8 × 12 cm) that extended from a common central platform (8 × 8 cm). The apparatus elevated to a height of 50 cm above the floor. Mice were habituated to the testing room for 2 days before behavioral testing, with gentle handling performed twice per day to minimize stress. For each test, individual animals were placed in the center square, facing an open arm, and allowed to move freely for 5 min. The number of entries and time spent in each arm were recorded with a DigBehv software (Jiliang).

Social interaction test

This social interaction test (SIT) was performed to assess social interest (preference for social over non-social stimuli), social novelty recognition (discrimination between familiar and novel social stimuli), and social preference for defeated cagemates (reflecting social empathy) in mice, as previously described with minor modifications67. Social interest and social novelty recognition were conducted one day before the first STD training, while social preference for defeated mice was performed immediately after the first STD session. We used a transparent acrylic single-chamber arena (40 × 40 × 30 cm) to eliminate spatial partition-induced bias, three identical wire mesh cages (5 × 5 × 8 cm) with perforated walls (facilitating odor and visual communication)—two for housing social stimuli and one as a non-social control—and a digital video tracking system (DigBehv Software, Jiliang) to record all behavioral data throughout the experiment; two days prior to STD, test mice were habituated to the empty single-chamber arena for 10 minutes per day to acclimate to the test environment, and naive, age-matched male conspecifics (with no prior exposure to test mice) were selected as two distinct social stimuli (“Familiar Stranger [S1]” and “Novel Stranger [S2]”) to avoid pre-existing social familiarity; during Phase 1 (Social Interest and Preference Assessment), two wire mesh cages were placed 15 cm apart at the center of the arena (left/right positions randomly assigned), with one containing S1 (social stimulus) and the other empty (non-social control), the test mouse was introduced to the arena and allowed 10 minutes of free exploration, and social exploration duration (defined as sniffing, approaching within 1 cm of the wire mesh cage, or touching the cage with the nose/paws) toward S1 and the empty cage was recorded separately; after Phase 1, the test mouse was returned to its home cage for a 30-minute rest period to consolidate familiarity with S1; for Phase 2 (Social Novelty Recognition Assessment), the wire mesh cage with S1 was repositioned at its original location, the empty cage was replaced with a third wire mesh cage containing S2 (novel social stimulus), the test mouse was reintroduced to the arena and allowed another 10 min of free exploration, and social exploration durations toward S1 (familiar) and S2 (novel) were documented; outcome measures for the first two phases included a social preference index calculated as [time exploring S1 / (time exploring the empty cage)] × 100% (a value exceeding 100% indicates a preference for social over non-social stimuli), a social novelty index calculated as [time exploring S2 / (time exploring S1)] × 100% (a value above 100% reflects successful recognition of novel social stimuli and intact social memory), total social exploration time (sum of exploration durations toward all social stimuli: S1 in Phase 1, S1 and S2 in Phase 2) to evaluate overall social interest, and side preference control (analysis of the proportion of time spent in the left versus right quadrants of the arena around the two wire mesh cages) to exclude inherent positional bias; immediately after the first STD session, the test mouse was reintroduced to the arena, with two wire mesh cages placed 15 cm apart at the center (left/right positions randomly assigned)—one housing an undefeated naive conspecific (S3, distinct from S1 and S2) and the other containing the test mouse’s defeated cagemate—and allowed 10 minutes of free exploration, with social exploration durations toward the undefeated S3 and defeated cagemate recorded separately; the outcome measure for this phase was a social empathy index calculated as [time exploring the defeated cagemate / (time exploring S3)] × 100% (a value exceeding 100% indicates a preference for the defeated over the undefeated conspecific, reflecting social empathy); after each test session, the single-chamber arena, wire mesh cages, and all contact surfaces were thoroughly wiped with 75% ethanol and air-dried completely to remove residual odors, feces, and contaminants, thereby preventing cross-interference between test mice.

Tail suspension test

The tail suspension test (TST) was implemented within a sound-isolated chamber following a previously validated experimental protocol68. Individual mice were elevated 50 cm off the ground by securing roughly 2 cm of the distal tail segment to a wooden rod using lightweight adhesive tape. Opaque partition panels were placed around the testing arena to eliminate visual distractions for each subject. The total recording duration for every animal spanned 6 min; only immobility durations recorded during the final 4 min window were quantified for statistical analysis. A mouse was classified as immobile when it ceased active struggling and remained largely stationary, indicative of a behavioral despair phenotype.

Forced swimming test

The forced swimming test (FST) was performed to assess behavioral despair in mice using separate transparent cylindrical glass tanks (10 cm inner diameter, 30 cm total height) filled with prewarmed water maintained at 23–25 °C to a depth of 25 cm. This water depth prevented animals from resting their paws or tail against the tank base for physical support. Animals underwent two sequential swimming trials separated by a 24 h interval: a 15 min preliminary swim to elicit despair-like behaviors, then a 5 min formal test session for subsequent behavioral quantification. Automated analysis software was only used to score immobility within the final 4 min of the formal trial. Upon completion of each swimming trial, all mice were gently dried and placed under a heating source before being returned to their respective housing cages.

Sucrose preference test

As reported previously69, before the first time of the test, all mice were trained to get accustomed to 1% sucrose solution (w/v) in their home cages. Two bottles of 1% sucrose solution were placed on each cage for 24 h. For the following period of 24 h, 1% sucrose in one bottle was replaced with pure water. After adaptation, mice were deprived of food and water for 24 h, followed by the next morning, in which the mice were fed with two pre-weighed bottles of liquid at the same time: the one containing 1% sucrose solution and the other pure water. The bottles were counterbalanced across the left and right sides of the cages throughout the experiment to avoid position preference effects. After 12 h, the two bottles were re-weighed. Sucrose preference (%) = sucrose consumption / (sucrose consumption + water consumption) × 100%.

Novelty-suppressed feeding test

This test was performed as described previously70. Prior to testing, all experimental mice underwent a 24 h fasting period without access to chow within their home enclosures. A single food pellet was positioned atop circular filter paper (12.5 cm diameter) at the central zone of an opaque acrylic arena measuring 50 × 50 × 40 cm, whose base was lined with standard animal bedding. Each subject was individually introduced into one corner of the testing arena at the start of each trial. We recorded two primary readouts: the time taken for each mouse to first reach the food target and the latency until initial feeding behavior emerged, with a total trial cutoff of 5 minutes. Lighting gradients were standardized across the arena, delivering 140 lux brightness at the central region and a reduced 120 lux intensity around the peripheral areas throughout data collection.

Buried food test

This test was performed to assess olfactory sensitivity and foraging-related alertness in mice, as previously described with minor modifications71. Two days prior to the experiment, mice were habituated to sweetened cereal pellets to ensure recognition of the food as a foraging target. On the test day, each mouse was first allowed a 10 min acclimation to a clean cage. A single sweetened cereal pellet was then buried 3–5 cm beneath the cage bedding, and the mouse was reintroduced to the cage. The latency to locate and consume the buried pellet was recorded as the primary outcome measure.

Novel object recognition test

The novel object recognition test (NORT) was used to evaluate visual mediated recognition memory in mice. Briefly, each mouse was placed in a 40 × 40 cm open field arena, and object exploration behavior was recorded using the animal tracking system (DigBehv software, Jiliang). During the habituation phase, the mouse was placed in the center of the arena and allowed 10 minutes of free exploration of two identical familiar objects. Following this, the mouse was returned to its home cage for a 10-minute rest interval. In the test phase, one familiar object was replaced with a novel object of distinct shape and texture. The mouse was reintroduced to the arena for another 10 minutes, and the duration of exploration (defined as sniffing, touching, or approaching the object within 1 cm) for each object was documented for subsequent statistical analysis.

STD score

A STD score was calculated based on all 6 different behavioral tests, including OFT, EPM, TST, FST, SPT, NSF. All behavioral tests were performed during the light cycle. The sequence of behavioral tests was depicted in Suppl. Fig. 1a. If their performance were 1 standard deviation (SD) worse than mean values of control mice, their depression score was added for 1 point. If their performance were 1 SD better than mean values of control mice, their score was subtracted for 1 point. If their performance were within 1 SD of the mean values for control mice, their score in this test were 0. After summing all their scores, if it was greater than or equal to 1, this mouse was considered STD sensitive (STDs) individual. If it was lower than less than or equal to −1, this mouse was considered STD insensitive (STDi) individual respectively, with remaining mice characterized as “undefined”. This way was modified from that used to separate depression resilient and depression sensitive mice as reported previously72.

Proteomics

The proteomics analysis was carried out by Beijing Genomics institution (BGI, Beijing, China) as described previously73. Mice were deeply anesthetized via inhalation of isoflurane prior to rapid whole-brain dissection. Medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) tissues were manually isolated immediately post-mortem and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for downstream proteomic profiling. Tissue specimens were collected from three matched animal pairs belonging to the STDs and STDi experimental groups for proteome-wide detection. Harvested mPFC tissues were homogenized in cold lysis buffer, followed by ice-bath sonication to fully lyse cellular components. The homogenate underwent centrifugation at 17,000 × g and 4 °C over a 30-minute period, after which clear supernatants were recovered to measure total protein concentration using the BCA colorimetric assay. Equal protein loads (100 μg per individual sample) were digested overnight (16 h, 37 °C) with Trypsin Gold (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) at a protein-to-enzyme mass ratio of 30:1. Digested peptide fragments were then tagged using 4-plex iTRAQ labeling kits (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA), strictly adhering to the manufacturer’s standard operating instructions. Combined labeled peptide pools were fractionated by strong cation exchange chromatography utilizing the Shimadzu LC-20AB HPLC Pump platform (Kyoto, Japan). Subsequent peptide separation for mass spectrometric detection was carried out on a Shimadzu LC-20AD nano high-performance liquid chromatography system (Kyoto, Japan). Chromatographic eluents were ionized through nano-electrospray ionization and further analyzed by tandem mass spectrometry on a Q Exactive Orbitrap instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA). The mass spectrometer was operated in positive ion mode and data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode. The MS1 scan was performed over a mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) range of 350–1500 with a resolution of 120,000 at m/z 200, an automatic gain control (AGC) target of 3 × 10⁶, and a maximum injection time of 50 ms. For MS2 scans, the top 20 most intense precursor ions from each MS1 scan were selected for higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) fragmentation. The MS2 resolution was set to 30,000 at m/z 200, with an AGC target of 1 × 10⁵, a maximum injection time of 100 ms, a normalized collision energy (NCE) of 28%, an isolation window of 1.2 m/z, and a dynamic exclusion time of 30 s. The spray voltage was set to 2.2 kV, and the capillary temperature was maintained at 275 °C. No sheath gas or auxiliary gas was used for nanospray ionization. Intact peptides were detected at a resolution of 70,000 and then selected for MS/MS using high-energy collision dissociation operating with a normalized collision energy setting of 27. Ion fragments were detected in the Orbitrap at a resolution of 17,500. A data-dependent procedure was applied for the 15 most abundant precursor ions above a threshold ion count of 20,000 in the MS survey scan. Raw MS data files (.raw) were processed and searched using MaxQuant software (version 1.6.14.0) against the UniProt canonical database for the target species [Mus musculus, UniProt ID: UP000000589, release 2023_01], concatenated with a common contaminant database (downloaded from the MaxQuant website) to exclude contaminating proteins (e.g., keratin, trypsin). The output data from MaxQuant (proteinGroups.txt) were imported into Perseus software (version 1.6.15.0) for further processing and statistical analysis. First, LFQ intensities were log₂-transformed to normalize the data distribution. Proteins with ≥70% valid LFQ values in at least one experimental group were retained for subsequent analysis; proteins with excessive missing values (more than 30% missing in all groups) were excluded. Missing values were imputed from a normal distribution with a width of 0.3 and a downshift of 1.8 relative to the mean of the measured values, to simulate the detection limit of the instrument. Differential protein expression analysis was performed using Student’s t-test (for two-group comparison) or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA, for multi-group comparison). Proteins with an adjusted P-value (Benjamini-Hochberg correction) < 0.05 and an absolute log₂(fold change) ≥ 1 were considered significantly differentially expressed.

Bioinformatics analysis

Protein identification and quantification were performed in Protein Pilot™ 4.5 software (AB Sciex, Framingham, MA, USA). Peptide identification relied on the Paragon algorithm coupled with embedded FDR assessment. High-confidence proteins required ≥ 2 distinct unique peptides and an FDR < 1%; only proteins with iTRAQ ratios ranging 0.5–20 were retained. We set a 95% confidence standard and an unused ProtScore cutoff ≥ 1.3 to limit false positives. Functional annotation and GO enrichment of DEPs were analyzed via DAVID, covering biological process, cellular component, and molecular function from the Gene Ontology database. Enriched signaling pathways were mined against KEGG, with significance defined as Benjamini-adjusted P < 0.05 following minor DAVID-recommended parameter tweaks. Protein interaction networks were built using STRING v10.5 (http://string-db.org/), and expression heatmaps were plotted with MeV 4.9.

Single-cell transcriptomic reanalysis of public mouse brain datasets

Public 10× Genomics single-cell RNA-seq data of mouse mPFC were downloaded from the Allen Brain Atlas database (https://portal.brain-map.org/atlases-and-data/rnaseq/mouse-whole-cortex-and-hippocampus-10x), in which cell subtype annotation and mouse sex information had been defined in the original published study29. The AAV2/9-hSyn-Cre viral vector was acquired from Taitool Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). To achieve MUP1 overexpression, custom AAV particles were packaged commercially by Obio Technology Corp. Ltd. (Shanghai, China) using the AAV9-hSyn-mCherry-P2A-MUP1-3Flag transfer plasmid. Stereotaxic virus injection

As reported previously in ref. 75, mice were maintained under continuous inhaled isoflurane anesthesia and secured firmly onto a stereotaxic frame manufactured by RWD Life Technology Co. Ltd. (China). A thin layer of ophthalmic ointment was applied over both ocular surfaces to mitigate corneal dehydration throughout surgical procedures. After fur removal above the cranial region, the exposed skin was sequentially disinfected with iodophor solution and medical ethanol prior to a midline scalp incision. Subcutaneous connective tissue overlying the calvaria was gently cleared using sterile cotton swabs to fully expose the skull surface. A compact burr hole (approximately 1 mm in diameter) was created at the predefined injection coordinate using an RWD dental drill (Model 78011, Shenzhen, China), with drilling operations guided under stereoscopic microscopic visualization.

Viral suspensions were stereotaxically infused bilaterally into the mPFC at stereotaxic coordinates: anteroposterior (AP) + 2.3 mm, mediolateral (ML) ± 0.3 mm, dorsoventral (DV) − 2.0 mm. Infusion was performed using glass micropipettes coupled to a Nanoliter microsyringe pump with a Micro21 controller unit (World Precision Instruments, Sarasota, FL, USA), with a consistent infusion rate fixed at 30 nL per minute. Upon completion of microinjection, the pipette was held stationary within the brain parenchyma for a 5 min dwell period to prevent viral backflow, followed by slow and gradual retraction. Co-immunoprecipitation and liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry

Affinity chromatography for mass spectrometric identification of MUP1 receptors were performed as described previously in ref. 76. Mice mPFCs were lysed in ice-cold immunoprecipitation lysis buffer (50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 1% NP-40, 0.5% deoxycholate) containing proteinase inhibitor mixture (Roche, UK). The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C and the concentration of the protein was estimated using the BCA kit. The resulting supernatant was adjusted to 1 mg/mL by adding lysis buffer. The lysates were pre-cleared with the mouse IgG (R&D Systems, USA) and anti-His magnetic beads (Catlog. 10104D, Invitrogen, USA), followed by overnight incubation with 20 µg recombinant mouse MUP1-His (LifeSpan BioSciences, Inc, USA) or normal IgG at 4 °C. Immunocomplexes were enriched using anti-His magnetic beads incubated for 2 h at 4 °C with continuous rotation. After three lysis buffer rinses, beads were boiled in SDS loading buffer (100 °C, 5 min). Short centrifugation (12,000 × g, 4 °C, 30 s) yielded supernatants separated by SDS-PAGE. Protein bands visualized via Coomassie staining were excised, destained, reduced with DTT, alkylated with iodoacetamide, and digested with trypsin. Peptide samples were sent to BGI (Beijing, China) for nano-LC-MS/MS analysis. Tryptic peptides were fractionated on an Ultimate 3000 UHPLC fitted with a C18 column (75 μm × 150 mm), eluted by a linear 0–60% acetonitrile gradient (400 nL/min). Mobile phases A and B were water and acetonitrile, each supplemented with 0.1% formic acid. Eluted peptides were ionized by nanoESI (1800 V) and analyzed on a Q Exactive Orbitrap mass spectrometer. Full scans were acquired at resolution 70,000 (m/z 200) with an AGC target of 3 × 10⁶. Raw spectra were converted to MGF files via Protein Discovery 1.3 and searched against the mouse Swiss-Prot database using Mascot 2.3. Search parameters: precursor tolerance 15 ppm, fragment tolerance 20 mmu, variable modification of methionine oxidation. PANTHER and UniProt databases were applied to screen and retain only cell-surface and transmembrane proteins.

Preparation of recombinant mouse proteins and peptides

cDNAs encoding full-length recombinant mouse MUP1, full-length STXBP1, or GLT1(aa143–234) were cloned into the pMAL-c5x vector (New England BioLabs) for the expression of N-terminal hexahistidine-tagged fusion proteins. MUP1, STXBP1, and GLT1(aa143–234) were heterologously expressed in E. coli BL21-Gold (DE3) pLysS cells (Agilent Technologies). In parallel, the GLT1-derived peptide fragments corresponding to GLT1(aa146–163) and GLT1(aa186–234) were obtained via chemical synthesis.

For protein expression, 80 mL of an overnight bacterial culture was inoculated into 800 mL of 2 × TY medium supplemented with 100 mg/L ampicillin. The culture was incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 210 revolutions per minute (rpm) until the optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) reached 0.6. Protein expression was induced by adding 0.1 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), followed by continued incubation at 30 °C for 3 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 7000 × g for 15 min, and the resulting pellets were resuspended in PBS containing one complete protease inhibitor cocktail tablet (Roche) per 50 mL of buffer and 0.2 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF). The resuspended pellets were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C prior to purification.

For protein purification, frozen cell pellets were thawed slowly on ice and lysed by sonication at 4 °C. Bacterial lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 35,000 × g and 4 °C for 60 min, filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane filter, and loaded onto a 5 mL HisTrap HP column (GE Healthcare) pre-equilibrated with PBS containing 20 mM imidazole. The eluate was further subjected to anion-exchange chromatography using a 5 mL HiTrap Q HP column (GE Healthcare), followed by size-exclusion chromatography on a HiLoad Superdex 200 PG 16/600 column (GE Healthcare). Purified proteins were resolved by 4–12% Bis-Tris sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), visualized by staining with PageBlue™ Protein Staining Solution (Catalog No. 24620, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and verified by Western blot analysis. Fractions containing homogeneous target proteins were pooled, dialyzed against PBS, concentrated, aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C.

The truncated GLAST (aa146–236) and SYT1 (aa1–57) peptides were custom synthesized via conventional solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS) by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Peptide assembly was conducted on a Protein Technologies PS3 automated synthesizer at a 0.1 mmol scale, with Fmoc-L-tyrosine-preloaded Wang resin (Novabiochem) serving as the stationary support. Fmoc-blocked amino acids (Chem-Impex) were coupled using HBTU (Novabiochem) as the condensation additive, together with N-methylmorpholine dissolved in DMF, with each coupling reaction lasting 20 min. Fmoc group removal was completed via two 5 min treatments with 20% piperidine diluted in DMF. Acetic anhydride combined with N-methylmorpholine was applied to acetylate the peptide N-terminus. After full peptide chain elongation, side-chain cleavage and resin detachment were implemented by incubating resin-conjugated peptides in neat trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) for 2 h under nitrogen protection. TFA was then volatilized with nitrogen gas, and crude peptides were harvested via precipitation using pre-cooled diethyl ether, followed by three ether rinses and ambient air drying. Target peptides were purified by RP-HPLC equipped with an Agilent ZORBAX EclipsePlus C18 column (1.8 μm, 2.1 × 50 mm). A 40 min linear acetonitrile gradient (3–70% in 0.1% TFA aqueous solution) was adopted for gradient elution. Final peptide purity and molecular identity were verified by LC-MS/MS detection.

Biolayer Interferometry Assay (BLI)

Intermolecular binding affinities between recombinant MUP1 and its candidate interaction partners, including full-length STXBP1, truncated GLT1 (amino acids 143–234), truncated GLAST (amino acids 146–236), as well as the N-terminal fragment of SYT1 (amino acids 1–57), were quantified via biolayer interferometry using the Octet RED 96 platform (Pall ForteBio Corp., Menlo Park, CA, USA)77. All liquid handling procedures were carried out within black 96-well polypropylene flat-bottom microplates manufactured by Greiner Bio-One (Frickenhausen, Germany), with each well loaded with 180–200 μL of assay buffer. The entire binding acquisition workflow ran under a constant temperature of 30 °C, accompanied by continuous plate shaking set to 1000 rpm throughout data recording. Two distinct biosensor types were deployed based on the immobilization strategy of each target analyte. Amine Reactive second-generation (AR2G) sensors were adopted to covalently attach recombinant mouse MUP1, enabling subsequent binding measurements against STXBP1 and the truncated GLT1 peptide spanning residues 143 to 234. For truncated GLAST (aa 146–236) and SYT1 fragment (aa 1–57), streptavidin-coated (SA) biosensors from Sartorius were utilized instead; in this setup, MUP1 served as the mobile-phase analyte flowing across sensor surfaces preloaded with the two peptide substrates. Real-time kinetic traces documenting pairwise molecular associations were captured on the Octet RED instrument for all experimental groups. A standardized multi-step measurement workflow was followed for every biosensor probe. First, each sensor was submerged in pure assay buffer for a 1 min wash interval to strip loosely adhered contaminants and eliminate unbound residual protein molecules. Sensors were then transferred to blank buffer wells and maintained for 100 s to establish a stable baseline optical signal prior to binding recording. To capture association kinetics, surface-functionalized sensors were immersed in serial dilutions of each binding partner for a 180 s association phase. Immediately after association recording, sensors were moved back into buffer-only wells for a prolonged 300 s dissociation phase to track ligand detachment from immobilized protein. Raw sensor traces were baseline-corrected against blank buffer controls to eliminate background signal interference. Global curve fitting was completed using the proprietary analytical software bundled with the Octet RED system (Pall ForteBio, CA, USA). The magnitude of protein complex formation was reported as spectral wavelength displacement (nanometer shift), calculated as the net difference in optical signal intensity between the initial baseline and plateau binding signal within each complete kinetic cycle. All binding sensorgrams were modeled with a monovalent 1:1 binding algorithm to extract core kinetic parameters: association rate constant (Kon), dissociation rate constant (Koff), and equilibrium dissociation constant (Kd). Any kinetic traces exhibiting poor fitting reliability (coefficient of determination R² <0.90), typically arising from heterogeneous multivalent binding behaviors, were discarded and excluded from subsequent quantitative affinity comparisons.

Whole-cell patch-clamp recording

The fresh brain tissue sections (300 μm) were prepared as previously described in ref. 78 and incubated for 60 min in oxygenated artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF). The ACSF contained 124 mM NaCl, 25 mM NaHCO3, 2.5 mM KCl, 1 mM KH2PO4, 2 mM CaCl2, 2 mM MgSO4, and 10 mM glucose, and was continuously bubbled with 95% O2/5% CO2. Slices were visualized under an Olympus microscope with infrared differential interference contrast optics, and whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were performed using an Axon 200B amplifier (Axon Instruments, Union City, CA, USA). Glass microelectrodes with 3–5 MΩ tip resistance were filled with an internal solution (pH 7.2) consisting of 145 mM potassium gluconate, 5 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 0.2 mM EGTA, 10 mM HEPES, 2 mM MgATP, and 0.1 mM Na3GTP. mPFC pyramidal neurons were identified via morphological and electrophysiological characteristics and recorded under current-clamp mode to capture action potentials (APs) after 100 μM picrotoxin perfusion. Miniature excitatory postsynaptic currents (mEPSCs) were recorded with 1 μM tetrodotoxin (TTX) present to block voltage-gated sodium channels. Series resistance was continuously monitored during recordings. Data with resistance fluctuations over 15% were excluded from analysis. All signals were digitized at a 10 kHz sampling rate for offline processing. To assess the effects of L-2-Aminoadipic acid (L-AAA; Sigma, Cat. A7275) on MUP1-induced mEPSC potentiation, mice received stereotaxic bilateral mPFC guide cannula implantation and recovered for at least one week postoperatively. Mice were given daily bilateral mPFC microinjections of 100 μg/μL L-AAA or sterile saline (0.5 μL per hemisphere, 0.15 μL/min) for two consecutive days before electrophysiological recordings. To probe the mechanisms of MUP1-mediated synaptic regulation, pharmacological inhibitors were bath-applied prior to MUP1 treatment to stabilize baseline activity. Drug working concentrations were as follows: 10 μM UCPH-101 (GLAST inhibitor, HY-10914), 1 μM SCH-23390 (D1-like receptor antagonist, HY-19545A), 1 μM CGS 15943 (adenosine receptor antagonist, HY-100678), 1 μM MPEP (mGlu5 antagonist, HY-14609), and 300 μM dihydrokainic acid (GLT1 inhibitor, HY-100784R); all compounds were purchased from MedChemExpress unless otherwise noted.

Electron microscopic immunohistochemistry

Mice were deeply anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 1% sodium pentobarbital at a dosage of 50 mg/kg body weight. Transcardial perfusion was subsequently performed with 20 mL of sterile normal saline, followed by a 1-hour perfusion with 40 mL of ice-cold fixative solution consisting of 4% paraformaldehyde and 0.05% glutaraldehyde dissolved in 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Brains were harvested, and tissue blocks encompassing the mPFC region were dissected and post-fixed by immersion in the same fixative at 4 °C for 4 h. Coronal serial sections (50 μm thickness) were prepared using a vibratome (VT 1000S, Leica), with 5–10 sections containing the mPFC collected per mouse brain for subsequent analyses.

Immunogold-silver staining was employed to detect MUP1 localization. Briefly, tissue sections were incubated in a blocking buffer (5% bovine serum albumin [BSA], 5% normal goat serum [NGS], and 0.05% Triton X-100 in PBS) to eliminate non-specific binding. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with a rabbit polyclonal primary antibody against MUP1 (1:100 dilution; Abcam, Catlog. ab95198), diluted in a buffer composed of 1% BSA, 1% NGS, and 0.05% Triton X-100 in PBS. After thorough rinsing, sections were incubated for 4 h at room temperature with a secondary antibody: 1.4 nm gold particle-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (1:100 dilution; Nanoprobes, Yaphank, NY, USA). Post-incubation rinsing was followed by a 45 min fixation step in 2% glutaraldehyde (in PBS) to stabilize the immunocomplexes. Silver enhancement was conducted in complete darkness using the HQ Silver Kit (Nanoprobes) to amplify the gold signal for visualization of MUP1 immunoreactivity. Sections were rinsed extensively with deionized water both before and after the silver enhancement procedure to remove excess reagents.

Following immunolabeling, sections were fixed in 0.5% osmium tetroxide (in 0.1 M PBS) for 1 hour, subjected to gradient dehydration in an ethanol series (30–100%) and propylene oxide, and then flat-embedded in Epon 812 resin between two sheets of acrylic plastic. After resin polymerization at 60 °C for 48 h, the acrylic sheets were peeled off, and the embedded sections were screened under a light microscope to identify regions with positive MUP1 immunoreactivity in the mPFC. Three to four target sections per brain were selected, trimmed to a size of ~1 mm² under a stereomicroscope, and mounted onto blank resin stubs. Ultrathin serial sections (70 nm thickness) were cut using an EM UC6 ultramicrotome (Leica) fitted with a diamond knife (Diatome, Nidau, Switzerland), and mounted onto formvar-coated copper mesh grids (6–8 sections per grid). Grids were counterstained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate to enhance ultrastructural contrast, and examined under a JEM-1230 transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a charge-coupled device (CCD) camera (Model 832 SC1000, Gatan, Pleasanton, CA, USA) and its proprietary imaging software. Electron micrographs were randomly acquired from the mPFC. Quantification was performed at a final magnification of 60,000×. MUP1-immunoreactive gold particles were quantified in observed axon-dendrite-astrocyte tripartite synaptic terminals forming asymmetrical synapses on postsynaptic spines, as well as in dendritic shafts, axons, and astrocytes (identified by the presence of intermediate filaments and/or contact with capillaries).

Cell culture

Primary astrocyte cultures were purified from postnatal day 1–2 C57BL/6 J mouse neonates via a classic differential adhesion separation workflow. Following tissue dissociation, single-cell suspensions were plated in 25 cm² cell culture flasks and incubated in complete Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; catalog No. 11965092, Thermo Fisher Scientific). This basal medium was supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; catalog No. A5256701, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 10 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin as antibiotic additives. After 6–7 days of in vitro cultivation, monolayers reached full confluency. Culture vessels were then tightly sealed and placed on an orbital shaker for overnight agitation (250 rpm, 37 °C, 16 h) to remove loosely attached microglia and other contaminating cell populations. Subsequent immunofluorescence labeling targeting the astrocytic marker GFAP validated the purity of adherent cell monolayers, with astrocyte populations exceeding 95%. The human glioblastoma U251 cell line was procured from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) and propagated using the exact same complete culture medium described above. Mycoplasma surveillance testing was performed on all cellular cultures on a monthly basis throughout passaging; only batches confirmed to lack mycoplasma contamination were employed for subsequent functional assays.

Collection of human brain tissues and serum samples from children with ASD

Brain tissue specimens were collected from a 64-year-old female who underwent surgical resection for a brain abscess in the left prefrontal cortex. Written informed consent was obtained prior to sample collection. During the surgery, about 1.2 cm brain abscess tissue was removed and stored immediately in -80°C for further analyses. All experimental protocols were performed in accordance with the guidelines for human‑subject research established by the Medical Ethics Committee of Xijing Hospital, Fourth Military Medical University (approval No. KY20193098).

ASD children aged 4–12 years old (30 males, 5 females) were recruited from nationwide rehabilitation training institutions and special education schools. Written informed consent was obtained from the guardians of all participants before serum sample collection. ASD diagnoses were made by experienced clinicians in accordance with the criteria of the DSM-IV or DSM-V, and detailed diagnostic criteria have been described in our previous studies79. Screening materials, including ASD-related background information, the Childhood Autism Rating Scale (CARS) and relevant instructions, were distributed to the children’s parents for questionnaire completion. Trained neurologists were available to answer questions raised by the respondents. CARS scores were applied to evaluate the severity of ASD symptoms and analyze their correlation with hPAEP levels. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Central South University (Approval No. KY-2021-1-6).

Molecular docking

All pairwise protein interaction simulations were carried out via the web-based ClusPro docking platform 1. Experimentally resolved three-dimensional structures of target proteins were retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank, including mouse MUP1 (PDB entry 1I04), human STXBP1 (PDB entry 3C98), and human GLT1 (PDB entry 7VR8). Since high-resolution crystallographic templates were unavailable for SYN1, SYT1, SYT7, and GLAST, their full-length structural conformations were computationally predicted using the Iterative Threading ASSEmbly Refinement (I-TASSER) pipeline. During docking setup, the polypeptide chain with fewer amino acid residues was designated as the ligand partner, while the longer protein was defined as the receptor molecule. The ligand underwent exhaustive conformational sampling covering a total of 70,000 distinct rotational orientations. For every rotational pose, translational shifts along the x, y, and z spatial axes were sampled across a predefined grid relative to the fixed receptor structure; only the translation variant yielding the most favorable energy score was retained per rotation cycle. From the full set of 70,000 rotation–translation combinations, the top 1000 conformations with minimal energy scores were screened out for downstream clustering. A greedy grouping algorithm was subsequently applied to these 1000 ligand configurations using a 9 Å Cα RMSD cutoff. This procedure identified cluster centroids corresponding to ligand poses bearing the highest density of neighboring structures within the 9 Å spatial threshold. The ten centroid structures containing the largest numbers of clustered conformers were extracted individually and subjected to manual visual inspection. Ultimately, intermolecular contact interfaces derived from the highest-probability binding conformations were characterized and compared.

Virtual screening

Structure-based virtual screening was carried out with the Dock module of Molecular Operating Environment (MOE, Version 1, Chemical Computing Group, Montreal, Canada). The Chemdiv compounds library was selected as VS small molecule library. All compounds were prepared with the Wash module in MOE. After that all compounds were docked to proteins, ranked by high throughput rigid docking with London △G scoring. Then the ranked top 15 K compounds were further selected to dock with proteins again, ranked by flexible docking with the “induced fit” protocol. Prior to docking, the force field of Assisted Model Building and Energy Refinement 12 (AMBER, version 12): Extended Hückel Theory and the implicit solvation model of Reaction Field were selected. The protonation state of the proteins and the orientation of the hydrogens were optimized by Quickprep module at the pH 7 and temperature of 300 K. For flexible docking, the docked poses were ranked by London △G scoring first, then a force field refinement was carried out on the top 10 poses followed by a rescoring of Generalized Born with Volume Integral / Weighted Surface Area (GBVI/WSA) △G, and the best ranked pose was selected for each compound. After flexible docking, the 15 K compounds were divided into structural clusters through fingerprint-based clustering (the distance parameter among clusters was set to 0.5), and the best-ranked compound in each cluster was set as cluster center. The best ranked 100 cluster centers were finally identified as potential hits.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism software was utilized to complete all statistical calculations. Normality distribution and homogeneity of variance were examined for every dataset prior to intergroup comparison. All quantitative results are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) unless specified otherwise. Two-sided statistical tests were adopted throughout the study, with significance markers annotated in corresponding figures: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001.
