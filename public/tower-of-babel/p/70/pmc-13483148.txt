An ECM-mimetic hydrogel for disc repair: reconstituting hypoxia and alleviating NPC senescence to halt intervertebral disc degeneration
Authors: Yifan Wang, Minglang Zou, Junyao Cheng, Cuiping Chen, Chuyue Zhang, Taoxu Yan, Xianzheng Wang, Zheng Tian, Zhenyue Zhang, Kaige Mao, Bo Li, Xuanhui Liu, Haoming Liu, Pengfei Chi, Zhimin Pan, Zuquan Weng, Da Huang, Jianheng Liu, Zheng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Intervertebral disc degeneration, the leading cause of chronic low back pain, remains incurable with traditional conservative therapies limited to symptomatic alleviation. We present an ECM-mimetic injectable hydrogel (HPTC) synthesized via dynamic crosslinking of hyaluronic acid-phenylboronic acid (HA-PBA) and tannic acid-cerium(III) metal-polyphenol networks (TA-Ce³⁺ MPNs), which faithfully recapitulates native nucleus pulposus ECM to enable functional tissue regeneration. In vitro, HPTC presented broad-spectrum reactive oxygen species scavenging and downregulated pro-inflammatory cytokine expression (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6), while upregulating anti-inflammatory markers (IL-4, IL-10). Crucially, Ce³⁺ effectively reduced dissolved oxygen levels (to 105% vs. 115% in control at 15 min), thereby promoting HIF-1α signal expression and mitigating nucleus pulposus cells senescence under H₂O₂-induced oxidative stress. In rat and rabbit intervertebral disc degeneration models, a single, minimally invasive injection of the ECM-mimetic HPTC hydrogel preserved the disc height index and magnetic resonance imaging signal intensity, enhanced aggrecan and collagen II deposition, suppressed inflammatory mediators, and elevated HIF-1α while reducing p21 expression in situ. Transcriptomic analysis further implicated HIF-1α pathways in ECM regeneration. All these findings demonstrate that the HPTC hydrogel leverages metal-polyphenol chemistry within an ECM-inspired framework to synergistically modulate oxygen homeostasis, oxidative stress, and inflammation, offering a bifunctional and biomimetic platform for disc regeneration.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04779-3.

Introduction

Intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD) represents a primary etiological factor for chronic low back pain and functional impairment [1]. The intervertebral disc (IVD) consists of three structural components: the nucleus pulposus, annulus fibrosus, and cartilaginous endplates [2–3]. Nucleus pulposus cells (NPCs), residing in the central region of the nucleus pulposus (NP), play a pivotal role in synthesizing and preserving key extracellular matrix (ECM) constituents, such as proteoglycans and type II collagen [2]. Healthy NP tissue possesses a storage modulus (G′) at the kilopascal (kPa) level and remarkable viscoelasticity, allowing it to cushion mechanical shocks and evenly distribute intervertebral disc loads [4–5]. The pathological hallmarks of IVDD include the progressive loss of ECM components within the NP tissue, accompanied by inflammation, oxidative stress, and cellular senescence [6–8]. Current conventional treatments, including physical therapy, analgesic medication, or surgery, can only alleviate symptoms but fail to restore the disc’s native structure [9–11]. Therefore, there is an urgent need for novel strategies to remodel the degenerative microenvironment and promote tissue regeneration.

Oxygen homeostasis plays a critical role in maintaining the IVD microenvironment. Under physiological conditions, NPCs reside in a hypoxic environment and maintain matrix homeostasis via metabolism regulated by HIF-1α [12]. However, during degeneration, neovascularization and structural disruption allow oxygen infiltration into the NP region [13]. Excessive oxygen promotes reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and exacerbates ECM degradation [14–15]. Moreover, the hyperoxic environment disrupts HIF-1α stability, impairing its role in promoting glycolysis and ECM synthesis [12], which further accelerates IVDD progression.

In addition to oxygen homeostasis, immune regulation is also essential for maintaining IVD homeostasis [6]. In the degenerative process, inflammatory factors and degrading enzymes continuously act to break down major ECM components, such as proteoglycans and type II collagen [16], while also compromising NPC viability and synthetic function [17], thereby amplifying ECM depletion. Thus, dysregulated oxygen homeostasis and immune imbalance often act synergistically to drive ECM dysregulation and NPC dysfunction, accelerating structural deterioration of the IVD.

Within this pathological context, restoring oxygen homeostasis and immune balance is considered a crucial approach to halting ECM degradation. Existing strategies, such as delivery of growth factors, cell transplantation, or inhibition of degrading enzymes, have shown limited efficacy within the complex IVD microenvironment [18–20]. Hao Liu et al. developed a microsphere-based system that recruits endogenous progenitor cells to promote IVD repair via ECM remodeling, though evidence of mechanical functional recovery remains lacking [21]. A more promising current strategy involves the implantation of biomaterial substitutes that mimic the native ECM or ECM-mimetic hydrogels. These materials replicate the mechanical properties, biochemical composition, and structural features of natural ECM [22–24]. By delivering bioactive factors, they modulate the pathological microenvironment, promote the synthesis and deposition of key components such as type II collagen and proteoglycans, and ultimately enhance cell survival and restore the biomechanical properties of the IVD.

Injectable hydrogels have become powerful tools for IVDD repair due to their high-water content and tunable mechanical properties [25]. Through designing dynamic cross-linking networks, these hydrogels can achieve self-healing properties and tissue adhesion. Moreover, physical cross-linking networks were introduced to endow hydrogels with additional biological functions, such as metal-phenolic networks (MPNs), which are supramolecular structures formed by the coordination-driven self-assembly of polyphenol ligands with metal ions [26–27]. Hyaluronic acid (HA), a natural polymer, serves as the hydrophilic backbone and recapitulates the native microenvironment of the nucleus pulposus. Grafting phenylboronic acid (PBA) onto HA generated dynamic boronate ester linkages with polyphenol catechols, which dynamically immobilized MPNs within the polymeric network [28–29]. On this basis, cerium-based MPNs mimic the activity of antioxidant enzymes via the redox cycling between Ce³⁺ / Ce⁴⁺. Ce³⁺ can directly scavenge various ROS and consume oxygen generated by free radical decomposition. Simultaneously, Ce³⁺ downregulates inflammatory factors such as IL-6, IL-1β and TNF-α, thereby inhibiting inflammation-induced ECM degradation [30–31]. Thus, TA-Ce³⁺ MPN-based hydrogels can locally scavenge ROS and reduce oxygen concentration, thereby restoring a hypoxic microenvironment conducive to ECM repair.

This study developed an ECM-mimetic injectable hydrogel (HPTC) aimed at remodeling the ECM microenvironment of degenerated IVD by modulating oxygen homeostasis and delaying cellular senescence. The HPTC hydrogel is formed by cross-linking HA-phenylboronic acid (HA-PBA) and TA-Ce³⁺ MPNs through reversible boronate ester bonds and coordination bonds, mimicking the structure of the natural NP’s ECM and conferring anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and hypoxia-inducing functions (Fig. 1A). In vitro experiments demonstrated that the HPTC hydrogel can broadly scavenge ROS and inhibit inflammatory factors, while significantly reducing dissolved oxygen concentration, thereby restoring HIF-1α signaling and delaying NPC senescence (Fig. 1B). In rat and rabbit IVDD models, a single injection of HPTC maintained IVD height and structure, promoted the deposition of proteoglycans and type II collagen, simultaneously suppressed inflammatory mediators, upregulated HIF-1α, and downregulated the senescence marker p21, enhancing ECM regeneration (Fig. 1C). In summary, this ECM-mimetic HPTC hydrogel synergistically remodeled the degenerative IVD microenvironment by restoring a hypoxic environment and delaying cellular senescence, thereby presenting a promising minimally invasive therapeutic approach for the treatment of IVDD.

Materials and methods

Materials

TA, cerium (III) sulfate, potassium persulfate, 1-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-3-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDCI), and 2,2′-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) diammonium salt (ABTS) were purchased from Aladdin Industrial Corporation (Shanghai, China). HA was obtained from Bloomage Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). 2,2-Diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), N-hydroxy succinimide (NHS), 3-aminophenylboronic acid (3-APBA), and pyrogallol were obtained from Energy Chemical (Shanghai, China). DMEM medium was acquired from Gibco (USA), and bovine serum albumin was purchased from Bioind (Israel). Lipopolysaccharide (LPS), Calcein AM cell viability assay kits, DCFH-DA ROS assay kits, and senescence-associated β-galactosidase staining kits were obtained from Beyotime (Shanghai, China). Other chemicals were also purchased from Aladdin and used without further purification.

Synthesis of hyaluronic acid-phenylboronic acid (HA-PBA)

HA-PBA was synthesized with minor modifications based on previously reported protocols [26]. Briefly, 1 g (2.5 mmol) of HA was dissolved in 100 mL of ultra-pure water, followed by the addition of EDCI and NHS at a molar ratio of -COOH: EDCI: NHS = 1:4:2. The solution was stirred for 1 hour before the addition of 2.74 g (20 mmol) of 3-APBA. The reaction was continued under constant stirring at 600 rpm for 24 h. The product was purified by dialysis (molecular weight cut-off: 14 kDa) and lyophilized. Product identity was confirmed by 1H NMR spectroscopy in D₂O (600 MHz, Bruker, USA).

Preparation and characterization of HPTC hydrogels

To prepare HPTC hydrogels, HA-PBA, TA, and Ce³⁺ were dissolved in ultrapure water at concentrations of 20, 40, and 20 mg mL⁻¹, respectively. The solutions were mixed and stirred, followed by the addition of 30 µL of 1 M NaOH to initiate gelation. For morphology observation, hydrogels were freeze-dried, sectioned, and platinum-coated before imaging with scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Quanta 250, FEI, USA).

To investigate interactions between components, four mixtures (HA-PBA-TA, HA-PBA-Ce³⁺, TA-Ce³⁺, and HPTC hydrogel) were dropped onto glass slides and dried at 60 °C. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were recorded on a Spectrum 3 spectrometer (Perkin-Elmer, UK) from 4000 to 400 cm⁻¹ (64 scans, resolution: 1 cm⁻¹).

Rheological measurements were performed using an MCR302 rheometer (Anton Paar, Austria) equipped with a 20 mm parallel-plate geometry at 25 °C. Strain sweeps were carried out at 10 Hz with strains ranging from 0.01% to 100%. Time sweeps were conducted at 10 Hz using strain amplitudes of 1% or 300%. Shear rate sweeps were recorded across a range of 0.1–100 s⁻¹.

Self-healing, injectability, and adhesion of HPTC hydrogel

Self-healing was visualized using two hydrogel samples, one stained pink with rhodamine B. The hydrogels were cut in half and rejoined by placing differently colored halves in contact for 20 min. Injectability was tested by loading 0.5 mL of HPTC hydrogel into a syringe and extruding it into PBS through a 24 G needle. Adhesion was assessed by applying 50 µL of HPTC hydrogel to fractured isolated rat spines and allowing bonding for 5 min. All procedures were documented with a digital camera.

Dissolved oxygen measurements

To evaluate Ce³⁺-mediated oxygen consumption during H₂O₂ decomposition, 16 mM H₂O₂ in PBS (pH 6.0) was treated with 64 mM Ce³⁺ or PBS for 16 min. Dissolved oxygen was measured at multiple time points (0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 16 min) using a portable oxygen meter (JPB-607 A, Shanghai Lecai Instruments, China).

Cell culture

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and 3T3-L1 preadipocytes were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Beijing, China). Primary nucleus pulposus cells (NPCs) were isolated from the caudal intervertebral discs of 6-week-old male Sprague-Dawley rats. All cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. Cells were passaged every 2–3 days, and only logarithmic growth phase cells were used for experiments.

Cytotoxicity assays

Hydrogel extracts were used to evaluate the cytotoxicity of potentially released substances, including tannic acid, cerium ions, tannic acid-cerium coordination fragments, unreacted residual components and hydrogel degradation products. HUVECs, 3T3-L1 and NPCs were seeded in 48-well plates at a seeding density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well. Following 24 h of incubation, the culture medium was replaced with an equivalent volume of fresh medium supplemented with hydrogel exudate solutions at concentrations spanning 0 to 200 mg mL⁻¹. After a further 24 h of incubation, the culture medium was substituted with a medium containing Calcein AM (1 µg mL⁻¹). Thirty minutes later, fluorescence intensity was detected at 520 nm (excitation wavelength: 490 nm) using a microplate reader. Cell viability was assessed by normalizing the fluorescence intensity of treated samples to that of the untreated control group. All in vitro cytotoxicity assays were independently repeated three times, with three technical replicates per group.

Hemolysis assays

A 10% red blood cell suspension was prepared from fresh rabbit blood and diluted with PBS. This suspension (0.95 mL) was added to centrifuge tubes containing HA-PBA (0.05 mL, 20 mg mL⁻¹), TA (0.05 mL, 40 mg mL⁻¹), Ce³⁺ (0.05 mL, 10 mg mL⁻¹), and HPTC hydrogel (50 mg). Tubes containing deionized water and PBS were employed as positive and negative controls, respectively. Following incubation at 37 °C for 1 h, the samples were centrifuged at 3,500 rpm for 5 min. The absorbance of the resulting supernatant was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader. In vitro antibacterial assays

Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), Escherichia coli (E. coli), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa), and Acinetobacter baumannii (A. baumannii) were chosen to assess hydrogel antibacterial activity by plate counting. Either the HA‑PBA solution (20 mg mL⁻¹) or the HPTC hydrogel (100 mg) was placed in a centrifuge tube containing 2 mL bacterial suspension (1.0 × 10⁷ CFU mL⁻¹). After 6 h incubation, OD₆₀₀ was recorded with a microplate reader, and surviving bacteria were quantified on agar plates.

Intracellular ROS scavenging and anti-inflammatory properties

RAW 264.7 macrophages (purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, Beijing, China) were seeded in 48-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well and cultured for 24 h, then stimulated with 100 ng mL⁻¹ LPS for 6 h (400 µL well⁻¹). Controls received medium alone. LPS‑primed cells were incubated for 24 h with HA‑PBA (20 mg mL⁻¹), HPT (8 mg), or HPTC hydrogel (8 mg). Cells were loaded with DCFH‑DA (10 µM, 30 min, dark) and fluorescence (Ex 488 nm/Em 525 nm) was measured. ROS localization was visualized by CLSM (Nikon) and fluorescence intensity was quantified via flow cytometry (Attune™ NxT). Expression of IL‑1β, IL‑4, IL‑6, IL‑10, and TNF‑α was analyzed by RT‑qPCR. Total RNA was isolated with TRIzol, reverse‑transcribed, and amplified using SYBR Green premix (TransGen, Beijing). Primer sequences (Table S1) were synthesized by Shan Ya (Shanghai, China). GAPDH served as the endogenous control.

Senescence-associated β-galactosidase staining and RT-qPCR

NP cells (1 × 10⁴ cells well⁻¹, 24‑well plates) were cultured for 24 h, then incubated with 100 µM H₂O₂ for 6 h (400 µL well⁻¹) to induce senescence; controls received fresh medium. Cells were subsequently treated for 24 h with HA‑PBA (20 mg mL⁻¹), HPT (8 mg), or HPTC hydrogel (8 mg). Senescence was assessed with β‑galactosidase staining (Beyotime) per kit instructions. Expression of p16, p21, ERK2, HIF‑1α, and GLUT1 was quantified by RT‑qPCR as above using primer sets as detailed in Table S2; GAPDH was the internal reference.

Western blot

Cell lysis was carried out using a pre-chilled lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors, followed by incubation on ice for 30 min to extract total proteins. Protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE electrophoresis and then transferred onto PVDF membranes via the wet transfer method. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk at room temperature for 1 h and subsequently incubated sequentially with primary antibodies (incubated overnight at 4 ℃) and horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (incubated for 1 h at room temperature). Details of the key antibodies used are as follows: p16 (ab51243, Affinity), p21 (ab109520, Affinity), ERK2 (ab32081, Affinity), GLUT1 (ab115730, Affinity), HIF-1α (BF8002, Affinity), Aggrecan (DF7561, Affinity), ColⅡ (AF0135, Affinity), TNF-α (AF7014, Affinity), and IL-1β (AF5103, Affinity).

In vivo evaluation of IVDD model in rats

Male Sprague-Dawley rats (6 weeks old, 250–300 g, Beijing MDKN Biotechnology Co., LTD) were acclimatized for one week under standard conditions (22 °C, 55% humidity, 12-hour light/dark cycle). This experiment was approved by the Animal Welfare Ethics Committee of Beijing MDKN Biotechnology Co., LTD. (No. MDKN-2024-108) and was conducted in strict accordance with the experimental animal care and use guidelines of Beijing Animal Control Committee. Animals (250–300 g, n = 5 per group) were randomly assigned: control group (no puncture), defect group (puncture, injection with 10 µL of PBS), HA-PBA group (puncture, injection with 10 µL HA-PBA solution), HPT group (puncture, injection with 10 µL HPT mixture solution), and HPTC hydrogel group (puncture, injection with 10 µL of HPTC hydrogel). Under 3% isoflurane anesthesia, caudal discs (Co3/4 to Co7/8) were percutaneously punctured using a 21-gauge needle (5 mm depth) with 360° rotation for 30 s. Immediately upon needle withdrawal, 10 µL of corresponding formulations were injected into the NP via 33-gauge micro-syringe. Postoperative analgesia (buprenorphine 0.05 mg/kg SC) was administered for 48 h.

The radiographic examination of lumbar IVDs was conducted utilizing a digital X-ray imaging system (Kubtec Model XPERT.8, USA). The disc height index (DHI) was subsequently determined, employing the established triple midline method using ImageJ software. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed using a high-field 3.0 T MRI system (Philips Intera Achieva 3.0 T MR, USA). T2-weighted MR images were acquired to evaluate the water content and structural health of the IVDs using imaging parameters, including a repetition time of 3,500 milliseconds and an echo time of 120 milliseconds. The field of view for the scans was specified at 200 × 200 mm, with a slice thickness of 1.4 mm to ensure detailed cross-sectional images. Quantitative analysis of the MRI scans involved measuring the gray values of the NP in the different samples using ImageJ software.

The IVD specimens were harvested after 4 or 8 weeks and then underwent decalcification using a 10% EDTA solution for a period of two months and were embedded in paraffin and subsequently sliced into sections with a thickness of 5 μm stained with Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, Safranin O-fast (S-O) green staining, and immunohistochemistry staining. Sections were imaged using a fluorescence microscope (ECLIPSE Ts2R, Nikon), and semi-quantitative analysis of the intensity of immunohistochemical staining was performed using ImageJ software.

Moreover, the NP tissue in PBS and HPTC hydrogel groups were lysed with Trizol reagent and immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen for RNA stabilization. All RNA was extracted, and its purity was evaluated using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. Subsequently, strand-specific cDNA libraries were constructed and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw sequencing results were trimmed with fastp software, aligned to the reference genome via HISAT2, and quantified with HTSeq; gene expression levels were normalized as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were screened using DESeq2 with the criteria of Q < 0.05 and |log2 fold-change| ≥ 1. Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs was performed utilizing the Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Reactome databases within the R software (version 3.2.0). Significant enrichment terms were also visualized with the ggplot2 package. Additionally, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted to uncover pathway-level molecular alterations.

In vivo evaluation of IVDD model in New Zealand white rabbit

Male New Zealand white rabbits (6–8 months, 2.5–3.5 kg, Beijing MDKN Biotechnology Co., LTD) were anesthetized with ketamine/xylazine (35/5 mg/kg IM). This experiment was approved by the Animal Welfare Ethics Committee of Beijing MDKN Biotechnology Co., LTD. (No. MDKN-2024-107) and was conducted in strict accordance with the experimental animal care and use guidelines of Beijing Animal Control Committee. Using a minimally invasive paramedian approach, lumbar spine segments (L1–L6) were exposed. An 18-gauge needle was inserted to induce partial annulus fibrosus rupture and NP removal. Segments were randomly assigned to five experimental groups using a random number table: Control group (no puncture), PBS group (puncture and injection with 20 µL PBS), HA-PBA group (puncture and injection with 20 µL HA-PBA solution), HPT group (puncture and injection with 20 µL HPT mixture solution), and HPTC hydrogel group (puncture and injection with 20 µL HPTC hydrogel). All injections were administered immediately post-puncture. Postoperative analgesia (buprenorphine 0.03 mg/kg SC) was provided for 72 h.

The examination of X-ray and MRI was conducted using the same methods as the rat’s model. Lumbar discs were harvested after 4 or 8 weeks, and the IVD samples underwent grinding-section histology. IVD samples were sawed into sheets 1–2 mm thick and fully fixed with neutral buffered formaldehyde. Use a grinding machine to grind until the disc structure was clearly visible and then terminate grinding in due course. The bone slices were then transferred carefully in enough water until the liquid was clear. H&E and SO/FG staining were then performed.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). An unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-test was used for comparisons between two groups. For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001). All statistical analyses and plotting were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.5.0.

Results

Preparation and characterization of the HPTC hydrogel

The reduced ECM synthesis, disrupted redox homeostasis, and elevated oxygen levels observed in degenerated NP tissues provide important cues for hydrogel design. The fabrication of HPTC hydrogel was achieved through a process wherein TA and Ce³⁺ ions formed MPNs following a reaction with HA-PBA under deprotonated conditions (Fig. 2A). HA was functionalized with 3-aminophenylboronic acid (3-APBA) via an amination reaction to yield the hyaluronic acid-phenylboronic acid conjugate (HA-PBA) (Fig. S1A). Successful grafting of PBA was confirmed by 1H NMR spectroscopy, revealing a grafting efficiency of 36.25% (Fig. S1B). HA-PBA was subsequently mixed with TA-Ce³⁺ MPNs, and rapid gelation was induced by pH adjustment and stirring (Fig. 2B). Given the critical role of hydrogel pore size in drug release, degradation, and cellular or tissue interactions, the cross-sectional morphology of the hydrogel was examined using scanning electron microscopy. As shown in Fig. 2C, the HPTC hydrogel exhibited a porous structure with pore sizes ranging from 50 to 80 μm, conducive to mass exchange and cellular infiltration.

FTIR spectroscopy was further employed to investigate molecular interactions and the cross-linking mechanism. In the HA-PBA-Ce³⁺ (HP-Ce³⁺) system, compared to HA-PBA alone, the characteristic contraction and stretching vibrations of key groups were absent in the FTIR spectra of the HP-Ce³⁺ mixture (Fig. 2D) suggesting minimal interactions between HA-PBA and Ce³⁺. In the HA-PBA-TA (HPT) system, attenuation of the 3411 cm⁻¹ peak (-OH from TA) and enhancement of the 3443 cm⁻¹ peak (-OH from HA-PBA) indicated borate bond formation between phenolic hydroxyl and phenylboronic acid groups (Fig. 2E). Similarly, as shown in Fig. 2F, attenuation of the 3411 cm⁻¹ peak in TA-Ce³⁺ mixtures confirmed the formation of coordination bonds in the TA-Ce³⁺ MPNs. These spectral changes were also observed in the HPTC hydrogel (Fig. 2G), verifying the coexistence of borate bonding and TA-Ce³⁺ coordination as physical cross-linking mechanisms stabilizing the hydrogel network.

To fulfill the requirements of intradiscal injection into the NP, the hydrogel must possess well-defined rheological behaviors that demonstrate mechanical integrity, self-healing, injectability, and retention at the target site. A prerequisite for such functionality is the successful formation of a stable hydrogel network, which was confirmed by oscillatory strain sweeps. As shown in Fig. 2H, the storage modulus (G′) of the HPTC hydrogel remained consistently higher than the loss modulus (G″) across a wide strain range (0.01–100%), with G′ reaching approximately 230 Pa. This indicates the formation of a soft, yet solid-like viscoelastic network suitable for minimally invasive delivery.

During injections through narrow-bore needles and subsequent exposure to biomechanical stress within the disc, the hydrogel must not only flow easily under pressure but also rapidly re-establish its network structure post-deformation. Thus, the self-healing and injectable behaviors of HPTC were also confirmed. As shown in Fig. 2I, when blocks of HPTC hydrogel with different colors were cut, brought into contact, and then incubated at 25 °C for 30 min, they formed a new hydrogel that could be lifted with tweezers and did not detach when inverted. Rheological recovery tests further demonstrated that under alternating strains (1% and 300%), G′ fully recovered within three cycles (Fig. 2J), indicating reversible network dynamics driven by borate ester bonds and TA-Ce³⁺ coordination. Injectability was validated through syringe extrusion tests. As shown in Fig. 2K, the HPTC hydrogel could be smoothly injected into water through a syringe, maintaining cohesion upon extrusion. Concurrently, shear-thinning behavior was evident as the viscosity of the hydrogel decreased with increasing shear rate (Fig. 2L), facilitating ease of injection while ensuring re-solidification post-deployment.

Given the compressive and dynamic microenvironment of the IVD, tissue adhesion is equally essential to ensure hydrogel retention and prevent migration or leakage after injection. In an ex vivo rat spinal model, the HPTC hydrogel effectively adhered two transected vertebral segments upon injection (Fig. 2M). This adhesive behavior is attributed to the catechol groups of tannic acid, which mimic muscle-inspired wet adhesion and enable strong interfacial bonding. Such adhesion enhances therapeutic localization and reduces the need for repeated injections. Collectively, these results highlight the multifunctionality of the HPTC hydrogel, including self-healing, injectability, and adhesiveness, making HPTC hydrogel highly suitable for intradiscal therapeutic delivery under the biomechanical constraints of the spinal environment.

In vitro and in vivo biocompatibility of the HPTC hydrogel

Injected hydrogels intended for the treatment of degenerative NP in IVDD must demonstrate high biocompatibility to avoid adverse immune responses. The in vitro and in vivo biocompatibility of HPTC hydrogel were assessed by evaluating its cytocompatibility, hemocompatibility, and histocompatibility. For the cytocompatibility assays, the HUVECs, 3T3-L1 and NPCs, as typical model cells, were incubated with hydrogel exudate of different concentrations for 24 h. As shown in Fig. 3A, fluorescence microscopy showed that live cells (green-stained) retained normal morphology and were comparable in number to 0 mg mL− 1, until displaying a reduction of live cells at 150 and 200 mg mL− 1, of which is much larger than concentration in actual application. Quantitative viability assays confirmed the continuous decrease of cell viability with concentrations of hydrogel exudate increasing, but most of them were over or approaching 75%, indicating excellent cytocompatibility of HPTC hydrogel (Fig. 3B-D).

To assess the blood compatibility of the HPTC hydrogel, a hemolysis assay was conducted, as the hemolytic rate serves as a critical indicator of biomaterial compatibility. Red blood cells were incubated with HPTC hydrogel or its different compositions for 1 h. As shown in Fig. 3E, the hemolytic rates of the HA-PBA, TA, Ce3+, and HPTC hydrogel groups were below 5%, although the supernatant of red blood cells treated with Ce3+ appeared faint red, indicating excellent blood compatibility. Furthermore, histological examination of major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys, demonstrated no pathological abnormalities in either rats treated with HA-PBA, HPT, HPTC hydrogel or control animals at 2 months (Fig. 3F). These results underscore the systemic biosafety of the HPTC hydrogel, which can be attributed to its clinically relevant and biocompatible constituents: HA and TA are natural products and clinically applied agents with well-known biocompatibility, and cerium(III) nitrate is a raw material to synthesis MOFs biomaterials for biomedical applications. These in vitro and in vivo findings confirm the excellent biocompatibility of HPTC hydrogel and suggest its potential for biomedical applications.

Intradiscal injections, especially under non-ideal aseptic conditions such as those in resource-limited clinical settings, carries a tangible risk of introducing pathogenic bacteria into the intervertebral disc space. To address this, the antibacterial capacity of the hydrogel was evaluated to determine its potential to reduce infection-related complications following injection. HA-PBA and HPTC hydrogels were co-incubated with four clinically relevant pathogens including S. aureus, E. coli, P. aeruginosa, and A. baumannii and their antibacterial effects were assessed via optical density (OD₆₀₀) measurements and colony-forming unit (CFU) enumeration. As shown in Figs. S2A–D, both groups had markedly reduced OD₆₀₀ values, indicating a substantial decline in bacterial density. Plate counts corroborated these findings that cultures treated with either type of hydrogel displayed sparse colonies, whereas the untreated controls exhibited dense bacterial growth (Fig. S2E). Quantitative analysis further revealed that the TA-Ce MPNs-containing HPTC hydrogel achieved > 90% inhibition against all four pathogens, whereas the HA-PBA hydrogel alone afforded only 55–90% inhibition (Figs. S2F–I). These results indicate that the boronic acid moieties in HA-PBA are insufficient for excellent antibacterial performance and that the incorporation of the natural antimicrobial TA significantly enhances the hydrogel’s bactericidal efficacy, thereby mitigating the risk of infection caused by injection.

In vitro antioxidative and anti-inflammatory activities of the HPTC hydrogel

Oxidative stress level is balanced in a healthy IVD environment. However, degenerative NP tissue induces excessive oxidative reactions and activates immune cells triggering severe inflammatory responses. This pathological process may lead to the formation of an acidic microenvironment characterized by elevated levels of ROS and inflammatory cytokines. Therefore, hydrogels with potent ROS-scavenging capabilities can significantly delay IVDD. The antioxidative activity of HPTC hydrogels was first evaluated through free radical scavenging assays. As shown in Fig. 4A–C, with increasing concentrations of HPTC hydrogel extract and decreasing pH, the free radical scavenging activity of the hydrogel against DPPH• and •O2⁻ progressively increased to 60–80%, although no significant pH-dependent scavenging response was observed for ABTS+•. Previous studies have demonstrated that TA-based MPNs exhibit pH-responsive dissociation and release behavior. Accordingly, the TA-Ce MPNs inherit this characteristic, enabling them to respond to the acidic microenvironment of the degenerated NP by accelerating dissociation and cargo release. This facilitates more rapid clearance of ROS, thereby enhancing their therapeutic efficacy in situ.

Subsequently, intracellular ROS scavenging in vitro was evaluated by inducing oxidative stress in RAW 264.7 macrophages using LPS, followed by treatments with HA-PBA, HPT, and HPTC hydrogel. The ROS-sensitive fluorescent probe DCFH-DA was employed to detect intracellular ROS levels. As shown in Fig. 4D, where ROS are stained with green fluorescence, macrophages in the LPS group and HA-PBA group exhibited prominent green fluorescence covering with blue fluorescence (nucleus) compared to the control group, indicating that these macrophages were under oxidative stress and high levels of ROS within the macrophages. In contrast, macrophages treated with HPT and HPTC hydrogel appeared as minimal green fluorescence, indicating a lower level of ROS within these macrophages. Quantitative analysis of the fluorescence intensity of ROS confirmed these observations, showing that the average fluorescence intensity in the LPS and HA-PBA groups were significantly higher than that in the control group, HPT group, and HPTC hydrogel group (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, the average fluorescence intensity in the three groups was similar with no significant differences. These results indicate that TA plays a critical role in radical scavenging within the HPTC hydrogel system, thereby contributing significantly to mitigating IVDD.

Beyond the challenges posed by oxidative stress, dysregulated inflammation significantly promotes IVDD with macrophages playing a pivotal role in this inflammatory response. To explore the anti-inflammatory properties of the HPTC hydrogel, RAW264.7 macrophages were stimulated with LPS to induce an inflammatory state. Subsequently, they were incubated with HA-PBA, HPT, and HPTC hydrogel, followed by examination of the levels of inflammatory cytokines TNF- IL-1, IL-4, IL-6, and IL-10. As shown in Fig. 4F–H, macrophages treated with HPT or HPTC hydrogels exhibited significantly reduced levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, compared to the LPS and HA-PBA groups, which showed markedly elevated levels relative to the untreated control. Notably, the groups containing TA (HPT and HPTC hydrogels) also demonstrated a significant upregulation of anti-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-4 and IL-10 (Fig. 4I–J). These findings suggest that the TA-Ce MPNs can modulate the inflammatory cytokine profile of macrophages, offering a potential strategy to suppress the inflammatory cascade associated with IVDD. Moreover, DCFH-DA flow cytometry (Fig. 4K–L) confirmed that the HPTC hydrogel significantly attenuated LPS-induced ROS generation, reduced early oxidative stress levels, and mitigated injury-associated microenvironment disruption.

The ability to alleviate NPCs senescence of the HPTC hydrogel

In IVDD, excessive oxidative stress and inflammation contribute to increased local oxygen levels, for example, the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide releases molecular oxygen. This oxygen-enriched microenvironment can be detrimental to NPCs with cell senescence occurrence or ECM breakdown, which are physiologically adapted to hypoxic conditions. Therefore, reducing oxygen overaccumulation is beneficial for promoting disc repair. To verify the capacity of Ce³⁺ ions in the HPTC hydrogel to lower oxygen concentration, the hydrogel was co-incubated with hydrogen peroxide, and the dissolved oxygen content in the mixture was measured at various time points using a dissolved oxygen meter (Fig. 5A). As shown in Fig. 5B, in the absence of treatment, hydrogen peroxide underwent spontaneous decomposition, leading to a gradual increase in dissolved oxygen concentration over time. In contrast, upon the addition of Ce³⁺, although a slight increase in dissolved oxygen was still observed, the oxygen concentration after 15 min reached only 105% of the initial level, significantly lower than that of the untreated hydrogen peroxide group (115%). This indicates that Ce³⁺ ions effectively attenuate oxygen accumulation by promoting the catalytic conversion of ROS. Specifically, in the pathological microenvironment of IVDD, oxygen-containing free radicals react to generate H₂O₂, which subsequently decomposes and contributes to elevated oxygen levels (Phase 1, Fig. 5C). However, Ce³⁺ can undergo redox reactions with hydrogen peroxide, preventing the increase of molecular oxygen (Phase 2, Fig. 5C).

To further evaluate the ability of HPTC hydrogel to mitigate hydrogen peroxide–induced cellular senescence resulting from elevated oxygen levels, NPCs were co-incubated with hydrogen peroxide and subsequently treated with HA-PBA, HPT, or HPTC hydrogels. Senescence was assessed using senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) staining and the expression of senescence-associated markers p16 and p21. As shown in Fig. 5D, extensive blue staining indicative of high β-galactosidase activity and a hallmark of cellular senescence was observed in the H₂O₂ and HA-PBA groups. In contrast, NPCs treated with hydrogels, particularly the HPTC hydrogel, showed minimal blue staining, suggesting reduced senescence. This effect is attributed to the presence of Ce³⁺ ions, which likely suppressed oxidative stress and its downstream effects. Quantification of SA-β-Gal positive cells further supported these findings that the percentage of senescent cells was significantly higher in the H₂O₂ and HA-PBA groups compared to both the HPT and HPTC groups, with no significant difference between the HPTC and untreated control groups (Fig. 5E). Moreover, the expression of senescence-associated markers p16 and p21 showed a similar trend, where HPT and HPTC hydrogel significantly downregulated the over-expressions of p16 and p21 genes caused by H₂O₂, with no obvious difference compared to the control groups (Fig. 5F-G).

Moreover, excess oxygen within the IVD attenuates or inactivates the hypoxia‑inducible factor (HIF) signaling cascade, undermining glycolysis, ECM synthesis, and other essential cellular processes, thereby accelerating NPC senescence. Accordingly, we quantified the transcriptional levels of HIF‑1α and its pathway genes. As shown in Figs. 5H–J, NPCs treated with either HPT or HPTC hydrogels displayed marked up‑regulation of HIF‑1α, the upstream kinase ERK2, and the downstream glycolytic transporter GLUT1, with the Ce³⁺‑containing HPTC hydrogel producing the greatest enhancement. In contrast, the hydrogen‑peroxide and HA‑PBA groups showed decreased, or at a best, baseline expression relative to the untreated control. These data suggest that the oxygen‑rich milieu generated by radical decomposition suppresses HIF‑1α, which in turn diminishes GLUT1‑mediated glycolysis, impairs cellular energy metabolism, and promotes senescence; a negative feedback loop then further down‑regulates the upstream regulator ERK2. Western blot analysis further confirmed that the protein expression of HIF-1α and ERK2 was significantly increased in the treatment groups, while the protein levels of GLUT1, p16 and p21 were notably reduced (Fig. 5K–L).

In summary, these findings demonstrate that the TA-Ce³⁺ MPNs within the HPTC hydrogel effectively scavenge ROS and mitigate hyperoxia-induced cellular senescence by restoring the expression of the HIF signaling pathway. Among the components, Ce³⁺ ions play a pivotal role in driving these protective effects. These results highlight the therapeutic potential of HPTC hydrogel for the treatment of IVDD.

In vivo therapeutic effects of HPTC hydrogel in a rat IVDD model

The in vitro findings that HPTC hydrogel exerts antioxidative, anti-inflammatory activities and reduces oxygen levels to prevent NPC senescence are motivated further through in vivo investigation. To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of the HPTC hydrogel in mitigating IVDD, an in vivo rat caudal needle-puncture model of IVDD was carefully established (Fig. 6A). Following IVDD induction using a 21G needle, the HA-PBA, HPT, HPTC hydrogels, or PBS (IVDD group) groups were injected into the rat’s IVD using a micro-syringe. Changes in DHI and signal intensity were evaluated using X-ray and MRI, respectively.

Qualitative X-ray observations (Fig. 6B) revealed that DHI reduction was minimal in the HPTC group, and the images closely resembled those of the control group. In contrast, the IVDD group showed significant collapse and narrowing of the intervertebral space. The HA-PBA and HPT groups maintained disc height better than the IVDD group, but signs of progressive degeneration remained, suggesting that these hydrogels alone were insufficient for long-term protection (Fig. 6C).

MRI T2-weighted imaging, commonly used to assess IVDD, reflects water content and proteoglycan loss; healthy, hydrated discs appear bright (‘white discs’) and degenerated discs appear dark (‘black disc’). According to the Pfirrmann grading scale, MRI images are classified from Grade I (healthy) to Grade V (severely degenerated). MRI scans (Fig. 6D) showed that the control group maintained healthy disc signals, and similar preservation was observed in the HPTC-treated group after 8 weeks. In contrast, the IVDD group exhibited complete signal loss and evidence of disc space collapse and vertebral fusion. The HA-PBA group retained partial signal but continued to show degeneration, indicating a transient anti-degenerative effect. The HPT group maintained disc space structure but experienced significant signal loss, likely due to ongoing inflammatory necrosis of NPCs. Overall, consistent improvements in both the DHI percentage and the MRI grading in the HPTC group support its therapeutic efficacy in vivo (Fig. 6E).

Histopathological analysis is widely recognized as the gold standard for disease diagnosis. To rigorously evaluate the temporal dynamics of therapeutic efficacy, histological examinations of IVD tissues were performed at two postoperative time points: 4 weeks and 8 weeks after treatment. This approach enabled longitudinal assessment of the treatment’s impact on tissue morphology and integrity. H&E staining was used to characterize the structure of the NP [2], fibrous tissues, and disc margins. In the HPTC hydrogel-treated group, the NP region exhibited minimal contraction between weeks 4 and 8, with preservation of clear tissue boundaries – findings that contrasted markedly with other experimental groups. In the defect, HA-PBA, and HPT groups, the NP region showed progressive degeneration, with blurred borders and increasing replacement of central NP tissue by fibrous annular tissue (Fig. 6F). To assess matrix composition, Safranin-O/Fast Green staining was employed to visualize proteoglycans (red) and collagen (blue). As shown in Fig. 6G, the HPTC and control group exhibited strong proteoglycan staining in the NP region, indicative of preserved disc composition. In contrast, the defect, HA-PBA, and HPT groups showed progressive collagen accumulation and loss of proteoglycan-rich matrix, with blue staining gradually replacing red, reflecting tissue degeneration.

Therapeutic mechanism of the HPTC hydrogel in rats IVDD model

To fully elucidate the therapeutic potential of the HPTC hydrogel in vivo, its effects on the expression of aggrecan, collagen II, IL-1β, and TNF-α in IVD tissues were assessed via immunohistochemistry at 4 weeks (Fig. S3) and 8 weeks (Fig. 7A). A significant reduction in IL-1β and TNF-α signals was observed in the HPTC group compared to other treatment groups. In contrast, the expression of aggrecan and collagen II, key indicators of ECM integrity, was markedly enhanced in the HPTC group. These trends were further supported by semi-quantitative scoring of immunohistochemical staining (Fig. 7B–E).

In addition, the hydrogel’s impact on the disc hypoxic niche and cellular senescence was evaluated. Immunohistochemical analysis revealed a significant increase in HIF-1α expression in the HPTC-treated group compared to the defect group (Fig. 7F–G). As a master regulator of hypoxia adaptation, elevated HIF-1α levels in the HPTC group suggest the establishment of a therapeutically beneficial hypoxic microenvironment in the NP. This effect is attributed to the hydrogel’s ROS-scavenging capability and the reduction of oxygen levels caused by Ce3+, which mitigates oxidative stress and supports the maintenance of the NP cell phenotype and ECM synthesis. Concurrently, immunostaining for the senescence marker p21 showed a notable reduction in the HPTC group relative to the defect group (Fig. 7H–I), indicating effective suppression of senescence pathways. These findings collectively highlight the dual role of the HPTC hydrogel in enhancing HIF-1α expression and reducing p21 levels, thereby promoting regeneration while counteracting degenerative senescence.

Histological analysis further confirmed the preservation of NP tissue architecture, attenuation of inflammation, and enhanced ECM regeneration in HPTC-treated discs. Taken together, these data demonstrate a multifaceted therapeutic mechanism of action: restoration of hypoxic homeostasis via HIF-1α upregulation, suppression of senescence through p21 downregulation, and modulation of inflammation and ECM remodeling.

RNA sequencing reveals potential signaling pathways underlying the therapeutic effects of HPTC hydrogel in rats IVDD model

To further elucidate the mechanisms underlying ECM regeneration following HPTC hydrogel treatment, mRNA sequencing (mRNA-seq) was performed on NP tissues. A volcano plot illustrates the differential gene expression profile after HPTC treatment (Fig. 8A). DEGs were identified using stringent criteria (fold-change ≥ 2, p < 0.05). Heat map analysis revealed that these genes were mainly associated with inflammation, ECM remodeling, cell signaling, and cell adhesion. Notably, genes related to ECM synthesis, such as Col6a1, Col6a2, BMP-6, and the hypoxia-related HIF-1α, were upregulated (Fig. 8C).

GO analysis indicated that these DEGs were predominantly involved in ECM activity, including ECM catabolism and biosynthesis, supporting the observed regenerative effects of HPTC hydrogel (Fig. 8D). KEGG enrichment analysis further revealed activation of the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway following HPTC treatment (Fig. 8E). This pathway has been widely reported to promote ECM regeneration upon activation, suggesting that HPTC may facilitate ECM repair in NPCs via this mechanism.

GSEA further confirmed upregulation of Hif-1α, supporting the hypothesis that HPTC promotes ECM remodeling by scavenging ROS and restoring a hypoxic microenvironment (Fig. 8B). To validate RNA-seq results, Western Blotting (Fig. 8F) was conducted for representative genes, including aggrecan, collagen II, IL-1β, TNF-α, HIF-1α, and p21, which reflect inflammation, oxidative stress, and senescence states (Fig. 8G–L). The data confirmed the activation of ECM synthesis and PI3K-Akt signaling modulation after HPTC treatment. In addition, HIF-1α upregulation suggested a hypoxia-adaptive response, while p21 downregulation indicated reduced cellular senescence.

In summary, the TA-Ce3+ MPNs in HPTC hydrogel effectively scavenges ROS, re-establishes a beneficial hypoxic niche, suppresses senescence, and synergizes with anti-inflammatory effects to support comprehensive IVD regeneration.

In vivo therapeutic effects of HPTC hydrogel in a rabbit IVDD model

The regenerative efficacy of the HPTC hydrogel was further validated in a translational rabbit IVDD model, whose spinal anatomy more closely resembles human disc dimensions and biomechanical loading characteristics (Fig. 9A). Longitudinal radiological assessments revealed distinct therapeutic responses among the treatment groups (Fig. 9B–C). The defect group exhibited a progressive disc space collapse over the 8-week period. While HA-PBA and HPT hydrogels offered moderate initial protection against disc height loss, their effects diminished by the study endpoint. In contrast, the HPTC-treated group maintained near-physiological disc height between weeks 4 and 8, closely approximating the control group. This structural preservation was further supported by T2-weighted MRI findings (Fig. 9D–E), in which HPTC-treated discs retained a high-signal intensity characteristic of hydrated, healthy tissue. In comparison, the HA-PBA and HPT groups demonstrated gradual signal loss, consistent with disc dehydration and degeneration.

High-resolution histological analysis using grinding-section techniques (avoiding decalcification) enabled detailed visualization of calcified endplates and disc–bone interfaces. Histomorphometry analysis (Fig. 9F–G) revealed that, although HA-PBA and HPT treatments modestly delayed NP fibrosis, they did not prevent endplate erosion or annular disorganization. Only the HPTC hydrogel preserved the biphasic disc architecture, characterized by proteoglycan-rich NP domains (Safranin-O-positive), lamellar annulus fibrosus, and structurally intact cartilage endplates. This anatomical integrity was comparable to that of the control group.

These findings underscore the unique regenerative capacity of the HPTC hydrogel in restoring functional spinal units, extending beyond symptomatic relief and structural mitigation observed in small animal models.

Discussion

In this work, we have demonstrated that the ECM-mimetic HPTC hydrogel, integrating HA-PBA and TA-Ce³⁺ MPNs, effectively promotes IVDD regeneration by simulating the native NP ECM both structurally and functionally. The viscoelasticity comparable to native nucleus pulposus tissue is derived from dynamic boronate ester bonds and tannic acid-cerium(III) (TA-Ce³⁺) coordination bonds within the hydrogel, which effectively prevents mechanical damage to the intervertebral disc. Featuring a storage modulus (G′) consistent with healthy nucleus pulposus, HPTC hydrogel offers instant load-bearing support and delivers physiological mechanical signals to nucleus pulposus cells. Additionally, its 50–80 μm interconnected pores closely mimic the structural framework of native nucleus pulposus ECM, enabling efficient nutrient and oxygen transport, supporting cell migration and proliferation, and allowing newly formed ECM to deposit and integrate within the network [4–5]. Furthermore, TA-Ce³⁺ MPNs confer potent free-radical scavenging capacity across ABTS⁺•, DPPH•, and •O₂⁻ assays, with enhanced activity under acidic pH that mimics the degenerated NP microenvironment. Concurrently, the HPTC hydrogel markedly suppresses the LPS-induced macrophage expression of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 while promoting IL-4 and IL-10 upregulation, shifting macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype. This dual immunomodulatory function, combined with its ECM-like structural support, mitigates the chronic inflammation that perpetuates matrix catabolism, thereby creating a favorable microenvironment for functional ECM restoration.

A hallmark of degenerated discs is aberrant oxygen accumulation due to excessive ROS decomposition, which undermines the hypoxia‑dependent HIF‑1α signaling crucial for NPCs senescence and ECM synthesis [32–12]. Our dissolved oxygen measurements reveal that Ce³⁺ within HPTC substantially attenuates oxygen rise during H₂O₂ decomposition (ending at 105% vs. 115% in controls at 15 min). By catalyzing redox cycling (Ce³⁺/Ce⁴⁺), the hydrogel mimics natural antioxidant enzymes, converting ROS while consuming O₂, thereby restoring a hypoxic niche. This engineered oxygen modulation is unprecedented among injectable hydrogels and directly underpins downstream cellular effects. HIF‑1α orchestrates glycolytic metabolism, ECM production, and cell survival under low‑oxygen conditions. Elevated oxygen levels in NP tissue trigger HIF‑1α degradation, impairing GLUT1‑mediated glucose uptake and promoting senescence [33–34]. And HPTC treatment rescues HIF‑1α, ERK2 (upstream kinase), and GLUT1 expression in H₂O₂‑challenged NPCs, aligning with the reduction of NPCs senescence exhibiting with minimal SA‑β‑Gal positivity and normalized p16/p21 levels, comparable to untreated controls. These findings confirm that oxygen reduction is both necessary and sufficient to reinstate hypoxic signaling, which cannot be achieved by antioxidant activity alone [35]. Previous studies have demonstrated the therapeutic potential of hypoxia modulation for IVDD repair, yet achieving sustained delivery through biomaterials remains a critical challenge. Zhou et al. successfully developed hypoxia-inducible interpenetrating polymer network (IPN) hydrogel microspheres. The incorporated laccase may exhibit compromised enzymatic activity in the acidic microenvironment characteristic of degenerated discs, potentially limiting its oxygen-depleting efficacy [36]. Tong et al. constructed engineered MSC-derived small extracellular vesicles (MSC-sEVs) that lack the dual functionality inherent to hydrogel systems – the capacity for sustained bioactive factor release coupled with essential mechanical support properties [37].

While previous studies have employed animal models beyond the standard rat tail needle-puncture for in vivo validation, limitations remain. Lin et al. utilized a rat tail surgical incision followed by photo-cross-linking, which may cause secondary trauma [38]. Wang et al. chose to use genetically modified mice undergoing lumbar surgery, which may involve genetic compensatory mechanisms and fail to adequately mimic human disc structure [39]. In this work, we adopted a two-tiered animal study design that combines the strengths of both rat and rabbit models. The rat caudal model enables high-throughput screening and rapid assessment of early therapeutic effects, while the rabbit lumbar model provides a more clinically relevant platform for evaluating long-term efficacy and biomechanical performance under physiological loading. This complementary approach minimizes the limitations of any single model and enhances the translational relevance of our findings. Single injections of the ECM-mimetic HPTC hydrogel in both models yielded robust therapeutic outcomes, including preservation of DHI, restoration of T2-weighted MRI signal, and histological evidence of aggrecan and collagen II regeneration – results rivaling healthy controls. Immunohistochemistry confirmed an increased HIF-1α and a reduced p21 in NP tissue, underscoring the synergy between hypoxia restoration and senescence mitigation in a biomimetically supportive ECM environment. RNA-seq and subsequent qPCR validated the upregulation of ECM-related genes (Col6a1, Col6a2, BMP6) and HIF-1α pathway genes, alongside enrichment of PI3K -Akt signaling, which is critical for cell survival and matrix biosynthesis. These multi-omics data position HPTC as an ECM-inspired regenerative platform that concurrently targets key molecular drivers of degeneration while providing a conducive microenvironment for functional matrix deposition.

In contrast to prior hydrogel systems that have incorporated anti-inflammatory or antioxidant agents, our design uniquely converges ECM biomimicry with active oxygen regulation to holistically address IVDD pathophysiology [40–44]. Our HPTC hydrogel’s unique oxygen-consuming MPNs differentiate it from other dynamic hydrogels (e.g. chitosan-based or collagen-TA systems) by not only mimicking the mechanical and structural properties of native NP ECM to achieve tissue adhesion and mechanical adaptation, but also actively restoring the physiological hypoxia essential for NPC phenotypic maintenance – a core feature of its integrated design. Moreover, its self-healing and tissue-adhesive properties ensure structural conformity and prolonged retention within the disc space, overcoming issues such as rapid degradation or leakage commonly observed in conventional injectable scaffolds, thereby providing stable structural support and continuous microenvironment modulation.

Naturally, the present study has several limitations. The structural stability and long-term durability of the hydrogel under the complex mechanical environment of the human intervertebral disc require further validation in large animal models with similar mechanical loading characteristics. In addition, although favorable histocompatibility of the material was preliminarily confirmed within a limited observation period, as a deep implanted biomaterial, the long-term in vivo degradation behavior, excretion pathways, and potential trace accumulation effects of the cerium-based metal-polyphenol network and dual-network system remain unclear. Therefore, systematic safety evaluations including long-term toxicology, immunogenicity and pharmacokinetics should be conducted in future investigations.

In summary, by integrating TA-Ce³⁺ metal-polyphenol chemistry into an ECM-mimetic hydrogel framework, HPTC represents a pioneering strategy that concurrently addresses mechanical matching and microenvironmental reprogramming. It uniquely restores the hypoxic niche through active oxygen consumption, rescues HIF-1α signaling, and delivers synergistic antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, thereby effectively promoting ECM reconstruction and cellular homeostasis. This multifaceted, bioinspired approach not only resolves critical limitations in existing IVDD therapies but also opens a promising avenue for functional disc regeneration via biomaterial-enabled holistic microenvironment remodeling.

Conclusion

In this research, a dual cross-linked ECM-mimetic injectable hydrogel (HPTC) was developed by composing the dynamic borate ester bonds formed between HA-PBA and TA and coordination of TA-Ce³⁺ MPNs. By simulating the native NP ECM both structurally and functionally, HPTC hydrogels exhibited favorable rheological properties, injectability, self-healing, and tissue adhesion abilities. In vitro assays demonstrated that the ECM-biomimetic HPTC hydrogel mitigates the core pathological features of IVDD by scavenging ROS, reducing oxygen levels, modulating inflammatory cytokine profiles, and recovering the HIF-1α signaling pathway. Animal studies in rat and rabbit IVDD models confirmed these findings, showing preserved disc height, restored MRI signal intensity, reduced levels of IL-1β and TNF-α, and increased collagen II and aggrecan expression – consistent with successful ECM remodeling. Moreover, RNA-seq analysis revealed that HPTC treatment promotes HIF-1α pathways in ECM regeneration, further supporting its bioinspired regenerative potential. In summary, the HPTC hydrogel offers an innovative, minimally invasive, and ECM-mimetic strategy for IVD regeneration by combining the structural benefits of injectable hydrogels with the therapeutic advantages of metal-polyphenol chemistry, showing promise for restoring disc homeostasis and delaying or reversing the progression of IVDD.
