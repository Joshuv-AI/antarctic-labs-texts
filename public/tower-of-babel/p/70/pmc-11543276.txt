Variability in Breast Density Estimation and Its Impact on Breast Cancer Risk Assessment
Authors: Hye Ji, Myoung-jin Jang, Jung Min Chang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBreast density is an independent risk factor for breast cancer, although variability exists in measurements. This study sought to evaluate the agreement between radiologists and automated breast density assessment software and assess the impact of breast density measures on breast cancer risk estimates using the Breast Cancer Surveillance Consortium (BCSC) model (v.2). A retrospective database search identified women who had undergone mammography between December 2021 and June 2022. The Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS) breast composition index assigned by a radiologist (R) was recorded and analyzed using three commercially available software programs (S1, S2, and S3). The agreement rate and Cohen’s kappa (κ) were used to evaluate inter-rater agreements concerning breast density measures. The 5-year risk of invasive breast cancer in women was calculated using the BCSC model (v.2) with breast density inputs from various density estimation methods. Absolute differences in risk between various density measurements were evaluated. Overall, 1,949 women (mean age, 53.2 years) were included. The inter-rater agreement between R, S1, and S2 was 75.0–75.6%, while that between S3 and the others was 60.2%–63.3%. Kappa was substantial between R, S1, and S2 (0.66–0.68), and moderate (0.49–0.50) between S3 and the others. S3 placed fewer women in mammographic density d (14.9%) than R, S1, and S2 (40.5%–44.0%). In BCSC risk assessment (v.2), S3 assessed fewer women with a high 5-year risk of invasive breast cancer than the other methods, resulting in an absolute difference of 0% between R, S1, and S2 in 75.0%–75.6% of cases, whereas the difference between S3 and the other methods occurs in 60.2%–63.3% of cases. Breast density assessment using various methods showed moderate-to-substantial agreement, potentially affecting risk assessments. Precise and consistent breast density measurements may lead to personalized and effective strategies for breast cancer prevention.Keywords: Breast Density, Breast Neoplasms, Risk Assessment

INTRODUCTION

Breast density refers to the fibroglandular tissue that has both fibrous (stromal) and glandular (lobular and ductal) components [1]. To standardize clinical image interpretation, the American College of Radiology (ACR) developed the Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS) with four categories for digital mammography and digital breast tomosynthesis based on breast density. In the fifth edition, these categories include: almost entirely fatty (a), scattered areas of fibroglandular density (b), heterogeneously dense, which may obscure the detection of small masses (c), and extremely dense, which lowers the sensitivity of mammography (d) [2].

High breast density is associated with decreased sensitivity in screening tests and is an independent risk factor for breast cancer [3,4]. Recently, in the context of growing interest in supplemental screening beyond mammography, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) updated mammography guidelines to mandate the notification of breast density to patients [5]. The reporting of breast density is important in deciding whether to use supplemental screening methods, such as ultrasound and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [6]. Furthermore, several breast cancer risk models incorporate breast density information [7]. However, concerns persist regarding density assessments owing to their subjectivity in clinical practice. Sprague et al. [8] observed considerable variation in qualitative density assessments among radiologists using BI-RADS, with interpretations ranging from 6% to 85% of mammograms categorized as heterogeneous or extremely dense.

Currently, several commercially available breast-density measures for full-field digital mammography are used in clinical practice to improve the objectivity and reliability of density assessments. Nevertheless, the agreement between these automated programs and radiologists falls short of expectations when compared to visually assessing density in two or three dimensions [9,10]. Furthermore, the impact of these measures on clinical practice and their association with breast cancer risk using mammograms obtained prior to cancer diagnosis remains unclear.

Therefore, we aimed to evaluate the agreement on breast density across radiologists and a commercial density measurement system, and the differences in breast cancer risk estimates when applied to the Breast Cancer Surveillance Consortium (BCSC) risk calculator.

METHODS

Patient inclusion

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of our institution (IRB No. 2204-065-1315), which waived the requirement for informed consent owing to the retrospective nature of data collection and anonymous analysis.

A retrospective analysis was conducted using the breast examination database of our institution to identify 7,801 women who underwent mammography for screening or diagnostic purposes between December 2021 and June 2022. From this population, we excluded women with a history of breast cancer who were treated for breast cancer or newly diagnosed with breast cancer (n = 4,562), those aged 75 years or older (n = 1,100), and those aged 34 years or younger (n = 190). Finally, 1,949 women were included in this study (Supplementary Figure 1).

Image acquisition

All imaging data were prospectively obtained as part of routine clinical practice and stored in picture archiving and communication system (PACS) software (M-view; INFINITT Healthcare, Seoul, Korea). Mammographic imaging data were acquired using a full-field digital mammography unit (Selenia Dimensions; Hologic, Bedford, USA). Standard mammography protocols, including bilateral two-view (mediolateral oblique and craniocaudal) mammography, were used.

Breast density measurement and risk assessment

Breast density assessment was performed by one of eight dedicated breast radiologists with 2–30 years of experience who interpreted screening and diagnostic mammograms and classified breast density into four categories: almost entirely fatty (a), scattered areas of fibroglandular density (b), heterogeneously dense (c), and extremely dense (d), following the guidelines of ACR BI-RADS 5th edition. The radiologists evaluated imaging findings and reported final assessment categories. In addition to the radiologists’ assessments, all mammograms were assessed using three commercially available automated density assessment programs (Volpara®, v.3.4.1 [Volpara Solutions Inc., Wellington, New Zealand], Lunit INSIGHT MMG, v.1.1.4.3 [Lunit Inc., Seoul, Korea], and Quantra, v.2.1.1 [Hologic, Bedford, USA]) integrated into the PACS system. Volpara® and Quantra are volume-based methods, whereas Lunit INSIGHT MMG was trained on mammographic density with the ground truth based on radiologists’ density assessments (Figure 1). Density information obtained from the three programs was available at the time of mammography interpretation. Women’s 5-year risk of invasive breast cancer using various density estimation methods was evaluated using the BCSC risk calculator, v.2 [11], with patient clinical and density information. The risks were categorized as low (< 1.00%), average (1.00%–1.66%), intermediate (1.67%–2.49%), high (2.50%–3.99%), and very high (> 3.99%) [11,12].

Data collection and statistical analyses

Data concerning the patient’s age, first-degree family history of breast cancer, history of breast biopsy, and results, were collected. The mammographic density of all patients was measured using four methods: the qualitative radiologist’s BI-RADS assessment (R) and automated density assessment methods (S1, S2, and S3). Risk scores were calculated for each density measurement by incorporating both radiologist BI-RADS assessments (R) and automatic density assessment methods (S1, S2, and S3), using the BCSC risk calculator (v.2) [11].

Inter-rater agreement was evaluated using Cohen’s weighted kappa score and agreement rates between radiologist assessments and various density measurement methods. The κ values were interpreted as follows: 0.00–0.20, slight agreement; 0.21–0.40, fair agreement; 0.41–0.60, moderate agreement; 0.61–0.80, substantial agreement; and 0.81–1.00, excellent agreement. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. For breast cancer risk assessment, the absolute difference between the radiologist’s assessment and the various density measurement methods was evaluated. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS software (v.9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, USA).

RESULTS

A total of 1,949 women (mean age ± standard deviation [SD], 53.2 ± 9.5 years; range, 35–74 years) were included in the study (Table 1). The results of the mammographic density assessments using R, S1, S2, and S3 are shown in Figure 2. Among the 1,949 women, breast density in 838 (43.0%) was assessed to be the same by R1, S1, S2, and S3, whereas at least one method noted different density measurements in 1,111 women (57.0%). The agreement rate among R, S1, and S2 was 75.0–75.6%, but that between S3 and the others was 60.2%–63.3%. The κ values of inter-rater agreement between the visual assessments and the two software programs, S1 and S2, was substantial (0.663–0.684), and that between S3 and the others was moderate (0.486–0.500) (Table 2). R, S1, and S2 placed 796 (40.8%), 790 (40.5%), and 857 (44.0%) women, respectively, on breast density d, whereas S3 placed only 291 (14.9%) women on density d. In contrast, there was a higher percentage of breast density c in S3, 1,286 (66.0%) than in the other methods (38.3%–46.0%).

When women’s 5-year risks of invasive breast cancer were calculated using the results in a BCSC model (v.2) with breast density inputs from various breast density estimation methods, an absolute difference (AD) of 0.0% < AD ≤ 0.5% between R, S1, and S2 was noted in 21.6%–22.4% of cases, with AD > 0.5% in 2.6%–2.8%, whereas 0.0% < AD ≤ 0.5% between S3 and the other methods was noted in 33.7%–37% of cases, with AD > 0.5% in 2.6%–3.0% (Supplementary Table 1). Among the 1,949 women, 358 (18.4%), 336 (17.2%), 340 (17.5%), and 281 (14.4%) had intermediate to very high BCSC 5-year risk scores (≥ 1.67%) according to the visual assessment and software programs S1, S2, and S3, respectively (Supplementary Figure 2). When measured using S3, the number of individuals in the group with intermediate to very high BCSC 5-year risk scores decreased. Among 1,111 women with different densities by different measurements, 125 women (11.3%, 125/1,111) had their BCSC 5-year risk scores upgraded from low to average (< 1.67%) to intermediate to very high (≥ 1.67%) due to increased breast density.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we evaluated the agreement between radiologists and automated breast density assessment software and assessed the impact of breast density measures on breast cancer risk estimates using the BCSC model (v.2). While density measurements and risk assessments exhibited substantial inter-rater agreement (κ values: 0.663–0.684) among radiologists’ assessments and the two software density results, the others showed lower agreement (κ values: 0.486–0.500).

Since Wolfe first proposed the Breast Pattern Scale in 1976, various breast density classifications have been introduced [13]. BI-RADS is the most common breast density classification system used in clinical practice. Despite its widespread use, it uses a subjective visual assessment of the volume of attenuating tissue, which allows for the assessment of an otherwise scattered-density breast as heterogeneously dense, if a focally dense area may mask cancerous tissue. Consequently, the major drawbacks of this method are intra- and inter-rater variabilities. In this context, numerous semi-automated and automated density assessment techniques have been developed. These tools use two general methodologies: area- and volume-based percentage density assessments. To date, volume-based approaches have demonstrated fair-to-substantial agreement with the fourth- and fifth-edition BI-RADS visual assessments [10]. Recently, deep-learning-based methods of density assessment with qualitative and quantitative outputs have been developed, with promising results demonstrating high agreement with radiologists’ interpretations or other quantitative estimates [4].

In this study, three commercially available density estimation software packages were used. Volpara® and Quantra are the two most-studied commercially available volume-based tools. Although we used a volume-based method, the most recent version of the software incorporates artificial intelligence. Another tool employed was Lunit INSIGHT MMG a recently developed deep learning-based software that aids in both breast cancer detection and breast density measurements. This software was trained on mammographic density with the ground truth based on radiologists’ density assessments; thus, it differs from volume-based programs [14]. Consequently, variations in the training and development principles of the software led to differences in the agreements between the estimation methods used in our study.

The most significant clinical implications of breast density include the prediction of breast cancer risk and determination of the need for supplemental screening imaging due to decreased mammography sensitivity as breast density increases. Several studies have shown that adding breast density measurements (either qualitative or quantitative) to clinical risk factors more accurately estimates breast cancer risk, and tools such as the Tyrer-Cuzick and BCSC risk models incorporate breast density determinants [7]. Breast cancer risk models, including those incorporating density, demonstrate moderate discrimination [15]. In our study, we employed the BCSC model (v.2) which is known to be a well-validated risk assessment tool for breast cancer that may be particularly useful when assessing the utility of supplemental screening in women with dense breasts [16]. In addition, the BCSC model (v.2) incorporates factors such as age, race/ethnicity, first-degree familial history of breast cancer, breast biopsy history, and mammographic breast density, which are readily accessible in clinical practice.

Regarding decisions surrounding supplemental imaging tests, breast density, with or without breast cancer risk information, can be used to guide women and practitioners as to when supplemental imaging may be most beneficial and when it is not. The appropriate criteria for supplemental breast cancer screening based on breast density includes six variants of supplemental screening based on breast density and lifetime risk [17]. The European Society of Breast Imaging recommends breast MRI screening every 2–4 years for women aged 50–70 years who have extremely dense breasts [6]. It has been reported that women with heterogeneously dense breasts and a BCSC 5-year risk of 2.5% or higher, or extremely dense breasts and a 5-year risk of 1.0% or higher, have the highest risk of advanced cancer, and discussing supplemental imaging with these populations may represent the most efficient strategy [11,12]. As observed in our study, the number of individuals with dense breasts may vary depending on the estimation method employed. In addition, BCSC risk was affected by density changes in 11.3% of women who showed variable densities across different measurement methods (125/1,111). Thus, breast cancer risk and the target population recommended for supplemental screening may also vary [18]. Although density assessment in its current state suffers from the fact that there is no ideal “ground truth” for breast density and the identification of the most reliable or accurate estimation method is beyond the scope of this review, understanding this variability between different methods could guide more optimal use of these tools [19].

This study had certain limitations. First, most of the women included in our study had dense breasts. The number of women with fatty breasts was limited in our Korean population and data were collected from a single academic hospital enriched with diagnostic women with dense breasts. Secondly, we used only one risk model (the BCSC model, v.2) and excluded individuals who could not be accommodated within this model. Although this model was developed using a cohort that included 2.7% (29,180/1,095,484) Asian or Pacific Islander women [20], it has not been separately validated in Asian women. Evaluations using different models are necessary to better understand how the diversity of density measurements affects breast cancer risk assessment. Third, the radiologists’ BI-RADS assessments were collected retrospectively from clinical reports, and inter-reader variability was not assessed. Additionally, because the density measurement results from the three software programs were shared at the time of mammography interpretation, there may have been variability among radiologists in terms of whether they referred to these software results or which results they trusted the most. Finally, while we estimated breast cancer risk, the actual occurrence of cancer was not assessed, as our study did not aim to evaluate performance of the risk model.

In conclusion, our study found moderate-to-substantial agreement between breast density assessments conducted by radiologists and automated software. This variability significantly affected the calculated risk scores. Understanding this variability is crucial for ensuring precise and consistent breast density measurements, ultimately leading to more personalized and effective breast cancer prevention strategies.
