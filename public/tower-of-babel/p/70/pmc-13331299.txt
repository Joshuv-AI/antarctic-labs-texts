Deficiencies in Faculty Educator Development: A Survey Perspective from the Emergency Department
Authors: Sharon Lai Ching Chong, Yuan Helen Zhang, Eunizar Binte Omar, Fatimah Lateef
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Faculty development is crucial to sustaining medical education excellence, yet structured, targeted approaches remain limited in emergency medicine. This study explores emergency physicians’ perceptions of their educator roles and identifies faculty development needs in Singapore.Methods:A cross-sectional electronic survey was conducted from March to June 2024 across three emergency departments. Emergency physicians (associate consultant level and above) rated familiarity, confidence, and perceived importance of 12 educator roles based on the Association for Medical Education in Europe (AMEE) framework. Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics software.Results:Sixty-two emergency physicians participated. Familiarity with AMEE educator roles was low (Mean = 2.24, standard deviation [SD] = 1.155), but confidence in clinical teaching roles was moderate to high (Mean = 3.90, SD = 0.670). Most of the respondents (71%) identified primarily as engaging educators. Confidence increased with years of experience, with a majority (54.8%) gaining confidence within 3–5 years. Lower confidence was reported in curriculum development, technology-enhanced learning, and academic leadership domains.Conclusions:Emergency physicians in Singapore are confident clinical educators but less familiar with broader educational roles. Faculty development initiatives should address gaps in curriculum planning, educational leadership, and technology integration. Recommendations such as structured pathways, longitudinal mentorship, and protected time for more educator responsibilities are critical to supporting professional growth and enhancing educational excellence.

INTRODUCTION

Medical education is an ever-evolving complex system of practice, and faculty educators play crucial roles in teaching, feedback, and assessment to ensure the competencies and confidence of future physicians. Some key trends include competency-based education, technology-enhanced learning (TEL), social accountability, academic leadership, interprofessional education, transition curricula, and continued personal/professional development certification. To create a psychologically safe environment for both learners and teachers, faculty development is essential. It is crucial in fostering frameworks and skill sets to address both personal and professional growth. It encompasses a planned program with specific milestones, or a set of multi-dimensional programs, designed to prepare institutions and faculty members for their various roles, with the goal of improving individual instructors’ knowledge and skills in the areas of teaching, research, and administration. It addresses attitudes and beliefs; provides knowledge about approaches to teaching and learning based on current understanding of these practices; and promotes the development of skills in teaching, curriculum design, and assessment of learners while providing opportunities for repetitive reflection.[1]

The career sustainability of medical educators is increasingly tied to their personal professional identities and how they navigate their ever-changing career trajectories. The question of what constitutes a good medical educator goes beyond just information giving but rather an art of communicating “motivations and aspirations.” The 12 role-modeling coined by Harden and Crosby,[2] for the teacher provides an understanding of the different views and functions fulfilled by the teacher and a framework for capacity building. It has also promoted the need for faculty development to equip the teachers with either improvement in the existing array of expertize or expansion of commitments to various other domains of interest.

The faculty development program should be individualized, adopting an andragogical approach rooted in evidence-based practices. It should not be a top-down approach but rather a self-motivated, self-assessed, specific, and explicit pathway that interested clinicians can potentially embark on with the appropriate endorsement to ultimately improve the quality and effectiveness of medical education. Balancing the demands of residency training with faculty readiness and interest in education presents challenges. It has been recognized that essential deficiencies exist among the emergency specialists who are actively involved in education. Faculty development could potentially assist in addressing these deficiencies by instituting multi-instructional interventions; experiential learning and reflective practice; individual and group projects; peer support and the development of communities of practice; mentorship; and institutional support. Balancing faculty development with institutional priorities is a challenge faced by educators at all levels, both junior and senior. Faculty development must be a part of the institution’s priority, with a strategic thrust and masterplan or framework. It should be exciting, stimulating, and appealing to the variety of educators to help them grow and expand their educator role.[34]

Currently in Singapore, different institutions have put forth broad faculty development frameworks, for example: Educator Development Framework (EDF) (by AME-I of SingHealth Duke-NUS Academic Medical Centre),[5] and Faculty Development Framework (by Centre for Medical Education under National University of Singapore) based on principles from the Ministry of Health. To better understand the needs and perspectives of emergency specialists regarding best practices in medical education, a survey was conducted to identify areas for growth and satisfaction in faculty development. From cognizance to implementation, the knowledge gained from our study should provide guidance and promote dialogues among the educators with regard to informal or formal approaches,[6] individual or team approaches, work-based or degree/fellowship approaches, and mentorship or community approaches.

METHODS

This study uses a cross-sectional survey to assess the relevance of the 12 educator roles proposed by the Association for Medical Education in Europe (AMEE) and to determine specific faculty development needs among emergency physicians in Singapore. The survey was conducted electronically using a digital platform (FormSG, 2024) between March and June 2024, targeting emergency physicians who had completed specialty training (i.e., associate consultants and above) and were currently practicing at ABC institutions – Hospital A, Hospital B, and Hospital C. A total of 86 participants were invited to complete the anonymous survey, of which 62 participants responded, resulting in a 72% response rate. The respondents represented a range of experience levels, genders, age groups, training backgrounds, and subspecialty interests. Participation was voluntary, and completion of the survey was considered implied informed consent.

The survey questionnaire was structured into two main sections. The first section gathered baseline characteristics of participants, such as years of experience as emergency physicians, gender, age, training background, and current place of practice. The second section explored participants’ perceptions of their roles as educators and the availability of faculty development programs. This section included questions assessing their confidence in various educator roles, their familiarity with the 12 educator roles outlined by AMEE, and their overall preparedness for transitioning into an educator role.

The questionnaire comprised Likert scale items, ranking questions, and open-ended responses to assess participants’ perspectives on faculty development. Likert scale items measured agreement or confidence levels on a scale from 1 (not at all) to 5 (very), evaluating familiarity with educator roles, confidence in teaching, and perceived faculty development needs. Ranking questions required participants to rank educator roles (e.g., engaging, reflective, inspiring educator) from the EDF[5] based on their self-perception and self-evaluation. Open-ended questions allowed participants to provide insights on the adequacy of current training resources and suggest improvements for educator preparation post-specialist training. This survey was an adaptation of the AMEE Guide No. 20 to meet the institution-specific physician educator longitudinal outcomes as advised by the Ministry of Health. To ensure the survey was relevant and effective, it was tested with a small group of participants before being sent out to all participants.

Data analysis involved calculating means and standard deviations (SDs) to assess overall confidence and familiarity with different educator roles. SPSS Inc. SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 17.0. Chicago (IL): SPSS Inc.; 2008 was used for analysis. This study was designed as a descriptive needs assessment; therefore, all analyses were descriptive in nature, and no inferential statistical testing was performed. Responses to open-ended questions were reviewed, categorized, and coded to identify recurring themes with a focus on the educator roles and development.

Familiarity with the 12 educator roles outlined by AMEE was low (Mean = 2.24, SD = 1.155), indicating a gap in awareness of this teaching framework.

Confidence in assuming educator roles varied with training and experience. Participants reported moderate confidence posttraining (Mean = 3.23, SD = 0.818). However, confidence in current educator roles was significantly higher (Mean = 3.90, SD = 0.670), suggesting that teaching experience contributes to confidence-building over time. Structured faculty development programs may support skill acquisition; however, given the descriptive nature of the data, these observations should not be interpreted as causal relationships.

Access to educational support during training was rated moderately (Mean = 3.35, SD = 1.010). Despite available programs, participants noted challenges such as limited publicity, oversubscription, and a lack of structured development pathways. In addition, the absence of mandatory foundational training and an emphasis on theory over practical application further hinders engagement.

Overall, while emergency physicians have access to various training opportunities, a more structured, well-publicized, and practical approach – including clearer development pathways, enhanced mentorship, and improved accessibility – would better support their educator roles.

Years to confidence in transitioning into an educator role

Distribution of participants based on the number of years it took for them to feel confident transitioning into the role of an educator is summarized in Table 1. The majority (54.8%) reported gaining confidence within 3–5 years, while 22.6% achieved this within 0–2 years. A smaller proportion required 6–10 years (9.7%) or more than 10 years (4.8%) to feel confident. Notably, 4.8% of respondents indicated that they still did not feel confident, and 3.2% remained unsure. This suggests variability in the timeline for developing confidence as an educator, highlighting potential gaps in faculty development support. As the study was not powered for inferential comparison, these observations represent descriptive patterns only and should not be interpreted as statistically significant differences.

Level of confidence by years of experience and age

Descriptively, confidence levels tend to increase with years of experience, with more experienced physicians generally reporting higher confidence in their educator roles [Table 2]. Physicians with 16–20 years of experience demonstrated the highest confidence (Mean = 4.17, SD = 0.753), while those with <5 years of experience reported moderate confidence levels (Mean = 3.71, SD = 0.644). Interestingly, physicians with 26–30 years of experience achieved a perfect confidence score (Mean = 5.00, SD = 0.000). No inferential statistical tests were conducted, and these patterns should not be interpreted as statistically significant trends.

Age also influenced confidence levels. Participants aged 41–50 years exhibited the highest confidence (Mean = 4.20, SD = 0.632), while younger physicians aged 30–40 years had lower confidence (Mean = 3.70, SD = 0.608). Older participants (51–60 years and >60 years) reported relatively high confidence levels (Mean = 4.17 and 4.50, respectively), likely reflecting accumulated teaching experience and greater involvement in formal educational roles.

Preferred educator role

Survey results indicate that most emergency physicians (71%, n = 44) align with the “engaging educator” role, characterized by effective teaching, fair assessment, promoting active learning, and ongoing personal development in education. In contrast, fewer educators fully embrace the “reflective educator” role (19.4%, n = 12), which involves implementing best practices, engaging in reflective practices to improve teaching, contributing to faculty development, and participating in educator scholarship. Likewise, the “inspiring educator” role (9.7%, n = 6) – defined by ensuring safe, high-quality education, innovating in education, leading faculty development initiatives, advancing educational practices through scholarly work, mentoring peers, and influencing others through role modelling – appears to be less commonly adopted by faculty. This finding is not surprising given that emergency medicine requires real-time, critical decision-making and immediate teaching opportunities, making hands-on learning and instant feedback essential for effective education.[7] Given the fast-paced nature of the emergency department (ED), physicians often emphasize skill development through direct patient care and procedural involvement, reinforcing learning through active participation.[8]

The latter two roles require more time for self or group-reflection, leadership, and mentoring, which can be challenging to prioritize in the time-constrained and high-pressure ED environment. While these roles remain important, the urgent and dynamic nature of emergency care often precludes opportunities for reflective or inspirational practices beyond immediate patient interactions.[9]

These findings suggest that while engaging educators dominate, there is recognition that reflective and inspiring roles are crucial for long-term development as they encourage a mindset of ongoing improvement and innovation.[10] However, the demands of emergency medicine often make immediate, hands-on teaching the most practical and effective approach.

The highest confidence levels were observed in roles involving direct teaching and mentorship. For instance, the role of “Teacher in clinical/practical setting” (Mean = 4.16, SD = 0.706) and “On the job role model” (Mean = 3.92, SD = 0.855) received the highest ratings. These roles align with the core duties of emergency physicians, where hands-on teaching and supervised mentorship are essential components of their practice. This finding is consistent with the literature on medical education, which emphasizes the value of experiential learning and role modelling in clinical environments.[1112]

Roles that require broader educational responsibilities, such as “Curriculum evaluator” (Mean = 2.94, SD = 1.084) and “Enabling strategic Academic Clinical Centre initiatives/programmes” (Mean = 2.50, SD = 1.156), were rated lower. These roles typically demand specialized knowledge or even qualifications in educational theories, frameworks, governance, and quality assurance processes, which may be less familiar to most emergency physicians. The lower confidence levels suggest a need for more focused training in educational planning and curriculum development. Emergency physicians typically prioritize clinical teaching and patient care, which can limit their exposure to broader educational roles; therefore, encouraging a community of collaborative practice would potentially enhance the exposure or awareness of interested individuals.

Roles involving TEL (Technology Enhanced Learning and Innovation), such as “Developing and implementing TEL strategy to enhance education” (Mean = 2.52) and “Equip other educators with TEL skills and insights” (Mean = 2.31), received the lowest ratings. These roles suggest a gap in familiarity and comfort with technology-enhanced educational tools. Given that TEL has become increasingly important in medical education for simulation-based learning and remote teaching,[13] the results indicate a need for targeted training in TEL to help emergency physicians effectively integrate these impactful tools into their teaching practices beyond the clinical or classroom settings.

DISCUSSION

In response to the final survey question, participants shared their perspectives on how they could better prepare for educator roles after becoming specialists. Several key themes emerged from their responses.

A significant number of participants emphasized the need for a mindset shift, recognizing teaching as an inherent responsibility of their role as emergency physicians in the academic centres. They highlighted the crucial role of institutions in fostering this shift by providing mentors and professional development resources.[14] In addition, self-motivation and lifelong learning were seen as essential components of educator preparation. Participants emphasized the value of pursuing and fostering personal interests, such as bedside teaching or simulation, and committing to ongoing professional development to enhance their teaching skills.[15]

Many participants advocated for a more structured approach or awareness to educator training, suggesting the implementation of clear development pathways that outline recommended courses and training programs, especially in the current climate of surging technologies and innovations targeted at different aspects of enhancing educational experience or outcomes. Several respondents believed that initiating educator training early during residency would be particularly beneficial in establishing a strong foundation.[16] Alongside structured training, consistent mentorship and role modeling were identified as critical factors in fostering teaching skills.[17]

Administrative support and adequate time allocation for teaching responsibilities were also recurring concerns. Participants highlighted the difficulty of balancing clinical with teaching responsibilities and emphasized the need for institutions to allocate dedicated time and resources to support active involvement in educational activities.[18] Furthermore, continuous feedback from both learners and peers was viewed as instrumental in refining teaching practices, identifying areas for self-reflective growth, and excelling as educators.[19]

Many respondents highlighted the importance of having a genuine passion for teaching and dedicating time to professional development. They emphasized that participation in targeted educator courses and active engagement in teaching roles would enhance their effectiveness as educators.

In general, the responses indicate that preparing emergency physicians for educator roles requires a multifaceted approach. A combination of personal motivation, strong institutional backing, structured training pathways, mentorship or role-modeling, and continuous professional development is essential to support sustainable growth and confidence in teaching as future educators.

This study provides valuable insights into the relevance of the 12 educator roles outlined by AMEE and the specific faculty development needs of emergency physicians in Singapore. The findings indicate that while emergency physicians demonstrate significant confidence in hands-on, clinically integrated teaching roles within the working environment, together with personal characteristics tend to shape the educational interests; there is a considerable gap in familiarity with broader or more diverse educator roles, such as curriculum development, TEL, and academic leadership.

The study highlights the need for a more structured, accessible, and well-publicized faculty development framework that supports emergency physicians in transitioning into or embarking on new educator domains. Key recommendations include the integration of faculty development training early on into residency programs, improved mentorship opportunities, and the provision of dedicated time for teaching responsibilities to ensure sustainability. In addition, greater emphasis on TEL and curriculum planning could enhance educators’ preparedness for evolving medical education demands.

Overall, addressing the identified gaps in faculty development programs has the potential to improve the effectiveness of emergency physicians as educators, hopefully creating a sense of community that would ultimately benefit medical trainees and enhancing the quality of education in emergency medicine. Future research should explore the long-term impact of targeted faculty development initiatives, such as promotion or remuneration, implications of educational theories, and assess strategies for optimizing educational training in emergency environments.

Limitation

There were several limitations in this study. It relied on self-reported data, which may be influenced by subjective perceptions. The sample size was small and drawn from only three hospitals in Singapore, limiting generalizability. With a 72% response rate, nonresponders may have differed in experience or views, introducing response bias. Finally, the cross-sectional design captures a single time point and cannot assess changes in confidence or skills over time. In addition, as the study employed descriptive analysis without inferential testing, observed differences between groups should be interpreted cautiously.

CONCLUSIONS

Emergency physicians in Singapore are confident clinical educators but less familiar with broader educational roles. Faculty development initiatives should address gaps in curriculum planning, educational leadership, and technology integration. Recommendations such as structured pathways, longitudinal mentorship, and protected time for more educator responsibilities are critical to supporting professional growth and enhancing educational excellence.
