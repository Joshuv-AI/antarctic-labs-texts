Polyhydroxyalkanoate/seashell powder biocomposites with degradability and 3D printing applications
Authors: Wenchao Min, Bin Guo, Zhenjie Zhao, Shunfeng Hu, Jingqi Shang, Yuhan Bai, Xizhi Zhang, Ximing Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To alleviate environmental pollution and energy crisis, the development of biopolymers with degradability is highly desirable. Polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHA) are produced and accumulated in bacteria cells, possessing good mechanical properties and the features of biodegradability and biocompatibility, becoming a promising substitute of petroleum-based polymers. In this work, seashell powders (SP) are adopted as a renewable biofiller to blend with PHA to obtain the PHA/SP biocomposites. It is found that the seashell powders are uniformly distributed in PHA matrix. Although 10 wt% seashell powder content increases the crystallinity of these biocomposites, their thermal transition temperatures and thermal stability decrease with seashell powder contents. Also, the tensile strength and elongation at break values decrease with seashell powder contents, while the elastic modulus of these biocomposites increase to 3104 MPa due to the enhanced rigidity of filler effect. Of note, these PHA/SP biocomposites can degrade under alkaline conditions within six days, and more seashell powder content facilitates the degradation process. Additionally, they can be used in 3D printing applications to fabricate various items via fused deposition modelling (FDM) technique. Therefore, this work develops a PHA/SP biocomposite system with good mechanical properties and degradability, and they are potential for 3D printing applications to serve diverse fields by fabricating customized items.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55877-z.

Introduction

Petroleum-based polymers are widely applied in various fields due to their versatility, and the utilization of these polymers as plastics is prevalent and representative1–3. It is well-known that, plastics are vastly used in packaging, electronics, medicines, buildings, textiles and other areas as they possess the features of softness, good strength-to-weight ratio, heat seal ability, and transparency4,5. The above advantageous characteristics make them potential to substitute different materials like glass, wood, and metals in terms of functionalities6,7. However, the non-biodegradability of petroleum-based plastics causes tremendously environmental issues and becomes the focus of worldwide attention because most of plastics will accumulate in environment after their actual applications8–10. The accumulation of plastics in environment potentially causes risks of soil and water contamination, and they even can be fragmented into microplastics or nanoplastics by means of thermal and mechanical processes, both of them will be detrimental to the environment and living beings11–13. The incineration and the recycling of plastics are the commonly adopted ways to reduce plastic pollution, while none of them are perfect. Although the incineration of plastics allows to recover some of energy, the resultant toxic gas causes air pollution14–16. The recycling of plastics is a great tendency to address the plastic waste, whereas it is still a slow and difficult process17–19. In addition, some additive substances make the recycling process more complicated and challenging, and the cost of such practice is relative high.

Considering the environmental concerns of petroleum-based plastics, biopolymers become an attractive alternative due to their biodegradable property, and bioplastics are highly demanded20–24. Bioplastics are derived from biobased raw materials or produced by microorganisms25,26. Interestingly, polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHA) are produced and accumulated as granules in Gram-positive or Gram-negative bacteria cells under stressed growth conditions, and the physicochemical properties of these biopolyesters vary with the producer organism and cultivation conditions27–29. PHA can act as bioplastics to replace conventional plastics in an environment-friendly and sustainable perspective due to their non-toxicity, biodegradability, and biocompatibility30–32. Therefore, they are widely used in the field of food, medicine, agriculture, engineering, and industry as packaging materials, cosmetic product, antibacterial materials, medically engineering implant materials, drug delivery carriers, mulching films, etc33–35. Up to now, there are more than 150 types of PHA monomers with different chain lengths that are able to leverage the mechanical and thermal properties36. Among PHA family, poly [3-hydroxybutyrate-co-4-hydroxybutyrate] (P3HB4HB) is one of most promising bioplastics with great technical performance. The inclusion of hydroxybutyrate (HB) blocks is able to improve the flexibility and toughness of PHA that are generally restricted by high crystallinity and brittle behavior37,38. To leverage the physicochemical properties of PHA, physical or chemical modifications are generally adopted39–41.

As we know, seashell is considered to be a safe resource since it does not contain any harmful substances. However, seashell waste will cause many environmental pollution problems if mismanagement occurred42,43. Recycling of seashell becomes an appropriate way to address such problems and necessary for sustainable living. The main component of seashell is calcium carbonate, and the seashell particles can act as a great biofiller to blend into PHA matrix to obtain bio-based PHA/SP composites. For example, Cangiotti et al. incorporated powdered seashell into a polypropylene matrix, and found that the elastic modulus of polypropylene was enhanced along with the filler contents44. Aside from calcium carbonate, seashell powders contain organic components (such as polysaccharides and protein)45, which will be conducive to the their dispersion in polymeric matrix.

It is noteworthy that, due to the biodegradability of PHA and their compatibility with human tissue, they can be generally applied in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine through 3D printing technique to produce complicated and intricate items, scaffolds for instance46. In terms of 3D printing technique, fused deposition modelling (FDM) has become widely adopted by industries and even individual users due to the ease of operation and low equipment costs47,48. As for the PHA with short chain lengths, poly(3-hydroxybutyrate) for instance, it is not suitable for 3D printing due to its rigidity and brittleness33. However, in the case of the PHA with middle chain lengths, such as P3HB4HB, is capable of reducing brittleness and increasing flexibility, becoming a promising candidate for 3D printing. To acquire sufficient mechanical properties, Cecen et al. blended PHA with polylactide (PLA) to obtain the PLA/PHA scaffolds with improved mechanical features and biocompatibility via FDM-based 3D printing49. It must be pointed out that the composites consisting of inorganic fillers and polymeric matrix may be subjected to poor compatibility and inferior performance. Considering the organic components of seashell powders, they are expected to be well dispersed in PHA matrix, and the properties of resultant composites need to be explored.

In this work, PHA was used as the polymeric matrix, and seashell powders (SP) were utilized as a filler to develop the PHA/SP biocomposites. This work intend to investigate the effect of seashell powder contents on the physicochemical properties of these biocomposites, including the thermal stability, mechanical properties, degradability, and 3D printing applications. The fabrication route of PHA/SP biocomposites and their utilization in 3D printing applications are shown in Fig. 1. The chemical structures of the PHA/SP biocomposites were confirmed via FTIR, the thermal properties were evaluated using TG and DSC, and the mechanical properties were determined through a universal testing machine. It was found that seashell powders were evenly distributed in the PHA matrix. The incorporation of seashell powders not only compromised the thermal stability of the biocomposites, but also reduced their glass transition temperature, crystallization temperature, and melting temperature. Also, the tensile strength and elongation at break values of the biocomposites decreased while the elastic modulus increased with the seashell powder contents, and the presence of seashell powders accelerated the degradation of PHA/SP biocomposites at alkaline conditions. Furthermore, these biocomposites are suitable for fabricating complicated items via fused deposition modelling (FDM) process, and the addition of color masterbatches made colorful 3D items possible. Therefore, this work develops a PHA/SP biocomposite system with good mechanical properties and degradability, and they are potential to fabricate the customized items with high forming quality.

Experimental section

Materials

PHA (P3HB4HB with a molecular weight about 540000 Da) was provided by Beijing Weigou Workshop Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Seashell powders (SP) were provided by Hebei Hongyao Mining Products Co., Ltd., and the particle size was below 1 μm (seen in Figure S1). Sodium hydroxide and sulfuric acid were purchased from Aladdin. Color masterbatches were purchased from Dongguan Shunxinlong Plastic Pigment Co., Ltd.

Preparation of the PHA/SP biocomposites

PHA and seashell powders were added in a high-speed mixer (SHR-50 A, Suzhou Haoda Machinery Co., Ltd., China) mixing at 400 rpm for 15 min. Above mixture was then transferred to a twin screw extruder (SHJ-35B, Nanjing Shengchi Rubber Plastic Machinery Co., Ltd., China), and subjected to different temperatures (zone 1, 100 ℃; zone 2, 120 ℃; zone 3, 140 ℃, zone 4, 140 ℃; zone 5, 145 ℃; zone 6, 145 ℃; zone 7, 150 ℃; zone 8, 150 ℃; zone 9, 155 ℃; zone 10, 155 ℃; zone 11, 160 ℃; die head, 165℃). The rotational speed was 150 rpm, melt pressure was about 4.5 MPa, and the main residence time was about 140 s. Afterwards, the PHA/SP biocomposites was obtained. Specifically, the resultant biocomposites were denoted as PHA/SP10, PHA/SP20, PHA/SP30, PHA/SP40 when the weight ratios of seashell powders to PHA were 10 wt%, 20 wt%, 30 wt%, and 40 wt% respectively. To prepare the filaments (diameter of 1.75 mm) with colors, the color masterbatches were mixed with PHA and seashell powders at a weight ratio of 1:19, and the extruded filaments were then used for printing using a 3D printer (Bambu Lab, China). As for the 3D printing process, the nozzle temperature was 180℃, the bed temperature was 60℃, the printing speed was 200 mm/s, the layer thickness was 0.2 mm, and the infill density was 100%.

Characterization and measurement

The chemical structures of the PHA/SP biocomposite were confirmed via a Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR, Thermo Fisher Nicolet iS10, USA). To investigate the thermal stability of the biocomposites, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA, Mettler-Toledo TGA2, Switzerland) was used, the temperature was ranging from 40℃ to 600℃, and the heating rate was 10℃/min with a nitrogen flow of 25 mL/min. To determine the glass transition temperature, crystallization temperature, and melting temperature of the biocomposites, differential scanning calorimetry (DSC, TA Instruments Q2000, USA) was utilized. The testing temperature was from − 10℃ to 200℃, the heating rate was 10℃/min, and the nitrogen flow rate was 50 mL/min. The samples were cooling down naturally under nitrogen atmosphere. The crystallinity (Xc) of diffferent samples was calculated according to the following formula: Xc = ΔHm/ΔH0m×100%. ΔHm is the melting enthalpy of different PHA samples, and ΔH0mis the theoretical melting enthalpy of 100% PHA (146 J/g)50,51. The morphology of the biocomposites was observed via a scanning electron microscope (SEM, Carl Zeiss Sigma 300, Germany). The tensile test was carried out using a universal testing machine (UTM-1423, Chengde Jinjian Testing Instrument Co., Ltd, China) according to ASTM D63852, and the dumbbell-shaped samples with a gauge length of 50 mm and a thickness of 4 mm were stretched with a constant rate of 50 mm/min. Five specimens were tested for each biocomposite with different seashell powder content. To investigate the degradability of the biocomposites, the samples were immersed into acid solution (pH = 1) or alkaline solution (pH = 13) for 6 days, and the degradation status was recorded and analyzed. The corresponding mass loss after degradation was calculated after the degraded samples were filtered with a 250 mesh filter screen.

Results and discussions

FTIR analysis of the PHA/SP biocomposites

The FTIR spectra of the PHA/SP biocomposites are shown in Fig. 2. The peak at 2997 cm− 1 represented -CH3 stretching vibration. The peaks at 2927 cm− 1 and 2853 cm− 1 were attributed to -CH2- asymmetric stretching vibration and symmetric stretching vibration respectively53. The peaks at around 1746 cm− 1 was attributed to C = O stretching vibration, and the peaks around 1085 cm− 1 was ascribed to C-O-C stretching vibration, which revealed the presence of ester bonds inherent in PHA. After the addition of seashell powders, the peak intensity at around 870 cm− 1 enhanced, which was mainly due to the C-O in-plane bending vibration of calcium carbonate. In addition, the increase of seashell powder content weakened the peak intensity corresponding to PHA to some extent. Moreover, it can be seen from the cross-section of the PHA/SP biocomposites that seashell powders were evenly distributed in PHA matrix (Fig. 6b-c). Above results indicated the successful preparation of the PHA/SP biocomposites.

Thermal stability of the PHA/SP biocomposites

To investigate the thermal stability of the PHA/SP biocomposites, the biocomposites with different seashell powder contents were thermally treated. The TGA and DTG curves of different samples are shown in Fig. 3a-b, and the corresponding thermal decomposition temperatures and residue amounts are listed in Table 1. Obviously, as seen in Fig. 3a; Table 1, the residue amount of pure PHA was about 3.8 wt% at 600℃, and the PHA/SP biocomposites showed greater residue amounts due to the addition of seashell powders. In Fig. 3b, it was observed that the decomposition temperatures toward 5%, 10%, 50% weight loss of the pure PHA were 284.8℃, 294.0℃, and 355.8℃, respectively. However, they decreased to 249.2℃, 260.0℃, 301.2℃ respectively as the seashell powder content increased to 40 wt%. In addition, these biocomposites showed two-stage decomposition, which was mainly due to the different chain lengths and compactness of these biocomposites. Similarly, the incorporation of seashell powders reduced the maximum decomposition temperature of these biocomposites from 359.1 ℃ to 296.9 ℃ (Table 1), and the decrease in decomposition temperature was mainly because seashell powders undermined the compactness of these biocomposites, thus facilitating the thermal diffusion and accelerating decomposition.

DSC analysis of the PHA/SP biocomposites

The DSC curves of the PHA/SP biocomposites with different seashell powders are displayed in Fig. 4. The DSC curves showed that, the glass transition temnperature (Tg), crystallization temperature (Tc) and melting temperature (Tm) of the pure PHA was about 61.7℃, 88.9℃, and 176.3℃, respectively. When 10 wt% seashell powder was added into PHA matrix, the above temperatures decreased to 52.5℃, 85.0℃ and 165.6℃, respectively. Further increasing seashell powder content resulted in lower temperature values toward glass transition temperature, crystallization temperature, and melting temperature, which was mainly because the incorporation of seashell powders decreased the compactness of these biocomposites. Additionally, the crystallinity of different PHA/SP biocomposites was calculated, and the results are shown in Table S1. It was found that the sample with 10 wt% seashell powders exhibited the highest crystallinity of 26.62%, while more addition of seashell powders gave rise to the lower crystallinity of the samples. This phenomenon was mainly because the seashell powders could act as a heterogeneous nucleating agent and facilitate the formation of high crystallinity. Nevertheless, excessive incorporation of seashell powders generally resulted in prominent steric hindrance and disrupted the integrity of crystal growth, leading to the decrease in crystallinity of the samples.

Mechanical properties of the PHA/SP biocomposites

The effects of seashell powder contents on the mechanical properties of PHA/SP biocomposites were investigated. Figure 5a-b display the tensile strength and elastic modulus of the PHA/SP biocomposites with different seashell powder contents. It is well-known that, ultimate tensile strength is defined as the maximum stress that a material is able to withstand under a given load without breaking. In this work, the tensile strength of the pure PHA was turned out to be about 58.6 MPa. A general decreasing trend toward tensile strength was found along with the addition of seashell powders. Although the biocomposites with 10 wt% seashell powder content exhibited the highest crystallinity, the non-covalent interactions between the organic components of seashell powders and PHA were unable to effectively enhance the tensile strength of the biocomposites, leading to a tensile strength of 53.4 MPa. Additionally, it decreased by 7.51% as the seashell powder content increased to 20 wt%. Further addition of seashell powders resulted in inferior tensile strength. When the seashell powder content reached to 40 wt%, the tensile strength of the biocomposite decreased to 40.9 MPa. Similarly, the addition of seashell powders also gave rise to the decrease in elongation at break values. The elongation at break value of the pure PHA was about 3.8%, while it decreased to 3.4%, 2.5%, 2.2%, and 2.0% as the seashell powder content was 10 wt%, 20 wt%, 30 wt%, 40 wt%, respectively (seen in Figure S2). Above results indicated the incorporation of seashell powders restricted the chain mobility of the polymeric matrix, increasing the brittleness of the composites and thus reducing the elongation at break value. Noteworthily, the elastic modulus of these biocomposites increased with the seashell powder content. When the seashell content increased to 40 wt%, the corresponding elastic modulus value increased from 1980 MPa to 3104 MPa (Fig. 5b). The increase in elastic modulus was mainly due to the enhanced rigidity of filler effect. Razali et al. prepared PLA biocomposite with calcined seashell particles, and the biocomposites with 10wt% calcined seashell content achieved the maximum tensile strength of 49.06 MPa and elastic modulus of 241.9 MPa54. Kothandaraman et al. fabricated the PLA composites reinforced with seashell powders, and found that 6wt% seashell content yielded the optimal mechanical performance55. The tensile strength was about 9 MPa, and the elastic modulus was 315 MPa. By comparison, the PHA/SP biocomposites in this work exhibited superior mechanical properties than the above PLA biocomposites. Considering the mechanical properties of these biocomposites, they are potential for various application areas, serving as packaging materials or mulching films for instance.

Degradability of the PHA/SP biocomposites

The non-degradability of petroleum-based polymers generally brings about long-term environmental pollution. As for the PHA/SP biocomposites prepared in this work, their degradability was investigated. As seen in Fig. 6a, the pure PHA exhibited compact structure. In the case of PHA/SP biocomposites, seashell powders were distributed evenly in PHA matrix (Fig. 6b-c), which was mainly attributed to the presence of organic components inherent in seashell powders. It is noteworthy that PHA contains ester bonds in the main chains, and the ester bonds are susceptible to alkaline conditions, which may led to the degradation of PHA. To confirm such hypothesis, the PHA/SP biocomposites with different seashell powder contents were immersed in alkaline solution, and the degradation status was recorded. As seen in Fig. 6e, these biocomposites were not visibly degraded on the first day. After 6 days of immersion in alkaline solution, it was found that the biocomposite with higher seashell powder contents suffered from greater degradation. As for the pure PHA, its surfaces were slightly corroded (Fig. 6f), and it remained intact as a piece (Fig. 6e). This result can be explained by the compact structure of the pure PHA preventing the penetration of corrosive liquid into the polymer matrix. However, the biocomposite with 10 wt% PHA content exhibited severer surface corrosion. As demonstrated in Fig. 6g, its surface was disintegrated to some extent due to the alkaline corrosion, leading to the thinner piece of the composite (Fig. 6e). This phenomenon was resulted from the hydrophilic seashell powders facilitating the penetration of alkaline solution into PHA matrix and then accelerating the degradation of ester bonds inherent in the main chains of PHA. Such corrosion process intensified as more seashell powders were incorporated. The degradation of these biocomposites became more obvious when the seashell powder content was above 20 wt%, and the plate-shape biocomposite started to collapse. In the case of PHA/SP30, it was almost fully disintegrated into powders owing to the alkaline corrosion. This degradation behavior could be evidenced by Fig. 6e and h. The mass losses of these biocomposites are shown in Figure S3, and the results also revealed that more seashell powder content was conducive to the degradation of PHA/SP biocomposites at alkaline conditions.

Aside from alkaline solution, these biocomposites were immersed in acidic solution to investigate whether they could degrade in acidic condition. However, after 6 days of immersion, these biocomposites remained intact in appearance as shown in Figure S4b. To explain this phenomenon, the surfaces of different biocomposites after treament with acidic solution were observed and compared. It was found that the pure PHA was suffered from little corrosion mainly due to the relative stability of PHA in acidic condition (Figure S4c). However, some cavities appeared on the surfaces of PHA/SP biocomposites (Figure S4d), and such cavities were mainly attributed to the dissolution of seashell powders in the acidic solution. Although more cavities appeared as the seashell powder contents increased (Figure S4e), these biocomposites were not collapsed that occurred in the alkaline condition. Therefore, to make these PHA/SP biocomposites degrade, alkaline conditions are more effective and efficient.

The 3D printing applications of the PHA/SP biocomposites

In addition, we found that these PHA/SP biocomposites could be used for 3D printing to fabricate some items with complicated shapes. To evaluate their 3D printing applications, the biocomposites with different seashell powder contents were employed, and fused deposition modelling (FDM)-based 3D printing technique was adopted. The 3D printing process of the pristine boat-shaped sample (without color masterbatches added) and blue boat-shaped sample (with color masterbatches added) are presented in Fig. 7a and b respectively. The final 3D-printed items with different seashell powder contents (0 wt%, 10 wt%, 20 wt%, 30 wt%, 40 wt%) and colors are displayed in Fig. 7c. During the 3D printing process, we found that all of these biocomposite filaments exhibited stable extrusion without the occurrence of nozzle clogging and wire breakage, indicating that the addition of seashell powders did not significantly impair the thermoplastic processability of the PHA matrix. All boat-shaped items were successfully fabricated, and they possessed intact structures and clear outlines, demonstrating favorable layer-to-layer adhesion and dimensional forming accuracy under the adopted printing parameters. Also, no obvious delamination, crack, or powder agglomeration was observed on the surface of the 3D-printed boats, confirming the good printability of this biocomposite system. Above results verify that these PHA/SP biocomposites can act as a promising feedstock for 3D printing. Considering the degradability and processibility of these biocomposites, they can also be utilized to fabricate degradable customized structural items for various fields.

Conclusions

In summary, a PHA/SP biocomposite system was developed in this work by blending seashell powders into PHA matrix. The chemical structures of these biocomposites were confirmed via FTIR analysis, and the uniform distribution of seashell biofillers was verified by SEM. It was found that 10 wt% seashell powder content increased the crystallinity of these biocomposites, while their thermal transition temperatures (including glass transition temperature, crystallization temperature, and melting temperature) and thermal stability decreased with seashell powder contents. Furthermore, although the tensile strength and elongation at break values decreased with the seashell powder contents, the elastic modulus of these biocomposites increased due to the enhanced rigidity of filler effect. In addition, these PHA/SP biocomposites could be degraded under alkaline environment owing to the hydrolysis of the ester bonds inherent in the main chains of PHA, and more seashell contents accelerated the degradation process because seashell powders not only undermined the compactness of PHA but also facilitated the penetration of alkaline solution into biocomposite matrix. Moreover, these biocomposites could be used to fabricate the items with complicated structures via fused deposition modelling technique. Therefore, this work develops a PHA/SP biocomposite system with good mechanical properties and degradability, and they are potential for 3D printing applications to fabricate customized items for diverse fields.
