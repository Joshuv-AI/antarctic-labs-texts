Variation in coronary revascularisation and mortality after myocardial infarction across three public health insurance schemes in Thailand: an observational analysis from nationwide claims data
Authors: Woranan Witthayapipopsakul, Orawan Anupraiwan, Gumpanart Veerakul, Anne Mills, Ipek Gurol-Urganci, Jan van der Meulen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEvidence on the impact of diverse healthcare insurance arrangements on healthcare variation is limited in low-income and middle-income countries. In Thailand, the Civil Servant Medical Benefit Scheme (CSMBS), Social Health Insurance (SHI) and Universal Coverage Scheme (UCS) have different provider choice and reimbursement arrangements and cover different populations. We explored to what extent use of revascularisation in patients with ST elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) varied by insurance scheme.MethodsWe used claims data, including all admissions for patients with STEMI between 2015 and 2020. Outcomes were any type of revascularisation, primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PPCI) and mortality. Regression models were used to estimate absolute differences (ADs) by scheme, adjusted for age, sex, comorbidities and admission year.ResultsOf 98 142 patients, 75.7% were covered by UCS, 13.3% by CSMBS and 11.0% by SHI. Overall, 76.3% underwent revascularisation and 53.8% received PPCI. Mortality rates were 13.2% in-hospital and 20.7% at 180 days. Compared with UCS, use of revascularisation was slightly higher with CSMBS and slightly lower with SHI (AD: CSMBS 1.3% (95% CI −0.2 to 2.8), SHI −0.8% (−2.6 to 1.0), p=0.0264) and use of PPCI was slightly higher with CSMBS and SHI (AD: CSMBS 2.4% (−0.3 to 5.2), SHI 5.2% (3.1 to 7.2), p<0.0001)). CSMBS and SHI-insured patients had lower mortality compared with UCS (AD for in-hospital: CSMBS −1.3% (−2.1 to –0.5), SHI −0.9% (−1.8 to −0.1), p<0.0001; AD for 180-day mortality: CSMBS −4.5% (−5.3 to –3.6), SHI −1.9% (−3.0 to −0.8), p<0.0001). Effects of insurance scheme varied by hospital type for all outcomes (p for interaction<0.0001).ConclusionThree-quarters of patients with STEMI received coronary revascularisation, suggesting potential undertreatment. We identified relatively small differences in access to revascularisation by insurance scheme which are unlikely to explain the lower mortality with CSMBS and SHI. Claims data can be used to assess the impact of insurance on access to effective treatments.Keywords: Public Health, Cross-Sectional Studies, Cardiovascular Diseases, Emergencies

Background

Healthcare variation and its sources and consequences have been a long-established research theme in high-income countries.1,4 Evidence suggests that patient factors (eg, sociodemographic background, comorbidities, healthcare-seeking behaviour), clinical factors (eg, availability, knowledge, capacities, practice styles), hospital resources, health systems regulations and healthcare financing environments contribute to the disparities.5,7 Research on healthcare variation from low-income and middle-income countries is limited, although practice variation is likely to be common, given that their healthcare financing arrangements are typically more fragmented than in high-income countries.8,10 Health insurance arrangements such as budget size, benefit package, provider payment mechanisms, choice of providers, user fees, administration or beneficiary characteristics may influence both providers and patients and in turn contribute to variation.11,13

In Thailand, studies exploring variations in use of emergency services,14 diabetes management,15 digestive disease management, epilepsy care, lung cancer treatments,8 caesarean section, cholecystectomy and expensive prescriptions and in length of hospital stay16 found mixed results. A particular concern is whether health insurance arrangements have an impact on access to ‘effective treatments’, which are treatments that have been shown to improve outcomes for patients.17

Myocardial infarction is a leading cause of morbidity and premature death in Thailand, and there is strong evidence that coronary revascularisation procedures improve outcomes.18 19 The aim of this study is to explore variations in the use of revascularisation for ST elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) and mortality among patients with STEMI according to insurance scheme, using Thailand as an example of an upper-middle-income country with diverse health financing arrangements.

Health insurance schemes and STEMI management

Public health insurance schemes in Thailand cover nearly the entire population through three main schemes20: the Civil Servant Medical Benefit Scheme (CSMBS) for civil servants and their dependants, Social Health Insurance (SHI) for private sector employees and the Universal Coverage Scheme (UCS) for everyone else.21 Since membership depends on employment status, there are stark differences in sociodemographic characteristics across schemes. For instance, most SHI members are working age while UCS and CSMBS have many members over 60 years of age.20 A majority of SHI and CSMBS members live in urban areas, while a majority of UCS members work in the informal sector in rural areas. About 50% of CSMBS members are in the richest wealth quintile, whereas the corresponding percentage is 15% for UCS.22

Most healthcare provision is managed by the Ministry of Public Health (MOPH), supplemented by public hospitals run by other ministries, and private hospitals. The route of accessing healthcare differs by scheme. UCS members register at a primary care unit, which they must visit first, with referral to higher level hospitals if necessary.23 SHI members register at a public or private hospital contracted by the SHI, which must have at least 100 beds and provide both primary care and more advanced treatment. CSMBS members can go to any public provider.23 In an emergency, patients are permitted to access any nearby hospital, irrespective of whether it is public or private.24 25 All three schemes offer a similarly comprehensive benefit package but they pay healthcare providers differently.21 Eligibility criteria for some service provision, payment methods and rates may also vary by scheme.26,28 While the benefit packages of all three public schemes include STEMI management, there are some differences in fibrinolytic agent options and reimbursement rates (online supplemental table 1).2628,31

In Thailand, STEMI management is arranged as a service network organised within 13 health regions. A ‘STEMI fast track’ network connects a percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) centre with a specific referral protocol. These networks are based on the public healthcare facilities of the MOPH, but they also include non-MOPH PCI centres. The MOPH structure has four levels operating all over the country outside Bangkok: health centre, district hospital, provincial hospital and regional hospital.23 Bangkok Metropolitan Administration has its own primary care centres. Other public hospitals such as specialised MOPH hospitals, military hospitals and university hospitals generally provide similar capacity to provincial and regional hospitals.32 All healthcare facilities should refer patients with signs of a possible myocardial infarction to a PCI-capable hospital in their network. Primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PPCI), that is, PCI without prior fibrinolysis, is a preferred method if it can be performed within 120 min from STEMI diagnosis.33 In 2020, most district hospitals were able to perform fibrinolysis, and there was at least one PCI centre per region, varying between 1 and 18 centres.34 There may be other PCI-capable hospitals not registered as such, especially private hospitals.

Methods

Data sources

We used insurance claims data collected for reimbursement purposes, covering all hospital admissions for the three public insurance schemes. The data included a patient identification number, an admission number, age (in years), sex, hospital code, admission date, discharge date, principal and secondary diagnosis codes according to the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision (ICD-10)35 and procedure codes according to the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM).36 Patient records were linked to the civil registration and vital statistics system for date of death until October 2021.

Participants

The study included UCS, SHI and CSMBS members aged 18 years and older admitted to hospital with a principal diagnosis of STEMI (ICD-10 codes: I21.0-I21.3) between 1 January 2015 and 31 December 2020. Figure 1 describes the cohort selection. We excluded records with implausible death dates (27 records). ‘Super-admissions’, that is, when the same patient had multiple immediately consecutive admissions due to transfers within or between hospitals, were treated as a single admission and all information about procedures and secondary diagnosis codes during the super-admission was collated. If the super-admission involved transfers between hospitals, the admission was assigned to the hospital that performed the most invasive coronary revascularisation (ie, coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG)>PCI>fibrinolysis) or coronary angiography if no revascularisation was performed. If two or more hospitals performed equally invasive procedures, the hospital code of the latest record was retained. We randomly selected one admission if a patient was hospitalised more than once within the study period.

Patient selection flowchart. STEMI, ST elevation myocardial infarction.

Main exposures and outcomes

Our main exposure was insurance scheme. The study outcomes were use of coronary revascularisation (ie, whether the patients received any revascularisation treatment and whether they received PPCI) and all-cause mortality (in-hospital and within and 180 days from the day of admission). Revascularisation procedures included PCI (ICD-9-CM: 00.40–00.48, 00.66, 36.06–36.07), fibrinolysis (99.10) and CABG (36.10–36.19). We considered patients had received PPCI when they had received PCI alone, without fibrinolysis or CABG in combination.

Statistical analysis

We used mixed-effects generalised linear regression models (GLMs) with a Gaussian distribution of errors and identity link function to estimate absolute differences (ADs) and their 95% CI according to insurance scheme in the use of coronary revascularisation and mortality.37 A positive AD means that the use of revascularisation in a comparison group is higher than in the reference group.38 We included a random intercept for each health region as we expected between-region variation due to varying density of PCI-capable hospitals across regions and differences in local care policies. These models were also used to estimate ADs adjusted for patient characteristics (sex, age and comorbidities) and year of admission. We also explored whether the effects of insurance scheme on the study outcomes differed by hospital type by including interaction terms.

Age was modelled using a linear and a quadratic form after centring age by subtracting the average age of the whole cohort. Comorbidity information was taken from secondary diagnoses included in the record of the index admission and categorised according to the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI). The number of comorbidities was grouped as 0, 1, 2, 3 or more, according to the Royal College of Surgeons of England’s CCI,39 but we excluded acute conditions (eg, acute renal failure) because they may be a complication of the STEMI or its treatment rather than a pre-existing comorbid condition. We grouped hospitals into four types: district hospitals, provincial hospitals and equivalent, regional hospitals and equivalent, and private hospitals. We considered university hospitals equivalent to regional hospitals and other public hospitals equivalent to provincial hospitals.

All analyses were done using Stata 17.0 SE.40 A p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Justification for using the GLM for mortality outcomes, along with a comparison of adjusted proportions from both the GLM and logistic models, is provided in online supplemental table 2.

Patient and public involvement

This study was a retrospective analysis based on insurance claim data. As such, no patients were directly involved in the design, recruitment, conduct or dissemination of the research. The research questions, outcome measures and study design were developed independently by the research team without patient or public involvement.

Results

Baseline characteristics

98 142 patients met the study eligibility criteria and 71.4% were men (figure 1, table 1). Three-quarters were members of UCS (75.7%), followed by CSMBS (13.3%) and SHI (11.0%). UCS and CSMBS members were generally older than SHI members (mean age 63.3, 67.5 and 50.2 years, respectively). 61.4% of patients did not have a comorbidity, 30.0% had one, 7.6% two and 1.1% three or more. The comorbidity profile was very similar across schemes. Our 6-year data showed a slight increase in the annual number of STEMI admissions.

Insurance scheme and in-hospital and 180-day mortality

Overall, the mortality rates among patients with STEMI were 13.2% (in-hospital) and 20.7% (at 180 days) (table 3). Mortality rates were higher in patients insured through UCS (14.0% in-hospital and 22.2% at 180 days) and CSMBS (14.8% in hospital and 21.5% at 180 days) than in patients insured through SHI (6.3% in hospital and 9.4% at 180 days). The unadjusted models, including hospital region as a random effect, only made a slight change in these differences (AD compared with UCS for in-hospital: CSMBS 0.9% (−0.1 to 2.0), SHI −6.8% (−7.9 to –5.7), p<0.0001); for 180-day: CSMBS −0.5% (−1.6 to 0.6), SHI −11.6% (−13.0 to –10.1), p<0.0001)). Discussion

Key results

This study is the first to investigate variations by insurance scheme in STEMI management in Thailand. We found a complex pattern of results. Overall, three in four patients admitted with STEMI received coronary revascularisation and about one in two received PPCI. The unadjusted use rates of any coronary revascularisation method and PPCI were higher in SHI-insured patients than those of CSMBS-insured and UCS-insured patients. The differences in the use of any revascularisation method disappeared when patient characteristics were taken into account, but the use of PPCI remained slightly higher in SHI-insured patients. At the same time, the unadjusted mortality rate was lower in SHI-insured patients than in those insured through UCS and CSMBS. However, when patient characteristics were taken into account, mortality was lower in CSMBS-insured and SHI-insured patients than UCS-insured patients.

We also found evidence that the differences in use of any revascularisation or PPCI between the three insurance schemes varied according to hospital type, with the largest increases in use seen in CSMBS-insured and SHI-insured patients who were treated in provincial hospitals. Similarly, the differences in mortality between the insurance schemes varied according to hospital type, with the largest decreases in mortality rate seen in CSMBS-insured and SHI-insured patients treated in provincial hospitals.

Strengths and limitations

One important strength of this study is that we used national health insurance claims databases which covered all patients with STEMI admitted during the study period, thus avoiding sampling bias. To the best of our knowledge, it is also the first study to assess variation in the use of an effective treatment in a middle-income setting.17 Its results may be relevant for other middle-income countries because it demonstrates the potential impact of diverse health insurance arrangements on the use of effective care in emergency and life-threatening situations.

A limitation is that clinical severity upon arrival, such as cardiac arrest and heart failure, which would indicate poorer prognosis, was not recorded. We were also unable to explore further clinical outcomes after treatment (eg, whether successful ST-segment resolution was achieved or whether there were PCI complications). Time to treatment was also not available, so we could not exclude from our study the patients who presented beyond 12 hours of symptom onset.41 Moreover, we did not know the cause of death and we could use only all-cause mortality. More information on supply-side factors such as facility infrastructure and the characteristics of the physicians providing the services (such as medical specialty and experience) was also unavailable. We did not adjust for ‘day of week’ as a weekend effect is unlikely to differentially affect any particular scheme.

Comparison with other studies

In line with other studies of variations in effective treatments in other settings,3 17 42 and specifically for STEMI patients,43 44 we found that the observed variation in STEMI revascularisation treatment is small. Also, our results are consistent with previous research on coronary revascularisation and mortality among UCS beneficiaries with STEMI between 2011 and 2017.45

In contrast, the mortality rates that we found in this study are considerably higher than the rates reported in the Thai MOPH indicator dashboard (in-hospital mortality of 5.2–10.2% during 2020–2022),46 or found in the Thai ACS registry cohort (12-month mortality rate of 14.1% for 2007–2008),47 but the ACS registry included only 39 hospitals for 2007–2008, suggesting lower coverage in this registry than in our study.

Interpretation of the results

The results of our study suggest that the impact of the diverse healthcare insurance arrangements on variations in STEMI revascularisation treatment and all-cause mortality in Thailand is relatively minor. The existence of clear clinical practice guidelines for STEMI management, coverage of STEMI treatment in all insurance schemes and the Universal Coverage for Emergency Patients (UCEP) policy requiring all hospitals to accept patients under emergency and life-threatening situations regardless of whether they are contracted providers are likely to explain these findings. The remaining variation favouring CSMBS may be rooted in differences in patient characteristics (eg, income, higher education, health literacy) that could not be included in the regression models which were used to estimate the adjusted results.

Also, CSMBS-insured patients may be more likely than others to visit PCI-capable hospitals without referral since their scheme policy does not require members to initiate contact at the primary level. Despite the UCEP policy, SHI and UCS members with STEMI symptoms may visit their registered provider first in order to obtain a formal referral, potentially prolonging the time between onset of symptoms and revascularisation. CSMBS and SHI patients might get better post-acute care than UCS after discharge, resulting in lower 180-day mortality. Queues at public hospitals might result in delayed diagnosis which could partly explain the lower rate of revascularisation compared with private hospitals.

Policy implications

Given that we found that three in four admitted patients with STEMI underwent a form of revascularisation, suggesting undertreatment, all three insurance schemes should focus on ensuring access to revascularisation. Also, given the relatively small differences in the use of revascularisation according to insurance scheme, it is unlikely that differences in use of revascularisation explain the lower mortality observed in patients insured through CSMBS and SHI.

Our research is not able to identify the drivers of access to revascularisation. We recommend qualitative research, building on our quantitative findings. Also, a study of variation in post-revascularisation management is especially important as this may explain the observed variation in 180-day mortality. It may be worthwhile looking at variation in thrombolytic options given the clear difference between the healthcare benefits of the three schemes.

The observed potential underuse of revascularisation demonstrates that all insurance schemes and the MOPH should work together to improve access to revascularisation overall and to PCI centres in particular, and should focus on regional differences in capacity. Finally, the current quality monitoring frameworks that focus on specific subgroups (eg, MOPH hospitals, the voluntary Thai ACS registry or scheme-level platforms) are not adequate to capture disparities in access to treatment and outcomes for the whole population. Suitably anonymised electronic health records, capturing the care provided to individual patients and their outcomes, should be made available for quality assessment and assurance and for clinical and health systems research to support policy development, as has been done in Taiwan48 and South Korea.49

Conclusions

Our study found that the differences in use of revascularisation overall, and in the use of primary PCI in particular, varied slightly according to insurance scheme. It is unlikely that the observed small variations in the use of revascularisation according to insurance scheme explain the differences in longer-term mortality. All insurance schemes need to review the observed potential undertreatment. Further research is still needed for STEMI treatment in Thailand, especially on improving access to revascularisation and further exploring whether differences in patient management after initial treatment of myocardial infarction can be linked to observed mortality differences. Also, this study has demonstrated that health insurance claims data can be used to assess access, utilisation and quality of hospital-based care in countries such as Thailand, with national-level databases.
