Understanding human embryogenesis by building programmable stem cell-based models
Authors: Carly Guiltinan, Gerrald A Lodewijk, Sayaka Kozuki, S Ali Shariati
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractStem cell-based embryo models provide an alternative system to study an elusive period of development. Programmed mouse embryo models have recently been generated by activating two endogenous regulatory elements via epigenome editing. In this forum article, we discuss this achievement along with the potential of translating it to engineering models of human embryogenesis.

Potential for advancing developmental biology using synthetic biology tools

Despite being one of the most conse-quential times in human development, the peri-implantation period is one of the least understood due to technical and regulatory constraints that make it inaccessible for direct study [1]. Over the past decade, scientists have realized the potential of pluripotent stem cells (PSCs) to form structures that capture key aspects of embryogenesis, leading to the development of stem cell-based embryo models (SEMs) [2–5]. These structures can mimic different stages of natural embryogenesis morphologically and molecularly, offering an accessible system for studies spanning what had been considered as a ‘black box’. Concurrent with the emergence of SEMs, synthetic biology tools – including CRISPR-based approaches – have significantly advanced our ability to precisely program cellular decisions. CRISPR activation (CRISPRa) is an epigenomic editing technique that initiates the expression of genes in their native genomic context by inducing chromatin changes that trigger cell fate decision-making [6,7]. Integrating these tools with SEM production systems enables researchers not only to observe embryogenesis, but also to intrinsically program this process in controllable manner [8]. For example, CRISPRa-programmed embryo models (CPEMs) have been generated from mouse PSCs by activating two endogenous regulatory elements driving each of the extraembryonic lineages [9]. Remarkably, CPEMs were capable of reproducibly self-organizing into 3D structures similar to natural pre-gastrulation embryos without the need for exogenous cell-specific morphogens or changes to the DNA sequence. This forum article introduces epigenome editing, summarizes the recent progress generating mouse CPEMs and how they compare to existing SEMs, considers the potential for applying similar methods to enable the production of human CPEMs, and explores prospective studies that such progress would open the door to.

Epigenome editors enable intrinsic control of cell fate

Stem cells undergo lineage commitment in response to biochemical and biomechanical signals received from their environment. Their intrinsic state, shaped by their transcriptional, epigenomic, and metabolic profiles, further refines how they interpret and respond to these external signals. To initiate differentiation, stem cells must ultimately activate or repress a set of specific genes by modifying the chromatin state of their regulatory elements, thus triggering new transcriptional programs that drive cell fate transitions. However, the full potential of engineering endogenous regulatory elements to control PSC development into SEMs has not been explored. Epigenome editing tools offer the potential to directly and precisely target endogenous regulatory elements to investigate the role of individual factors in cell fate decision-making [6,7]. At the heart of this innovation is a CRISPRa device: a fusion of bacterial immune defenses, viral transcriptional activators, and chromatin regulators that repurpose the original CRISPR–Cas system by stripping it of its DNA-cutting ability and transforming it into a molecular switch that can activate specific genes by changing the chemical composition of nearby chromatin. The result is a molecular tool for precise epigenetic modulation and control of gene expression that can reprogram cell identity and engineer multicellular embryo-like systems (Figure 1A–D). In this model, gene activity is no longer dictated by external cocktails of signaling molecules, but rather is intrinsically controlled by engineered regulatory circuits that respond to user-defined inputs. By targeting key transcriptional elements that drive cells to assume a particular fate, even minimal epigenome editing can guide cells toward a lineage of interest.

(A) A CRISPRa system is introduced into pluripotent embryonic stem cells along with vectors expressing lineage-specific single guide RNAs (sgRNAs). (B) CRISPRa activates key regulatory elements at differentially accessible regulatory elements near key cell fate-determining factors to induce extraembryonic fates, including trophoblast and extraembryonic endoderm (ExEn) lineages. Generation of reproducible mouse embryo models by CRISPRa programming

This year, the first described CPEMs were generated from mouse PSCs by epigenomic activation of endogenous regulatory elements driving the trophoblast (Cdx2) and extraembryonic endoderm (Gata6) lineages (Figure 1A,B) [9]. Upon CRISPR activation of these elements, along with a non-targeting guide to maintain an undifferentiated population akin to the epiblast, cells spontaneously underwent collective migration and proliferation to self-organize into structures resembling mouse pre-gastrulation embryos (Figure 1C,D). Mouse CPEMs are just about the size of mouse embryos at embryonic days 5–6 with similar cellular composition and spatial organization, including cavity formation and precursors of signaling centers involved in axis patterning. Formation of these structures occurred without the addition of lineage-specific morphogens, demonstrating that intrinsic induction of cell fate through CRISPRa enabled PSCs to follow their natural morphogenetic program to reproducibly assemble themselves into embryo-like models. To this effect, 80% of CPEMs formed cavities, and single-cell transcriptome analysis confirmed the presence of the three major embryonic lineages in CPEMs, which had similar profiles to those of natural embryos. Furthermore, the utility of mouse CPEMs for genetic perturbation experiments was demonstrated by activating genes associated with basement membrane remodeling, including large and complex genes, which affected overall morphological features of CPEMs [9].

Harnessing the potential of programmable embryo models for mechanistic studies of human embryogenesis

Developmental biology studies using embryos from model species have provided an important context for these events in humans. However, species-specific differences in embryogenesis (especially in humans versus rodents) warrant studies for which h uman SEMs are a viable and accessible alternative with significantly less ethical and logistical constraints [2,10]. The generation of mouse CPEMs serves as a proof-of-concept that this approach can be applied to PSCs of other species, including humans [2–4]. Determining regulatory elements whose activation is sufficient to drive self-organization of human embryo models will be a crucial first step in identification of factors that faithfully drive the extraembryonic lineages in species that form as a bilaminar embryonic disc (Figure 2A), as they have likely diverged from those of rodents, both in timing and nature [10]. Beyond reproducibly creating models, CPEMs can be tailored in numerous ways by targeting different developmental pathways in a line-age-specific manner, enabling in-depth mechanistic studies that are otherwise challenging to do with natural embryos or morphogen-induced SEMs (Figure 2A–D). For instance, integration of base editors will allow for high-resolution mapping of cell fate at single-cell level for building precise lineage trees (Figure 2B) [11]. Screening experiments with CRISPRa can uncover which genes drive the emergence of specific lineages and their spatial arrangements. Further, targeted perturbations via inhibition (CRISPRi) or even simultaneous bidirectional activation and repression (CRISPRai) can be introduced to go beyond gene knock-in/out studies (Figure 2C) [6,7]. Precise gene expression manipulations followed by single-cell transcriptomics will be critical to ascertain the determinants of successful early development [12]. These models also allow studies of the crosstalk between the embryonic and extraembryonic lineages, which have been shown to be critical for self-organization but cannot be ascertained when relying on lineage-specific exogenous signaling (Figure 2D) [8,9]. Beyond enabling more faithful replication of how the human embryo forms, CRISPR-programming will allow us to understand embryogenesis by building models that let us control and study this process from the bottom up.

Potential applications of CRISPR-programmed embryo models.

(A) CRISPR activation (CRISPRa)-based programming enables the generation of embryo models across species by activating species-specific regulatory elements near key transcription factors that drive extraembryonic lineage specification. (B) Integration of CRISPR base editors (BEs) allows for the introduction of unique genetic barcodes into individual cells, enabling reconstruction of high-resolution lineage trees and insights into cell fate relationships. (C) Combining orthogonal CRISPRa [e.g., ‘dead’ CRISPR-associated protein 9 (dCas9)-based CRISPRa] and CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) (e.g., dCas12a-based CRISPRi) enables simultaneous activation and repression of multiple genes, which can be used to dissect gene function and uncover epistatic interactions during embryonic development. (D) Lineage-specific targeting of ligand–receptor signaling pathways enables precise manipulation of intercellular communication, providing a versatile approach to decoding signaling principles underlying self-organization. Advantages and limitations of CRISPR-programmed embryo models

Many of the existing SEMs are produced by combining PSCs with differentiated extraembryonic cell types that are either derived from embryos or generated from PSCs by the addition of lineage-specific morphogens to the medium. One short-coming of morphogen-based approaches is the incompatibility of differentiation media required for induction of the hypoblast and trophoblast, which often prevents proper co-development of these lineages into an integrated embryo model. This hinders the establishment of a shared developmental history and coordinated cellular crosstalk during model formation. By contrast, CPEMs enable intrinsic cell fate induction within a simple, common culture condition, allowing embryonic and extraembryonic cells to co-develop in a coordinated manner [9]. Beyond capturing this dynamic cell–cell communication, CPEMs also provide a versatile platform for precise, lineage-specific perturbations that target ligand–receptor pairs to dissect the molecular basis of signaling between embryonic and extraembryonic compartments [8]. Compared with cDNA overexpression, which has also been used to generate SEMs, CRISPRa can readily activate large and complex genes in their native chromatin context in a multiplexable manner, widening the potential transcriptional targets and refinement of cell lineages by affecting multiple genes at once [6]. The ability of CRISPRa to activate specific targets can reduce variability in cellular compositions and limited control over cell fate specification, which are known barriers to enhancing the reproducibility of existing SEM production approaches [5,8].

However, CPEMs come with their own challenges. While CRISPRa-based programming enables tight control over cell fate decisions, it may reduce physiological variability in gene expression by enforcing specific transcriptional programs, thereby limiting the natural heterogeneity that morphogen-based systems are more likely to support. This enforced uniformity could mask emergent behaviors critical for complex tissue patterning [13]. Strategies such as transient activation of lineage markers, sequential activation of cell fates, and optimization of gene expression levels could be incorporated into CRISPRa systems to support emergent heterogeneity through cellular crosstalk after epigenomic activation. Additionally, it remains unclear whether cells in CPEMs are able to erase or reset epigenetic marks deposited by CRISPRa, which could delay or impair the reprogramming of the epigenetic landscape needed to advance to later developmental stages [14]. Consequently, the potential of CPEMs to model advanced stages of development, such as organogenesis, remains untested, and represents an exciting direction for future research. Taken together, CPEMs can complement existing embryo models by offering a platform in which differentiation is intrinsically controlled and perturbations can be precisely applied to reveal the mechanisms of embryonic lineage establishment and self-organization.

The demonstration that CRISPRa can be incorporated to engineer programmable mouse embryo models highlights the potential to integrate the fields of synthetic and developmental biology to enable non-invasive studies of human development. As this field is rapidly progressing, new advancements will help scientists further push the boundaries of what CPEMs can teach us. Ultimately, such findings can be applied back to natural embryos with the goals of both understanding and improving the outcomes of early pregnancy.
