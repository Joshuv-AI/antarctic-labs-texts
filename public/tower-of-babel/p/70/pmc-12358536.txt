Differential effects of 17β-estradiol on knee and temporomandibular joints in ovariectomized rats
Authors: Ji Hye Oh, Jiwon Do, Hoon Joo Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to investigate the therapeutic effects of different concentrations of 17β-estradiol (E2) on knee joints (KJ) and temporomandibular joints (TMJ) in ovariectomized rats, with a focus on bony changes and inflammation. Bilateral ovariectomy was performed to induce estrogen deficiency, and various concentrations of E2 were administered (0, 10, 50, or 100 μg/100 g body weight (BW)) to explore joint responses. Microcomputed tomography and histological and immunohistochemical staining techniques were used to evaluate bony changes and inflammatory markers. We observed significant differences in joint responses between the two joints, with 10 μg/100 g BW of E2 showing promising results in promoting bone recovery in the TMJ with minimal harmful effects on KJ. However, the high E2 concentration of 100 μg led to adverse effects, including bone sclerosis and increased inflammation in both joints. This study suggested that a low dose (10 μg /100 g BW) of E2 may have a therapeutic effect on osteoporotic changes in the TMJ following estrogen deficiency, with minimal harmful effects such as inflammation and side effects on other joints.Electronic supplementary materialThe online version of this article (10.1038/s41598-025-15224-0) contains supplementary material, which is available to authorized users.

Introduction

Progressive condylar resorption (PCR), a type of osteoarthritis, usually occurs in females with preexisting temporomandibular joint (TMJ) dysfunction in adolescent girls and young women. PCR may be caused by dysfunctional articular remodeling as a result of functional overloading or reduced adaptive capacity of the TMJ articular structure, although the etiology is unknown. The disease has an obvious skeletal pattern characterized by decreased condylar head volume, shortened ramal height, and development or worsening of the class II occlusal relationship1–8.

Previous studies reported that female patients with PCR had abnormally low levels of serum 17β-estradiol (E2) at midcycle9,10. In addition, decreased E2 levels have long been associated with bone cell function deterioration and osteoporotic changes in various contexts7,11. On the other hand, reports suggest that E2 exacerbates TMJ inflammation in ovariectomized rats through the NF-κB pathway12, highlighting the need for further investigations into how circulating E2 levels affect the TMJ. Furthermore, what is even more intriguing is that E2, which is associated with the development of PCR and functions as a systemic hormone, is expected to have widespread effects on the body.

However, despite systemic changes in circulating estrogen levels, PCR patients do not exhibit signs or symptoms of osteoarthritis in other joints such as the knee joint (KJ). This clinical observation highlights the unique sensitivity of the TMJ to estrogen and raises compelling questions about joint-specific responses to hormonal changes. Therefore, directly comparing the TMJ with the KJ provides a valuable opportunity to elucidate the differential effects of estrogen on various synovial joints, and to better understand why PCR selectively affects the TMJ.

The significance of unraveling the role of estrogen in joint health extends to both the clinical and research domains. These joint-specific responses may be attributed to several factors, including differences in anatomical structure, mechanical loading patterns, and molecular expression profiles. For instance, variations in the distribution and density of estrogen receptors between the TMJ and other joints such as the KJ may underlie differential sensitivity to circulating estrogen levels. Additionally, the TMJ is subject to unique loading forces due to its role in mastication and speech, which may influence its vulnerability to hormonal fluctuations7. Based on these considerations, we hypothesize that the TMJ’s distinct anatomical and molecular characteristics contribute to its divergent response to estrogen compared to other synovial joints.

At the clinical level, comprehending how estrogen affects joint health is crucial for the development of tailored treatment strategies, especially in patients who experience joint degeneration prematurely. Young women exhibiting TMJ degeneration present a unique challenge, and understanding the role of estrogen in this context may open new avenues for treatment and intervention.

The primary objective of this research was to investigate the effects of various concentrations of E2 on the TMJ and KJ in ovariectomized rats, with a focus on bone changes and inflammation. By conducting comprehensive analyses of these joints, we aimed to elucidate the differences in their responses to estrogen fluctuations and assess the potential benefits and risks associated with E2 therapy. These findings will contribute to a deeper understanding of how estrogen influences musculoskeletal health and pave the way for more personalized and effective treatments in the future.

KJ

In the KJ group (Fig. 2B), OVX primarily reduced the BV/TV and Tb.N (p < 0.002 and p < 0.001, respectively). The administration of various doses of E2 increased the BV/TV and Tb.Th, restoring or exceeding the levels observed in the sham group.

Tibia

In the tibia (Fig. 2C), OVX led to significant reductions in BMD, BT/TV and Tb.N (p < 0.001). The administration of E2 did not affect BMD. Compared with that in the OVX group, the BV/TV significantly increased in the 10 µg group (p < 0.033), but no significant changes were observed in the 50 µg or 100 µg groups compared with the OVX group. Compared with the sham group, all the E2-treated groups presented a significant decrease in the BV/TV (p < 0.001). Tb.N was significantly greater in the 10 µg group than in the OVX group (p < 0.002), and Tb.N was also significantly greater in the 50 µg group than in the OVX group (p < 0.033). However, all the E2-treated groups presented a significant decrease in Tb.N compared with the sham group (p < 0.001). No significant differences in Tb.Th were observed in any of the E2-treated groups compared with either the OVX or sham groups. OVX led to a sparse trabecular pattern with an increased Tb.Sp (p < 0.001), which did not change when E2 was administered.

Histological staining of the articular cartilage of the TMJ and KJ revealed the effects of estrogen deficiency and E2 administration on cartilage health and structure. Toluidine blue (TB) staining indicated that estrogen deficiency led to an imbalance in the cartilage layer, characterized by a significant reduction in the zone of hypertrophic chondrocytes (ZHC), which was reflected in diminished staining intensity. Conversely, E2 administration improved the balance of the cartilage layer and increased ZHC, restoring the staining intensity (Fig. 4A). Hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining demonstrated that estrogen deficiency resulted in larger lacunae, indicating the presence of fewer chondrocytes. However, the E2-treated group presented a reduction in lacuna size, suggesting a more uniform distribution of chondrocytes (Fig. 4B). Discussion

The primary objective of this study was to explore the effects of varying concentrations of E2 on the TMJ and KJ in an estrogen-deficient rat model. Our findings reveal a clear distinction in the responses of these two synovial joints to estrogen administration, underscoring the complexity of the role of estrogen in joint health.

Initially, we observed that a 10 μg dose of E2 significantly promoted bone recovery in the TMJ, resulting in bone parameters close to those observed in the sham group. These results suggest that low doses of estrogen replacement could be beneficial in preventing or reversing bone loss associated with estrogen deficiency in the TMJ. Importantly, the positive impact of the 10 μg dose highlights the potential for low-dose E2 therapy as a therapeutic option for conditions such as PCR, where estrogen deficiency plays a crucial role.

Conversely, the administration of a higher E2 dose (100 μg) presented several concerns. While this dose further increased the BMD in the TMJ, it also led to bone sclerosis and the upregulation of the proinflammatory marker CD86. CD86 is commonly associated with M1 macrophages, which are proinflammatory, whereas markers such as Arg1 are indicative of M2 macrophages, which are generally anti-inflammatory and associated with tissue repair13,14. Under normal conditions, articular cartilage contains few macrophages. However, in inflammatory conditions such as TMJ osteoarthritis, macrophages can become activated and infiltrate the synovium and surrounding tissues of the joint13,15. The increase in CD86 levels suggests that high doses of E2 may inadvertently promote inflammation within the joint, potentially offsetting the benefits of bone recovery13,16–19. An imbalance between M1 and M2 macrophages has been associated with the progression of knee osteoarthritis, suggesting that macrophage polarization may play a crucial role in disease exacerbation14. These findings suggest that while high-dose E2 may enhance bone mineral density, the concurrent upregulation of proinflammatory markers such as CD86 indicates a shift toward M1 macrophage dominance. This proinflammatory environment may interfere with the regenerative processes and ultimately offset the benefits of bone recovery by promoting tissue sclerosis, cartilage degradation, or impaired remodeling. Therefore, the therapeutic window for E2 supplementation must be carefully determined to balance its anabolic effects with the risk of inflammation-induced joint damage.

The KJ, in contrast, demonstrated a remarkable resilience to estrogen fluctuations. Regardless of the E2 dosage administered, the KJ group maintained stable bone density and structural integrity, with an increase in bone thickness compared with those of the sham group. This stability was further reflected in the consistent levels of MMP13 and CD86, which are markers typically associated with joint degradation and inflammation20,21. However, Arg1 was significantly decreased in the OVX group, and despite estrogen supplementation, all the E2-treated groups (10, 50, and 100 μg) presented lower levels of Arg1 than did the sham group, especially the 100 μg group. This finding suggests a possible limitation in counteracting inflammation in the joint22,23. This dose-dependent effect of estrogen on the KJ suggests that while the joint is generally resistant to hormonal changes, excessively high estrogen levels could still negatively influence its inflammatory profile.

These findings emphasize the joint-specific nature of the influence of estrogen on musculoskeletal health23. These results underscore the differential effects of estrogen on the TMJ and KJ. While the TMJ appears more responsive to estrogen fluctuations, benefiting from low-dose supplementation but potentially harmed by higher doses, the KJ remains largely unaffected by moderate doses but may experience adverse effects at higher levels. This differential response likely arises from variations in estrogen receptor expression, cellular sensitivity, or the local tissue microenvironment between the two joints24–26.

The findings from this study also highlight the importance of individualized approaches to estrogen-based therapies. Given the varied responses of the TMJ and KJ to E2 supplementation, personalized treatment plans that consider the specific needs and characteristics of each joint may be essential for optimizing outcomes and minimizing side effects. For exmple, in clinical settings, a low-dose E2 regimen might be preferred for managing TMJ-related conditions, whereas higher doses should be approached with caution because of the risk of inducing inflammation and sclerosis.

Future research should focus on extending the duration of low-dose E2 administration to determine its long-term safety and efficacy, especially in preventing inflammation and tissue sclerosis. Additionally, investigating the effects of E2 in TMJ osteoarthritis models will be critical for understanding the potential therapeutic benefits and risks of estrogen supplementation in conditions involving joint degeneration. Furthermore, molecular biological studies are needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms driving the differential responses of the TMJ and KJ to estrogen. Another limitation of this study is the lack of direct evaluation of estrogen receptor (ERα and ERβ) expression in the TMJ and KJ. Considering that differential receptor distribution may underlie the joint-specific responses to E2, future studies should include receptor detection and localization analyses to clarify the molecular mechanisms driving these divergent effects. Such research could provide deeper insights into how estrogen influences joint health at the molecular level, potentially leading to the development of more targeted and effective therapies.

This study provides important insights into the differential effects of estrogen deficiency on joint health and the various responses of the TMJ and KJ. Using an estrogen-deficient rat model, we demonstrated that low-dose E2 supplementation can effectively promote bone recovery in the TMJ, whereas the KJ remains largely unaffected by E2 fluctuations. These findings highlight the complexity of estrogen responsiveness and the necessity for individualized treatment approaches.

Methods

Animal model

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines for the ethical treatment and safety of laboratory animals as recommended by the Experimental Animal Safety and Ethical Handling Regulations Committee at Seoul National University, School of Dentistry (Approval no. SNU-190731-3-1). The animals were housed in a specific pathogen-Free laboratory, with a controlled environment maintained at a constant temperature of 21 ± 1℃, humidity of 55%, and a 12-h light/dark cycle (light: 07:30–20:00, dark: 20:00–07:30). The rats were provided standard chow (Purina Rodent Chow, Purina Co., Seoul, Republic of Korea) and water ad libitum. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

The study utilized 30 six-week-old female Sprague–Dawley rats (Orientbio Inc., Seongnam, Republic of Korea). This specific age was selected to coincide with the adolescent stage of the rats. An estrogen deficiency model was established through bilateral OVX to assess the impact of E2 deficiency on the TMJ and KJ. The animals were randomly assigned to five groups, each consisting of six rats: Sham, OVX, and OVX with E2 injections at doses of 10, 50, and 100 μg/100 g body weight. All injections were prepared with a normal saline solution as the base. The E2 used for injections was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and was β-estradiol-water soluble (E4389, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA).

Body weight changes were measured before OVX surgery and weekly after surgery. An automatic electronic scale (ARC120, OHAUS Corp., Parsippany, USA) was used for all weight measurements. Measurements were taken one day before drug administration and weekly thereafter to adjust drug dosages according to body weight. Daily intraperitoneal injections of E2 at the specified concentrations were administered in the afternoon for four weeks from beginning at 8 weeks after OVX. Final body weight measurements were taken just before sacrifice. All animals were humanely sacrificed 12 weeks after OVX for subsequent evaluations, including micro-CT scanning and histological examinations.

Ovariectomy

The experimental animals were anesthetized via the intraperitoneal injection of a mixture of ketamine (15 mg/kg, Yuhan Co., Seoul, Republic of Korea) and Rompun (10 mg/kg, Bayer Korea, Seoul, Republic of Korea). Following standard procedures, the abdomen was shaved and disinfected with 10% povidone-iodine (Potadines; Sam-Il Pharm, Seoul, Republic of Korea). Local anesthesia was administered with 2% lidocaine hydrochloride containing 1:100,000 epinephrine (Lidocaine: Huons, Seongnam, Republic of Korea). A 2 mm incision was made on each side of the ventral abdomen, and the ovaries were carefully exteriorized to avoid damage to major organs. The ovaries were ligated with nonabsorbable 5–0 sutures and then excised. The incision was then closed with absorbable 4–0 sutures in a reverse fashion. Post-surgery, all the animals received an intraperitoneal injection of antibiotics (cefazolin, 55 mg/kg; Chong Kun Dang Pharmaceutical Corp., Seoul, Republic of Korea).

Micro-CT analysis

Micro-CT scans were taken for quantitative evaluation of bony changes in the condyles via the SkyScan 1172VR microfocus X-ray system (SkyScanVR, Kontich, Belgium). This system is equipped with a microfocus X-ray tube with a focal spot of 2 μm, producing a cone beam that is detected by a 12-bit, cooled X-ray camera with a charge-coupled device fiberoptically coupled to a 0.5-mm scintillator. The resulting images were 2000 × 2000–pixel square images with an aluminum filter used to produce optimized images. The source voltage and current were set to 100 kV and 100 μA, respectively. The exposure time was 316 ms for optimized clarity. An optimized 3-dimensional (3D) cone beam reconstruction algorithm with a cluster volumetric reconstruction (Feldkamp algorithm) was used. Two-dimensional cut images were subsequently obtained via a computed tomography–analyzing program to construct a 3D model. The scan parameters were 20–100 kV and 10 W with rotation. Reconstruction and analyses were performed via NRecon reconstruction software (SkyScanVR, Kontich, Belgium) and CT Analyzer version 1.18.4.0 (CTAn, Bruker, Belgium), respectively. The volume of interest (VOI) was defined as a 0.35 × 0.35 × 0.35 mm and 1 × 1 × 1 mm cube at the anterior, concentric, and posterior parts just below the articular cartilage of the TMJ and KJ, respectively (Fig. 7A, B). Histochemical and immunohistochemical staining

The mandibles of the rats were isolated and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, followed by decalcification in 500 mM EDTA, pH 8.0 (E2002, BIOSESANG, Seoul, Republic of Korea) for 14 days. The midsagittal plane of the mandibular condyle was aligned parallel to the base during paraffin embedding. Continuous sections of 4 μm thickness were obtained and stained with HE and TB for histological evaluation of cartilage and cartilaginous tissue differentiation. Additionally, IHC staining was performed to detect specific proteins within the tissue. The avidin–biotin complex (ABC) method was used to observe MMP13, which promotes cartilage destruction, CD86, which promotes inflammation in immune responses, and Arg1, which promotes anti-inflammatory responses in immune reactions.

After decalcification, the paraffin-embedded blocks were sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm, dried, and deparaffinized. To enhance antigenicity, the sections were treated with proteinase K (S302030-2, DaKo, Glostrup, Denmark) solution for 20 min, followed by blocking of endogenous peroxidase activity with 0.3% hydrogen peroxide for 20 min. To block nonspecific proteins, 2.5% normal horse serum from the Vectastain ABC elite kit (PK-7200, Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, USA) was applied for 1 h. The primary antibodies used were against MMP13 (ab39012; Abcam, Cambridge, USA; 1:100), CD86 (BS-1035R; BIOSS, Woburn, MA, USA; 1:100), and Arg1 (PA5-29645; Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA; 1:400). After incubation with the primary antibodies, the sections were treated with the Vectastain ABC elite kit, which binds to biotin. Diaminobenzidine (DAB; SK-4100, Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, USA) was used for visual detection of the proteins, and counterstaining was performed via modified Mayer’s hematoxylin (H-3404-100, Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, USA).

After the staining process, the sections were mounted with Permount solution (SP15-100; Fisher Chemicals, Fair Lawn, USA) and observed with a digital pathology scanner (Leica Aperio CS2, Leica Biosystems, Deer Park, USA). Positive staining was identified by the presence of a brown interaction around the cell membrane caused by DAB. The absence of positive cells in the cartilage of the mandible indicated the loss of MMP13, CD86, and Arg1 expression. The same protocol was applied to KJ.

Immunofluorescence staining was performed by preparing slides via the IHC diaminobenzidine method, followed by incubation with primary antibodies against CD86 and Arg1. After incubation, the slides were treated with the secondary antibody Alexa 488 (#4408; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, USA). After the staining process, additional DAPI (D3571; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) staining was performed. Finally, the slides were mounted with Permount solution (SP15-100; Fisher Chemicals, Fair Lawn, USA) and observed via a fluorescence microscope (KI-400F) and a fluorescence device (NFP-1 model).

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed via GraphPad Prism software (version 9.4.1; La Jolla, USA), with the data presented as the mean ± standard errors of the means (SEMs). When significance was observed by ANOVA, post hoc analysis was conducted via the Benjamini, Krieger, and Yekutieli two-step methods to control the false discovery rate (FDR) for multiple group comparisons. All reported P-values were adjusted for FDR via this method. Significance was set at p < 0.033.
