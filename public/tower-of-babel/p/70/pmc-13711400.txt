Ginsenoside CK, a major active metabolite of ginsenosides, ameliorates calcium overload-induced arrhythmias and myocardial oxidative injury by targeting CaMKII
Authors: Sihan Xu, Yixu Wang, Ziyue Zhang, Jin Zhang, Wei Yang, Wenjuan Liu, Min Jiang, Gang Bai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCalcium overload is a central pathological factor in cardiovascular disorders, including arrhythmia and myocardial injury. Recent studies suggest that ginsenoside metabolites may alleviate calcium overload; however, the principal active metabolite and its direct molecular target remain unclear.MethodsThis study systematically screened the active components of enzymatically hydrolyzed total ginsenosides by integrating UPLC-Q/TOF-MS, principal component analysis, and calcium antagonistic activity assays. An alkynylated probe of the key active component was then synthesized for target identification using chemical biology approaches. Molecular biology methods were used to investigate the binding mechanisms. Rat models of arrhythmia and myocardial oxidative injury were further used to evaluate its pharmacological effects in vivo and to examine the associated signaling pathway.ResultsThe study revealed that the calcium antagonistic activity of ginsenosides was closely associated with secondary saponins, particularly ginsenoside CK (G-CK). CaMKII was identified as a key target through which G-CK alleviates calcium overload. G-CK bound to CaMKII and suppressed its autophosphorylation. Molecular biology experiments collectively supported an ATP-competitive mode of CaMKII inhibition by G-CK. Functionally, G-CK reduced intracellular calcium overload, attenuated mitochondrial Ca2+ accumulation and ROS generation, and ameliorated calcium overload-induced arrhythmia and myocardial oxidative injury in vivo.ConclusionThese findings identify G-CK as a major active metabolite of ginsenosides that alleviates calcium overload-associated cardiac injury by targeting CaMKII. This work provides mechanistic insight into the cardiovascular protective effects of ginseng metabolites and suggests that G-CK may serve as a potential lead compound for the development of novel calcium antagonists.

Introduction

Intracellular calcium (Ca2+) acts as a second messenger, and calcium homeostasis is essential for maintaining cardiac electrophysiological stability, contractile function, mitochondrial function, and redox balance [1,2]. In cardiomyocytes, calcium overload can promote aberrant activation of L-type calcium channels (LTCCs) and ryanodine receptors (RyRs), leading to electrophysiological disturbances such as delayed afterdepolarizations [3]. It can also induce mitochondrial calcium overload and excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, thereby causing mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative injury [4,5]. Accordingly, calcium antagonists have substantial therapeutic value in cardiac disorders, including arrhythmia and myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury. Currently used calcium channel blockers are mainly LTCC blockers, such as nifedipine, which effectively inhibit extracellular Ca2+ influx. However, these agents primarily act on membrane ion channels and do not directly address sustained calcium leakage caused by aberrant RyR opening in the sarcoplasmic reticulum. In addition, long-term use may increase arrhythmic risk through reflex sympathetic activation [6]. Therefore, the development of calcium antagonists that act through new targets remains an important unmet need.

Ca2+/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII), a serine/threonine kinase, is a central hub in calcium signaling and plays a pivotal role in calcium overload-induced pathological cascades. Under physiological conditions, CaMKII regulates calcium-handling proteins such as RyRs and LTCCs to maintain excitation-contraction coupling [7]. Under pathological calcium overload, however, sustained elevation of intracellular Ca2+ induces aberrant CaMKII activation and promotes persistent kinase activity [8]. This hyperactivation aggravates sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium leak, disturbs cytosolic calcium oscillation, and further amplifies calcium dysregulation, thereby creating a vicious cycle. Because CaMKII is both a responder to and a driver of calcium dyshomeostasis, it has emerged as an attractive therapeutic target for interrupting this pathological process.

Ginsenosides, a class of triterpenoid saponins, are the major bioactive constituents of ginseng (Panax ginseng Meyer.). Previous studies have shown that ginsenosides exert calcium antagonistic and anti-arrhythmic effects, but the active metabolites responsible for these actions and their molecular mechanisms remain unclear. Notably, protopanaxadiol- and protopanaxatriol-type ginsenosides account for more than 90% of total ginsenosides in raw ginseng, yet they are poorly absorbed in their intact forms. Instead, metabolically transformed rare saponins and aglycones are considered the principal bioactive metabolites responsible for therapeutic efficacy [9]. However, the specific metabolites that mediate these calcium antagonistic effects, and whether they act through CaMKII signaling, remain to be determined.

In this study, we generated total ginsenoside metabolites using a semi-bionic enzymatic hydrolysis method and identified ginsenoside CK (G-CK) as the key bioactive component through principal component analysis (PCA) and calcium antagonistic activity screening. We then developed an alkynylated G-CK probe and combined chemical biology, biochemical, and pharmacological approaches to investigate the mechanism by which G-CK alleviates arrhythmia and myocardial oxidative injury.

Results

Screening identified G-CK as the most active ginsenoside-derived calcium antagonist

To identify the Ca2+ antagonistic components among ginsenoside metabolites, total ginsenosides were subjected to pectinase-mediated enzymatic hydrolysis, followed by UPLC-Q/TOF-MS and PCA to screen for active secondary metabolites (Fig. 1A). No calcium antagonistic activity was detected in the original total ginsenosides, whereas the hydrolyzed products exhibited significant activity at 5 and 50 μg/mL (Fig. 1B). The basic peak ion (BPI) spectra and UV chromatograms showed marked changes in peak shape and retention time before and after enzymatic hydrolysis, indicating substantial compositional alterations (Fig. 1C). With the assistance of SIMCA-P 14.1 software, the PCA score plot and loading plot exhibited clear clustering based on their compositional similarity before and after enzymatic treatment (Fig. 1D). Based on the PCA loading analysis, components with loading values greater than 0.001 were selected as differential candidates. By comparing the molecular fragments and retention times from both primary and secondary mass spectrometry, a total of 41 distinct ginsenosides were identified and the composition analysis is shown in Table S1. Among these candidates, compound No. 35, with a retention time of 17.14 min, showed the greatest contribution to group separation (Fig. 1E) and was identified as G-CK (Fig. 1C). Differential compounds identified before and after enzymatic hydrolysis were further tested for calcium antagonistic activity. G-CK targets CaMKIIγ to regulate calcium antagonism

S1–S5). The Ca2+ antagonistic activity assay confirmed that the chemical modification did not impair the ability of G-CK to inhibit calcium overload (Fig. 2B). ACK was then used to capture interacting proteins in H9c2 cells through click chemistry with azide-functionalized magnetic beads. SDS-PAGE and Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining were performed to detect the target proteins collected by the magnetic ACK beads. As shown in Fig. 2C, the ACK probe-based magnetic capture system successfully enriched target proteins compared to the negative control. A Venn analysis was further performed to compare the captured proteins with calmodulin-related proteins and cardiovascular disease-associated genes (GeneCards Suite, https://www.genecards.org/). This analysis highlighted CaMKII as a candidate target, which was further validated by western blotting (Fig. 2E). Because the ACK probe can react with the BDP fluorophore through click chemistry to generate a stable fluorescent triazole product (Ex/Em: 542/574 nm; Fig. 2F), we further examined the interaction between ACK and cellular CaMKIIγ by confocal co-localization analysis in H9c2 cells. As illustrated in Fig. 2G, specific imaging of the ACK click products was distinctly detected, with no fluorescence signal observed in either the G-CK group or the Con group. A clear merged yellow signal, representing the colocalization of CaMKIIγ protein (pseudo-red) and ACK (pseudo-green), was observed within the cells. This colocalization was significantly attenuated by competition from a 10-fold excess of G-CK in the Com group. Additionally, CETSA was employed to evaluate the thermal stability of CaMKIIγ following G-CK treatment. As shown in Fig. 2H, in the control group, CaMKIIγ began to degrade between 61 and 73°C, and was entirely degraded at 73°C. After G-CK treatment, significant protein bands were still detectable even at 76°C, suggesting that G-CK enhances the thermal stability of CaMKIIγ. Together, these findings support a direct interaction between G-CK and CaMKIIγ in H9c2 cells.

G-CK interferes with ATP binding to CaMKII

Because G-CK directly interacted with cellular CaMKIIγ, we next examined its effects on CaMKII activity and autophosphorylation in cells. As shown in Fig. 3A and B, similar to the CaMKII inhibitor KN-93, G-CK (10 μM) significantly reduced CaMKII kinase activity and CaMKII Thr286 phosphorylation in H9c2 cells. It is well established that CaMKII self-assembles into a dodecameric structure, in which each monomer consists of an ATP catalytic domain (N-terminus), a regulatory region, an autoinhibitory domain, and an association domain (C-terminus). Notably, the ATP-binding region is conserved across different subtypes of CaMKII (Fig. 3C, left panel). Binding of ATP to the CaMKII protein induces self-phosphorylation at threonine 286 (T286) within the autoinhibitory domain, thereby ensuring prolonged and sustained activation of CaMKII [10]. To explore the binding mode between G-CK and CaMKII (PDB ID: 6AYW), molecular docking analysis was performed. The simulation results demonstrated that G-CK stably binds within the ATP-binding pocket of CaMKII with a binding energy of −8.14 kcal/mol. Specifically, G-CK forms hydrogen bonds with residues E97 (distance: 1.8 Å) and N141 (distance: 2.0 Å) (Fig. 3C, right panel). MST analysis showed that the dissociation constant (KD) for G-CK binding to CaMKII was 43 μM, whereas the KD for ATP binding to CaMKII was 22.6 mM (Fig. 3D and E). Notably, co-incubation with G-CK markedly reduced ATP binding to CaMKII in this assay (Fig. 3F). SPR analysis further supported these findings. The KD for G-CK binding to CaMKII was 16 μM, whereas ATP bound CaMKII with a KD of 6.13 mM. After G-CK treatment, ATP binding was no longer detectable under the assay conditions (Fig. 3G–I). G-CK suppresses CaMKII activation and downstream pathological calcium signaling

Activated CaMKII can phosphorylate and modulate the function of various Ca2+ associated proteins, such as LTCCs and RyRs, thereby enhancing Ca2+ influx from the extracellular space and Ca2+ release from the SR/ER, which may lead to intracellular calcium overload. We therefore examined the effects of G-CK on LTCC-associated extracellular Ca2+ influx and RyR-related intracellular Ca2+ mobilization. As shown in Fig. 4A and B, G-CK significantly suppressed the KCl-induced increase in intracellular Ca2+ fluorescence, which was used here to reflect LTCC-associated Ca2+ influx, and attenuated the RyR-related intracellular calcium mobilization response in H9c2 cells. Cardiac contractility is regulated by continuous alterations in [Ca2+]i, and calcium overload in cardiomyocytes is a well-established cause of arrhythmia. Because BaCl2 induces arrhythmia by disrupting ion-channel function and calcium homeostasis in cardiomyocytes, a BaCl2-induced rat arrhythmia model was established to investigate whether G-CK could alleviate arrhythmias. As shown in Fig. 4C, a typical bidirectional ventricular tachycardia occurred immediately after the administration of BaCl2 in rats. This phenomenon was alleviated by KN-93 or G-CK injection. Compared with the model group, the time required for restoration of sinus rhythm was significantly shortened in both the KN-93 group (1.5 mg/kg) and the G-CK groups (3, 1.5, and 0.75 mg/kg) (Fig. 4D), indicating that G-CK can alleviate calcium overload-induced arrhythmias. Meanwhile, we also confirmed that G-CK can significantly inhibit the increase of CaMKII phosphorylation in cardiac tissue under the arrhythmia model in vivo (Fig. 4E). Taken together, these results provide a physiological link between CaMKII inhibition by G-CK and improved arrhythmic outcomes in vivo.

G-CK reduces calcium overload-induced oxidative stress injury in cardiomyocytes by targeting CaMKII

Previous studies have shown that elevated [Ca2+]i levels activate CaMKII, which in turn triggers the mitochondrial calcium overload-oxidative stress-apoptosis axis. We therefore investigated the effects of G-CK on mitochondrial calcium homeostasis, ROS production, and cardiomyocyte apoptosis in the context of CaMKII signaling. As shown in Fig. 5A–C, both G-CK and KN-93, a widely used CaMKII inhibitor, significantly suppressed ACh/CaCl2-induced mitochondrial Ca2+ accumulation and ROS overproduction, while markedly reducing cardiomyocyte apoptosis. Moreover, the myocardial injury biomarkers CK, CK-MB, LDH, and AST were also significantly improved, indicating attenuation of cardiomyocyte injury (Fig. 5D–G). To further determine whether CaMKII mediates the protective effects of G-CK, we performed CaMKII loss-of-function experiments using specific siRNA, as well as ATP competition experiments, in H9c2 cells. CaMKII silencing markedly weakened the ability of G-CK to reduce mitochondrial Ca2+ accumulation and mitochondrial ROS production, supporting a causal role for CaMKII in the protective effects of G-CK (Fig. S6). In addition, G-CK partially reversed ATP-induced CaMKII phosphorylation and similarly attenuated ATP-induced mitochondrial Ca2+ overload and mitochondrial ROS production (Fig. S7). G-CK alleviates calcium overload-associated arrhythmia and myocardial oxidative injury in vivo

We next evaluated the therapeutic effects of G-CK on calcium overload-induced cardiac injury in vivo. H&E staining of cardiac tissue revealed well-organized cardiomyocyte alignment and minimal inflammation in the control group. By contrast, ACh-CaCl2 exposure resulted in significantly disorganized cardiomyocyte arrangement and extensive inflammatory infiltration in atrial tissue. Notably, both G-CK and KN-93 treatments markedly attenuated these pathological alterations (Fig. 6A). ROS staining demonstrated significantly increased cardiac ROS accumulation in model rats, which was reversed by G-CK and KN-93 interventions (Fig. 6B). Compared with the control group, TUNEL staining showed a significant increase in cardiac apoptosis in model rats, whereas treatment with G-CK or KN-93 reduced the number of apoptotic cells (Fig. 6C). Additionally, G-CK and KN-93 interventions significantly decreased serum activities of cardiac injury biomarkers, including CK, CK-MB, LDH, and AST (Fig. 6D–G). Discussion

Calcium overload is a fundamental pathological driver of arrhythmia, oxidative stress, and cardiomyocyte injury in multiple cardiovascular diseases [3]. Previous studies have suggested that ginsenosides can alleviate calcium overload [11,12], but the active metabolites responsible for this effect have remained unclear. In the present study, we found that the calcium antagonistic activity of total ginsenosides was primarily associated with secondary ginsenosides, especially G-CK.

Previous studies on ginsenosides such as Rb1, Rg3, and Re have mainly focused on membrane ion channels, electrophysiological regulation, or general antioxidant pathways. For example, Rb1 has been reported to inhibit L-type calcium channels, whereas Re has been associated with calcium-related electrophysiological modulation [13,14]. In addition, Rg3 and Re have been linked to antioxidant pathways such as Nrf2/HO-1, PI3K/Akt/Nrf2, and inflammasome-related signaling [[15], [16], [17], [18]]. In contrast, our study identifies G-CK as a major active metabolite generated after enzymatic hydrolysis and provides evidence that its cardioprotective effect is associated with direct targeting of CaMKII, a central intracellular kinase that links calcium overload to both arrhythmogenesis and oxidative injury. Thus, the unique contribution of G-CK lies not only in its strong calcium antagonistic activity among the tested ginsenosides, but also in its characterization as a CaMKII-targeting small molecule. This distinction may be pharmacologically important because G-CK appears to modulate an intracellular signaling node integrating LTCC-, RyR-, mitochondrial calcium-, and ROS-related pathways.

In this study, we found that the calcium antagonistic and antioxidant effects of total ginsenosides are closely associated with the secondary ginsenoside G-CK. Our data support that G-CK binds to the ATP-binding region of CaMKII, suppresses CaMKII autophosphorylation, and thereby attenuates downstream LTCC- and RyR-related pathological calcium signaling. Contemporary studies have demonstrated that CaMKII can amplify pathological signaling and promote cellular injury during calcium overload via mechanisms such as autonomous activation, modulation of ion channels, and mitochondrial dysfunction, ultimately leading to arrhythmias and oxidative stress-induced injury in cardiomyocytes [19,20]. These findings suggest a previously underappreciated mechanism by which the secondary ginsenoside G-CK exerts calcium antagonistic and antioxidant effects through CaMKII inhibition, offering new insights into the therapeutic potential of ginsenosides against calcium overload-related cardiovascular pathologies.

CaMKII primarily consists of four domains: the kinase domain, the regulatory domain, the variable domain, and the self-association domain [21]. The kinase domain is responsible for catalytic activity, while the regulatory domain plays a crucial role in activation and autophosphorylation processes. The variable domain influences isoform specificity, and the self-association domain is essential for multimer formation and stability [22]. Existing CaMKII inhibitors can be categorized into three classes based on their mechanisms of action: allosteric inhibitors (e.g., KN-93), peptide-based competitive inhibitors (e.g., AIP), and ATP-competitive inhibitors (e.g., Rimacalib) [23]. However, existing inhibitors have certain limitations that affect their clinical application. For example, studies have confirmed that the allosteric inhibitor KN-93 shows limited inhibitory effect on CaMKII and exhibits certain toxicity in animal models [24]. Although the peptidic competitive inhibitor AIP can mimic the substrate peptide sequence, it suffers from poor cell membrane permeability, easy degradation by proteases, and lack of subtype selectivity [25]. Notably, in the field of small-molecule inhibitors, ATP-competitive inhibitors of CaMKII exhibit unique advantages. Such inhibitors may inhibit both phosphorylated and unphosphorylated CaMKII, which may be associated with the kinase activity mechanism of CaMKII itself [25,26]. As the ATP-binding domain is a key region for CaMKII catalytic activity, targeting this domain may more directly regulate its catalytic efficiency [27]. In principle, targeting the ATP-binding domain may provide a more direct means of modulating catalytic activity, although specificity remains a general challenge for ATP-competitive kinase inhibitors. Our data support that G-CK acts in a manner consistent with ATP-competitive inhibition by interacting with the ATP-binding region in the kinase domain, thereby limiting CaMKII activation. This finding provides new insights for exploring the cardiovascular protective activity of ginsenosides.

Comparative docking of representative ginsenosides, including Rb1, Re, Rg3, and G-CK, showed that all could access the CaMKII ATP-binding pocket, whereas G-CK exhibited the most favorable docking score (G-CK: −8.14 kcal/mol; Rg3: −7.509 kcal/mol; Rb1: −6.079 kcal/mol; Re: −5.158 kcal/mol), indicating a relatively stronger interaction with the kinase (Fig. S8). Notably, G-CK displayed a binding pattern distinct from representative ATP-competitive CaMKII inhibitors (Hesperadin and Rimacalib), involving hydrophobic accommodation of its tetracyclic scaffold together with hydrogen bonding by its sugar hydroxyl groups within the ATP-binding region. These features may favor stable pocket occupancy. Thus, our findings suggest that ATP-binding-site-directed CaMKII modulation by G-CK may represent a distinct and previously underappreciated pharmacological mode of certain ginseng metabolites, rather than a universal property of all ginsenosides.

Conclusion

This study suggests that the calcium antagonistic effects of total ginsenosides are largely attributable to their major active metabolite, G-CK, which targets CaMKII. G-CK acts in an ATP-competitive manner to inhibit CaMKII autophosphorylation and attenuate downstream pathological calcium signaling. These findings identify G-CK as a potential CaMKII-targeting lead compound and provide mechanistic insight for the development of CaMKII-based therapeutic strategies against calcium overload-related cardiovascular diseases.
