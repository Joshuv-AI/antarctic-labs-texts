Incidence and risk factors for fixation failure of femoral neck fractures in adults: a single-center multifactorial analysis
Authors: Shuo Feng, Heng-Heng Yu, Li Wang, JiaChuan Wu, Shang Qiu, Sheng-Li Li, Xiao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo identify risk factors for fixation failure, avascular necrosis (AVN), and nonunion after reduction and internal fixation of femoral neck fractures in adults.MethodsThis single-center retrospective study included 217 adult patients who underwent reduction and internal fixation for femoral neck fractures between January 2012 and December 2023. Demographic variables, comorbidities, smoking status, excessive alcohol consumption, mechanism of injury, fracture type, time interval from injury to operation, fixation method, reduction quality, and implant removal were extracted from medical records. Outcomes (fixation failure, AVN, and nonunion) were ascertained during follow-up. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with each outcome.ResultsAmong 217 patients, fixation failure occurred in 24.0% (n = 52), AVN in 14.3% (n = 31), and nonunion in 9.7% (n = 21). Fixation failure was associated with displaced fracture pattern (Garden III/IV) (OR = 3.314, P = 0.007), poor reduction quality (OR = 2.571, P = 0.036), and excessive alcohol consumption (OR = 3.510, P = 0.010). AVN was associated with excessive alcohol consumption (OR = 5.603, P = 0.002), Garden III/IV fractures (OR = 3.802, P = 0.023), and poor reduction quality (OR = 4.020, P = 0.006). Cannulated screw fixation (OR = 0.255, P = 0.014) and earlier surgery (≤ 1 day: OR = 0.100, P = 0.006; 1–3 days: OR = 0.307, P = 0.028) were associated with lower odds of nonunion.ConclusionIn adult femoral neck fractures treated with internal fixation, fracture displacement, poor reduction quality, and excessive alcohol consumption were independently associated with fixation failure and avascular necrosis. Earlier surgery was associated with reduced odds of nonunion. Cannulated screw fixation was also associated with lower odds of nonunion versus other methods, although residual confounding cannot be excluded. These findings highlight the importance of optimizing reduction quality and addressing modifiable risk factors to improve outcomes.Levels of EvidenceLevel IIIKeywords: Femoral neck fractures, Femoral head necrosis, Internal fixation, Avascular necrosis, Nonunion

Introduction

Femoral neck fractures are a common orthopedic injury in adults and warrant serious clinical attention [1]. They account for approximately 3% of all adult fractures, with an incidence that continues to rise annually due to the aging population and increased life expectancy [2]. In young and middle-aged adults, early reduction and internal fixation remain the preferred treatment to preserve the native hip joint [3]. However, in elderly patients over 65 years old, due to reduced bone density, even low-energy trauma can lead to femoral neck fractures. In such cases, total hip arthroplasty (THA) or hemiarthroplasty is often preferred to improve functional recovery and reduce the risk of secondary surgery [4–6].

Despite advances in surgical techniques and perioperative management, fixation failure—including avascular necrosis (AVN) of the femoral head and nonunion—remains a significant complication, with reported rates ranging from 10% to 30% [7–10].Preventing these complications is essential, and identifying relevant risk factors is crucial for developing effective prevention and treatment strategies.

Previous studies have suggested that age, sex, fracture type, injury mechanism, reduction quality, fixation method, injury-to-surgery interval, and implant removal may be associated with fixation failure [8, 11, 12]. However, the relative contribution of each factor remains debated [10, 12]. Most available evidence comes from large multicenter cohorts in Europe and North America; these studies, while broadly representative, often analyze AVN and nonunion together, may undercapture lifestyle factors (e.g., alcohol consumption and smoking), and can be affected by heterogeneity in diagnostic criteria and treatment strategies [13, 14]. Additionally, many studies are limited by small sample sizes or inconsistent definitions of failure, and certain potentially important risk factors may therefore be underappreciated [14–16].

Therefore, we conducted a single-center retrospective study to analyze the clinical and radiographic data of adult patients who underwent reduction and internal fixation for femoral neck fractures. We aimed to systematically evaluate the potential risk factors for AVN and nonunion, including both clinical and lifestyle variables. We hypothesized that certain patient- and fracture-related characteristics are associated with an increased risk of fixation failure. By identifying these risk factors, we hope to improve patient outcomes by enabling clinicians to recognize high-risk individuals who may benefit from alternative treatment options or closer postoperative monitoring.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University, including patients with femoral neck fractures who underwent internal fixation surgery between January 2012 and December 2023. Eligible patients were identified through the hospital’s electronic medical records system.Inclusion criteria: (1) diagnosis of femoral neck fracture; (2) Patients aged 18-65 years (inclusive); (3) follow-up for more than 1 year. The exclusion criteria for patients were as follows: (1) had an ipsilateral limb fracture or pathological femoral fracture and (2) incomplete follow-up data. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University (Ethics Approval Number: XYFY2024-KL497-01).

Data collection

Demographic and clinical data were extracted from medical records, including age, sex, smoking status, excessive alcohol consumption, mechanism of injury, fracture type, time interval from injury to operation, fixation method, reduction quality, implant removal, and reasons for fixation failure and subsequent treatments.Excessive alcohol consumption was defined as intake exceeding the World Health Organization recommendations (more than 2 units per day for either men or women).Mechanism of injury was classified as low-energy trauma (e.g., fall from standing height, sports injury) or high-energy trauma (e.g., fall from height, traffic accident).Interval from injury to operation was categorized as ≤ 1 day, 1–3 days, or > 3 days.Fracture classification was performed using the Garden system [17, 18], based on anteroposterior radiographs: types I and II were considered nondisplaced fractures, while types III and IV were considered displaced fractures.Preoperative radiological assessments included anteroposterior and lateral radiographs of the proximal femur and three-dimensional spiral CT scans. The initial diagnosis was confirmed by both radiological and clinical findings.The choice of internal fixation method was determined by a multidisciplinary team of at least two senior orthopedic surgeons, taking into account the degree of fracture displacement and stability, patient age, comorbidities, osteoporosis status, pre-injury hip function, and social factors.Follow-up data were collected from outpatient records, telephone interviews, and electronic communication (WeChat), and the last available follow-up for each patient was used for outcome assessment. Because this was a retrospective study, follow-up intervals and imaging were not fully standardized. Routine assessment relied on serial radiographs, and computed tomography (CT) was obtained selectively when healing status was equivocal.

Surgical information

All surgeries were performed under general or combined spinal-epidural anesthesia in a dedicated orthopedic operating room. Surgeries were performed by multiple orthopedic surgeons, including surgeons with over 10 years of experience in trauma and hip surgery, assisted by residents who had received standardized training.Patients were positioned supine on a traction table. Fracture reduction was achieved under C-arm fluoroscopic guidance. For displaced fractures, closed reduction was attempted first; if satisfactory alignment could not be achieved, a limited open reduction via a small incision was performed as needed.Once reduction was confirmed, a 3–5 cm lateral incision was made at the tip of the greater trochanter under fluoroscopic guidance, and the lateral femoral cortex was exposed. The fixation method was selected according to fracture type and surgeon’s experience: Typically, three parallel cannulated screws were inserted in an inverted triangular configuration.For unstable fractures or patients with osteoporosis, screw and plate fixation was used.In rare cases, Kirschner wires were used.During surgery, the quality of reduction and implant position were repeatedly assessed using fluoroscopy to ensure proper placement and to avoid articular surface penetration. After fixation, the wound was irrigated and closed in layers.

Implant product information: Cannulated screws (Sanyou, Youbeite, Anger, Huasen); screws and plates (Tianjin Weiman, Weigao, Dabo); Kirschner wires (Shandong Weigao).

Postoperatively, patients received routine prophylactic antibiotics and thromboprophylaxis. For antibiotic prophylaxis, cefazolin sodium (1 g intravenously every 12 h) was administered for 48 h; in patients with cephalosporin allergy, clindamycin hydrochloride (0.6 g intravenously every 12 h) was administered for 48 h. Thromboprophylaxis consisted of enoxaparin sodium 4,000 IU subcutaneously once daily during hospitalization, followed by rivaroxaban 10 mg orally once daily for 30 days after discharge. Early functional exercises were encouraged. Postoperative radiographs were obtained to evaluate the positioning of the fixation and the quality of fracture reduction.

Outcome assessment and definitions

The reduction quality was determined according to the Garden’s Alignment Index [18]: if the index was less than 155° on the anteroposterior film or greater than 180° on the lateral film, it indicated unsatisfactory reduction. A garden index between 155°and 180° was considered to indicate a satisfactory reduction. Reduction was considered satisfactory only when both criteria were met; if one or both criteria were not met, the reduction was classified as unsatisfactory. Fixation failure was defined as the occurrence of either avascular necrosis (AVN) or nonunion. Reduction quality was independently assessed by two senior orthopaedic surgeons. Interobserver agreement was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa, and disagreements were resolved by consensus.Interobserver agreement for reduction quality was almost perfect (Cohen’s κ = 0.902, SE = 0.043, approximate 95% CI 0.818–0.986; P < 0.001).

Nonunion was defined as the absence of fracture healing at least 9 months after injury, together with a minimum of 3 months without radiographic progression, consistent with the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) criteria [19, 20]. Patients were generally followed in the outpatient clinic with serial radiographs at regular intervals (typically at 6 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months postoperatively) until union or failure was determined. When union status was equivocal on plain radiographs, computed tomography (CT) was obtained to assist adjudication. “Lack of tendency to union” refers to no signs of progress toward healing over 3 consecutive months on serial imaging, together with compatible clinical findings or persistent symptoms to adjudicate union status.

Avascular necrosis (AVN) was diagnosed according to the Ficat classification [9, 21], based on the presence of subchondral sclerosis or segmental collapse on radiographs. MRI was performed if AVN was suspected but not confirmed on plain radiographs.

Implant removal was defined as the surgical removal of any internal fixation device after the initial surgery, regardless of the indication [22]. This information was obtained from follow-up records and surgical notes.All radiological assessments and failure events were independently reviewed and confirmed by two senior orthopedic surgeons.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R (version 4.3.2). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median (IQR) and compared using the independent-samples t test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as n (%) and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Variables with P < 0.10 in univariable analyses and those considered clinically relevant based on prior literature were entered into multivariable binary logistic regression models (enter method) to identify factors associated with fixation failure, avascular necrosis (AVN), and nonunion. Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Post hoc power analyses were performed for the main outcomes. To mitigate potential sparse-data bias and quasi-separation, sensitivity analyses using Firth’s penalized logistic regression were conducted in R, with results reported in Supplementary Tables S1–S3.

Although no formal sample size calculation was performed prior to the study, A post hoc power analysis was conducted to assess the statistical power of the association between reduction quality and fixation failure identified in the multivariate logistic regression analysis. Based on the observed effect size (OR = 2.571, 95% CI: 1.062–6.225, P = 0.036), the sample size of 217 patients (52 fixation failures and 165 successful healings), and a significance level of α = 0.05, the calculated power (1-β) was approximately 84.95%. This indicates that the study had adequate statistical power to detect the association between reduction quality and fixation failure.

Results

Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 232 patients initially underwent surgical treatment for femoral neck fractures. According to the patients’ medical records, 2 patients died from nonsurgical causes, 7 patients were lost to follow-up, and 6 patients were not treated at this institution after surgery. A total of 217 patients(mean age: 46.78 ± 10.85 years; mean follow-up: 7.15 ± 2.13 years) were ultimately included in the analysis, among whom 52 (24.0%) experienced fixation failure, while 165 (76.0%) achieved successful healing, as shown in Fig. 1.

Patient demographics and univariate analysis

Among comorbidities, hypertension (P = 0.026), diabetes mellitus (P = 0.016), corticosteroid use (P = 0.038), and renal insufficiency (P = 0.003) were all significantly associated with an increased risk of fixation failure. In contrast, cardiovascular disease (P = 0.089) and respiratory disease (P = 0.749) were not significantly associated with fixation failure.(Table 2).

Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified several factors independently associated with fixation failure (Table 3). Displaced fracture pattern (Garden III/IV) (OR = 3.314, 95% CI 1.389–7.905, P = 0.007), poor reduction quality (OR = 2.571, 95% CI 1.062–6.225, P = 0.036), and excessive alcohol consumption (OR = 3.510, 95% CI 1.343–9.170, P = 0.010) were significantly associated with increased odds of fixation failure. Implant removal (OR = 0.539, 95% CI 0.259–1.122, P = 0.099) and cardiovascular disease (OR = 6.993, 95% CI 0.845–57.901, P = 0.071) did not reach statistical significance. Renal insufficiency was a sparse covariate and showed quasi-separation in the conventional model, resulting in an unstable (inflated) estimate. Sensitivity analyses using Firth’s penalized logistic regression yielded finite estimates and supported the same main inferences, with more conservative estimates for sparse covariates (Supplementary Table S1).

Univariate analysis revealed that age, fixation methods, and interval from injury to operation were significantly associated with the occurrence of nonunion (P < 0.05). Specifically, older age, delayed surgery, and non-hollow screw fixation were related to an increased risk of nonunion. Other factors, including sex, excessive drinking, smoking, mechanism of injury, type of fracture, quality of reduction, implant removal, hypertension, cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, steroid use, respiratory diseases, and renal insufficiency, showed no significant association with nonunion (P > 0.05).

Multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that fixation method and the interval from injury to operation were independently associated with nonunion (Table 5). Compared with other fixation methods, cannulated screw fixation was associated with lower odds of nonunion (OR = 0.255, 95% CI 0.086–0.757, P = 0.014). Undergoing surgery within ≤ 1 day or 1–3 days after injury was associated with lower odds of nonunion compared with surgery performed > 3 days after injury (OR = 0.100, 95% CI 0.019–0.512, P = 0.006; and OR = 0.307, 95% CI 0.107–0.883, P = 0.028, respectively). In sensitivity analyses using Firth’s penalized logistic regression, cannulated screw fixation remained associated with lower odds of nonunion (OR = 0.274, 95% CI 0.102–0.737, P = 0.0109; Supplementary Table S3). Discussion

Femoral neck fractures in adults remain a major clinical challenge due to the high risk of fixation failure, particularly nonunion and avascular necrosis (AVN), which are among the most serious complications after internal fixation [10]. The unique anatomical and vascular characteristics of the femoral neck predispose patients to these adverse outcomes [14]. Our study found a fixation failure rate of 24.0%, with AVN (14.3%) occurring more frequently than nonunion (9.7%). This highlights the need to identify and address key risk factors.

Fracture Displacement and Reduction Quality

Displaced fractures (Garden III/IV) were associated with a markedly increased risk of fixation failure (OR = 3.314, P = 0.007) and AVN (OR = 3.802, P = 0.023). Poor reduction quality was also an independent risk factor for both fixation failure (OR = 2.571, P = 0.036) and AVN (OR = 4.020, P = 0.006). These results underscore the critical importance of accurate fracture classification and achieving high-quality reduction during surgery. Our findings support previous reports that unsatisfactory reduction and severe displacement substantially increase complication rates [7, 10, 14].In addition, the study by Jiang et al. [23] indicated that in patients aged 60 years or younger, a contralateral neck-shaft angle (NSA) of less than 130° further increases the risks of nonunion, avascular necrosis, functional failure, and reoperation. Honkanen et al. [24] also reported that the overall reoperation rate after internal fixation for femoral neck fractures was as high as 25%, with 16% of patients requiring conversion to arthroplasty. Therefore, in clinical practice, thorough preoperative assessment of the contralateral anatomical structure and striving for anatomical reduction intraoperatively, as well as continuous optimization of surgical techniques, are essential. These measures help to minimize the risk of fixation failure and complications, thereby improving long-term outcomes for patients.

Timing of surgery and choice of fixation method

Early surgical intervention was found to significantly reduce the risk of nonunion. Specifically, fixation with cannulated screws (OR = 0.255, P = 0.014) and surgery performed within 1 day (OR = 0.100, P = 0.006) or 1–3 days (OR = 0.307, P = 0.028) after injury were protective factors. These findings are consistent with recent literature [5, 22, 25], which suggests that early surgery is particularly important for reducing nonunion risk, while its effect on AVN and overall fixation failure remains less conclusive. Wagner et al. [25]analyzed the timing of surgery in young patients and found that performing surgery within 8–12 h after injury can reduce the risk of necrosis. Furthermore, they identified 72 h as the upper limit for surgical timing in relevant guidelines.In our study, we included patients who underwent surgery more than 72 h after injury for several reasons. First, our aim was to reflect real-world clinical practice in a single-center setting, where various factors—such as late presentation, referral delays, preoperative medical optimization, or patient/family decision-making can result in unavoidable surgical delays.However, timely surgical intervention should be prioritized, especially for high-risk patients.

The choice of internal fixation method is an important consideration in adult femoral neck fractures. In our cohort, cannulated screw fixation was associated with lower odds of fixation failure nonunion compared with other fixation methods (OR = 0.255, 95% CI: 0.086–0.757, P = 0.014). However, this observational association is likely confounded by fracture pattern/stability and surgeon preference in this retrospective study; therefore, a causal effect of cannulated screws on nonunion (or fixation failure) cannot be inferred. In sensitivity analyses using Firth’s penalized logistic regression, the direction of association was similar but estimates were less precise, highlighting uncertainty in the magnitude of effect. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, and the choice of fixation strategy should remain individualized based on fracture configuration, bone quality, and patient factors.

Alcohol consumption as a risk factor and other factors

Our results demonstrate that excessive alcohol consumption is a significant independent risk factor for fixation failure (OR = 3.510, P = 0.010) and AVN (OR = 5.603, P = 0.002). This finding is in line with previous studies [25], Mitsuko et al. [26] reported that habitual alcohol consumption was associated with a 7.8-fold increased risk of AVN, with a clear dose-response relationship. Mechanistically, alcohol negatively affects bone healing and vascularity [27], emphasizing the importance of screening for and addressing alcohol use in patients undergoing internal fixation for femoral neck fractures.

While comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, corticosteroid use, and renal insufficiency showed associations with fixation failure in univariate analysis, only fracture displacement, reduction quality, and alcohol consumption remained significant in multivariate analysis. Although smoking is a well-established risk factor for AVN due to its detrimental effects on microvascular circulation and bone healing [28], our study did not find a statistically significant association between smoking and fixation failure. One possible explanation is the relatively small number of smokers or the limited overall sample size in our cohort. In addition, as a retrospective study, information on lifestyle factors such as smoking and alcohol consumption was mainly obtained from medical records and patient self-reports, which may be subject to recall bias or underreporting. These limitations could affect the accuracy of risk factor assessment. Nonetheless, the negative impact of smoking on bone healing and vascularization cannot be overlooked [29, 30]. Future studies with larger sample sizes and more detailed stratification of smoking exposure, as well as more objective assessment methods for lifestyle factors, are warranted to further clarify this relationship.

The diagnosis of avascular necrosis (AVN) and nonunion was primarily based on plain radiographs and clinical assessment, with MRI only performed in cases with suspected but inconclusive findings. However, MRI is known to be much more sensitive than plain radiographs, especially for detecting early-stage AVN and subtle nonunion, which may not be visible on X-rays. The absence of routine MRI in our study could have led to an underestimation of the true incidence of AVN and nonunion, as some early or asymptomatic cases might have been missed. This limitation may have affected the accuracy of our outcome assessment and the strength of the associations identified between risk factors and fixation failure. In addition, because CT was obtained selectively (rather than uniformly) when healing was equivocal, some cases may have been misclassified, which would tend to bias associations toward the null.

Clinical implications and decision-making

Our study underscores the importance of comprehensive preoperative assessment, optimization of reduction quality, early surgical intervention, and individualized management for patients with excessive alcohol consumption. For young and middle-aged patients, hip-preserving procedures such as internal fixation are generally preferred to retain the native joint and maximize long-term function. However, for patients with high-risk factors—such as Garden type III/IV fractures, poor reduction quality, excessive alcohol consumption, or advanced age-the risks of nonunion and femoral head necrosis are significantly increased. Our findings also indicate that patients aged 51–65 years have a higher risk of nonunion, suggesting that age should be considered an important factor when selecting the optimal treatment strategy. In our cohort, older age showed an association with nonunion in univariate analysis; however, age group was not statistically significant in the multivariable model (Table 5). For these patients, early surgery, anatomical reduction, and reliable fixation are essential, and patients should be advised to modify unhealthy lifestyles.

As reported by Lagergren et al. [31], for patients aged 60–69 years, if high-quality fracture reduction can be achieved, life expectancy is relatively limited, or the patient wishes to preserve the native joint, internal fixation remains a reasonable option, but the approximately 30% risk of secondary surgery should be clearly communicated. Based on previous literature and our findings [6, 14, 15], for patients around 60 years of age in whom satisfactory reduction cannot be achieved intraoperatively or who present with multiple high-risk factors, early consideration of hip arthroplasty is recommended to reduce the risk of reoperation and improve long-term outcomes. Shared decision-making between clinicians and patients is crucial. Surgeons should thoroughly explain the risks of internal fixation failure, the likelihood of secondary surgery, and the expected recovery and functional outcomes of different procedures. Ultimately, individualized treatment plans should be developed based on patient age, comorbidities, lifestyle, fracture type, and personal preferences, in order to minimize complications and optimize outcomes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective single-center design may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability. Follow-up intervals and imaging were not fully standardized, and CT was obtained selectively in equivocal cases, which may have introduced outcome misclassification. Second, the lack of routine MRI evaluation may have led to underdiagnosis of early AVN and subtle nonunion, potentially underestimating fixation failure rates. Third, reliance on self-reported lifestyle data may introduce recall bias. Fourth, our findings may not be generalizable beyond an exclusively Asian patient population. Fifth, bone mineral density (DXA) and other standardized measures of osteoporosis were not routinely available, and osteoporosis status was derived from medical records, which may result in residual confounding. Although reduction quality was assessed by two surgeons with high interobserver agreement (κ = 0.902,), we did not assess intraobserver reliability and could not fully account for surgeon-level or implant-specific variability. Finally, some comorbidities were rare (e.g., renal insufficiency) and concentrated in one outcome group, leading to imprecise estimates; we therefore performed Firth’s penalized logistic regression as sensitivity analyses.

Conclusion

Avascular necrosis (AVN) and nonunion are the most frequent complications following internal fixation of femoral neck fractures. Among adult patients, fracture displacement, suboptimal reduction quality, and excessive alcohol consumption were identified as factors independently associated with fixation failure and the development of AVN. Early surgical intervention was associated with lower odds of nonunion. Cannulated screw fixation was also associated with lower odds of nonunion compared with other fixation methods, although residual confounding cannot be excluded. These findings underscore the importance of optimizing reduction quality and actively managing modifiable risk factors to improve clinical outcomes in this patient population.
