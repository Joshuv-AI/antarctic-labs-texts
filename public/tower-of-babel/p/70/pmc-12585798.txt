Impact of Community‐Based Clinical Clerkship on Medical Students' Generalism‐Related Competences: A Mixed‐Methods Study
Authors: Kiyoshi Shikino, Naoto Ozaki, Nobuyuki Araki, Kazuyo Yamauchi, Hiroshi Tajima, Hajime Kasai, Ikuo Shimizu, Shoichi Ito
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundJapan's 2022 Model Core Curriculum (MCC) recommends community‐based clinical clerkships spanning at least three consecutive weeks to foster generalism competency, although empirical evidence supporting this guidance remains limited.MethodsWe used an explanatory sequential mixed‐methods design. Fifth‐year Chiba University medical students (n = 112) completed a three‐week community‐based clinical clerkship from September to November 2024. Self‐assessed attainment of the 18 MCC generalism learning objectives was measured pre‐ and post‐clerkship. Daily reflection sheets (n = 1680) were deductively coded to analyze the learning processes using content analysis.ResultsMean self‐assessment scores increased significantly for all the objectives (p < 0.001), with effect sizes ranging from small (d = 0.37) to very large (d = 1.40). Content analysis of the reflection sheets yielded 539 reflection codes across 16 objectives, with all the students contributing. The most frequently coded objectives were “primary care at home” (106 codes from 58 students), “geriatric stage” (79 codes from 44 students), “primary care in the community” (64 codes from 42 students), and “end‐of‐life care” (62 codes from 43 students).ConclusionsA three‐week community‐based clinical clerkship produced significant self‐assessed gains across all 18 generalism learning objectives and generated 539 reflection‐based learning units, including 16 objectives, confirming that students actively engaged with the full breadth of generalism in practice. These findings should be interpreted as indicating perceived growth rather than objective competence, given the reliance on self‐assessments, and exposure opportunities varied across clerkship sites. Further multi‐site studies are needed to clarify whether 3 weeks is the optimal duration.

Background

Healthcare systems worldwide are increasingly challenged by an aging population and the growing prevalence of multimorbidity, prompting a gradual shift from organ‐based, hospital‐centered care toward more community‐anchored, comprehensive models [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. International evidence highlights the importance of integrated, people‐centered primary health care, showing how strengthening primary health care systems improves primary care health outcomes and equity across health systems. Therefore, international reports and studies highlight generalism as an essential outcome for graduates, underlining the ability to integrate biomedical, psychosocial, and contextual factors across the life course in both global health practice and medical education [6, 7, 8]. Japan represents an illustrative context for demographic and workforce challenges. By 2035, more than one in three citizens will be aged ≥ 65 years, placing the country on course to become the world's first “super‐aged” society [9]. Concomitantly, a persistent urban–rural maldistribution of physicians limits access to primary and community care in underserved regions, even in the international context [10, 11, 12, 13]. These trends have been accompanied by policy reforms and educational initiatives in Japan, such as the 2022 revision of the Model Core Curriculum (MCC), which encourages a gradual shift from organ‐based, hospital‐centered services to comprehensive, community‐anchored models while highlighting the need to prepare graduates who can practice effectively in community settings [12, 14, 15]; this agenda resonates with the community‐based, integrated, and systematic tenets of the SPICES (Student‐centered, Problem‐based, Integrated, Community‐based, Elective, and Systematic) curriculum model [16].

In response, the Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science, and Technology revised the Model Core Curriculum (MCC) in 2022. The MCC introduced “generalism” (GE) as an essential competency, defined as “viewing patients comprehensively as persons living their lives within families and communities” [17, 18]. The MCC operationalizes GE into 18 third‐tier learning objectives across four perspectives: holistic (6 learning objectives), community (4 learning objectives), life‐course (6 learning objectives), and sociocultural (2 learning objectives) (File S1). To ensure that students achieve these objectives through experience, the MCC encourages participatory clinical clerkships spanning at least three consecutive weeks. Furthermore, it allows students to augment university‐based clinical clerkships with placements at external community teaching sites.

However, despite this policy shift, empirical evidence on the educational impact of MCC‐compliant community placements remains limited [14]. Previous Japanese studies on community‐based medical education largely focused on short immersions (≤ 1 week) and reported attitudinal outcomes rather than competence attainment [19, 20]. Moreover, prior evaluations seldom mapped learning achievements to the MCC framework, hindering schools from gauging achievement of program objectives. Internationally, research on longitudinal integrated clinical clerkships has demonstrated that extended single‐site rotations foster sustained relationships with patients and interprofessional teams, deepen learners' understanding of community care processes, and promote holistic, patient‐centered practice [21, 22]. These findings suggest that community‐based placements may be well suited for achieving Japan's GE objectives.

To address these gaps, we implemented a three‐week community‐based clinical clerkship. Fifth‐year medical students were placed at a single community facility—a primary care clinic, community hospital, or home‐visit clinic—providing immersion in community‐embedded care, with opportunities for students to observe aspects of patient care continuity. Methods

Study Design

We adopted a pragmatist paradigm approach for this study, emphasizing practical solutions and actionable insights. In mixed‐methods research, pragmatism is a well‐established philosophical foundation that emphasizes practical solutions and the integration of quantitative and qualitative data to generate actionable insights into complex, real‐world phenomena [23, 24, 25, 26]. This approach was particularly appropriate for our research question, which required both measurement of competence gains and exploration of students' reflections. Specifically, we used an explanatory sequential mixed‐methods design [27, 28, 29]. We administered pre‐ and post‐quantitative surveys to fifth‐year medical students in the clinical clerkship cohort. Qualitative data, in the form of daily reflection papers written during community‐based clinical clerkships, were analyzed using deductive content analysis to explain and enrich the quantitative findings [30].

Setting and Participants

The study was conducted at Chiba University School of Medicine from September to November 2024 with 112 fifth‐year students. These students had completed a year of core clinical rotations and participated in a community‐based clinical clerkship. Before their clinical clerkships, all students had received generalism‐related education through lectures and small‐group learning sessions, based on the MCC competencies. This approach ensured that the community‐based clerkship was conducted on top of a common educational foundation. All the participants provided written informed consent.

The 112 fifth‐year medical students were assigned to a community facility, either a primary care clinic (outpatient care with home visits), a community hospital (< 200 beds), or a home‐visit clinic/visiting nurse station, based on preferences and needs assessment. Their clinical activities included outpatient consultations, home‐visit medicine administration, convalescent ward care, secondary emergency coverage, and collaborative sessions at visiting nurse stations and pediatric clinics. Although the scale and case mix of these facilities varied, all clerkships were structured in accordance with the aims defined by the MCC. Supervising faculty members participated in faculty development sessions to ensure consistent understanding and provision of these aims. Each student remained at their assigned site for 3 weeks, managing patients through history records, physical examination, electronic charting, case presentations, and multidisciplinary conferences. Supervising physicians provided direct, formative feedback.

Quantitative Data Collection: Competences and Learning Objectives Related to GE

A self‐assessment inventory, aligned with the MCC's competences—“holistic,” “community,” “life,” and “social perspectives and approaches”—was used [17, 18]. The inventory includes 18 third‐tier learning objectives (e.g., a comprehensive biopsychosocial perspective and home‐based primary care) (File S1). For each objective, students rated their perceived attainment on a five‐point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). The students completed the inventory before and after the community‐based clinical clerkship.

Statistical Analysis

Paired‐samples t‐tests were used to compare pre‐ and post‐scores for each GE learning objective. Effect sizes (Cohen's d for paired samples) were specified a priori and interpreted according to conventional thresholds (small = 0.20, medium = 0.50, large = 0.80) [31]. All the analyses were performed with IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY).

Qualitative Data Collection: Daily Reflections

The students submitted an electronic reflection sheet every weekday detailing what they had learned, what they struggled with, and what they wanted to learn [32]. A total of 1680 reflections (112 students × 15 days) were collected.

Qualitative Analysis

The reflection statements were deductively coded to the 18 third‐tier GE objectives in the MCC using content analysis [30]. One medical education faculty member (N.O.) performed the primary coding, while another reviewed the dataset as a supervisory second rater (K.S.). Discrepancies were resolved through discussion. Both coders were experienced medical educators with clinical and educational backgrounds in general medicine and medical education research, which enabled them to interpret students' reflections with an awareness of their own perspectives. Codes were pooled across the three‐week clinical clerkship; for each GE objective, we calculated the number of unique students (out of 112) whose reflections contained that code, analyzing the coverage of GE‐related learning objectives across the cohort via deductive content analysis. The transcripts underwent deductive content analysis.

Integration of Quantitative and Qualitative Strands

Using an explanatory sequential mixed‐methods framework, we analyzed pre‐ and post‐educational intervention quantitative gains for each learning objective. The results were triangulated with the number of students whose reflections referenced the same objective. Objectives that exhibited substantial quantitative improvement and high qualitative salience were deemed to be strongly achieved. This integrated analysis clarified the nature and magnitude of how the three‐week community‐based clinical clerkship advanced MCC‐aligned GE learning objectives, addressing the evidence gap.

Ethical Considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Chiba University Ethics Committee (3425). Written informed consent was obtained from all the participants. All the data were anonymized before the analysis and reported in aggregate to maintain confidentiality.

Deductive Content Analysis of Daily Self‐Reflections

In their reflection sheets, several students described experiences that suggested elements of patient follow‐up and continuity of care. For example, some reported accompanying home visits that extended from initiation through end‐of‐life care, while others observed patient trajectories from acute hospital admission to subsequent transfer into community‐based integrated care. These reflections indicate that, even within a three‐week placement, students were exposed to meaningful aspects of continuity. From the 1680 reflection sheets, we coded 539 meaning units, contributed by the 112 participants, covering 16 MCC‐GE objectives (Figure 1 and File S2). Codes were clustered around domains that mirrored the case‐mix witnessed at the community sites. The most frequent was primary care at home (106 codes from 58 students, 51.8%), followed by geriatric stage (79 codes, 44 students, 39.3%), primary care in the community (64 codes, 42 students, 37.5%), and end‐of‐life care (62 codes, 43 students, 38.4%).

The result of content analysis using students' daily self‐reflection sheets.

Early reflections focused mainly on transdisciplinary care (44 codes, 31 students, 27.7%) and evidence‐based medicine (15 codes, 3 students, 2.7%). Weekly tallies (File S2) confirmed that these themes dominated Week 1, whereas community/home care, geriatric management, and palliative issues arose progressively in Weeks 2 and 3. However, pediatric life‐stage objectives—fetal, neonatal, and infant stages and school‐age, adolescence, young adulthood, and adulthood—received no codes, underscoring the limited exposure to younger patients at these sites.

Synthesis of Quantitative and Qualitative Strands

Learning objectives, such as geriatric stage and primary care in the community, exhibited large effect sizes and high‐frequency reflection codes. However, basic concepts in primary care and a comprehensive biopsychosocial perspective produced the greatest quantitative gains. Nonetheless, these were mentioned only a moderate number of times in the reflection sheets, indicating that medical students improved markedly on these items even though they referred to them less often. Conversely, primary care at home, although exhibiting a slightly smaller effect size, generated the highest number of reflection codes. Objectives with minimal qualitative salience, such as health in sociocultural contexts, demonstrated moderate score improvements.

Discussion

A three‐week immersion yielded significant self‐assessed gains across all 18 GE objectives and strengthened rural orientation. Self‐achievement ratings improved for all 18 third‐tier GE objectives, with large effect sizes, particularly in the comprehensive biopsychosocial perspective and context‐responsive primary care. Deductive coding of 1680 daily reflections mapped 539 meaning units across all GE objectives. Primary care at home, geriatric stage, primary care in the community, and end‐of‐life care were mentioned by 58 students (51.8%), 44 students (39.3%), 42 students (37.5%), and 43 students (38.4%), respectively, indicating that rich, practice‐based experiences were captured in these domains. Together, the integrated quantitative and qualitative findings suggested that a community‐based clinical clerkship enhanced students' self‐perceived attainment of MCC‐aligned GE competencies, while acknowledging that these findings do not represent objective confirmation of competence. These findings should be interpreted as indicating perceived growth rather than as objective confirmation of competence.

Interpretation of Quantitative‐Qualitative Convergence

Our findings underscored that a longitudinal, continuous placement is critical in community medicine, where opportunities to observe patient trajectories and team dynamics unfold over time [33, 34, 35]. Reflection codes shifted predictably—during Week 1, students focused on organ‐transcending reasoning and evidence‐based medicine, while Weeks 2–3 emphasized community/home care and geriatric/end‐of‐life issues (File S2). Although a three‐week clerkship does not constitute a long‐term longitudinal placement in the international sense, our findings suggest that students were nonetheless able to engage with elements of continuity of care. Reflection entries describing home visits extending to end‐of‐life care, or transitions from acute to community‐based care, illustrate how continuity can be experienced even within a limited timeframe. These results provide empirical support for the MCC's recommendation that a minimum of three consecutive weeks can offer sufficient opportunities for experiential learning in community settings. This pattern may suggest that students' focus broadened as their exposure to community care accumulated, which is consistent with the spiral‐curriculum theory [36, 37]. However, because content analysis only captures the frequency of predefined categories, these changes should be interpreted cautiously and do not provide direct evidence of increased confidence or deeper thinking [38].

Additionally, supervisors' feedback and case assignments may have influenced the timing of these reflections, but further research with complementary qualitative methods is required to confirm such mechanisms. The strong correlation between high‐frequency codes and large effect sizes (> 1.3) highlights the power of repeated, context‐rich practice [39]. Conversely, objectives linked to social perspectives and approaches were mentioned infrequently yet showed moderate gains, suggesting that salient single exposures (e.g., joint meetings with social‐service agencies) yield disproportionately high learning outcomes, which is consistent with transformative‐learning frameworks [40, 41]. Similarly, some objectives, such as fetal, neonatal, and infant stages, were rarely encountered at many clerkship sites. Although self‐assessment scores in these domains increased, these gains may reflect limited or indirect exposure rather than consistent experiential learning. In particular, pediatric care illustrates this point: while many students did not encounter children during their placements, some community clinics provided opportunities to engage in pediatric consultations. For those students, these experiences may have contributed to substantial increases in self‐assessment, thereby raising the overall mean scores despite the absence of corresponding reflections. This indicates that improvements in self‐perceived competence should be interpreted with caution for domains that are less frequently represented in community practice and highlights the importance of examining site‐specific opportunities in future studies.

Earlier Japanese studies revealed that short community rotations (≤ 1 week) produced attitudinal change [42, 43]. Consistent with these studies, our data also demonstrated improvements in self‐assessment and further showed that a continuous three‐week placement yielded large effect sizes across all 18 GE learning objectives, suggesting that a longer immersion may provide broader perceived gains. The most pronounced gains occurred in continuity‐sensitive domains, such as primary care at home and geriatric stage, suggesting that a three‐week placement can provide meaningful exposure in the Japanese educational context. The three‐week duration in this study was determined in accordance with the 2022 revision of the MCC, which recommends at least three consecutive weeks of community‐based clerkship. Our findings are consistent with this policy and indicate that such a duration may provide valuable opportunities for continuity of learning. However, these results alone do not justify generalizing 3 weeks as the definitive minimum, and further multi‐site research is needed to clarify the optimal or minimum duration for achieving GE competencies. International experience supports this finding. Medical schools in the United States, Europe, and Australia routinely devote multi‐week blocks to primary‐care or community‐based clinical clerkships; some programs adopt longitudinal integrated clerkships that extend for several months [44, 45, 46]. Longer placements allow students to observe patients over time and experience multiple care settings.

Objective‐level mapping in our study revealed variability in students' learning trajectories, mirroring international longitudinal integrated clinical clerkships [21, 22]. The comprehensive biopsychosocial perspective exhibited large gains in self‐rating, although it appeared only moderately in reflective entries. This discrepancy suggests that students may face challenges in expressing or internalizing abstract competencies, and that additional scaffolding could be valuable to support their articulation and deeper understanding. Conversely, highly practical domains, such as primary care at home, generated the greatest number of reflection codes yet yielded slightly lower GE‐related self‐assessments, indicating that extensive hands‐on exposure does not automatically translate into a sense of professional mastery [39].

These findings indicate that guided reflection, learning portfolios, and structured debriefings are necessary to help students verbalize and internalize abstract competencies [47], whereas longitudinal practice accompanied by targeted feedback and mentorship is essential for consolidating skill‐intensive domains [48, 49]. Therefore, integrating these complementary approaches into community‐based clinical clerkships can optimize the acquisition of the MCC's GE competency.

Educational Implications

The consistently large effect sizes across GE domains endorse the MCC requirement of at least three consecutive weeks and favor a single‐site model for sustained patient follow‐up. Moreover, the relatively low reflection frequencies for pediatric life stages and social‐perspective objectives identify specific areas for curricular enhancement, such as structured pediatric home visits and public health fieldwork, to achieve full GE coverage.

Limitations

Several considerations may influence the generalizability and interpretability of our findings. The study was conducted at a single medical school, where institutional culture, community resources, and supervisory styles may differ from other settings. Thus, replication across multiple sites is necessary before nationwide implementation. Second, although all clerkships were conducted in accordance with the learning objectives defined by the medical school, students were placed in diverse facilities with varying case mixes and emphases, which may have introduced heterogeneity in their learning experiences. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as overall cohort trends rather than as uniform outcomes across all sites. Furthermore, some domains (e.g., fetal, neonatal, and infant stages) were rarely encountered at many facilities. Increases in self‐assessment scores in such domains may reflect limited or indirect exposure, and thus these results should be interpreted with caution. In particular, for pediatric care, opportunities were unevenly distributed: while many students did not encounter children, some community clinics provided pediatric consultations, which may have contributed to substantial score increases for those students and raised the overall mean. This uneven distribution indicates that improvements in these domains should be interpreted with caution. Future studies should analyze facility‐specific differences in greater depth. Third, all GE data relied on students' self‐assessments. While daily reflection sheets, compiled in each student's portfolio, provide rich contemporaneous learning evidence, they do not constitute an external appraisal. Objective measures, such as workplace‐based assessment, GE‐focused OSCE station, and supervisor rating, are needed to corroborate self‐reported gains. This study captured only short‐term outcomes and did not assess competence retention or eventual career trajectories. Multicenter trials, incorporating objective assessments and longitudinal follow‐ups, are required to establish the long‐term impact of community clerkships on learning and workforce distribution.

Conclusion

A three‐week, single‐site community‐based clinical clerkship produced significant self‐assessed gains across all 18 GE learning objectives and generated 539 reflection‐based learning units, spanning 16 objectives. This confirmed that the students actively engaged with the full breadth of GE in practice. These results demonstrate that a focused three‐week immersion in community care can meaningfully foster holistic, patient‐centered clinical thinking.
