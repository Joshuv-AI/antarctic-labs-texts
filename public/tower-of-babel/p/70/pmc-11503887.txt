Association between Arsenic Methylation in Early Pregnancy and Gestational Diabetes Mellitus: A Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Min Jiang, Xiujuan Ma, Yin Zheng, Zhengmei Yang, Juanjuan Guo, Chengzhi Huang, Qingqing Liu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
The metabolism of arsenic (As) plays a crucial role in its health effects. However, the impact of arsenic methylation during early pregnancy on gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the associations between As methylation in the first and second trimesters and the incidence of GDM by conducting a prospective cohort study in Chongqing, China. Urine samples from first (n = 131) and second (n = 53) trimester pregnant women were analyzed for arsenite (AsIII), arsenate (AsV), monomethylarsonic acid (MMA), and dimethylarsinic acid (DMA) concentrations. Logistic regressions assessed associations between the concentrations of As species, methylation indices, and GDM risk. As species concentrations showed no significant differences between GDM and non-GDM groups. Higher MMA% (OR = 1.11; 95% CI = 1.02, 1.22) and lower secondary methylation index (SMI) (OR = 0.81; 95% CI = 0.71, 0.93) correlated with GDM risk, primarily in the first trimester. MMA% decreased and DMA% and SMI increased from the first to the second trimester. Results of stratified analyses revealed these associations in women under 28 or with normal BMIs (18–24 kg/m2). The study underscores inefficient arsenic methylation as a GDM risk, modified by age and BMI, with the first trimester as a critical period.

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is an impaired glucose metabolism that develops during pregnancy.1 It causes severe adverse health outcomes for both pregnant women and their offspring, including preeclampsia, macrosomia, and increased risks of type 2 diabetes and metabolic syndromes.2,3 Currently, there exists a substantial global prevalence of GDM ranging from 7.1% to 27.6%, exhibiting a discernible upward trend.4 In China, the prevalence of GDM increased 3.5 times over 12 years5 and reached 15% in 2019.6 To improve maternal and infantile health, there is a substantial need to determine the risk factors contributing to GDM for the prevention and control of GDM.

Although arsenic (As) has been reported to be one of the environmental factors contributing to the increased risk of GDM,7−10 information on the relationship between arsenic species and GDM is very limited. Determining the concentrations of arsenic species in the biospecimen is vital because the toxicity of arsenic species varies greatly.11−13 A small number of epidemiologic studies with varied assessment designs and methods have shown inconsistent results.14−18 The effect of specific arsenic species on GDM is not well understood.

Moreover, it is still unknown how arsenic methylation affects the incidence of GDM. Arsenic methylation is associated with the development of many arsenic-related diseases.19 Arsenite (AsIII) and arsenate (AsV) absorbed by the human body are methylated to monomethylarsonic acid (MMA) and then dimethylarsinic acid (DMA). Inefficient methylation, characterized by a high MMA% (the percentage of MMA in summed arsenic species) and low DMA%, is related to the increased risk of skin cancer and cardiovascular diseases,20,21 whereas highly efficient arsenic methylation is associated with an increased incidence of diabetes.19,22−24 The effect of arsenic methylation on the risk of GDM is seldom reported. Two studies compared the methylation indices between GDM and non-GDM groups and found no significant differences.15,16 These studies, however, have only focused on urine samples collected in the second trimester when an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) was taken. The metabolism of arsenic changes throughout the course of pregnancy.25,26 The first trimester was suggested to be the critical period of arsenic exposure in the etiology of GDM.15,27 Thus, it is important to investigate arsenic species and methylation capacity in the first trimester to adequately explore the relationship between arsenic methylation and GDM.

The present study aimed to investigate the relationship between arsenic methylation at different gestational stages (first and second trimesters) and the incidence of GDM in a prospective Chinese cohort. Speciation analysis of arsenic in the urine samples collected in the first and second trimesters was conducted. Profiles of arsenic species and methylation capacity in the first and second trimesters were compared. Quantitative results of individual arsenic species and arsenic methylation indices enable the exploration of the relationship between the exposure level of arsenic species, arsenic methylation, and the risk of GDM. Addressing the role of arsenic methylation in GDM might help to improve the risk assessment of arsenic and offer a potential strategy for GDM prevention and control.

Material and Methods

Study Population

This study was conducted at The Ninth People’s Hospital of Chongqing from July 2020 to April 2022. A total of 214 volunteers who met the following criteria were recruited: (1) <14 gestational weeks; (2) had lived in Beibei District, Chongqing, China, for over a year; (3) had undergone a physical examination and planned to give birth at The Ninth People’s Hospital of Chongqing; (4) was between the ages of 20 and 49. We excluded volunteers who met the following criteria: (1) had a history or family history of diabetes; (2) were unable to complete the questionnaire, physical examination, and OGTT. In the end, 131 pregnant women remained in the cohort. All study protocols were approved by the ethics review board of the College of Pharmaceutical Sciences at Southwest University (No. yxy2021122). All participants were informed before each visit and provided written informed consent.

Data Collection

When pregnant women created their pregnancy profiles at the hospital for the first time (around 8–13 weeks of gestation), the nurses conducted a face-to-face questionnaire to obtain baseline information from the volunteers. The baseline information included age, occupation, ethnicity, current residence, parity, gravidity, history of medication, history of smoking and alcohol consumption, past medical history (heart disease, chronic hypertension, diabetes, etc.), and family medical history (hypertension, diabetes, obesity, etc.). Smoking status included active and passive smoking. Drinking alcohol more than three times a week was regarded as having a history of alcohol consumption.28 In our study population, no one reported a history of alcohol consumption. The height, weight, BMI, blood pressure, blood routine, urine routine, liver function, and kidney function of volunteers were measured on the first visit.

GDM Diagnosis

GDM screening using 75 g of OGTT was routinely administrated between 24 and 28 weeks of gestation. According to the recommendations of the International Diabetes and Pregnancy Research Group (IADPSG),29 women were diagnosed with GDM if they have fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 5.1 mmol/L (≥92 mg/dL), 1 h plasma glucose (1 h PG) ≥ 10.0 mmol/L (≥180 mg/dL), or 2 h plasma glucose (2 h PG) ≥ 8.5 mmol/L (≥153 mg/dL).

As Speciation Analysis

Urine samples from pregnant women were collected in polypropylene tubes on site at the hospital on their first visit (for the first trimester) and when OGTT was taken (for the second trimester). The urine samples were transported on ice to the laboratory within 20 min and stored in −80 °C freezers until analysis. On the day of the experiment, the urine sample was thawed and a 1 mL aliquot was diluted 4-fold in deionized water and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane.

High-performance liquid chromatography (1260 Infinity, Agilent Technologies, USA) coupled with an inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometer (ICPMS) (Agilent 7700, Agilent Technologies, U.S.A.) was used to measure the concentrations of AsIII, AsV, MMA, DMA, and arsenobetaine (AsB) in the urine samples. The detailed analytical procedures are described in Supporting Information Section 1. We analyzed a certified reference material BWZ6824-2016 (North Weiye Metrology Institute, China) to evaluate the accuracy of the analytical method. Our measured results from 16 analyses of BWZ6824-2016 on multiple days showed that the concentration of AsV was 1001 ± 8 μg/mL with an intraday coefficient of variation (CV) of 8% and interbatch CVs of 8.5%, 4.0%, 6.0%, and 6.8%, which was in good agreement with the certified value (1001 ± 10 μg/mL). These results indicated good accuracy and reproducibility between days. In addition, a standard mixture (1 μg/L of As) was reanalyzed between every 10 samples. Calibration solutions were rerun after every 20 samples. The detection limits (LODs) of AsIII, AsV, MMA, DMA, and AsB were 0.2, 0.5, 0.1, 0.1, and 0.1 μg/L, respectively. Data below the LOD were assigned to LOD/√2 for statistical analysis.

Data Analysis

To account for urine dilution, the urinary concentration of each arsenic species was corrected by the urine-specific gravity (SG) using the formula in Just et al.301where Pc is the SG-corrected concentration (CC-As), Pi is the measured concentration, SGi is the SG of a urine sample, and SGm is the median SG of the cohort.

The concentration of inorganic arsenic (IAs) was the sum of the concentrations of AsIII and AsV. The concentration of methylated arsenic (MAs) was the sum of the concentrations of DMA and MMA. The concentration of summed arsenic species (SumAs) was the sum of IAs and MAs. The indicators of As methylation capability, namely, As methylation indices, include inorganic arsenic percentage (IAs% = IAs/Sum As), MMA percentage (MMA% = MMA/SumAs), DMA percentage (DMA% = DMA/SumAs), and the secondary methylation index (SMI), which is defined as the ratio of DMA to MMA.31 Although the primary methylation index (PMI), defined as the ratio of MMA to IAs, is also a good indicator of arsenic methylation capacity, it is not used in this study given the high proportion of nondetectable concentrations of IAs.

We used the Student’s t test and Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the Chi-square test for categorical variables to compare the baseline information and the concentrations of urinary arsenic species between the GDM and the non-GDM groups. The paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare differences in the distribution of arsenic species and methylation indices between the first and the second trimesters. Because the concentrations of arsenic species and arsenic methylation indices were slightly skewed, median and 25th and 75th percentiles were used to describe their distributions.

Binary logistic regression was performed to evaluate the associations (estimated by odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs)) of CC-As or arsenic methylation indices with the incidence of GDM for the entire population (model 1). Three covariates, including age, BMI, and smoking status, were integrated into the modified model (model 2) due to their statically significant coefficients (p < 0.1). In models 1 and 2, if CC-As were treated as continuous variables, they were logarithmic transformed (LnCC-As). If CC-As and arsenic methylation indices were treated as categorical variables, they were classified into tertiles, and the lowest tertile was assigned as the reference. If the number of GDM patients in a certain tertile was zero, we recategorized the population using dichotomy. The trend test was conducted using the median of each tertile as the score variable, and the Wald test was used to evaluate the statistical significance of the trend.

Stratified analyses were used to explore whether age and BMI affect the association of LnCC-As, categorized CC-As, or arsenic methylation indices with the incidence of GDM. The population was stratified by the median age of the cohort or by normal weight (18.5 kg/m2 < BMI ≤ 23.9 kg/m2) and overweight (BMI ≥ 24.0 kg/m2). If the population was stratified by age, model 2 adjusted for BMI and smoking status. Otherwise, model 2 adjusted for age and smoking status. Finally, linear regression was used to analyze the relationship (estimated by regression coefficient β and 95% CI) of the LnCC-As and arsenic methylation indices with FPG, 1 h PG, and 2 h PG. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 21.0 version. All statistical tests were two-sided.

Higher age (30.18 vs 28.09, p = 0.06) and BMI (22.35 vs 21.23, p = 0.09) were found in the GDM group compared to those in the non-GDM group. Of all participants, 51 (38.93%) reported a history of passive smoking. Women with GDM were more frequently exposed to passive smoking than those without GDM (57.10% vs 33.98%; p = 0.03). The two groups had no significant differences in gravidity, parity, and working status.

The typical chromatograms obtained from the analyses of urine samples from a GDM woman (top trace) and a non-GDM woman (bottom trace) are present in Figure S1. The urine samples from both groups showed the presence of DMA as the major arsenic species, with detectable AsB, AsIII, AsV, and MMA.

Profiles of Arsenic Species and Methylation Indices in Early Pregnancy

Figure 1 shows the percentage of arsenic metabolites as a function of gestational weeks. The result of regression analysis shows that IAs% and MMA% decreased in early pregnancy, while DMA% and SMI increased in early pregnancy. On average, IAs% and MMA% decreased by −0.40 (95% CI = −0.67, −0.12) and −0.31 (95% CI = −0.43, −0.18) percentage points per gestational week, respectively, while DMA% and SMI increased by 0.70 (95% CI = 0.43, 0.97) and 0.81 (95% CI = 0.53, 1.09) percentage points, respectively.

Scatter plots of (a) IAs%, (b) MMA%, (c) DMA%, and (d) SMI with the gestational week.

In addition, the paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test showed that the distribution of arsenic species and methylation indices in urine changed significantly during early pregnancy (Table S1). Specifically, the median MMA% of all pregnant women decreased significantly from 11.85% in the first trimester to 5.37% in the second trimester, DMA% increased significantly from 81.20% to 86.51%, and SMI increased significantly from 7.02% to 15.11%.

Correlation between Arsenic Species and the Incidence of GDM

In Table 3, logistic regression analysis shows that in the first trimester, the associations of LnCC-DMA and LnCC-MAs with the incidence of GDM (28 cases) is significantly negative, with adjusted ORs (95% CI) of 0.22 (0.06, 0.78) and 0.25 (0.07, 0.94), respectively. There is no significant correlation between As species and GDM in the second trimester, whether assessed by continuous variables or categorical variables (Tables 3 and S2). We did not examine the correlations of AsIII and AsV with the incidence of GDM because AsIII and AsV were not detectable in a large percentage of urine samples.

Correlation between Arsenic Methylation Indices and the Incidence of GDM

Table 5 shows the results from the logistic regression analyses of the relationships between arsenic methylation indices and the incidence of GDM. In the first trimester, after adjusting for age, BMI, and smoking status, high MMA% significantly contributed to the development of GDM with an OR of 1.11 (95% CI = 1.02, 1.22), whereas SMI was adversely and significantly correlated with the risk of GDM (OR = 0.81; 95% CI = 0.71, 0.93) (Table 5). When methylation indices were then converted to tertiles, the highest tertile of SMI had a significantly lower risk of GDM compared to its lowest tertile (adjusted OR = 0.17; 95% CI = 0.04, 0.72; p trend = 0.03) (Table S5). However, we did not find a significant association between the arsenic methylation indices and the risk of GDM in the second trimester (Tables 5 and S5).

Similarly, to evaluate whether BMI and age affect the association between arsenic methylation index and GDM in the first trimester, we conducted stratified analyses. From Table S6, we can see that the risk of GDM significantly increases with the increase of MMA% (adjusted OR = 1.16; 95% CI = 1.01, 1.33) and decreases with the increase of SMI (adjusted OR = 0.71; 95% CI = 0.52, 0.96) in women younger than 28 years. For women older than 28 years, this relationship is insignificant. In the analysis stratified by BMI (Table S7), the risk of GDM significantly increased with the increase of MMA% (adjusted OR = 1.10; 95% CI = 1.00, 1.20) and decreased with the increase of SMI (adjusted OR = 0.84; 95% CI = 0.71, 0.99) among women with normal BMI (18.5–23.9 kg/m2). This correlation is not significant in overweight women (BMI ≥ 24 kg/m2).

Correlation between Arsenic Species, Methylation Indices, and Plasma Glucose

The relationship of LnCC-As with PG was also examined, with all confounders (age, BMI, and smoking) included in the logistic regression models (Tables S8 and S9). In the whole study population, LnCC-MMA, LnCC-DMA, LnCC-MAs, or LnCC-SumAs was not significantly correlated to PG in either the first or the second trimester. The population was then stratified by age and BMI. In the first trimester, no statistically significant relationship between the concentrations of arsenic species and PG was found in either normal-weight or overweight women (Table S8). Similarly, there was no significant correlation between the concentrations of arsenic species and PG in the second trimester (Table S9).

We also looked into the relationship between arsenic methylation indices and PG concentrations (Tables S10 and S11). In the whole study population, there was no statistically significant relationship between PG and all arsenic methylation indices in the first trimester (Table S10), but a negative correlation between FPG and SMI was found in the second trimester. When the population was stratified by age or BMI, there was no statistically significant relationship between arsenic methylation indices and PG in all age and BMI groups (Tables S10 and S11).

Discussion

In this work, the median concentrations of MMA and DMA in the entire first trimester population were 1.40 and 12.37 μg/L, respectively. These values are consistent in magnitude with the concentration of arsenic species detected in other populations. For example, a cross-sectional study of Chinese pregnant women reported geometric mean concentrations of 0.33 μg/L for MMA and 10.18 μg/L for DMA.9 In an American case control study, non-GDM and GDM women exhibited median DMA concentrations of 2.90 and 2.59 μg/L, respectively, with MMA mostly nondetectable.15 In a Canada cohort study, pregnant women had a urinary median DMA concentration of 2.40 μg/L.14 Similar to the present work, previous studies indicate that AsIII and AsV are generally detectable at low rates. Methylation indices in this work, including MMA% (10.7%), DMA% (77.4%), and SMI (7.5), were comparable to those (8.6%, 82.8%, and 14.6, respectively) reported in a Chilian cohort study.16

In this study, statistically significant correlations were found between the concentrations of arsenic species, the arsenic methylation indices, and the incidence of GDM in the first trimester. We found that higher MMA% and lower SMI were positively related to the higher incidence of GDM. This finding agrees with the finding of a previous case control study that pregnant women with GDM had less efficient methylation metabolism than those without GDM,15 though its difference was insignificant. The occurrence and development of arsenic-related diseases are related to the metabolic efficiency of arsenic. The high methylation ability (lower MMA% and higher DMA%) has been shown to be associated with an increased risk of diabetes or insulin resistance.19,22−24,32 A prospective cohort study showed that for every 5% increase in MMA% and DMA%, the ORs of diabetes were 0.84 (95% CI = 0.76, 0.94) and 1.07 (95% CI = 1.00, 1.15), respectively.19 However, these studies investigated the relationship between arsenic methylation and diabetes rather than GDM. And, these cohorts included men who differ from women in arsenic methylation.33,34 Pregnancy is also a unique time for women’s metabolism.35,36 Therefore, previous finding that arsenic methylation is positively correlated with diabetes is insufficient to explain its association with GDM. Our results imply that inefficient methylation of arsenic is a risk factor for the development of GDM. Our results are in line with earlier evidence that decreased methylation capacity reflects the body’s poor detoxification ability and increases the risk of arsenic-related diseases.37,38

Further stratified analyses indicated that these significant links might be limited to women aged ≤28 years and with normal BMI. These results suggest that differences in arsenic metabolism may lead to significant differences in the occurrence of GDM, and maternal age and BMI are two important risk factors to influence this process. There is evidence that the association between As exposure and GDM is affected by prepregnancy BMI. Farzan et al.39 and Marie et al.40 found that the association between arsenic and GDM was exacerbated in pregnant women with high BMI, which suggests that obesity may increase the effect of As on GDM. However, in our study, the relation between arsenic metabolism and the risk of GDM was statistically significant for normal-weight women but not for those with BMI > 24 kg/m2. This finding is in line with results from the study of Xia et al.27 in which the association between serum arsenic levels and GDM was of statistical significance for normal-weight women (adjusted OR = 1.77; 95% CI = 1.18, 2.66; p = 0.043) and not significant for overweight women (adjusted OR = 0.82; 95% CI = 0.35, 1.91; p = 0.246). The disparity could be attributed to the small number of samples in each layer after stratification in our study, particularly in the high BMI layer. In addition, the substantial baseline risk of obese pregnant women may mask the comparatively minor consequences of arsenic exposure. Therefore, a cohort with a larger sample size will be required in the future to investigate the effect of BMI on the relationship between arsenic and GDM.

Research shows that a woman’s arsenic levels may change significantly in the second or third trimester,41 which may significantly affect the development of arsenic-related diseases. In this study, we found that the association of arsenic metabolism with GDM was significant during the first trimester of pregnancy rather than the second trimester. We also found that MMA% decreased while DMA% and SMI increased in early pregnancy, and methylation indices were significantly different between the first and the second trimesters. This indicates that the first trimester is the critical period of As methylation for the development of GDM.

Logistic regression analysis showed that urinary DMA concentration was negatively associated with the risk of GDM, which is consistent with Shih et al.17 and Chen et al.,15 who found that a low risk of GDM was associated with a high DMA concentration in the second or third trimester. Nonetheless, Ashley-Martin et al.14 found that the risk of GDM significantly increased with DMA concentration (OR = 3.86; 95% CI = 1.18, 12.57; p trend = 0.04). The reason higher DMA concentration is linked with a lower risk of GDM is unclear. Diet, nutritional status, individual methylation ability, ethnicity, and other environmental risk factors may explain the discrepancies in results.42,43

The underlying mechanism of the relationship between arsenic methylation and GDM remains unknown. Highly toxic intermediates of arsenic methylation may play a crucial role. Trivalent methylated arsenic compounds (MMAIII and DMAIII) have higher cytotoxicity, genotoxicity, and enzyme inhibition efficiency than AsIII, AsV, MMAV, and DMAV.44 MMAIII and DMAIII have been demonstrated to inhibit insulin signaling in cells, leading to insulin resistance and impaired glucose uptake45,46 as well as to interfere with insulin transport mechanisms, resulting in decreased glucose-stimulated insulin secretion.47 Walton et al.45 found that micromolar doses of MMAIII and DMAIII inhibited insulin-stimulated glucose uptake in adipocytes by interfering with the PKB/Akt-dependent mobilization of insulin-responsive glucose transporter. In cultured pancreatic β cells, exposure to 0.375 μmol/L MMAIII for 24 h limited glucose-stimulated insulin secretion, while no significant decrease of glucose was detected following exposure to even 2 μmol/L DMAIII for 24 h.48 Therefore, arsenic methylation intermediates may play a role in the development of GDM, and MMAIII is more detrimental to blood glucose homeostasis than DMAIII. If using urinary DMA, MMA, MMA%, or DMA% as the indicator of MMAIII and DMAIII levels, the fact that high MMA% significantly increased the risk of GDM suggests that MMAIII possibly promotes the development of GDM.

The current study has several advantages. First, a prospective cohort study explains the temporal relationship between arsenic methylation and GDM in early pregnancy. Biomarkers obtained in the early stages of pregnancy reveal arsenic exposure and metabolism at a vital time in the development of GDM. Second, we separated and distinguished AsB from other species relevant to arsenic methylation. Many previous studies used total arsenic that includes AsB to calculate the percentages of individual species. By excluding nontoxic nonmetabolized AsB, our analysis eliminated the interference of AsB in the data. Third, in the regression models, we accounted for some of the risk factors related to GDM, including maternal age, BMI, and smoking status.

This study also has several limitations. First, the sample size is small, which did not allow for the delineation of weaker associations. In addition, the concentrations of arsenic species in the participants’ urine were low, and thus, the detectable proportion of inorganic arsenic was relatively low. Although LOD/√2 is used to replace samples below the LOD, this may be more prone to biased estimation than more complex imputation methods. Then, the confounding factors taken into consideration are limited. Some other risk factors, such as exposure to other environmental contaminants, are not integrated into the regression models, which may also affect the estimation. Finally, the ethnicity of most volunteers is Han. Future studies could involve more ethnic groups to ensure that the findings are applicable to broader populations.

Conclusions

The current study provides evidence that inefficient arsenic methylation in early pregnancy (first trimester), as represented by higher MMA% and lower SMI, significantly increased the risk of GDM. This finding is significant in women younger than 28 years or of normal BMI. To our knowledge, this is the first report on the association between arsenic methylation in the first trimester and GDM. Given the small sample size (131 pregnant women), model adjustment utilizes limited confounding factors. More studies with larger cohorts are needed to validate this association between arsenic methylation and GDM and better understand the mechanisms underlying the association.
