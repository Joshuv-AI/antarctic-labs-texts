Single-cell RNA transcriptome analysis reveals regulatory programs of RNA binding proteins in diabetic foot ulcers
Authors: Qiong Hu, Lei Liu, Jie Pei, Yiting Yan, Yujie Chen, Songtao Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetic foot ulceration poses a serious threat to the health of patients with diabetes, and its pathogenic mechanism remains unclear. In this study, we analyzed the expression profiles of genes encoding RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) in the foot and peripheral blood mononuclear cells using single-cell sequencing data from GEO database. Our analysis revealed that RBP genes were abnormally expressed in DFUs, highly cell population-specific, and can be used as signature genes for different cell clusters. Co-expression and Gene Ontology enrichment analyses showed that differentially expressed RBP genes were highly related to the wound healing process. Genes encoding heat shock proteins were associated with ‘healing-related’ fibroblast differentiation. Western blotting of wound tissues from normal control and diabetic patients were performed, confirming the significant upregulation of HSP90 and downregulation of ZFP36. Analysis of the combined foot samples and peripheral blood mononuclear cell samples revealed common differentially expressed RBP genes, indicating consistency between foot and blood samples. Our results demonstrate the important roles of RBP in the healing process of chronic diabetic wounds, provide clues for further research on RBP regulatory mechanisms in certain cell types, and help develop novel therapeutic treatments for DFUs.Keywords: Single-cell sequencing, Diabetic foot ulcers, RNA binding protein, HSP90, ZFP36Subject terms: High-throughput screening, Endocrine system and metabolic diseases

Introduction

The development of diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) is a common complication in patients with diabetes, affecting up to 34% of this patient population in their lifetime. Wound chronicity, high recurrence rate, and high risk of lower-limb amputation in patients with DFUs cause financial strain and emotional distress1,2. Therefore, deciphering the pathogenic mechanisms and developing novel treatments for DFUs are urgently required.

DFU development involves complex pathological processes. Compared to normal skin wound healing, patients with DFUs typically experience delayed and disrupted wound healing, which is characterized by persistent inflammation and impaired extracellular matrix deposition3. Fibroblasts, keratinocytes, endothelial cells, and immune cells are all involved in skin wound healing. Fibroblasts and keratinocytes are the main cell types in the dermal and epidermal layers of the skin, and play key roles in wound repair. Keratinocytes and fibroblasts that produce extracellular matrix are involved in inflammatory reactions4,5. Both cell proliferation and migration contribute to wound closure. However, the cellular differences and heterogeneity of fibroblasts and keratinocytes in chronic wounds, such as DFUs, are not widely understood.

Single-cell RNA sequencing which can amplify and sequence the entire transcriptome at the cellular level, has rapidly developed over the past decade. Advancements in this technology have enabled the accurate determination of the overall gene expression status within a single cell, detection of heterogeneity between cells6–8and uncovering the diversity of immune cells in tissues9. Recently, single-cell transcriptomes have been increasingly used to investigate the complex cellular heterogeneity of DFUs. These studies have characterized various gene expression patterns and different subpopulations of cell types such as fibroblasts and macrophages10,11shedding light on the underlying mechanisms of disease progression and potential targets for treatment.

RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) bind to RNA and participate in its regulation and metabolism. Recent studies have shown that RBPs play a critical role in post-transcriptional regulation by recognizing specific RNA binding domains and interacting with RNA. This enables RBPs to participate in several processes including RNA splicing, transport, polyadenylation, intracellular localization, translation, and degradation12,13. Recent studies have demonstrated that RBPs play key roles in the development and pathogenesis of diabetes. RBPs are involved in various processes, such as mitochondrial dysfunction, fibrosis, inflammation, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition, all of which are known to contribute to the development and progression of diabetes14,15. However, the expression patterns of RBPs in different cell types and their associations with diabetic wound healing have not been thoroughly studied. Understanding the functions of RBPs in these processes may provide new insights into potential therapeutic targets for diabetes.

In this study, we aimed to identify differentially expressed RBPs and the expression patterns of RBPs associated with subpopulations of different cell types, especially fibroblasts and keratinocytes, in diabetic wounds. To achieve this goal, we utilized published scRNA data (GSE165816)16 from four clinical groups: the H group (healthy subjects without diabetes), DFU-H group (patients with diabetes and healing DFUs), DFU-NH group (patients with diabetes and non-healing DFUs), and DM group (patients with diabetes without DFUs). Data were extracted from each group regarding foot skin and peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) samples. The expression patterns of RBPs in different cell types were analyzed. The RBPs that were differentially expressed in fibroblasts and keratinocytes between the DFU_NH_Foot and H_Foot groups were further identified. In particular, differentially expressed RBPs were closely related to ‘healing-associated’ fibroblasts. Finally, we compared foot skin and PBMC samples from patients with DFUs and characterized the common differentially expressed RBPs in foot skin and PBMC samples between the DFU_NH and H groups.

Methods

Data retrieval and processing

A unique molecular identifier (UMI) count matrix of single-cell RNA-seq data from foot skin and PBMC samples was downloaded from the GSE165816 dataset. Data from 19 samples of subjects were included for further analysis (2 samples from H_Foot, 2 samples from DM_Foot, 4 samples from DFU_H_Foot, 2 samples from DFU_NH_Foot, 2 samples from H_PBMC, 2 samples from DM_PBMC, 3 samples from DFU_H_PBMC, 2 samples from DFU_NH_PBMC). R package ‘Seurat’ (version 4.0.4) was applied to convert the UMI count matrix into a ‘Seurat’ object17. Cells with UMI numbers < 1,000, detected genes < 500, or over 50% mitochondria-derived UMI counts were considered low-quality cells and were removed. Genes detected in fewer than three cells were excluded from further analyses.

Single-cell RNA sequencing data pre-processing

The UMI count matrix was log-transformed and quartile-normalized after the quality control. Then the potential anchors were produced using the top 2,000 variable genes by running the ‘FindIntegrationAnchors’ and ‘IntegrateData’ function from the ‘Seurat’ package (version 4.0.4). Canonical correlation analysis (CCA) from ‘Seurat’ was applied to remove batch effects. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed and ranked by ‘Elbowplot’ function of ‘Seurat’. Top 50 principal components were selected for further analysis. Cell clusters were identified by the ‘FindClusters’ function and visualized with UMAP plots. Previously published marker genes were selected16,18–21 and used to detect different cell types for each cell clusters using the ‘FindMarkers’ function and ScType tool22. A total of 2,141 RBP genes were compiled from four published datasets18–21 (see Supplementary Table 1), each validated experimentally using methods such as UV crosslinking or RNA interactome capture. The UMI expression matrix was filtered to include only these RBPs, and clustering was performed using Seurat’s ‘FindClusters’ function. Representative RBP genes for each cluster were identified using the ‘FindAllMarkers’ function.

Analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

Gene expression from single-cell RNA sequencing data were calculated on single cell level by ‘FindMarkers’/ ‘FindAllMarkers’ function from the ‘Seurat’ package. A one-tailed Wilcoxon rank sum test was performed to calculate p values between the two groups and p values were further adjusted using Bonferroni correction. Specific cell numbers collected for analysis in different cell types from clinical groups were listed in Supplementary Table 2 online. Only the absolute value of fold changes of gene expression on a natural log scale over 0.3 and an adjusted p value less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Correlation analysis

Metacells originating from the same biological sample were constructed as reported previously23. The k-nearest neighbor algorithm was applied to obtain a metacell gene expression matrix. Pearson coefficients between genes were calculated at the end and were considered significantly correlated when |correlation| > 0.5 and p value < 0.05.

Pseudotime analysis

‘Monocle2’ (version 2.18.0) was used as previously described24 for pseudotime analysis, trajectory construction, and calculation of pseudotime-dependent gene expressions. Raw counts were normalized by total transcript counts and log-transformed. Genes expressed in < 10% of cells were filtered. The root state was determined by specifying progenitor cell clusters. Trajectory robustness was assessed through at least 100 bootstrap iterations. Genes with significant expression changes along the pseudotemporal trajectory were identified using the ‘differentialGeneTest’ function in Monocle2 with Benjamini-Hochberg corrected q-value < 0.05.

Functional enrichment analysis.

Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways with which DEGs are involved were identified and annotated using KOBAS 2.0 25. Enriched GO terms and KEGG pathways were identified using the hypergeometric test and Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate control procedure.

Collection of clinical wound margin tissue

Patients with non-healing DFUs (not healed within 12 weeks after regular treatments such as surgical debridement, glycemic control and antibiotic therapy) admitted to the Department of Endocrinology of the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University and non-diabetic inpatients with acute wounds on their feet admitted to the Department of Orthopedics of the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University were included in this study. Wound margin debridement samples within a 0.5-cm range of the wound edge were collected and subjected to Western blotting assays.

All methods involving clinical samples were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant regulations. All experimental protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University (approval number: PJ-2023-04-10). Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Western blotting

Wound skin tissue was homogenized and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm/minute for 10 min at 4℃. The protein concentrations were determined using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) method. Protein was extracted and denatured at 100 °C for 10 min. 20 µg total protein was loaded, separated using electrophoresis, and transferred into polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Millipore, USA). The membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies ZFP36 (Proteintech, China), Hsp90 alpha/beta rabbit mAb (Zenbio, China), and GAPDH (Zenbio, China). Membranes were then incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h. Signal detection was performed using enhanced chemiluminescence substrates, and the protein band intensities of the target proteins were analyzed and normalized to that of GAPDH using ImageJ software.

Immunohistochemistry

Clinical skin samples were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 2 days, followed by dehydration, paraffin embedding, and sectioning into 4-µm slices using a microtome. Then slices were immersed sequentially in xylene and graded ethanol solutions. Antigen retrieval was performed by incubating sections in citrate buffer and heating the sections using a microwave oven for 15 minutes. 3% hydrogen peroxide was then added to inhibit endogenous peroxidase activity. Sections were then incubated with primary antibody ZFP36 (Proteintech, China) or Hsp90 alpha/beta rabbit mAb (Zenbio, China) overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase -conjugated secondary antibodies for 30 minutes at room temperature. Signal was visualized using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin for 1 min. Images were scanned and captured by a 3D Histech scanner. Positive staining intensity was quantified by Image J and means of optical density were calculated.

Other statistical analyses

Module score on single cell level for the defined RBP gene set was calculated by ‘AddModuleScore’ function and a Student’s t-test was used to compare the module scores in different cell types. The ‘pheatmap’ package from R was applied to draw clustered heat maps using Euclidean distance. Student’s t-test was used for comparisons between two groups.

Results

RBPs are abnormally expressed in foot skin samples of DFUs

We first analyzed the single-cell sequencing data of foot skin samples (GSE165816) from four clinical groups as described above. UMAP plots of single-cell transcriptome profiles are depicted according to the group (Fig. 1A), ‘Seurat’ cluster (Fig. 1B), and cell type (Fig. 1C). The expression levels of marker genes in different cell types and the proportions of different cell types in different clinical groups are shown (Fig. 1D, E). Genes encoding RBPs were identified from literature18–21 in which the RBP genes were validated by covalent UV crosslinking of RBPs to RNA, or proteome-wide approaches such as RNA interactome capture. 2,141 RBP genes are listed in Supplementary Table 1. The numbers of differentially upregulated (‘up_deg’) and downregulated (‘down_deg’) genes, and the overlaps between these and RBPs (‘up_deg_olp_RBP’ and ‘down_deg_olp_RBP’), were quantified across different cell types comparing DFU_NH_Foot and H_Foot (Fig. 1F). Genes encoding RBPs were abnormally expressed in almost every identified cell type, and there were more differentially downregulated RBP genes than upregulated RBP genes in most cell types, except NK cells, keratinocytes, and macrophages (Fig. 1F). A box plot showing the module score of the RBP gene set in different cells and a UMAP plot showing the module score of the RBP gene set in different clinical groups are depicted in Fig. 1G and H, respectively, demonstrating that RBP genes in the DFU_NH_Foot group were obviously lower in most cells compared to those in the H_Foot group (p value < 0.05). These results indicate that many RBPs were abnormally expressed in foot skin samples of DFUs, and most differentially expressed RBP genes were downregulated in the DFU_NH_Foot group compared to those in the H_Foot group.

Single-cell analysis reveals expression heterogeneity and pattern of cell type specific RBPs in foot skin samples of DFUs

We then used single-cell sequencing data from the four clinical groups to perform UMAP analysis based on the RBP-cluster (R0-R13, Fig. 2A). Similar to the results shown in Fig. 1A–C, the UMAP plot of single-cell transcriptome profiles according to RBP clusters showed that RBP genes in foot skin are also promising markers of different cell types, as the UMAP plots of RBP clusters highly corresponded to the UMAP plots of cell types. The relative expression of the top three marker RBP genes from the 14 RBP clusters is presented in Fig. 2B. The relative proportions of RBP clusters in different cell types (Fig. 2C) and the four clinical groups (Fig. 2D) are depicted. Notably, keratinocytes were mainly composed of clusters R5 and R6, whereas fibroblasts were mainly composed of clusters R1 and R3. Interestingly, proportions of some clusters such as R0 cluster differed significantly among clinical groups after ANOVA analysis. Representative marker RBP genes (DCN18,19,21DSP18,19,21RNASE120, and CORO1A18,21 in different cell clusters was selected based on their high expression levels in specific cell types, and UMAP feature plots depicting the expression of these genes are shown in Fig. 2E. We found that DCN was mainly expressed in fibroblasts, DSP was mainly expressed in keratinocytes, RNASE1 was mainly expressed in macrophages and endothelial cells, and CORO1A was mainly expressed in macrophages, B lymphocytes, and T lymphocytes. These results suggest that the RBP genes are highly cell population-specific and could be differentially influenced by diabetes in foot ulcers.

Differentially regulated RBP genes were associated with ‘healing-related’ fibroblasts in foot skin

Next, we studied DEG-encoding RBPs in dermal fibroblasts as the main cell type in the skin. DEG encoding RBPs in the DFU_NH group compared with the H_Foot group were identified, and the top 10 upregulated and downregulated RBP genes are shown in Fig. 3A. All differentially expressed RBP genes in fibroblasts can be found in Supplementary Table 3. Correlation analysis was performed to search for genes co-expressed with these 20 RBP genes, and the co-expression network is shown in Fig. 3B. GO enrichment analysis of genes co-expressed with 20 RBP genes was conducted, and the heat map was illustrated to show the most significantly enriched GO terms (Fig. 3C). We found that genes co-expressed with the differential RBP genes were involved with GO terms such as ‘angiogenesis,’ ‘response to hypoxia,’ and ‘positive regulation of angiogenesis,’ which are closely related to chronic wound healing. Notably, some DEG-encoding RBPs were heat shock proteins, such as HSP90AA118,21 and HSP90AB118,21(adjusted p value < 0.0001). Therefore, genes co-expressed with these RBP genes were enriched in processes such as protein refolding and chaperone cofactor-dependent protein refolding.

A previous study identified a subgroup of dermal fibroblasts with a high expression of MMP1, MMP3, CHI3L1, and TNFAIP6 16. We calculated the expression levels of these signature genes in three sub-cell clusters of fibroblasts (‘Seurat’ clusters 0, 9, and 12) from the DM_Foot, DFU_NH_Foot, and DFU_H_Foot groups (Fig. 4A) and found that these genes distinguished the four clinical groups well, especially in ‘Seurat’ cluster 9 of fibroblasts. The UMAP plot of MMP1 and MMP3 expression showed that they were mainly expressed in ‘Seurat’ cluster 9 (Fig. 4B and C). These results indicate that ‘Seurat’ cluster 9 is a key cell subpopulation of healing-associated fibroblasts. We then performed a pseudo-time analysis based on ‘Seurat’ cluster 0, 9, and 12 of dermal fibroblasts. Cell development was divided into five stages over pseudotime, and two bifurcation points were identified using pseudotime analysis (Fig. 4D). Fibroblasts from ‘Seurat’ cluster 9, the considered ‘healing-associated’ fibroblasts, were located on the trajectory at an earlier pseudotime point than cluster 0 and 12, indicating potential transdifferentiation events. Although pseudotime analysis places cluster 9 at an earlier trajectory point, we interpret this cluster as representing a more differentiated fibroblast state that emerges during wound healing. This interpretation is supported by its transcriptional profile and enrichment in DFU_H samples. Thus, pseudotime should be interpreted in the context of biological function rather than literal temporal order. In the context of wound healing process, more produced ‘healing associated’ fibroblasts during wounds closure should be more plausible. Therefore, considering the whole wound healing process, differentiation of regular fibroblast (‘Seurat’ cluster 0 and 12) into ‘healing-associated’ fibroblast (‘Seurat’ cluster 9) might be closer to the physiological process of wound healing. Especially when pseudotime plots of dermal fibroblasts was split by four clinical groups as shown in Fig. 4E, it is easy to be found that fibroblasts from DFU_H_Foot group and H_Foot group contained enriched ‘Seurat’ cluster 9 in contrast to DFU_NH_ Foot group and DM_Foot group. These results suggest that ‘healing-associated’ fibroblasts could be important for the successful healing of diabetic wounds and the failure of differentiation into ‘healing-associated fibroblast’ might be a vital signature for nonhealing diabetic foot ulcers.

Validation of differential RBPs in clinical wound tissue

We hope to choose DEGs in both fibroblasts and keratinocytes RBP genes with the greatest fold changes, making these genes easier to be validated in our wound specimens. Ideally, they should be pseudotime-dependent genes as shown in Fig. 4F and should not encode typical ribosomal proteins which mainly make up ribosomes. In practice, we took the intersection of the top ten DEGs encoding RBPs in both fibroblasts and keratinocytes with the greatest fold changes between H_group and DFU_NH group. Then from the intersection of differentially expressed RBP genes, we excluded genes encoding ribosomal proteins such as RPS1720,21, RPL3118,20,21, and RPL1718,20,21. Finally, we identified the intersection between the remaining RBPs and pseudotime-associated genes (Fig. 4F), which yielded two genes, ZFP36 and HSP90AA1, for further investigation and experimental validation. We then performed Western blotting on the clinical wound margin tissue collected from non-healing DFUs and normal acute wounds. As shown in Fig. 6, ZFP36 protein expression was significantly enhanced (p < 0.0001), while HSP90 protein expression was dramatically reduced in DFU compared to those in normal acute wound margin tissue (p < 0.001). Immunohistochemistry experiments further confirmed the differentially expressed ZFP36 (p < 0.01) and HSP90 (p < 0.001) in the epidermis and dermis, which is consistent with our single-cell RNA sequencing analysis.

Analysis of common differential expressed RBP genes in foot skin and PBMC samples of patients with DFUs

While studies on the differential genes in skin samples from ulcers help clarify the pathogenic mechanism, blood tests are more convenient in clinical practice. Therefore, we next compared the common differentially expressed RBP in both foot skin and PBMC samples. The UMAP plots according to ‘Seurat’ clusters (Fig. 7A) and cell types (Fig. 7B) are shown. The numbers of DEGs and RBP genes in different PBMC cells were shown separately in Fig. 7C. T lymphocytes contained the most DEGs as well as differential RBP genes, followed by NK cells and B lymphocytes. Fifteen common differentially expressed RBP genes were identified (Fig. 7F). The relative expression levels of these 15 differentially expressed RBP genes in the DFU_NH_Foot, H_Foot, DFU_NH_PBMC, and H_PBMC groups are shown in Fig. 7G, indicating a high consistency between the foot and blood samples.

Discussion

In recent years, the urgent need to elucidate the pathogenesis and pathophysiological changes in non-healing DFUs has driven the growing application of single-cell sequencing technologies. This powerful technique at the single-cell level makes it possible and convenient to study cellular heterogeneity, uncover connections between even tiny cell clusters, and detect subtle changes in the DFU microenvironment, making it feasible to elucidate the novel regulatory and differentiation mechanisms in specific cell types or subpopulations. Currently, researchers have identified unique differentiation profile of macrophages26special ‘healing-associated’ fibroblasts16altered molecular signature of mast cells27and impaired epidermal differentiation28 in the DFU microenvironment. However, exploring the expression pattern and regulatory mechanism of a given gene set associated with certain cellular functions with single-cell sequencing data has not been widely leveraged. RBPs have been found to be closely associated with RNA modification, post-transcriptional gene regulation, etc. With the development of proteomic techniques such as RNA interactome, more proteins were considered to be RBPs as they can bind to RNA through interaction with other proteins, even though they lack canonical RNA-binding domains themselves20. Various studies have revealed important roles for RBPs such as LIN28A/B and MBNL1, as well as proteins like MMP-9, in normal wound healing15. However, there are few studies on the expression pattern and regulatory role of RBPs in chronic wounds, especially DFUs. In this study, to our knowledge, we for the first time employed single-cell RNA sequencing data to analyze expression patterns and potential regulatory programs of RBP genes in various cell populations of DFU tissue, especially keratinocytes and fibroblasts, providing important clues to pathogenesis mechanisms of DFUs for further studies.

Our data analysis showed that RBPs were abnormally expressed in DFUs compared to normal wounds, especially in fibroblasts and keratinocytes. Combined analysis of these differentially expressed RBPs and their highly co-expressed genes indicated their close association with extracellular matrix (ECM) production and angiogenesis. One typical feature of diabetic ulcers is earlier degradation of the ECM29. Consistently, our analysis found that genes encoding ECM components such as FN-1 and LGALS1 were downregulated in diabetic non-healing foot ulcers. Notably, ZFP36 and ZFP36L1 expression was enhanced in our analysis and Western blot experiments. Previous studies reported that ZFP36 and ZFP36L1 encode the ZFP36 family of RNA-binding zinc finger proteins which bind to and destabilize mRNA of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α30,31 and the VEGF transcripts32thus playing important roles in controlling inflammatory response and angiogenesis. Some studies also unraveled that ZFP36 family of RBPs suppress the function of regulatory T Cells33 and restrain T cell activation34 to regulate immune response. Interestingly, Georgios et al. pointed out that the early acute inflammatory response may be more helpful for final wound closure of diabetic foot ulcers16. Similarly, activated T cells secreting growth factors are beneficial for healing wounds while impaired T cell function and defective T cells response lead to delayed healing35. Therefore, in our analysis, the observed upregulated ZFP36 family genes in fibroblasts and keratinocytes of unhealing diabetic foot ulcers probably contribute to the delayed healing by inhibiting the inflammatory response, repressing angiogenesis and inactivating T cell function. It will be of great interest to decipher the potential mechanism by which ZFP36 regulates the wound healing of diabetic foot ulcers in future.

Our analysis also revealed that certain RBPs, such as DCN, DSP, RNASE1, and CORO1A, exhibited strong specificity toward particular cell populations, suggesting their potential role in regulating gene expression in specific cell types. DCN is an ECM protein that is typically used as a fibroblast marker in single-cell RNA sequencing data analysis36. DSP are essential for desmosome assembly and epidermal integrity37. Recently, endothelial extracellular RNase1 was found to protect vascular endothelial cells from inflammation38,39. CORO1A is also closely associated with lymphocyte immune function40. Our analysis showed that DSP, RNASE1, and CORO1A are highly specific to keratinocytes, vascular endothelial cells, and lymphocytes, respectively, and may be potentially useful for cell subpopulation classification.

Another important result of our analysis was that several genes encoding heat shock proteins were found to play crucial roles in the differentiation of fibroblast subsets involved in diabetic wound repair. Heat shock proteins belong to a highly conserved family of stress proteins that were first characterized in response to heat shock41,42. The main roles of heat shock proteins are to chaperone and repair misfolded or denatured proteins induced by a variety of stress conditions, such as inflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress43. Later studies associated heat shock proteins with the pathogenesis of diabetes mellitus. HSP70, HSP60, and HSP72 levels are significantly lower in the tissues of patients with type 1 or type 2 diabetes or diabetic rats44–46. Decreased HSP90 has also been detected in the skeletal47 and liver tissues48 of patients with diabetes. Downregulation of heat shock proteins is closely associated with delayed diabetic wound healing49. Some heat shock proteins such as HSP90 and HSP70 regulate ECM production and degradation in fibroblasts50. Although studies have proposed that heat shock proteins affect oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptosis in diabetes51detailed molecular mechanisms describing the role of heat shock proteins in diabetes remain scarce. In recent years, heat shock proteins have been studied as RNA-binding proteins. HSP70 and HSP90 are regulated by, or interact with, canonical RBPs to participate in RNA processing and regulate disease pathogenesis. They can also regulate biological processes by binding to and regulating ncRNAs52. In our analysis, several genes encoding heat shock proteins (HSP90AA1, HSP90AB1, HSPD118,19,21, HSPA5, and HSPA1A21 were not only inhibited in DFUs compared to healthy controls, which was also verified by Western blotting in wound margin tissue, they were also found to be closely related to the differentiation of the ‘healing-associated’ fibroblast subpopulation by pesudo-time analysis. GO enrichment analysis of genes co-expressed with these genes encoding heat shock proteins revealed that these proteins regulate genes largely associated with inflammation, cell proliferation, and apoptosis, indicating the potential significance of heat shock proteins in the wound healing process.

In summary, in this study, we present the expression profiles and regulatory patterns of RBP genes and their potential involvement in the differentiation of specific cell types in DFU samples using single-cell RNA sequencing data. These results provide clues for follow-up research and technological development by characterizing novel RBPs and deciphering the underlying mechanisms in specific cell populations during the wound healing process of chronic diabetic ulcers, thus providing new perspectives on therapeutic targets for DFUs.
