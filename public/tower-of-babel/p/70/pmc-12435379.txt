Pedicle screws are a cornerstone of spinal fusion surgeries, providing essential biomechanical stability and facilitating successful arthrodesis across motion segments. As the prevalence of multilevel spinal fusion surgeries continues to rise, postoperative imaging has become indispensable for evaluating surgical outcomes, assessing hardware integrity, and monitoring the progress of fusion. However, the presence of metallic implants poses a significant challenge due to the generation of imaging artifacts, which can obscure or distort the visualization of instrumentation and surrounding structures on CT and MRI due to distortion and signal voids.1 To address these challenges, metal artifact reduction (MAR) techniques have been developed, including iterative reconstruction algorithms for CT and specialized pulse sequences for MRI.2,3 Modern commercially available MAR algorithms generally follow four main steps. First, on the original CT scan, metal portions of the image are detected by identifying very high-density regions. Next, the locations of artifacts are determined based on areas distorted by the presence of metal. These distorted sections are then removed and replaced by estimated values based on unaffected areas. Finally, a new image is reconstructed using the cleaned-up data.4 Applying MAR techniques to CT scans with artifacts produces corrected scans, typically lessening artifacts’ effects and improving imaging quality.5 In one study focusing on lumbar implants, subjective and objective evaluations showed that MAR technology significantly enhances image clarity, lowers noise across various tissues, and improves diagnostic accuracy compared to scans without MAR.6

While MAR techniques help to decrease the artifact signal surrounding surgical instrumentation, they can also lead to the appearance of fractures through hardware demonstrated in multiple imaging planes.3 These apparent fractures on imaging, or “pseudofractures,” have been reported in some instances in the literature to have led to revision surgery due to concern for hardware failure.7 A high index of suspicion for artifact-related distortions is crucial, particularly in cases in which imaging findings do not align with the patient’s clinical presentation.8

The following case report describes an example of a patient who underwent a thoracolumbar fusion with pedicle screw instrumentation. The patient had an uncomplicated perioperative and postoperative period and was discharged with instructions to use a thoracolumbar sacral orthosis (TLSO) brace. Because of concern regarding brace compliance, a CT was obtained at the 2-month follow-up:the screws appeared fractured bilaterally from T11 to L2, raising concerns of hardware failure. However, after modifying imaging parameters to reduce metal artifacts and a high degree of clinical doubt that all screws could be fractured without any repeat trauma, repeat imaging was performed and revealed the screws to be intact.

Illustrative Case

A 65-year-old male initially presented to the emergency department following a bicycle accident. CT imaging revealed bilateral T12 pars interarticularis fractures and 5-mm anterolisthesis of T12 on L1 concerning for instability. MRI confirmed T12–L1 fracture extending through the disc space with ligamentous interruption and an epidural hematoma. The patient underwent posterior instrumented fusion and decompression of T11–L2 the following day. The instrumentation used for this procedure included eight Medtronic ModuLeX Awl Tip Screw shank pedicle screws. His postoperative hospital course was uncomplicated. He was discharged to a skilled nursing facility and educated on use of the TLSO brace.

Because of concerns about brace compliance and transport difficulty delaying follow-up appointments, CT scans were obtained at the 3-month postoperative appointment. The imaging revealed hardware failure with multiple fractures of the bilateral T11, right T12, bilateral L1, and right L2 transpedicular screws (Figs. 1 and 2). Radiographic imaging was not consistent with the CT findings for potential screw fractures (Fig. 3).

The concern for hardware failure was discussed with the patient as well as the need for possible revision surgery. However, given that the patient denied any new back pain or recent trauma, the neurosurgeon elected to obtain additional imaging due to concern for suppression-induced artifact. The follow-up CT imaging with MAR again demonstrated fractures of the T11–L2 pedicle screws, but these were not evident in the radiographic or CT imaging when MAR was removed, thus increasing diagnostic suspicion of pedicle screw pseudofracture (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Observations

This case highlights the importance of considering imaging technique particularly in the context of pedicle screw pseudofractures. The initial postoperative period CT scan appeared to demonstrate multiple fractures involving the pedicle screws, which were inconsistent with the patient’s postoperative course. Further investigation, including optimized imaging techniques, ultimately clarified that these findings were artifacts rather than true hardware failure.

Imaging Artifacts and Misdiagnosis

The initial misdiagnosis of pedicle screw fracture was likely due to use of the MAR technique on the CT scans. Metallic implants, such as pedicle screws, are known to cause such artifacts on radiographic imaging due to beam hardening, scatter, photon starvation, and edge effects.9,10 MAR algorithms primarily suppress artifacts through their action on photon starvation. This function is primarily performed by replacing corrupt images with neighboring uncorrupted images.4 For neurosurgeons, understanding MAR and non-MAR imaging allows for appropriate orders to be placed if the integrity of hardware is questioned. This avoids the improper use of resources and reduces unnecessary scans.

In the context of metallic screws and their association with MAR, it has been found that titanium screws may lead to new and more severe suppression-induced artifacts after the use of MAR compared to without it.11 Since titanium screws are known to produce less artifact initially, MAR use may cause exorbitant suppression, leading to the generation of suppression-induced artifact and the appearance of pedicle screw pseudofracture.12 Because MAR often introduces additional suppression-induced artifacts, caution should be exercised when using it for titanium spinal implants specifically.13

Artifacts in cobalt-chrome and stainless steel implants are known to improve with the introduction of MAR, and the generation of suppression-induced artifact is limited.11 At baseline, cobalt-chrome and stainless steel implants have been shown to reveal more significant artifacts than titanium implants.14 Additionally, titanium has been shown to exhibit stronger reduction in signal with the use of MAR as compared with cobalt-chrome and stainless steel.14 Table 1 presents a comparative overview of the imaging characteristics of various implant types and the impact of MAR techniques.

In this case, the pseudofractures observed on the initial CT scans were inconsistent with the perioperative and postoperative course, prompting further investigation. The follow-up imaging consisted of axial images of the lumbar spine with sagittal and coronal reconstructed imaging and compared to the previous CT scans. In addition, contrast was not administered, and the metal reduction protocol was utilized. For dose reduction, milliamperes and/or kilovolts were adjusted by automated exposure control software based on the patient’s height and weight.

Clinical Correlation and Diagnostic Errors

It is important to correlate the patient’s clinical presentation and history with imaging prior to decision for revision surgery for hardware failure. In this case, following a stable clinical course and lack of symptoms suggestive of hardware failure prompted further instigation. This case presentation aligns with other reported cases in trauma and radiology literature, such as the case described by Salem et al., in which a patient with scoliosis was initially thought to have bilateral Harrington rod fractures based on imaging. Surgical reexploration revealed intact fixation and fusion.7 An additional case demonstrated pseudofractures due to software failure to recognize the pedicle screws and thus mimicking implant failure.10 Consistent with the conclusions made by Ge et al.,10 follow-up MAR and non-MAR imaging for this patient were interpreted together to fully understand his clinical scenario. At follow-up, the patient in this case denied any new onset of weakness, numbness, tingling, pain, or bladder or bowel changes that would suggest changes in the hardware that was placed previously. Revision was discussed with the patient as a possibility, but follow-up imaging was performed to avoid such revision.

Lessons

It is imperative to acknowledge the significant influence of imaging artifacts on clinical decision-making and patient management. A thorough understanding of these artifacts, including their characteristic patterns and underlying mechanisms, is critical to enhancing diagnostic precision, informing appropriate therapeutic interventions, and minimizing the likelihood of unnecessary procedures. When encountering imaging findings suggestive of pedicle screw fractures at multiple levels without interval history of trauma or new clinical symptoms concerning for hardware fracture, clinicians must consider the potential for image distortion or artifactual alterations. If imaging with MAR shows potential hardware fractures that do not align with the clinical presentation, surgeons should communicate with radiologists concerning suppression-induced artifact and request non-MAR imaging to compare. By leveraging advancements in imaging technology, refining operator expertise, and implementing patient-specific diagnostic strategies—while maintaining a heightened awareness of potential imaging pitfalls—clinicians can more effectively address the challenges posed by imaging artifacts. This approach not only mitigates diagnostic errors but also contributes to improved patient outcomes and optimized clinical practice.
