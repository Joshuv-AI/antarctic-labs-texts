Substrate recognition by the human mitochondrial processing peptidase and its processing of PINK1
Authors: Andrew N. Bayne, Danielle M. Simons, Naoto Soya, Karl Grenier, Gergely L. Lukacs, Edward A. Fon, Jean-François Trempe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Nuclear-encoded mitochondrial proteins rely on N-terminal targeting sequences (N-MTS) for their import. Most N-MTSs are cleaved in the matrix by the mitochondrial processing peptidase (MPP), a heterodimeric metalloprotease composed of (α) and catalytic (β) subunits, essential for the maturation of imported proteins. Import and processing of phosphatase and tensin homolog–induced putative kinase 1 (PINK1), a kinase implicated in Parkinson’s disease, govern its ability to sense mitochondrial damage. The current paradigm suggests that PINK1 undergoes two sequential processing steps: first, MPP removes the PINK1 N-MTS in the matrix; second, the inner mitochondrial membrane protease PARL cleaves the PINK1 transmembrane domain, leading to PINK1 degradation. Upon depolarization, PINK1 escapes proteolysis and accumulates on mitochondria to initiate mitophagy. However, the MPP cleavage site on PINK1, the role of MPP in PINK1 signaling, and the mechanisms of substrate recognition by human MPP remain unclear. Here, we define the MPP cleavage site on PINK1 between Ala28 and Tyr29 and show that it is inefficiently processed compared with canonical N-MTSs. In cells, MPP cleavage is dispensable for both PARL processing and PINK1 function, decoupling PINK1 import and damage sensing from its N-MTS removal. However, in vitro, the PINK1 N-MTS binds potently to MPP, inhibits the cleavage of other substrates, and traps MPP in a slowly processing complex. Exploiting PINK1 as a mechanistic probe, we use hydrogen–deuterium exchange mass spectrometry to map the PINK1 binding site on MPPα. We identify a two-step mechanism involving MPPα lid rearrangement followed by active site engagement, providing key insight into PINK1’s unique import pathway and fundamental MPP processing mechanisms.

Mitochondria are multifaceted signaling hubs that regulate an array of cellular processes from oxidative phosphorylation to immunity, proteostasis, and more (1, 2, 3). While mitochondria contain their own genome, 99% of resident mitochondrial proteins are encoded in the nucleus and must be translated and imported into mitochondria from the cytosol (4). As such, mitochondria rely on a diverse set of translocases, chaperones, and proteolytic machinery to coordinate the import and processing of these imported substrates. The most common class of imported proteins features an N-terminal mitochondrial targeting sequence (N-MTS), which tends to form amphipathic helices (5, 6). These N-MTS proteins are guided via chaperones to the outer membrane of mitochondria, where they interact with the translocase of the outer membrane (TOM) complex (7, 8). Upon exit from the TOM pore, N-MTS precursors interact with components of the translocase of the inner membrane 23 (TIM23) complex in the intermembrane space and inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM) (9, 10). At this stage, most N-MTS precursor proteins will either be sorted into the mitochondrial matrix or laterally released into the IMM if they contain a hydrophobic stop-transfer signal (11, 12). This substrate fate between IMM release or matrix localization hinges on the presequence translocase–associated import motor of mitochondria (PAM) complex associated to TIM23 as well as the mitochondrial membrane potential (13). In both cases, the N terminus of the imported protein can be matrix exposed and accessible for cleavage.

In the matrix, these N-MTSs are cleaved off by the mitochondrial processing peptidase (MPP), a heterodimeric metalloprotease consisting of MPPα and MPPβ subunits (14). While both subunits are required for MPP processing activity in vitro (15), each monomer imparts a distinct role during MTS binding and cleavage: MPPβ coordinates Zn2+ via a reversed thermolysin binding motif (HXXEH) and is responsible for cleaving the scissile bond within the MTS (16, 17); MPPα is a pseudoprotease that does not coordinate Zn2+ and instead mediates early MTS binding and recruitment, primarily via its glycine-rich loop (GRL) (18, 19, 20). After MTS removal by MPP, precursor proteins may be pulled into the matrix to fold with the matrix chaperone machinery, or they may undergo subsequent proteolytic steps before reaching their mature form (21). This processing by MPP is essential for the folding and function of its mitochondrial substrates. Homozygous knockout of PMPCB (encoding MPPβ) in mice results in embryonic lethality (22). In humans, mutations in PMPCA (encoding MPPα) cause autosomal recessive ataxias (23, 24, 25), severe mitochondrial disease (26), and encephalopathy (27). Likewise, mutations in PMPCB lead to childhood neurodegeneration (28) and Leigh syndrome with ataxia (29). MPP thus plays a critical role in maintaining mitochondrial function across species.

Our current understanding of MPP has been largely limited to studies of its yeast ortholog. The structure of yeast MPP was determined in 2002, revealing how MTS peptides bind to MPPβ in an extended conformation for proteolysis (17). This study, along with others, confirmed the preferred recognition motif of yeast MPP: an arginine in the P2 position (i.e., N terminal to the scissile bond) and a bulky, hydrophobic residue (denoted Φ) in the P1′ position (i.e., C terminal to the scissile bond) (30). While these MPP cleavage motifs were also shown to be conserved in humans via broad mass spectrometry (MS) N-terminomics experiments (31), the structural mechanisms of MTS binding to human MPP, particularly the substrate recognition role of MPPα, remain underexplored. This research gap also precludes our ability to study metazoan-specific MPP substrates that have no yeast ortholog.

One such metazoan-specific MPP substrate is phosphatase and tensin homolog–induced putative kinase 1 (PINK1)—a mitochondrial kinase whose mutations cause early-onset Parkinson’s disease (32). PINK1 functions as a sensor of mitochondrial damage by accumulating on depolarized mitochondria to initiate their selective engulfment by the autophagosome, a process coined mitophagy (33). Strikingly, mitochondrial import and proteolysis of PINK1 dictate its ability to sense mitochondrial damage: (1) full-length PINK1 (64 kDa) localizes to the TOM complex via N-MTS, specifically via its amino acids 1 to 90, where it is threaded through the Tom40 pore (34). (2) The N-MTS of PINK1 is imported into the mitochondrial matrix via an atypical mechanism, resistant to Tim23 or PAM subunit mtHSP70 impairment (35, 36). (3) The N-MTS binds to and is cleaved by MPP at an unknown residue (37). (4) The transmembrane domain (TMD) of PINK1 is cleaved by PARL between Ala103 and Phe104, which generates the 52 kDa PINK1 fragment that is retrotranslocated to the cytosol and degraded by the proteasome (38, 39, 40).

In the absence of damage, PINK1 is turned over rapidly to prevent the errant removal of healthy mitochondria. In response to damage-induced import stress, PINK1 accumulates as part of a TOM–TIM23 supercomplex (41, 42), where it dimerizes, phosphorylates ubiquitin (Ub) at Ser65 to generate phosphoubiquitin (pUb), recruits the E3 ligase Parkin, and initiates mitophagy (43, 44, 45). The most well-characterized damage paradigms for PINK1 accumulation include mitochondrial depolarization by the protonophore carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenyl hydrazone (CCCP) (46) or the accumulation of misfolded proteins in the matrix, induced by overexpression of a deletion mutant of ornithine carbamoyltransferase (ΔOTC) (47).

Still, the determinants for PINK1 steady-state import remain cryptic, along with the roles of its N-MTS and MPP during this process. While the PARL cleavage site has been validated both in cells and using recombinant proteins in vitro (48), the cleavage site of the PINK1 N-MTS by MPP remains poorly defined aside from one study, which revealed an intermediate “MPP-cleaved” PINK1 fragment on immunoblots upon PARL knockdown (37). Overall, there is a critical need to characterize the human MPP dimer, both to shed light on the mechanisms of mitochondrial import and processing in mammals and to clarify the role of MPP in PINK1 function.

Herein, we produce recombinant human MPPαβ, determine the MPP cleavage site within the PINK1 N-MTS between Ala28 and Tyr29, and compare its processing to other canonical MTSs. We demonstrate that MPP cleavage of PINK1 is not required for its import, processing by PARL, or downstream accumulation, defining an MPP cleavage–independent import route. We also develop a broadly applicable quenched fluorescent peptide to probe human MPP activity and unveil the dynamics of MPP upon binding to the PINK1 MTS, providing a model for substrate recognition by human MPP. Together, our work provides key insight into the function of human MPP and its role in PINK1 import.

Results

Recombinant human MPP can be purified from Escherichia coli in an active form

First, we sought to develop a platform for producing recombinant human MPPαβ. To provide structural context for protein engineering, we generated a model of the human MPPα–MPPβ dimer using AlphaFold3 (49) (Fig. 1A). Based on a previous report that rat MPPα was largely insoluble when purified as a monomeric MBP-fusion protein (15), we attempted to purify human and mouse glutathione-S-transferase (GST)-MPPα and human GST-MPPβ from E. coli. While GST-MPPβ yielded soluble protein following GST elution, both human and mouse GST-MPPα resulted in low-yielding GST-fusion protein (Fig. S1A). To this end, we opted for a coexpression system in which both human genes are expressed simultaneously to enhance MPPα folding and solubility. However, full-length mature MPPα–MPPβ was not expressed in E. coli until we truncated the hydrophobic and proline-rich N terminus of MPPα (Fig. S1B), which is conserved across vertebrates but not invertebrates or yeast (Fig. S2A). Still, small-scale purifications of Δ60-MPPα-MPPβHis6 resulted in significant loss of untagged MPPα (Fig. S1B). We therefore tagged the less soluble Δ60-MPPα subunit to maximize dimer recovery, which yielded reproducible purifications upon scaling up, despite inclusion body formation (Fig. S1C). Gel filtration of affinity-purified Δ60-MPPαHis6–MPPβ resulted in resolvable peaks corresponding to MPPαβ dimer and excess MPPα monomer, as visualized on SDS-PAGE (Fig. 1B), corroborating that our N-terminal deletion did not inhibit MPP dimerization. We also confirmed the masses of our recombinant human MPP using MS and verified the purity of both MPPαhis6β and Δ60-MPPαhis6 to be >90% (Fig. S3). MPP cleaves PINK11–45 between Ala28 and Tyr29 on a distinctly slow timescale

To determine the MPP cleavage site on PINK1, we incubated MPPαβ with a synthetic peptide corresponding to the PINK1 N-MTS (amino acids 1–45) and monitored the reaction products using MS. Extended incubation of MPP with PINK11–45 led to the selective processing of PINK1 between Ala28 and Tyr29 (Fig. 1C), with Arg27 acting as the canonical “R-2” MPP cleavage motif (Fig. 1D). This is consistent with the migration shifts observed following PARL knockdown, which locates the cleavage within the first 40 amino acids (37). Next, we compared the kinetics of PINK1 processing to other canonical MTSs by monitoring MPP cleavage of various peptide substrates across time (Fig. 1E). In this assay, MDH21–24, SOD21–32, and UQCRC11–42 were all 95%+ processed within 2 min after incubation with 0.1 μM MPP at 37 °C (Fig. 1E). Strikingly, only 5% of PINK11–45 was cleaved under these conditions after 40 min. Upon incubation with 0.5 μM MPP, PINK11–45 displayed significant processing by MPP but was still slower than the other substrates and was only 50% cleaved after 40 min (Fig. 1E). While it was previously shown in yeast MPP that longer MTSs are more dependent on MPPα for their processing (50), the slow turnover of PINK11–45 cannot be explained only by the length of its MTS, given that UQCRC11–42 was still rapidly turned over (Fig. 1E and F). This difference points to the presence of unique determinants within the PINK1 MTS that alter its binding to and processing by MPP.

PINK1 Ala28–Tyr29 is the only MPP cleavage site within the PINK1 N terminus

Since PINK1 harbors high MTS propensity across its first 95 amino acids (51), which also contains multiple possible R-2/Φ + 1 motifs (namely R88/W91), we asked whether there may be a second upstream cleavage site that would be processed on a more rapid timescale, like other canonical MTSs. First, we tested in vitro MPP cleavage of synthetic peptides spanning the entire PINK1 N-MTS upstream of the PARL cleavage site (Fig. 2A). We found that only PINK11–45 and PINK11–35 were cleaved, in both cases between Ala28 and Tyr29. We also interrogated the effect of mutating Arg27, given that previous studies in yeast had shown that mutation of R-2 motifs should severely reduce MTS cleavage by MPP (52). The R27A mutation indeed abolished cleavage of PINK11–45, as predicted. Both PINK135–60 and PINK161–95 displayed minimal processing by MPP. We confirmed these findings using full-length in vitro translated PINK1 arginine mutants in reticulocyte lysate, incubated overnight with GST-MPPα or GST-MPPα/MPPβ on GST beads. We observed a singular cleavage event (∼3 kDa reduction), abolished only by R27G and R33G mutants or MPPβ zinc (Zn)-binding mutations (E104A, E181A), confirming the singular MPP cleavage site at Ala28–Tyr29 (Fig. 2B). MPP proteolysis of PINK1 is not required for downstream PARL cleavage

Given our findings of a singular, unique MPP cleavage site within the PINK1 N terminus, we set out to resolve whether MPP cleavage of PINK1 is a prerequisite for downstream PARL cleavage within its TMD. To this end, we transfected designer PINK1 mutants into U2OS PINK1 KO cells and treated them with CCCP to depolarize mitochondria and stabilize full-length PINK1 (64 kDa) or MG132 to stabilize PARL-cleaved PINK1 in the cytosol (52 kDa). PINK1 R27A was still cleaved by PARL in cells, as evidenced by the 52 kDa PINK1 band upon MG132 treatment (Fig. 2C). We also introduced charge reversals to the R-2 motif with R27E–R27D PINK1, which should further reduce any residual MPP processivity that R27A might harbor in cells. Still, PINK1 R27E–R27D behaved comparably to WT PINK1 by maintaining CCCP responsiveness and generating the MG132-dependent, PARL-cleaved fragment (Fig. 2C). We also profiled pUb generation across PINK1 R27 mutants as a proxy readout for downstream Parkin recruitment and mitophagy. In this assay, all PINK1 R-2 mutants behaved comparably to WT PINK1 in terms of PARL cleavage and pUb generation (Fig. S4A). The R27A mutation also did not induce any sensitization in the accumulation or PARL processing of PINK1 in response to lower concentrations of CCCP (Fig. S4B). These findings suggest that MPP proteolysis of PINK1 is required neither for downstream PARL processing nor for accumulating in response to CCCP-induced depolarization.

Nevertheless, PINK1 binding to MPP during import may still play a role in its ability to sense mitochondrial damage, independent of cleavage or R27 mutation. Consistent with this hypothesis, the PINK1 R-2 motif is poorly conserved across vertebrates, whereas the regions on either side of the scissile bond are highly conserved, as well as the PARL cleavage site (Fig. 2D). Deletion of PINK1 amino acids 2 to 34, as well as longer deletions 2 to 60 and 2 to 90, indeed abolish PINK1’s ability to respond to loss of membrane potential, instead accumulating constitutively on mitochondria (Fig. 2E), as observed previously (34). While basal accumulation of PINK1Δ2–34 may be driven by other import machinery, exploring MPP–PINK1 binding is necessary to provide additional context for its unique import behavior.

Internally quenched fluorescent-FRET cleavage assays show that PINK11–45 inhibits MPP cleavage of other substrates

To profile MPP activity in a high-throughput manner and to further characterize its interaction with PINK1, we established an FRET-based activity assay similar to an approach used to characterize yeast MPP (30). Briefly, internally quenched fluorescent (IQF) peptides with a cleavable sequence were synthesized with a fluorophore (2-aminobenzoyl [Abz]) and a quencher (e.g., 2,4-dinitrophenyl [Dnp]) positioned on either side of the scissile bond. When the substrate is intact, Abz fluorescence is quenched because of the proximity of the Dnp quencher. Cleavage increases the distance between the fluorophore–quencher pair, permitting Abz fluorescence emission (Fig. 3A). To implement this approach, we designed two IQF peptides based on the human MDH2 (amino acids 1–22) MTS sequence: one with N-terminal Abz fluorophore and C-terminal Dnp quencher, and one with Leu13 (in the P4 position of the cleaved bond) mutated to Lys-Abz and C-terminal Dnp, referred to as “P4K” or “MDH-P4K” in this text (Fig. S5A). While both substrates were cleaved by MPP in a concentration-dependent manner, the reduced proximity between Abz–Dnp in the P4K substrate resulted in a higher dynamic range of fluorescence from baseline to complete proteolysis (Fig. S5A), and as such, we proceeded using the P4K peptide. Using MS, we confirmed the canonical R-2 cleavage site within the MDH2-P4K peptide, between Ser17 and Thr18 (Fig. 3B), and estimated the kinetic parameters of the P4K peptide to be KM = 4.6 μM (95% confidence interval [95% CI]: 2.3–9.1) and Vmax = 0.058 μM/s (95% CI: 0.048–0.069) (Fig. S5B). Using our P4K-FRET assay, we reconfirmed that human MPPαβ is only active when both subunits are present, that is, neither monomeric MPPα nor MPPβ was able to cleave MDH-P4K (Fig. S5C). Human MPPαβ displayed optimal proteolytic activity between pH 7.5 and 8.0 in buffers devoid of primary amines (Fig. S5D). As expected, human MPPαβ processing of P4K was also abrogated by the addition of EDTA in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. S5E). We screened the effect of divalent cation addition on MPP activity, given a previous report that molar excess of Zn2+ inhibited yeast MPP processing with a Ki of 3.1 μM (16). Remarkably, Zn2+ was also the only cation that significantly inhibited human MPP in our screen (Fig. S5F), yielding a congruent IC50 of 3.6 μM (95% CI: 2.3–5.5 μM) (Fig. S5G). Together, these results show that the IQF-FRET assay reliably captures MPP’s cleavage activity and could be exploited to screen for inhibitors or modulators.

To characterize metal binding in recombinant MPPαβ, we used a spectrophotometric assay with 5-Br-PAPS for Zn determination (53). MPPαβ purified using cobalt–nitrilotriacetic acid (Co–NTA) resin contained mixed Zn2+–Co2+ ions (Fig. S6A). To determine whether Co originated from the purification, we purified Δ60-MPPαhis6β using Zn–NTA resin, which yielded near-stoichiometric 1:1 Zn2+ occupancy and comparable gel filtration profiles (Fig. S6B). Both preparations showed equivalent activity in our IQF-FRET assay (Fig. S6C), suggesting that Co2+ can also activate human MPPαβ. Co–NTA purified MPP activity also did not increase across subinhibitory concentrations of exogenous Zn2+ (Fig. S5G), further suggesting that Co–NTA purification leads to a mix of active Co–Zn-occupied MPP. Notably, excess Δ60-MPPαhis6 and monomeric GST-purified MPPβ lacked Zn (Fig. S6A), suggesting MPPα acts as a metallochaperone for MPPβ during coexpression and folding, though this requires further investigation.

Next, we screened how various truncations of the PINK1 N-MTS would inhibit the processing capacity of the P4K substrate. We find that 500 μM PINK11–45 or PINK11–35 resulted in a complete inhibition of P4K processing, as measured by the initial velocity (v0) compared with apo-MPP against P4K in the absence of any competing peptides (Fig. 3C). The cleavage product PINK11–28 also inhibited MPP processing of P4K, albeit to a lesser degree. Removal of the first five N-terminal amino acids resulted in a significant decrease in the inhibitory capacity of the PINK1 N-MTS, suggesting that these residues are critical in anchoring PINK1 to MPP and inhibiting cleavage. All other N-terminally truncated peptides did not inhibit MPP at concentrations of 500 μM. Following this broad screen, we used dose–response curves to determine the potency for PINK11–45, PINK11–35, and PINK11–28, yielding IC50s of 476 nM, 1.6 μM, and 71 μM, respectively (Fig. 3D). In the same assay, MDH21–24 displayed an IC50 of 73 μM. Last, PINK129–45 alone did not significantly inhibit MPP processing (IC50 >100 μM) (Fig. S5H), suggesting that PINK11–45 binds more potently to MPP than canonical substrates and that this potency is driven by both N- and C-terminal portions of the intact PINK1 N-MTS.

PINK11–45 is a competitive inhibitor of MPP and traps MPPαβ dimers

To further contextualize the potency and slow turnover of the PINK11–45 peptide, we asked how PINK11–45 binding might alter MPPαβ complex formation, either by preventing or augmenting MPPαβ dimerization. To answer this, we utilized mass photometry, a bioanalytical technique that measures the molecular mass of native complexes using single biomolecule interferometric scattering microscopy. In this assay, the gel filtration peak of monomeric Δ60-MPPαhis6 displayed a uniform distribution, with nearly 100% of the particles at 63 ± 13.5 kDa, corresponding to the expected mass of monomeric MPPα (Fig. 3E). The dimeric MPPαβ peak, which was resolved at 4 °C on gel filtration, revealed that ∼53% of MPPαβ was monomeric in the absence of substrate and only ∼41% MPPαβ was dimeric. Given that these mass photometry experiments were conducted at room temperature using 10 nM of each protein, this suggests that apo-MPP is in dynamic exchange between monomer and dimer, with a dimerization constant in the nanomolar range. Upon addition of PINK11–45 in 7.5-fold molar excess, MPPαβ dimerization was promoted, increasing the dimeric MPP populations to 75%. This enhancement of MPPαβ dimerization appears unique to the PINK11–45 peptide, that is, MDH1–24 did not significantly alter the monomer–dimer distribution of active MPPαβ when added in 200-fold molar excess (Fig. S5J).

To determine the mechanism of PINK11–45 inhibition, we performed steady-state kinetic LC–MS analysis by monitoring the cleavage of unlabeled MDH1–24 in the presence of increasing concentrations of PINK11–45 (Fig. 3F). Global fitting of this data revealed a mixed model of inhibition, which was overwhelmingly preferred over all other standard models (Akaike information criteria probability >99.9%; Fig. S5K). The analysis yielded a potent inhibition constant (Ki) of 26.6 ± 4.7 nM and an alpha (α) value of 19.0 ± 5.9. The large α value (α > 10) indicates that PINK11–45 inhibition by PINK11–45 is predominantly competitive. This kinetic profile provides quantitative support for our mass photometry findings, confirming that the primary inhibitory action of PINK11–45 is the sequestration of free MPP into a slowly processing, dimer-trapped state.

Structural dynamics measured by hydrogen–deuterium exchange MS reveals how MPPα orchestrates substrate recognition

To investigate MPP conformational changes upon PINK1 binding and elucidate its mechanism of action, we performed hydrogen–deuterium exchange mass spectrometry (HDX–MS) on Δ60-MPPαhis6β with and without the PINK11–45 peptide and mapped deuterium exchange changes onto the AlphaFold structure of MPPαβ. To probe early stages of MPP–substrate binding, we incubated Δ60-MPPαhis6β with or without PINK11–45 (sixfold molar excess) on ice for 1 min, followed by HDX deuterium labeling (10 s, 1 min, and 5 min) and quenching. In this assay, we achieved nearly complete sequence coverage of both MPPα (98%) and MPPβ (99%) (Figs. S7A and S8A, Table S1). In general, the exchange rates of apo-MPPαβ after 5 min labeling agreed with the structure predictions, with loops and termini showing higher exchange rates than helices and β-sheets.

To identify PINK1-induced conformational changes in MPP, we also calculated the difference in percent uptake for each peptide in the presence of PINK11–45 (%DMPPαβ+PINK1 - %DMPPαβ-apo), denoted as Δ%DPINK1. It is important to note that interpretation of changes in deuterium exchange (Δ%D) depends on local secondary structures and hydrogen-bonding patterns. Protection (negative Δ%D) and deprotection (positive Δ%D) are therefore relative measures reflecting decreased or increased exchange rates upon ligand binding, resulting from either direct ligand shielding or allosteric conformational changes. Strikingly, PINK11–45 induced significant protection in several MPPα structural features (Figs. 4A and S7B). Two of the most highly PINK1-protected regions of MPPα were located at the top face of MPPα, which we term the MPPα “lid” or “gate.” In this region, we observed protection of the GRL, previously reported as a feature of MPP that regulates substrate recognition (18, 19, 20). Within this lid region, we observed significant protection within a novel loop–helix–loop structure immediately after the GRL, which we termed the RLY-helix loop. This protected region extended into a β-strand beneath the MPPα lid, forming part of the β-sheet that constitutes the upper MPPα substrate-binding site. Closer inspection of the MPPα lid showed PINK1-dependent protection of the PRQ-helix (adjacent to the RLY-helix) and the short HIQT-helix (adjacent to the PRQ-helix) (Fig. 4B). On the opposite face of MPPα, helices forming the distal part of the MPPα active site were also protected in the presence of PINK11–45—namely, the NLE helix–loop–helix and RSR loop–helix (Figs. 4A and S7B). Taken together, these findings suggest that PINK11–45 induces extensive conformational rearrangements within the MPPα lid while also binding to lid-proximal and lid-distal faces of the MPPα substrate–binding site. We did not identify significant regions of protection on the PINK11–45 peptide, likely because of the large molar excess of the peptide used in our assay.

To investigate the temporal progression of MPPα conformational changes following PINK1 binding, we analyzed deuterium incorporation of specific MPPα features across the labeling time points. We stratified conformational changes into two distinct steps: regions significantly protected within 10 s, representing the earliest stage of substrate binding, and those protected within 1 to 5 min, representing subsequent active site engagement by PINK1. In the first step, we observed rapid protection of distinct MPPα lid components upon D2O exposure—namely, the RLY helix–loop, GRL, and to a lesser degree the LMG loop (Fig. 4C). These observations suggest that the earliest step in MPPα substrate recognition involves a large conformational shift within the GRL and RLY helix–loop to accommodate an incoming substrate. Notably, while our AlphaFold model confidently predicts the RLY helix (amino acids 362–370), this region displayed higher D2O exchange rates in the absence of substrate when compared with other solvent-accessible helices within MPPα (Fig. S7A). This enhanced deuterium incorporation suggests that the lid region of MPPα exists in a highly dynamic state prior to substrate engagement, poised to rearrange for substrate capture. In the second step, we observed significant protection within 1 to 5 min for MPPα active site features, namely the NLE-hinge, the RSR helix–loop, and portions of the two active site β-strands below the lid, in addition to the sites in the first step (Figs. 4C and S7B). These conformational changes suggest that PINK1 also binds within the MPPα active site and that these changes may be secondary to the lid opening.

Regarding MPPβ conformation, most regions did not change in the presence of PINK1, though one EIE α-helix–β-strand (amino acids 122–130) displayed modest, yet significant, protection (<−4%) (Fig. 4D). In the AlphaFold MPPαβ model, the protected α-helix portion lies proximal to the MPPα GRL and dimerization interface, whereas the β-strand extends within the MPPβ active site adjacent to the coordinated Zn2+ (Fig. 4D). The β-strand lies across from the MPPα active site β-strand (amino acids 375–383), which also showed PINK1-dependent protection (Fig. 4B and S7B), forming part of the active site cavity closest to the MPPα lid. We did not observe significant protection deeper within the MPPβ active site. Taken together, we propose a model in which MPPα–substrate recognition proceeds in two putative steps: first, lid opening leading to substrate capture, and second, active site engagement (Fig. 4E).

Structural analysis of disease-associated mutations in MPP

We examined whether disease-associated MPPα mutations localize to some of the dynamic structural features outlined above (Fig. 5A). Strikingly, most MPPα mutations clustered within the lid region of MPPα, including four mutations directly within the RLY helix–loop (Y364C, L365F, V367M, and Y375F), one within the GRL (G356S), and four within the PRQ-helix (D397N/Y, R402G/P). The PINK1 N-MTS confers MPP cleavage–independent import regulation

Given the potent inhibition of MPPαβ by PINK11–45 in vitro (Fig. 3, D–F), binding to the MPPαβ interface (Fig. 4), and lack of cellular phenotype for PINK1 R-2 mutants (Figs. 2B and S4A), we investigated whether the PINK1 MTS might alter MPP activity in cells. We designed a construct replacing the PINK1 MTS linker (amino acids 35–60), which we determined was not cleaved by MPP (Fig. 2A), with a Twin-Strep tag to detect the cleaved PINK1 N terminus. We further investigated how different MTSs might alter PINK1 import and MPP processing activity by generating PINK1–MTS chimeras. We replaced the PINK1 N-MTS, PINK11–35, with the rapidly processed MDH21–24, creating variants with or without MPP cleavage–deficient mutations (R-2/Φ + 1). To assess whether PINK1 expression affects cellular MPP activity, we monitored precursor accumulation of endogenous MDH2 and frataxin (FXN), two established human MPP substrates. Across all PINK1 mutants, we observed no accumulation of MDH2–FXN precursors (Fig. 6C), indicating that the PINK1 MTS does not significantly impair MPP processing in cells, regardless of R-2/Φ + 1 status.

In terms of PINK1 import and accumulation, the MDH2–PINK1 chimera still accumulated in response to CCCP and generated pUb (Fig. 6C). This construct was also cleaved by PARL on a comparable timescale to WT PINK1, suggesting that the rate of MPP cleavage does not affect PARL processing kinetics (Fig. 6D). While MDH2–PINK1 levels were reduced compared with WT PINK1 in these assays, we did not observe this reduction in U2OS cells (Fig. S4C), suggesting cell line differences in proteolysis, damage response, or transfection efficiency. Strikingly, abrogating the MPP cleavage motif within MDH2 led to PINK1 accumulation in the absence of depolarization across cell types (Fig. S4C) and constitutive pUb signaling (Fig. 6C). This chimeric phenotype was distinct from the equivalent cleavage-abrogating mutations in the native PINK1 N-MTS, which showed no effect across multiple assays (Figs. 2C and S4A, B). These findings demonstrate that the native PINK1 N-MTS regulates PINK1 import and accumulation independently of MPP processing, rendering PINK1 function insensitive to MPP cleavage.

Discussion

Determinants of slow PINK1 turnover and potent binding to MPP

Our results show that the N-MTS of PINK1 binds potently to MPP. While direct measurement of binding affinity (Kd) was precluded by the instability of catalytically inactive Δ60-MPPαhis6β(E104Q) and insufficient yields of the active Δ60-MPPαhis6β dimer (Fig. S5I), the Ki of 26 nM obtained from the kinetic profiling provides a reliable measure of its affinity (Fig. 3F). The slow processing (Fig. 1E) and inhibitory kinetics (Fig. 3F) of PINK1 may be attributed to multiple sequence levels and structural factors. Studies using bovine MPP highlighted the criticality of hydrophilic MTS residues in the +2 and +3 positions for catalytic turnover by MPP—namely, Ser, His, and Thr (55, 56). Of the substrates we examined, PINK11–45 was the only one lacking Ser/Thr/His in both +2 and +3 positions (Fig. 1F).

PINK11–45 also harbors glycine and proline residues immediately flanking (i.e., proximal to) its cleavage site, including Pro25 (−4), a site implicated in GRL binding (18). This contrasts with canonical substrates, where distal (i.e., more N terminal from the scissile bond) prolines/glycines (56) and RGP motifs (57) promote efficient MPP processing. The importance of MTS flexibility is demonstrated by the lack of MPP processing on long, continuous α-helical MTSs and by experiments where removing helix-breaking residues abolishes MPP cleavage despite successful mitochondrial import (57, 58). This principle is further supported by studies incorporating flexible ether-based linkers into the MDH2 MTS, which were more efficiently MPP processed than both proline-deficient and native MDH2 (59). Collectively, MTS flexibility is critical for MPP recognition, whereas an R-2 specific, nonrigid conformation is required for cleavage (60).

In PINK11–45, the combination of proximal proline–glycine clusters and lack of +2/+3 hydrophilic residues could induce high-affinity recognition by MPP while favoring a cleavage-incompetent MTS orientation within the MPP active site. Consequently, the PINK1 N-MTS would act as a structural trap for MPP in vitro, as demonstrated by our mass photometry (Fig. 3E) and inhibition (Fig. 3F) experiments, leading to a catalytically stalled, slowly processing complex. This mechanism likely involves MTS misorientation and kinetic barriers such as slow cis–trans proline isomerization. Our HDX data support this putative trapping model by revealing extensive conformational changes in the GRL and RLY-helix-loop upon PINK1 binding (Fig. 4E), consistent with a substrate that becomes stalled after initial MPPα lid engagement but cannot achieve the proper orientation for the productive MPPβ active site engagement required for catalytic turnover. Taken together, our findings posit PINK1 as a conformational trap of active MPPαβ in vitro and suggest that PINK11–45 inhibits MPP by sequestering free MPPαβ and preventing the dissociation of MPP subunits in a slow-processing manner. The mode of PINK1-mediated dimer trapping suggests that MPPαβ could require dynamic interconversion between monomeric and dimeric states to facilitate multiple rounds of substrate binding and catalysis.

An outlook on PINK1 import and MPP-independent mitochondrial targeting

Based on the potent binding (Fig. 3D), slow turnover (Fig. 1E), inhibition (Fig. 3F), and dimer trapping capacity (Fig. 3E) of PINK11–45 in vitro, we investigated how this segment affected PINK1’s processing and function in cells, as well as its impact on other substrates. The PINK1 N-MTS clearly drives PINK1 import (Fig. 2E) independent of its cleavage by MPP (Fig. 6C). Along with the poor evolutionary conservation of the R-2 motif (Fig. 2D), this provides evidence that the native PINK1 MTS is inherently insensitive to MPP cleavage status. Specifically, the critical PINK1 R-2 motif is poorly conserved beyond primates, whereas its proximal and distal prolines are completely conserved across vertebrates (Fig. 2D). This discrepancy provides further evidence that the conformation of the PINK1 N-MTS is critical to its function, whereas its cleavage by MPP is dispensable. The accumulation of cleavage-deficient MDH2–PINK1 chimeras in the absence of depolarization (Figs. 6C and S4C) highlights the uniqueness of the PINK1 N-MTS in conferring “damage-sensing” activity.

Our findings must be contextualized within the broader mitochondrial import machinery, since it remains unclear how the PINK1 TMD gains access to the PARL cleavage site and what state the PINK1 N-MTS adopts during this process. Remarkably, PINK1 import and PARL processing are maintained during Tim23 or mtHSP70 impairment (35, 36), distinguishing it from canonical N-MTS substrates. The acquired sensitivity to MPP processing in our MDH2–PINK1 chimeras suggests that canonical MTSs may redirect PINK1 toward a TIM23-dependent import pathway reliant on PAM complex motor activity and subsequent MPP processing. However, we cannot exclude that these R15A/F17A mutations in the MDH2 sequence impair import through the TIM23 complex, which could induce constitutive PINK1 accumulation.

Notably, the apparent difference in MPP cleavage requirements between PINK1 and MDH2–PINK1 chimeras is paralleled in yeast by two substrates of Pcp1, the PARL ortholog: Mgm1, which is cleaved first by MPP and requires the PAM complex for subsequent Pcp1 cleavage (61), and Ccp1, which requires the ATPase activity of an m-AAA protease to dislocate its N-MTS from the IMM for Pcp1 cleavage (62, 63). Ccp1 was also still processed by Pcp1 following inactivation of MPPα/β (64). Strikingly, the human m-AAA protease AFG3L2 has been implicated in cooperative processing of PINK1 by PARL (37), akin to the role of yeast m-AAA protease in Ccp1 processing. Our findings support a similar mechanism for PINK1 IMM transit, whereby PINK1 bypasses MPP cleavage and PAM-dependent import, though the PINK1 N-MTS exhibits uniquely potent MPP binding (Fig. 3F) that warrants further investigation. To fully understand this mechanism, it will be essential to compare how the native PINK1 N-MTS and canonical N-MTS interact with IMM import machinery to enter the matrix.

While our data suggest that PINK1 does not inhibit the majority of MPP in cells, we cannot rule out whether PINK1 might exhibit local inhibition or dimer trapping of MPP subpopulations in vivo. This is supported by the stark differences in protein abundance for MPPα/β and PINK1 across human tissues (Fig. S9A), where MPPα/β are over 1000-fold more abundant than PINK1 in the whole organism, the cerebral cortex, and the testis, and 140-fold more abundant in the liver (65). This trend is maintained in proteomics of isolated human embryonic kidney 293T mitochondria (66), where MPPα/β are highly abundant, whereas PINK1 remains undetected (Fig S9B). As such, it remains possible that PINK1 may bind to an MPP subpopulation, particularly under damage conditions. Determining the structure of PINK1 bound to MPP and designing targeted binding-deficient mutants will be essential to clarify whether MPP–PINK1 binding is required in response to specific stressors. Clarifying the landscape of human MPPα/β interactors in cells will also provide key context for these phenomena.

Concluding remarks

Our findings establish a workflow for the purification of human MPP, provide a broadly applicable IQF-FRET assay to profile MPP activity, reveal the precise MPP cleavage site on PINK1 between Ala28 and Tyr29, and demonstrate that MPP cleavage of PINK1 is not a prerequisite for PARL processing in cells. We further characterize the MPP–PINK1 interaction, highlighting its slow turnover and ability to trap MPPαβ into slowly processing dimers in vitro. We utilize PINK1 as a molecular probe to study the conformational changes of the active MPP dimer during substrate recognition, revealing a novel lid rearrangement mechanism of MPPα. Our work lays the foundation for future studies of MPP–substrate interactions and sheds light on the determinants of PINK1 import, providing a foundation for investigating how MPP substrate recognition and processing vary across different physiological contexts and disease states.

Experimental procedures

Expression and purification of human MPP from E. coli

Codon-optimized sequences of MPPα (amino acids 35–525) and MPPβhis6 (amino acids 46–489) were gene synthesized and cloned into various vectors (pGEX-6p1, pRSF-Duet-1, and pET-Duet-1) using restriction digests or Gibson Assembly (NEB). N-terminal deletions to produce Δ60-MPPα were achieved using Gibson Assembly. His tags were removed from MPPβ and added to MPPα using the Q5 Mutagenesis Kit (NEB). Following cloning, all plasmids were verified using whole plasmid sequencing (Plasmidsaurus). For cloning and plasmid preparations, plasmids were transformed into NEB 5-alpha-competent E. coli and isolated using QIAprep Spin Miniprep or Maxiprep Kits. For protein expression, plasmids were freshly transformed into BL21(DE3)-competent E. coli (NEB). BL21 cells were grown in Luria broth at 37 °C to an absorbance of 0.6 to 0.8 at 600 nm, induced with 0.1 mM or 0.5 mM IPTG, and incubated for 12 to 16 h at 16 °C, unless otherwise indicated in the article. For Δ60-MPPαhis6β his-tagged purifications, cells were harvested and lysed via sonication in lysis buffer (50 mM Hepes–NaOH [pH 7.5], 300 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 0.1 mg/ml lysozyme, 25 μg/ml DNase I, 5 mM MgSO4, cOmplete protease inhibitor tablet EDTA-free). Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 18,000 rpm for 45 min. His-tagged protein capture was performed using a HiTrap TALON crude (Cytiva) column on an ÄKTA pure 25 equilibrated in buffer A (50 mM Hepes–NaOH [pH 8.0], 300 mM NaCl, and 5% glycerol). Lysates were loaded onto the HiTrap column at 1 ml/min in 8% buffer B (50 mM Hepes–NaOH [pH 8.0], 300 mM NaCl, 250 mM imidazole, and 5% glycerol). The columns were washed in buffer A and 8% buffer B until baseline 280 nm absorbance, and proteins were eluted across a 20 min gradient from 8% to 100% buffer B. Eluted protein was concentrated using 4 ml Amicon-Ultra concentrators (10-kDa cutoff; EMD Millipore). Eluates were resolved via gel filtration on a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL (Cytiva) at 0.5 ml/min in 20 mM Hepes–NaOH (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCl, and 1 mM Tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP). Designated peaks were then pooled, concentrated using Amicon Ultra 0.5 ml Centrifugal Filters (10 kDa cutoff), and frozen at −80 °C as aliquots. All lysis, purification, and chromatography steps were performed at 4 °C.

During our optimizations, we noticed colony-to-colony variability in MPPβ expression (Fig. S1D), which we hypothesized may be due to selective pressure induced by the high copy number pRSFDuet-1 plasmid. This variability led us to screen different coexpression vectors of varying copy numbers and to utilize the pETDuet-1 vector for the rest of the studies (Fig. S1E). Still, even with these modifications, the amount of soluble MPPαβ dimer after purification was only 30 to 50 μg/l of E. coli culture. These yields were not improved by the typical techniques used to increase soluble protein expression in E. coli, including autoinduction media or osmolyte addition (67, 68) (Fig. S1, F and G).

MPP activity assays using MS

Synthetic MTS peptides were ordered from BioBasic at a minimum of 80% purity. Peptides were resuspended in ddH2O, and concentrations were measured on a DeNovix DS-11 spectrophotometer by absorbance at 280 nm (amino acid sequence permitting) or 205 nm. For 205 nm concentration calculations, the extinction coefficient of each peptide was calculated according to a previously published protocol (69). For LC–MS MPP cleavage time-course assays, 0.1 μM or 0.5 μM Δ60-MPPαhis6β dimer was incubated with 10 μM synthetic peptide in reaction buffer (20 mM Hepes–KOH [pH 7.5], 1 mM TCEP) and incubated at 37 °C. To stop the reaction at specific time points, 10 μl of each reaction was incubated with 10 μl of 2× stopping buffer (1% formic acid/10% acetonitrile), spun down, and kept on ice. For all other cleavage assays, MPP and substrate concentrations were varied as indicated in the article, incubated at either 25 °C or room temperature, and reactions were stopped by addition of stopping buffer to a final concentration of 0.5% formic acid/5% acetonitrile. Eluents were analyzed on a Bruker Impact II Q-TOF mass spectrometer equipped with an Apollo II ion funnel electrospray ionization (ESI) source. Data were acquired in positive-ion profile mode, a capillary voltage of 4500 V, dry nitrogen heated at 200°C, and a mass range of 200 to 3000 m/z. Spectra were analyzed using the software DataAnalysis (Bruker). For MS analysis, samples were captured onto an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 Column, 130 Å, 1.7 μm, 3 mm × 100 mm column in 0.1% formic acid/5% acetonitrile at a flow rate of 40 μl/min. Peptides were eluted with a 15% to 40% acetonitrile gradient over 25 min, followed by a washing step at 90% acetonitrile for 6 min and a re-equilibration step at 5% acetonitrile for 14 min. Eluates were analyzed on a Bruker Impact II Q-TOF mass spectrometer equipped with an Apollo II ion funnel ESI source. Data were acquired in positive-ion profile mode, a capillary voltage of 4500 V, dry nitrogen heated at 180°C, and a mass range of 150 to 2200 m/z. Spectra were analyzed using the software DataAnalysis. Total ion chromatograms were manually inspected for expected full-length peptides and cleavage products. Extracted ion chromatograms (EICs) were generated for all reactant and product peptides with a mass tolerance of 0.01 Da, and EIC intensities in each run were exported for further data processing and visualization in GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, Inc).

MPP activity assays using internally quenched MDH-like peptides

IQF peptides were ordered from BioBasic at a purity of >95%. For Km determination, P4K peptide (MLSALARPASAAK(Abz)RRSFSTSAQ(Dnp)) in varying concentrations was incubated with 0.05 μM MPPαβ dimer in 20 mM Hepes–KOH (pH 7.5) in 96-well Nunc F96 MicroWell Black polystyrene plates (ThermoFisher). Fluorescence was measured on a Tecan Spark multimode plate reader with an excitation wavelength of 320 nm and an emission wavelength of 420 nm. Gain settings were obtained for each plate by measuring substrate fluorescence in the absence of enzyme for 5 min and then applying the optimal gain value for the plate to the subsequent measurement. For PINK1 peptide screens, IC50 determination, and divalent cation screens, 10 μM P4K peptide was incubated with 0.05 μM MPPαβ dimer in 20 mM Hepes–KOH (pH 7.5) in 96-well Nunc F96 MicroWell Black polystyrene plates (ThermoFisher). For IC50 determination, fluorescence was measured using the Tecan Spark, as stated above, except with a manual gain value of 165. For PINK1 peptide and cation screens, fluorescence was measured on a PerkinElmer EnSpire Multimode Plate Reader with an excitation wavelength of 320 nm, an emission wavelength of 420 nm, a measurement height of 8 mm, and 100 flashes. All reactions were performed at room temperature (22 °C) and monitored with plate readings every 10 s. Resulting curves were exported and processed using in-house Python scripts for nonlinear curve fitting and v0 determination. IC50 calculations were performed on normalized v0 values (% activity) in GraphPad Prism 10 using nonlinear curve fitting with variable slope.

PINK1 inhibition assay and Ki determination

MDH2 peptide (50 μl) in 20 mM Hepes (pH 7.5) at varying concentrations was prepared from threefold serial dilutions from 300 μM to 1 μM MDH2. Six microliters of 10× concentrated PINK11–45 peptide in 20 mM Hepes (pH 7.5) was added to final concentrations of 1 μM, 500 nM, 100 nM, or 0 nM. Δ60-MPPαhis6β was added to a final concentration of 30 nM, and the reaction was incubated at room temperature for 2 min before acidification with 6.6 μl of 10× stopping solvent to 5% acetonitrile and 0.5% formic acid. To generate standard curves, each reaction without PINK11–45 was performed in duplicate and incubated for 90 min at 37 °C to ensure 100% cleavage. Samples were frozen at −80 °C until LC–MS analysis. Samples were analyzed on a Bruker Impact II Q-TOF mass spectrometer equipped with an Apollo II ion funnel ESI source. Data were acquired in positive-ion profile mode, a capillary voltage of 4500 V, dry nitrogen heated at 200 °C, and a mass range of 200 to 3000 m/z. Spectra were analyzed using the software DataAnalysis. For MS analysis, samples were captured onto an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 Column, 130 Å, 1.7 μm, 3 mm × 100 mm column in 0.1% formic acid/5% acetonitrile at a flow rate of 40 μl/min. Peptides were eluted with a 5% to 50% acetonitrile gradient over 20 min, followed by a washing step at 90% acetonitrile for 6 min and a re-equilibration step at 5% acetonitrile for 14 min. Eluates were analyzed on a Bruker Impact II Q-TOF mass spectrometer equipped with an Apollo II ion funnel ESI source. Data were acquired in positive-ion profile mode, a capillary voltage of 4500 V, dry nitrogen heated at 180 °C, and a mass range of 150 to 2200 m/z. Spectra were analyzed using the software DataAnalysis. EICs were generated for MDH1–16 and MDH7–24 and were converted to concentrations based on the standard curve. Global nonlinear curve fitting was performed in GraphPad Prism 10 for competitive, noncompetitive, uncompetitive, and mixed-model fits. The global fits were compared using the Akaike information criteria.

Cell culture and immunoblotting

U2OS PINK1 KO cell lines were a gift from the Fon and Durcan labs. HeLa PINK1 KO cells were a gift from the Youle lab. Cells were cultured at 37 °C/5% CO2 in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1× penicillin–streptomycin. pCMV(d1)TNT PINK1(WT)-3HA, denoted as PINK1-HA throughout the article, was obtained from Noriyuki Matsuda. Q5 site–directed mutagenesis (E0554) kits from New England Biolabs were used to generate the corresponding PINK1 mutants and chimeras used in this study. Cells were transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 according to the manufacturer’s instructions for 24 h. After 24 h, cells were treated with dimethyl sulfoxide, CCCP, or MG132 at the denoted concentrations and time points. Following treatment, cells were washed twice with 20 mM Hepes (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCl, harvested, pelleted at 500g for 5 min, and were resuspended in lysis buffer (20 mM Hepes [pH 7.5], 100 mM NaCl, 1% Triton-X-100, 0.2% SDS, supplemented with Halt Protease Inhibitor Cocktail [ThermoFisher], and PhosSTOP phosphatase inhibitors [Roche]). Samples were incubated on ice for 30 min and then were spun at 16,000g for 30 min. Clarified lysates were collected, and protein concentrations were measured with the Pierce BCA Protein Assay according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples were heated at 72 °C for 10 min, and equal amounts were resolved by SDS-PAGE on 4% to 20% or 10% Mini-PROTEAN TGX gels (Bio-Rad). For Western blots, gels were transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. Following transfer, membranes were blocked using 5% bovine serum albumin in Tris-buffered saline + 0.1% Tween. All primary antibodies were prepared in Tris-buffered saline + 0.1% Tween and 3% bovine serum albumin and were incubated overnight at 4 °C. Imaging was performed using horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibody (1:10,000 dilution) and Clarity Western ECL Substrate (Bio-Rad). Primary antibodies used in this study were anti-PINK1 (D8G3; Cell Signaling, #6946), anti-TOM40 (E6Q3Z; Cell Signaling, #55959), anti-TOM20 (D8T4N; Cell Signaling, #42406), anti-ACTB (A2228; Sigma–Aldrich), anti-PMPCA/MPPα (Sigma; HPA021648), anti-PMPCB/MPPβ (Proteintech; 16064-1-AP), anti-Phospho-Ubiquitin Ser65, denoted as anti-pUb in the article (E2J6T; Cell Signaling, #62802); anti-MDH2 (D8Q5S; Cell Signaling, #11908); anti-Strep Tag II (Genscript; A02342S); anti-HSP60 (D307; Cell Signaling, #4870), and anti-FXN (F4V2S; Cell Signaling, #64668).

Intact protein MS

One microgram of Δ60-MPPαhis6 monomer or Δ60-MPPαhis6β dimer in 0.1% formic acid/5% acetonitrile was loaded onto a BioResolve RP mAb Polyphenyl Column, 450 Å, 2.7 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm at 200 μl/min in 0.1% formic acid/5% acetonitrile and was eluted with a 5% to 90% acetonitrile gradient over 20 min. Eluents were analyzed on a Bruker Impact II Q-TOF mass spectrometer equipped with an Apollo II ion funnel ESI source. Data were acquired in positive-ion profile mode, a capillary voltage of 4500 V, dry nitrogen heated at 200 °C, and a mass range of 200 to 3000 m/z. Spectra were analyzed using the software DataAnalysis. The total ion chromatogram was used to determine where the protein eluted, and spectra were summed over the entire elution peak. The multiply charged ion species from 15,000 to 75,000 m/z were deconvoluted at 10,000 resolution using the maximum entropy (MaxEnt) and MaxEntX methods. Observed masses were searched against the corresponding protein sequences using Bruker SequenceEditor software.

In vitro translation cleavage assay

Recombinant mouse GST-MPPα and rat GST-MPPβ were transformed in BL21 E. coli, grown to an absorbance of ∼0.5 at 600 nm and induced with 50 μM IPTG at room temperature for 3 h. Bacteria were pelleted and resuspended in GST binding buffer (20 mM Tris [pH 7.4], 200 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 100 μM ZnSO4, protease inhibitors, and 0.1% lysozyme), incubated on ice for 10 min, and sonicated 3 × 10 s. Triton X-100 was added for a final concentration of 1% and incubated for 30 min at 4 °C. Debris was pelleted by ultracentrifugation, and the supernatant was incubated overnight on Glutathione Sepharose beads. Beads were washed three times in PBS, resuspended in GST binding buffer, and evaluated by SDS-PAGE. For GST-tagged proteins, elution was performed using 20 mM glutathione in GST binding buffer. To cleave GST tags, on-bead digestion was performed with recombinant GST-3C protease. Purified proteins were concentrated using 10 kDa Amicon Ultra Centrifugal filters. Recombinant full-length, untagged human PINK1 (pcDNA 3.1 vector) was synthesized with the TnT Quick Coupled Transcription/Translation System (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and incubated overnight with a combination of GST-MPPα and/or GST-MPPβ or MPPβ in GST binding buffer. Beads were washed the next morning three times with GST binding buffer with 5 min incubation between each wash. Beads were then boiled and processed by immunoblotting against PINK1.

Mass photometry of MPPαβ

Mass photometry measurements were obtained using a Refeyn TwoMP mass photometer. Prior to sample analysis, a calibration curve was generated using sweet potato β-amylase (Sigma–Aldrich) in MPP purification buffer to correlate ratiometric contrast to molecular mass. For MPP–MTS measurements, 100 nM of Δ60-MPPαhis6 or Δ60-MPPαhis6MPPβ in 20 mM Hepes (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM TCEP were kept at room temperature prior to peptide addition. Immediately before mass photometry measurement, PINK11–45 or MDH1–24 were diluted into the protein stocks at the denoted concentrations, and 2 μl of MPP–MTS or apo-MPP mixtures were pipetted into 18 μl of MPP purification buffer on a glass coverslip for MP measurement. Mass histograms were binned, fitted with Gaussian curves, and analyzed in DiscoverMP software (v2024 R1; Refeyn).

HDX-MS of MPPαβ

HDX-MS experiments were carried out as described (70). Briefly, 15 μM of Δ60-MPPαhis6MPPβ in 20 mM Hepes (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM TCEP preincubated alone, or with PINK11–45 peptide in sixfold molar excess for 1 min on ice. HDX was initiated by diluting the preincubated Δ60-MPPαhis6MPPβ (apo) solution or Δ60-MPPαhis6MPPβ + PINK11–45 solution using 1:14 dilution ratio into D2O-based buffer (same components as H2O-based MPP stock solution dissolved in D2O. For Δ60-MPPαhis6MPPβ + PINK11–45, D2O-based buffer also contains 6 μM of PINK11–45). The HDX incubation period and temperature were set to 10 s, 1 min, and 5 min, and 22 °C, respectively. HDX was quenched with chilled quenching buffer (300 mM glycine, 8 M urea in H2O, pH 2.4) using 3:7 dilution ratio. Quenched solutions were flash frozen in methanol-containing dry ice, and samples were stored at −80 °C until use. For the undeuterated control, initial dilution was made in H2O-based buffer. Prior to ultra-HPLC–MS analysis, the deuterated Δ60-MPPαhis6β ± PINK11–45 was digested on an online immobilized pepsin column prepared in-house at a flow rate of 30 μl/min for 1.5 min. The resulting peptides were trapped on a C8 EXP Trap cartridge (2.7 μm HALO, 2.1 × 5 mm; Optimized Technologies). Following desalting for 1.5 min at a flow rate of 200 μl/min, peptides were loaded onto a Hypersil GOLD C8 LC column (1 mm × 50 mm, 1.9 μm; Thermo Fisher Scientific) connected to an Agilent 1290 Infinity II ultra-HPLC system. Peptide separation was achieved using a 5% to 40% linear gradient of acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid over 8 min at a 65 μl/min flow rate. To minimize back-exchange, the columns, solvent delivery lines, injector, and other accessories were placed in an ice bath. The C8 was directly connected to the ESI source of an LTQ Orbitrap Eclipse mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and mass spectra of peptides were acquired in positive-ion mode for m/z 200 to 2000. Triplicate measurements were performed for each time point. Identification of peptides was carried out in separate experiments by tandem MS analysis in data-dependent acquisition mode, using collision-induced dissociation. All tandem MS spectra were analyzed using Proteome Discoverer 2.4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Peptide searching results were further manually inspected, and only those verifiable were used in HDX analysis. We quantified the average deuterium uptake percentage (%D) for each peptide of apo-MPPαβ and MPPαβ + PINK11–45 at each labeling time point using HDExaminer 3.4 (Trajan Scientific and Medical).
