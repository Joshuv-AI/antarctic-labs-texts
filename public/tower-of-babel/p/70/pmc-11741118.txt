First-trimester Doppler Ultrasound for Predicting Successful Management of Pregnancy with Recurrent Pregnancy Losses Due to Antiphospholipid Syndrome and Thrombophilia: A Cohort Study
Authors: Muhammad Adrianes Bachnas, Uki Retno Budihastuti, Eriana Melinawati, Nutria Widya Purna Anggraini, Robert Ridwan, Lini Astetri, Agung Sari Wijayanti, Atthahira Amalia Hafiizha, Muhammad Denny Gagah Pradana, Aliffudin Nur, Muhammad Alamsyah Azis, Wiryawan Permadi, Luthfi Rahman, Jimmy Yanuar Annas, Daniel Jonathan Mangapul Siagian, Neissya Nastiti Firmanto, Gilang Rizqy Perdana Aries Rohman, Muhammad Ilham Aldika Akbar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Recurrent pregnancy loss (RPL) often stems from a hypercoagulable state that exacerbates conditions such as antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) and thrombophilia, leading to early placental issues. Although treatments such as low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) and low-dose aspirin (LDA) are used, outcomes vary. This study proposes using first-trimester Doppler ultrasound – specifically, uterine radial artery resistance index (URa-RI) at 8 weeks and uterine artery pulsatility index (Ut-PI) with pre-diastolic notching (Ut-notch) at 11–13 weeks — to better predict successful pregnancies and reduce risks of adverse outcomes.Aim:The aim of this study was to evaluate URa-RI, Ut-PI and Ut-notch between successful pregnancy and not successful and between pregnancy with adverse events and without.Settings and Design:The study was conducted in a clinical setting, using a retrospective cohort design on a sample of 72 patients with a history of two or more RPL episodes.Materials and Methods:Data on URa-RI measured at 8 weeks and Ut-PI and Ut-notch measured at 11–13 weeks were collected. Maternal characteristics – including age, BMI, number of pregnancy losses, etiopathology and immuno-inflammatory response – were considered in the analysis.Statistical Analysis Used:Comparative statistical analysis was performed on URa-RI, Ut-PI and Ut-notch data, evaluating their associations with pregnancy success and adverse outcomes. Variables were statistically compared between successful and unsuccessful pregnancies and also analysed for adverse events. Analysis was performed using SPSS (IBM, 27th edition). Chi-square tests were applied to nominal categories and multivariate logistic regression adjusted for age, body mass index (BMI), previous pregnancy losses, etiopathology and antinuclear antibody positivity. Primary outcomes (URa-RI, Ut-PI and Ut-notch) were reported with odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).Results:Low URa-RI (<0.45) at 8 weeks gives a 16.4 times higher chance for the management to be successful and result in a healthy take-home baby (95% CI = 4.4–61, P < 0.0001). The mean URa-RI was also significantly higher in unsuccessful pregnancy (0.50 ± 0.09 vs. 0.38 ± 0.04, P < 0.0001). High Ut-PI and positive Ut-notch result in a significant increase of adverse event risk, which are 19.4 times and 8.1 times, respectively (95% CI = 4.2–51.6 and 3.12–20.5, P < 0.0001).Conclusion:This study demonstrates the utility of first-trimester Doppler ultrasound (URa-RI, Ut-PI and Ut-notch) in predicting pregnancy success and adverse events in patients with RPL, particularly those managed with LMWH and LDA. These ultrasound markers may provide valuable guidance in managing and anticipating outcomes in RPL cases related to thrombophilia and APS.

INTRODUCTION

Recurrent pregnancy loss (RPL) remains a significant challenge in maternal-foetal medicine, with a reported prevalence of 2%–5%.[1] This condition is associated with considerable psychological trauma for affected couples, who often experience heightened anxiety throughout pregnancy, seeking reassurance about the effectiveness of current management strategies.[2] Numerous approaches have been proposed for evaluating ongoing management in RPL cases; however, until recently, no specific examination had been identified as definitive.[3456] Doppler ultrasound, which enables visualisation and measurement of vascularisation, offers potential in this area.[7] With Doppler ultrasound, favourable blood flow and low vascular resistance indicate effective nutrient and oxygen delivery to tissues and organs. This, in turn, suggests that uterine vasculature adequately supports placental and foetal development, potentially contributing to a favourable pregnancy outcome.[8]

The timing of the examination and the choice of vessel for Doppler ultrasound evaluation are crucial for obtaining meaningful results.[9] The uterine artery, as the largest artery in the uterus, is widely utilised as a primary indicator to assess risk factors associated with major obstetric syndromes, including pre-eclampsia, foetal growth restriction and iatrogenic prematurity. The presence of pre-diastolic notching (Ut-notch) with a high pulsatility index (Ut-PI) in the uterine artery at 11–13 weeks of gestation has been shown to predict early-onset pre-eclampsia and foetal growth restriction.[1011] Early-onset pre-eclampsia, typically manifesting clinically before 34 weeks of gestation, has a strong association with antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) and thrombophilia, both of which may lead to placental infarction due to microthrombosis and immuno-inflammatory responses.[121314] In cases of RPL, uterine blood flow may be influenced by antithrombotic and anticoagulant therapies.[15] Generally, uterine artery resistance remains stable until approximately 10 weeks of gestation, after which it progressively declines as pregnancy advances. Failure of placental development, however, results in a high resistance index.[1011]

The uterine radial artery resistance index (URa-RI) has been proposed as a potential predictor of miscarriage in RPL cases complicated by thrombophilia and managed with low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) and low-dose aspirin (LDA).[16] Pregnancy with a URa-RI below 0.45 at 8 weeks of gestation is highly predictive of viability, with a 97% negative predictive value.[16] Bao et al. monitored URa-RI biweekly from 0 to 32 weeks of gestation, concluding that 8 weeks is the optimal time to assess pregnancy viability in RPL cases associated with thrombophilia.[16] However, in surviving cases, adverse outcomes such as pre-eclampsia, foetal growth restriction and foetal or neonatal mortality were not evaluated.

This study aims to investigate the utility of measuring the URa-RI at 8 weeks of gestation, along with uterine artery pulsatility index (Ut-PI) and pre-diastolic notching (Ut-notch) at 11–13 weeks, in predicting successful pregnancy outcomes in women with RPL associated with APS and thrombophilia. We anticipate that the findings may support the inclusion of first-trimester Doppler ultrasound as a standard assessment protocol for ongoing pregnancy evaluation in RPL cases due to APS and thrombophilia.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study participants

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Maternal-Fetal Medicine Division, Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology, Dr. Moewardi General Hospital, a tertiary referral and type A academic hospital affiliated with the Faculty of Medicine, Sebelas Maret University. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics and Research Committee of Dr. Moewardi General Hospital and Sebelas Maret University. The study was conducted in accordance with Helsinki Declaration.

A total of 213 women diagnosed with RPL were enrolled between January 2022 and June 2024. Inclusion criteria required a confirmed diagnosis of APS or thrombophilia, as all participants underwent a comprehensive aetiological evaluation for RPL and met established criteria for APS or thrombophilia. Among the participants, 18 were diagnosed with APS, and 54 were diagnosed with thrombophilia. Additionally, 24 patients exhibited immuno-inflammatory responses, as indicated by antinuclear antibody (ANA) positivity.

All patients underwent ANA-indirect immunofluorescence testing, followed by ANA profiling if results were positive. Additional evaluations included testing for lupus anticoagulant (LAC), anticardiolipin antibody (ACA), anti-β2 glycoprotein I (anti-B2GPI), protein C, protein S, platelet aggregation and D-dimer levels. A history of APS and thrombophilia was considered if patients had experienced one or more episodes of arterial, venous or small vessel thrombosis in any tissue or organ. Thrombosis was confirmed by objective, validated criteria, including unequivocal findings from appropriate imaging studies or histopathology.[31217]

Pregnancy morbidity was defined by the presence of one or more of the following: (1) an unexplained foetal death of a morphologically normal foetus at or beyond 10 weeks of gestation, confirmed by ultrasound; (2) a premature birth before 34 weeks due to pre-eclampsia, eclampsia or placental pathology indicative of infarction or (3) three or more consecutive unexplained spontaneous abortions before 10 weeks of gestation in the absence of maternal anatomical or hormonal abnormalities, with standard chromosomal analysis for both partners.[31217] APS diagnosis was confirmed in accordance with the Sydney modified international consensus, requiring persistently positive tests for LAC, ACA and/or anti-B2GPI, in addition to at least one clinical criterion as mentioned above.[18] Thrombophilia cases, with clinical features resembling mild manifestations of APS, required at least one positive laboratory finding accompanied by a history of RPL.[17] Patients with a positive ANA test underwent further profiling to identify specific autoimmune conditions; those meeting diagnostic criteria for systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), Sjögren’s syndrome or other severe autoimmune diseases were excluded from the study.

All patients included in the analysis had complete records for all required parameters and provided verbal consent for participation and for the use of their secondary data in research.

All 72 study participants received LMWH and LDA therapy. Patients with ANA positivity who did not meet criteria for SLE, Sjögren’s syndrome or other severe autoimmune conditions were additionally prescribed low-dose methylprednisolone, with a maximum daily dose of 8 mg.

Detailed ultrasound scans for structural anomalies were conducted by maternal-foetal medicine specialists at 11–13 and 20–22 weeks of gestation. Participants were subsequently monitored every 2 weeks or as recommended by their maternal-foetal medicine specialists.

First-trimester Doppler ultrasound examination

The evaluation of uterine blood flow to obtain the URa-RI at 8 weeks of gestation was initiated by performing an ultrasound scan of the uterus, adnexa and uterine cavity to confirm gestational sac regularity, visualisation of the embryo and normal foetal heart motion (110–170 beats per minute).[19] The radial artery was identified in the midsection of the myometrium along the path of the developing placenta, using colour Doppler vascular mapping. The gate was set at 2 mm, and auto-calculation was applied. A spectral Doppler image showing three consecutive sea wave patterns was acquired after the screen was filled with spectral images of blood flow this is shown in Figure 1.[71516]

The uterine artery was assessed at 11–13 weeks of gestation, following standard measurement guidelines by the International Society of Ultrasound in Obstetrics and Gynecology (ISUOG) and the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO), with gestational age confirmed by crown-rump length (CRL) measurement. The scanning procedure began with a suprapubic midsagittal section, followed by angling the probe to the left and right to allow colour Doppler mapping of the large vessels near the level of the internal cervical os. The gate was set at 2 mm and aligned to cover the entire vessel at an insonation angle of less than 30°. When three consecutive, similar spectral Doppler waveforms were observed, automatic measurement was conducted. The peak systolic velocity (PSV) exceeded 60 cm/s, and the spectral wave pattern matched the typical Doppler profile of uterine artery blood flow this is shown in Figure 2.[71020212223] The ultrasound machines used for this examination were the Voluson E10 and S8 models from GE Medical Systems.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Chi-square tests were conducted for each parameter with nominal categorisation. A multivariate logistic regression model was employed, adjusting for age, body mass index (BMI), number of previous pregnancy losses, etiopathology and ANA test positivity. The URa-RI, uterine artery pulsatility index (Ut-PI) and uterine artery pre-diastolic notching (Ut-notch) were categorised and analysed as primary outcomes. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for interpretation.

A power simulation model was utilised to assess the effect of sample size on statistical power, with power evaluated across different sample sizes. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Data were reported as mean ± standard deviation or as proportions. Parametric analysis was performed using the Student’s t-test to compare mean URa-RI and Ut-PI values between successful pregnancies and adverse events, as the data demonstrated homogeneity and normal distribution.

Based on the study by Bao et al., a URa-RI cut-off point was defined, with values <0.45 considered normal and values ≥0.45 considered high.[16] According to the ISUOG and FIGO guidelines, pre-diastolic notching of the uterine artery and a Ut-PI above the 90th percentile were classified as abnormal.[7202123]

Successful pregnancy was defined as a pregnancy resulting in a take-home baby, specifically a healthy infant surviving until 1 month post-partum. Adverse events were defined as pregnancy complications including early-onset pre-eclampsia, foetal growth restriction and prematurity.

RESULTS

A total of 72 subjects met the inclusion criteria and were followed until the neonatal period, achieving either a surviving baby or experiencing another miscarriage or pregnancy loss. Among these, 55 subjects (76.4%) had successful pregnancies, resulting in a healthy neonate. Unfavourable outcomes occurred in 17 pregnancies, comprising 14 miscarriages, 1 foetal death at 29 weeks of gestation and 2 neonatal deaths. Uterine artery measurements were performed on the remaining 58 patients who did not experience a miscarriage. Adverse events, including early-onset pre-eclampsia, foetal growth restriction and prematurity, were analysed in these 58 subjects. Adverse events were observed in 18 subjects (31.1%), with 66.7% experiencing all three complications concurrently.

Maternal characteristics, including age, BMI, number of miscarriages, etiopathology and ANA test results, did not significantly differ between successful and unsuccessful pregnancies [Table 1]. The mean maternal age was 32–33 years, with 86% of subjects under 40 years old. Fifty per cent of participants were of normal weight, 18% were underweight and 32% were overweight or obese. Seventy-two per cent of subjects had fewer than five prior pregnancy losses, while the remaining 28% had five or more. Thrombophilia was the most common cause of RPL, affecting 75% of the subjects, while the remaining 25% were diagnosed with APS. ANA tests were positive in one-third of all subjects; however, ANA positivity was not associated with successful pregnancy outcomes (P = 0.695) or adverse events (P = 0.703). Advanced maternal age (≥40 years) did not significantly increase the risk of unsuccessful pregnancy (P = 0.772) or adverse events (P = 0.923). Although the mean BMI was higher in the successful pregnancy group, the difference was not statistically significant (23.95 ± 6.01 vs. 21.24 ± 4.2, P = 0.089), nor was there any significant impact on adverse events (P = 0.195). The number of previous pregnancy losses approached significance for predicting adverse events (P = 0.053), with subjects having a history of ≥5 miscarriages showing a lower risk of adverse events (OR = 0.42; 95% CI = 0.32–5.13). Thrombophilia was associated with a greater proportion of adverse events compared to APS (37.3% vs. 23.4%), though this finding was not statistically significant (P = 0.207).

The primary findings of this study concerned the comparison of blood flow in the largest and smallest measurable vessels in the uterus to assess the effectiveness of management for clotting disorders, as detailed in Table 2. The URa-RI was significantly lower in successful pregnancies (0.38 ± 0.04) compared to unsuccessful ones (0.50 ± 0.09, P < 0.0001). A high resistance index in the uterine radial artery (URa-RI >0.45) was associated with a 16-fold increase in the risk of pregnancy loss (OR = 16.4; 95% CI = 4.4–61). Additionally, adverse events were associated with higher vascular flow resistance in the uterine radial artery. Subjects with adverse events had a significantly higher URa-RI (P = 0.023; P < 0.05), and those with a URa-RI ≥0.45 had approximately three times the risk of adverse events during pregnancy compared to those with lower indices.

In addition to URa-RI, the uterine artery pulsatility index (Ut-PI) and uterine artery pre-diastolic notching (Ut-notch) demonstrated a stronger association with adverse pregnancy outcomes (P < 0.0001). Subjects with elevated Ut-PI or Ut-notch were at increased risk for adverse events, with ORs of 19.4 (95% CI = 4.2–51.6) and 8.1 (95% CI = 3.12–20.5), respectively. Furthermore, subjects who experienced adverse events had a significantly higher mean Ut-PI compared to those without adverse events (2.48 ± 0.55 vs. 1.45 ± 0.38, P < 0.0001).

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrated that the URa-RI at 8 weeks of gestation may be a valuable parameter for evaluating RPL associated with thrombophilia and APS, with or without an immuno-inflammatory response. Our findings revealed that mean URa-RI was significantly higher in unsuccessful pregnancies, supporting prior research by Bao et al., who identified a similar relationship.[16] Bao et al. also established 0.45 as an optimal cut-off for URa-RI at 8 weeks, yielding 83% sensitivity and 87% specificity, with a high negative predictive value (97%). This cut-off point (URa-RI <0.45) may serve as a useful threshold in assessing the likelihood of successful pregnancy outcomes, as cases below this threshold generally avoided miscarriage. Conversely, elevated resistance (URa-RI ≥0.45) was associated with an increased risk of miscarriage (OR = 49.68; 95% CI = 8.01–307.95; P < 0.0001).[16] Our study similarly indicated that a low resistance index significantly enhanced the probability of pregnancy survival, increasing the odds of avoiding miscarriage more than 16-fold.

Placental trophoblast cells naturally invade the inner third of the myometrium and connect with the maternal spiral arteries.[24] Placentation typically occurs in two phases: the first wave from 8 to 10 weeks of gestation and the second wave from 18 to 20 weeks.[2526] Severe failure of the first wave often results in embryonic demise, manifesting clinically as miscarriage.[2728] Poor vascularisation, indicated by high resistance in the uterine radial artery at 8 weeks, likely corresponds to an elevated risk of miscarriage due to compromised early placental development. Partial placentation failure, by contrast, may result in lower CRL and elevated resistance in the uterine artery, which can be detected by Doppler ultrasound at 11–13 weeks and is associated with complications such as pre-eclampsia and foetal growth restriction.[2930]

This study further corroborates these findings, demonstrating a significant increase in adverse events when the Ut-PI is elevated and pre-diastolic notching is observed. High Ut-PI and Ut-notch values were associated with a 19-fold and 8-fold increase in risk of adverse events, respectively. Patients exhibiting these findings may require close monitoring and potential management adjustments. Interestingly, the URa-RI at 8 weeks also showed predictive value for adverse events, albeit to a lesser extent than Ut-PI or Ut-notch. Lower resistance in the radial artery was associated with nearly a threefold increase in the likelihood of an event-free pregnancy (OR = 2.9; 95% CI = 1.19–1.54). There was a modest but significant difference in mean URa-RI between pregnancies with and without adverse events (P = 0.023; P < 0.05). Favourable uterine vascularisation observed early in pregnancy, as indicated by low URa-RI on Doppler, may support robust placental development during the first wave (8–10 weeks of gestation) and subsequently facilitate optimal placentation in the second wave.[8] This favourable early vascular profile corresponds with a normal uterine artery Doppler pattern, characterised by a low Ut-PI and absence of notching, and is associated with a reduced risk of adverse outcomes.

Additionally, our analysis of maternal characteristics, including age, BMI, number of pregnancy losses, etiopathology and ANA positivity, revealed no significant differences between successful and unsuccessful pregnancies or between pregnancies with and without adverse events. These findings enhance the validity of our results, as confounding factors did not significantly impact the outcomes. However, the sample size in this study was relatively small, which limits our ability to determine the relationship between maternal characteristics and pregnancy outcomes definitively. Therefore, we cannot conclusively rule out the influence of these factors, as previous studies have reported their association with RPL.[3132] Notably, the number of prior pregnancy losses approached statistical significance as a predictor of adverse outcomes (P = 0.061; OR = 0.21; 95% CI = 0.11–1.12), suggesting a potential fivefold increase in the risk of further losses in patients with a history of ≥5 pregnancy losses, even with prompt management.

CONCLUSION

This study provides evidence to support the use of first-trimester Doppler ultrasound evaluation in the management of RPL, particularly in cases associated with thrombophilia and APS, with or without an immuno-inflammatory response. By assessing the URa-RI at 8 weeks and both the uterine artery pulsatility index (Ut-PI) and pre-diastolic notching (Ut-notch) at 11–13 weeks, clinicians may better predict the likelihood of a successful pregnancy outcome, including achieving a take-home baby and minimising adverse events. Further studies are needed to provide more robust evidence, which could potentially lead to the establishment of these evaluations as an international standard or guideline.
