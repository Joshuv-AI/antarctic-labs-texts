Protocol for longitudinal two-photon calcium imaging and holographic optogenetic manipulation to investigate memory in mice
Authors: Huaiqin Guo, He Yang, Feng Su, Yuzhi Zhu, Qingyi Tang, Rui Cao, Qiao Wang, Siyi Tong, Shaoli Wang, Chen Zhang, Wei Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHere, we present a protocol for longitudinal two-photon calcium imaging and holographic optogenetic manipulation to investigate memory in mice. We describe chronic cranial window implantation, stereotaxic viral delivery, and head-fixed contextual fear conditioning synchronized with imaging acquisition and optical stimulation of identified neurons. We further detail procedures for image data processing pipelines for cross-session registration and longitudinal tracking of the same neuronal population. This protocol enables simultaneous neuronal activity recording, behavioral monitoring, and causal interrogation of neuronal ensembles.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Wang et al.1

Before you begin

This protocol describes long-term two-photon calcium imaging combined with holographic optogenetic manipulation in head-fixed mice undergoing contextual fear conditioning training and remote memory retrieval. Foundational methods for chronic cranial window implantation and two-photon imaging have been established in prior work.2,3 Our workflow extends these methods to enable tracking and manipulation of identified neuronal ensembles across learning and later retrieval for up to 63 days post-training while maintaining single-cell resolution.

Remote memory refers to memory retrieved weeks to months after acquisition and is widely proposed to involve systems-level reorganization of neuronal ensembles.4,5,6 A major technical challenge in investigating this process is the ability to identify and track the same neurons over extended time periods while animals perform behaviorally relevant tasks. Two-photon calcium imaging has therefore emerged as a powerful approach for such studies, particularly owing to its precise spatial resolution, which permits repeated tracking of a relatively stable neuronal population across extended periods.7 Furthermore, it supports longitudinal registration at single-cell resolution, providing a robust foundation for subsequent optogenetic targeting and manipulation of defined neurons.7,8,9

While conventional benchtop two-photon imaging requires head fixation, freely moving fear-conditioning paradigms are not directly compatible with this approach. Although two-photon Miniscopes enable calcium imaging in freely moving animals, their integration with precise three-dimensional holographic optogenetic stimulation remains technically challenging. This protocol therefore employs a head-fixed fear-conditioning design10,11 to ensure stable optical access, reliable longitudinal field relocation, and synchronized behavioral recordings, imaging, and optogenetic manipulation. Context delivery, nociceptive stimulation, piezoelectric behavioral monitoring, imaging acquisition, and holographic optogenetic stimulation12 are integrated within a single platform. This configuration allows synchronized measurement of neural activity and behavior during both memory encoding and remote retrieval.

As a special note, this workflow below is originally developed for the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) during remote memory retrieval, but it is adaptable to other cortical regions accessible to two-photon imaging. Thus, it is essential to define the target brain region and appropriate imaging depth for longitudinal tracking according to experimental requirements. The protocol assumes stable expression of a genetically encoded calcium indicator (e.g., GCaMP6f) and, when required, an excitatory or inhibitory opsin for holographic stimulation. Successful implementation depends on sustained cranial window optical clarity, robust transgene expression, sufficient habituation to head fixation, and accurate longitudinal registration of the imaging field across sessions.

Innovation

This protocol integrates head-fixed contextual fear conditioning with longitudinal two-photon calcium imaging, enabling repeated tracking of the same neuronal population from pre-training baseline to remote memory retrieval. By combining behavioral readout, context delivery, nociceptive stimulation, two-photon calcium imaging, and holographic optogenetic manipulation within a single synchronized platform, it allows precise alignment of neural activity, behavioral state, and experimental perturbation across time. In addition, three-dimensional holographic optogenetics enables causal interrogation of identified neuronal ensembles across time, making the protocol suitable for investigating ensemble dynamics and circuit mechanisms during remote memory retrieval.

Preparation of glass coverslip for cranial window

Timing: 10 min

Timing: 10 min

This procedure describes the preparation of a custom-cut glass coverslip for cranial window implantation. Commercial preassembled glass plugs, typically circular coverslips pre-mounted onto a supporting glass ring can also be used when craniotomy size is standardized.1.Cut a circular glass coverslip into an approximately 5 × 5 mm2 square segment with surgical scissors.Note: We recommend the customized commercial preassembled coverslip as the preferred option. This coverslip consists of a 5-mm-diameter circular glass coverslip pre-mounted onto a glass ring with a 6-mm outer diameter and 4-mm inner diameters.2.Smooth and refine the edges with a fine file until the coverslip slightly exceeds the intended craniotomy size.Note: For a manually prepared square coverslip, polish the glass until it matches the craniotomy size or slightly overlaps the surrounding skull surface. This helps ensure complete sealing of the cranial window while avoiding excessive pressure on the exposed dura mater. For the preassembled commercial cranial window coverslip, the inner glass disk should fit within the craniotomy and gently cover the exposed dura, whereas the outer supporting ring should rest on the surrounding skull surface to ensure stable positioning and complete sealing.Note: Use the proximal portion of the scissors and apply slow, steady pressure to avoid uncontrolled cracking. Apply uniform forces while holding the glass to avoid uneven stress distribution and fracture. After refinement, please check the coverslip for any fractures or microcracks, as fractures may develop or propagate during later longitudinal imaging.

Cranial window implantation, headplate fixation, and virus injection

Timing: 45 min

Timing: 45 min

This protocol describes location of the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) for virus injection and chronic cranial window implantation. Detailed procedures of opening the cranial window, implanting glass coverslip, fixing the headplate and injecting virus are shown below (Methods Video S1).11.Leveling the skull by aligning bregma and lambda in the same horizontal plane with the stereotaxic micromanipulator.a.Lower the micropipette tip until it gently touches the bregma point, defined as the intersection point of the coronal and sagittal skull sutures, and set all coordinates at zero.b.Move the pipette tip to the lambda position and lower it to the skull surface again. Adjust the nose bar until dorsal-ventral (DV) values is 0 ± 0.02 mm.c.Check lateral tilt by measuring the DV coordinates at symmetrical points on the left and right sides of the skull. Adjust the ear bars if needed and proceed only when the skull is level along both the anterior–posterior (AP) and medial–lateral (ML) axes.12.Locate the ACC region using standard stereotaxic coordinates AP 0.26 mm, ML -0.35 mm, and DV 0.3 mm and mark it as the injection site.Note: Verify coordinates against standard stereotaxic atlas and adjust them according to strain-specific anatomical variation if necessary.13.Mark a 5 × 5 mm2 square centered on the targeted injection site.Note: We use a sterile 5 × 5 mm2 template or a calibrated ruler as a visual reference to manually outline an approximately square window on the skull surface. Please confirm that the target site is positioned near the center of the marked area and middle artery should be included in the area before drilling.14.Thin the skull along the marked perimeter using a microdrill and carefully remove the bone flap without damaging the dura (Figures 1C and 2B).15.Gently rinse exposed cortex with sterile saline and control minor bleeding with the syringe prior to viral injection.Note: When optogenetic manipulation is required, load the micropipette with a 1:1 mixture of rAAV2/9-hSyn-GCaMP6f-WPRE and an opsin-expressing viral vector, with 500 nL of each viral vectors and a total injection volume of 1000 nL. Expected outcomes

When this protocol is successfully executed, the cranial window remains optically clear for at least 12 weeks, with stable vascular landmarks visible across longitudinal imaging sessions (Figures 5A, 5B, and S1A). This stability permits reliable relocation of the same imaging field and repeated two-photon recording of the same neuronal population over extended periods. We use stable vascular landmarks as primary reference for the imaging field relocation across sessions (Figures 5B and S1B), supplemented by characteristic neuronal patterns to confirm imaging depth and enable long-term tracking of the same field of view (Figures 5C and S1C).

ΔF/F traces can be reliably extracted and calculated from identified neurons across sessions, enabling longitudinal analysis of neuronal activity dynamics throughout the experimental timeline. The integrated head-fixed platform also allows synchronized acquisition of behavioral and neural data, such that piezo-derived fidgeting events can be temporally aligned with visually identifiable behavior, stimulus delivery timing, and calcium fluorescence signals (Figure 3C).

In addition, holographic stimulation or inhibition enables causal interrogation of selected neurons within the imaging field. With appropriate calibration and properly optimized stimulation parameters, targeted neurons can show rapid stimulus-locked calcium responses with limited off-target activation of neighboring neurons (Figure 4E).

Limitations

This protocol requires invasive cranial window surgery and extended postoperative maintenance. Successful longitudinal tracking critically depends on sustained optical clarity of the cranial window and consistent headplate geometry throughout the experimental sessions. As a result, the workflow is highly sensitive to surgical quality and postoperative care. Long-term maintenance of a clear and stable cranial window suitable for repeated imaging remains technically challenging.

In addition, inter-animal and inter-session variability in viral expression efficiency, environmental conditions, and animal behavioral state may affect stimulation efficacy and experimental reproducibility. Accordingly, holographic optogenetic stimulation parameters, including stimulation duration, ROI size, and laser power, generally require empirical optimization for each experimental batch prior to formal testing.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Bleeding and lesions during craniotomy (related to Steps 12–14).

Damage to dural or cortical vessels during drilling may result in bleeding, which can compromise imaging quality and cause neuronal damage. Persistent postoperative bleeding may also induce inflammation under the cranial window and reduce long-term window stability.

Potential solution

•Perform drilling with gentle, controlled pressure using a shallow approach angle to minimize mechanical or thermal damage to surrounding tissue.•Thin the skull evenly around the craniotomy margin and reduce drilling force as the bone becomes thinner.•Control intraoperative bleeding immediately using sterile saline irrigation and application of a hemostatic sponge to the bleeding site.•In the event of post-operative hemorrhage, administer dexamethasone sodium phosphate (2 mg/kg, subcutaneously) and ceftriaxone sodium (200 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) to mitigate post-operative inflammation and reduce infection risk.

Problem 2

Cloudy cranial window after surgery (related to Steps 10–22).

Post-operative complications, including the formation of opaque tissue layers and heterotopic bone deposition, may develop within several days following cranial window implantation, potentially compromising optical clarity and imaging quality. These complications typically result from incomplete removal of meningeal and connective tissue, post-operative inflammation, intraoperative or post-operative hemorrhage, or inadequate coverslip sealing. Such optical obstruction substantially impairs the reliability and consistency of longitudinal two-photon imaging and may necessitate window replacement or removal of obstructing tissue.

Potential solution

•Proper postoperative care. As steps 19–22, inject dexamethasone sodium phosphate subcutaneously (2 mg/kg) and ceftriaxone sodium intraperitoneally (200 mg/kg) daily for a week. Also inject ceftriaxone sodium daily for 12 weeks during the entire experiment to prevent inflammation. Continuing anti-infection care helps avoid infection within the cranial window.•Use Kwik-Sil, a silicone elastomer for sealing the gap between the glass coverslip and the skull. Kwik-Sil provides a more stable and hermetic seal which can reduce the risk of infection.•Sterilization of surgical instruments and using sterile reagents. Surgical instruments may be sterilized through multiple methods: autoclaving at high pressure and temperature, alcohol immersion (70% ethanol), and ultraviolet (UV) chamber exposure.•Adequate cleaning of connective tissue on the skull. Ensure thoroughly and completely removal of meningeal and connective tissue from the skull surface. Use hydrogen peroxide (3% H2O2) irrigation to remove residual connective tissue and bone debris from the surgical site, which facilitates visualization and reduces the risk of osteogenic complications. Following H2O2 treatment, irrigate the cranial window area extensively with sterile normal saline (0.9% NaCl) to remove residual peroxide and minimize post-operative inflammation and heterotopic bone formation.•Ensure complete and hermetic sealing of the craniotomy by positioning the glass coverslip to achieve firm, continuous apposition with the entire craniotomy margin and surrounding skull.•Maintaining a sterile and clean housing environment for post-operative mice substantially reduces the risk of infection at the cranial window site and associated implant-related complications.

Problem 3

Weak calcium signals during imaging (related to Steps 15–19 and 30–38).

Sometimes, the fluorescence may be weak during two-photon imaging or even absent in the target imaging region. It can result from insufficient viral expression, inaccurate injection coordinates or reflux of viral solution along the pipette track. Weak fluorescence reduces signal-to-noise ratio and makes both imaging and stimulation unreliable.

Potential solution

•Use high-quality virus with verified titer and avoid repeated freeze-thaw cycles.•Reconfirm stereotaxic coordinates for the target region before surgery. Apply a staining step of the target region before formal surgery.•Lower or upper the pipette slowly and inject at a low constant rate to reduce tissue damage and reflux.•Hold the pipette in place for an extended period after injection (about 10 min), and then retract slowly. This step is essential for reducing backflow.•Allow sufficient time for stable expression before behavioral training and imaging. For optimal imaging quality and experimental reliability, a post-infection expression period of 3–4 weeks is recommended rather than earlier timepoints, as this duration permits sufficient fluorescent protein maturation, neuronal integration of viral constructs, and stabilization of neuronal labeling.

Problem 4

The same imaging field cannot be relocated across days (related to Steps 30–38).

Failure to precisely relocate the same view may result from inadequate documentation of the original imaging field or insufficient use of vascular landmarks. Small differences in imaging depth or angle, together with gradual deterioration of window clarity, can further increase this difficulty.

Potential solution

•Acquire and save low-magnification vascular reference images during the first session before collecting high-magnification neuronal data.•Record high-resolution reference images at the designated imaging depth and adjacent depths (at least 3) for every imaging session, using the first session as the fixed anatomical reference.•When relocating to previously imaged fields, establish alignment sequentially by first matched major surface vasculature, then intermediate vessel branches, and finally local neuronal patterns.•Maintain consistent objective angle and optical axis orientation across all imaging sessions.•If the exact field of view cannot be reliably matched with high confidence, exclude those cells from longitudinal analyses rather than including uncertain or forced matches.

Problem 5

Drift of imaging views during imaging (related to Steps 19 and 36–40).

During two-photon imaging, respiratory motion and involuntary movement following head fixation can produce substantial axial and lateral drift of the imaging field, compromising image registration and longitudinal tracking reliability. Additionally, inadequate mechanical coupling between the headplate and skull can generate significant image drift independent of animal motion, resulting in loss of focal plane stability and degradation of quantitative imaging data.

Potential solution

•Thoroughly clean and dry the skull surface before fixing headplate to ensure stable attachment.•Ensure sufficient habituation on the imaging platform in advance to reduce stress-related movement.•Mild residual drift can be corrected during post hoc image processing using motion-correction procedures (Step 39), whereas datasets with excessive drift should be excluded from further analysis.

Problem 6

Inconsistent activation during sequential spiral stimulation (Related to Step 49).

Sequential spiral stimulation may fail to induce reliable calcium responses in target neurons because of inaccurate SLM-to-FOV registration, weak opsin expression, insufficient stimulation power, inappropriate ROI size, or poor cranial window clarity.

Potential solution

•Repeat fluorescent plate calibration to verify spatial registration between the stimulation pattern and imaging fields.•Confirm sufficient GCaMP6f and opsin expression before stimulation.•Adjust the stimulation ROI size according to soma diameter and gradually increase laser power within the empirically optimized safe range.•Re-align the imaging plane if lateral or axial drift occurs.•Exclude animals if reliable activation cannot be restored after calibration and parameter optimization.
