Post-traumatic growth in young and middle-aged patients with stroke: a qualitative systematic review and meta-synthesis
Authors: Shuyang Liu, Wei Zhou, Xiaorong Wu, Jie Ding, Yi Liu, Haiyan Shao, Xia Duan, Jinxia Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWith the increasing incidence of stroke among young and middle-aged individuals, addressing their psychological well-being has become a critical concern. Negative emotional states such as depression, anxiety, and conditions like post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) can hinder recovery and increase the risk of recurrence. Therefore, this study aims to conduct a meta-synthesis of qualitative research on post-traumatic growth (PTG) in this population, examining the factors that contribute to PTG, its developmental trajectory, and the coping mechanisms involved.MethodsA systematic search of 14 databases was conducted, with reference tracking, to ensure comprehensive literature retrieval. A total of 1,753 articles were initially identified. After removing duplicates and excluding articles that did not meet the criteria, 11 studies were included, involving 156 participants in total, with 5 studies conducted in China and the remaining from other regions. The review followed the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) approach: systematic search with predefined criteria, critical appraisal of study quality using the JBI Checklist (assessing rigor in data collection/analysis), and meta-synthesis via iterative theme development (coding, categorizing findings) and validation (cross-checking by reviewers) to derive integrated results.ResultsA total of 42 research findings were synthesized into 9 new categories, further grouped into 3 integrated results: (1) trauma and stress period, which includes physical, psychological, and social stress; (2) adjustment and reconstruction period, involving seeking support, cognitive processing, self-mobilization, and life reorganization; and (3) growth period, characterized by new possibilities and self-actualization.LimitationsThe limited time frame of the studies and the predominance of research from China (5 out of 11 included studies) may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Excluding non-English and non-Chinese studies introduces a potential language bias. Furthermore, as the review only included qualitative studies with generally small sample sizes (total 156 participants), the findings should be interpreted with caution.ConclusionThis meta-synthesis is the first to systematically synthesize qualitative evidence on PTG in young and middle-aged stroke patients, offering a novel three-stage framework (trauma and stress, adjustment and reconstruction, growth) that integrates physical, psychological, and social dimensions of recovery. By clarifying distinct emotional, cognitive, and behavioral features at each stage, this study advances existing research—previously fragmented in exploring PTG elements—by providing a holistic developmental trajectory. In clinical practice, this framework informs targeted interventions (e.g., stage-specific emotional support, cognitive guidance, and social reintegration strategies) to facilitate PTG and enhance patients’ reintegration into society.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03321-8.

Introduction

Global stroke incidence and burden

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), stroke is characterized by cerebrovascular damage resulting from various etiologies, leading to focal or global brain tissue injury and causing persistent clinical symptoms lasting for more than 24 h or resulting in fatality [1]. Stroke is a leading cause of disability, recurrence, and mortality worldwide, ranking third in disability and second in global mortality [2]. In France, it causes 140,000 hospitalizations annually [3]. In the U.S., it is the fifth leading cause of death, while in China, it is the leading cause of mortality. Despite advancements in diagnostic and treatment technologies, the incidence and mortality rates of stroke in high - income countries have only experienced a marginal decline [4, 5]. A study published in The Lancet in 2020 analyzed data from the Global Burden of Disease Study and reported that between 1990 and 2016, the age - standardized incidence rate of stroke in high - income countries decreased by 11.7 [6]. This reduction is attributed to various factors, including improved access to healthcare and lifestyle changes. Regarding diagnostic advancements, neuroimaging techniques such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and computed tomography (CT) angiography have significantly enhanced the accuracy of stroke diagnosis. For instance, CT angiography can rapidly identify the location and extent of blood vessel blockages in ischemic stroke patients, guiding timely intervention [7]. In terms of treatment, thrombolytic therapies like the use of tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) have been a cornerstone in the acute treatment of ischemic stroke. A meta - analysis in 2021 showed that intravenous tPA within 4.5 h of stroke onset reduced disability in patients [8]. However, despite these efforts, the mortality rate associated with stroke in high - income countries has only decreased slightly. The same Lancet study mentioned above indicated a 19.2% decline in the age - standardized mortality rate of stroke in high - income countries from 1990 to 2016 [6]. This slow progress suggests that there is still much room for improvement in stroke prevention, diagnosis, and treatment strategies in these regions. Meanwhile, low-and middle-income countries still experience high incidence and mortality [9]. Stroke can cause a variety of sequelae, including cognitive and physical impairments, fatigue, epilepsy, and psychological issues like depression, anxiety, and apathy [10]. Although traditionally seen as an elderly condition, stroke incidence is rising among younger and middle-aged adults, with approximately 25% of stroke patients under 65 years old [11, 12]. Recent evidence suggests that young patients with stroke have a substantially increased risk of early death compared with the general population [13].

Psychological health challenges in young and middle-aged stroke patients

Young and middle-aged individuals are typically in a critical stage of building their careers and families, assuming unique social roles and greater responsibilities compared to older adults. As a result, when stroke occurs at this stage, it can disrupt their life expectations, turning the condition into a significant disability during their most productive years. This disruption may lead to consequences such as unemployment, divorce, and the loss of family roles, significantly impacting their life trajectory and imposing a disproportionate economic burden [14]. This simultaneously engenders a range of psychological issues, such as depression and anxiety, and may even lead to PTSD [15]. Studies indicate that negative psychology not only impedes the rehabilitation process and clinical outcomes of patients but also elevates the risk of stroke recurrence [16]. Currently, available data indicate that the prevalence of post-stroke depression is as high as 33% [17], highlighting the need for increased attention to the emotional well-being of patients with stroke.

Positive impact and significance of post-traumatic growth (PTG)

Existing research has also found that patients with stroke may experience positive emotional changes and enhanced self-awareness during the recovery process through internal factors such as optimism, positivity, resilience, and self-identity. They also benefit from external factors like support and understanding from family members, friends, medical professionals, and colleagues. This phenomenon is known as PTG. Experiencing positive emotions can boost patients’ confidence in life while significantly reducing negative emotions. It also improves disease management skills by encouraging active participation in rehabilitation activities and fostering a stronger sense of identity [18]. The concept of PTG was first introduced by Tedeschi and Calhoun [19], describing the positive changes individuals experience following a significant traumatic event. These changes may include improved relationships, new possibilities, enhanced personal strength, spiritual growth, and a deeper appreciation of life. With the rise of positive psychology in the medical field, research on PTG has expanded. In cancer populations, PTG correlates with adaptive coping strategies and meaning-making processes during survivorship [20], for instance, identified PTG as a critical factor in psychological adjustment among breast cancer survivors, particularly in redefining life priorities post-treatment [21]. Among natural disaster survivors, PTG is closely linked to community support systems and collective resilience, with Norris et al. [22] highlighting that social cohesion significantly predicts PTG in disaster-affected populations, reducing the risk of prolonged psychological distress. In trauma victims such as those affected by accidents or violence, PTG often emerges through trauma memory integration and safety restoration [23, 24]. However, when contextualizing these findings within stroke research, critical gaps emerge. Unlike cancer (a progressive illness with structured treatment phases) or natural disasters (a sudden but collective trauma), stroke is a sudden, individual neurological event that disrupts physical function, cognitive abilities, and social roles simultaneously - particularly for young and middle-aged patients (18–65), who are often in peak career and family caregiving phases. Existing stroke-specific PTG research remains fragmented: it primarily focuses on older adults, where functional recovery may be framed within a context of aging rather than life disruption [25]; rarely explores how PTG unfolds dynamically alongside rehabilitation; and overlooks the unique stressors of younger populations, such as career interruption, altered family roles, and the tension between “premature” disability and ongoing life responsibilities. These gaps highlight that PTG in young and middle-aged stroke survivors cannot be fully inferred from other trauma contexts, as their PTG trajectories are shaped by a distinct interplay of physical recovery, social role renegotiation, and life-stage-specific expectations.

This study aims to synthesize qualitative research on PTG in young and middle-aged stroke patients, with the primary objective of developing a stage-specific framework that integrates three core components: the developmental trajectory of PTG, key influencing factors (including those unique to young and middle-aged populations), and effective coping strategies. The secondary objectives involve elaborating on the distinct emotional, cognitive, and behavioral characteristics of each PTG stage and their association with rehabilitation progress; identifying critical internal and external factors that shape PTG in this population, with a focus on age-specific stressors (e.g., career interruption, altered family roles); extracting evidence-based coping strategies that facilitate PTG; and proposing targeted clinical interventions derived from the integrated framework. This framework is intended to provide healthcare providers with stage-specific guidance for supporting PTG, ultimately aiding young and middle-aged stroke patients in psychosocial adaptation.

Aims

The aim of this study is to integrate qualitative research on PTG in young and middle-aged stroke patients, deeply explore the influencing factors, developmental stages, and coping strategies of PTG experienced by this group during stroke rehabilitation. Questions in this review: How do young and middle-aged stroke patients experience the developmental stages of PTG, and what coping mechanisms do they employ to navigate these stages?

Methods

Design

The meta-synthesis method was used to conduct a systematic review of qualitative studies, and results were reported using the Enhancing Transparency in Reporting the Synthesis of Qualitative Research (ENTREQ) [26] to allow readers to judge the reliability, credibility, and transferability of the article. This review used the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) [27] as the basis for reporting the review.

Search strategy

This review searched a total of 14 databases, including CINHAL, PubMed, Ovid, Elsevier, Science Direct, SinoMed, Medline, Cochrane Library, LWW, VIP, CNKI, Wanfang Database, Embase, and Web of Science, with references of included studies thoroughly traced to ensure the inclusion of important literature and gray literature supplemented via Google Scholar and OpenGray; search terms were categorized into four domains—Population (“Middle-aged” OR “Young adults” OR “Younger stroke patients” OR “18–65 years”), Condition (“Stroke” OR “Cerebrovascular accident” OR “Ischemic stroke” OR “Hemorrhagic stroke” OR “Brain infarction”), Phenomenon of interest (“Post-traumatic growth” OR “PTG” OR “Positive psychological change” OR “Resilience” OR “Coping strategies” OR “Psychological growth”), and Study design (“Qualitative research” OR “Interview” OR “Focus group” OR “Phenomenology” OR “Grounded theory”)—and combined using Boolean operators with the core formula: (Population terms) AND (Condition terms) AND (Phenomenon terms) AND (Study design terms), with PubMed serving as a representative example of the full search string: (“Middle-aged“[MeSH] OR “Young adults“[MeSH] OR “Younger stroke patients“[Title/Abstract] OR “18–65 years“[Title/Abstract]) AND (“Stroke“[MeSH] OR “Cerebrovascular accident“[Title/Abstract] OR “Ischemic stroke“[MeSH] OR “Hemorrhagic stroke“[MeSH]) AND (“Post-traumatic growth“[MeSH] OR “PTG“[Title/Abstract] OR “Resilience“[MeSH] OR “Coping strategies“[Title/Abstract]) AND (“Qualitative research“[MeSH] OR “Interview“[Title/Abstract] OR “Phenomenology“[MeSH]); the same Boolean logic was applied across all databases, with adjustments made to align with each platform’s specific syntax and indexing systems, and the search period spanned from the establishment of each database to April 2025, with a detailed search strategy for PubMed provided in Appendix 1 to ensure transparency and reproducibility.

Through a systematic and comprehensive search, 1,753 articles were initially identified. After removing duplicates using Endnote software, 1,341 articles remained. A preliminary screening of titles and abstracts excluded 956 articles, followed by full-text assessment which eliminated 374 additional studies—primarily due to misalignment with the research focus, non-qualitative designs, or non-Chinese / non-English language. Non-Chinese and non-English articles were excluded due to the research team’s limited linguistic expertise: accurate interpretation of qualitative data (e.g., culturally specific expressions of growth) requires direct language proficiency, and external translation risked losing contextual nuances, which would compromise the integrity of the synthesis. Ultimately, 11 studies were included for quality assessment. The literature selection process was conducted independently by two researchers, with cross-verification of results; a third researcher was consulted to resolve any discrepancies. Quality assessment

Two researchers with systematic training in evidence-based nursing evaluated the quality of the literature using the Australian JBI Centre for Evidence-Based Health Care Qualitative Research Quality Evaluation Criteria (2016 edition) [28]. To ensure consistency in application, they first underwent standardized training to align their understanding of the ten criteria, which cover dimensions such as the study’s philosophical basis, methodology, data collection methods, representativeness of participants, researchers’ reflexivity, ethical compliance, and the validity of conclusions. The evaluation results were categorized into four ratings—“Yes,” “No,” “Unclear,” and “Not applicable”—with corresponding scores of 2, 0, 1, and 0 points, respectively. A detailed operational manual was developed to guide scoring: for example, “ethical approval (Item ⑨)” was rated “Yes” if the study explicitly stated the approving committee and reference number (e.g., Liu et al. [29]), “Unclear” if only informed consent was mentioned without formal approval documentation (e.g., Murphy et al. [30]), and “No” if no ethical considerations were addressed. Both researchers independently assessed each study, with cross-checks conducted afterward. In cases of discrepancies (e.g., conflicting ratings on “methodological congruence”), they first reviewed the original text together to resolve differences through consensus. If disagreements persisted, a third researcher with over 5 years of experience in qualitative research appraisal was consulted to make a final decision, with all resolution processes documented for traceability. Studies were classified into levels A, B, or C based on their total scores (converted to percentages), with those scoring over 70% included to ensure quality. All included studies fell into levels A or B, with no level C studies retained. Data extraction and synthesis

At the beginning of the research, two researchers carefully reviewed 11 relevant literature sources and extracted key information, covering aspects such as the authors, regions, publication years, data collection methods, the number and characteristics of research subjects, the phenomena of interest, and research results. During the data extraction process, based on the theoretical model of Tedeschi and Calhoun [19] — specifically targeting descriptions of participants’ experiences, coping behaviors, and emotional shifts that aligned with the trauma and stress, adjustment and reconstruction, or growth phases. Notably, the conclusions from each original study were classified into three levels of certainty [39].Unequivocal (U): Conclusions directly supported by explicit descriptions in the article; Credible (C): Conclusions reasonably inferred or plausible based on the content; Unsupported (NS): Conclusions irrelevant to the article content or not reported. Two researchers independently rated the certainty of each conclusion, with discrepancies resolved through consultation with a third researcher to ensure reliability. Only findings rated as U or C were included in subsequent synthesis, ensuring the robustness of the 42 initial findings.

The synthesis of these 42 findings into 9 categories followed a systematic, iterative process rooted in JBI’ s meta-synthesis guidelines [40]. (1) Initial coding of raw data: Each U/C-rated finding (e.g., participant quotes, thematic statements) was labeled with descriptive codes capturing its core meaning. For example, a quote from a Chinese participant—“My mother lives with me, my children need to attend school, and I cannot fathom how challenging the future will be [29]” (rated U)—was coded as “anxiety about unfulfilled family responsibilities.”(2) Clustering into subthemes: Codes with semantic similarity were grouped into subthemes. For instance, U-rated codes like “anxiety about family responsibilities” [29], “fear of becoming a family burden [32] ”, and “worry over disrupted caregiving roles [34]” were merged into a subtheme labeled “familial role-related stress.” (3) Refining into categories: Subthemes were further integrated into broader categories based on their shared relevance to PTG stages. The subtheme “familial role-related stress,” along with others like “career disruption anxiety” (C-rated, from[35]) and “fear of recurrence” (U-rated, from[29]), were grouped into the overarching category “psychological stress” under the trauma and stress period. (4) Validation and consistency checks: All categories were reviewed iteratively by the research team to ensure alignment with the theoretical framework [19] and internal consistency. Discrepancies in categorization (e.g., debates over whether “peer support-seeking” belonged to “social support” or “cognitive adjustment”) were resolved through group discussion and reference to original study contexts, with final categories confirmed for clarity and relevance to PTG development.

This rigorous process—combining certainty assessment of original conclusions with systematic thematic synthesis—ensured that the 9 categories accurately reflected the core of the 42 initial findings while maintaining methodological rigor.

ConQual-Assessment of confidence of evidence

The ConQual system, developed by the JBI Center for Evidence-Based Health Care in 2014, is designed to assess and rate meta-synthesized bodies of evidence derived from qualitative studies [28]. It evaluates credibility (consistency between integrated results and supporting data) and dependability (quality of original studies) across three and five aspects, respectively, yielding ratings of high, medium, low, or very low. The ConQual scores for this review (presented in Table 4) rated all three synthesized findings (trauma and stress period, adjustment and reconstruction period, growth period) as “moderate,” with dependability downgraded due to methodological limitations in included studies—most scored suboptimally on “appropriateness of conduct,” aligning with gaps like unreported researcher positions, incomplete ethical documentation [30, 38], and uneven sampling [37]. This moderate rating means findings are credible (unequivocal with original data) but require caution: downgraded dependability weakens cross-context consistency, limiting transferability—especially given overrepresentation of Chinese studies. Future work should prioritize rigorous reporting of researcher reflexivity/ethics, expand geographic diversity, and standardize methodological assessments. Despite limitations, core PTG trajectories and influences remain robust for similar populations.

Results

The quality evaluation results of the 11 qualitative studies included in this review were generally between 85% and 90%, and the quality was in the upper-middle level. Unfortunately, none of the 11 studies described the researchers’ culture and values, and the other two studies failed to fully explain the impact of the researchers themselves on the research, or the impact of the research on the researchers [34, 35]. It was not clear whether the two articles were approved by the Ethics Committee, but they were included considering that other aspects of the articles met the ethical standards [30, 38]. Finally, one study failed to elucidate the nexus between philosophical underpinnings and research methodologies [12], while the other study solely comprised female participants, thus rendering the sample representation unbalanced [37]. The quality evaluation results of all studies were grade B, and no studies were excluded.

Eleven qualitative studies were reviewed: five from China [29, 31, 32, 33, 34], two from Australia [35, 41], and one each from the UK [12], Germany [38], Ireland [30], and Sweden [37]. Two studies used phenomenological methods [29, 41], three used grounded theory [30, 31, 38], and two used descriptive qualitative research [35, 37]. The remaining studies did not specify their qualitative research methods [12, 32, 33, 34]. The included studies included a total of 156 participants and covered a period from 2014 to 2022. Synthesized finding 1:trauma and stress period

Physical stress

Physical impairments (e.g., limb dysfunction, dysphagia, and language deficits) varied in manifestation across participants, particularly by stroke severity and gender. For patients with severe initial symptoms, such as unilateral paralysis, the fear of permanent disability was more acute: “When I first got sick, half of my body couldn’t move, I was afraid that I would be like this for the rest of my life” [31]. In contrast, those with milder symptoms (e.g., hand inflexibility) reported frustration with slow recovery: “Until now, my hand is still not flexible enough, and the recovery is particularly slow” [31]. Other survivors have reported sagging faces, impaired appearance, inflexibility in typing, extreme fatigue, blurred memory, reduced quality of life, unmet personal needs, and limited social activity due to physical impairment [35].

Psychological stress

Currently, scholars acknowledge that stroke can lead to significant psychological distress, encompassing emotional experiences ranging from vulnerability and sadness to potentially disabling conditions, such as depression and anxiety [42]. Young and middle-aged people are in a rising stage of career development, and the impaired working ability and psychological distress caused by stroke pose great challenges for them. Psychological distress showed variations across cultural contexts and life stages. In Chinese studies, familial responsibility emerged as a key stressor: “My mother lives with me, my children need to attend school, and I cannot fathom how challenging the future will be [29].” underscoring collectivist values that link personal stress to family burdens.

Younger patients (e.g., those in their 30s) often expressed confusion due to perceived “age mismatch” with stroke: “I have always felt that this disease is for the elderly, never understood, and I am still very skeptical about the diagnosis [31]”, whereas middle-aged patients (50–60 s) focused more on career disruption: “The driver has a lot of pressure and responsibility, and it is impossible not to be afraid when he suddenly collapses [29]” .

Gender also plays a role, particularly for working-age women, who often report stress tied to unmet expectations in both household and workplace roles. As one female survivor mentioned, “Since the stroke, I feel very tired every day, unable to participate in housework and work, and feel useless [37].”

Beyond these group-specific variations, there are shared stressors: Stroke rehabilitation is expensive and burdensome for families, leading patients to express anxiety about changing roles and reduced workability [41]; lack of knowledge about stroke causes negative emotions, as echoed by survivors [31]; and fear of recurrence is common, with one asking, “I heard the disease will come back, will it? I am afraid! [29].”

Social stress

Many survivors of stroke have lost friends and even their partners since the onset of the disease, leading to their shrinking social circle. In severe cases, social isolation and self-closure prevent them from participating in normal social activities [12]. Social stress manifested distinctly across cultural and gender lines. In Chinese contexts, social isolation was often tied to lost family roles: “I am eager to communicate with people, but no one came to my house. That’s bad [35].” while Western studies highlighted workplace stigma: “I tried to find a job, but a leader told me frankly that he was afraid that I would relapse [35].”

Female participants reported heightened social role strain, as one working-age woman noted: “Since the stroke, I feel useless—unable to participate in housework and work [37]”, reflecting gendered expectations of caregiving and productivity that exacerbate social stress. In workplace settings, stroke symptoms (e.g., speech disorders, fatigue) further exacerbate social stress—this was particularly noted in Western studies, where workplace stigma intertwined with functional limitations to hinder employment [35].

Synthesized finding 2: adjustment and reconstruction period

Seek support

Over time, young and middle-aged stroke survivors gradually emerge from initial stress and adapt to new life through diverse strategies, with active pursuit of multi-dimensional social support being key—though the sources and forms of support vary by age, cultural context, and gender. Younger survivors tend to leverage digital resources for information support, as one noted: “Disease-related knowledge and other things can be found on the internet, and people send some links to help can reduce a little burden [32].” reflecting their familiarity with online tools. In contrast, middle-aged survivors often prioritize familial support, as captured by a participant whose family “accepted him and provided unwavering encouragement,” giving him “courage to triumph over the disease [32] —a pattern more pronounced in Chinese contexts, where collectivist values emphasize family as a core support system.

Gender differences also emerge: female survivors frequently highlight emotional support from friends (“gratitude to friends who visited and comforted me [32]”), while male participants more often reference practical medical support. For example, one male survivor emphasized: “The physiotherapist helped me a lot. taught me how to stand with balance, and I recovered quickly [34].” aligning with gendered tendencies to seek instrumental vs. emotional support.

Across groups, medical staff play a critical role, particularly in addressing helplessness: “I sometimes feel helpless; why me? But the medics told me it was not my fault, that I was doing well, and that they had lifted my mood [34],” with nurses facilitating peer communication to reinforce support networks—a strategy that resonates more strongly with survivors lacking strong family ties [34].

Cognitive processing

Survivors’ cognitive adjustments to post-stroke life vary by age, gender, and life experience, shaping how they reframe challenges. Younger survivors (e.g., in their 30s) often adopt flexible problem-solving shifts, moving from internalizing struggles to active communication: “In the past, I kept things in my heart, but now I’ve learned the importance of communication [32],” reflecting adaptability to new coping styles. Middle-aged survivors, drawing on life experience, tend to use comparative framing to manage distress: “Now there are many people with this disease; some can’t even walk—we are relatively lucky [31],” a strategy rooted in broader life perspective.

Gender influences expression: female participants more frequently mention “sharing experiences to process emotions,” while male survivors often describe internalized reflection before acting (“You need to cheer yourself up and think about relieving symptoms rather than regretting [29]”, highlighting differing paths to cognitive acceptance.

Self-mobilization

Self-mobilization—timely adjustment of negative emotions and cultivation of positive attitudes—varies by occupation, age, and gender, reflecting differing resources for resilience. Professionals with healthcare backgrounds (e.g., nurses) exhibit quicker self-regulation: “I am a nurse myself, I’ve seen similar things, so I first need to adjust [32],” leveraging professional knowledge to normalize their experience. Younger survivors, driven by future-oriented goals, often display stronger motivation: “I tell myself every day, be optimistic, never give up! I’ll get better [33],” while middle-aged survivors may balance realism with resolve, focusing on incremental progress. Gender nuances appear in focus: women more often emphasize emotional self-compassion (“learning to ask for help [37]” as a form of self-care), while men tend to frame mobilization as action (“focusing on what I can do, not what I can’t”).

Reorganize one’s life

Reconstructing life involves role updates, strategy shifts, and future planning, with variations tied to age, gender, and cultural values. Age shapes priorities: younger survivors often aim for rapid reintegration into work to avoid familial burden: “After discharge, I plan to apply for a new job, not to become a burden [29],” while middle-aged survivors focus on redefining family roles: “My family was full of patients—I didn’t want to treat them, but I thought I couldn’t fall, so I didn’t give up [32],” balancing caregiving with self-recovery.

Gender influences role focus: women more frequently reference adjusting household responsibilities (“learning to delegate chores I can’t manage”), while men emphasize professional repositioning (“rethinking workplace roles to match my current abilities [12]”). Cultural context amplifies family-centric goals among Chinese participants, as seen in their emphasis on “not burdening loved ones,” whereas Western studies (less represented here) highlight individual independence—a contrast reflecting broader value systems. Practical strategies like scheduling workloads and using memos are universal, but their purpose varies: younger survivors use them to regain productivity, while older ones prioritize consistency in daily life [37].

This layered analysis reveals that adjustment strategies are not uniform but shaped by age, gender, culture, and personal background—insights that inform tailored support to foster resilience across diverse survivors.

Synthesized finding 3: growth period

New possibilities

After self-adjustment and multi-dimensional social support, young and middle-aged stroke survivors gradually experience growth, with “new possibilities” manifesting differently across age, gender, and life stage. Age differences shape specific hopes: Younger survivors (e.g., those in their 30s) often focus on future-oriented life milestones, as one noted, “After leaving the hospital, I thought about having another child [29]”—reflecting a desire to reclaim disrupted life plans. Middle-aged survivors, by contrast, tend to prioritize quality of life and social reintegration: “After I leave the hospital, I often go to the square to dance [31]” using community activities to rebuild daily routines. Gender nuances appear in health behaviors: Female survivors more frequently emphasize holistic lifestyle changes, such as “changing past high-salt and high-fat habits, quitting smoking and alcohol, and living more lovingly [29],” aligning with gendered social expectations of nurturing self and family. Male survivors, while also adopting healthy habits, may frame these changes as “regaining control” over their bodies—a subtle difference in motivation.

Self-actualization

Self-actualization—marked by strengthened relationships, personal growth, and role reclamation—varies by age, gender, and social role, reflecting diverse paths to post-traumatic growth. Interpersonal growth differs by family role: Parents often highlight deepened parent-child bonds: “My daughter cares more about me than I thought [29],” whereas spouses emphasize rediscovered partnership: “My husband stayed with me the whole time. He has never spent so much time together before [29].” Gender plays a role here: women more frequently describe emotional intimacy gains, while men focus on practical support (e.g., “my wife helped me rebuild daily routines”).

Altruism and gratitude are universal but context-specific: Survivors of all ages express compassion for others, as seen in one’s account: “Met a neighbor with worse symptoms—I told him about my rehabilitation experience to help [29].” However, middle-aged survivors often link gratitude to community roles (e.g., “thanking volunteers who supported my recovery [31]”), while younger ones tie it to peer connections (“grateful to friends who pushed me to keep exercising”).

Life perspective and role reclamation vary by age and career: Younger survivors, driven by ambition, often set new goals to “prove I can still contribute”: “Established new life goals and felt highly motivated [36].” Middle-aged survivors, meanwhile, prioritize restoring family roles, with many viewing “resuming caretaking duties at home” as the ultimate marker of recovery: “Restarting interrupted family life feels like winning [38].” Across groups, trauma fostered increased resilience: “Survivors reported more personal strength, resilience, and maturity [29]”—a growth that manifests as “taking life one step at a time” for older survivors, and “chasing bigger dreams” for younger ones.

Discussion

Our study found that young and middle-aged stroke survivors often experience a process of PTG as they gradually develop positive emotions, influenced by both internal and external factors, after the trauma caused by their condition. The results of our research suggest that PTG typically unfolds in three key stages: (1) Trauma and Stress Period: This initial stage is characterized by the physical, psychological, and social stresses that stroke patients endure. It is a time of intense emotional upheaval, often marked by feelings of anxiety, depression, and helplessness. While these emotions are distressing, they play a crucial role in the development of PTG by prompting patients to reevaluate their lives and values, setting the stage for personal growth. (2) Adjustment and Reconstruction Period: In this stage, patients begin to accept their condition and adapt to the challenges of a new lifestyle. Interaction with others and external support become essential in helping them find the path to recovery. As patients work through these adjustments, they often experience a shift in their understanding of life’s meaning and their self-worth, coupled with emotional regulation and cognitive restructuring. This stage marks the beginning of emotional and psychological healing. (3) Growth Period: After enduring trauma and undergoing significant adjustment, patients reach a more mature psychological state. In this final stage, individuals are able to achieve growth beyond the pain of their initial trauma. This growth is often reflected in enhanced self-efficacy, greater life satisfaction, and more optimistic expectations for the future. This trajectory of PTG follows a dynamic developmental process that aligns closely with the findings of previous studies. For instance, Wang et al. [43] identified a similar sequence of phases in individuals recovering from accidental trauma, categorizing them as ‘destruction phase,’ ‘acceptance phase,’ and ‘reconstruction and integration phase.’ Similarly, Gao et al. [44] outlined the PTG trajectory for women who experienced perinatal loss, which they divided into ‘stress phase,’ ‘acceptance phase,’ and ‘growth phase.’ Importantly, the stages of PTG are not strictly linear but instead represent an overlapping and cyclical process. As highlighted in the study by Liu et al. [31], the transition from one stage to the next can be fluid, with some individuals bypassing certain stages or entering the growth phase directly. Across all patients, the general trend is that negative emotions precede periods of adjustment and growth. Our findings also support the idea that PTG is a dynamic process, in which patients often experience multiple emotional fluctuations and cognitive shifts before achieving lasting growth through ongoing cognitive reevaluation and restructuring. Another key finding from our study is the important role of social support in promoting PTG. During the adjustment and reconstruction phase, high-quality social support helps patients rebuild their self-perception and redefine their life goals. This finding is consistent with previous research, which has demonstrated a positive, albeit small, correlation between PTG and social support [45]. Social support influences PTG through distinct mechanisms [46, 47], each relevant to the adjustment and growth stages. Emotionally, the adjustment stage often brings confusion, loneliness, and anxiety due to the stroke’s impacts. Companionship and listening from family and friends make patients feel cared for, alleviating negative emotions and laying an emotional foundation for psychological reconstruction. A stable emotional state is essential for patients to progress to the growth stage with a positive mindset, as emotional comfort in adjustment paves the way for embracing growth challenges. Cognitively, in the adjustment stage, patients may be confined by fear and uncertainty. Interactions like sharing in online support groups expose them to diverse viewpoints and coping strategies, expanding their horizons and breaking cognitive limits [32]. In the growth stage, these new insights prompt self-examination and future planning. For example, others’ adaptation stories can inspire new goals and proactive recovery steps. This cognitive growth and self-reflection, facilitated by social support, are key to transitioning from adjustment to growth. In conclusion, establishing a robust social support network and offering emotional and practical support is vital for cultivating trauma survivors’ growth mindsets. Moreover, social support is essential for stroke survivors’ health-related quality of life, enhancing mental health, work efficiency, and social reintegration. It helps patients through the adjustment phase and empowers them to achieve positive growth and better adapt to post-stroke life [47].

Based on previous studies and our own research, it is clear that PTG is a complex, non-linear process influenced by multiple factors [31]. In this study, the factors affecting PTG in young and middle-aged stroke patients are categorized into four key areas: individual psychological factors, social support networks, cultural backgrounds, and pathological aspects: (1) Individual Psychological Factors: these include psychological resilience, self-efficacy, and emotional regulation. Patients with higher psychological resilience have stronger adaptability and recovery ability after trauma, which is conducive to promoting PTG. For example, some stroke patients develop strong self-regulation skills, using cognitive restructuring and emotional management to boost their self-efficacy and life satisfaction [35, 37]. Self-efficacy refers to an individual’s belief in their ability to accomplish tasks [48]. Stroke patients who maintain confidence in their recovery are more likely to experience PTG. For instance, some patients actively engage in physical therapy during rehabilitation, gradually rebuilding their lives and improving motor functions [34]. These positive experiences strengthen their self-confidence and sense of purpose. From a theoretical perspective, according to the ABC model of psychological resilience (Adversity, Belief, Coping) [49], patients with high psychological resilience hold more positive beliefs when facing trauma (adversity) and can adopt more effective coping strategies, thus adapting and recovering better. Self - efficacy plays a role in motivating and maintaining behavior during the rehabilitation process. Patients with high self - efficacy are more proactive in participating in rehabilitation training and can persevere in the face of difficulties for a longer time. (2) Social Support: Family support, as well as broader networks such as friends, peers, and community organizations, is crucial for PTG. Emotional support and practical assistance from family members—such as helping with rehabilitation exercises or daily care—play a significant role. In our study, many patients relied on family encouragement during rehabilitation, which strengthened their confidence and facilitated personal growth [32]. Some patients also turned to online support groups to share experiences and receive emotional support, or received help from physical therapists and community organizations [32]. Social support enhances patients’ sense of belonging and participation, driving PTG. The forms and effects of social support vary in different cultural backgrounds. In collectivist cultures, family and community support are closer and more comprehensive; while in individualist cultures, the support from friends and professional organizations may be more prominent [50]. (3) Cultural factors significantly shape PTG experiences, with notable differences between Chinese (collectivist) and Western (individualist) contexts—particularly relevant given the overrepresentation of Chinese studies in our sample. Most included studies were from China, where values like familial responsibility shape patients’ experiences: stress often stems from fears of failing family roles (“My mother lives with me, my children need to attend school… [29]”), and recovery motivation is tied to restoring family functions, driven by “close family relationships” and collective responsibility. Growth here is often defined by resuming caregiving or reducing family burden [29, 38]. In contrast, Western studies (e.g., Sweden’s Alenljung et al. [37], Ireland’s Murphy et al. [30]) highlight individual autonomy: stress centers on personal identity/career disruption (e.g., workplace stigma [35]), coping relies on peer/professional support [37], and growth focuses on self-redefinition (“re-purposing myself” [30] or rediscovering personal strengths [35]). Religious beliefs, more prominent in Western contexts, provide spiritual frameworks for rebuilding goals [30, 37], a role less emphasized in Chinese studies where familial ties predominate. These differences reflect cultural mediation: Chinese PTG is tied to collective well-being, while Western PTG centers on individual self-actualization [50], a distinction that should be considered when interpreting the generalizability of our findings. (4) Pathological Aspects: The severity of the stroke, the progress of rehabilitation, and medical psychological interventions all impact PTG. Previous research suggests that patients with mild strokes generally recover more easily and experience greater personal growth. In contrast, those with severe strokes face more challenges, and their PTG may develop more slowly [29]. The severity of the condition often limits the pace of both recovery and PTG. Moreover, psychological interventions, such as Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) and Mindfulness, have proven effective in helping patients reframe negative thought patterns, reduce anxiety and depression, and enhance resilience and self-efficacy [51]. These therapies promote emotional regulation and cognitive restructuring, improve patients’ coping skills, and ultimately drive PTG. For example, CBT helps patients better cope with difficulties in rehabilitation by guiding them to identify and change negative thoughts [52]. Mindfulness training enables patients to focus on the present moment, reduce anxiety about the future, and enhance psychological resilience [52].

In clinical practice, healthcare providers should implement stage-specific, actionable interventions tailored to the unique needs of young and middle-aged stroke patients across the three PTG stages, with clear integration into existing rehabilitation protocols and evaluation metrics: (1)Trauma and Stress Period: Targeted Negative Emotion Regulation: Individual cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) should be delivered by trained psychologists or advanced practice nurses, focusing on identifying and reframing catastrophic thoughts (such as “I will never recover”)—a key driver of anxiety and depression during this stage [42]. Interventions can combine cognitive restructuring exercises (e.g., challenging fears of “permanent disability” with evidence of rehabilitation progress) and stress management techniques (e.g., diaphragmatic breathing). The specific frequency can be flexibly adjusted according to the patient’s acute treatment schedule (e.g., interspersed on days of physical therapy, and using gaps between medical examinations to conduct brief relaxation exercises). Meanwhile, group relaxation training (e.g., guided imagery, progressive muscle relaxation) should be supplemented, with the duration and interval dynamically adjusted based on the patient’s tolerance, and implemented concurrently with acute medical care to minimize disruption to the treatment workflow. Effectiveness assessment should use the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) [53] and physiological indicators (e.g., heart rate variability) to track emotional improvement and stress reduction. (2) Adjustment and Reconstruction Period: Strengthening Support Networks and Adaptive Coping: Community-based peer support groups (facilitated by stroke nurses) should be established to encourage patients to share coping strategies (e.g., “using memos to manage memory deficits“ [37]or “negotiating household roles“ [32]). The meeting frequency can be determined in conjunction with the patient’s outpatient rehabilitation cycle (e.g., aligned with physical therapy sessions). Family members should be invited to participate in training sessions quarterly to address overprotectiveness and guide them in supportive communication [54]. Health education workshops should be conducted, focusing on stroke recovery trajectories and self-management skills (e.g., identifying early signs of recurrence to reduce fear [29]), The specific arrangements can be flexibly adjusted according to patient participation and rehabilitation progress, co-led by rehabilitation therapists to align with physical therapy goals. Effectiveness assessment should use the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS) [55], the Brief Cope Inventory, and track attendance at rehabilitation sessions to evaluate the impact of interventions on participation. (3) Growth Period: sustaining Positive Growth and Life Reintegration: “Growth-oriented” art or music therapy should be introduced to encourage the expression of positive emotions (e.g., gratitude, hope), drawing on research findings that creative activities can enhance PT [56]. Patients can create visual journals or share stories of “rediscovered life meaning“ [36], The duration and frequency of activities should be designed by relevant healthcare professionals (e.g., occupational therapists) based on the patient’s functional recovery goals (e.g., arranging art creation to meet the needs of fine motor training). Regular goal-setting workshops should be held to help patients translate growth into concrete plans (e.g., “applying for a new job“ [29] or “joining community dance groups“ [31]). The interval can be flexibly set according to the long-term follow-up cycle (e.g., interspersed during follow-up visits 6–12 months after discharge), and linked to community health programs (e.g., exercise classes for stroke survivors) to support sustained participation. Effectiveness assessment should use the Posttraumatic Growth Inventory (PTGI) [57] to quantify growth trajectories and the Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS) [58] to assess quality of life, with qualitative feedback from patients on how interventions facilitate social reintegration (e.g., “returning to work” or “strengthened family bonds”). These stage-specific interventions bridge broad recommendations with actionable steps, ensuring alignment with existing rehabilitation workflows and enabling systematic evaluation of their impact on PTG. Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the time span of the included articles was relatively short, and a significant proportion originated from China, resulting in limited geographical diversity. The exclusion of non-Chinese/non-English studies may restrict generalizability, as PTG experiences could vary across linguistic and cultural contexts, potentially underrepresenting regions with high stroke incidence but limited publications in these languages. Notably, while the study focused on PTG among young and middle-aged stroke survivors, it did not explore subgroup variations—specifically, potential differences in PTG trajectories based on sex, stroke severity, or time since stroke onset. For instance, female participants in some included studies (e.g., Alenljung et al. [37]) highlighted unique challenges related to work-family role renegotiation, but insufficient data precluded systematic analysis of gender-specific patterns. Similarly, variations in PTG experiences between mild and severe stroke cases, or between acute and chronic phases of recovery, remain underexplored due to the small sample sizes of individual studies and inconsistent reporting of these variables. Additionally, only qualitative studies (generally with small samples) were included, and authors’ cultural values may have introduced bias—for instance, collectivist-context researchers might emphasize family support, while individualist-context ones focus on personal resilience—shaping interpretation of findings. Despite these limitations, the study’s focus on understudied young and middle-aged stroke patients fills a critical gap. The identified dynamic PTG process can inform individualized rehabilitation and stage-specific social support in clinical practice. Future research should use mixed methods to expand samples and explore subgroups (sex, severity, onset time), while involving multilingual teams to include diverse linguistic studies—enhancing cross-cultural relevance and robustness.

Conclusion

This pioneering meta-synthesis advances understanding of PTG in young and middle-aged stroke patients through three key contributions: a novel three-stage framework (trauma/stress, adjustment/reconstruction, growth) that articulates PTG trajectories by identifying unique emotional, cognitive, and behavioral markers tied to this life stage’s career and family responsibilities—filling gaps in fragmented prior research; clarification of stage-specific influences (e.g., cultural variations in familial roles, age/gender differences in coping), which are previously underexplored factors explaining subgroup differences in PTG; and actionable clinical guidance linking stages to targeted interventions (e.g., CBT for trauma, peer support for adjustment), moving beyond “one-size-fits-all” approaches to accelerate social reintegration. Limitations highlight critical future directions, including expanding geographic and linguistic diversity to explore cross-cultural PTG variations and test framework generalizability, investigating how stroke severity and time since onset shape PTG stages to refine the framework for clinical heterogeneity, and using mixed-methods (longitudinal cohorts, quantitative validation) to strengthen empirical support for stage-specific interventions. In sum, this study redefines PTG conceptualization and care for this population, with future research building on this foundation to enhance global, nuanced understanding of PTG and improve rehabilitation outcomes.
