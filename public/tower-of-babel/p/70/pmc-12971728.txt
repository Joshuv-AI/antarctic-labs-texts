From imaging to omics: deep learning is bridging MRI and liquid biopsy in bone tumor diagnosis
Authors: Feng Wang, Jingxian Chen, Lei Zheng, Xingwen Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•MRI radiomics and liquid biopsy omics efficiently enable tumor profiling.•Multimodal deep learning improves prognosis and risk stratification.•AI fusion aids therapy monitoring in osteosarcoma and Ewing sarcoma.•Omics integration with MR can advance precision oncology in bone tumors.

Introduction

Primary bone tumors such as osteosarcoma and Ewing sarcoma represent one of the most complex diagnostic challenges in oncology because of their biological heterogeneity, overlapping radiological features, and diverse molecular landscapes. [1], [2], [3]. Despite advances in screening, imaging, and molecular diagnostics, early and accurate detection, subtyping, and treatment monitoring remain persistent challenges in oncology [4], [5]. Traditional diagnostic approaches, such as tissue biopsy and anatomical imaging, often provide limited or fragmented information, especially in the context of tumor heterogeneity, metastatic spread, or treatment resistance [6], [7], [8]. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) plays a central role in oncologic diagnosis and staging, including the evaluation and management of primary bone tumors.[9], [10], [11], [12]. It is widely used to treat breast, prostate, liver, brain, and pelvic cancers [10], [13], [14], [15]. However, MRI interpretation remains largely qualitative and dependent on radiologist expertise, which may introduce subjectivity and variability [16], [17]. More importantly, while MRI captures tumor morphology and perfusion dynamics, it lacks direct access to the molecular alterations that drive tumor behavior [18], [19], [20]. In parallel, liquid biopsy has emerged as a transformative tool in oncology, enabling the detection and characterization of tumors through analysis of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), RNA, exosomes, and other biofluids [21], [22], [23], [24], [25]. Liquid biopsies provide a minimally invasive means to assess tumor burden, mutational profiles, clonal evolution, and even minimal residual disease (MRD) [26], [27], [28], [29]. They are already being used for early detection, therapy selection, and longitudinal monitoring in cancers such as non-small cell lung cancer, breast cancer, colorectal cancer, and melanoma [30], [31], [32]. Nevertheless, liquid biopsy data alone often lack spatial context and are sometimes limited by technical sensitivity, particularly in early-stage disease [33]. This is where deep learning (DL), a subset of artificial intelligence (AI), offers a new paradigm [34], [35], [36]. DL models excel at recognizing complex, high-dimensional patterns across various data types [37], [38]. When applied to cancer imaging, deep learning enables automated lesion detection, segmentation, and radiomics-based feature extraction with remarkable precision [39], [40], [41]. Recent developments in multimodal deep learning have allowed the integration of imaging data (e.g., MRI) with genomic, transcriptomic, or epigenomic information obtained via liquid biopsy, creating a more holistic, systems-level view of the tumor [42], [43], [44], [45]. Such multimodal fusion approaches promise to unlock diagnostic capabilities that go beyond what either imaging or omics can achieve independently. For example, integrating MRI-derived features with ctDNA mutational data has improved subtype classification, treatment response prediction, and survival modeling in cancers such as glioblastoma, breast, and lung cancer [46], [47]. This review explores the synergistic integration of MRI and liquid biopsy through deep learning-driven multimodal frameworks. We discuss the state-of-the-art in MRI-based lesion characterization, the evolving role of ctDNA and other omics in bone oncology and how artificial intelligence can harmonize these modalities to deliver more precise, noninvasive and personalized diagnostics. By emphasizing bone tumor–specific evidence while contextualizing broader oncology applications, this review delineates current progress and outlines future translational pathways for multimodal deep learning in sarcoma care.

MRI in bone tumor evaluation

Magnetic resonance imaging remains useful for assessing bone tumors [48]. Studies highlight the ability of MRI to differentiate benign lesions from malignant lesions, map the intra–osseous extent, evaluate soft–tissue invasion, and guide staging and surgical planning [49], [50], [51]. Benign lesions often display sharply defined margins, homogeneous signal intensity, and low-grade enhancement [52], [53], [54], whereas malignant tumors tend to show ill-defined borders, heterogeneous marrow replacement on T1-weighted images, high signals on T2/STIR, and aggressive perfusion or diffusion characteristics [55], [56], [57]. MRI is also critical in local staging, assessing cortical breakthrough, neurovascular involvement, and skipping metastases, effectively guiding biopsy planning and multidisciplinary team decisions [58], [59], [60], [61], [62]. Recent architectures based on U–Net, especially 3D U–Net variants, have been deployed for automated lesion delineation in bone MR images (and analogous CT/PET studies) [63], [64], [65]. These approaches often report high segmentation accuracy [66], [67], with Dice coefficients ranging from approximately 0.85–0.9 [68], [69], enabling reproducible and time-efficient tumor volume estimation for radiomic analysis [70], [71]. A landmark work using EfficientNet-B0 on routine T1– and T2–weighted MR images of over 1000 histologically confirmed bone lesions achieved AUC values of ∼0.79–0.82 on external validation, with performance comparable to that of experienced radiologists (accuracy of approximately 76%) [72]. In this study, clinical factors such as age, sex, and lesion location were also ensembled into the decision pipeline, increasing performance and reflecting real–world diagnostic workflows. Other studies, such as those that fuse machine learning with radiomic or deep models, show promise in subcategorizing bone tumors (e.g., osteosarcoma vs. chondrosarcoma), although large prospective trials remain limited [72]. MRI–based radiomic features, texture, shape, and intensity extracted from segmented lesions, paired with deep features, are increasingly used to predict clinically relevant outcomes such as metastasis risk or overall survival [73], [74]. For example, in prostate cancer bone metastasis, multiparametric MRI radiomics combined with clinical data (e.g., PSA levels, Gleason score) achieved an AUC of ∼0.92 in predicting bone metastatic status pre–treatment [75], [76], [77]. Similarly, models integrating deep feature extraction and conventional radiomic signatures have been shown to stratify patients by metastasis risk and prognosticate outcomes, particularly in spinal metastases where T2–weighted and fat–suppressed sequences deliver complementary data [74], [78], [79], [80], [81].

Liquid biopsy and omics in bone tumors

Liquid biopsy for bone tumors is an emerging but still immature field. Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) refers to short fragments of DNA shed into the bloodstream by dying tumor cells, typically constituting less than 1% of total cell‑free DNA (cfDNA) [82], [83], [84], [85]. cfDNA has a rapid clearance half‑life of only a few hours, meaning that its levels can reflect the tumor burden in real time, a key advantage in monitoring and early detection [86]. Digital PCR (ddPCR) achieves detection down to ∼0.01% mutant allele frequency, which is ideal for tracking known hotspot mutations or fusions [85], [87], [88], [89]. CAPP‑Seq enables the quantification of extremely rare variants, detecting one mutant molecule per 10,000 wild‑type molecules with error suppression[87], [90], [91], [92], [93]. Targeted NGS panels, including tumor–informed MRD assays (e.g., Signatera), begin by profiling a patient’s tumor tissue, selecting tens to thousands of personalized somatic variants, and then tracking them in serial plasma samples to monitor MRD with ultra–high specificity [94], [95]. In contrast, tumor–agnostic MRD assays use fixed mutation or methylation panels and do not require prior tumor sequencing, offering faster turnaround and broader applicability, although potentially with reduced sensitivity [96], [97], [98]. Together, these methods form the backbone of cfDNA analysis, with PCR methods offering targeted sensitivity and NGS methods unlocking broader genomic profiling and MRD tracking. Next-generation liquid–biopsy approaches now go beyond simply detecting mutations; they analyze fragmentation patterns and epigenetic markers embedded in cfDNA to infer gene expression or the tissue of origin [99]. One notable example is EPIC–Seq (Epigenetic Expression Inference by Cell-free DNA Sequencing), which targets promoter regions, including transcription start sites, of selected genes and uses fragmentomic metrics such as promoter fragmentation entropy (PFE) and nucleosome-depleted region (NDR) depth to infer transcriptional activity from cfDNA [100], [101], [102], [103]. This method has proven effective in classifying lung cancer subtypes and lymphomas and even in predicting response to immunotherapy by correlating inferred gene expression with clinical outcomes [101]. More generally, studies of cfDNA fragmentation profiles, such as ratios of short versus long fragments, nucleosome positioning patterns, and end–motif preferences, have shown high accuracy (AUC of approximately 0.9+) in detecting multiple cancer types when combined with mutation data [104], [105], [106], [107]. Epigenomic signals, including DNA methylation signatures and nucleosome footprints, have also been used to classify the tissue of origin and distinguish cancer versus healthy states [108], [109].

Liquid biopsy applications have shown significant promise across breast, lung, colorectal, prostate, and hematologic malignancies, particularly in areas such as mutation testing, resistance monitoring, and MRD assessment. In advanced lung and colorectal cancers, ctDNA analysis is increasingly integrated into clinical practice for guiding therapy and tracking resistance mechanisms, whereas in hematologic malignancies, it is used for MRD monitoring. However, in solid tumors such as breast and prostate tumors, applications in early detection and MRD monitoring remain largely investigational, with ongoing clinical trials but limited incorporation into standard guidelines [85], [86]. In contrast, applications in primary bone tumors (e.g., osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma, and leiomyosarcoma) remain nascent, although emerging studies are encouraged. In osteosarcoma, targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS) studies have revealed TP53, ATRX, DLG2, and MET mutations and chromosomal copy–number gains (e.g., 8q) in ctDNA, with levels correlated with the tumor burden and clinical course [83], [110], [111], [112], [113]. Sarcoma liquid biopsy reviews indicate that simple–karyotype tumors (e.g., Ewing, GIST, DSRCT) are amenable to ddPCR or mutation-targeted assays, whereas complex–karyotype malignancies (e.g., osteosarcoma, leiomyosarcoma) require broader panels and are currently more challenging, but preliminary NGS panels have detected ctDNA in approximately 75–86% of samples in leiomyosarcoma cohorts [89], [114], [115], [116], [117]. Circulating miRNAs and long noncoding RNAs have been evaluated in osteosarcoma. For example, several oncogenic miRNAs (miR-21, –221, –214, etc.) are elevated in patient serum and linked to poor prognosis, whereas tumor–suppressor miRNAs are often depleted, with certain signatures showing diagnostic sensitivity and prognostic relevance in large cohorts [83], [118], [119], [120], [121], [122]. Recent liquid biopsy biomarkers in osteosarcoma have focused mainly on circulating nucleic acids (miRNAs, long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs), circular circular RNAs (circRNAs), and circulating small DNA (ctDNA)) and cellular/vesicular components (CTCs and EVs)[123]. These panels remain exploratory, but several panels now show diagnostic and prognostic value in human cohorts. Circulating miRNAs have emerged as noninvasive markers of diagnosis, stage, metastasis, and therapy response–. For example, miR–497 and miR–125b are typically downregulated in osteosarcoma, with lower levels associated with advanced stage, distant metastasis, unresectable disease, and poor chemotherapy response[124], [125], [126]. Other microRNAs, including miR‑21, miR‑27a, miR‑199a‑5p and members of the miR‑195 family, have been reported to be dysregulated in osteosarcoma, with several studies linking their expression levels to metastatic disease or survival outcomes, thereby supporting their evaluation within multiplex miRNA signatures rather than as standalone biomarkers[127], [128], [129]. Serum lncRNAs, including 91H and UCA1, are elevated in OS and correlate with increased tumor size, advanced stage, and poor prognosis, whereas circulating circRNAs show altered expression in blood and appear to be associated with disease burden and outcome [130], [131].

ctDNA carrying tumor–specific mutations or CNVs can be detected in subsets of osteosarcoma patients, although sensitivity remains limited; fragmentomic analysis of cfDNA may improve reliability. Fragmentomic analysis may enhance sensitivity because it captures genome-wide patterns of DNA fragmentation, nucleosome positioning, and fragment length distribution, which reflect tumor-associated chromatin organization independently of mutation abundance. This enables detection of tumor-derived signals even when mutant allele fractions are extremely low. Classical serum markers such as LDH and alkaline phosphatase remain in use but are nonspecific and are now viewed as complementary to molecular assays [132]. EVs from osteosarcoma cells carry tumor–-derived RNAs and proteins, offering stable biomarker cargo, whereas CTCs show promise for prognostication but remain technically challenging [133]. In Ewing sarcoma, ddPCR assays for fusion transcripts such as EWSR1–FLI1 are under evaluation for relapse monitoring. In leiomyosarcoma, broader NGS panels have demonstrated feasibility despite their genomic complexity [134], [135].

Most diagnostic miRNA evidence in osteosarcoma supports panels rather than single markers. panels of circulating miRNAs provide superior diagnostic performance to single markers in osteosarcoma, and a repeatedly validated four‑miRNA signature (miR‑195‑5p, miR‑199a‑3p, miR‑320a, and miR‑374a‑5p) shows consistent pre‑operative elevation and post‑operative decline [136].

Overall, liquid biopsy in bone tumors remains at an early stage, but multi–layered approaches combining ctDNA, fragmentomics, miRNA/lncRNA/circRNA panels, EV cargo, and CTC metrics are likely to provide the sensitivity and specificity needed for clinical translation. The main challenges include low analyte abundance, high tumor heterogeneity, and a lack of assay standardization, yet ongoing studies suggest that integrated liquid biopsy strategies could become central to diagnosis, risk stratification, and dynamic monitoring of primary bone tumors.

Multimodal deep learning: architectures and fusion strategies

Bone tumors represent one of the most complex diagnostic challenges in oncology because of their marked biological heterogeneity, overlapping radiological appearances, and diverse molecular landscapes. Traditional diagnostic paradigms that rely on either imaging or molecular profiling alone often provide fragmented and incomplete insights [137]. MRI remains indispensable for delineating tumor morphology, local extent, marrow involvement, and soft-tissue invasion, whereas liquid biopsy approaches offer dynamic molecular information through circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), RNA, and epigenomic signals [138], [139]. However, each modality in isolation fails to capture the full spatial–molecular complexity of bone tumor biology. The integration of these complementary data streams through multimodal deep learning has therefore emerged as a transformative strategy, enabling more holistic, noninvasive, and personalized diagnostic and prognostic frameworks in bone oncology[140]. Deep learning architectures such as graph neural networks (GNNs) and transformer-based models are particularly well suited for multimodal fusion. GNNs excel at modeling relational and structured data, in which radiomic features extracted from MRI, ctDNA alterations, and clinical parameters can be represented as interconnected nodes, with spatial, biological, or molecular interactions encoded as edges [141], [142]. This graph-based paradigm enables the capture of complex dependencies across heterogeneous data sources, whereas attention-based GNN variants further enhance interpretability by identifying the most informative imaging–molecular relationships [143], [144].

In contrast, transformers leverage self-attention mechanisms to dynamically weight and integrate high-dimensional features across modalities. In bone tumors, multiparametric MRI sequences—including T1-weighted, T2-weighted, diffusion-weighted, and contrast-enhanced imaging—can be fused with ctDNA mutational profiles, copy number alterations, or methylation signatures [145]. This capacity to learn both intramodal and intermodal dependencies within a unified architecture makes transformer-based models particularly powerful for tumor subtype classification, treatment response prediction, and outcome stratification [146].

Radiogenomic studies in other malignancies provide compelling evidence supporting the feasibility and translational relevance of multimodal fusion strategies. In glioblastoma, MRI-derived radiomic features have been associated with IDH mutation status and MGMT promoter methylation, whereas in lung cancer, CT and PET radiomic features have been linked to EGFR and KRAS alterations [147], [148]. Similarly, breast cancer studies have demonstrated that combining MRI features with gene expression profiles improves predictions of therapeutic response and recurrence risk. These findings validate the central premise that imaging phenotypes can reflect underlying molecular processes when analyzed through appropriate deep learning frameworks, offering important conceptual guidance for applications in bone tumors [140], [149].

Although multimodal deep learning in bone oncology remains at an early stage, emerging studies provide encouraging preliminary evidence. Pilot investigations have integrated MRI-derived features such as tumor margin characteristics, enhancement kinetics, intratumoral heterogeneity, and peritumoral edema with liquid biopsy biomarkers, including TP53 mutations, copy number variation patterns, and DNA methylation signatures [150], [151]. These fusion models have demonstrated potential for improving diagnostic accuracy, distinguishing aggressive phenotypes, and predicting chemotherapy response in patients with osteosarcoma. Furthermore, the integration of MRI with serial ctDNA measurements enables longitudinal monitoring of minimal residual disease and early relapse detection, offering a noninvasive alternative to repeated tissue biopsies [152].

Beyond diagnosis and surveillance, multimodal deep learning frameworks hold promise for broader clinical applications in bone tumors. By aligning structural, spatial, and molecular information within a single model, these approaches may support individualized prognostication, guide therapy intensity, and facilitate patient stratification for clinical trials [140]. Spatially informed architectures such as GNNs can incorporate tumor location and extent alongside omics data to refine survival prediction, whereas transformer-based fusion models can capture global dependencies across modalities to enhance subtype discrimination [153]. As multimodal datasets expand and interdisciplinary collaborations mature, the integration of MRI and liquid biopsy through deep learning is poised to become a cornerstone of precision oncology in bone tumors, enabling richer, more accurate, and clinically actionable insights into tumor biology.

Clinical applications and impact

The fusion of MRI and liquid biopsy data, particularly circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), offers transformative potential in the clinical management of bone tumors [30], [154], [155], [156]. When integrated through deep learning models, especially multimodal architectures, this combined approach enhances diagnostic precision, treatment monitoring, and outcome prediction. Early and accurate diagnosis of bone tumors is notoriously difficult because of overlapping radiological features between benign lesions and aggressive malignancies [157], [158], [159], [160]. Moreover, subtyping, such as distinguishing osteosarcoma from Ewing sarcoma or chondrosarcoma, requires invasive biopsy and expert pathology [161], [162], [163], [164]. Here, multimodal deep learning models that integrate MRI radiomics and ctDNA profiling offer a less invasive and more precise solution [42], [142], [165], [166]. For example, radiomic features extracted from multiparametric MR images (e.g., T1, T2, and DWI sequences) can be used to quantify tumor heterogeneity, margin sharpness, and periosteal reactions, features known to differ between tumor types [167], [168], [169], [170]. Simultaneously, liquid biopsy can provide molecular clues via mutational landscapes, copy number variations, and methylation signatures [171], [172], [173]. When combined, this dual-source information enables models to learn nuanced patterns that correlate with tumor biology. Recent work in other solid tumors (e.g., glioblastoma and lung cancer) has shown that such fusion models outperform single-modality systems in classifying subtypes and predicting high-risk disease [174], [175]. For bone tumors, preliminary data suggest similar potential: early studies have demonstrated that ctDNA can detect TP53, RB1, and ATRX mutations, which may aid in distinguishing aggressive osteosarcoma phenotypes [155], [176], [177], [178]. By training deep learning models on aligned MRI and ctDNA data, it is possible to create decision-support systems that flag atypical or high-grade tumors even at an early stage, enabling timely intervention [149], [179]. Predicting and monitoring the response to chemotherapy remains a cornerstone of bone tumor management, especially in patients with osteosarcoma [180], [181], [182], [183]. Traditionally, tumor necrosis postchemotherapy is used as a retrospective marker of response, but this requires surgical resection and is available only after treatment is complete [184], [185], [186], [187]. In contrast, a multimodal deep learning model could provide real-time, noninvasive assessments during therapy [44], [188], [189], [190]. MRI can capture dynamic changes in tumor volume, vascularity, and cellularity during treatment, which are associated with treatment efficacy [191], [192], [193], [194]. Deep learning models can be trained to detect subtle imaging features, beyond human visual capacity, that correlate with histologic necrosis [195], [196], [197]. Several studies using CNNs and transformer-based networks have shown promise in predicting tumor necrosis before surgery in osteosarcoma patients, with the use of only MRI scans [198], [199], [200]. When fused with serial ctDNA measurements, which capture reductions in tumor-specific variants or methylation changes, these models become even more powerful [201], [202]. Additionally, MRD detection via ctDNA can serve as a surrogate for recurrence risk [95], [203], [204]. For example, longitudinal ctDNA profiling has been used in Ewing sarcoma and rhabdomyosarcoma patients to anticipate relapse months before imaging detects a recurrence [205], [206], [207]. These findings suggest that MRI plus ctDNA models could support personalized surveillance strategies, reducing both overtreatment and the risk of late-stage relapse.

Extrapolating from established multimodal models in other solid tumors such as glioblastoma and lung cancer, where MRI–omics fusion has demonstrated improved subtype classification and outcome prediction, similar frameworks are now being explored in primary bone tumors. However, unlike these more extensively studied malignancies, bone tumor applications remain largely at a pilot or early translational stage, with limited cohort sizes and ongoing validation efforts.

Prognostic models for bone tumors have traditionally relied on clinical variables such as tumor size, location, histologic grade, and response to chemotherapy [208], [209], [210]. However, these findings provide limited insight into tumor biology. Integrating imaging and omics data allows for the development of more biologically grounded prognostic models, with implications for both treatment planning and clinical trial stratification [42], [211], [212]. In glioblastoma and breast cancer, multimodal deep learning models combining MRI with gene expression or proteomic data have been used to stratify patients into survival risk groups with significantly improved accuracy [213], [214], [215]. Applying similar models to bone tumors trained on combined MRI radiomics and ctDNA-based molecular profiles could lead to individualized survival predictions [216]. These models may identify aggressive molecular subtypes, such as those harboring chromothripsis or high genomic instability, which have been linked to poorer outcomes in osteosarcoma [155], [207]. Moreover, spatially aware models such as graph neural networks can incorporate tumor location and extent from imaging alongside omics data to refine predictions. Such personalized prognostication could guide decisions about therapy intensity, surgical margins, and inclusion in experimental treatment trials, bringing a new level of precision to bone tumor care. By integrating imaging and molecular data through deep learning, clinicians gain access to a unified, data-rich view of each tumor’s behavior. MRI provides structural and spatial insights, whereas ctDNA and other omics methods provide molecular context. Together, they empower a more accurate, dynamic, and personalized approach to diagnosis, monitoring, and prognosis. As more multimodal datasets become available, the real-world deployment of these fusion models could dramatically reduce diagnostic delays, optimize therapy selection, and improve survival outcomes, marking a paradigm shift in the clinical management of bone tumors. From a future clinical perspective, multimodal deep learning frameworks integrating MRI and liquid biopsy data could be embedded into a realistic diagnostic and monitoring workflow for primary bone tumors. In such a pathway, patients presenting with a suspicious bone lesion would first undergo standardized multiparametric MRI alongside blood-based liquid biopsy sampling. Radiomic features extracted from MR images and molecular features derived from circulating nucleic acids or extracellular vesicles can then be jointly analyzed via multimodal deep learning models to enable prebiopsy risk stratification and tumor characterization [217]. During treatment, serial MRI and liquid biopsy assessments could support fusion-based monitoring of therapeutic response and early detection of minimal residual disease. Following definitive therapy, integrated ctDNA and MRI surveillance may facilitate earlier identification of relapse and guide personalized follow-up strategies. Challenges and limitations

One of the most pressing issues in multimodal modeling is the heterogeneity of the input data [222], [230], [231]. MRI acquisition protocols vary significantly across institutions in terms of magnet strength, pulse sequences, resolution, and contrast settings [232], [233], [234]. These inconsistencies introduce variability in radiomic features and limit cross-cohort comparability. Similarly, ctDNA assays are not standardized; different platforms (e.g., targeted NGS panels, whole-genome bisulfite sequencing, or digital PCR) can yield differing sensitivities, depths, and bioinformatic outputs [235], [236]. Additionally, annotation remains a major bottleneck. Tumor segmentation via MRI is often performed manually, requiring expert radiologists and lacking uniformity across datasets [237], [238]. In the molecular space, the labeling of ctDNA mutations with clinical significance requires extensive validation and is often incomplete [239], [240]. This creates a noisy and sparse data environment that hinders model robustness. To mitigate these issues, initiatives such as harmonized imaging protocols (e.g., QIBA standards) and common bioinformatics pipelines for ctDNA analysis are gaining traction [241], [242], [243]. Nonetheless, achieving truly interoperable, multi-institutional datasets remains a work in progress.

Another critical concern is the limited generalizability of current deep learning models [244], [245], [246]. Compared with cancers such as lung or breast cancer, bone tumors are rare, resulting in relatively small datasets for training complex multimodal models [247], [248], [249], [250]. This often leads to overfitting, where a model performs well on the training set but poorly on unseen data. Moreover, bone tumor-specific omics data, especially from liquid biopsy sources, are sparse in public repositories [251], [252], [253]. Many existing studies rely on extrapolation from broader sarcoma datasets or other solid tumors, which may not capture the unique molecular profile of bone cancers such as osteosarcoma or Ewing sarcoma [161], [254], [255], [256]. To overcome this, several strategies have emerged. Transfer learning, where models pretrained on large datasets (e.g., from other cancers) are fine-tuned on smaller cohorts of bone tumors [257], [258], [259], [260]. Data augmentation techniques for imaging include synthetic image generation via generative adversarial networks (GANs) [261], [262], [263], [264], [265]. Federated learning, which allows models to be trained across multiple institutions without data leaving local servers, addresses both privacy concerns and dataset size concerns [266], [267], [268], [269], [270]. Existing collaborative initiatives such as the INSTRuCT (International Soft Tissue Sarcoma Consortium) pediatric sarcoma network illustrate how multinational data-sharing frameworks can accelerate research in rare tumors. Although not specifically designed for multimodal AI development, such consortia provide a structural model for federated learning approaches, enabling multi-institutional model training while preserving patient privacy and regulatory compliance [271], [272]. Despite these advances, the development of large, curated, and labeled multimodal datasets specific to bone tumors is essential for robust model training and external validation. Deep learning models, especially those involving complex data fusion architectures, are often criticized as “black boxes.” [273], [274], [275]. The field of explainable AI (XAI) is working to address this by developing tools such as saliency maps and Grad-CAM for imaging, which highlight regions of MRI that influence the model’s decision [276], [277], [278], [279]. Feature attribution methods (e.g., SHAP, LIME) for omics data are used to identify which mutations or methylation patterns are most predictive [280], [281], [282], [283]. In a multimodal setting, the challenge is even greater; clinicians need to understand not only what the model predicts but also how it integrates and weighs the imaging and molecular signals [284], [285], [286], [287], [288]. Even with strong technical performance, real-world integration of MRI plus liquid biopsy models faces regulatory and ethical hurdles. Combining radiologic and genomic data raises new concerns about data privacy, consent, and biobanking ethics [289], [290], [291], [292]. From a regulatory standpoint, the approval of AI tools in healthcare requires extensive external validation across diverse populations [293], [294], [295], [296], [297]. Furthermore, integrating ctDNA assays into routine practice remains logistically complex and cost intensive [298], [299], [300]. While MRI is already embedded in care pathways, liquid biopsy adoption requires investment in specialized lab infrastructure, reimbursement frameworks, and clinician education. On the practical front, workflow integration is another barrier. A multimodal model must seamlessly plug into hospital systems, combining data from PACS (for imaging) and molecular laboratories in real time, which is a nontrivial engineering challenge [301], [302], [303], [304], [305]. From a strategic perspective, addressing validation and generalization challenges in bone tumor multimodal modeling will require coordinated global efforts. Federated learning represents a particularly promising approach for rare tumors, as it enables collaborative training across multiple institutions without the need to centralize sensitive patient data, thereby expanding the dataset size while preserving privacy [306]. In addition to technical solutions, the establishment of international consortia and public–private partnerships is essential for building large, curated, and multimodal datasets that integrate imaging, liquid biopsy, and clinical metadata [307]. Without such coordinated initiatives, there is a substantial risk that even technically sophisticated models will fail in clinical translation, as small, fragmented datasets cannot support robust external validation or regulatory approval [308]. A clear roadmap therefore involves harmonizing protocols, investing in federated infrastructures, and fostering global collaboration to ensure that multimodal deep learning frameworks for bone tumors achieve both scientific rigor and real-world clinical impact.

Conclusions

The integration of deep learning, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and liquid biopsy techniques redefines how we understand and diagnose bone tumors. On the one hand, MRI, which is enhanced by AI and deep learning, has evolved from a mere imaging tool to a powerful platform for extracting meaningful features related to tumor structure, vascularity, and progression. Deep learning models are now capable of detecting subtle patterns in MR images that often correlate with tumor grade, metastatic potential, and therapeutic response. These advancements allow clinicians to make more accurate and earlier diagnoses, with less dependence on invasive biopsies. On the other hand, liquid biopsy technologies, particularly those analyzing circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), RNA, exosomes, and other biofluids, are opening new windows into the molecular and genetic landscape of bone tumors. These tools offer real-time insights into tumor dynamics, mutational profiles, and even minimal residual disease, making them ideal for monitoring treatment response and disease recurrence. The synergistic power of combining these imaging and molecular approaches is truly transformative. When radiomic features from MR images are linked with multiomic data from liquid biopsies, we gain a far more holistic view of tumor behavior. This radiogenomic fusion has shown significant promise in improving prognostic accuracy, personalizing therapy decisions, and guiding clinical management strategies. Although this field is still in an emerging phase, with challenges such as data standardization, interpretability of AI models, and the need for multicenter validation, the direction is clear. We are heading toward a future where noninvasive, data-driven diagnostics will play a central role in bone oncology. Deep learning is not just a tool; it is a bridge between imaging and molecular diagnostics. Connecting these two worlds enables a more precise, timely, and personalized approach to bone tumor care, with the ultimate goal of improving survival outcomes and quality of life for patients with bone tumors.
