Applying the Supporting Features for MOGAD Diagnosis to Patients With Multiple Sclerosis
Authors: Pietro Zara, Giacomo Greco, Francesco Masi, Stefania Leoni, Valentina Floris, Sabrine Othmani, M. Margherita Sechi, Sara Carta, Eduardo Caverzasi, Anna Pichiecchio, Stefano Sotgiu, Paolo Solla, Elena Colombo, Rosa Cortese, Sara Mariotto, Matteo Gastaldi, Elia Sechi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesIn the 2023 diagnostic criteria, supporting clinical/MRI features are required to diagnose myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody–associated disease (MOGAD) in patients at high risk of false MOG-IgG positivity (low/unavailable titer, cerebrospinal fluid-restricted), to help differentiation from multiple sclerosis (MS). However, overlap is possible and determining the frequency of the MOGAD supporting features at MS onset may help prevent misdiagnosis. We assessed the frequency of the MOGAD supporting features and potential risk of misdiagnosis at first attack of MS/clinically isolated syndrome (CIS).MethodsIn this observational study, we retrospectively identified consecutive patients hospitalized between 2021 and 2024 at 2 Italian centers, with: (1) relapsing-remitting MS/CIS, and (2) first attack MRI available. The frequency of the MOGAD supporting features was assessed at MS/CIS presentation, and potential risk of misdiagnosis determined based on the probability of concurrent false MOG-IgG positivity. Other clinical-MRI features typical of MOGAD but not included in the 2023 diagnostic criteria were also evaluated.ResultsA total of 244 patients with MS/CIS were included (median age, 34 [range, 13–78] years; 66% were female). Overall, 65/244 (27%) patients with MS/CIS showed at least 1 MOGAD supporting feature during the presenting attack. “Central cord lesion” (27/82 [33%]) and “deep gray nuclei involvement” (24/86 [28%]) were more represented than other supporting features (0%–7%); p < 0.001. MOG-IgG was tested in 207/244 (85%) patients, showing false positivity in 3 (1.4%) patients. The combined probability of detecting a false MOG-IgG positivity and meeting 1 of the MOGAD supporting features was 0.4%. Among other typical MOGAD features not included in the diagnostic criteria, the following had a 0% frequency at MS/CIS presentation: (1) encephalopathy, (2) marked CSF pleocytosis (>50 cells/mm3), (3) wheelchair need during myelitis, and (4) MRI T2-lesion resolution postattack.DiscussionAlthough the MOGAD supporting features can be met in one-fourth of patients at MS/CIS presentation, the risk of misdiagnosis in similar unselected cohorts remains low. Future refinements of the MOGAD supporting features should take into account their relative frequency in MS/CIS and possible integration with additional clinical-MRI features that are more specific for MOGAD.

Introduction

Myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG)-IgG–associated disease (MOGAD) is the most recently characterized demyelinating disease of the CNS, distinct from multiple sclerosis (MS) and aquaporin-4-IgG-positive neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD).1

According to the diagnostic criteria published in 2023 by an international panel of experts, a diagnosis of MOGAD requires demonstration of a clear (high titer) serum positivity for MOG-IgG by cell-based assay (CBA), in patients with a compatible clinical-MRI phenotype (optic neuritis, myelitis, acute disseminated encephalomyelitis [ADEM], cerebral monofocal or polyfocal deficits, brainstem/cerebellar syndrome, or cortical encephalitis).2 When MOG-IgG positivity is less clear (low titer, titer unavailable, CSF-restricted), the risk of “false” or clinically irrelevant positive results increases.3-6 In these patients, supporting features are needed to confirm the diagnosis of MOGAD.2

The supporting features are clinical-MRI features commonly associated with MOGAD that were selected by the international panel to help differentiate the disease from its mimics, primarily MS.2,6,7 However, patients with MS or other demyelinating CNS disorders may sometimes show clinical-MRI features that overlap with those seen in MOGAD, potentially leading to diagnostic uncertainty and incorrect diagnoses.8-11 The frequency of the MOGAD supporting features in unselected cohorts of patients with MS and potential risk of misdiagnosis have not been specifically investigated. In this retrospective observational study, we assessed the frequency of the MOGAD supporting features at the presenting attack of MS/clinically isolated syndrome (CIS), and determined the potential risk of misdiagnosis based on the probability of concurrent false MOG-IgG positivity. We also evaluated other clinical-MRI features typical of MOGAD that are currently not included in the diagnostic criteria.

Methods

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of the University Hospital of Sassari and IRCCS Mondino Foundation (project code: 0020308/23). All involved patients consented to the use of their medical records for research.

Study Population

Among patients with demyelinating CNS disorders consecutively admitted for diagnostic evaluation between January 1, 2021, and December 31, 2024, at the Neurology Units of the University Hospital of Sassari and the IRCCS Mondino Foundation of Pavia (Italy), we retrospectively identified those with (1) relapsing-remitting MS or CIS, (2) brain and/or spinal cord MRI available within 6 weeks of the presenting disease attack, and (3) sufficient clinical information.

MS was diagnosed according to the 2017 McDonald diagnostic criteria.12 CIS was defined as a presenting disease attack suggestive of MS with (1) at least 2 typical CNS regions involved on MRI (including supratentorial brain periventricular or juxtacortical, infratentorial brain, and spinal cord) and lack of dissemination in time (e.g., a patient with myelitis, asymptomatic MS lesions on brain MRI, and lack of evidence of dissemination in time), or (2) one typical CNS region involved plus the optic nerve, with or without dissemination in time (e.g., a patient with optic neuritis and asymptomatic MS lesions on brain MRI, with or without evidence of dissemination in time). We excluded patients with (1) insufficient clinical information, (2) unavailable brain and/or spinal cord MRI or MRI performed after 6 weeks from the presenting attack, (3) primary progressive disease course, or (4) radiologically isolated syndrome.

Patients with MOGAD or other relapsing demyelinating CNS disorders, including NMOSD (with or without AQP4-IgG), seronegative ADEM, isolated myelitis, isolated optic neuritis, and isolated brain/brainstem syndromes, hospitalized during the study period were used for comparison.

MOG-IgG Testing and Potential Risk of Misdiagnosis

MOG-IgG serostatus was assessed by fixed or live CBA, as part of the diagnostic work-up or for research purposes. The live tests were performed at the Neuroimmunology Laboratory of the IRCCS Mondino Foundation of Pavia or the Neuropathology Laboratory of the University Hospital of Verona, with previously described methodologies.13,14 Fixed CBA was performed according to manufacturer's instructions (Euroimmun; serum dilution, 1:10). The potential risk of misdiagnosis in MS/CIS patients meeting the MOGAD supporting features was assessed based on the probability of concomitant false MOG-IgG positivity.

Application of the Supporting Features for MOGAD Diagnosis to Patients With MS

In each center, 2 neurology investigators with experience in demyelinating CNS disorders (E.S. and P.Z.; G.G. and F.M.) reviewed together the presenting attack of included patients with MS/CIS to determine the presence of clinical-MRI features meeting the MOGAD supporting features per the 2023 diagnostic criteria.2 The supporting features were applied exclusively to the symptomatic CNS domains (optic nerve, brain, spinal cord, or combinations thereof). An initial training session was conducted among investigators from both centers to discuss how to interpret and apply the supporting features for MOGAD diagnosis from the 2023 diagnostic criteria.2 MRI features were applied by reviewing all available sequences obtained with different scanners at 1.5T or 3T during the diagnostic work-up. Each MRI feature was assessed only in the subgroup for whom the relevant imaging was available. A description of the supporting features for MOGAD diagnosis and the way they were interpreted for this study are summarized (Table 1). In cases of uncertainty on interpretation of certain MRI findings, a final agreement was reached after discussion with an external rater (R.C.) on representative images.

Other Clinical-MRI Features Suggestive of MOGAD

In addition to the MOGAD supporting features, the frequency of other clinical-MRI features characteristic of MOGAD was investigated. These were selected by the investigators according to literature data and included: (1) visual acuity at optic neuritis nadir of hand movement, 20/200 or less in the worst eye; (2) presence of encephalopathy (ranging from somnolence to coma); (3) need for gait aid (EDSS: 6–6.5) or wheelchair (EDSS: 7) at myelitis nadir; (4) normal brain MRI during a brain attack; (5) normal spinal cord MRI during a myelitis attack; (6) complete resolution of brain and/or spinal cord T2-lesions on MRI follow-up >3 months; (7) marked CSF pleocytosis (>50 white blood cells/mm3); and (8) lack of CSF-restricted oligoclonal bands (≥2).15-23

Statistics

Continuous and categorical variables were reported as median (range) and numbers (%) and compared by the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. The potential risk of MOGAD misdiagnosis in patients with MS was determined by multipling the frequency of the MOGAD supporting features (at least 1) at MS/CIS presentation by the frequency of false MOG-IgG positivity in the same cohort (combined probability).

Data Availability

Anonymized data from this study are available by the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Results

A total of 244 patients with MS (n = 213) or CIS (n = 31) were included in the study from the 2 centers (Figure 1). The median age at disease onset was 34 (range, 13–78) years; 160 (66%) were female, and 8 (3%) were children. The presenting attacks were optic neuritis in 60 (25%), brain/brainstem attacks in 85 (35%), myelitis in 96 (39%), and combinations thereof in 3 (1%). Frequency of the MOGAD Supporting Features in Patients With MS/CIS

Overall, 65/244 (27%) patients with MS/CIS presented 1 or more supporting features for a diagnosis of MOGAD at disease onset. The frequency was higher in patients with myelitis (37%) or brain attacks (29%), compared to patients with optic neuritis (10%); p < 0.001. The highest feature-specific frequency was observed for “central cord myelitis lesion” (33%) and “deep gray nuclei involvement” (28%), while the frequency of other supporting features ranged between 0% and 7%. The frequency of the different supporting features was similar in the 2 cohorts (Table 3), suggesting good alignment after the initial training session. Uncertainty with interpretation of MRI findings occurred almost exclusively for patients with possible “ill-defined” T2-hyperintense lesions, initially indicated in 7 cases but later confirmed only in 2 cases in the infratentorial region after review with the external rater (Figure 2, E and F). None was felt to meet the definition of “multiple ill-defined T2 hyperintense lesions in supratentorial white matter,” although some showed similar supratentorial lesions in the context of other typical, well-demarcated MS lesions (eFigure 2).

MOG-IgG Serostatus and Potential Risk of Misdiagnosis

Serum MOG-IgG was tested in 207/244 (85%) patients diagnosed with MS or CIS. While 204 tested negative, “false” MOG-IgG positivity was detected in 3 (1.4%) patients, by fixed CBA only (n = 1; positivity subsequently not confirmed by live CBA) or by both fixed and live CBA (n = 2; 1 with a high antibody titer of 1:20,480). Overall, 2 patients (1%) would meet the diagnostic criteria for MOGAD if the requirement for exclusion of alternative diagnoses is not applied. Only the patient with false MOG-IgG positivity exclusively detected by fixed CBA showed 1 of the MOGAD supporting features (“deep gray nuclei involvement”). The combined probability of detecting a false MOG-IgG positivity and meeting at least 1 of the MOGAD supporting features was 0.4%.

Comparison With MOGAD and Other Demyelinating CNS Disorders

The frequency of the supporting features was significantly lower in patients with MS/CIS (61/245 [25%]) when compared to MOGAD patients (12/14 [86%]; p < 0.0001) or patients with other relapsing demyelinating CNS disorders (23/42 [55%]; p = 0.0002) seen during the study period (eTable 1).

Evaluation of Additional Clinical-MRI Features Suggestive of MOGAD in Patients With MS/CIS

Nine additional clinical-MRI features were selected by the authors as characteristic of MOGAD based on literature data and authors' experience. Their overall frequency in patients with MS/CIS was 4% (Table 4), with 4 features exhibiting a frequency of 0%, including (1) presence of encephalopathy, (2) need for wheelchair at myelitis nadir, (3) complete resolution of MRI T2-abnormalities after brain or myelitis attacks, and (4) marked CSF pleocytosis. These additional clinical-MRI features were more common in patients with other relapsing demyelinating CNS disorders (19/42 [45%]) than in MS; p < 0.001 (eTable 2).

Discussion

We showed the supporting features for MOGAD diagnosis can be met in one-fourth of cases of MS/CIS at disease presentation, potentially leading to diagnostic uncertainty. Clinicians should be aware of this possibility when evaluating rare patients with concurrent MOG-IgG positivity of unclear significance and typical MS phenotype to avoid assigning an incorrect diagnosis of MOGAD and inappropriate treatment.

MS is the most common demyelinating CNS disorder and represents the main alternative diagnosis in patients with false MOG-IgG positivity.6,7,24 In the 2023 MOGAD diagnostic criteria, it is emphasized that patients with typical MS should not be tested for MOG-IgG considering the low pretest probability.2 The supporting features further help reduce the probability of misdiagnosis in patients at high risk of false MOG-IgG positivity, playing a crucial role in differentiating MOGAD from MS.2 However, the judgment of typical vs atypical MS is not always straightforward and strongly relies on individual expertise; moreover, the detection of clinical-MRI features suggestive of MOGAD in patients with otherwise genuine MS may lead to diagnostic uncertainty. In this context, understanding the prevalence of the MOGAD supporting features in an unselected cohort of MS patients may aid in correctly stratifying disease phenotypes and identifying cases with false MOG-IgG positivity.

Our findings show that, although the MOGAD supporting features are not uncommonly met in patients with MS, the probability of obtaining a concurrent false MOG-IgG positivity is low (0.4% in this study), confirming the high specificity of the MOGAD diagnostic criteria.2,8,11,25-27 However, caution is needed in clinical practice as this probability might increase if antibody testing is performed in larger unselected populations, when MOG-IgG testing is routinely performed in parallel in the CSF (not done in this study), and by using fixed rather than live CBA (as shown by 1 case in this study who falsely tested positive for MOG-IgG exclusively by fixed assay).4,6,28 Assessment of MOG-IgG titer, when possible, is also important as application of the supporting features is not required to diagnose MOGAD in patients with high antibody titers, although high-titer false MOG-IgG positivity may rarely occur (as observed in 1 patient in this study).

The feature-specific frequency varied in our cohort of patients with MS/CIS, with 2 MOGAD supporting features being overrepresented. In particular, deep gray matter involvement was observed in 28% of cases (Figure 2, C and D), differently from other brain supporting features that were rare (0%–2%). This discrepancy can be explained by the lack of specific morphologic requirements for deep gray matter lesions (generally smaller and better demarcated in MS compared with MOGAD) in the diagnostic criteria.29,30 On the contrary, the criteria require T2-lesions in other brain locations (i.e., supratentorial and infratentorial white matter) to be “ill-defined.” Despite increasing the separation between MS and MOGAD lesions, this requirement represented a frequent cause of disagreement in our study (Figure 2, E and F; eFigure 2). Since the concept of “ill-defined” or “fluffy” lesions, often used in the literature, is prone to interobserver variability, the use of more objective features that can help differentiate MS from MOGAD (e.g., lesion size) might be considered in future revisions of the diagnostic criteria.31 Variability in the real-life application of the MOGAD diagnosti criteria was also suggested in a recent study.32

Patients with myelitis showed a central cord lesion in 33% of cases, often involving the entire axial section due to marked perilesional edema acutely (Figure 3A; eFigure 1). In these cases, the concomitant presence of typical MS lesions on brain MRI and extension of the myelitis lesion for less than 3 vertebral body segments, strongly orient the diagnostic suspicion toward MS rather than MOGAD.16 Interestingly, four patients with MS myelitis (4%) had lesions extending over 3 contiguous vertebral body segments on sagittal images (Figure 3E). These lesions were all located in the cervical spinal cord (where vertebral bodies are shorter) and never exceeded the length of 3 vertebral body segments. The detection of unusually long lesions in MS can be explained by severe inflammation or coalescence of contiguous lesions that can be demonstrated on axial images (not available for all cases in our study).33 When axial images are assessed carefully, true longitudinally extensive myelitis lesions are very rare in MS, even in pediatric populations, where such lesions were historically reported to be more frequent before the ability to distinguish MOGAD.34 Finally, patients with optic neuritis were less likely to meet the supporting features for MOGAD diagnosis (Figure 2, A and B), although orbital MRI was not obtained for all patients during the diagnostic workup.

We identified additional clinical-MRI features typical of MOGAD that were rarely observed in our cohort of MS/CIS (Table 4) and might be integrated in future revisions of the MOGAD diagnostic criteria. For instance, the presence of acute/subacute encephalopathy and inflammatory CSF findings might probably be sufficient to confirm the diagnosis of MOGAD in patients with low/unavailable MOG-IgG titer, independently of the morphology and location of accompanying MRI abnormalities. Resolution of MRI abnormalities at follow-up is also quite specific for MOGAD, although lacks applicability acutely.21 Similarly, a normal MRI during brain or myelitis attack is extremely rare in patients with MS/CIS but occurs in 10% of MOGAD cases and is currently not contemplated in the MOGAD diagnostic criteria,22,35 although the specificity of this feature has not been formally assessed.

This study is mainly limited by the retrospective design and lack of standardized MRI protocols, which is reflective of real-life practice. In particular, the lack of axial images in a subgroup of patients with myelitis might have biased assessment of central cord lesions toward cases with more severe myelitis potentially more likely to undergo an extensive evaluation by spinal cord MRI. Assessment of the supporting features in the different disease groups was not blinded (some patients were known to the investigators from clinical practice), with potential for observer bias. However, definition and application of the supporting features was carefully planned and involved multiple investigators in 2 independent cohorts, thus reducing this possibility. For this study, we created specific definitions to standardize the application of the MOGAD supporting features in 2 different cohorts. Future revisions of the MOGAD criteria might benefit from a list of definitions to limit interpretative variability. Finally, pediatric patients were underrepresented in this study and may show a different frequency of the MOGAD supporting features compared with adults.

In conclusion, although patients with MS/CIS may fulfil the supporting features for MOGAD diagnosis at disease presentation, the risk of misdiagnosis in similar unselected cohorts is still low. A careful selection of patients with higher pretest probability to test for MOG-IgG remains crucial to minimize the risk of misdiagnosis, also taking into account the possible overlaps between MS and MOGAD that we have highlighted in our study. Future refinements of the MOGAD supporting features should take into account their relative frequency in patients with MS/CIS and potential integration with additional clinical-MRI features more specific of MOGAD.
