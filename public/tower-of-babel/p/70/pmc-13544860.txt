Prevalence of Depression among Children with Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy: A Cross-sectional Survey Study
Authors: Maddukuri Sriram, Neelu Desai, Vrajesh Udani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and Objectives:Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is a common muscular dystrophy of childhood with the devastating consequence of loss of independent ambulation by teenage years and death by the second or third decade. We aimed to determine the prevalence of depression among children with DMD and to explore the relationship of depression with age, socio-demographic parameters, family history, steroid use, and current motor ability of children.Methods:We performed a cross-sectional questionnaire survey in 50 boys with DMD (aged 8–16 years) in an outpatient clinic. Self-reported symptoms of depression were assessed using the children’s depression rating scale-revised.Results:We observed that 28% of children were clinically depressed, while 12% of children had subthreshold depressive symptoms. The cognitive domain of depression was observed to be the most affected in the majority of study participants. A significant association was found between socioeconomic status, positive family history of DMD, and school difficulties with co-existing depression (P < 0.05). Our results suggest that depression is a common comorbidity in children with DMD.Conclusions:We recommend regular psychological screening and appropriate intervention for every DMD child, with special emphasis on those belonging to extremes of socioeconomic classes and with a positive family history of DMD.

Introduction

Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is the most common muscular dystrophy in childhood affecting 19.8 in 1,00,000 live male births.[1] It occurs because of a nonfunctional dystrophin gene, leading to muscle fiber necrosis and ultimately replacement by adipose and connective tissue.[2] Progressive muscle degeneration leads to loss of independent ambulation, with most becoming wheelchair-bound by a mean age of 13 years.[3] Affected children also suffer from other primary complications such as spinal deformity, cardiomyopathy, and respiratory failure. Premature mortality is noted by the second or third decade.

Noninvasive positive pressure ventilation and other therapeutic measures such as prolonged glucocorticoid therapy have contributed significantly over the last few decades to improvement in the survival period.[4] Newer gene-targeted and exon-skipping therapies are yet out of reach for most children in developing nations (e.g., India). With improvement in the child survival period, the relevance of health-related quality of life (HRQOL) and other psychosocial aspects has been increasingly acknowledged in DMD.[2] Profound physical limitation, impaired schoolwork, social isolation, and dependency for routine activities may contribute to the development of secondary complications. Although the focus on primary complications is important in management, these patients also develop secondary conditions such as depression, anxiety, and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder that may further amplify their disease burden.[5] Recent literature states that deficiency of dystrophin protein contributes to some of these secondary comorbidities,[6] but psychological reactions to the illness (reactive symptoms) might be as, or even more, important. Validated self-reported measures are a way to assess the burden of these secondary conditions, by obtaining patients’ own perspectives rather than relying on caregiver’s opinions.[2]

To the best of our knowledge, there is very limited data on depression among DMD patients in resource-limited countries such as India.[7] Hence, our goal was to determine the prevalence of depression among Indian children and adolescents with DMD. We also evaluated the relationship of co-existing depression with socio-demographic parameters, family history, steroid use, and current motor ability.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was an outpatient study conducted in the pediatric neurology department at a tertiary care hospital in a metropolitan city in India between January 2021 and March 2022. Clearances from the institutional scientific research committee and institutional ethics committee were obtained (reference no. 1497-21-ND). A total of 50 children with genetically established diagnosis of DMD between 8 and 16 years of age were enrolled after written, informed consent from parents of every child and assent from children above 12 years of age. Children with known or clinically obvious mental disabilities and/or other additional medical comorbidities (asthma, epilepsy, etc.) were excluded from our study.

Scales used in this study

A pre-validated children’s self-reported depression scale, children’s depression rating scale-revised (CDRS-R), was used for assessing depression among DMD patients.

We utilized this scale as it is widely used for assessing depression among children and adolescents.[8] It is easy to administer and takes 15–20 min, containing 17 domains of symptom assessment covering behavioral, somatic, cognitive, and affective domains aligned with Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders – IV (DSM-IV) criteria for depression.[8910] It is available in shorter forms and has been successfully validated in several languages across the globe including India.[911] The total score ranges from 17 to 113, and a score of 30 or above (based on normative data from Indian adolescents) is indicative of depression.[9] Although this is a self-reported scale, a direct interview with children serves as an opportunity for early therapeutic intervention in children who appear withdrawn.

We considered an overall CDRS-R cutoff score of 30 for diagnosing clinical depression in our study, consistent with a study that validated this scale in Indian adolescents, as this score improved the test’s discriminant properties by attaining a sensitivity of 83% and specificity of 84% and is more applicable to the Indian population.[9] The mean CDRS-R score observed in their study was 23.1 for nondepressed children. Hence, we termed children with scores of 25–29 as borderline depressed or subthreshold depressed and in need of further evaluation by a professional psychologist.

Current motor ability of children was assessed using the Vigno’s rating scale for lower limbs. Scores range from 0 to 10, where a score of 1 indicates normal function (ability to walk and climb independently) and a score of 10 indicates severe impairment (bed ridden, dependent for activities of daily living). Patients were divided into ambulatory and nonambulatory groups based on their current motor ability. They were termed “ambulatory” if they were able to walk, at a minimum, with assistance or using long leg braces. While those termed “nonambulatory” were, at a maximum, able to stand with long leg braces, but could not walk even with assistance.

Socioeconomic status of the family was assessed using the modified Kuppuswamy scale,[12] which includes three broad domains, i.e., occupation, total family income, and education.

Data collection

Fifty children satisfying our inclusion criteria were enrolled through sequential sampling. Demographic and baseline characteristics, including socioeconomic status, family details, educational status, treatment history (current/past), and history of additional comorbidities, were recorded. CDRS-R was administered on each of these children. This scale was translated into a local Indian language best understood by the child to help him clearly understand the domains. Each interview took approximately 20 min.

Statistical analysis

Data was recorded in MS Excel. Results for quantitative variables were expressed as mean [±standard deviation (SD)], median [range], and interquartile range (IQR). The results for qualitative variables were expressed as frequency and percentage. Chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test were used to analyze associations between co-existing depression and various variables. P ˂ 0.05 was considered statistically significant, wherever statistical tests were used. Variables showing association with univariate analysis and those of clinical relevance were included in the multivariate logistic regression model to identify independent predictors of depression. Due to a small sample size and limited number of outcome events, the model was restricted to key variables to avoid overfitting. MedCalc v22 was utilized.

Clinical depression was observed in 17% children in the younger age group (8–12 years) and 42% children in the older age group (13–16 years). On evaluating the medication history, we observed that 54% (n = 27) of the enrolled children were taking corticosteroids either alone or in conjunction with multivitamins. Parents of 6% (n = 3) children had shifted to other forms of treatment such as ayurveda and homeopathy. The mean duration of medication use was found to be 3.77 ± 1.81 years (range: 0.4–9). Among those taking corticosteroids, 33% had clinical depression and 18% had borderline depression. Most of the children were being followed up in clinic either 6 months (44%) or ≥12 months (46%), with only a minority (5%) coming at the recommended 3 months. Of the enrolled children, 66% (n = 33) were receiving physiotherapy either at home or at a hospital.

A majority (60%, n = 30) of the children were nonambulatory, while 40% (n = 20) of the children were ambulatory, and half of them needed orthotic aids (n = 10). Of the nonambulatory children, 56% (n = 28) were wheelchair-bound, while 4% (n = 2) were bed-bound. Mean Vigno’s scale score observed was 8 ± 0.4. Clinical depression was present in 25% of ambulatory children and 30% of nonambulatory children.

Only 2 patients (4%) had respiratory issues, one of whom also had scoliosis. None were using any kind of respiratory support devices, and no child had severe back pain that could indicate vertebral fractures or symptomatic scoliosis.

We found a significant correlation between depression and the family’s socioeconomic status (P < 0.05), impaired schoolwork (P < 0.01) and a positive family history of DMD (P < 0.01). However, no significant association was found between depression and age (P = 0.14), family type (P = 0.31), motor ability (P = 0.36), and steroid use (P = 0.13). On multivariate logistic regression analysis, lower socioeconomic status was independently associated with higher odds of depression (adjusted odds ratio = ~3.8). Positive family history of DMD showed a very strong association with depression; however, due to the complete separation of data (i.e., all patients with positive family history had depression), a stable adjusted odds ratio could not be estimated.

Discussion

DMD is a chronic, life-limiting degenerative muscle disorder. With an increasing focus on medical management, including gene replacement therapy, the survival period has significantly increased in these children.[4] Affected children may have associated secondary psychosocial complications that may augment their disease progression. Despite being at risk of developing these complications, the management of mental health is often neglected with the predominant focus on managing medical complications, especially in resource-limited nations.

Our study suggests that DMD leads to impaired psychosocial health and depression with a major effect on the cognitive domain of depression. Although recent literature discusses the contributory role of dystrophin deficiency in some of these comorbidities, the observed phenotype is likely a reactive phenomenon resulting from the chronic progressive nature of the disease. Supporting this statement, Yao et al.[13] reported a similar prevalence of depressive symptoms (25.2%) in children with another progressive disorder—spinal muscular atrophy—aged 8 to 18 years, using the Depression Self-Rating Scale for Children. These findings further emphasize that the observed affective symptoms may be more reflective of a reactive process rather than direct brain involvement, as proposed in DMD children.

The symptoms of depression appear to vary according to the age group. Our findings demonstrate higher rates of depressive symptoms in the older age group (13–16 years) though these findings are not statistically significant probably due to the small sample size. This may be brought on as age-related cognitive development along with progressive symptoms of muscle weakness makes patients realize the enormity of the problems they face. The loss of independent ambulation is a watershed moment when all hope may be lost from a patient’s perspective. This understanding aligns with the finding from a survey investigating parents’ perceptions of their children with DMD: parents are most affected emotionally at the time of ambulation loss.[14]

Most studies have considered neither a positive family history of DMD in a sibling or relative nor the family’s socioeconomic status while assessing secondary psychosocial comorbidities in these children.[21516] In developing countries like India, where the majority of the population comes from lower or middle-class backgrounds, our finding of a significant correlation between depressive symptoms and lower socioeconomic class is relevant because of the unequal distribution of healthcare resources across socioeconomic strata. Even a community-based study from northern India found that 14.5% of healthy school-going children aged 11–18 exhibited depressive symptoms, with a significantly higher burden in the lower-middle socioeconomic group compared with their more affluent peers.[17] This suggests that lower socioeconomic status itself is a key driver of depressive symptoms. Parents and families usually bear the entire financial burden with little or no state funding. In chronic disabling conditions, such as DMD, that necessitate prolonged, often expensive medical support, this lack of resources coupled with out-of-pocket expenses can negatively impact the quality of care that the child receives. A significant association between a positive family history of DMD and co-existing depression can be attributed to the child’s enhanced understanding of disease progression as observed in their affected sibling.

Social isolation and absenteeism from school have a major negative impact on the psychological well-being of children with DMD. A little less than half the children (22/50) in our study population were not involved in any kind of educational activities. There is also a need for increased awareness among peers. In resource-limited settings, the lack of infrastructure (e.g., ramps and elevators) for these differently abled children causes physical barriers for mobility, further adding to school absenteeism. Individualized education plans should be developed in collaboration with parents to decrease school absenteeism and to prevent social detachment.

In our study, corticosteroids were initiated at a mean age of 8.2 years, which is slightly higher (7 years) than that reported by Conway et al.[18] The use of corticosteroids has significantly delayed the rate of disease progression in DMD patients across the western world. The same is not evident in developing countries due to delays in symptom recognition and treatment initiation as well as poor compliance. Our study revealed that about 51% of those taking corticosteroids showed depressive symptoms. However, we did not find a significant statistical correlation between child’s corticosteroid use and depressive symptoms, which is consistent with Conway et al.’s study.[18]

None of the enrolled children underwent psychological screening at any point in time nor received any form of counseling. This emphasizes the need for regular psychological assessment, early identification, and treatment of these secondary mental health disorders in this population.[16]

No statistically significant correlation was observed between motor ability and co-existing depression. Our observations are consistent with previous studies in DMD patients, indicating that the degree of physical impairment or the severity of the disease is not an accurate reflection of the psychosocial issues that these children face.[2]

On evaluating the behavioral symptoms of depression, we found a significant correlation between impaired schoolwork and co-existent depression. Majority of the children (76%) in our study cohort thought they were well socialized. Contrary to our results, Schmid et al.[2] reported significantly lower scores on social inclusion and exclusion scales while measuring HRQOL in DMD children between the ages of 8 and 12 years. This discrepancy can be attributed to the constant social interaction of DMD children with members of the extended family, which is often the case in India.

We observed no significant effect on somatic symptoms of depression in the study group. In our cohort, complaints of physical problems (42%) and mild fatigue (22%) were lower than those reported by Pangalila et al.,[15] where 40.5% of the cohort reported fatigue and 73.4% experienced pain. However, theirs was a predominantly adult cohort with a mean age of 28.2 years, much higher than ours.

Cognitive symptoms reflect the child’s internal emotional state, while affective symptoms are outwardly observable. In our cohort, the cognitive symptoms of depression were more prominently affected than the affective ones. Although most children did not overtly display depressive behaviors, many reported internal issues. About 14% claimed to experience extreme guilt, while moderate depressive feelings were reported by 36% of children. Approximately 22% of children indicated moderate affection in their self-esteem, although referring to oneself in derogatory terms may be strongly related to depression.[18] None of the enrolled children expressed suicidal thoughts. Only two (4%) children acknowledged experiencing mildly morbid thoughts. In our study population, affective symptoms of depression were not greatly affected, in line with the findings of Pangalila et al.[15]

We found clinical depression in 28% of the study population, while 12% of children were borderline depressed. This observation is consistent with a study by Latimer et al.,[19] who reported a prevalence of depression of 27.4%, which was higher than that reported by Pangalila et al.[15] and Conway et al.[18] with prevalences of 19% and 17%, respectively. On the contrary, Schmid et al.[2] reported that clinically relevant depressive symptoms were not found in DMD children. However, the comparison between our study and other studies is not ideal because most of the studies were conducted in culturally different backgrounds and varied healthcare settings; they also included adults in the study group and used different study methods.

Strengths and limitations

To the best of our knowledge, there is very limited data on depression among DMD patients in resource-limited nations. Most studies have taken the caretaker’s perspective into consideration while assessing the secondary conditions among affected children, which may underestimate or overestimate the disease burden. We aimed at a self-reported survey study along with socio-demographic parameters that significantly impact the medical management in developing countries.

The sample size of this study was relatively small. The intelligence quotient or achievement tests were not assessed separately in this study; however, children’s ability to attend school currently or in the past was considered adequate for completing the questionnaire. This study was cross-sectional; thus, the exact time of onset and duration of depression was not known.

Conclusions

We conclude that depression is a common comorbidity in children with DMD in our study cohort. Cognition seems to be the most affected domain. We encourage multi-disciplinary treatment, including regular psychosocial screening for all patients with special emphasis on children belonging to extremes of socioeconomic classes and those with positive family history.

Even today, mobility aids such as electronic wheelchairs remain expensive and out of reach for the majority of DMD children in India. Many educational institutions, public transportation systems, and recreational centers do not provide ramps and elevators, severely restricting the mobility of these children. We suggest that the state contribute to provide a better environment for people with disabilities with special financial assistance or subsidized medical care for chronic, relatively rare, disabling conditions such as DMD. Schools should raise awareness among peers regarding the disease and its physical and psychosocial implications to help promote a friendly environment in schools; for example, the institution of a “buddy” system where healthy children are encouraged to be a buddy of a disabled child to improve acceptance in the class.
