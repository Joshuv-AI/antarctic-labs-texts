Predictive value of different long-term (10-year) blood pressure parameters variability in cardiovascular disease: A prospective cohort study
Authors: Yixiu Chen, Hongmin Liu, Mo Zhang, Zhihui Liu, Qian Liu, Shihe Liu, Wenchao Yao, Yajing Liang, Yicheng Liao, Yufeng Bian, Renjie Fu, Shuohua Chen, Shouling Wu, Yuntao Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveWe aimed to compare the predictive value of long-term variability in systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and pulse pressure (PP) over a 10-year period for cardiovascular diseases (CVDs).MethodsA prospective cohort of 44,938 participants were categorized into four groups based on the quartiles of variability of SBP, DBP, and PP. Cox proportional hazards regression models were applied to assess the impact of SBP, DBP, and PP variability on CVDs. The predictive value of SBP, DBP, and PP variability for CVDs and specific cardiovascular outcomes was evaluated based on the China-PAR model.ResultsDuring a mean follow-up period of 5.04 years, compared to the Q1, the HR and 95 %CI of CVDs was 1.28 (1.11,1.48) in the Q4 of SBP, 1.33 (1.17,1.51) in the Q4 of DBP, and 1.25 (1.06,1.47) in the Q4 of PP (all P < 0.01). When incorporated into the China-PAR model, the C-index for SBP, DBP, and PP variability in predicting CVDs were 0.6866, 0.6787, and 0.6822, with SBP variability offering the strongest predictive improvement to the China-PAR model. Subgroup analysis revealed a significant interaction between age and SBP, DBP, and PP variability (P < 0.01).ConclusionIncreases in SBP, DBP, and PP variability independently elevate the risk of CVDs and its subtypes, regardless of absolute blood pressure levels. Furthermore, SBP, DBP, and PP variability can improve the predictive value of the China-PAR model for CVDs risk, with SBP variability demonstrating the strongest predictive capacity.Keywords: Blood pressure variability, 10-year, Predictive value, Compare, Cardiovascular Diseases

Introduction

Hypertension is a well-recognized risk factor for CVDs. Increases in SBP, DBP, and PP have all been associated with a higher risk of adverse cardiovascular events[1]. Studies have shown that starting from a SBP of 110 mmHg, each 10 mmHg increase in SBP raises the risk of cardiovascular events by 36 %. Similarly, each 10 mmHg increase in DBP and PP is associated with a 14 % and 23 % increase in cardiovascular event risk, respectively[2,3]. Moreover, the relationship between blood pressure and cardiovascular event risk is not solely dependent on blood pressure levels but is also independently and positively associated with blood pressure variability. Relevant studies indicate that both short-term (24-hour) and long-term (several months to years) increases in blood pressure variability elevate the risk of cardiovascular events[4,5]. A study examining three-year long-term variability in SBP and DBP found that, compared to the lowest tertile, the highest tertile of SBP and DBP variability was associated with a 31 % and 30 % increased risk of CVDs, respectively[6].

Although previous studies have extensively examined the association between blood pressure variability and cardiovascular risk and found the results were consistent, most of those have focused on short-term blood pressure variability. Even among studies investigating long-term blood pressure variability, the longest observation period has been limited to five years[[7], [8], [9], [10]]. However, blood pressure fluctuations persist throughout life, and short-term blood pressure variability does not fully reflect long-term blood pressure variability. To address this gap, this study utilized data from the Kailuan Study, adopting a prospective cohort design. Building on previous research, this study is the first to incorporate PP variability into the analysis, aiming to compare the predictive value of 10-year long-term variability in SBP, DBP, and PP for CVDs risk.

Method

Study population

The data used in this study were derived from the Kailuan Study, the detailed design of which has been described elsewhere [[11], [12], [13]]. Beginning in 2006–2007, the Kailuan General Hospital and its 11 affiliated hospitals conducted biennial health examinations for active and retired employees, with blood pressure measurements performed on all participants. Since 2006, annual follow-ups have been conducted to monitor incident status of CVDs, including myocardial infarction and stroke.

This study included individuals who had completed health examinations in both 2006 and 2016 and had participated in at least one additional examination during this period, with complete blood pressure data. Finally, we included 44,938 participants after further excluding 6166 individuals with a history of CVDs or cancer. The inclusion and exclusion of study participants is showed in Fig. 1. This study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kailuan General Hospital. All participants provided written informed consent.

Study Object Selection Flow Chart.

BP variability

Blood pressure measurements were conducted and recorded by trained physicians and nurses using a calibrated desktop mercury sphygmomanometer to measure the right brachial artery blood pressure. Measurements were taken between 7:00 and 9:00 AM on the day of the health examination. Participants were instructed to refrain from smoking, tea, or coffee consumption within 30 min before the measurement. After resting in a seated position for 15 min, blood pressure was measured three times at 1–2 min intervals, with the average value as the final blood pressure value. SBP was determined by the first Korotkoff sound, and DBP by the fifth Korotkoff sound. PP was calculated as the difference between SBP and DBP.

Since 2014, blood pressure measurements have been conducted using the HEM-8102A electronic sphygmomanometer manufactured by Omron (Dalian) Co., Ltd [14]. Studies have demonstrated good alignment between desktop sphygmomanometers and electronic blood pressure monitors[15,16]. Blood pressure variability was calculated based on methods from previous studies[[17], [18], [19]]. In previous studies, statistical measures for quantifying variability have included the standard deviation (SD), coefficient of variation (CV), average real variability (ARV), and variation independent of the mean (VIM). This study employed the standard deviation as the primary method for calculating blood pressure variability, calculated using the formula: SD=1n−1Σi−1n(xi−x¯)2, and the group status is presented in Table 1, Table A1, and Table A2. The calculation methods for the other variability metrics are detailed in Data A1.

Follow-up and identification of endpoint events

The follow-up period began at the time of the 2016 health examination, with the occurrence of CVDs as the study endpoint. CVDs was defined as myocardial infarction and stroke, with stroke further categorized into ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke. For individuals experiencing multiple CVDs, the outcome was defined as the first event and its corresponding time. In the specific endpoint analysis of CVDs, myocardial infarction and stroke were treated as two separate endpoints, with the first occurrence of each event and its timing recorded accordingly. For participants who did not experience any events, the follow-up period was considered complete as of December 31, 2022. If a participant died during follow-up without experiencing a CVD event, the follow-up period ended at the time of death.

The diagnosis of myocardial infarction was based on clinical symptoms, changes in serum concentrations of myocardial enzymes and biomarkers, and electrocardiographic findings, according to the criteria defined by the World Health Organization[20]. Stroke events were diagnosed based on neurological signs, clinical symptoms, and neuroimaging examinations, including computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). If imaging results indicated an infarct lesion, the event was classified as an ischemic stroke; if a hemorrhagic lesion was detected, it was classified as a hemorrhagic stroke[21]. Information on cardiovascular events was collected annually by trained medical personnel from hospitals affiliated with the Kailuan Group and designated municipal health insurance hospitals. All diagnoses were independently confirmed by two specialized physicians based on hospital records. The investigating physicians were blinded to the baseline information of the study subjects. All endpoint events were preliminarily categorized according to the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes. While mortality data were obtained from the social security system.

Covariate definitions

The design of the epidemiological survey and methods for anthropometric measurements have been described in previously published studies [22]. Participants were fasted for at least 8 h before venous blood samples were collected from the elbow in the morning of the health examination day. Biochemical parameters, including fasting blood glucose (FBG), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), total cholesterol (TC), uric acid (UA), and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (Hs-CRP), were measured using a Hitachi 7600 automatic analyzer. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) creatinine equation[23]. Hypertension was defined as meeting any of the following criteria during the exposure period: (1) a documented history of hypertension, (2) a history of hypertension with current antihypertensive medication uses and normal blood pressure levels, or (3) no history of hypertension but SBP≥140 mmHg and/or DBP ≥90 mmHg at the health examination[24]. Diabetes was defined as FBG ≥7.0 mmol/L, a documented history of diabetes, or current use of antidiabetic medication despite FBG <7.0 mmol/L[25]. Hyperlipidemia was defined as TC≥5.17 mmol/L, a documented history of hyperlipidemia, or current use of lipid-lowering medication despite TC <5.17 mmol/L[26]. Smoking was defined as smoking an average of at least one cigarette per day in the past year or having quit smoking for less than one year. Alcohol consumption was defined as consuming an average of at least 100 mL of liquor (alcohol content >50 %) per day over the past year, with a duration of at least one year. Physical exercise was defined as engaging in physical activity at least three times per week, with each session lasting for least 30 min. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²).

Statistical methods

Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s), and intergroup comparisons were conducted using analysis of variance (ANOVA). Skewed continuous variables were expressed as median (P25, P75), and intergroup comparisons were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis non-parametric rank-sum test (We assessed the normality of continuous variables based on their skewness and kurtosis. Variables were considered approximately normally distributed if the absolute value of skewness was <1 and the absolute value of kurtosis was <2.). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, with intergroup comparisons conducted using the chi-square test.

Kaplan-Meier analysis was used to calculate the cumulative incidence of CVDs in different groups, and the Log-rank test was used for intergroup comparisons. Cox proportional hazards regression models were applied to analyze the effects of SBP variability, DBP variability, and PP variability on CVDs risk. The first quartile group was used as the reference, and the model adjusted for variables including sex, age, smoking, alcohol consumption, physical exercise, use of antihypertensive, antidiabetic, and lipid-lowering medications, BMI, FBG, TG, LDL-C, Hs-CRP, serum creatinine, and baseline SBP, DBP, and PP. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals, with no violations detected. Restricted cubic spline plots were used to analyze the dose-response relationship between SBP, DBP, and PP variability and CVDs by setting knots at the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles.

Stratified analyses were conducted based on age (≤60 or >60), hypertension status (yes or no), and diabetes status (yes or no). Cox models were used to explore potential multiplicative interactions between SBP, DBP, and PP variability and these factors. To assess the robustness of the findings, the following sensitivity analyses were conducted: (1)Excluding participants who were taking antihypertensive, antidiabetic, or lipid-lowering medications, as well as smokers and alcohol consumers, and repeating the Cox regression analysis.(2)Examining the effects of other variability indices on CVDs risk.(3) Treating mortality during follow-up as a competing event and using the Fine-Gray model to analyze the effects of SBP, DBP, and PP variability on CVDs risk.(4)Using restricted cubic spline plots to analyze the long-term changes in SBP, DBP, and PP by setting knots at the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles. The long-term changes were assessed by slope of SBP, DBP, and PP.

The predictive value of SBP, DBP, and PP variability for CVDs and its specific endpoints was assessed using the China-PAR model [27]. The variables of China-PAR model included age, treated or untreated SBP, TC, HDL-C, current smoking status, diabetes, WC, region (north or south), urban or rural residence, family history of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), and interaction between age and SBP, smoking status, and family history of ASCVD. C-index, net reclassification index (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were calculated for the following models: (1) China-PAR model alone, (2) China-PAR model + SBP variability, (3) China-PAR model + DBP variability, (4) China-PAR model + PP variability, (5) China-PAR model with SBP, DBP, and PP variability included simultaneously. To further assess the predictive value of different blood pressure variability parameters for CVDs across age groups, participants were stratified into three age categories: <45 years, 45–59 years, and ≥60 years.

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina) and R (version 4.2.1). A two-sided P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Among 44,938 eligible participants, 76.94 % were male, with a mean baseline age of 58.03 ± 11.20 years. The general characteristics of the study participants according to the quartiles of SBP variability are presented in Table 1. Compared to the lowest quartile group, individuals with higher SBP variability were more likely to be male, habitual smokers, have a lower level of education, and be diagnosed with hypertension or diabetes. Additionally, BMI, FBG, cholesterol, and hs-CRP levels exhibited an increasing trend with rising SBP variability. Similar trends were observed across the quartiles of DBP and PP variability, as shown in Table A1-A2. The comparison of baseline characteristics between participants with missing systolic or diastolic blood pressure data and the study subjects included in this research is presented in Table A3.

Association between blood pressure variability and CVDs risk

During a mean follow-up period of 5.04 years, 1999 cases (4.45 %) of CVDs were recorded, including 325 cases (0.72 %) of myocardial infarction and 1697 cases (3.78 %) of stroke. The cumulative incidence of CVDs increased with higher variability in all blood pressure parameters, with statistically significant differences between groups (Log-rank test, P < 0.01) (Fig. 2). Compared to the Q1 group, the incidence density (per 1000 person-years) in the highest quartile of SBP, DBP, and PP variability was 13.86, 11.50, and 13.34 per 1000 person-years, respectively. After adjusting for potential confounders, blood pressure variability remained significantly associated with an increased risk of CVDs. Compared to the Q1 group, the HRs and 95 %CIs for the highest quartiles of SBP, DBP, and PP variability were 1.28 (1.11, 1.48), 1.33 (1.17, 1.51), and 1.25 (1.06, 1.47), respectively (P < 0.01), as shown in Table 2. The restricted cubic spline (RCS) curve demonstrated a significantly positive correlation between increasing blood pressure variability and CVDs risk (Figure A1).

Analysis of specific CVDs endpoints revealed that the cumulative incidence of myocardial infarction and stroke increased with rising blood pressure variability, with statistically significant differences between groups (P < 0.01, Log-rank test), as shown in Figures A2-A3. The risk of stroke increased with greater blood pressure variability, with adjusted HRs (95 % CIs) of 1.26 (1.07, 1.47), 1.37 (1.20, 1.58), and 1.19 (1.00, 1.42). However, in the subtype analysis of myocardial infarction, only SBP and PP variability demonstrated similar associations, whereas DBP variability did not show a significant correlation with myocardial infarction (Table A4-A5).

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

The results of the subgroup analysis are presented in Table 3. Overall, age exhibited a significant interaction with SBP, DBP, and PP variability on the risk of CVDs (P < 0.01), with a stronger association observed in individuals aged ≤60 years. However, no significant interactions were found between hypertension, diabetes, and blood pressure variability.

In sensitivity analyses, we excluded individuals taking antihypertensive, antidiabetic, and lipid-lowering medications, as well as those who were habitual smokers, alcohol consumers, or had high-sodium dietary preferences. The result of sensitivity analyses was consistent with the primary findings (Table A6). When we assess the variability by other variability indices, including coefficient of variation (CV), average real variability (ARV), and variation independent of mean (VIM), similar associations were observed only in DBP variability (Table A7, Figure A4). Taking account of the impact of mortality on CVDs risk, we conducted a competing risk model analysis, which did not truly affect the primary results (Table A8). Additionally, to assess the impact of directional changes in SBP, DBP, and PP on CVDs risk, we treated the slopes of these blood pressure parameters as continuous variables. And after multivariable adjustment, a U-shaped dose-response relationship was observed between each blood pressure parameter and CVDs (Figure A5).

Predictive value of blood pressure variability for CVD risk

Based on the China-PAR model, the C-index (95 % CIs) for SBP, DBP, and PP variability in predicting CVDs were 0.6866 (0.6758, 0.6974), 0.6787 (0.6678, 0.6895), and 0.6822 (0.6712, 0.6932), respectively. Furthermore, SBP variability demonstrated slightly superior discrimination and risk reclassification compared to DBP and PP variability, with IDI (95 % CI) values of 0.0011 (0.0007, 0.0015), 0.0001 (0.0001, 0.0005), and 0.0001 (0.0001, 0.0012), respectively, and NRI (95 % CI) values of 0.1737 (0.1289, 0.2185), 0.1119 (0.0671, 0.1567), and 0.0852 (0.0405, 0.1300). When SBP, DBP, and PP variability were included simultaneously, the C-index (95 % CI) increased to 0.6869 (0.6761, 0.6977). Similar results were observed for myocardial infarction and stroke events (Table 4). Further analysis stratified by age showed that SBP variability consistently had the strongest predictive performance across all age groups (Table A9).

Discussion

The primary findings of this study indicate that increased long-term variability in the three commonly used blood pressure parameters—SBP, DBP, and PP—is positively associated with an increased risk of CVDs, independent of the corresponding absolute blood pressure levels. Furthermore, the association between SBP variability and CVDs risk is stronger than that observed for DBP and PP variability, with SBP variability providing the greatest improvement in the predictive value of the China-PAR model.

In this study, we found that higher long-term variability in SBP, DBP, and PP was independently associated with an increased risk of CVDs, myocardial infarction, and stroke. A meta-analysis including 36 cohort studies with over 10 million participants demonstrated that increased long-term SBP variability was associated with standardized hazard ratios of 1.18, 1.10, and 1.15 for incident CVDs, coronary heart disease, and stroke, respectively[5]. Another large cohort study involving >2 million individuals found that individuals with greater SBP variability had a higher risk of coronary heart disease and stroke[28]. Additionally, a study by Muntner et al., which observed 25,814 participants over 28 months, reported that increased DBP variability was associated with HR of 1.36 and 1.38 for coronary heart disease and stroke, respectively[8]. Currently, research on the relationship between increased long-term PP variability and CVDs remains limited. Only Poortvliet’s study found that increased PP variability was associated with stroke, with a HR of 1.60[29]. However, this study had a relatively small sample size and, despite assessing long-term variability, had an exposure period of only one year. In contrast, our study extended the exposure period for PP variability to 10 years and demonstrated that increased PP variability was not only associated with a higher risk of stroke but also positively associated with an increased risk of myocardial infarction. These findings suggest that PP variability may be an important predictor of CVDs risk and contribute to the development of more effective prevention and treatment strategies for CVDs, addressing a gap in research regarding the relationship between long-term PP variability and CVDs risk.

Incorporating 10-year long-term SBP, DBP, and PP variability into the China-PAR risk prediction model increased the C-index by 0.0111, 0.0032, and 0.0067, respectively (P < 0.01), and improved the NRI for CVDs risk prediction by 0.1737, 0.1119, and 0.0852, respectively (P < 0.01). When all three variability parameters were included simultaneously, the C-index increased by 0.0114 (P < 0.01). Rothwell et al. reported in the ASCOT-BPLA trial that SBP variability is a strong predictor of cardiovascular events and is more effective than DBP variability in predicting stroke [30]. While previous studies have identified PP variability as an independent risk factor for stroke, the research investigating predictive efficacy of PP variability for overall CVDs risk is lacking. Furthermore, no prior study has compared the predictive value of SBP, DBP, and PP variability for CVDs risk using the China-PAR model. Although the improvement in the China-PAR model’s predictive performance was relatively small, the differences were statistically significant. It is noteworthy that the NRI for SBP variability was 0.1737, indicating that approximately 17.37 % of patients could be more accurately reclassified—a proportion with potential clinical significance, particularly for intermediate-risk individuals where treatment decisions remain uncertain. When comparing the C-index and NRI for the three blood pressure variability parameters, SBP variability provided the greatest improvement to the China-PAR model’s predictive value. This may be especially useful for identifying high-risk patients with normal mean BP but high variability, who are often overlooked in current practice. Additionally, incorporating SBP, DBP, and PP variability simultaneously into the model further improved the predictive power of the model, slightly surpassing the use of SBP variability alone. These results suggest that long-term variability in SBP, DBP, and PP can serve as independent predictors, offering incremental predictive value for CVD risk stratification. At the same time, these variability metrics could be seamlessly integrated into EHR systems using existing BP records, enabling risk stratification without additional clinical burden. Without altering standard clinical practices, utilizing SBP, DBP, or PP variability for CVDs risk prediction appears to be feasible.

Moreover, our subgroup analysis revealed an age-dependent relationship between increased blood pressure variability and CVDs risk. In middle-aged and young individuals, increased SBP and DBP variability exhibited a significant association with CVDs risk, whereas in individuals over 60 years old, this association was more pronounced with increased PP variability. Although no prior research has explicitly examined whether the relationship between blood pressure variability and CVDs differs by age, the Framingham Heart Study was the first to reveal that the importance of SBP and PP in predicting coronary heart disease risk increases with age, while the relative importance of DBP declines. Among individuals aged ≤50 years, DBP was a better predictor of coronary heart disease than SBP, but in those aged ≥60 years, SBP and PP became the primary predictive factors[31]. Similar conclusions were drawn by Khattar et al. [32]., they have examined the association between the 24-hour mean values of SBP, DBP, and PP and CVDs risk. However, single blood pressure measurements are subject to randomness and may not fully capture the long-term impact of blood pressure fluctuations on CVD risk. Our study, by assessing 10-year long-term blood pressure variability, revealed distinct effects of SBP, DBP, and PP variability on CVDs risk across different age groups, providing new evidence for age-stratified CVDs risk assessment with greater reliability and clinical relevance.

Although the underlying mechanisms linking blood pressure variability and CVDs risk are not yet fully understood, previous studies suggest that this relationship is likely mediated by atherosclerosis and complex activities of the autonomic nervous system (ANS). First, repeated fluctuations in blood pressure and blood flow can damage the vascular endothelial cells, leading to endothelial dysfunction, platelet activation, and inflammatory responses, all of which contribute to the progression of atherosclerosis and structural and functional alterations in the heart[9,10,33,34]. Second, long-term exposure to high-pressure environments leads to vascular wall thickening and hardening, while the threshold of baroreceptors in the carotid sinus increases, impairing the regulation of blood pressure and triggering excessive activation of the sympathetic nervous system along with reduced vagal tone. This autonomic nervous system dysregulation is significantly associated with endothelial dysfunction, myocardial hypertrophy, and an increased risk of cardiovascular events[35,36]. Third, with aging, vascular relaxation capacity declines, arterial stiffness increases significantly, and the impact force of cardiac contractions on arterial walls intensifies[37]. Additionally, in older populations, wave reflection increases and peripheral resistance decreases, leading to higher SBP, lower DBP, and increased PP[38,39]. These changes result in differential effects of SBP, DBP, and PP variability on CVDs risk across age groups.

The strengths of this study include its large-scale prospective cohort design, based on the Kailuan Study, which is the first to incorporate PP variability into the analysis. Considering that long-term blood pressure variability better reflects blood pressure fluctuations, we extended the exposure period to 10 years. This study is also the first to apply the China-PAR risk prediction model within the same cohort to evaluate the predictive performance of SBP, DBP, and PP variability for CVDs risk. Our findings demonstrate that blood pressure variability improves the predictive efficacy of the China-PAR model, addressing gaps in previous research and aiding in the identification of the most appropriate risk markers for CVDs prediction. Additionally, our study conducted age-stratified subgroup analyses, revealing that the impact of blood pressure variability on CVDs risk is closely related to age, which may help clinicians develop individualized treatment strategies for patients of different age groups.

Nevertheless, this study has some limitations. Although we adjusted for several confounding factors, residual or unmeasured confounding cannot be completely ruled out due to the observational nature of the study. Moreover, the study population was derived from health examination data of active and retired employees of the Kailuan Group, with a predominantly male cohort, introducing potential selection bias. Since the study cohort was derived from an occupational setting with a predominantly male workforce, the findings may not be directly generalizable to women. Further research in more gender-balanced populations is needed to confirm these associations. Finally, it is important to acknowledge that the exclusive use of the China-PAR model to estimate diagnostic performance is a limitation. Due to potential differences in genetic background, lifestyle, and environmental factors, our findings may not be directly generalizable to other racial or ethnic populations. The applicability of our results to other internationally established risk prediction models, such as SCORE2, also requires further investigation. Therefore, the generalizability of our findings needs to be validated in more diverse populations in future studies.

In conclusion, long-term 10-year blood pressure variability is significantly and positively associated with a higher risk of CVDs and its subtypes. Increased SBP, DBP, and PP variability independently elevate the risk of CVDs and its subtypes, independent of absolute blood pressure levels. And, incorporating SBP, DBP, and PP variability into the China-PAR model improves its predictive performance for CVDs risk, with SBP variability demonstrating the strongest predictive value.
