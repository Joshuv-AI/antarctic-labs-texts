Fertility and mode of delivery after ileal pouch‐anal anastomosis for ulcerative colitis: A population‐based study
Authors: Mantaj S Brar, Jane Jungyoon Park, Anthony de Buck van Overstraeten
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsFertility and mode of delivery are major concerns for women with ulcerative colitis (UC) undergoing ileal pouch‐anal anastomosis (IPAA). Although infertility after IPAA has been attributed to pelvic adhesions, the impact of minimally invasive approaches remains uncertain. Similarly, evidence guiding Caesarean section (CS) versus vaginal delivery is limited.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, population‐based study using Ontario health administrative databases (1994–2022). Female UC patients aged 18–45 years undergoing IPAA were matched by birth year, parity and rural residence to UC controls (without surgery) and non‐UC controls. Primary outcome was live birth after IPAA. Subgroup analyses included nulliparous patients and laparoscopic versus open IPAA. Among patients with live births, delivery modes were compared.ResultsWe included 1,094 eligible women with IPAA (median age 32). After IPAA, 13.8% had ≥1 live birth, significantly lower than UC controls (27.2%; RR 0.53, 95% CI 0.46–0.60) and non‐UC controls (22.5%; RR 0.53, 95% CI 0.47–0.59). Among nulliparous women, live birth rates remained significantly reduced (18.5% vs. 32.7% in UC controls; RR 0.66, 95% CI 0.57–0.77). Laparoscopic IPAA did not significantly increase live birth rates (13% vs. 10%, RR 1.41, 95% CI 0.75–2.65), though the number of births per patient was higher. CS rates were substantially elevated in women who delivered after IPAA versus controls.ConclusionsIn this population‐based study, IPAA for UC was associated with significantly reduced live birth rates compared with both controls. Laparoscopic IPAA did not eliminate fertility disadvantage, though higher birth numbers suggest possible benefit. CS rates were markedly higher after IPAA.Keywords: delivery, epidemiology, ileal pouch‐anal anastomosis, pregnancy, ulcerative colitis

What does this paper add to the literature?

Ulcerative colitis often affects women of reproductive age. Ileal pouch‐anal anastomosis (IPAA) has been associated with reduced fertility. This population‐based study demonstrates significantly lower live‐birth rates after IPAA compared with controls. Laparoscopy did not mitigate fertility reduction. Findings highlight the impact of IPAA on reproductive health, supporting discussions on family planning.

INTRODUCTION

Half of women are younger than 35 years at the time of diagnosis of ulcerative colitis (UC) [1]. Fertility in women with UC is therefore a major concern and remains generally normal before surgery [2]. After ileal pouch‐anal anastomosis (IPAA), however, female infertility has been reported to increase three‐fold, with rates up to 63% [3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. The leading mechanism is thought to be pelvic adhesions, from pelvic dissection during IPAA, which impair Fallopian tube function [8, 9]. Laparoscopy lessens pelvic adhesion formation after IPAA and thereby reduces infertility, as suggested by two smaller studies, but the evidence remains limited [10, 11].

Most studies on fertility after IPAA are single‐centre and retrospective, with relatively few women attempting to conceive. Even the largest cohort included 323 patients, of whom only 153 tried to conceive [12]. Studies reporting on population‐based cohorts also include relatively small samples [13]. Generalizability is further constrained by methodological heterogeneity—varying definitions of fertility, different methods for data capture and survey response bias—limiting precise estimation of any protective effect of laparoscopy.

Another contentious issue post IPAA surgery is whether women should be delivered via Caesarean section (CS) or vaginal delivery (VD) [14]. Owing to concerns about anal sphincter injury with vaginal delivery and its impact on pouch function, many surgeons recommend CS. A Swedish population‐based study reported a two‐fold increase in Caesarean section after IPAA [15], with rates around 50%, although a meta‐analysis concluded that vaginal delivery appears safe [16]. As with fertility, these studies are largely single‐centre with small numbers, hampering robust comparison of outcomes.

Accordingly, the objectives of our study were to measure:

live birth rates—as a surrogate for fertility—among reproductive‐age women who underwent IPAA for UC in Ontario, compared with population controls and with women with UC who did not undergo surgery.

live birth rates—as a surrogate for fertility—among reproductive‐age women who underwent IPAA for UC in Ontario, compared with population controls and with women with UC who did not undergo surgery.

the proportion of Caesarean section deliveries among women with IPAA in Ontario, compared with women with UC who did not undergo surgery.

the proportion of Caesarean section deliveries among women with IPAA in Ontario, compared with women with UC who did not undergo surgery.

METHODS

This retrospective population‐based cohort study employed linked administrative data from Ontario, Canada, held at the Institute for Clinical Evaluative Sciences (ICES)—an independent, non‐profit research institute whose legal status under Ontario's health information privacy law allows it to collect and analyse health care and demographic data, without consent, for health system evaluation and improvement. Data were accessed through the ICES Data & Analytic Services division. The population of Ontario was approximately 11 million in 1994 and 15 million in 2022. We employed the following linked databases to characterize baseline characteristics, exposures and outcomes: Discharge Abstract Database (DAD), Same Day Surgery (SDS), Ontario Health Insurance Plan Claims Database (OHIP), National Ambulatory Care Reporting System (NACRS), Registered Persons Database (RPDB), Ontario Cancer Registry (OCR), Ontario Crohn's and Colitis Cohort (OCCC), and MOMBABY. Ethics approval was obtained from the Mount Sinai Hospital Research Ethics Board. This study conforms to the RECORD statement for reporting observational studies using routinely collected data.

We selected female patients aged 18–45 years old who underwent IPAA surgery for UC from 1994 to 2022 using the Ontario Crohn's and Colitis Cohort and the Ontario Health Insurance Plan Claims database (IPAA‐group) [17]. The validated algorithm used to generate the Ontario Crohn's and Colitis Cohort has demonstrated a sensitivity of 76.8% and specificity of 96.2% for identifying UC patients using administrative data [17]. Patients with a history of colorectal cancer, identified using the Ontario Cancer Registry and patients with a revision or redo IPAA were excluded. The IPAA‐group was matched with two different control groups who did not receive an IPAA: the first group consisted of patients with UC without previous IPAA surgery or any bowel surgery (UC‐group; 1:1) and patients without inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and no history of bowel surgery (non‐UC‐group; 1:4). We matched patients from the IPAA‐group with both unexposed groups (UC‐group and non‐UC‐group) together, matching for birth year (to control for both age and time of intervention), parity at the age of IPAA (nulliparous women were defined as women without any record of a live birth after 20 weeks of gestation—GA) and rural residence (defined as living in communities with a population of less than 10,000). The index date for the unexposed groups (UC‐group and non‐UC‐group) was the corresponding age at which the IPAA occurred in the IPAA‐group, in an attempt to better define the attributable risk of infertility associated with the IPAA surgery. We excluded exposed patients if suitable matched unexposed patients were not identified.

Statistical analysis

We calculated descriptive statistics with proportions for categorized data, and medians with inter‐quartile ranges (IQR) or means and standard deviations (sd) for continuous data as appropriate. We calculated the proportion of patients who had a live birth after IPAA surgery for the exposed cohort. For the unexposed control groups, we matched them to the exposed cohort. The control groups' observation window began at the same year as their matched pair in the exposed group had pouch surgery (to account for both confounding by age and calendar year). We calculated the rate ratio between the exposed and unexposed groups using a modified‐Poisson model. Patients were censored at age 50, death or at the end of follow‐up period (31 December 2022). Lastly, we wanted to examine the age of first live birth between the three groups without matching for parity between the exposed and unexposed groups (while still matching for birth year and rural residence); to observe whether UC patients with IPAA and without surgery were delaying pregnancy due to their diagnosis, compared with population controls. All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

RESULTS

We identified 1154 female patients with UC who underwent IPAA surgery between the ages of 18 through 45 years old during the study period. After excluding 60 patients (5.2%) who could not be matched, 1094 patients were included in the IPAA‐group. The median age at time of IPAA was 32 (IQR: 26–38) years, with 94.9% of the cohort from urban areas. Among patients in the IPAA‐group, patients from the lowest income quintile neighbourhoods were slightly under‐represented when compared to population controls (13.53% vs. 21.48%). At the time of IPAA, 61.15% of the IPAA‐group had not had a live birth (Table 1). The patients in the IPAA‐group were matched with 1094 patients in the UC‐group and 4376 in the non‐UC group. Median follow‐up time was 13.0 years for the IPAA‐group, 12.6 years for the UC‐group and 12.2 years for the non‐UC group.

Live birth after IPAA

Following IPAA surgery, 151 patients (13.80%) had at least one live birth, compared with 298 (27.24%) patients in the UC‐group and 984 (22.49%) in the non‐UC‐group (Table 2). This corresponds to a significantly lower rate of live birth among IPAA patients compared to UC‐group patients (RR 0.53, 95% CI 0.46–0.60) and non‐UC‐group patients (RR 0.53, 95% CI 0.47–0.59). The median age at first born for patients with IPAA was 29 (IQR: 25–31) years and did not significantly differ between IPAA patients and the UC‐group (p = 0.09) and the non‐UC group (p = 0.77). Among IPAA patients who had a live birth after IPAA surgery, the mean number of live births was 1.70 (sd: 1.03), with a median of 1 (IQR: 1–2) live birth and did not significantly differ between IPAA patients and the unexposed groups (UC‐group and non‐UC‐group, p = 0.90 and p = 0.46, respectively).

Nulliparous patients

Of the 669 (61.15%) patients who had no live births prior to IPAA surgery, 124 (18.54%) had at least 1 live birth after IPAA. This was significantly lower than in the UC‐group (32.74%; RR 0.66; 0.57–0.77) and the non‐UC‐group (26.05%; RR 0.62; 0.55–0.71) (Table 3), when matched by birth year and rural status.

Laparoscopy versus open IPAA

A total of 406 eligible patients underwent IPAA surgery between 2006 and 2023, of which 125 (30.79%) underwent a laparoscopic IPAA. The proportion of patients with a live birth after IPAA did not significantly increase with a minimally invasive approach (13% vs. 10%, RR 1.41, 95% CI 0.75–2.65, p = 0.28). In addition, the mean number of live births after IPAA was not significantly different in those who underwent a minimally invasive approach (1.66 vs. 1.08, p = 0.09) (Table 4).

Mode of delivery after IPAA

A total of 151 patients had a live birth after IPAA surgery, and 142 of these patients had no prior history of caesarean section prior to their IPAA surgery. Among these 142 patients, 70.4% of the deliveries were by caesarean section, significantly higher than the observed proportion of caesarean section in the UC‐group (37.2%, p < 0.0001) and among the non‐UC group (33.3%, p < 0.0001) without a prior history of caesarean section.

DISCUSSION

This population‐based study including all eligible patients in Ontario examined the proportion of live births before and after IPAA surgery in females between 18 and 45 years old. We observed a significantly lower rate of live birth among women who underwent IPAA compared not only with the general population, but also with women diagnosed with UC who had not undergone pouch surgery. Within the subgroup of patients having received a laparoscopic pouch, the proportion of women achieving at least one live birth after surgery was similar to those who underwent open surgery; however, the median number of live births was higher in the laparoscopic group. Finally, significantly more women delivered by Caesarean section after IPAA than the control groups.

A majority of patients with IPAA for UC (61%) had no history of live births at the time of surgery. This likely reflects the relatively young age at which surgery is performed, as UC often presents during the reproductive years. As a result, many women delay childbearing until after surgery, despite preserved fertility in UC [12, 18]. Voluntary childlessness is well documented in UC and has been linked to gaps in reproductive knowledge [19, 20, 21]. Selinger et al., using the CCPKnow tool—a validated, self‐administered IBD and pregnancy‐related knowledge assessment tool—in a large cohort in the United Kingdom, demonstrated that lower pregnancy‐related knowledge in the context of IBD specifically was associated with voluntary childlessness [22]. Similarly, Huang et al. reported comparable findings in Canadian women with IBD, using the same tool, underscoring the impact of disease‐specific reproductive knowledge gaps on family planning decisions [23]. This may in part explain the high childlessness in our population before IPAA surgery.

In addition to a relatively low proportion of live births prior to surgery, we also found a significantly lower rate of live births after IPAA compared with UC patients without IPAA and with healthy controls. While voluntary childlessness may explain part of this difference, infertility attributable to surgery must also be considered. IPAA surgery is well established as being associated with reduced fertility in women, largely due to pelvic adhesions leading to tubal infertility [8, 13, 24, 25]. This has prompted some surgeons to delay pouch surgery until after childbearing or, in selected cases, to perform an ileorectal anastomosis as a fertility‐preserving strategy, with the option of later conversion to IPAA in persistently symptomatic patients [25].

Several studies have suggested that laparoscopic surgery may reduce postoperative infertility risk by limiting pelvic adhesions [10, 11]. However, in our study, laparoscopic IPAA did not significantly increase the proportion of women with live births to open IPAA, similar to a recently published population health analysis from Denmark [26]. Although factors such as voluntary childlessness may contribute to these findings, our results suggest that laparoscopic surgery does not fully mitigate the fertility risks in IPAA. Importantly, live birth is only a surrogate marker for fertility, and caution is warranted in drawing strong conclusions.

Finally, the proportion of women with an IPAA who delivered by Caesarean section was higher than among women with UC who did not undergo surgery and the healthy controls. This finding is consistent with prior studies and likely reflects concerns—shared by clinicians and patients—about sphincter injury and subsequent pouch dysfunction. Although a similar functional outcome between patients with Caesarean section and vaginal delivery has been reported in a systematic review, reduced anal sphincter pressures have been described after vaginal birth, raising the possibility of diminished continence over time [16, 27]. In another study, colorectal surgeons strongly preferred Caesarean delivery for women with pouches; although this preference was less pronounced among obstetricians and gastroenterologists, it may help explain the high Caesarean rate observed in our cohort [14]. Conversely, some modern series have demonstrated a high proportion of vaginal deliveries among IPAA patients, with a low rate of complications [28]. Unfortunately, further stratified analyses on mode of delivery and their effects on pouch function were limited by small numbers.

This study has notable strengths, including its population‐based design and large sample size. Nevertheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. Administrative health data lack clinical granularity, including information on disease severity, the presence of postoperative complications or patient‐reported reproductive intentions. We were unable to assess the use of fertility treatments (such as in‐vitro fertilization), which may have impacted our estimates of infertility unrelated to or related to the surgery. Although validated algorithms minimize misclassification of diagnoses and procedures, some degree of error is possible. Finally, as with all observational studies, residual confounding cannot be excluded.

CONCLUSION

In this large, population‐based study, IPAA for UC was associated with significantly reduced live birth rates compared with both UC patients without surgery and non‐UC controls. Laparoscopic IPAA did not eliminate the fertility disadvantage, though higher birth numbers suggest possible benefit. CS rates were markedly higher after IPAA, reflecting prevailing concerns regarding vaginal delivery. These findings highlight the reproductive impact of IPAA and underscore the need for informed preoperative counselling.
