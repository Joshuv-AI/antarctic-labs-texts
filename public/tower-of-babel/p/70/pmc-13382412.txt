Biomaterials at the interface of bone-derived factors and inter-organ communication: Current evidence and future perspectives
Authors: Luli Ji, Fuwei Zhu, Xiaogang Wang, Jing Wang, Changsheng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBone is increasingly recognized not as a passive structural scaffold but as a dynamic endocrine organ that secretes a diverse repertoire of bone-derived factors (BDFs) which establish communication axes with the brain, kidney, cardiovascular system, immune network and metabolic organs. Harnessing this endocrine potential for therapy requires technologies that interface with skeletal secretory programs with spatial, temporal and biological precision. Bioactive materials have emerged as a promising platform for this purpose. However, complete examples demonstrating that biomaterials regulate distant organ function through clearly defined BDF-dependent mechanisms remain limited; therefore, this Review discusses biomaterial-based intervention as an emerging framework built on current evidence from local skeletal repair, BDF-related marker regulation and controlled release studies. Concurrently, artificial intelligence may assist osteokine discovery, guiding biomaterial design and enabling patient-specific digital twins of bone–organ axis dynamics. This Review synthesizes current understanding of BDF-mediated inter-organ communication, critically evaluates biomaterial strategies and clinical lessons from BDF-related therapeutics, and proposes a framework for developing biomaterials at the interface of skeletal endocrine regulation and inter-organ communication, with attention to validation requirements, translational safety and off-target systemic effects.Keywords: Bone-derived factors, Inter-organ communication, Materiobiology, Biomaterial strategy

Introduction

The skeleton has long been regarded as a passive structural scaffold, primarily responsible for providing mechanical support, protecting vital organs, and serving as a mineral reservoir for calcium and phosphate homeostasis. However, this traditional view has undergone a profound transformation over the past two decades. Pioneering work, particularly from the Karsenty laboratory, has established bone as a bona fide endocrine organ that actively participates in whole-body physiology through the secretion of a diverse array of signaling molecules [[1], [2], [3]]. This conceptual shift was initially driven by the discovery that bone-derived fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23) could regulate phosphate homeostasis in the kidney [4]. Subsequent research has revealed that bone cells, particularly osteocytes which constitute 90–95% of all bone cells in the adult human skeleton, serve as master orchestrators not only of local skeletal remodeling but also of systemic physiological processes extending far beyond the skeleton itself [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. The recognition that the skeleton functions as an integral component of the endocrine system has fundamentally reframed our understanding of bone biology, positioning it at the center of a complex inter-organ communication network that coordinates metabolic, neural, immune, and cardiovascular functions [10,11].

Central to this endocrine function are bone-derived factors (BDFs), a collective term encompassing cytokines, hormones, metal ions, extracellular vesicles (EVs), and other proteins or peptides released from the skeletal system into the circulation. Among the most extensively characterized BDFs are osteocalcin (OCN), FGF23, the receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL) and its decoy receptor osteoprotegerin (OPG), sclerostin, and lipocalin 2 (LCN2). These factors exhibit remarkable functional diversity, establishing distinct bone–organ axes that collectively orchestrate systemic homeostasis [1,4,8,[12], [13], [14], [15]]. More recently, bone-derived EVs have emerged as novel vehicles for inter-organ communication, carrying microRNAs and proteins that modulate distant organ function [[16], [17], [18], [19], [20]]. Through these diverse molecular mediators, bone exerts targeted regulatory control over the brain, kidney, cardiovascular system, immune system, metabolic organs, and even reproductive tissues [11].

The growing recognition of bone as a systemic regulatory hub has opened unprecedented opportunities for therapeutic intervention, yet translating this knowledge into clinical applications remains challenging. Direct administration of recombinant BDFs or their antagonists faces significant hurdles, including short circulating half-lives, poor bioavailability, off-target effects, and the difficulty of achieving tissue-specific delivery without disrupting physiological feedback loops. In this context, bioactive materials have emerged as a transformative platform for precisely modulating BDF-mediated signaling. Recent advances in Materiobiology, a discipline studying how biomaterial properties influence biological functions, have demonstrated that engineered materials can achieve sophisticated regulation of the bone microenvironment through biomimetic design and functionalization strategies [21].

Nevertheless, direct evidence demonstrating that biomaterials can modulate distant organ function through BDF-dependent endocrine mechanisms remains limited. Most currently available biomaterial studies have focused on local skeletal regeneration, modulation of the bone immune microenvironment, controlled delivery of osteogenic factors, or changes in local markers such as OCN, sclerostin, RANKL and OPG. Whether these local material-induced changes are sufficient to reshape circulating BDF profiles and thereby regulate the function of distant organs remains largely unresolved. Therefore, this Review does not aim to present biomaterial-mediated BDF regulation as an established therapeutic paradigm. Instead, we propose a conceptual and translational framework that integrates current advances in bone endocrinology, materiobiology, controlled delivery systems, engineered extracellular vesicles, organ-on-chip models and artificial intelligence-guided biomaterial design. By distinguishing validated evidence from emerging hypotheses, we seek to clarify the opportunities, limitations and key validation criteria required for developing biomaterials that can precisely modulate BDF-mediated inter-organ communication (Fig. 1).

Bone-derived factors orchestrate systemic organ crosstalk and can be modulated by bioactive materials. Bone functions as an endocrine regulatory organ by releasing BDFs. These signals connect bone with multiple distant organs, forming bone–kidney, bone–brain, bone–metabolic, bone–cardiovascular, bone–immune/hematopoietic and bone–muscle axes. Current biomaterial evidence mainly supports local modulation of BDF-related signaling through exogenous delivery and material-induced regulation of skeletal secretion. Future strategies may further enable bone–organ axis reconstruction and closed-loop systems for studying and potentially regulating BDF-mediated inter-organ communication.

Bone as a regulatory hub: classification and secretion of BDFs

Cellular constituents and secretory functions of bone tissue

Bone tissue comprises three principal cell types that orchestrate skeletal homeostasis through coordinated activities: osteoblasts, osteoclasts, and osteocytes [22]. Osteoblasts, derived from mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), are located along bone surfaces and exhibit prominent secretory and bone-forming functions [23,24]. These cells synthesize the organic bone matrix, which consists predominantly of type I collagen (∼90%) and non-collagenous proteins including bone sialoprotein II (BSPII), OCN, fibronectin, osteonectin, and osteopontin (OPN) [23]. Osteoclasts, in contrast, are multinucleated giant cells of hematopoietic origin that are specialized for bone resorption (Fig. 2) [25]. Beyond their resorptive function, osteoclasts actively participate in cell-cell communication by secreting stage-specific molecules that modulate osteoblast activity and contribute to the coupling of bone resorption and formation [[26], [27], [28]].

Cellular sources and regulatory control of bone-derived factors. BDFs originate from multiple cellular and matrix compartments within bone, which can be grouped into bone-enriched endocrine osteokines with relatively strong evidence for skeletal cellular origin, bone-associated but non-bone-specific factors and bone-derived extracellular vesicles, mineral ions and matrix-associated factors, and their secretion is regulated by mechanical loading, remodeling activity, aging, inflammation and metabolic stress.

Osteocytes, which constitute approximately 90–95% of all bone cells in the adult human skeleton, represent the most abundant and functionally versatile cell population in bone [28,29]. Once regarded as terminally differentiated dormant cells lacking metabolic functions, osteocytes are now recognized as highly active multifunctional cells indispensable for normal skeletal function [30,31]. These cells reside within the mineralized bone matrix and form an extensive interconnected lacunar-canalicular network, enabling them to sense mechanical stimuli and coordinate the activities of osteoblasts and osteoclasts on bone surfaces [[32], [33], [34]]. Critically, osteocytes possess robust secretory functions that extend beyond local skeletal regulation to influence distant organs via paracrine and endocrine pathways [8,35]. This recognition has positioned osteocytes as master orchestrators of both skeletal homeostasis and systemic physiological regulation.

Definition and classification of bone-derived factors

BDFs, also referred to in some contexts as osteokines, comprise a heterogeneous group of soluble molecules, EVs, ions and matrix-associated mediators that are produced, stored, released or functionally regulated within the skeletal microenvironment (Fig. 2). In response to the broad and sometimes ambiguous use of this term in the literature, we define BDFs in this Review in a stratified rather than uniform manner. Specifically, we distinguish among three categories: (i) bone-enriched endocrine osteokines with relatively strong evidence for skeletal cellular origin, circulating release and distant-organ activity; (ii) bone-associated but non-bone-specific factors that are produced by bone-resident cells but are also widely expressed in other tissues and often act through local autocrine or paracrine mechanisms; and (iii) bone-derived EVs, ions and matrix-associated factors that reflect the structural and remodeling status of bone and may contribute to local or systemic signaling under specific physiological or pathological conditions.

The first category comprises bone-enriched endocrine osteokines, which are secreted predominantly by bone-resident cells and have relatively well-characterized systemic actions. A quintessential example is undercarboxylated OCN (Glu-OCN), a 49-amino-acid protein belonging to the bone γ-carboxyglutamate family. It is produced predominantly by mature osteoblasts and odontoblasts and is characterized by post-translational γ-carboxylation at three specific glutamic acid residues [36]. Its undercarboxylated form has been implicated in endocrine regulation of metabolism, brain function, muscle adaptation and male fertility. FGF23 represents another major endocrine factor within this category. This 251-amino-acid phosphatonin, a member of the FGF19 subfamily, is secreted primarily by osteocytes and to a lesser extent by osteoblasts [37]. Notably, FGF23 requires the transmembrane protein αKlotho as an obligate co-receptor to engage its cognate fibroblast growth factor receptors with high affinity, thereby regulating phosphate and vitamin D metabolism in distant organs, particularly the kidney [38]. Sclerostin, encoded by the Sost gene, is a 22-kDa secreted glycoprotein with a cystine-knot fold characteristic of the DAN family of glycoproteins. Because its expression in the adult skeleton is highly enriched in osteocytes, sclerostin is generally regarded as a representative osteocyte-derived secretory factor, although its systemic effects remain context-dependent. LCN2 adds further structural and functional diversity to this group. Secreted by osteoblasts, LCN2 is a 25-kDa glycoprotein of the lipocalin superfamily, characterized by an eight-stranded β-barrel fold that creates a hydrophobic pocket capable of binding and transporting small lipophilic ligands [39,40].

The second category includes bone-associated but non-bone-specific cytokines and signaling proteins. These molecules are produced or regulated by bone-resident cells and can influence bone remodeling, osteoimmunology and local skeletal repair, but they are not exclusive to bone and may also be generated by immune cells, vascular cells, adipocytes, hepatocytes, neurons, stromal cells or tumor-associated cells. Therefore, their designation as BDFs requires explicit qualification of cellular source, disease context and mechanism of action. RANKL is a representative example. It is a type II transmembrane protein of the tumor necrosis factor (TNF) superfamily, expressed on the plasma membrane of osteoblasts, osteocytes, and activated T lymphocytes [41]. A soluble circulating form can be generated through proteolytic cleavage from the cell surface [42]. RANKL signaling is tightly governed by its soluble decoy receptor, OPG. OPG is a secreted member of the TNF receptor superfamily that entirely lacks a transmembrane anchor, enabling it to function as a diffusible neutralizing sink [43,44]. Although the RANKL/OPG system is central to skeletal remodeling and osteoimmune regulation, its expression in immune and vascular contexts underscores that it should not be interpreted as a bone-specific endocrine system in all settings. Similarly, OPN, bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs), C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 12 (CXCL12), Prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), Dickkopf-1 (DKK1) and platelet-derived growth factor-BB (PDGF-BB) are important bone-associated mediators, but their widespread tissue distribution and context-dependent biological activities require caution when discussing them as BDFs. For example, the bone matrix serves as a reservoir for BMPs, a substantial subgroup within the transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) superfamily, among which BMP-2, BMP-4, BMP-6, and BMP-7 are particularly abundant [45]. These signaling molecules are synthesized as large precursor proteins that undergo proteolytic processing to liberate the mature, biologically active C-terminal domain [46].

The third category consists of bone-derived EVs, ions and matrix-associated mediators. Unlike soluble proteins, bone-derived EVs are lipid bilayer-delimited particles released by osteoblasts, osteoclasts and osteocytes into the bone microenvironment and, in some cases, the systemic circulation [35,47,48]. These vesicles span a range of sizes and biogenic origins from endosome-derived exosomes (30–150 nm) and plasma membrane-shed microvesicles (100–1000 nm) to larger apoptotic bodies (1–5 μm), but it is their molecular cargo that defines their skeletal identity [49]. Bone-derived EVs are enriched in osteoclast-regulatory microRNAs (e.g., miR-214-3p, miR-23a), fragments of bone matrix proteins such as OCN and alkaline phosphatase, and even membrane-bound RANKL [48,50]. This selectively packaged payload positions bone-derived EVs as organ-specific vectors capable of transmitting the functional status of the skeleton to distant anatomical sites. In addition, bone releases a repertoire of metal ions and matrix-associated factors that are stored within the mineralized extracellular matrix and liberated during bone remodeling. The most abundant are calcium (Ca2+) and inorganic phosphate (PO43−), the primary constituents of bone hydroxyapatite. Trace ions including magnesium (Mg2+), zinc (Zn2+), and strontium (Sr2+) are also mobilized from the matrix reservoir, contributing to the local ionic milieu within the skeletal microenvironment [[51], [52], [53], [54], [55]]. These ions contribute to the local ionic milieu and may influence skeletal cell behavior, vascular calcification, mineral metabolism and tissue repair.

Recent advances in multi-omics approaches have substantially expanded the catalog of known BDFs. A landmark study by Liang et al. integrated RNA-seq, single-cell RNA-seq, and proteomics analyses across multiple biological models including normal adult mice, aged mice, mechanically stimulated mice, and cultured osteoblasts to systematically identify osteokines involved in global homeostasis [56]. This comprehensive approach identified 375 potentially functional BDFs and precisely mapped their cellular origins, thereby establishing a dynamic network of osteokines that influence bone remodeling and systemic homeostasis. The study also revealed previously unrecognized osteokines, such as fatty acid-binding protein 3 (FABP3), which mediates senescence-related communication between aged osteoblasts and vascular smooth muscle cells, exemplifying the expanding landscape of bone-derived signaling.

Regulation of BDF secretion: mechanotransduction, bone remodeling, and aging

The secretion of BDFs is not constitutive but rather tightly regulated by multiple physiological inputs, ensuring that bone's endocrine output is appropriately matched to systemic demands. Among the most critical regulatory mechanisms is mechanotransduction, the process by which osteocytes convert mechanical strain into biochemical signals [57]. The osteocyte lacunar–canalicular network functions as a mechanosensory system, sensing fluid shear stress generated by interstitial fluid flow during mechanical loading [[58], [59], [60]]. This mechanical stimulation activates intracellular Ca2+ oscillations in osteocytes, which trigger downstream signaling cascades that modulate the secretion of key BDFs [61,62]. Specifically, mechanical loading suppresses sclerostin expression, thereby disinhibiting Wnt/β-catenin signaling and promoting bone formation, while simultaneously enhancing the production and release of EVs containing bone regulatory proteins [63]. Experimental evidence demonstrates that blocking Ca2+ signaling in vivo significantly attenuates the anabolic bone formation response to mechanical loading, underscoring the critical role of Ca2+-mediated mechanotransduction in regulating BDF secretion and bone adaptation [61]. Other mechanical contexts also influence osteocyte-derived factors. In orthodontic tooth movement, mechanical tension induces osteocyte autophagy and increases FGF23 secretion, thereby promoting osteogenesis [64]. In OCY454 osteocyte cultures, steady fluid flow (SFF) stimulates intracellular calcium oscillations and induces load-duration-dependent release of sclerostin, OPG and RANKL. Notably, mechanically stimulated osteocytes may release RANKL in EV-associated form, whereas OPG and sclerostin are predominantly released directly into the extracellular medium [65]. These findings suggest that mechanical cues can differentially regulate both the amount and packaging route of BDF-related molecules. Future biomaterial systems could exploit this principle, but additional work is required to connect material-induced local secretion with circulating BDF profiles and distant-organ responses.

Bone remodeling, the continuous process of bone resorption followed by formation, represents another fundamental regulator of BDF secretion. This process is conducted by the basic multicellular unit (BMU), wherein osteoclast-mediated resorption precedes osteoblast-mediated formation on the same bone surface [66]. Active bone remodeling phases are mechanistically associated with the release of bone-derived endocrine factors into the circulation [[67], [68], [69]]. Osteoclasts release coupling factors at each stage of the remodeling sequence including factors liberated from the bone matrix through resorptive action as well as proteins synthesized de novo by osteoclasts [70,71]. For instance, the acidic microenvironment generated during osteoclastic bone resorption facilitates the decarboxylation of OCN, converting it from the carboxylated matrix-bound form (Gla-OCN) to the undercarboxylated endocrine-active form (Glu-OCN) that enters the circulation [72,73]. This coupling between bone turnover and BDF activation provides a direct mechanistic link between skeletal metabolic activity and systemic endocrine output.

Aging exerts profound effects on BDF secretion patterns, contributing to age-related multi-organ dysfunction. Recent evidence demonstrates that aged osteoblasts undergo senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) changes, characterized by altered secretion of specific osteokines including FABP3, which transmits senescence signals to vascular smooth muscle cells [56]. Moreover, aging and metabolic diseases including type 2 diabetes, liver disease, and cancer disrupt the coordinated secretion of osteokines, promoting bone loss and altered osteokine levels [[74], [75], [76], [77], [78], [79], [80]]. Notably, osteocyte-derived sclerostin levels increase with aging and have been implicated in cognitive decline among elderly individuals and those with Alzheimer's disease [8], establishing a direct mechanistic link between age-related changes in BDF secretion and neurodegenerative pathology. These findings underscore the importance of understanding BDF secretion dynamics across the lifespan for developing targeted interventions against age-associated multi-system disorders (Fig. 2).

BDF-driven organ communication

Having established the cellular sources and secretory dynamics of bone-derived factors in the preceding chapter, we now turn to the functional architecture of inter-organ communication orchestrated by these molecular mediators. The endocrine actions of BDFs are best understood not as isolated signaling events, but rather as integrated regulatory axes through which the skeleton maintains systemic homeostasis in concert with distant organs. In this section, we do not aim to provide an exhaustive catalogue of all bone–organ axes. Instead, we focus on representative BDF-mediated communication routes that are most relevant to biomaterial intervention, either because they involve relatively well-characterized endocrine factors, clinically actionable pathways, material-responsive secretory programs, or target-organ functions that may be modeled using biofabricated systems (Table 1).

Representative BDF axes relevant to biomaterial design

Bone–kidney axis: phosphate and Vitamin D homeostasis

Among all bone–organ axes, the bone–kidney axis represents the most extensively characterized and clinically significant endocrine circuit, centered on the FGF23–Klotho signaling system (Fig. 3). FGF23 functions as the master regulator of phosphate homeostasis and vitamin D metabolism [[104], [105], [106], [107]]. The molecular basis of FGF23 action involves its binding to FGF receptor (FGFR) with membrane-bound αKlotho serving as an obligate co-receptor [38,108,109]. The Klotho–FGFR1c–FGF23 ternary complex is indispensable for high-affinity FGF23 signaling in the kidney and parathyroid gland, enabling precise regulation of mineral metabolism [38,110].

Bone–kidney axis. Osteocyte-derived FGF23 enters the circulation and acts on the kidney as a major endocrine target. Under physiological conditions, this bone–kidney axis regulates phosphate handling, vitamin D metabolism and systemic mineral homeostasis through feedback between bone and kidney. In CKD-MBD, nephron loss, phosphate retention, reduced renal Klotho and FGF23 resistance disrupt this regulatory loop, leading to excessive FGF23 secretion, hyperphosphatemia, secondary hyperparathyroidism and disordered bone turnover. Therapeutic strategies targeting FGF23 signaling or restoring Klotho function may help rebalance this dysregulated endocrine circuit.

In the kidney, FGF23 exerts two principal endocrine actions. First, it suppresses sodium-dependent phosphate reabsorption in the proximal tubule by downregulating the expression of type IIa and IIc sodium-phosphate cotransporters (NaPi-IIa and NaPi-IIc), thereby promoting phosphaturia [111]. Second, FGF23 inhibits the renal 1α-hydroxylase (CYP27B1) while stimulating 24-hydroxylase (CYP24A1), leading to reduced circulating levels of active vitamin D (1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D3) [112]. This coordinated regulation establishes a negative feedback loop wherein elevated serum phosphate and 1,25(OH)2D3 stimulate FGF23 production by osteocytes, which in turn suppresses both phosphate reabsorption and vitamin D activation [113,114].

The physiological significance of the FGF23–Klotho axis is most dramatically illustrated in the context of chronic kidney disease-mineral and bone disorder (CKD-MBD) [115,116]. In CKD, progressive nephron loss leads to phosphate retention, which stimulates a compensatory increase in FGF23 secretion [117,118]. However, concomitant reduction in renal Klotho expression renders the kidney progressively resistant to FGF23 action, creating a pathological state characterized by hyperphosphatemia, secondary hyperparathyroidism, and disordered bone turnover [119]. Therapeutic strategies targeting this axis, including FGF23 modulation and Klotho supplementation, have been explored for restoring mineral balance, but their effects are highly disease-context dependent and require careful safety evaluation [119,120]. For example, whereas FGF23 inhibition is clinically effective in conditions such as X-linked hypophosphatemia, FGF23 neutralization in CKD may disrupt compensatory phosphate regulation and produce adverse outcomes.

Beyond the FGF23–Klotho system, other bone-derived factors also participate in bone–kidney communication. Sclerostin, primarily known as an osteocyte-derived negative regulator of bone formation via Wnt/β-catenin antagonism, has been implicated in renal pathophysiology, though its precise role remains under investigation [121,122]. Recent evidence suggests that the PHEX–DMP1–MEPE pathway in osteocytes functions as an upstream regulator of FGF23 expression, adding further complexity to the bone–kidney regulatory network [123,124].

Collectively, these findings establish the bone–kidney axis as a central regulator of mineral homeostasis, wherein skeletal signals direct renal phosphate handling and vitamin D metabolism. Disruption of this endocrine circuit, as occurs in chronic kidney disease, transforms a precisely tuned feedback loop into a driver of systemic mineral dysregulation. Because FGF23 has a defined skeletal source, measurable circulating levels, established renal targets and clinically relevant functional readouts, the bone–kidney axis provides one of the clearest templates for testing biomaterial-mediated BDF intervention. Potential material strategies include local modulation of osteocytic FGF23 secretion, controlled delivery of FGF23 antagonists or Klotho-related agents, and bone–kidney-on-chip systems for testing phosphate-regulatory responses. However, direct evidence that biomaterials can regulate kidney function through bone-derived FGF23 remains limited. Future studies should therefore move beyond local FGF23 expression and assess serum FGF23 dynamics, renal target engagement, mineral metabolism and BDF-dependent causality.

Bone and metabolic system: energy homeostasis and glucose metabolism

The skeleton also participates in systemic energy metabolism by releasing factors that act on the pancreas, adipose tissue, liver, skeletal muscle and hypothalamus. Compared with the bone–kidney axis, the bone–metabolic axis is more heterogeneous and more context dependent, because several metabolic mediators are not bone-specific and may also be produced by adipose tissue, liver, skeletal muscle, immune cells or vascular cells. Therefore, in this section we focus primarily on factors for which the evidence of skeletal origin and systemic metabolic function is relatively strong, particularly OCN, LCN2 and sclerostin (Fig. 4).

Bone and the metabolic system. Bone-derived metabolic factors coordinate systemic energy homeostasis by acting on multiple metabolic organs. OCN supports pancreatic β-cell function and insulin secretion and also contributes to skeletal muscle nutrient utilization and exercise capacity. LCN2 acts on the hypothalamus to regulate appetite, food intake and body weight. Sclerostin, DKK1, IL-11 and SLIT2 participate in adipose tissue remodeling, insulin sensitivity, hepatic lipid metabolism and muscle metabolic adaptation. Through these coordinated endocrine signals, bone influences glucose homeostasis, body weight control, lipid metabolism, energy expenditure and metabolic disease susceptibility, including obesity, type 2 diabetes and fatty liver disease.

OCN remains the prototypical mediator of bone-metabolic communication. Glu-OCN, as the endocrine-active isoform, signals through the G protein-coupled receptor GPRC6A to exert pleiotropic metabolic effects [125]. In the pancreas, Glu-OCN stimulates β-cell proliferation and enhances glucose-stimulated insulin secretion through a cyclin D1-dependent mechanism [81]. Mice lacking OCN (Ocn−/−) develop glucose intolerance, hyperlipidemia, and increased adiposity, phenotypes that are reversed by exogenous Glu-OCN administration [81]. In white adipose tissue, Glu-OCN promotes the expression and secretion of adiponectin, an insulin-sensitizing adipokine that enhances fatty acid oxidation and glucose uptake in peripheral tissues [126]. This induction proceeds via GPRC6A-mediated cAMP accumulation, subsequent PAK activation, and downstream Src/ERK/CREB signaling, culminating in PPARγ-dependent transcriptional activation of the adiponectin gene [126]. Notably, the dose-response relationship is biphasic: low-dose Glu-OCN (≤10 ng/mL) promotes adiponectin expression, whereas high-dose exposure (≥20 ng/mL) triggers Fas/FasL-mediated necroptosis in adipocytes [127]. In the liver, Glu-OCN suppresses de novo lipogenesis and promotes fatty acid oxidation, thereby attenuating hepatic steatosis in diet-induced obesity models [128]. In skeletal muscle, OCN signaling enhances glucose and fatty acid uptake and utilization, contributing to improved exercise capacity and metabolic flexibility [129]. Critically, this muscle–bone crosstalk operates as a feed-forward loop: exercise stimulates OCN release from bone, which in turn promotes myofiber nutrient uptake and the secretion of IL-6 from exercising muscle; muscle-derived IL-6 then signals to osteoblasts to enhance RANKL expression, driving osteoclast differentiation and further liberating bioactive Glu-OCN from the bone matrix [130]. This integrated circuit positions OCN as both a sensor and an effector of physical activity.

LCN2 represents another bone-derived factor with metabolic activity. LCN2 was initially characterized as an adipokine promoting insulin resistance. Subsequent studies revealed that osteoblasts express LCN2 at levels at least ten-fold higher than white adipocytes, establishing bone as its primary source [131]. Osteoblast-derived LCN2 crosses the blood–brain barrier and acts on hypothalamic neurons via the melanocortin 4 receptor (MC4R) to suppress appetite and reduce food intake [132,133]. Beyond its anorexigenic function, circulating LCN2 maintains glucose homeostasis and β-cell function. Mice overexpressing LCN2 in osteoblasts (Col1a1-Lcn2Tg) exhibit decreased food intake, reduced fat mass, and improved glucose metabolism, whereas systemic LCN2 silencing exacerbates hyperphagia, weight gain, and glucose intolerance in both db/db and diet-induced obese mice [87]. In humans, serum LCN2 correlates positively with insulin levels and β-cell function, yet paradoxically also with body mass index and insulin resistance, reflecting the complex interplay between LCN2's metabolic actions and the underlying pathophysiological state [87].

Sclerostin, an osteocyte-enriched Wnt antagonist, has also been implicated in adipose tissue biology and systemic glucose metabolism. Clinical evidence demonstrates that circulating sclerostin concentrations rise progressively with advancing age, as documented in healthy adult cohorts [84]. Furthermore, serum sclerostin exhibits a positive correlation with adiposity, showing associations with body mass index, fat mass, and particularly visceral adipose tissue accumulation [84]. In individuals with impaired glucose regulation, sclerostin levels are elevated and correlate with whole-body insulin resistance, reflecting decreased insulin sensitivity across skeletal muscle, hepatic, and adipose tissue compartments [134]. Mice with global Sost deletion (Sost−/−) exhibit high bone mass, reduced adiposity, and enhanced insulin sensitivity, whereas sclerostin overexpression drives adipocyte hypertrophy and glucose intolerance [135]. Mechanistically, sclerostin modulates adipogenesis through dual pathways: it activates BMP signaling in white adipose tissue while simultaneously inhibiting Wnt/β-catenin signaling in bone marrow adipocyte progenitors [135,136]. However, sclerostin biology is complex. Conditional deletion studies indicate that its metabolic effects depend on the cellular compartment in which Wnt co-receptor signaling is altered, and a subpopulation of bone marrow adipose lineage cells can also express sclerostin [137,138]. Therefore, although sclerostin is highly relevant to bone-derived endocrine regulation, its systemic metabolic effects should be interpreted in a cell-type- and context-dependent manner.

Other bone-associated factors may also participate in metabolic regulation. FGF23, canonically recognized for its role in phosphate and vitamin D homeostasis, can influence inflammatory and metabolic pathways, including hepatocyte FGFR4 signaling and cytokine production [139]. DKK1, a Wnt antagonist produced by osteolineage cells and other tissues, has been implicated in metabolic bone disease and hepatic lipid metabolism [94,[140], [141], [142]]. Similarly, molecules such as OPN, BMPs and Irisin have documented metabolic functions, while much of the available evidence reflects production by adipose tissue, liver, skeletal muscle, immune cells or local metabolic tissues rather than direct skeletal endocrine output.

The bone–metabolic axis is relevant to biomaterial design because OCN, LCN2 and sclerostin are closely linked to osteoblast maturation, osteocyte mechanotransduction and remodeling-dependent skeletal secretion. Existing material studies have shown that surface chemistry, topography, stiffness, fluid flow, ion release and immunomodulatory cues can regulate local OCN expression, sclerostin secretion, RANKL/OPG balance and osteogenic differentiation. These findings suggest that biomaterials may influence metabolic BDF-related outputs by reshaping the local bone secretory environment. However, most studies have not further examined circulating active BDF levels or distant metabolic outcomes, such as glucose tolerance, insulin sensitivity, adipose remodeling, hepatic lipid metabolism or muscle nutrient utilization. Therefore, the material-based regulation of the bone–metabolic axis remains an important but incompletely validated direction, and representative biomaterial strategies will be discussed in detail in the following section.

Bone and the immune/hematopoietic system: osteoimmunology and niche regulation

The bone marrow functions as the body's primary hematopoietic organ, housing hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) and immune progenitor cells within a specialized microenvironment where skeletal and immune cells coexist and reciprocally regulate one another through shared cytokines, chemokines, and receptors. The interdisciplinary field of osteoimmunology, which formally emerged in the early 2000s, has illuminated the intricate interactions between the skeletal and immune systems, revealing that bone cells are not merely passive residents of the marrow cavity but active participants in immune cell development, homeostasis, and pathological responses (Fig. 5).

Bone and the immune/hematopoietic system. The bone marrow niche provides a specialized microenvironment in which bone-resident cells regulate hematopoiesis and immune homeostasis. Osteoblasts, osteocytes, osteoclasts and stromal cells release bone-derived signals, including RANKL/RANK/OPG, IL-7, CXCL12, DKK1 and OPN, to regulate HSC retention, niche anchoring, progenitor maintenance and immune-cell differentiation. These signals support lymphoid and myeloid development while also shaping immune tolerance, inflammatory responses and inflammation-associated bone loss.

The RANKL/RANK/OPG system serves as the cornerstone of osteoimmunology, bridging skeletal remodeling with immune regulation. Beyond this canonical role in bone resorption, RANKL-RANK signaling is essential for the development and organization of lymphoid organs [91,143]. Mice lacking either Rank or Rankl exhibit a complete absence of peripheral lymph nodes and display defects in early T and B lymphocyte differentiation, establishing that this axis is indispensable for both osteoclast formation and lymph node organogenesis [91,143]. RANKL signaling initiates the clustering of lymphoid tissue inducer cells, a critical step in lymph node formation, and also controls the differentiation of M cells in mucosal tissues and medullary thymic epithelial cells, which are essential for the elimination of self-reactive T cells and the maintenance of central tolerance [92]. Because RANKL is also expressed by activated immune cells, this pathway should be viewed as a shared osteoimmune signaling module rather than a purely bone-derived endocrine factor.

Interleukin-7 (IL-7), secreted by osteoblasts, plays a critical role in sustaining common lymphoid progenitors (CLPs) within the bone marrow. Osteoblasts support early B lineage differentiation through IL-7 secretion, and inducible deletion of IL-7 specifically in osteoblasts leads to T and B lymphopenia accompanied by reduced CLP numbers without affecting HSCs [144]. The physiological relevance of this osteoblast–immune axis is underscored by the finding that sepsis rapidly ablates osteoblasts, thereby diminishing CLP numbers and contributing to sepsis-associated immunodeficiency; pharmacological activation of osteoblasts ameliorates sepsis-induced lymphopenia, indicating that bone cells represent a viable therapeutic target in life-threatening immune responses [145].

CXCL12, a chemokine abundantly produced by osteoblasts and other stromal cells, is essential for HSC retention and lymphoid progenitor maintenance in the bone marrow. Conditional deletion studies have revealed a striking spatial segregation of hematopoietic niches: deletion of Cxcl12 from perivascular stromal cells depletes HSCs, whereas deletion from osteoblasts specifically depletes certain early lymphoid progenitors without affecting HSCs or myeloerythroid progenitors [101]. These findings establish that different stem and progenitor populations occupy distinct cellular niches within the bone marrow—HSCs predominantly in a perivascular niche and early lymphoid progenitors in an endosteal niche adjacent to osteoblasts. Osteoclasts further modulate this niche through the proteolytic degradation of CXCL12 by MMP9 and cathepsin K, thereby destabilizing HSC anchorage and facilitating their egress into the circulation [86].

Dicer1, an RNase III endonuclease essential for microRNA biogenesis and RNA processing, when deleted specifically in mesenchymal osteoprogenitors, induces markedly disordered hematopoiesis affecting multiple lineages and recapitulating key features of human myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS), including progression to acute myelogenous leukemia. These hematopoietic abnormalities are microenvironment-dependent and are not observed when Dicer1 is deleted from mature osteoblasts, indicating a central role of osteoprogenitor cells in the regulation of hematopoiesis [146]. Dicer1−/− osteoprogenitors express reduced levels of Sbds, the gene mutated in Shwachman-Bodian-Diamond syndrome, a human bone marrow failure and leukemia predisposition disorder, and deletion of Sbds in osteoprogenitors largely phenocopies Dicer1 deletion. These findings demonstrate that primary genetic perturbations confined to the bone marrow microenvironment can initiate secondary neoplastic disease, underscoring the critical importance of skeletal niche integrity for hematopoietic health [146].

DKK1 has also been reported as an immunomodulatory factor with significant implications for tumor-induced immune suppression. In mouse models of extraskeletal tumors, bone-derived DKK1 levels are markedly elevated relative to the tumor site. DKK1 targets β-catenin in myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), suppressing β-catenin target genes and thereby promoting MDSC accumulation and immune suppressive function. Neutralization of DKK1 reduces tumor growth and MDSC numbers by restoring β-catenin in these cells and enhances T cell recruitment to the tumor site, demonstrating that bone-derived DKK1 contributes to systemic immune suppression during cancer progression [147].

Macrophage polarization represents an important interface between biomaterials, bone remodeling and immune regulation. As one of the first immune cell populations responding to implanted materials, macrophages can profoundly influence osteogenesis, osteoclastogenesis and fibrous encapsulation. Pro-inflammatory M1-like macrophages produce cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) that promote inflammatory bone resorption, whereas pro-regenerative M2-like macrophages support angiogenesis, matrix remodeling and osteogenic differentiation. Biomaterials can modulate this balance through surface chemistry, topography, stiffness, ion release and degradation products. For example, titanium nanotopographies as well as calcium-, magnesium- and strontium-releasing materials have been reported to promote M2-like polarization and improve bone regeneration. By regulating macrophage phenotype, biomaterials can indirectly influence RANKL/OPG balance, osteoclast formation and local cytokine secretion, thereby reshaping the osteoimmune microenvironment. This makes osteoimmunology one of the most material-responsive components of BDF-related regulation.

Emerging BDF axes and future validation needs

The bone–brain axis represents a communication channel through which BDFs exert profound influences on brain development, cognitive function, mood regulation, and neuroinflammation. This axis operates through endocrine mechanisms, involving circulating osteokines that traverse the blood–brain barrier (BBB) to engage specific neuronal receptors, as well as through neuronal pathways mediated by the sympathetic and sensory nervous systems. Among the BDFs that participate in this inter-organ crosstalk, OCN, LCN2, OPN, and the RANKL/RANK/OPG system have emerged as key molecular mediators with distinct and sometimes overlapping functions in the central nervous system (CNS) (Fig. 6A). Among reported mediators, OCN provides the clearest example of a circulating osteokine with neuroactive potential. Glu-OCN is released into the circulation, crosses the BBB, and acts on neurons to regulate multiple CNS processes including neurotransmitter synthesis, synaptic plasticity, and neurogenesis. In the brain, OCN influences the synthesis of monoamine neurotransmitters while suppressing γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA)ergic neurotransmission, thereby modulating neural circuits involved in cognition and emotion [148]. Beyond its postnatal roles, OCN acts as a critical maternal factor during fetal brain development. Maternal OCN crosses the placenta to prevent neuronal apoptosis before the embryo begins its own OCN synthesis, thereby contributing to the maternal programming of brain architecture [12]. For biomaterial research, this axis is attractive but still largely prospective. Sustained peripheral BDF delivery, brain-targeted nanoparticles, intranasal nanocarriers, receptor-mediated transcytosis systems, focused ultrasound-assisted BBB delivery and bone–BBB–neuron chips may provide tools for testing bone-to-brain signaling, but direct evidence that biomaterials can reshape CNS function by modulating skeletal BDF secretion remains lacking.

Emerging BDF-mediated bone–organ axes. BDFs participate in emerging communication axes linking bone with the brain, cardiovascular system and skeletal muscle. (A) In the bone–brain axis, OCN, LCN2, OPN and RANKL/OPG may influence BBB crossing, neuronal receptor signaling, neurotransmitter regulation and neuroinflammation. (B) In the bone–cardiovascular axis, FGF23 and PDGF-BB regulate FGFR4-or PDGFR-mediated signaling, contributing to cardiac remodeling, vascular calcification and arterial stiffening. (C) In the bone–muscle axis, OCN–GPRC6A signaling supports nutrient uptake and exercise adaptation, whereas loading-dependent sclerostin regulation contributes to musculoskeletal adaptation and osteosarcopenia.

BDFs may influence vascular integrity, myocardial function, and systemic hemodynamics through the bone–cardiovascular axis. Emerging evidence has established that osteokines, including PDGF-BB, OPG, FGF23, and sclerostin, mediate critical crosstalk between the skeleton and the cardiovascular system, regulating pathological processes such as vascular calcification, arterial stiffening, atherosclerosis, and cardiac remodeling (Fig. 6B). Among the reported mediators, FGF23 provides the strongest disease-linked example. While FGF23 canonically governs phosphate homeostasis via klotho-dependent renal signaling, its pathological elevation in CKD triggers klotho-independent FGFR4 activation in cardiomyocytes, driving left ventricular hypertrophy, myocardial fibrosis, sodium retention and increased cardiovascular mortality [[149], [150], [151]]. PDGF-BB has been identified as a key skeleton-derived mediator of arterial stiffening. Preosteoclasts constitute a major source of circulating PDGF-BB during aging and metabolic stress [28]. In aged mice and those fed a high-fat diet, preosteoclasts secrete excessive amounts of PDGF-BB into the circulation, contributing to elevated serum PDGF-BB levels that correlate with both bone loss and arterial stiffening [152]. Beyond its role in arterial stiffening, PDGF-BB has been implicated in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis by promoting vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) proliferation, migration, and dedifferentiation, as well as in pulmonary arterial hypertension through excessive proliferation of pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells [153,154]. Biomaterials designed to regulate skeletal BDFs should incorporate systemic safety readouts, including serum FGF23 and PDGF-BB levels, phosphate metabolism, vascular calcification, arterial stiffness, cardiac remodeling and blood pressure, before claiming material-mediated regulation of the bone–cardiovascular axis.

Skeletal muscle and bone constitute a functional unit interconnected throughout life by both biomechanical forces and biochemical signaling (Fig. 6C). Among bone-derived mediators, OCN provides the clearest example of a bone-to-muscle signal. During physical activity, Glu-OCN is released from the bone matrix and binds to the G protein-coupled receptor GPRC6A on myofibers, where it promotes nutrient uptake and catabolism during exercise [82]. OCN also regulates the endocrine function of skeletal muscle by enhancing the expression and secretion of IL-6, a prototypical myokine released from contracting muscle fibers [82]. IL-6 acts systemically to drive the generation of bioactive OCN, thereby closing a feed-forward loop that is both necessary and sufficient for optimum exercise capacity [82]. Sclerostin is another bone-related factor relevant to musculoskeletal coupling. Mechanical loading suppresses osteocytic Sost expression, whereas unloading increases sclerostin and inhibits bone formation [155]. Although some studies also suggest that sclerostin may be produced by skeletal muscle under specific pathological conditions, its best-established role in this axis remains as a mechanosensitive osteocyte-derived regulator connecting physical activity, bone formation and musculoskeletal adaptation. Therapeutic sclerostin inhibition has been reported to improve bone mass and, in some models, muscle function, suggesting potential relevance to osteosarcopenia. This loading-sensitive biology provides a natural rationale for mechanoresponsive biomaterial design. By tuning scaffold stiffness, dynamic mechanical responsiveness or rehabilitation-coupled stimulation, biomaterials may influence osteocyte and osteoblast secretory programs, including OCN release and sclerostin suppression. However, whether these local skeletal responses are sufficient to improve muscle metabolism or function remains unresolved. Future studies should distinguish bone-derived endocrine effects from muscle-local myokine or inflammatory responses and should include muscle strength, nutrient utilization, mitochondrial function and exercise performance as functional readouts.

Biomaterials and BDF modulation: current evidence and a conceptual framework for inter-organ intervention

The recognition of BDFs as regulators of systemic physiology raises an important translational question: can biomaterials be used to modulate BDF signaling and thereby influence distant organ function? At present, the evidence supporting this possibility remains emerging rather than definitive. Most biomaterial studies related to BDF biology have focused on local skeletal regeneration, controlled delivery of osteogenic or immunomodulatory factors, regulation of the bone immune microenvironment, or changes in local markers such as OCN, sclerostin, RANKL and OPG. These studies provide important evidence that biomaterials can interface with bone cells and reshape the local skeletal secretome. However, they generally do not yet demonstrate the complete endocrine sequence of material-induced BDF modulation, altered circulating BDF profiles, distant-organ signaling, functional organ responses and BDF-dependent causality.

Therefore, in this section, we do not present biomaterial-mediated BDF regulation as an established therapeutic paradigm for inter-organ diseases. Instead, we organize the available evidence into two levels. First, we summarize current biomaterial studies that regulate BDF-related molecules or BDF-producing cells within local bone environments. Second, we propose a conceptual and translational framework for future biomaterial strategies aimed at BDF-mediated inter-organ intervention.

Current evidence: biomaterial regulation of BDF-related outputs in local bone environments

Biomaterial delivery systems for BDFs and BDF-related molecules

The most direct approach to modulating BDF-mediated signaling involves the exogenous delivery of recombinant factors or their neutralizing antagonists via engineered biomaterial carriers (Fig. 7A). This strategy addresses the fundamental pharmacokinetic limitations of bolus protein administration, including rapid systemic clearance, enzymatic degradation, and supraphysiological concentration spikes that trigger adverse effects. Hydrogels, nanoparticles and composite delivery systems are particularly relevant because their physicochemical properties can be tuned to regulate loading efficiency, release kinetics, degradation rate and spatial retention.

Current evidence for biomaterial regulation of BDF-related outputs in local bone environments. Bioactive materials modulate bone-derived factor (BDF)-mediated signalling through two complementary routes: (A) exogenous delivery of BDFs or their modulators, and (B) endogenous regulation of BDF secretion via physicochemical cues. This figure summarises current evidence for both pathways, linking material design to local bone homeostasis and ultimately to systemic bone–organ crosstalk.

Hydrogels are widely used as local reservoirs for protein and peptide delivery because they form water-rich three-dimensional networks that can protect labile bioactive molecules and provide sustained release. Smart or stimuli-responsive hydrogels can further release their payloads in response to local cues such as pH, enzyme activity, oxidative stress or externally applied stimuli. In principle, this controlled-release logic could be applied to endocrine-acting BDFs, such as OCN, OPG or FGF23 antagonists. However, most current hydrogel studies remain focused on local tissue repair rather than systemic BDF-dependent distant-organ regulation. Therefore, hydrogel-mediated BDF delivery should currently be regarded as a promising but incompletely validated strategy for future endocrine intervention.

Nanoparticle-based delivery systems provide complementary advantages, including prolonged circulation, surface functionalization, tissue targeting and intracellular delivery. These platforms have been extensively explored for delivering osteogenic and angiogenic factors, such as BMP-2, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and TGF-β, in bone repair. Similar design principles may be adapted for BDF-related delivery, including delivery across biological barriers or targeting to bone, kidney, brain or inflamed vascular tissues. For example, receptor-mediated transcytosis, focused ultrasound-assisted delivery and intranasal administration have been investigated as strategies for improving central nervous system access [156]. A recent bone-based delivery strategy using skull bone marrow and skull–meninges channels further suggests that skeletal compartments may provide unconventional routes for central nervous system drug delivery [157]. Nevertheless, whether such systems can deliver BDFs or BDF modulators in a manner that produces BDF-dependent functional changes in distant organs remains to be rigorously tested.

For the bone–kidney and bone–cardiovascular contexts, nanocarriers and hydrogel systems have also been explored for kidney disease therapy, renal targeting and cardiovascular delivery. These studies are relevant to BDF biology because FGF23, Klotho and phosphate-related pathways are central to CKD-MBD and cardiovascular complications. However, most current material studies in these areas do not yet establish that the therapeutic effect is mediated by skeletal BDF production or systemic bone-derived endocrine signaling. Accordingly, these examples should be interpreted as delivery strategies that may inform future BDF-directed interventions rather than direct evidence of biomaterial-regulated bone–organ endocrine communication.

Biomimetic nanoparticles and hybrid systems also provide examples of local modulation of BDF-related inflammatory pathways. Macrophage membrane-liposome hybrid nanovesicles embedded in hydrogels have been shown to scavenge excess RANKL, TNF-α, and IL-6, suppress osteoclastogenesis and preserve osteoblast function, whereas mannose-functionalized nanoparticles can target CD206-positive macrophages and promote pro-regenerative immune response [158]. Antibody-decorated nanoparticles can deliver RANKL antagonists or OPG selectively to activated T lymphocytes, mitigating pathological bone destruction in arthritis and periodontitis [159]. Nanoparticle-based vaccines delivering osteoclast antigens or tolerogenic cytokines are also under exploration for restoring immune tolerance in autoimmune bone diseases [160]. In a rat periodontitis model, nanoscale rice husk liquid smoke elevated OPG and suppressed RANKL expression within seven days [161].

Hierarchical hydrogel–nanoparticle composites combine the spatial retention of hydrogels with the targeting and release properties of nanoparticles. Such platforms may enable staged or stimuli-triggered delivery of BDF-related agents. However, translational challenges remain, including scalable GMP manufacturing, nanomaterial accumulation, PEG immunogenicity, dosing control and long-term safety. Thus, while delivery systems provide important technical tools for manipulating BDF-related signals, their endocrine relevance requires additional validation through serum BDF measurements, distant-organ readouts and mechanistic blocking experiments.

Material cues that regulate endogenous BDF-related secretion

An alternative and conceptually elegant strategy involves engineering biomaterials that modulate the endogenous secretion of BDFs by resident bone cells. This approach leverages the native regulatory machinery of osteoblasts, osteocytes, and osteoclasts, harnessing their capacity to sense and respond to material-derived physical and biochemical cues (Fig. 7B). However, current evidence primarily supports local regulation of gene expression, protein secretion or osteoimmune markers rather than fully validated systemic endocrine effects.

Physical cues are particularly relevant because osteocytes convert mechanical stimulation into biochemical signals that regulate sclerostin, FGF23, and RANKL/OPG. The osteocyte lacunar–canalicular network senses fluid shear stress, matrix deformation and mechanical strain, which can activate Ca2+ signaling, mechanosensitive ion channels and downstream mechanotransduction pathways. From a biomaterial perspective, these processes suggest that scaffold stiffness, viscoelasticity, pore architecture, fluid transport and dynamic mechanical responsiveness may be used to regulate endogenous BDF-related secretion by reshaping the mechanical microenvironment perceived by osteocytes and osteoblast-lineage cells. Several material systems illustrate this possibility. Early work using MLO-Y4 osteocyte-like cells cultured on three-dimensional collagen–glycosaminoglycan scaffolds showed that scaffold stiffness increased Fgf23 expression, and that perfusion-induced fluid flow further enhanced this response [162]. This study provides evidence that material stiffness and dynamic mechanical cues can regulate a key osteocyte-derived endocrine factor at the cellular level. In addition, dynamic scaffolds activated by external physical stimulation, such as ultrasound-responsive 3D-printed scaffolds, have also been shown to enhance OCN release, suggesting that remotely delivered mechanical cues can modulate osteogenic secretory activity [163]. However, current evidence remains largely local or cellular. Future studies should therefore connect material mechanics with a complete validation chain, including local mechanosensing, BDF secretion and packaging, serum BDF dynamics, target-organ signaling and functional outcomes.

Biochemical cues at the material–bone interface also regulate BDF-related outputs. Surface chemistry influences integrin engagement and downstream osteogenic signaling. Microroughened titanium surfaces modulate OCN, OPG and angiogenic factor production through integrin α2β1 signaling [164]. Fluoride-modified titanium surfaces further amplify OCN secretion and osteoblast differentiation [165], while plasma-polymerized allylamine (PPAAm) nanocoating of polymeric scaffolds restores OCN expression and matrix deposition on otherwise adhesion-compromised topographies [166]. Beyond these metallic and polymeric systems, polished silicon nitride ceramics elevate OCN production relative to unpolished counterparts [167], and nacre-mimetic cerium-doped hydroxyapatite/chitosan layered scaffolds actively lower the RANKL/OPG ratio, thereby inhibiting osteoclast activation and promoting osteogenesis [168].

Metal ions released from degrading biomaterials including Zn2+, Mg2+, and Sr2+ serve as potent biochemical cues that modulate BDF secretion through immunoregulation and direct actions on bone cells. Zinc released from sulfonated polyetheretherketone (SPEEK) surfaces drives M2 macrophage polarization, creating a local cytokine milieu that favors osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow stromal cells [169]. Proteomic analyses of Ca-Zn sol-gel coatings confirm that these ions broadly reshape cytokine and matrix protein secretion to promote bone regeneration [170]. Strontium simultaneously stimulates osteoblast activity and suppresses osteoclastogenesis, with strontium ranelate increasing OPG while decreasing RANKL in vitro [171]. Magnesium deficiency elevates circulating FGF23 in vivo [172], while cerium-doped hydroxyapatite/chitosan scaffolds lower the RANKL/OPG ratio and upregulate RUNX2 and OCN [168]. Bone-targeting calcium-magnesium nanoplatforms further contribute by suppressing NF-κB-driven inflammation while promoting osteogenesis [173].

Collectively, current material studies provide convincing evidence that biomaterials can regulate local BDF-related markers, osteocyte mechanotransduction, osteoblast differentiation, osteoclastogenesis and osteoimmune responses. The key limitation is that most studies stop at local molecular or skeletal outcomes. To establish true BDF-mediated inter-organ regulation, future studies must demonstrate a complete causal chain linking material design to BDF secretion, circulating BDF changes, target-organ receptor engagement, distant-organ functional outcomes and loss of effect after BDF or receptor blockade.

Clinical lessons from BDF-targeted therapeutics

Although direct evidence that biomaterials regulate distant organ function through BDF-dependent endocrine mechanisms remains limited, several clinically validated or clinically explored therapies targeting BDF-related pathways provide important lessons for biomaterial design. These examples demonstrate that skeletal secretory pathways are druggable and clinically relevant, but they also reveal the limitations of systemic intervention, including broad tissue exposure, incomplete source specificity, difficulty in reproducing physiological feedback, and potential off-target effects in distant organs (Fig. 8). Therefore, clinical BDF-targeted therapeutics should not be viewed as direct evidence for biomaterial-mediated inter-organ regulation, but rather as translational precedents that define both the opportunities and constraints for future material-based strategies.

Clinical lessons from BDF-targeted therapeutics. Representative therapies targeting sclerostin, FGF23 and RANKL demonstrate that BDF-related pathways are clinically actionable. However, romosozumab, burosumab and denosumab also highlight key limitations of systemic intervention, including cardiovascular concerns, disease-context dependence and non-bone-specific effects. These lessons support future biomaterial strategies that achieve more localized, temporally controlled and safer modulation of BDF-related signaling.

Sclerostin inhibition provides one of the clearest examples of a BDF-related pathway that has been successfully translated into osteoporosis therapy. Sclerostin is an osteocyte-enriched secreted Wnt antagonist that suppresses bone formation by inhibiting LRP5/6-mediated Wnt/β-catenin signaling. Romosozumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting sclerostin, increases bone formation, decreases bone resorption and reduces fracture risk in postmenopausal osteoporosis. This clinical success validates the therapeutic potential of targeting an osteocyte-derived secretory factor. However, sclerostin also has extra-skeletal associations, particularly in cardiovascular biology. Experimental and human genetic studies suggest that sclerostin signaling may influence vascular calcification, atherosclerosis, hypertension and myocardial infarction risk in a context-dependent manner [174]. Consistently, clinical use of romosozumab has raised concerns regarding serious cardiovascular adverse events, including myocardial infarction and stroke [175]. This case illustrates a key challenge for BDF-targeted intervention: systemic inhibition of a bone-enriched factor can improve skeletal outcomes, but may also perturb signaling functions in distant organs. Biomaterials may help address this limitation by enabling localized, low-dose, temporally controlled or mechanically responsive modulation of sclerostin within the skeletal microenvironment, potentially preserving bone anabolic efficacy while reducing systemic exposure.

FGF23-targeted therapy provides a second instructive example, particularly for the bone–kidney axis. FGF23 is secreted primarily by osteocytes and osteoblasts and acts on the kidney through FGFR–αKlotho signaling to regulate phosphate excretion and vitamin D metabolism. Excessive FGF23 activity is central to X-linked hypophosphatemia and tumor-induced osteomalacia, and burosumab, a monoclonal antibody against FGF23, has shown clinical efficacy in improving phosphate homeostasis, rickets severity, growth-related outcomes and physical function in patients with X-linked hypophosphatemia [176]. This example demonstrates that a bone-derived endocrine factor can be therapeutically targeted to correct distant-organ mineral-handling defects. However, the FGF23 pathway also highlights the need for disease-specific caution. In chronic kidney disease, FGF23 elevation may be compensatory for phosphate retention, and neutralization of FGF23 improved hyperparathyroidism but increased mortality in a CKD model [177]. Moreover, FGF23 can contribute to cardiovascular remodeling through Klotho-independent FGFR4 signaling in cardiomyocytes [178]. Thus, material-based FGF23 modulation would require precise control over dose, timing and disease context. Future biomaterial systems aimed at this axis should monitor not only local bone responses, but also serum phosphate, active vitamin D, renal signaling, parathyroid hormone, cardiovascular outcomes and survival-related safety endpoints.

The RANKL/RANK/OPG pathway illustrates how a bone-associated but non-bone-specific signaling system can be clinically exploited while also requiring careful interpretation. Denosumab, a fully human monoclonal antibody against RANKL, inhibits osteoclast formation, function and survival, thereby reducing bone resorption and fracture risk in osteoporosis and other osteolytic conditions. Beyond bone, RANKL/RANK/OPG signaling participates in immune regulation, lymphoid organogenesis, muscle function and neuroinflammatory responses. These broad biological roles underscore both the power and the risk of systemic pathway blockade. For biomaterial design, this pathway provides a rationale for local or bone-targeted regulation of RANKL/OPG balance, particularly in inflammatory bone loss, periodontitis, implant osseointegration and osteoimmune disorders.

Taken together, these clinical examples provide three major lessons for biomaterial-mediated BDF intervention. First, BDF-related pathways are clinically actionable, as demonstrated by therapies targeting sclerostin, FGF23, and RANKL. Second, systemic intervention can produce unintended effects because many BDFs or BDF-related pathways also act in non-skeletal tissues or participate in multi-organ feedback loops. Third, future biomaterial strategies should aim to improve spatial specificity, temporal precision, dose control, reversibility and source selectivity. Therefore, the major translational opportunity for biomaterials is not simply to replicate systemic antibody or hormone therapy, but to create local or bone-targeted platforms that can regulate BDF production, presentation or release while minimizing off-target endocrine disruption. This clinical perspective provides the rationale for the conceptual framework proposed below.

Conceptual framework: from local BDF modulation to inter-organ intervention

Because direct evidence remains limited, the next stage of this field should move from descriptive material effects toward a defined validation framework. We propose that biomaterial-mediated BDF inter-organ intervention should be evaluated across five evidence levels: (i) material-induced changes in local BDF-related gene or protein expression; (ii) altered secretion of BDFs from defined bone-resident cells or bone tissue; (iii) measurable changes in circulating BDF levels in vivo; (iv) activation or inhibition of signaling pathways and functional responses in distant target organs; and (v) BDF-dependent causality demonstrated by genetic deletion, receptor blockade, neutralizing antibodies or rescue experiments. Only studies reaching the latter levels should be considered direct evidence of biomaterial-mediated regulation of BDF-dependent inter-organ communication.

Delivery-based BDF modulation

The first future direction is to transform BDF delivery systems from local bone-repair depots into quantitatively controlled endocrine exposure platforms (Fig. 9A). Hydrogels, nanoparticles and hierarchical composites may be designed to release OCN, OPG, FGF23 antagonists, Klotho-related agents or other BDF modulators with defined pharmacokinetic profiles. To support an endocrine claim, these systems should not only demonstrate local release but also quantify serum BDF levels, duration of exposure, target-organ biodistribution and downstream receptor activation. For example, a hydrogel designed to release Glu-OCN for metabolic intervention would need to show sustained circulating Glu-OCN, activation of relevant receptors in pancreas, adipose tissue, muscle or brain, and functional improvement that is abolished by receptor blockade. Similarly, an FGF23-modulating system for CKD-MBD would require careful assessment of phosphate metabolism, vitamin D signaling, renal responses and cardiovascular safety.

Strategy modules for future BDF-oriented biomaterial intervention. Future BDF-oriented biomaterials may advance through four complementary strategy modules: (A) delivery-based BDF modulation, (B) endogenous skeletal secretome reprogramming, (C) biofabricated models for validating BDF-mediated axes and (D) engineered EVs as biomimetic BDF carriers. These modules aim to move the field from local bone-repair depots and local osteogenic readouts toward controlled endocrine exposure, skeletal secretome profiling, human-relevant bone–organ modeling and organ-targeted BDF delivery.

Endogenous skeletal secretome reprogramming

The second future direction is to engineer materials that reprogram endogenous BDF secretion by osteocytes, osteoblasts, osteoclasts or bone marrow immune cells (Fig. 9B). Mechanoresponsive scaffolds, topographical surfaces, ion-releasing biomaterials and immunomodulatory matrices could be used to tune sclerostin, FGF23, RANKL/OPG, OCN or EV-associated cargos. However, this strategy requires a shift from measuring local osteogenic markers to profiling the skeletal secretome. Multi-omics secretome analysis, isotope or lineage tracing, cell-specific deletion models and longitudinal serum measurements will be needed to determine whether material-induced local changes are transmitted into the circulation and whether they affect distant organs. Such studies should also consider dose-response relationships, disease stage, age, sex, mechanical loading status and comorbidities, all of which may alter BDF secretion and systemic responsiveness.

Biofabricated models for validating BDF-mediated axes

Biofabricated models can provide controlled platforms for testing whether material-induced BDF changes are capable of influencing distant tissues (Fig. 9C). Organ-on-a-chip technology integrates microfluidic systems with three-dimensional cell culture to recapitulate tissue-level structure and function in a miniaturized, perfusable format. Bone/cartilage organoid on-chip (BCoC) platforms, which faithfully emulate the essential elements, biologic functions, and pathophysiological responses of skeletal tissues under physiologically relevant conditions. The BCoC system integrates multiple tissue modules on a single microfluidic device, enabling the study of inter-tissue communication including BDF-mediated crosstalk in a controlled and manipulable environment [179]. This platform holds particular promise for high-throughput screening of biomaterial formulations that modulate BDF secretion and for elucidating the mechanisms by which material-derived cues influence bone–organ axis communication. Primary human 3D organotypic bone cultures have also been shown to generate mature osteocytes that secrete FGF23 and sclerostin in hormonally responsive ways and remain viable for long-term culture [180]. These systems could be connected with kidney, vascular, muscle or neural modules to test whether material-conditioned bone secretomes alter target-organ signaling in a human-relevant setting.

Bone organoids provide complementary advantages by recapitulating multicellular architecture, extracellular matrix deposition and mineralization. Recent advances in bone/cartilage organoid construction, hydroxyapatite hybrid bioinks, large-scale mineralized bone organoids and standardized bone organoid protocols provide a foundation for modeling BDF secretion under defined matrix and mechanical conditions [181,182]. When combined with microfluidics, biosensors and target-organ modules, these platforms could help bridge the gap between local material-induced skeletal responses and systemic bone–organ communication.

Engineered EVs as biomimetic BDF carriers

Engineered EVs represent another potential component of the future BDF-intervention framework (Fig. 9D). EVs are natural carriers of proteins, RNAs and lipids, and bone-derived EVs can transmit osteoclast-, osteoblast- or osteocyte-related signals. Current EV engineering strategies include endogenous cargo loading through donor-cell engineering, exogenous loading by electroporation, sonication, freeze–thaw cycling or membrane permeabilization, and surface modification using LAMP2B, CD63, CD9, click chemistry, lipid insertion, PEGylation or targeting peptides [[183], [184], [185], [186]]. These approaches may enable EVs to deliver BDFs or BDF-regulatory RNAs to specific organs.

When considering the specific application of these engineering platforms to BDFs, the therapeutic rationale is conceptually compelling yet experimentally nascent. The direct engineering of EVs to carry BDFs as therapeutic cargo has been demonstrated in proof-of-concept settings. Endothelial progenitor cells genetically modified to overexpress OCN were shown to secrete OCN-loaded exosomes which transferred OCN protein to recipient endothelial cells and enhanced angiogenesis through OCN–GPRC6A signaling, establishing the feasibility of using engineered EVs to deliver a functional BDF to target cells [187]. The cargo versatility of EVs, which can simultaneously deliver proteins, RNAs, and small molecules, opens the possibility of engineering single vesicle populations that carry multiple BDFs with complementary functions, for example, OCN combined with OPG for simultaneous metabolic regulation and bone protection, or FGF23 antagonists co-delivered with Klotho mimetics for CKD-MBD therapy. Beyond simply encapsulating existing BDFs, future EV therapeutics could be endowed with stimulus-responsive release capabilities. The same metabolic glycan labeling and click chemistry approaches already established for EV surface engineering could be adapted to engineer BDF-loaded EVs with glucose-responsive or phosphate-responsive release, enabling physiological feedback-regulated delivery that mimics the body's own endocrine control mechanisms. The convergence of EV engineering methodologies with the expanding molecular understanding of bone–organ axes positions engineered EVs as a uniquely promising platform for translating the skeleton's endocrine functions into systemic therapeutics. Further elucidation of EV cargo specificity, organ-level biodistribution, and functional outcomes in preclinical bone–organ axis models will be essential for fully realizing this therapeutic potential.

Future perspectives

The preceding chapters have established a comprehensive framework for understanding BDF-mediated cross-organ communication and the biomaterial strategies that can therapeutically intervene in these complex signaling networks. However, translating this integrated knowledge into clinically actionable therapies is fundamentally challenged by the sheer complexity of the BDF regulatory landscape. The skeleton secretes over 375 potential osteokines whose production is dynamically modulated by mechanical loading, aging, metabolic status, and the reciprocal input from multiple distant organs. Simultaneously, the multidimensional parameter space that defines biomaterial design is far too extensive to be navigated through exhaustive experimentation alone. Artificial intelligence (AI) is uniquely positioned to address these challenges, offering computational tools capable of extracting meaningful patterns from complex multidimensional data, predicting structure-function relationships, and guiding rational therapeutic design (Fig. 10).

Roadmap for future BDF-oriented biomaterial intervention. A stepwise roadmap is proposed to advance BDF-oriented biomaterials from discovery to personalized intervention. AI-assisted multi-omics can identify candidate BDFs, machine learning can optimize biomaterial design, and standardized validation is required to link local BDF changes with circulating dynamics, distant-organ responses and causal mechanisms. Human-relevant models and closed-loop systems may further support predictive translation and personalized regulation of BDF-mediated bone–organ communication.

AI in deciphering BDF networks and identifying therapeutic targets

Advances in high-throughput sequencing technologies have generated unprecedented volumes of bone-related multi-omics data including genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, yet extracting biologically meaningful insights from these datasets remains a formidable challenge. AI and machine learning (ML) approaches are uniquely suited to integrate such heterogeneous data, revealing hidden patterns and functional relationships that escape conventional analytical methods. A landmark study by Liang et al. integrated RNA-seq, single-cell RNA-seq, and proteomics analyses across multiple biological models including normal adult mice, aged mice, mechanically stimulated mice, and cultured osteoblasts to systematically identify osteokines involved in global homeostasis, yielding 375 potentially functional bone-derived factors and precisely mapping their cellular origins [56]. This work exemplifies how computational integration of multi-omics data can substantially expand the catalog of known BDFs and provide a foundation for understanding their coordinated actions. Importantly, this type of study should be interpreted as AI- or computation-assisted BDF discovery rather than direct therapeutic validation. The identification of candidate osteokines represents an early discovery step, and additional experiments are still required to confirm skeletal source specificity, circulating release, target-organ activity and functional causality.

Beyond cataloging, AI-driven multi-omics integration enables the identification of functional modules and regulatory networks that govern BDF secretion. Machine learning algorithms can compress high-dimensional gene expression data into interpretable latent variables that represent functional modules, revealing both “core genes” that directly drive phenotypes and “peripheral genes” that influence outcomes through regulatory networks. Such approaches have proven particularly valuable in bone genomics, where genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified hundreds of loci associated with bone mineral density and fracture risk, yet only a fraction of the genetic heritability has been explained. Emerging AI methods that incorporate gene-environment interactions and rare variant contributions promise to bridge this gap, potentially revealing novel regulators of BDF secretion that could serve as therapeutic targets. Furthermore, when single-cell sequencing data are limited for certain bone cell types, generative AI models can infer cell-type-specific expression patterns from bulk or progenitor cell data.

The translation of BDF biology into therapeutic interventions requires not only identifying osteokines but also prioritizing which factors or which nodes in the regulatory network represent the most promising targets for a given clinical indication. AI-powered knowledge graphs, which integrate diverse biomedical data types, offer a powerful framework for systematic target prioritization. Such approaches evaluate the statistical significance of connections between biological entities while accounting for network topology biases, thereby identifying targets that are both biologically plausible and therapeutically tractable. The application of knowledge graph approaches to BDF biology holds particular promise for conditions where multiple bone–organ axes are simultaneously dysregulated, such as in CKD-MBD, where elevated FGF23 contributes to both disordered mineral metabolism and cardiovascular pathology. AI-driven target prioritization could identify interventions that simultaneously normalize FGF23 signaling while preserving protective aspects of the bone-kidney and bone–cardiovascular axes. Moreover, knowledge graphs that incorporate data from diverse human populations will be essential for developing BDF-targeted therapies that are effective across the full spectrum of human genetic diversity. The systematic integration of multi-omics data with AI-driven network analysis is expected to uncover previously unrecognized osteokines and regulatory relationships, providing a roadmap for next-generation therapeutic development.

Machine learning for biomaterial design and high-throughput screening

While AI accelerates BDF discovery, its important application also lies in the rational design of biomaterials that modulate BDF signaling. As detailed in previous sections, these materials span controlled-release depots for exogenous BDFs, scaffolds that tune endogenous BDF secretion through biophysical and biochemical cues, and biofabricated microenvironments including organoids, organ-on-chip platforms, and engineered EVs. The design space for each application is vast and combinatorial. Hydrogel crosslinking density and degradation kinetics govern BDF release profiles, surface chemistry and stiffness dictate mechanotransduction-dependent osteokine secretion, and the interplay of cell sourcing, matrix composition, and dynamic culture conditions determines organoid fidelity and EV cargo composition. Machine learning offers a systematic framework for navigating these high-dimensional spaces, replacing labor-intensive trial-and-error experimentation with data-driven prediction and optimization.

Recent work has demonstrated the potential of ML-guided biomaterial design across several domains that are relevant to BDF regulation [178]. An automated high-throughput platform coupled with Bayesian optimization efficiently identified alginate hydrogel formulations that achieve near-zero-order protein release over two weeks [188]. This study provides an important methodological precedent for optimizing release kinetics of specific BDFs such as OCN, OPG, FGF23 modulators, or sclerostin-related agents. The key relevance of this example is that it demonstrates how closed-loop experimental optimization can rapidly search formulation space and identify release behaviors that would be difficult to obtain through conventional trial-and-error screening. Similarly, ML-based prediction of protein corona composition on biomaterial surfaces may help understand how implanted materials acquire biological identities and influence downstream osteoinductive or immunomodulatory responses [182]. Recent protein-corona databases and supervised learning models have shown that nanoparticle properties can be linked to quantitative protein adsorption profiles or predicted protein–nanoparticle interactions. However, whether such approaches can be used to rationally design surfaces that specifically regulate BDF-related outputs remains to be experimentally demonstrated.

In the biofabrication area, AI-driven optimization is accelerating bone organoid development by predicting the combinatorial effects of matrix cues, growth factor cocktails, and mechanical stimulation on tissue self-organization [189]. A landmark study integrating 3D-printed scaffolds with 24 distinct architectures, in vivo evaluation, and machine learning identified specific surface area and permeability as the dominant drivers of osteoinductivity, establishing a data-driven methodology applicable to diverse material platforms [190]. In addition, mechanobiology-guided machine learning models trained on simulated bone-healing trajectories have been reported to accelerate prediction of healing outcomes without repeated finite element analyses, suggesting that ML may help connect implant design parameters with tissue-level repair dynamics [191]. For EV engineering, AI is being harnessed to guide cargo selection, predict targeting ligand efficiency, and optimize culture conditions for producer cells, with computational frameworks such as the “artificial intelligence virtual extracellular vesicle” (AIVEV) proposed to simulate EV biogenesis and rationally design their therapeutic payloads in silico [[192], [193], [194]]. The convergence of high-throughput experimentation, active learning, and deep learning is creating a computational engine for biomaterials discovery uniquely positioned to accelerate the development of materials that modulate BDF secretion, delivery, and systemic function across the bone–organ axis network.

Digital twins and predictive modeling of the bone–organ axes

The concept of a “digital twin”, a virtual representation of a physical object or system across its lifecycle, informed by patient-specific data and used for clinical decision support or therapeutic personalization, is rapidly transitioning from engineering curiosity to a cornerstone of personalized medicine. In the context of BDF biology and biomaterial intervention, digital twins offer the potential to simulate the complex, multiscale dynamics of bone–organ axis communication, predict patient-specific responses to therapeutic interventions, and guide the design of personalized biomaterial-based treatments. The European Commission's Virtual Human Twin initiative represents an ambitious effort to construct a federated public infrastructure that will facilitate the creation and credibility assessment of digital twins across the human body. This platform will connect multiscale models, data, and standards across Europe, providing an unprecedented resource for simulating complex physiological interactions including bone–organ axis crosstalk and evaluating therapeutic interventions in silico before committing to costly and time-consuming clinical trials. At present, however, digital twins for BDF-mediated bone–organ communication remain largely conceptual. Their development will require standardized multimodal datasets that link material properties, local skeletal cell responses, BDF secretion profiles, circulating BDF dynamics, target-organ signaling, organ-level functional outcomes and patient-specific clinical metadata. Without such datasets, digital-twin models cannot reliably predict how a biomaterial intervention in bone will influence systemic BDF signaling or distant-organ physiology.

The ultimate vision for AI-guided BDF therapeutics is the development of closed-loop systems wherein implantable or wearable biosensors continuously monitor relevant BDF concentrations, transmit these data to AI-driven predictive models, and trigger adaptive responses from BDF-responsive smart biomaterials. Such systems would represent a fundamental advance beyond current “open-loop” therapeutic paradigms, enabling dynamic, personalized modulation of BDF-mediated signaling that maintains physiological homeostasis rather than merely correcting pathological deviations. Extending this concept to the bone–kidney axis, a digital twin model of an individual CKD patient could integrate real-time measurements of serum phosphate and FGF23 with computational simulations of mineral metabolism, predicting the optimal timing and dosing of therapeutic interventions from FGF23-responsive smart materials. The AI model would continuously refine its predictions based on observed patient responses, enabling truly personalized, adaptive therapy that accounts for individual variations in disease progression, drug metabolism, and comorbidity profiles. Network-driven digital twins that link multi-layer data to drug and biomarker discovery represent a directly transferable approach to complex diseases, with established utility in predicting, preventing, and personalizing treatment across diverse clinical contexts. The integration of closed-loop BDF-responsive materials with digital twin models of individual patients points toward a future in which therapeutic biomaterials are not passive implants but rather active, intelligent components of a distributed therapeutic network, continuously sensing, learning, and adapting to maintain bone–organ axis homeostasis.

Standardized in vivo models for validating BDF-mediated inter-organ effects

A major obstacle in translating BDF biology and biomaterial-mediated BDF modulation is the lack of standardized in vivo models capable of distinguishing local skeletal effects from genuine systemic endocrine communication. Many current studies evaluate local bone regeneration, osteogenesis, osteoclastogenesis, immune-cell infiltration, or local expression of BDF-related markers. However, these endpoints alone are insufficient to demonstrate that a biomaterial alters distant organ function through a BDF-dependent mechanism. To establish such a conclusion, in vivo models must be designed to evaluate the full sequence from material–bone interaction to systemic endocrine output and distant-organ response.

First, animal models should identify the skeletal source of the candidate BDF using approaches such as cell-specific reporters, lineage tracing, or conditional gene deletion. This is particularly important for broadly expressed mediators which may originate from multiple tissues. Without source validation, systemic changes cannot be confidently attributed to bone-derived endocrine signaling. Second, systemic BDF dynamics should be quantitatively monitored by assessing local expression, circulating levels, bioactive forms, and temporal changes following biomaterial intervention. For example, OCN carboxylation status, intact versus cleaved FGF23, and EV cargo and biodistribution should be considered. These analyses should be integrated with material pharmacokinetics and biodistribution studies. Third, distant target organs should be evaluated using both molecular and functional endpoints relevant to the specific bone–organ axis. Changes in circulating BDF levels alone are insufficient; receptor activation, downstream signaling, and organ-level outcomes should also be demonstrated. Fourth, causality should be verified through loss-of-function or rescue strategies, including neutralizing antibodies, receptor blockade, conditional gene deletion, or supplementation with recombinant factors or engineered EVs. Such approaches are essential to distinguish BDF-mediated effects from indirect influences such as inflammation or material degradation. Finally, standardized experimental designs are needed. Key variables including species, age, sex, disease background, material dose, implantation route, and sampling schedule should be reported consistently. Longitudinal studies are particularly valuable given the dynamic nature of BDF secretion during bone repair, remodeling, aging, and disease progression. Establishing standardized validation criteria will improve the reproducibility and translational relevance of biomaterial-regulated BDF research.

Challenges

Context-dependent and disease-specific effects of BDF-related mediators

A major challenge in translating BDF biology into biomaterial-based therapies is the context-dependent activity of many BDF-related mediators. Several factors discussed in this Review do not exert uniformly beneficial or detrimental effects, but instead display disease-stage-, tissue- and microenvironment-dependent functions. Sclerostin provides a clear example. Although sclerostin inhibition increases bone formation and has become a clinically effective strategy for osteoporosis, sclerostin may also exert protective effects in certain vascular contexts, and systemic anti-sclerostin therapy has raised cardiovascular safety concerns. Therefore, material strategies designed to suppress sclerostin should consider spatial restriction, dosing kinetics and cardiovascular monitoring rather than assuming that global inhibition is uniformly beneficial.

Similar caution is required for OPN and LCN2. OPN can support tissue repair in some acute injury settings, but may promote chronic inflammation, fibrosis, tumor immune suppression or neurodegenerative pathology depending on the cellular source and disease context. LCN2 may participate in appetite regulation and glucose homeostasis when produced by osteoblasts, yet in neuroinflammatory or ischemic conditions it can also be induced locally by astrocytes, microglia or injured tissue and contribute to oxidative stress, blood–brain barrier disruption and inflammation. These examples highlight a central principle: BDF-related molecules should not be treated as fixed therapeutic targets independent of cellular source, pathological stage, dose, bioactive form or target-organ context. For broadly expressed mediators, source tracing and cell-type-specific analysis are essential before attributing beneficial or adverse effects to bone-derived signals.

Translational risks and safety considerations for biomaterial-based BDF modulation

Biomaterial-based BDF modulation also raises important translational safety issues. Materials that regulate local bone repair may alter osteoblast, osteocyte, osteoclast and immune-cell secretory programs, but the resulting systemic consequences may be difficult to predict and control. Biosafety remains a primary concern, particularly for advanced biomaterials incorporating bioactive molecules, extracellular vesicles, nanoparticles, gene-delivery systems or engineered cells. Potential risks include excessive or prolonged BDF release, ectopic activation of target-organ pathways, immune responses to material components or engineered vesicles, inflammatory reactions to degradation products, disturbance of mineral metabolism, and off-target effects in organs that share the same receptors or signaling pathways. In addition, biomaterial-induced changes in the local immune microenvironment may unintentionally amplify inflammatory signaling, alter macrophage polarization, affect adaptive immune responses, or trigger foreign-body reactions that ultimately influence both bone regeneration and systemic endocrine communication. The immunogenicity of exogenous vesicles, biologically derived scaffolds and functionalized materials therefore requires careful evaluation, particularly when repeated administration or long-term implantation is anticipated.

Feasibility also represents an important translational challenge. Many studies demonstrate local regulation of BDF expression in experimental models, yet it remains unclear whether these changes are sufficiently robust, durable and reproducible to generate clinically meaningful systemic effects in humans. The magnitude of BDF modulation required to influence distant organs, the duration of treatment needed to maintain therapeutic benefit, and the variability introduced by age, sex, comorbidities and disease stage remain largely unknown. Furthermore, scaling biomaterial-based strategies from small-animal models to human patients may be complicated by differences in skeletal physiology, immune responses, material degradation behavior and endocrine regulation.

These concerns are particularly relevant for pathways such as FGF23–Klotho, sclerostin–Wnt, RANKL–RANK–OPG and PDGF-BB signaling, which have important functions beyond local bone regeneration. For example, unintended modulation of FGF23 signaling may affect phosphate homeostasis and cardiovascular function, while systemic alteration of Wnt signaling could influence vascular biology, tissue repair or tumor-related processes. Likewise, excessive activation or inhibition of RANKL-related pathways may disrupt immune regulation and bone remodeling balance. Therefore, future biomaterial studies should evaluate not only local bone formation, osseointegration or osteoimmune modulation, but also serum BDF dynamics, biodistribution, pharmacokinetics, immunogenicity, degradation kinetics, cardiovascular and renal safety, metabolic outcomes, reproductive and neurological effects where relevant, and long-term tissue responses. Comprehensive preclinical assessment integrating efficacy, biosafety, immune compatibility, feasibility and potential off-target effects will be essential before biomaterial-mediated BDF modulation can be translated into clinical practice.

Challenges and ethical considerations in AI-guided BDF and biomaterial research

Despite the transformative potential of AI in BDF biology and biomaterial engineering, several significant challenges must be addressed before these technologies can be responsibly translated into clinical practice. Data quality and standardization represent perhaps the most fundamental obstacles. AI models are only as reliable as the data on which they are trained, and the current landscape of bone biology and biomaterials research is characterized by substantial heterogeneity in experimental protocols, measurement techniques, and reporting standards. The establishment of community-wide data repositories with standardized metadata and quality control metrics will be essential for training robust, generalizable AI models. Model interpretability constitutes another critical challenge. Many of the most powerful AI approaches, particularly deep neural networks, function as “black boxes” that provide predictions without transparent explanations of the underlying reasoning. In biomedical applications, where patient safety depends on understanding why a particular therapeutic recommendation was made, such opacity is unacceptable. The development of explainable AI (XAI) methods that provide interpretable rationales for model predictions is therefore an urgent priority. Regulatory frameworks that establish standards for AI model validation and certification in medical applications are actively being developed but remain in their early stages.

Ethical considerations also demand careful attention. AI models trained predominantly on data from specific demographic groups may exhibit biased performance when applied to underrepresented populations, potentially exacerbating existing health disparities. The use of patient-specific data for training digital twin models raises significant privacy concerns, necessitating robust data governance frameworks and privacy-preserving machine learning techniques. The integration of AI and biomaterials also raises important questions regarding bioethics and roboethics, as the convergence of intelligent materials with autonomous decision-making capabilities creates novel moral and legal challenges at the intersection of science and society.

Finally, the computational and environmental costs of training large-scale AI models must be acknowledged. The energy consumption associated with training state-of-the-art deep learning models can be substantial, raising sustainability concerns that warrant consideration in research planning. Cross-disciplinary collaboration involving bone biologists, materials scientists, computer scientists, clinicians, and ethicists will be essential for navigating these challenges and ensuring that AI technologies are developed and deployed in a manner that is scientifically rigorous, clinically effective, and ethically responsible.

Concluding remarks

This Review has examined the emerging interface between BDFs, biomaterials and inter-organ communication. The skeleton is increasingly recognized as a dynamic secretory organ that contributes to systemic regulation across the kidney, brain, metabolic organs, cardiovascular system, immune/hematopoietic network and skeletal muscle. Bioactive materials offer promising tools for interfacing with skeletal secretory programs. Current studies demonstrate that controlled-release systems, material-derived physical and biochemical cues, immunomodulatory matrices, engineered extracellular vesicles and biofabricated platforms can regulate local BDF-related outputs, including OCN expression, FGF23 regulation, sclerostin signaling and RANKL/OPG balance. Nevertheless, complete in vivo evidence showing that biomaterials regulate distant organ function through clearly defined BDF-dependent endocrine mechanisms remains limited. Thus, biomaterial-mediated BDF modulation should currently be viewed as an emerging framework built on local skeletal evidence rather than an established systemic therapeutic paradigm. Clinical experience with sclerostin inhibition, FGF23-targeted therapy and RANKL blockade demonstrates that BDF-related pathways are clinically actionable, but also reveals the risks of systemic intervention, including context-dependent effects, off-target responses and safety concerns in distant organs. These lessons suggest that future biomaterials should aim to improve spatial specificity, temporal precision, dose control, reversibility and source selectivity rather than simply replicate systemic antibody or hormone therapy.

Moving forward, the field must progress from local marker changes toward causal validation of material-induced BDF secretion, circulating BDF dynamics, target-organ signaling, functional outcomes and BDF-dependent mechanisms. Standardized in vivo models, organ-on-chip systems, bone organoids, engineered extracellular vesicles and AI-assisted biomaterial design may help bridge this gap. With rigorous validation, safety assessment and cross-disciplinary integration, BDF-oriented biomaterials may evolve from local bone-regenerative tools into precise platforms for investigating and eventually modulating bone–organ communication.
