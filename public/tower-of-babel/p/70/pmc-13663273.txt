Relationship between cephalic index, facial index and dental arch widths among young Nepalese adults
Authors: Avinash Chaudhary, Samikshya Sangroula, Prabhat Ranjan Pokhrel, Rajesh Gyawali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFor a face to be aesthetically pleasant, it has to be well proportioned in all the three spatial planes. In addition to sagittal and vertical dimensions, transverse width of the face too needs to be addressed to ensure proper esthetics. Malocclusion in transverse dimension may not be limited to the dental arches but, it can also be associated with underlying skeletal abnormalities. So, this study aims to investigate the relation between dental arch widths, cephalic and facial indices.MethodsThe present study was a cross-sectional, descriptive study carried out in one hundred and thirty-two students meeting the inclusion and exclusion criteria in the Department of Orthodontics, BPKIHS, Nepal. Cranial and facial measurements were performed under natural light. Dental arch widths were measured on study models obtained from the students. The data were entered and analyzed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) 16. Mean and standard deviation was calculated for each variable. Correlation between the variables were determined with Pearson’s correlation test with Statistical significance set at P < 0.05.ResultsMean age of the students was 22.14 ± 3.81 years, mean cephalic index (CI) and facial index (FI) were 82.93 ± 7.72 and 90.89 ± 7.75 respectively. Brachycephalic head form (65.2%) and leptoprosopic facial form (49.2%) were more prevalent. A significant, weak negative correlation was found between CI and FI. No significant correlation was seen between CI and dental arch widths, but significant and weak negative correlation was found between FI and upper intercanine and upper intermolar width.ConclusionsThe facial index significantly correlates with the upper intercanine and intermolar widths. No correlation was seen between the facial index and lower dental arch width, between cephalic index and dental arch widths.

Introduction

Effective orthodontic diagnosis and treatment planning necessitate a thorough understanding of transverse craniofacial skeletal development in relation to concomitant dentoalveolar growth as already emphasized several decades ago by Meredith [1]. The final morphological outcome of an individual is the result of interactions between growth processes and muscular forces. These interactions give rise to a wide range of facial patterns, with specific head and facial types being associated with particular skeletal configurations and dental occlusions [2]. A particularly critical dimension of craniofacial morphogenesis concerns with the influence of neurocranial configuration upon facial architecture. It has been hypothesized that developmentally brain expansion is accompanied by differential enlargement of the frontal and parietal lobes [3].

The neurocranium, basicranium, and facial skeleton form semi-autonomous yet structurally interdependent units. The cranial base is widely regarded as a relatively stable foundation from which the facial complex grows in a downward and forward direction.[4] It has been shown to affect the growth of transverse facial width which in turn grows according to the size of the brain [5, 6].

Potential causal relationship between the cephalic index and both dental malocclusion and associated facial and skeletal morphologies has been postulated by Enlow [7]. Graber et al. have stated that leptoprosopic (dolichocephalic) individuals typically exhibit a narrow dental arch, whereas euryprosopic (brachycephalic) individuals tend to have a wide, rounded dental arch. Mesoprosopic (mesocephalic) individuals generally display a dental arch form that is intermediate between these two facial (head) types [8]. Several authors have documented relationships between distinct facial types and underlying jaw morphology through cephalometric evaluation and cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT). However, these methods expose patients to harmful radiation [9, 10].

Research on cephalic and facial indices has demonstrated population and gender specific norms [7, 11, 12]. Studies have emphasized the importance of individualized assessment of craniofacial morphology as this can serve as useful indicatorfor forensic facial reconstruction and human identification [13, 14]. Existing studies differ considerably with respect to study design, anthropometric methods, age distribution, ethnic background, and measurement techniques, making direct comparison difficult between craniofacial indices and dental arch widths. Although studies have examined correlations between craniofacial indices and dental arch dimensions, most of them are limited by ethnicity-specific samples and focus on isolated measurements [5, 12, 14–16]. Therefore, the present study aims to correlate cephalic index and facial index with dental arch widths using clinically accessible anthropometric parameters.

Materials and methods

This is a hospital-based cross-sectional descriptive study carried out in the Department of Orthodontics, College of Dental Surgery, B.P. Koirala Institute of Health Sciences (BPKIHS) from February 2023 to November 2025 after obtaining the ethical clearance from Institutional Review Committee (IRC), BPKIHS [IRC/2395/022]. Undergraduate and post graduate students between 18 years and 30 years were screened. Informed and written consent was obtained from 132 students meeting the inclusion criteria of having full complement of permanent teeth excluding third molars, well aligned arches with crowding or spacing not exceeding 4 mm and overjet and overbite in the range of 2–4 mm. Students who had previous orthodontic treatment, orthognathic surgeries, facial injuries, edentulous spaces, significant cuspal wear, extensive restoration or prosthetics and anterior or posterior crossbite were excluded from the study. The data were entered in Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) 16. Mean and standard deviation were calculated for each variable. Reliability for dental arch width measurement was done by remeasuring 28 dental study models two weeks apart and applying intraclass correlation coefficient. Correlation between the variables were determined with Pearson’s correlation test with Statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

Discussion

Various diagnostic aids are routinely employed for the assessment of dental and skeletal malocclusion, including clinical, radiographic, and photographic methods, each possessing distinct advantages and limitations. The cephalometric technique and CBCT have been widely used for evaluating and differentiating skeletal and dental malocclusions [19, 20]. Numerous studies have demonstrated an association between craniofacial morphology and malocclusion through the use of radiographic methods. Several investigators have correlated various facial types with jaw morphology [1, 2, 9]. However, the existing body of literature predominantly relies on radiographic techniques to establish these correlations. Therefore, this study was conducted to evaluate the correlation between cephalic index, facial index and dental arch widths using simple, non-radiographic, clinically measurable parameters.

Among one hundred and thirty-two students included in this study, sixty-two (47%) were male and seventy (53%) were female with mean age 22.14 ± 3.81 years. Mean cephalic index was 82.93 ± 7.72. Males and females had almost identical mean cephalic indices (82.89 and 82.96). Students with brachycephalic head form were more prevalent, followed by mesocephalic and dolicocephalic head form. Chaudhary et al. and Lobo et al. too found brachycephalic head as prevalent head form with mean CI of 82.48 ± 4.44 and 83.7 ± 5.7 [21, 22]. Whereas, Khatun et al. [23] and Mansur et al. [24] reported the dolichocephalic head form as the most prevalent, followed by the mesocephalic and brachycephalic head forms, with mean cephalic indices of 75.99 ± 4.97 and 72.52 ± 3.9, respectively, in the Nepali population. Conversely, Akinbami et al. identified the mesocephalic head form as the predominant head form in the Nigerian population [25].

Mean FI in the present study was 90.89 ± 7.75. Males had a slightly higher facial index than females, indicating somewhat narrower faces in males. Leptoprosopic face was more common followed by mesoprosopic and euryprosopic facial form. This is in close agreement with the findings of Mansur et al., Shrestha et al. and Jeremić et al. [24, 26, 27]. Shah et al. in their study in 17–26 years old normal population in eastern Nepal found an overall prosopic index of 88.80 ± 1.98, the value ranging from 86.91 ± 0.93 in Tibeto-Nepalese, 88.40 ± 1.18 in Indo-Nepalese to 91.09 ± 0.69 in Indigenous ethnic groups [28]. Whereas, in their study on Gujrati and non-Gujrati population Shah et al. found hypereuryprosopic followed by euryprosopic facial form to be more prevalent followed by mesoprosopic and leptoprosopic face [29]. The difference in head form and face form between various studies may be due to varying factors such as genetics, geographical location, climate, diet, age and sex [30–33].

The present study demonstrated a weak yet statistically significant negative correlation between the cephalic index (CI) and the facial index (FI). Gender based analysis revealed that this inverse association remained statistically significant in both males (r = − 0.299, p = 0.018) and females (r = − 0.256, p = 0.032). These findings suggest that individuals with broader, rounder head morphology (brachycephalic) tend to have relatively shorter, wider faces, while those with narrower, longer skulls (dolichocephalic) tend to exhibit taller, narrower facial forms (leptoprosopic). This is in agreement with the finding of Prachodh et al. [15]. They too found a weak negative correlation between the cephalic index and facial index ranging from − 0.28 in Class I to -0.08 in Class III malocclusion. Similarly, Muralidhar et al. and Doni et al. too found significant correlation between CI and FI [14, 16]. Whereas, Menapace et al. and Raghavendra et al. found no significant correlation between head and face form [34, 35].

Upon analysis of cephalic and facial index in relation to transverse dental parameters, FI showed negative but significant correlation with UICW and UIMW. Whereas, no significant correlation was seen between FI, LICW and LIMW. Similarly, no significant correlation was found between CI and all the measured transverse dental parameters. Kageyama et al. in their study on adolescent with Class II Division 1 malocclusion found that posterior segment width in the maxillary arch tended to increase from dolichofacial type to brachyfacial type whereas, mandibular arch form did not correlate with facial type [36]. Muralidhar et al. found a good correlation between FI, CI and UICW. However, no correlation was seen between FI, CI and other transverse dental parameters [14]. A positive correlation has been found between transverse dental width and CI [5]. Similarly, correlation ranging from weak to high have been seen between facial width and maxillary dental widths [37–39].

The pattern of association of CI and FI with dental arch dimensions differed markedly between males and females. In males, the cephalic and facial indices showed no significant correlations with any of the four dental arch width measurements. In females, however, the facial index showed significant negative correlations with all four arch width parameters which is in agreement with the findings of Nahid et al. [40]. The gender difference seen may be because of the differences in the head morphology which are influenced by sex-specific developmental trajectories and hormone-mediated skeletal responses, with male craniofacial structures exhibiting greater size but less proportional covariation with dental arch dimensions 41].

However, integrated research on the relationship between cephalic and facial indices and transverse dental arch dimensions remains limited. This study provides baseline anthropometric and dental arch width data for young Nepalese adults, offering reference values for future orthodontic, anthropological, and craniofacial research and supporting the development of population-specific orthodontic diagnostic standards.

Limitations

The present study has certain limitations. The study population consisted only of students from a single institution, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to the wider population. In addition, Angle’s classification of malocclusion and skeletal jaw relationships were not evaluated, which could have provided further insight into the relationship between craniofacial morphology and dental arch dimensions. Future studies with larger and more diverse populations incorporating skeletal and occlusal classifications are recommended.

Conclusions

A weak but statistically significant negative correlation was observed between cephalic index and facial index. Facial index demonstrated a weak negative correlation with maxillary intercanine and intermolar widths, whereas cephalic index did not show significant correlation with dental arch widths. These findings indicate that facial morphology may have a limited association with maxillary dental arch dimensions among young Nepalese adults.
