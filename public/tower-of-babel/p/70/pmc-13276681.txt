Induction of cytochrome P450 2C9 and P‐glycoprotein activity by antiseizure medications: A systematic review and network meta‐analysis
Authors: Hagar Cohen, Nahawand Bahash, Tomer Ben‐Shushan, Ilan Matok, Sara Eyal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesAntiseizure medications (ASMs) can induce the activity of drug‐metabolizing enzymes and drug transporters, including cytochrome P450 (CYP)2C9 and P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp). Our objective was to comparatively assess the effects of ASMs on exposure to clinical CYP2C9 and P‐gp substrates.MethodsThis systematic review and network meta‐analysis (NMA) was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42023473609) and performed following PRISMA 2020 guidelines. MEDLINE, EMBASE, and Cochrane Library were searched until October 22, 2025, with additional searches conducted in the FDA and EMA databases and ClinicalTrial.gov. Studies were included if they were prospective and the ASM was used as monotherapy for ≥5 days. The primary endpoint was the substrate area under the curve ratio (AUCR) with/without the ASM. Treatments were ranked by P‐scores (range 0–1, higher values reflect stronger induction). The point estimate for indirect pairwise comparisons was the standardized mean difference (SMD). Bias risk was assessed using the PKclin tool.ResultsTwelve and six interventional pharmacokinetic studies with 227 and 97 participants were included in the CYP2C9 and P‐gp NMAs, respectively. The ASM with the greatest CYP2C9 induction potential was carbamazepine (600 mg/day, P‐score .78). The only statistically significant effect size estimate for CYP2C9 was obtained in the comparison between carbamazepine 600 mg/day and cenobamate 200 mg/day (SMD −.42; CI −.76, −.09). Carbamazepine (300 or 600 mg/day) was also the strongest P‐gp inducer (P‐scores, .79 and .55, respectively). The effects of its two doses did not differ, and 300 mg/day had a stronger effect on P‐gp compared with the other ASMs.SignificanceDespite variability in populations, substrate drugs, and doses, our findings demonstrate that carbamazepine is an inducer at 300 mg/day, and that ASMs can rank differently as CYP2C9 versus P‐gp inducers. Therefore, the safety of ASM polytherapy cannot be extrapolated from one pathway to another for treatment selection, for example, for post‐stroke epilepsy.Keywords: anticoagulants, area under curve, drug interactions, enzyme induction, polytherapy

INTRODUCTION

People with epilepsy are often prescribed multiple medications. The reasons for polytherapy include treating multiple seizure types or seizures that do not respond to a single antiseizure medication (ASM), synergistic ASM effects, lowering the dose of individual drugs, or management of co‐morbidities such as depression and cardiovascular diseases. 1 , 2 This can lead to drug–drug interactions (DDIs) with reduced treatment efficacy or impaired safety.

At least 11 of the 30 FDA‐approved ASMs are inducers of drug‐metabolizing enzymes and drug transporters. 3 Induced proteins include enzymes of the cytochrome P450 (CYP) superfamily such as CYP3A4, CYP2C9, CYP2B6, and CYP2C19, enzymes that mediate drug conjugation such as UDP‐glucuronosyltransferases (UGTs), and drug transporters, for example, the efflux transporter P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp). 4 All these proteins play key roles in drug detoxification and elimination from the body. Induction of CYP enzymes can derease drug absorption due to greater presystemic metabolism or enhance hepatic drug elimination. P‐gp induction reduces the absorption of substrate drugs whose bioavailability is modest, for example, direct‐acting oral anticoagulants, and enhances the renal excretion of drugs with good bioavailability such as digoxin (Figure 1). 5 , 6

Role of cytochrome P450 (CYP)2C9 and P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp) in pharmacokinetics. CYP isozymes are expressed primarily in the liver, where they mediate Phase 1 metabolism of medications (oxidation, reduction, and hydrolysis). CYP2C9 accounts for approximately 20% of total hepatic P450 proteins and contributes to the metabolism of 15% of medications. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 Its induction enhances drug elimination. P‐gp is localized in tissues important for drug absorption and elimination and in barriers such as the blood–brain barrier and the placenta. P‐gp's most significant pharmacokinetic effects in humans usually occur at the gut, where it can reduce drug absorption by extruding them from enterocytes back to the intestinal lumen, and in the kidney, where it enhances drug efflux to the tubular lumen 5 , 11 (green arrows). In a recent network meta‐analysis (NMA), we demonstrated that the inductive effects of ASMs on CYP3A activity are a continuum, ranging from no effect to strong induction that can reduce the exposure to sensitive substrates by >90%. 12 Similar phenomena can characterize other drug‐metabolizing enzymes and drug transporters that are co‐induced with CYP3A, although the type and magnitude of the effect may differ across the affected proteins. For example, felbamate is a CYP3A inducer 13 but a CYP2C19 inhibitor. 14 , 15 , 16

In the current systematic review and NMA, our goal was to compare the magnitude of ASM‐induced changes in the exposure to substrates of CYP2C9 and P‐gp. CYP2C9 drug–drug interactions can have significant consequences for narrow therapeutic index drugs, including phenytoin and warfarin, and affect many others, for example, non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs. 7 , 8 Intestinal and/or renal P‐gp can alter the exposure to DOACs, digoxin, and other P‐gp substrates. 17 , 18 Although the available literature provides information on CYP2C9 and P‐gp induction by ASMs, the data have not been comparatively assessed. Systematic reviews and meta‐analyses can help address this gap by synthesizing available evidence while accounting for study heterogeneity and methodological limitations. 19 , 20

METHODS

Substrate selection

The affected compounds were selected based on the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) definitions of clinical substrates. 14 For CYP isozymes, the FDA defines sensitive substrates as “drugs that demonstrate an increase in AUC of ≥5‐fold with strong inhibitors of a given metabolic pathway”. Moderate sensitive substrates are “drugs that demonstrate an increase in AUC of ≥2 to <5‐fold with strong inhibitors of a given metabolic pathway”. Because the examples provided by the FDA included only one CYP2C9 sensitive substrate (celecoxib), we instead used the four moderately sensitive substrate examples to maximize the number of results. For P‐gp, we used the five FDA examples of clinical substrates.

Search strategy

This systematic review and network meta‐analysis (NMA) was performed following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analysis (PRISMA) 2020 framework guidelines 21 (Tables S1 and S2) with the protocol registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; CRD42023473609). A medical sciences librarian (T.B.‐S.) assisted in designing the search strategy.

Search terms included the proprietary and trade names of all FDA‐approved ASMs available in the US, as well as clinical moderately sensitive CYP2C9 and P‐gp substrates, in conjunction with the term “Area Under Curve” or its equivalents. We searched MEDLINE, EMBASE, and Cochrane Library. Articles and meeting abstracts in English, French, and Spanish were included. The initial search was conducted on January 11, 2023, with no initial date restriction. The searches were repeated on October 22, 2025, along with searches on the FDA's Clinical Pharmacology Biopharmaceutics Review(s) 3 and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) website for product information. 22 Another search was conducted in ClinicalTrials.gov (search terms CYP2C9, P‐glycoprotein), and in a book dedicated to ASM‐drug interactions. 23 The final search strategy is presented in Tables S3–S5.

Study selection

The inclusion criteria for the studies were as described previously. 12 Briefly, studies or FDA reviews were included if (1) they were prospective, (2) the ASM was administered as monotherapy or polytherapy with drugs that were unlikely to affect its pharmacokinetics, (3) the duration of the ASM treatment was at ≥5 days, (4) AUC values were reported, (5) ASM doses were categorical. The study population could include patients or healthy volunteers with no restriction related to other demographic characteristics. Case reports were excluded. In addition, we excluded studies in which the ASM acted as an inhibitor (mean AUCR >1.25 for CYP2C9 and mean AUCR >1.5 for P‐gp).

Outcomes

The primary parameter was the extent of change in the AUC of CYP2C9 or P‐gp substrates (AUC ratio; AUCR). When both the AUC from time zero (substrate administration) to infinity (AUCinf) and the AUC from time zero to the last time point (AUClast) values were available, we used the former.

Data extraction

Two reviewers (N.B. and H.C.) independently screened the titles, the abstracts, and then the full‐text articles for eligibility using the Rayyan web software for systematic reviews. 24 Disagreements were resolved through consensus with the help of a third reviewer (S.E.). The following characteristics were extracted: last name of the first author (for articles), year of publication, study design, sample size with number of study completers, mean participant baseline age, sex, medical conditions, concurrent medications, ASMs and doses, duration of ASM treatment, CYP2C9 or ABCB1 genotype, and AUC values with 90% CI of the outcomes.

Bias assessment

Two reviewers (H.C. and N.B.) evaluated article quality independently using the ClinPK scoring system. 25 The tool was not applied to FDA reviews, for which it was not designed.

We did not test for funnel plot asymmetry, because it has been recommended that the number of studies in the meta‐analysis may be substantially more than 10 to distinguish chance from real asymmetry (especially when there is substantial heterogeneity). 26

Network meta‐analysis

Only one study whose design met the inclusion criteria directly compared the outcomes between ASMs (gabapentin and pregabalin). 27 Hence, indirect comparisons were conducted using the pairwise method. The point of estimate was the standardized mean difference (SMD). The SMD is used when the outcome is reported as a continuous value. Zero implies that the effects of the new treatment and the placebo are equivalent. 28 The standard error (SE) was calculated from the reported 90% CI in individual studies as: SE = (upper limit − lower limit)/3.29. 12 The interventions were ranked using a P‐score from weak (0) to strong inducers (1). 12

Statistical analyses were conducted using NMAstudio 2.0 (level of significance, .05), and data were presented using GraphPad Prism 10.

Sensitivity analyses and external validation

We conducted an initial proof‐of‐concept analysis across the CYP2C9 studies, with an additional excluded study, 29 to estimate the effect of CYP inhibition on the ability to distinguish between inducers. Based on the findings of that analysis, our CYP2C9 sensitivity analysis was conducted without phenytoin as a substrate to reduce heterogeneity across CYP2C9 substrates. For P‐gp, we excluded digoxin, which is considered less specific than the other substrates, 30 and is mostly a probe of renal P‐gp, whereas fexofenadine, 31 dabigatran, 5 , 30 and edoxaban 32 are considered indicators of intestinal P‐gp activity. Another sensitivity analysis excluded edoxaban microdoses due to potentially nonlinear pharmacokinetics. 27

RESULTS

A total of 1987 articles were identified through systematic database searches, after duplicates were removed (Figure 2). One article was carried over from a previous work. 12 Additional items included NIH's clinical trials (803 entries; CYP2C9 380, P‐gp 423), EMA and FDA labels and reviews (46), and papers listed in a dedicated ASM interaction book (n = 64). 23 Following screening and eligibility assessment, 23 items were reviewed for full text. Ultimately, 12 studies were included in the CYP2C9 network meta‐analysis (NMA) and 6 in the P‐gp NMA. One manuscript presented data for both CYP2C9 and P‐gp. The studies were published between 1993 and 2021 (CYP2C9) and 2001 and 2025 (P‐gp).

Study characteristics

All studies were interventional pharmacokinetic trials conducted in adults aged 18–63 years (Tables S6 and S7). Four CYP2C9 studies included patients with epilepsy. The other studies were conducted in healthy volunteers. The CYP2C9 and P‐gp studies enrolled 227 and 97 participants, respectively. Women were underrepresented, especially in the CYP2C9 studies (up to 21% and up to 46% in the CYP2C9 and P‐gp studies, respectively). The reported completion rate ranged from 74% to 100% (median, 100%). In most cases, withdrawals were due to adverse events. Two studies considered pharmacogenetics. In one study, 33 participants were genotyped for CYP2C9, CYP2C19, and CYP2D6; however, CYP2C9 genotype‐specific data were not presented. In the other, 34 metabolic phenotyping was conducted for CYP2C19 but not CYP2C9.

In each study, the ASM was administered as monotherapy at fixed final daily doses for a minimum of five consecutive days (maximum, 88 days; median, 11 for CYP2C9 and 7.5 for P‐gp). Probe CYP2C9 substrates were phenytoin (seven studies), S‐warfarin (three), and tolbutamide (two). The P‐gp substrates were digoxin (two studies), fexofenadine (two), edoxaban (one), and dabigatran (one) (Tables S6 and S7).

Study quality

The overall quality of the included papers was considered moderate, with median scores of 14.5 (range 13–16) for the 10 full CYP2C9 articles (the FDA Review excluded) and 15 (range 14–19) for the P‐gp studies (Tables S8 and S9). The modest scores were partly related to four questions irrelevant to most drug‐interaction studies. 12

Magnitude of induction

Carbamazepine at 600 mg/day was the strongest CYP2C9 inducer (AUCR, .64; 90% confidence interval, CI, .62 to .66). Its effect was followed by that of 1200 mg/day eslicarbazepine acetate (AUCR .77; 90% CI, .72 to 2; Figure 3A, Table S6). The mean AUCR values of other ASMs were >.8, but the lower CI for valproate and vigabatrin was .78 and .65 (weak and moderate CYP2C9 induction, respectively).

DISCUSSION

This systematic review and NMA provide the first comparative assessment of ASMs as inducers of CYP2C9 and P‐gp. Individual studies have reported interactions between specific ASMs and CYP2C9 or P‐gp substrates, but the evidence has been fragmented. Our systematic approach consolidates these data and offers standardized effect measures that enable identifying several key important issues: (1) low carbamazepine doses are not devoid of drug interactions – the effect of 300 mg/day on P‐gp substrates is no less than that of 600 mg/day; (2) eslicarbazepine acetate does not appear to be a P‐gp inducer, but its effect on CYP2C9 might be of concern; (3) the studied newer ASMs, at their indicated doses, only weakly affect CYP2C9 and P‐gp activities, or do not affect them. One important exception is cenobamate, whose effects on P‐gp as an inducer have not been reported.

Carbamazepine has been suggested as an alternative to rifampin with similar inductive effects, based on AUCR measurements in studies with final carbamazepine doses of 400 or 600 mg/day and several population pharmacokinetic analyses or simulations. 36 Less attention has been paid to lower carbamazepine doses, for example, 200 or 300 mg/day, which are used as starting doses for several days before up‐titration, and sometimes as a maintenance dose. 3 Already during this period, induction can commence, with likely even more pronounced effects on substrates of CYP3A4 and CYP2B6. 14 Alternatives to carbamazepine, such as eslicarbazepine acetate, appear to be safer, particularly when combined with P‐gp substrates.

Differences in effects across drug‐metabolizing enzymes and drug transporters

The magnitude of CYP2C9 and P‐gp induction is generally considered more modest than that of CYP3A4. 4 Our study was not designed to compare maximal induction between CYP3A, CYP2C9, and P‐gp, because the selected CYP2C9 substrates were only moderately sensitive, and the sensitivity of P‐gp substrates has not been defined. 35 The underestimation of maximal CYP2C9 induction can be demonstrated through a comparison between the effects of CYP2C9 inducers on the sensitive substrate celecoxib and moderately sensitive substrates. In studies in healthy volunteers, celecoxib AUCR following treatment with 600 mg/day rifampin was .36 37 compared with .64 of the moderately sensitive substrate tolbutamide (600 mg/day carbamazepine as the inducer) 4 (Table S6).

A recent review suggested that most P‐gp modulators would have a limited impact on drug exposure. 38 For example, exposure to 600 mg/day carbamazepine resulted in a dabigatran etexilate AUCR value of .71, compared with .21 of oral midazolam (a sensitive CYP3A4 substrate) in the same study. 4

The differences in induction potential within and between enzymes/transporters may be explained by substrate specificity, distinct transcriptional regulatory mechanisms and their tissue distribution, higher inducer concentrations in the gut than systemically, and between‐subject variability. For example, CYP2C19 inhibition is likely to explain the 35% increase in phenytoin AUC by 1200 mg/day eslicarbazepine acetate. 29 However, esliarbazepine acetate at the same dose was associated with a reduction in S‐warfarin AUC (Figure 3A, Table S6). In addition, nuclear receptors such as the pregnane‐X receptor (PXR) and the constitutive androstane receptor (CAR) co‐regulate CYP3A4, CYP2C enzymes, and P‐gp 5 , 39 , 40 , 41 and are both expressed in the kidneys. However, only trace amounts of CAR are detected in the gut, which might explain the differences between the renal probe digoxin and the other P‐gp clinical substrates. 5 Moreover, intestinal P‐gp expression is inhibited by regulatory microRNAs (miRNAs), which can contribute to the differences in magnitude between P‐gp and CYP3A induction by ASMs such as carbamazepine. 5

Implications for treatment selection and monitoring

The induction potential should be considered when selecting the first ASM and when adding an ASM to other treatments. Oxcarbazepine and eslicarbazepine acetate are preferred over carbamazepine in most polytherapy regimens. Disadvantages of oxcarbazepine and eslicarbazepine include a higher risk of hyponatremia than with carbamazepine, 42 , 43 costs, and lower availability of therapeutic drug monitoring in some countries. Cenobamate has clinically established effects on several CYP isozymes, but its inducing effects on drug transporters have not been reported yet. If cenobamate activates nuclear receptors such as PXR and CAR, P‐gp substrates may be affected as well. Moreover, the major drug interaction study with cenobamate was not conducted at cenobamate's maximal daily dose, 400 mg. Therefore, the full scope and magnitude of enzyme and transporter induction by cenobamate remain unknown.

Compared with carbamazepine and its derivatives, weak CYP2C9 inducers such as clobazam and valproate are safer choices when combined with less sensitive CYP2C9 substrates or with medications that have a wide therapeutic index. However, their combination with narrow therapeutic index (NTI) medications requires caution and careful monitoring. This is also the case with P‐gp inducers (which co‐induce CYP3A). As of January 2024, the FDA specifies 14 distinct active ingredients as narrow therapeutic index (NTI) drugs in their respective product‐specific guidance. These include the CYP2C9 substrates phenytoin and warfarin, the P‐gp substrate digoxin, and the CYP3A/P‐gp substrates cyclosporine, everolimus, sirolimus, and tacrolimus. 44 NTI drugs are generally used for the prevention or treatment of serious illnesses, including arrhythmias, venous thrombosis, and organ transplantation. Even small changes in their doses or concentrations can lead to toxicity or treatment failure, especially in patients with advanced age, comorbid conditions, or those receiving multiple medications. 44 In the case of digoxin, the therapeutic range has shifted in recent years to lower values (e.g., .5–.9 ng/mL in heart failure) due to increased mortality at serum digoxin levels above 1.2 ng/mL. Hence, the risk of non‐efficacious concentrations with weak inducers has probably increased. 45 In England alone, 2 565 913 prescriptions for digoxin were issued by general practitioners in 2024, 46 demonstrating the scope of potential interactions between ASMs and digoxin.

In addition to the aforementioned drugs, CYP2C9 is responsible for >25% of the metabolism of many NSAIDS (e.g., celecoxib, diclofenac, etodolac, ibuprofen, indomethacin, and mefenamic acid), glibenclamide, losartan, bosentan, fluvastatin, and other medications. 7 P‐gp substrates include calcium channel blockers, antiretroviral drugs, and many chemotherapeutic agents. 11 All these medications can be considerably affected by enzyme or transporter induction, with potentially severe consequences such as drug resistance. With the exception of everolimus, ASMs themselves are not good P‐gp substrates 47 and, if affected by P‐gp inducers, the mechanism is more likely to involve drug‐metabolizing enzymes.

An important consideration in treatment selection is the ability to monitor the concentrations or the activity of the affected drug. For example, all DOACs are expected to be affected by strong CYP3A/P‐gp inducers, and such combinations are generally contraindicated. 5 , 6 Exposure to the alternative anticoagulant warfarin, with monitoring of the international normalized ratio (INR), provides a “safety net” for warfarin, which is also affected by the same ASMs. 6 Yet, the safety/efficacy balance of warfarin is poorer than that of DOACs. 6 At least in the case of carbamazepine, alternative ASMs can be oxcarbazepine or eslicarbazepine acetate, especially in patients treated with the “P‐gp DOACs” dabigatran etexilate and edoxaban. 6 Notably, valproic acid was not included in the P‐gp analysis but might interfere with DOAC activity, possibly due to P‐gp induction. 6

Limitations and strengths

An important limitation of the current analysis is the exclusion of studies with established inducers such as phenobarbital and primidone. These studies presented changes in concentrations and not in the more robust parameter AUC, lumped together several inducers, reported composite outcomes, or the substrate was a shared P‐gp/YP3A4 substrate (e.g., Data et al., 48 and Perlman et al., 49 ). The absence of representation for phenobarbital and primidone does not imply that they are safer than other inducers, such as phenytoin.

A study using 400 mg/day phenytoin was excluded because the substrate was β‐acetyldigoxin. 50 The data from another study, which used 600 mg/day carbamazepine as the inducer and phenytoin as the CYP2C9 substrate, did not allow for calculating the 90% CI. 51 In the edoxaban study, carbamazepine was excluded from the analysis because the daily dose was not categorical (≤400 mg/day). 27 This has also led to a relatively limited set of data, which reduced the power to detect differences across treatments. In addition, the analysis of moderately sensitive substrates, particularly phenytoin, does not capture the true “worst‐case” potential for more sensitive substrates. Similarly, in many cases, the daily ASM doses were not the maximal recommended doses.

All studies were conducted in adults. Accordingly, caution is required when extrapolating their findings and the outcomes of the NMA to pediatric or geriatric populations, 52 , 53 for example, older people with post‐stroke epilepsy. Given the under‐representation of women in these studies, the findings may not capture between‐sex variability (although CYP2C9 activity does not considerably differ between men and women 8 ) and changes in CYP2C9 or P‐gp activity that occur during pregnancy. 54

The strengths of our study include stringent inclusion criteria, independent data extraction and review by two reviewers, assessment of the quality of the included studies, and sensitivity analyses that further support the robustness. The analysis provided standardized effect measures and comparisons via two separate network meta‐analyses (CYP2C9 and P‐gp).

CONCLUSIONS

Our ranking of ASM effects is likely to represent other CYP2C9 and P‐gp substrates, but should not be interpreted as a “worst case scenario” for any ASM. This is because (1) we only assessed moderately sensitive CYP2C9 substrates; (2) no ranking has been established for P‐gp substrates by the FDA and some P‐gp substrates may be more sensitive to induction than others; and (3) the ASMs were not necessarily used at their highest recommended daily doses. The differential ranking of ASMs as inducers of distinct enzymes and transporters suggests that decisions on the safety of ASM combinations cannot be generalized from one enzyme or transporter to another; for instance, the relative safety of eslicarbazepine acetate combinations with dabigatran etexilate or edoxaban cannot be extrapolated to combinations with the CYP3A4/P‐gp DOACs apixaban and rivaroxaban, and to statins such as simvastatin or lovastatin. Finally, even low‐dose carbamazepine is not a safe choice in polytherapy that includes CYP and P‐gp substrates, and carbamazepine should be avoided whenever possible.
