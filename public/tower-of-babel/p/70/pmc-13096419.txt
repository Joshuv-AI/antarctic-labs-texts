Intra and inter-network functional connectivity among long-Covid patients with ongoing disease duration
Authors: Manuel Leitner, Stefan Ropele, Maria Fellner, Marisa Koini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLong-Covid can cause neurological and cognitive dysfunctions. The impact of disease duration on these neurobiological changes remains underexplored. This study aims to investigate how long-Covid disease duration influences intra and inter-network resting-state functional connectivity (rs-fMRI) and its association with clinical, demographic, cognitive, and mental health variables. We conducted a cross-sectional study using rs-fMRI to assess functional connectivity and stratified patients into short (n = 20, M = 8.88 months, SD = 3.88) and long (n = 19, M = 27.77 months, SD = 4.50) disease duration groups. We identified 17 resting-state networks using independent component analysis (ICA) and compared intra- and inter-network connectivity between groups. Additionally, clinical, demographic, cognitive, and mental health data were collected and analysed to explore their relationship with connectivity patterns. Our analysis revealed significant intra-network connectivity differences in six networks. Patients with longer disease duration exhibited increased functional connectivity in networks associated with cognitive functions (e.g. dorsal attention network). Inter-network connectivity analysis showed reduced connectivity between key networks, particularly involving the default mode network in patients with prolonged illness. Notably, there were no significant differences between the groups in demographic, clinical, cognitive, or mental health measures. The observed intra- and inter network connectivity changes might reflect adaptive compensatory mechanisms. Introduction

The diagnosis of long-Covid, also known as post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 infection (PASC)1, has emerged as a significant public health concern since the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. Long-Covid refers to a constellation of symptoms that persist for weeks or months beyond the acute phase of the infection, affecting various organ systems and significantly impacting patients’ quality of life2. Symptoms often include fatigue3, brain fog, memory issues, dyspnea, sleep disorders and concentration difficulties 3–6 months post-acute infection4–6. Recent estimates suggest that the prevalence of long-Covid ranges from 10 to 20%7–9 among individuals who have recovered from their acute infection, with variations depending on the study population and follow-up period.

Given the high prevalence and debilitating nature of long-Covid, understanding its underlying mechanisms is critical. Despite growing research in this area, there remain substantial gaps in our knowledge, particularly concerning the neurobiological impact of long-Covid. Functional neuroimaging studies have begun to shed light on the persistent changes in brain function associated with the condition10–15. However, the long-term effects of these changes and how they are influenced by the duration of the long-Covid illness remain poorly understood. The brain’s functional connectivity, especially as measured through resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI), has been linked to the cognitive and psychological symptoms reported by long-Covid patients16. However, the relationship between illness duration and functional brain alterations warrants further investigation.

Current evidence suggests that disease duration may play a critical role in the extent and nature of long-Covid’s physiological impact. For instance, it has been proposed that a longer disease duration is associated with more profound or persistent neurobiological changes, possibly due to prolonged inflammatory responses or ongoing immune dysregulation17. However, nothing is known about the specific patterns of functional connectivity alterations that occur over time or whether these changes represent adaptive or maladaptive processes. Furthermore, the lack of longitudinal data leaves questions unanswered about the trajectory of these alterations and their potential recovery or exacerbation.

The aim of our study was to address these knowledge gaps by investigating resting-state functional connectivity between individuals with a short and long disease duration unbiased by an a priori definition of regions or networks of interest. We focused on identifying intra- and inter-network connectivity alterations and exploring whether these changes could be linked to clinical, demographic, cognitive, and mental health factors. By doing so, we aimed to provide insights into the neurobiological underpinnings of long-Covid and contribute to a better understanding of how disease duration influences brain function in patients suffering from long-Covid.

Materials and methods

Participants

We performed a within-patient cross-sectional examination of n = 42 individuals after SARS-Cov-2 infection within the scope of the CogniReha study18. Patients were recruited between Oct 10th 2022 and Feb 2nd 2023 by the patient recruiter company “Probando GmbH”. All participants underwent a comprehensive neuropsychological assessment, structural and functional magnetic resonance imaging on the same day. Inclusion criteria were (a) ongoing or newly developed symptoms 3 months after a positive Covid-19 infection, (b) with these symptoms lasting for at least 2 months with no other explanation, and (c) symptoms leading to a new health impairment (self-report), or (d) deterioration of a pre-existing disease (self-report), and (e) none of the following pre-existing diseases: dementia, multiple sclerosis, Parkinson’s disease, stroke, and (f) no participation on any other pharmacological or psychological training study aiming to improve cognitive or psychological complaints. Acute symptoms, symptoms after the acute infection and still ongoing symptoms were assessed with an extended symptom checklist extracted from the NICE-Covid-19 rapid guideline19. Three participants had to be excluded from the imaging analyses due to claustrophobia, refusal, or a foreign metal object close to the eye. In total n = 39 participants were included in this study. Patients were then classified into the groups “long and short long-Covid disease duration” based on a median split in months since first infection (disease duration total sample [months] Mdn = 20.27) yielding two groups (disease duration short [months]: n = 20, Mdn = 8.55, M = 8.88, SD = 3.88, min-max: 4.11–20.27; disease duration long [months]: n = 19, Mdn = 26.48, M = 27.77, SD = 4.50, min-max: 20.93–34.17). Disease duration was used as the primary grouping variable. Visual inspection of the data revealed a bimodal distribution, with one cluster of participants showing a relatively short duration of symptoms ( < ~ 12 months) and another cluster with substantially longer durations ( > ~ 20 months), and few intermediate cases. Given our primary aim to contrast patients with distinctly different illness durations, we applied a median split to form short- and long-duration groups. This approach allowed for the comparison of clinically distinct subgroups while maintaining balanced sample sizes. We acknowledge that dichotomizing a continuous variable can reduce statistical power and obscure nonlinear effects; however, in the present dataset the bimodal distribution and our clinical focus on clearly distinct subgroups supported the use of a categorical grouping strategy.

Clinical and neuropsychological assessment

The following information was gathered during the assessment: age, sex, highest level of education completed, marital status, height, weight, date of Covid diagnosis, duration of acute illness, vaccination status, initial symptoms, current symptoms, hospitalization status, functional status20, pre-existing conditions, alcohol and/or nicotine consumption, as well as any current or previous psychiatric disorders. In addition, a comprehensive neuropsychological assessment battery and questionnaires on subjective cognitive complaints, fatigue, depression, and anxiety were administered to all participants. Global cognitive function was assessed by using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA)21. To assess attention/concentration, we included the digit span forward and backward task from the German version of the Neuropsychological Assessment Battery (NAB)22 and the trail making test (TMT) version A23. Executive functions were assessed with the subtests “planning” and “categories” of the NAB22 and the TMT version B23. Memory was examined with the word list learning test of the NAB22, providing subscores on immediate recall of list A, immediate recall of list B, short and long delayed recall of list A. With the subtests “S-words” and “animals” of the Regensburger Wortflüssigkeitstest (RWT), formal lexical and semantic verbal fluency were evaluated24. For the evaluation of subjective cognitive complaints, a German translation of the Questionnaire de Plainte Cognitive (QPC) by Thomas-Antérion and colleagues25 was used. Depression and anxiety were assessed by using the German version of the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS)26. Additionally, the General Depression Scale (Allgemeine Depressionsskala, ADS) was used to evaluate depression27. In order to assess the extent of fatigue in patients, the German version of the FIS (FIS-D) was administered28. Finally, grip strength was measured using a grip strength dynamometer for both hands29.

Neuroimaging

MRI was performed on a 3 Tesla Prisma scanner (Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany) using a 20-channel head coil. High-resolution structural 3D images were acquired by using a T1-weighted sequence with 0.8 mm isotropic resolution (TI = 1100 ms, TR = 2560 ms, TE = 4.1 ms, 224 slices). Rs-fMRI data were acquired with a single shot echo planar imaging sequence with 3 × 3 × 3 mm resolution (TR = 3000 ms; TE = 30 ms; 150 volumes, field of view = 192 × 192 mm², Matrix = 64 × 64, 36 slices, slice thickness = 3 mm, acquisition time = 7.5 min). Participants were asked to close their eyes during rs-fMRI and not to fall asleep.

Rs-fMRI data processing and analyses

FMRIBs Software Library (FSL Version 6.0.7.11, Oxford, UK) was used for fMRI data pre-processing and neuroimaging analyses. All functional images were initially reoriented to ensure alignment with the MNI152 standard space. Subsequently, brain extraction was carried out30. Pre-processing was performed using FEAT (FMRIB’s Expert Analysis Tool) and comprised motion correction with MCFLIRT, temporal high-pass filtering (cut-off 0.01 Hz), and spatial smoothing employing a Gaussian kernel with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 5 mm. Finally, the functional datasets were aligned to the corresponding individual high-resolution T1-weighted anatomical images using boundary-based registration (BBR).

Head motion was quantified using framewise displacement (FD) according to Power et al. (2012)31. FD was calculated from the MCFLIRT realignment parameters by summing the absolute translational displacements in x, y, and z directions and the rotational displacements (converted from radians to millimeters using a 50 mm head radius). For each subject, mean FD, median FD, maximum FD, and the proportion of volumes exceeding an FD threshold of 0.5 mm were computed. Across participants, head motion was generally low, with subject-level mean FD values ranging from 0.05 to 0.40 mm. The highest FD observed was 1.89 mm. Only few participants exceeded the threshold of 0.5 mm in more than a minimal proportion of frames. These data indicate that overall head motion was modest, although residual motion cannot be completely excluded. Group comparison revealed no significant group differences (U = 122.000, p = .056).

Independent component analysis (ICA) was performed at the single-subject level for each participant, with automatic estimation of the number of components and variance normalization of the time series. The plausibility of components was assessed by visually comparing components across subjects. The resulting ICA output consisted of a 4D image in which each volume represented an individual component. Based on inspection of spatial patterns, temporal dynamics, and frequency spectra, components were manually categorized as signal or noise in a subset of 20 out of 39 participants. These manually labelled components were subsequently used to train FSL FIX32,33, an automated multi-level classification framework, which was then applied to classify components in the remaining 19 participants. All components identified as noise were removed from the data using an aggressive cleaning approach, yielding denoised functional images which were finally registered into the MNI152 standard space.

Rs-fMRI datasets were decomposed into 20 independent components using MELODIC at the group level, with the dimensionality fixed based on prior literature34. Among the resulting 20 components (i.e. functional networks), three networks were identified to be noise.

Maps of all 17 networks can be found in Fig. Suppl. 1. Additionally, we ran correlation analyses of our networks with the ten networks reported by Smith et al.35 to ensure consistency. We found correlation coefficients between (r = 0.36–0.70). Dual regression was used for further analyses36. In the first step, the group-level spatial maps were entered as regressors in a multiple spatial regression against each participant’s four-dimensional fMRI dataset, producing subject-specific time series for each network. In the second step, these time series were used as temporal regressors in a multiple regression applied to the same dataset, resulting in individual spatial maps corresponding to each group-level component36.

For intra-network group differences, we then performed independent samples t-tests by using FSL’s randomise permutation-testing tool. Randomized voxelwise nonparametric permutation testing (5000 permutations) was used as a method for estimating and thresholding the p-value. Results were subsequently corrected for multiple comparisons across space using a threshold free cluster enhancement (TFCE).

To examine inter-network group differences, eight networks were visually identified from the 20 ICA components. The selection was guided by two key criteria: (1) the spatial correspondence of the components with well-established canonical large-scale networks (as described in37), and (2) their theoretical and empirical relevance to higher-order cognitive functions (e.g., reasoning, decision making), particularly the interaction between internally and externally oriented processing. Networks that did not meet these criteria - such as components classified as noise, low spatial coherence, or representing primary sensory regions not central to cognition - were excluded from the inter-network analysis. With this approach, we ensured a hypothesis-driven focus on functional networks most relevant to the cognitive and attentional domains typically affected in long-Covid. The default mode network was separated in three different networks (the anterior default mode network35,37,38, the posterior default mode network 1 and the posterior default mode network 237). The other networks comprised the dorsal attention network37, the executive right and left network35,37,38, the executive control network35,38 and the ventral stream network38(Fig. 1). The time-series associated with these eight components (functional networks) were obtained from each subject’s stage 1 analysis of the dual regression. Simple correlation analyses among these eight networks were calculated for each subject. The correlation coefficients were transformed into Fisher’s z’ values, and group differences (between patients with a short and long disease duration) were tested with independent samples t-tests when appropriate. In case of not normally distributed values, non-parametric Mann-Whitney-U-Tests were used. This approach was chosen equivalently to 37. False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction was applied to account for multiple comparisons.

Eight independent components derived from spatial ICA. Three default mode networks ((a) anterior default mode network, (b) posterior default mode network1, and (c) the posterior default mode network2) and five further cognitive networks were examined ((d) dorsal attention network, (e) ventral stream, (f) executive network right and (h) left, and (g) the executive control network). The IC maps were z-transformed and thresholded at z > 1.96.

Statistical analyses

For statistical analyses, IBM SPSS Statistics Version 29.0., IBM Corp., USA was used. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to test for normal distributions within the variables of interest. For normally distributed variables, group differences (short and long disease duration) were calculated using independent samples t-tests; otherwise, non-parametric Mann-Whitney-U-tests were performed. For all analyses, a two-sided significance level of 5% was assumed. To analyze the relationships between categorical variables, Chi-square tests were used. Fisher’s exact test was used as an alternative when the expected frequencies in more than 20% of the cells in the contingency table were less than 5.

Although exploratory in nature, we performed a post-hoc correlation analysis (Pearson correlation) between the TMT-A and inter-network connectivity scores between the posterior default mode network and the executive networks (left and right) to examine whether the loss of balance-association in the group of long disease duration participants is adaptive or maladaptive. The TMT-A was chosen as it was the only test to show a near-significant difference between the groups (p = 0.07). The inter-network connectivity between the posterior default mode network with the right and the left executive networks were selected based on their known involvement in attention and processing speed, which may render them particularly sensitive to alterations in these functions.

Ethics approval

The present study received approval from the ethics committee of the Medical University of Graz (34–206 ex 21/22). All experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulation. Study recruitment was carried out through advertisements in newspapers and social networks, managed by the company “Probando GmbH”. All eligible patients provided written informed consent for data recording and agreed to the study procedures. All data were pseudonymized (assignment of a subject code) and only the authors of the manuscript have access to the subject code. Hence, no identification of individual participants is possible for others. Participants had the right to withdraw from the study at any time without providing reasons, and without any disadvantage to them. In general, patients did not receive compensation for participating in the study; however, their travel expenses to and from the study site (Medical University of Graz) were reimbursed.

Results

Thirty-nine long-Covid patients (age: M = 49.97, SD = 8.63; IQR = 43.0–55.0, 84.6% female) participated in this study. During the acute phase, two patients (5.1%) were hospitalized. The functional status between groups revealed no difference (U = 158.00, p = .311). No significant group differences were observed in any of the demographic (age, education, sex), disease-related measures (symptom count during acute infection and during long-Covid; hospitalization), or pre-existing conditions and alcohol intake. Solely, vaccination status differed between the short and long disease duration groups (p < .001), with having more vaccinated participants in the short disease duration group. In addition and as expected due to group stratification, disease duration also showed a group difference (p < .001).Table 1 shows the main demographic and clinical characteristics, as well as patients’ vaccination status. No differences were found considering the cognitive tests, or any other psychological, or mental scale or grip strength (Table 2). Also, the sample was predominantly female (84.6%), with a similar sex distribution between groups (short duration: 17 females / 3 males; long duration: 16 females / 3 males; p > .999).

We performed a group-level ICA using MELODIC on pre-processed resting state data from all n = 39 available participants included in the study (three MRI-related exclusions due to claustrophobia, refusal, or a foreign metal object close to the eye, total sample n = 42). No participant hat to be excluded due to insufficient data quality or substantial movement. MELODIC decomposed the rs-fMRI data into 20 components, in line with previous literature on large-scale networks (e.g.35). Of these 20 components, eight networks were chosen based on visual inspection and comparison on spatial similarity with the networks published in37, due to their importance for the dynamic interplay between internally and externally directed cognitive processing.

Exploratory post-hoc analysis

A Pearson correlation between the TMT-A and the inter-network correlation coefficient of the executive right network with the posterior default mode network1 revealed a significant correlation for patients with long disease duration (r = 0.55, p = 0.014) (Fig. 3), whereas no such correlation was found for those within the short disease duration group (r = − 0.16, p = 0.510). We found no comparable results for the correlation between the executive left and the posterior DMN1 with the TMT-A (short disease duration: r = 0.01, p = 0.958; long disease duration: r = 0.40, p = 0.090).

Discussion

This study aimed to examine differences in resting-state functional connectivity between individuals with short and long disease durations following a COVID-19 infection, focusing on intra- and inter-network connectivity. We explored these differences in relation to demographic, clinical, cognitive, and mental health factors. Our findings reveal substantial variations in functional connectivity between these groups, underscoring the complexity of long-term neurological changes associated with prolonged illness duration in long-Covid patients. Overall, we interpret the intra as well as the inter-network changes in functional connectivity in the group with longer disease duration to be potentially beneficial since also no clinical, cognitive and mental differences were observed compared to individuals with a short long-Covid disease duration. While our findings suggest altered intra- and inter-network connectivity in individuals with longer disease duration, these results must be interpreted with caution. In the absence of a healthy control group, we cannot definitively attribute the observed changes to adaptive or compensatory mechanisms specific to long-Covid progression. The differences in connectivity may also reflect other post-infectious effects or pre-existing interindividual variability. Nevertheless, given that both groups were comparable with respect to clinical, cognitive, and demographic variables, and that the directionality of the effects aligns with previous studies describing compensatory network reorganization, our interpretation remains a plausible, though speculative, explanation.

Interestingly, no significant differences were found between the groups in terms of symptom burden, neither during the acute phase of infection nor during the long-Covid phase. Both groups reported a comparable number of symptoms, suggesting that symptom count alone does not distinguish between individuals with short versus long disease duration in this sample. This is noteworthy given prior literature reporting a gradual decline in symptom load over time in most long-Covid patients39,40. The lack of difference in symptom count, despite significantly different durations of illness, may indicate that some individuals experience a more persistent symptom profile, regardless of how long their condition lasts. This finding highlights the heterogeneity of long-Covid trajectories and suggests that symptom improvement is not solely time-dependent. Aside from vaccination status, which was higher in the short-duration group, and the expected difference in disease duration itself, no other demographic or clinical variables differed between groups. Below, we would like to outline our interpretation in further detail:

Intra-network connectivity differences

We identified seventeen distinct resting-state networks commonly reported in the literature35,37,38,41. Of these, we found significant differences in intra-network connectivity between the short and long disease duration groups. Notably, four of these networks are crucial for cognitive functioning: the dorsal attention network, the ventral stream network, the insular salience network, and, indirectly, the anterior default mode network. Additionally, we observed differences in the visual network, associated with visual processing42, and the sensorimotor network, linked to motor function43,44. The consistent increase in intra-network connectivity within the long disease duration group, in comparison to those with shorter durations, suggest potential compensatory mechanisms since the absence of significant differences in demographic, clinical, cognitive, or mental health measures may reflect adaptive neural processes. The brain might adjust to maintain functional stability by enhancing neuronal efficiency and integration, mitigating observable cognitive or clinical deficits. For instance, in the anterior default mode network and the frontal pole, altered connectivity may support executive control and self-monitoring, promoting cognitive stability despite disruptions45. Within the dorsal attention network, connections to the postcentral gyrus may compensate to improve sensory attention and motor coordination, in order to maintain sensory-motor integration46. Changes in the ventral stream network, particularly involving the inferior frontal gyrus (pars triangularis), could support language and semantic processing, preserving communication efficacy47. Finally, adaptations in the insular salience network, particularly involving the cingulate and superior temporal gyri, may enhance attentional allocation and emotional-sensory integration, supporting responsiveness to salient stimuli and social interactions48.

Some of the observed group differences in functional connectivity extended beyond the spatial boundaries (Fig. 2b, f) of the networks initially defined through group-level ICA. However regions outside the spatial extent of a network may still be functionally coupled to it. In addition, the components reflect a group average which may not capture individual-specific variations in network topography. Finally, the dual regression approach used in our design was intended to identify group differences in network connectivity across the whole brain, which may also capture connectivity changes extending beyond the predefined networks.

It remains plausible that the potentially compensatory increases in connectivity, as described before, could be unsustainable over time, potentially leading to long-term negative outcomes. Chronic local oxidative stress and subsequent neural exhaustion49 could eventually manifest as clinically relevant deficits. The persistence of numerous symptoms in the group with long disease duration, contrary to some reports suggesting symptom decline with extended disease duration39,40, may indicate ongoing pathological processes. These might involve persistent neurobiological or immunological disruptions, suggesting an incomplete recovery trajectory in patients with prolonged illness1. Prolonged low-level inflammation or microglial activation could influence functional connectivity without necessarily causing overt clinical symptoms50. Therefore, resting-state analyses may detect subtle neurobiological alterations that remain latent, highlighting the method’s sensitivity in uncovering these changes before they become clinically apparent.

The investigation of intrinsic resting-state networks is important for understanding the neural basis of human behaviour. Large-scale networks such as the default mode network, salience network, and executive control network have been shown to support attention, cognitive flexibility, memory, and complex social processing in healthy individuals51. For example, balanced integration and segregation among these networks can reliably predict individual differences in processing speed, memory, and general cognitive ability52. The default mode network is also involved in understanding internal thoughts during everyday experiences, which supports its role in complex information processing53. By studying these brain connectivity patterns in people with long-Covid, our work adds to the growing literature on this topic.

Inter-network connectivity differences

Our inter-network connectivity analysis revealed that individuals with a longer disease duration exhibited reduced balance between several key networks: posterior default mode network 1 and anterior default mode network, posterior default mode network 1 and the right executive network, posterior default mode network 2 and the right executive network, and posterior default mode network 1 and posterior default mode network 2. These findings indicate that a prolonged disease duration significantly disrupts inter-network communication, particularly involving the default mode network. This decline in inter-network connectivity suggests a loss of balance between functional networks, historically interpreted as reduced efficiency54, and hence being maladaptive. However, this reduced connectivity does not necessarily imply maladaptive changes55–57. It is plausible that this loss of balance between networks serves a potential adaptive role. In situations requiring high attention and focused cognitive control, reduced inter-network communication could enhance task-specific network efficiency58. Such a mechanism may result in improved cognitive performance, as less interference from unrelated networks, such as the default mode network, allows for more specialized and efficient processing55–57.

Specifically, decreased connectivity between the right executive network and the posterior default mode network 1 may support cognitive tasks requiring external attention. The right executive network is essential for various externally directed functions, including the coordination of behaviour in a rapid, accurate, and flexible goal-driven manner59 or directed attention48, while the default mode network is typically deactivated during these tasks and active during introspective processes45. Reduced communication, i.e. loss of balance, between these networks may therefore optimize cognitive performance, as the segregation allows each network to operate more efficiently and less collaboratively. This hypothesis gets very mildly supported by our post-hoc analysis revealing a positive correlation between the balance in these networks with the performance in the TMT-A, a cognitive test measuring processing speed and attention, in the group with long disease duration, i.e. the better the balance between the networks the higher the time needed for the TMT-A. Specifically, better network balance was associated with longer completion times on the TMT-A. In contrast, no such correlation was found for individuals with short disease duration, while at the same time no group differences in the TMT-A were observed. The interpretation of reduced DMN–executive network connectivity as an adaptive mechanism is limited by the absence of significant cognitive differences between groups and relies on a marginal, post-hoc correlation with a single cognitive measure. Therefore, conclusions regarding functional relevance remain speculative and require validation.

Another important finding in this regard is that the inter-network correlations of the entire sample in almost all cases is comparable in size and direction with37. Finding the same correlations supports the reproducibility and robustness of the results. It indicates that the observed relationships are not random but consistently appear across different samples or under varying conditions. This increases the reliability of our findings and supports the general validity of the conclusions.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, we did not include a prospectively recruited, site-matched healthy control group, which limits the generalizability of our findings beyond the investigated patient cohort. While we considered using publicly available healthy controls, such an approach would introduce a significant confounding of “group” with “site/scanner”, because all patients underwent the MRI scans at our site while external controls would come from different scanners and protocols (e.g., distinct TR/TE, multiband factors, spatial resolution, phase-encoding, and eyes-open vs. eyes-closed instructions). This would introduce residual variance related to the testing site, even after adjusting for it as a covariate, and hence limit interpretability. In addition, resting-state connectivity is highly sensitive to head motion and quality-control decisions. External datasets often differ in motion distributions and QC/exclusion criteria, making it difficult to ensure the same quality standards. For these reasons, we refrained from comparing our results to a site-matched healthy control group, as the results could be misleading if interpreted as a patient-vs-healthy contrast. Our analyses therefore focus on a-priori within-patient contrasts, with the groups being comparable in demographics and clinical characteristics. Moreover, the group allocation appears, although performed based on statistical considerations, fair and balanced without a selection bias leading to higher generalizability. Future studies should include healthy controls recruited at the same sites, using the same data collection and processing methods, to provide a clear comparison group and to improve generalizability of the results. Second, a further limitation of our study concerns vaccination status, which was strongly imbalanced between groups (short-duration: 19/20 vaccinated; long-duration: 18/19 unvaccinated). This near-perfect confounding makes it statistically impossible to disentangle the independent effects of disease duration and vaccination on functional connectivity, since including vaccination as a covariate would lead to collinearity and therefore uninterpretable estimates. Subgroup analyses are equally unfeasible given that two of the four possible group-by-vaccination cells contained only a single individual. Thus, we cannot exclude that vaccination contributed to the observed group differences. Future studies should balance or stratify vaccination status to directly investigate its potential impact on functional connectivity. Third, the lack of a longitudinal follow-up, particularly for the short disease duration group, represents a critical limitation. A follow-up study could provide valuable insights into whether functional connectivity changes over time, and whether the observed compensatory or adaptive patterns persist, diminish, or become maladaptive with disease progression. Understanding these temporal dynamics would be crucial for elucidating the trajectory of neurobiological changes in long-Covid. Additionally, our study was limited by potential variability in self-reported symptoms and the influence of unmeasured confounding factors, such as individual differences in genetic predispositions. Moreover, our findings are based on cross-sectional data, making it difficult to infer causal relationships or the directionality of the observed connectivity changes. Another limitation of this study is the significant gender imbalance, as the majority of participants were female. This limits the generalizability of the findings to male patients and may mask potential gender-specific effects on functional connectivity. The limited number of male participants in the present study did not allow for reliable and meaningful gender-stratified analyses. Therefore, future studies should aim to recruit more balanced samples to enable investigation of gender-related differences and to strengthen the external validity of the results.

A further limitation of our study is that the interpretation of altered connectivity as “adaptive” or “compensatory” remains hypothetical, as this study was cross-sectional. Hence, longitudinal data and correlations with behavioural or clinical measures are lacking. Future longitudinal studies are warranted to determine the functional relevance of these findings.

Also, disease duration was analysed using a dichotomization approach based on the median, which may reduce statistical power and mask potential nonlinear relationships. In our dataset, disease duration displayed a bimodal distribution, supporting the separation into two clinically distinct groups. Nevertheless, future work with larger samples should also examine disease duration as a continuous predictor. Lastly, the number of ICA components was chosen based on previous literature, but no formal stability metrics were computed. While this is a common approach in group-ICA studies, future work may benefit from the use of component stability analyses to further validate ICA dimensionality. Future research should also aim to include longitudinal designs, incorporate healthy control groups, and account for additional confounding variables to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying functional connectivity alterations in long-Covid.

Conclusion

Our findings emphasize the pronounced and potentially adaptive nature of functional connectivity changes in long-Covid patients experiencing prolonged disease duration. While intra-network connectivity alterations appear compensatory, inter-network connectivity reductions could maintain task-specific processing. These insights shed light on the neurobiological processes associated with long-Covid and underscore the importance of resting-state network analyses in detecting subtle brain changes that might precede clinical or cognitive manifestations.
