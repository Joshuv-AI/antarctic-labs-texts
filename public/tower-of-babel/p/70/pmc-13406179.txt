Integrated analysis of scRNA-seq and TCR-seq on T cells in aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage: A descriptive, hypothesis-generating case study
Authors: Ningqin Meng, Xingrao Li, Ziming Ye, Xufeng Xie, Ying Liu, Chao Qin, Ying Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This studthe transcriptomic profiles and T cell receptor (TCR) repertoires of T cells in paired cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and peripheral blood (PB) from 2 patients with aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH). This descriptive work primarily generates research hypotheses and does not explore causal biological mechanisms. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and T cell receptor sequencing (TCR-seq) were performed on T cells isolated from CSF and PB samples collected on day 7 post-onset from 2 male patients. A series of descriptive analyses were conducted, including T cell subset classification, pseudotime trajectory reconstruction, pathway enrichment, CellChat-based cell-cell communication analysis, and TCR clonality assessment. Public scRNA-seq data of CSF samples from 8 healthy individuals were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus database and used as external healthy controls. A total of 6058 T cells for scRNA-seq and 5008 T cells for TCR-seq were analyzed in this study. Five distinct T cell subsets were observed. In these 2 patients, CSF-derived T cells showed stronger signaling pathway activation and increased clonal expansion than PB-derived T cells. Compared with healthy controls from the public dataset, T cells from SAH patients presented enhanced signaling pathway activity and more extensive cell-cell interactions. All above observations are limited to these 2 individuals and cannot be generalized to all SAH patients without large independent cohort validation. This descriptive case-based study establishes a high-resolution single-cell atlas of T cells from 2 SAH patients. The observed differences between CSF and PB, as well as between SAH patients and healthy controls, provide directions for future mechanistic investigations.

1. Introduction

Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) is a life-threatening cerebrovascular disease caused by intracranial aneurysm rupture.[1] Although SAH only accounts for 5% to 10% of all strokes, SAH patients are much younger than those with other types of stroke.[2] Despite substantial advances in the treatment of SAH, such as neurosurgical clipping and endovascular coiling, the mortality and disability rates have not significantly decreased over recent decades. Furthermore, some SAH survivors develop neurological complications including delayed cerebral ischemia, epilepsy and hydrocephalus, leading to poor clinical prognoses. Neuroinflammation, characterized by a dysregulated immune microenvironment, plays a crucial role in brain injury following SAH.[3] Therefore, a better understanding of the pathophysiology of neuroinflammation could help mitigate the inflammatory response and improve clinical outcomes.

Following SAH onset, massive blood accumulates in the subarachnoid space, breaking down into hemoglobin and other components that trigger neuroinflammation.[4] These breakdown products activate multiple inflammatory pathways,[5] which recruit various immune cells[6] and generate numerous inflammatory factors.[7,8] Both innate and adaptive immunity are key components of the human defense system and play essential roles in initiating neuroinflammation following SAH. Innate immunity provides the initial defense against external threats, while adaptive immunity offers a specific response to antigens. These 2 mechanisms work closely to maintain immune system integrity and functional diversity.[9] However, unbalanced immune activation in innate and adaptive immunity leads to the overactivation of immune cells, dysfunctional signaling pathways, and excessive cytokine production after SAH.[10] Previous studies have primarily focused on the role of innate immunity in neuroinflammation, with limited attention given to adaptive immunity following SAH. The precise role of adaptive immunity in the underlying mechanisms of neuroinflammation following SAH remains unclear.

T cells are crucial mediators of adaptive immunity, playing vital roles in fighting infections, maintaining immune balance, and forming immune memory.[11] Adaptive immunity is primarily initiated by T cell receptors (TCRs), which are expressed on the surface of T cells and mainly composed of α and β protein chains. The β chain is encoded by V, D, and J gene segments, while the α chain is encoded by V and J gene segments. The diversity of the TCR repertoire arises from the random rearrangement of V, D, and J gene segments.[12] T cells rapidly proliferate upon antigen binding, generating clonally expanded T cells with identical TCRs. Increasing evidence suggests that T cells can infiltrate the brain and mediate neuroinflammation after SAH. The proportion of T cells in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) increases over time after SAH, particularly CD4+ T cells.[13] However, a previous study reported that lymphocyte counts decline initially within 24 hours post-SAH and then rise steadily thereafter.[14] In both CSF and peripheral blood (PB), T cells are activated and mediate an inflammatory response after SAH.[15] Furthermore, T cells are involved in the inflammatory response after SAH and have even been found in the walls of unruptured intracranial aneurysms.[16] CD4+ T cells exhibit dynamic and unique activation patterns after SAH and during the presentation of complications.[17] In contrast to other T-cell subsets, peripheral regulatory T cells may suppress the inflammatory response, leading to long-term outcomes after SAH.[18] These studies demonstrate that T cells are involved in the pathophysiology of neuroinflammation following SAH.

Until now, most T cell studies have focused on immune cell subsets and soluble cytokines or chemokines, with the transcriptome profile and TCR repertoire remaining unclear. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), a groundbreaking technology that explores gene expression at the single-cell level, has been applied to characterize the transcriptome and TCR repertoire heterogeneity in various diseases.[19,20] Herein, we conducted scRNA-seq and TCR-seq on T cells to comprehensively analyze the transcriptome profile and TCR repertoire, providing a descriptive characterization of T cell features in SAH as a first step toward hypothesis generation. This study does not aim to establish causal mechanisms.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study participants

This study enrolled 2 male SAH patients (aged 56 and 58 years) whose detailed clinical profiles had been described in a previous report.[21] Upon admission, these patients underwent brain computed tomography scans and digital subtraction angiography examinations. The inclusion criteria for SAH were as follows: (a) age ≥ 18 years; (b) SAH diagnosed through computed tomography and DSA. Exclusion criteria included (a) patients with systemic inflammatory diseases, (b) SAH caused by other diseases, and (c) patients using immunosuppressive medications. This study was approved by the ethics committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from the legally authorized representatives of the 2 enrolled patients prior to sample collection. In addition, publicly available CSF scRNA-seq profiles of healthy volunteers were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (accession GSE241385) and utilized as the HC group.[22]

Given the small sample size (n = 2), this study is explicitly designed as a descriptive, exploratory case-based analysis. Findings are presented as observations in these 2 patients and cannot be generalized without independent validation in larger cohorts.

2.2. Sample collection

Paired CSF and PB samples were collected from each SAH patient on day 7 post-onset via lumbar drainage. This study collected 2 CSF samples (CSF 1 and CSF 2) and 2 PB samples (PB 1 and PB 2) for sequencing. Mononuclear cells (MCs) were isolated from the CSF and PB samples using Ficoll density gradient centrifugation within 30 minutes of collection. The MC suspensions were frozen in an ultra-low-temperature freezer and transferred to liquid nitrogen for subsequent sequencing. Before scRNA-seq and TCR-seq, the MC suspensions were thawed and subjected to quality control.

2.3. ScRNA-seq and TCR-seq

In this study, each sample was individually sequenced using the Chromium Single Cell Immune Profiling Solution (10× Genomics) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, MC suspensions were loaded into a 10× Genomics Chromium Controller to create nanoliter-scale gel beads in emulsion (GEMs). This procedure ensured that only 1 T cell was captured in each GEM. The mRNA from the T cell was reverse-transcribed into cDNA. Subsequently, the cDNA was subjected to PCR amplification, purification, and quantification to fulfill the requirements for library construction. The cDNA libraries that were labeled and qualified were divided into 2 parts. One part was used to create gene expression libraries. This process involved fragmenting the cDNA, attaching sequencing adapters, performing sample index PCR, etc. The other part was subjected to nested amplification using primers targeting the VDJ region of the TCR to obtain the full-length VDJ sequence, which was used to construct TCR-enriched libraries. The gene expression and TCR-enriched libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

2.4. Sequencing data processing

The single-cell sequencing data were processed using Cell Ranger (10× Genomics) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Cell Ranger performed functions such as data format conversion, gene alignment, and generation of gene expression matrices. V(D)J sequencing data processing included sequence assembly, V(D)J annotation, and clonotype generation. Seurat (CRAN, Comprehensive R Archive Network), an R software package, was used for the standard processing of single-cell sequencing data,[23] including quality control, selection of highly variable genes, data dimension reduction, cell clustering, and cell annotation. Low-quality cells were excluded from the analysis based on the following criteria: gene numbers < 200 or >4500; (b) total unique molecular identifier counts < 1000 or >20,000; and (c) mitochondrial gene percentage > 10%. Sequencing data were visualized using t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding.[24]

SingleR was employed for initial cell type annotation,[25] which was subsequently refined through manual curation based on the expression of characteristic marker genes. T cells were classified into distinct subsets according to their specific marker gene profiles: CD4+ T cells were identified by elevated CD4 expression, CD8+ T cells by prominent CD8 expression, regulatory T cells (Tregs) by high FOXP3 expression, and naive T cells by CCR7 expression.

2.5. Pseudotime trajectory analysis

Monocle, a specialized R package designed for analyzing cellular differentiation and developmental processes, was utilized to investigate distinct gene expression patterns and reconstruct the T cell differentiation trajectory. Through pseudotime analysis, we constructed a comprehensive lineage differentiation trajectory of T cells, enabling the identification of differentially expressed gene modules.

2.6. Enrichment analysis of biological functions and signal pathways

We conducted a comprehensive enrichment analysis employing both the Gene Ontology (GO) and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases. The GO database, which systematically categorizes gene functional annotations into 3 distinct domains – cellular component, molecular function, and biological process (BP) – was utilized to characterize gene functions. Concurrently, KEGG, a bioinformatics resource that facilitates the prediction of intricate cellular pathways and biological behaviors of target genes, was employed to elucidate metabolic pathways and BPs in T cells.[26]

Furthermore, we implemented Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA), a powerful nonparametric method that calculates enrichment scores for specific gene sets across individual samples. This approach enables the assessment of signaling pathway activation states within distinct cell subpopulations or sample groups. In this study, GSVA was applied to investigate the activation patterns of enriched signaling pathways across T-cell subsets.

2.7. Cell communication interaction

Cell-cell communication, facilitated by ligand-receptor interactions, is essential for coordinating various BPs, such as cellular development, differentiation, immune regulation, and inflammatory responses. To investigate these complex interactions, we employed CellChat, an R package specifically designed for comprehensive analysis of cell–cell communication networks in single-cell RNA sequencing data.[27] This powerful tool enables systematic exploration of communication patterns between distinct cell populations and facilitates the identification of key signaling inputs and outputs within these populations. In this study, we implemented CellChat to perform a comparative analysis of communication networks among T-cell subsets derived from CSF and PB, with the specific objective of elucidating how these T cells interact to mediate neuroinflammatory processes.

2.8. TCR repertoire analysis

The third complementarity-determining region (CDR3), encoded by the V, D, and J gene segments, exhibits the highest degree of sequence variability and plays a critical role in antigen recognition. As the most functionally significant region of the TCR, CDR3 serves as the primary determinant of TCR repertoire diversity. In our comprehensive analysis of TCR repertoire characteristics, we systematically examined multiple parameters: the distribution profile of CDR3 length, the preferential usage patterns of V and J gene segments, and the predominant combinations of V–J gene pairings.

3. Results

3.1. Single-cell transcriptomic landscape of T cells

In this study, we analyzed 4 samples from 2 SAH patients, comprising 2 CSF samples (CSF 1 and CSF 2) and 2 paired PB samples (PB 1 and PB 2; Fig. 1A). For comparative analysis, we generated pooled datasets: sequencing data from CSF 1 and CSF 2 were defined as the CSF pool, while data from PB 1 and PB 2 were defined as the PB pool. Following quality control, data dimension reduction, cell clustering, and annotation procedures, we observed a total of 6058 T cells, consisting of 3873 cells from the CSF and 2185 cells from the PB (Fig. 1B). Using the Seurat R package, these T cells were classified into 13 clusters (numbered 0–12). The violin plot showed significant expression of specific marker genes in their corresponding T-cell clusters (Fig. 1D). Based on the expression patterns of these marker genes, the 13 T-cell clusters were further categorized into 5 subsets: CD4+ T cells, naive CD4+ T cells, regulatory T cells (Treg), CD8+ T cells, and naive CD8+ T cells (Fig. 1C). Specifically, CD4+ T cells were identified by elevated expression of CD4, CD8+ T cells by high CD8 expression, Treg cells by substantial FOXP3 expression, and naive T cells by characteristic CCR7 expression.[28-31] The histogram illustrated the total count and proportional distribution of T-cell subsets from the PB and CSF (Fig. 1E).

The above observations were obtained in these 2 patients. Whether these characteristics can represent the general features of SAH patients requires further verification with larger sample sizes.

3.2. Differentiation trajectory of T-cell subsets

T cells originate in the bone marrow and circulate through the lymphatic system and bloodstream to perform immune functions throughout various organs and tissues. The differentiation of T cells typically begins with naive T cells, advances to mature T cells, and ultimately results in effector T cells.[11] In this study, we employed Monocle to analyze the differentiation trajectory of T-cell subsets. The trajectory analysis showed that T-cell differentiation progressed from right to left (Fig. 2A). Notably, T cells from PB demonstrated a linear differentiation process without branching. In contrast, T cells from CSF exhibited multiple small branches during differentiation (Fig. 2A). This difference may be attributed to the distinct immune microenvironments present in PB and CSF. The trajectory plot depicted T-cell subsets in distinct colors, illustrating a clear transition from naive to mature T cells, including CD4+ T, CD8+ T, and regulatory T cells (Treg; Fig. 2B). A heatmap analysis highlighted 2 gene modules that were significantly differentially expressed during the differentiation process (Fig. 2C). Gene module 1 was highly expressed in naive T cells but showed low expression in CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. This module included genes such as CCR7, SELL, and members of the RPS family, which are associated with T-cell differentiation, rRNA processing, and cytoplasmic translation (Fig. 2C). In contrast, gene module 2 was highly expressed in CD4+ and CD8+ T cells but exhibited low expression in naive T cells (Fig. 2C). We observed that gene module 2 was linked to lymphocyte-mediated immunity, cell killing, and antigen processing and presentation, processes implicated in neuroinflammation following SAH (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, the dynamic expression patterns of key genes associated with T-cell differentiation (CCR7, SELL, SOCS3, and TCF7) and biological functions such as cytotoxicity and antigen clearance (S100A4, GZMK, CLIC1, and IL32) were visualized along the pseudotime trajectory (Fig. 2D). Collectively, these differentially expressed gene modules showed distinct expression features across T-cell subsets in this study. Further studies are needed to confirm the universality of these findings in a broader SAH population.

3.3. Comparison of biological characteristics in T-cell subsets between CSF and PB

We performed GO and GSVA to explore the biological functions and signaling pathway activation in CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. The GO analysis showed that CD4+ and CD8+ T cells derived from CSF were significantly enriched in BPs such as RNA and mRNA splicing, regulation of RNA splicing, and response to endoplasmic reticulum stress (Fig. 3A, B). We detected elevated pathway activation in CSF T cells, with distinct transcript profiles relative to peripheral counterparts; the underlying biological drivers cannot be determined in this descriptive cohort. Compared to PB, the activation of signaling pathways in CSF was markedly enhanced, including pathways such as the spliceosome, NOD-like receptor, and NOTCH signaling (Fig. 3C). Notably, the spliceosome, NOD-like receptor, and NOTCH signaling pathways were significantly upregulated in CD4+ T cells from CSF compared to those from PB after SAH (Fig. 3D).

To investigate the communication dynamics among CD4+ T, Treg, and CD8+ T cells, we employed CellChat for interaction analysis. In PB, CD4+ T cells were found to send signals to both CD4+ Treg and CD8+ T cells (Fig. 3E, Left), while Treg cells primarily communicated with CD8+ T cells and themselves (Fig. 3E, Middle). CD8+ T cells, on the other hand, exhibited self-directed communication without interacting with other subsets (Fig. 3E, Right). In the CSF, all three types of T-cell subsets interacted and communicated with one another (Fig. 3F). Comparative analysis showed significant differences in communication patterns between PB (Fig. 3G, Left) and CSF (Fig. 3G, Middle). The interaction strengths among T-cell subsets was markedly enhanced in CSF compared to PB (Fig. 3G, Right). A detailed comparison of significant ligand-receptor pairs across T-cell subsets further highlighted these differences (Fig. 3H). Additionally, signaling pathways were evaluated based on the overall information flow within the communication networks, demonstrating distinct pathway activation profiles between PB and CSF (Fig. 3I). In summary, T-cell subsets in CSF exhibited a more extensive communication network compared to those in PB following SAH in these 2 patients.

It remains unclear whether this phenomenon exists in most SAH patients, and more samples are required for validation.

3.4. Role of T-cell subsets in neuroinflammation after SAH

To further explore the potential involvement of the aforementioned signaling pathways in the pathogenesis of SAH, we obtained a scRNA-seq dataset from the CSF of 8 healthy individuals from the GEO database, which served as the healthy control (HC). We conducted KEGG and GSVA on CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell subsets to examine the biological functions and signaling pathway activation after SAH. Compared to HC, volcano plots identified significantly upregulated genes in CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell subsets from SAH patients (Fig 4A, B). Enrichment analysis revealed that terms related to ubiquitin protein ligase binding, ubiquitin-like protein ligase binding, and cadherin binding were significantly enriched in CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell subsets in SAH (Fig. 4C, D). GSVA further demonstrated that pathways including lysosome, spliceosome, and others were more highly activated in CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell subsets from SAH compared to HC (Fig. 4E). Additionally, pathways such as Aminoacyl tRNA biosynthesis, lysosome, and spliceosome were also upregulated in SAH (Fig. 4G, H). Moreover, the activation of transcription factors was significantly increased in SAH compared to HC (Fig. 4F). Our data showed altered signaling pathway activities in T cells of SAH patients relative to public healthy controls in this small cohort. Large-scale studies are needed to verify the above observations.

3.5. Cell communication was more abundant among T-cell subsets in SAH

To investigate the communication among T-cell subsets in SAH, we utilized CellChat to analyze interaction patterns. Compared to HC, the number and strength of interactions were significantly elevated in SAH (Fig. 5A), with a marked upregulation observed in SAH (Fig. 5B). We further examined the differences in signaling pathways involved in T-cell subset communication between SAH and HC. Several signaling pathways were uniquely identified in SAH (Fig. 5C), with CD8+ T-cell subsets exhibiting the highest relative signaling strength in both SAH and HC (Fig. 5D). Notably, the number of upregulated signaling pathways in SAH exceeded that of downregulated pathways (Fig. 5E). The MHC-I signaling pathway network was more extensive and abundant in SAH (Fig. 5F), and the expression levels of key genes associated with MHC-I signaling pathways were significantly elevated in SAH (Fig. 5H, I). The MHC-II signaling pathway network was also exclusively identified in SAH (Fig. 5G). In summary, T-cell subset communication was more prevalent and complex in SAH compared to HC in these 2 patients. Further research is required to determine the generalizability of this finding.

3.6. The characteristics of the TCR repertoire

Upon antigen binding, T cells undergo rapid proliferation, leading to the generation of clonally expanded T cells. In this study, clone sizes were defined based on the number of T cells sharing identical TCRs, and clonotypes were categorized as either clonally expanded (containing at least 2 T cells) or unique (containing only a single T cell). Following quality control and data filtering, a total of 5008 T cells were retained for analysis, comprising 2959 cells from CSF and 2049 cells from PB. Further analysis showed 971 clonally expanded T cells in the CSF and 446 in the PB. Among the 2959 T cells in the CSF, 2222 distinct clonotypes were identified, with 234 being clonally expanded. In contrast, among the 2049 T cells in the PB, 1691 distinct clonotypes were detected, with only 88 being clonally expanded. Notably, the CSF exhibited a higher proportion of both clonally expanded T cells and clonotypes compared to the PB (Fig. 6A). We observed a higher level of T cell clonal expansion in CSF than in PB in these 2 patients. Whether this is a common feature of SAH requires additional validation.

(B) No significant difference in CDR3 AA length of TRA or TRB between CSF and PB. (C, D) Percentages of the top 10 V and J genes for TRA and TRB, showing similar preferential usage in CSF and PB. CSF = cerebrospinal fluid, PB = peripheral blood, SAH = aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage TCR = T cell receptor, TRA = TCR α chain, TRB = TCR β chain.

The CDR3 of the TCR is encoded by the V, D, and J gene segments and plays a critical role in antigen recognition. The length of CDR3 may be associated with the diversity of TCR.[32] In this study, the length of the CDR3 amino acid (AA) sequence for the T cell receptor α chain (TRA) ranged from 9 to 21 in CSF and from 7 to 20 in PB. Similarly, the length of the T cell receptor β chain (TRB) CDR3 AA sequence ranged from 7 to 21 in CSF and from 9 to 21 in PB (Fig. 6B). Statistical analysis showed no significant differences in the CDR3 AA length of either TRA or TRB between CSF and PB.

This study investigated the preferential usage of V and J genes and V–J pairings for both the TRA and TRB. In the CSF, the most frequently used TRA V and J genes were V13-1 and J49, respectively, while in PB, the predominant TRA V and J genes were V9-2 and J49. For TRB, the most commonly used V and J genes in both CSF and PB were V20-1 and J2-7, respectively. These findings indicate that CSF and PB exhibit nearly identical V and J gene usage patterns (Fig. 6C, D). Furthermore, the most prevalent V–J pairings for TRA and TRB were TRAV1-2-J33 and TRBV20-1-J2-7 in both CSF and PB. The findings showed the common usage and pairing patterns of TCR genes in CSF and PB in these 2 patients. More cases are needed to confirm the conservation of TCR gene usage patterns.

4. Discussion

This descriptive study provides a high-resolution view of T cell transcriptomes and TCR repertoires in CSF and PB from 2 patients with SAH. The following discussion interprets the observed patterns without claiming mechanistic insights. The role of T cell-mediated adaptive immunity in neuroinflammation following SAH remains unclear. We conducted an integrated analysis of scRNA-seq and TCR-seq to delineate the single-cell transcriptomic landscape of T cells, explore the heterogeneity of transcriptomes and phenotypes across T-cell subsets, and characterize the features of the TCR repertoire. Our observations revealed that T cells could be categorized into 5 distinct subsets: CD4+ T, naive CD4+ T, Treg, CD8+ T, and naive CD8+ T. These subsets exhibited marked differences in transcriptomic profiles, activation states, signaling pathways, and biological functions. Differentially expressed gene patterns were found to regulate T-cell differentiation and biological function. Compared with healthy controls, T cells from SAH patients displayed remarkably enhanced signaling pathway activation and strengthened intercellular communication. Furthermore, CSF-derived T cells presented a higher degree of clonal expansion and more expanded clonotypes than PB-derived T cells in this study.

As key contributors to adaptive immunity, T cells play a pivotal role in neuroinflammation following stroke.[33] CD4+ T cells infiltrate the infarcted hemisphere and produce interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), subsequently inducing tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) in brain-infiltrating macrophages, exacerbating poststroke inflammation.[34] Similarly, the long-term infiltration of CD8+ T cells into the brain influences immune cell dynamics and functional outcomes after stroke.[35] Tregs have been shown to promote brain regeneration by enhancing microglial activity to repair damaged tissue following ischemic stroke.[36] The primary routes for T-cell infiltration into the brain include the blood-brain barrier, choroid plexus, and meninges.[37] T cells are also critically involved in the pathophysiology of neuroinflammation after SAH. CD4+ T cells exhibit distinct activation states in response to specific complications following SAH.[17] While CD4+ T cell levels increase over time, CD8 + T cell levels decrease after SAH.[13] CD8+ CD161+ lymphocytes have been implicated in neuroinflammation following SAH and have been identified in the walls of unruptured intracranial aneurysms.[16] Low-dose interleukin-2 (IL-2) administration has been shown to enhance the anti-inflammatory role of Tregs, thereby protecting neural tissue after SAH.[38] Advances in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) have provided new opportunities to explore the pathophysiology of SAH. For instance, scRNA-seq studies revealed that T cells gradually accumulate around meningeal lymphatic vessels, peaking at 72 hours after SAH.[39] Another scRNA-seq study identified that genes such as LTB and MAL are predominantly expressed in T cells from the CSF following SAH.[40] Beyond their role in SAH, T cells may also contribute to the rupture of intracranial aneurysms.[41,42]

In this study, we observed that the differentiation of T-cell subsets followed a stable developmental trajectory, transitioning from naive T cells to CD4+ T, CD8+ T, and Tregs. This process was regulated by differentially expressed gene modules, which may influence both differentiation and biological functions. For instance, genes associated with T-cell differentiation, such as CCR7 and SELL, exhibited low expression, while genes linked to functional specialization, such as S100A4, GZMK, and CLIC1, were highly expressed during differentiation. These findings align with previous studies demonstrating that T cells can differentiate into distinct subsets with diverse phenotypes in response to varying immune microenvironments across diseases.[43,44] Despite distinct transcriptomic and phenotypic variations across T-cell subsets, we detected extensive ligand-receptor communication networks in both CSF and PB compartments. The overall interaction strength between T-cell populations was notably elevated in CSF relative to PB, especially for crosstalk between CD8+ T cells and Tregs, as well as self-interactions among Tregs. Distinct cell-cell communication patterns between Treg and CD8+ T cells were observed when comparing CSF and PB compartments in the 2 patients, which reflects potential immunological crosstalk that requires follow-up functional validation. In addition, the quantity and intensity of intercellular communications were substantially greater in T cells derived from SAH patients than those from public healthy controls, reflecting more complicated interplays among T-cell subpopulations under SAH-related conditions. The divergent communication profiles between Tregs and CD8+ T cells observed in our cohort may imply potential immunological crosstalk associated with post-SAH immune responses; however, no causal regulatory relationship can be concluded from the present computational data. Prior research has reported associations between Treg abundance and alleviated neuroinflammation after SAH, which may offer preliminary reference for subsequent experimental verification.[17,38] Collectively, our descriptive data reveal more frequent and complex intercellular signaling among T-cell subsets in SAH, accompanied by distinct proliferative and differentiation signatures of T cells that provide candidate directions for future mechanistic research.

The role of T cells in neuroinflammation after SAH is not yet fully understood. In our study, we compared the transcriptomic characteristics and activation states of T-cell subsets between CSF and PB, as well as between SAH patients and HC. We observed that the biological functions of T-cell subsets are primarily associated with the regulation of gene expression, and numerous signaling pathways were upregulated in T-cell subsets from CSF, suggesting that T cells may undergo rapid differentiation in response to neuroinflammation. These findings align with previous studies demonstrating that CD4+ T-cell subsets exhibit distinct activation patterns following SAH, highlighting their potential as therapeutic targets.[13,15,17] Additionally, compared to HC, functional terms such as ubiquitin and ubiquitin-like protein ligase binding and cadherin binding were enriched in T-cell subsets from SAH. Similarly, signaling pathways, including Aminoacyl tRNA Biosynthesis, Spliceosome, and TLR, were also upregulated in SAH. The TLR pathway has received considerable focus due to its established role in neuroinflammation, yet its association with T cells remains inadequately explored.[6] Current research has predominantly focused on innate immune cells, with limited emphasis on T cells. While previous studies have described T cells’ transcriptomic profiles and activation patterns, the mechanisms by which T cells mediate neuroinflammation remain poorly understood.[17,40] Our observations show altered molecular features of T cells in SAH. Nevertheless, much about their biological characteristics in SAH is still unknown. Fortunately, recent advancements in scRNA-seq and bioinformatics analysis, including spatial transcriptome sequencing, offer powerful tools for further investigating the role of T cells in SAH.

The TCR is primarily composed of α and β protein chains and plays an essential role in initiating adaptive immunity via antigen recognition.[45] Characterizing the TCR repertoire is scientifically important because it enhances our understanding of T-cell roles in inflammatory responses across various diseases. The TCR repertoire has been proposed as a potential biomarker for diagnosing severe delayed cerebral ischemia[46] and as a predictor of prognosis after SAH.[47] However, unlike previous studies, our research did not focus on identifying biomarkers or therapeutic targets but aimed to characterize the TCR repertoire. Although previous research has reported that the CDR3 AA length is shorter in good-grade SAH compared to poor-grade SAH,[47] we found no significant difference in the CDR3 AA length of TRA or TRB between CSF and PB. This discrepancy may be attributed to our samples being derived from different anatomical sites (CSF and PB) within the same patient rather than from different SAH patients. The top V–J pairs identified in our study differed from those reported in previous research,[46,47] underscoring the high diversity of V–J pair usage. We also observed that CSF and PB shared their top TRA and TRB V–J pairs, suggesting a conserved V and J gene usage pattern. Based on previous studies and our findings, we highlight the potential clinical significance of the TCR repertoire in SAH, especially in predicting disease outcomes and assessing severity.

This study has 3 major inherent limitations that must be fully acknowledged. First, the sample size is extremely small, comprising only 2 male patients with SAH. All transcriptomic and TCR repertoire signatures identified herein may represent individual-specific traits or stochastic variation, and the dataset is insufficient to support robust population-wide generalizations. Second, samples were collected exclusively at a single cross-sectional time point (day 7 post-SAH onset). This design prevents us from discriminating whether the detected molecular shifts in T cells act as a primary trigger or a downstream byproduct of post-hemorrhage neuroinflammation; accordingly, no causal links can be established. Third, all findings are derived purely from bioinformatic computational modeling, such as pathway enrichment, cell-cell communication network prediction, and pseudotime trajectory inference. No complementary wet-lab functional assays, including flow cytometry, in vitro cell culture, or in vivo animal models, were conducted to validate our observational outputs. As such, all interpretations generated in this work merely serve as preliminary hypotheses and cannot confirm definitive biological mechanisms.

5. Conclusions

This descriptive case-based study provides a detailed single-cell transcriptomic and TCR repertoire atlas of T cells in 2 SAH patients. The observed differences between CSF and PB, as well as between SAH patients and healthy controls, highlight T cell-mediated neuroinflammatory features and offer valuable clues for future mechanistic investigations. No causal conclusions are drawn in this study.
