The gut microbiota-bone marrow axis: an emerging role in chemotherapy-induced myelosuppression
Authors: Li Gong, Yeqi Wang, Yongsheng Li, Junli Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accumulating evidence indicates that gut microbiota extensively modulates the toxicity of chemotherapeutics. However, the role of microbiota in chemotherapy-induced myelosuppression (CIM) remains poorly characterized. This review synthesizes the pivotal role of gut microbiota in regulating hematopoiesis, summarizes recent findings on the links between gut microbiota and CIM, and discusses emerging strategies for alleviating CIM through microbiota modulation. Our analysis pinpoints key gaps and novel insights for targeted CIM therapies.

Introduction

The gut microbiota represents the largest and most complex microbial ecosystem in humans, comprising diverse microorganisms that collectively function as a vital ‘organ’ within the host1. Emerging studies have revealed that the gut microbiota plays a crucial role in providing nutrients, protecting epithelial cells, metabolizing substances such as drugs and toxins, and regulating the host’s immune response2. Furthermore, it significantly contributes to carcinogenesis by promoting tumor progression and shaping anti-cancer therapeutic responses through various mechanisms3–5. For example, the gut microbiota can stimulate anti-tumor immune responses by modulating CD8+ T cells, T helper 1 cells, and tumor-associated myeloid cells6; it can also influence anti-cancer targeted therapy by regulating the signaling pathways of the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS)7.

Chemotherapy is a cornerstone of anticancer treatment. Recent evidence has demonstrated that chemotherapy can significantly alter the composition of the microbiota, characterized by a marked reduction in Gram-positive bacteria (Firmicutes) and a concomitant enrichment of Gram-negative phyla (Proteobacteria and Bacteroidetes)8,9. Gut microbiota could regulate both the efficacy and toxicity of chemotherapy. For instance, butyrate—a key microbial metabolite—enhances antitumor immunity by potentiating CD8+ T cell responses through ID2-dependent interleukin-12 (IL-12) signaling, thereby improving chemotherapeutic outcomes10. β-glucuronidase-producing bacteria metabolize the inactive irinotecan prodrug into its active form within the intestinal lumen, exacerbating dose-limiting toxicities such as severe diarrhea11. Probiotic administration shows promise in modulating chemotherapy-induced toxicity. For instance, Lactobacillus spp. probiotics have been found to mitigate irinotecan-related diarrhea12, while probiotics containing Lactobacillus reuteri and Clostridium butyricum have demonstrated efficacy against cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity13. Additionally, a combination of Bacillus mesentericus, Clostridium butyricum, and Enterococcus faecalis has effectively mitigated intestinal and liver damage caused by oxaliplatin14. Furthermore, it has been proposed that the combination of antibiotics with gemcitabine could enhance chemosensitivity in pancreatic cancer patients15. Similarly, the microbiota-derived tryptophan metabolite indole-3-acetic acid (3-IAA) has been shown to enhance the efficacy of the FOLFIRINOX regimen (5-fluorouracil, irinotecan, and oxaliplatin) in pancreatic cancer16, while Fusobacterium nucleatum has been implicated in enhancing chemotherapy resistance in colorectal cancer17. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that gut microbiota and their metabolites are critical modulators of cancer treatment outcomes.

Chemotherapy-induced myelosuppression (CIM) represents the most prevalent toxicity associated with chemotherapy. However, the relationship between CIM and gut microbiota remains poorly understood. In this review, we systematically summarize the characteristics of gut microbiota alterations linked to CIM resulting from various chemotherapeutic agents, as well as the significant role of gut microbiota in the regulation of hematopoiesis. Furthermore, we review the key mechanistic pathways and recent findings regarding how gut microbiota interacts with CIM. We also summarize and discuss available strategies for managing CIM through gut microbiota modulation. This review offers novel insights into the relationship between CIM and gut microbiota in cancer patients, highlighting critical knowledge gaps and suggesting promising directions for future research.

Regulation of hematopoiesis by gut microbiota and microbial signals

Hematopoiesis is the process by which all blood cell lineages are generated from hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) in the bone marrow (BM)18. HSCs maintain hematopoietic homeostasis through self-renewal, ensuring the stability of the stem cell pool while also producing various multipotent progenitors (MPPs) and mature blood cell lineages via differentiation19. Emerging evidence suggests that gut microbiota and microbial signals are significant factors that play a fundamental role in hematopoiesis. In this context, we focus on elucidating how gut microbiota, microbial metabolites, and microbe-associated molecular patterns regulate the progression of hematopoiesis. Gut microbiota and metabolites

Gut microbiota and its metabolites can directly and indirectly regulate hematopoiesis in the BM. Numerous studies have provided critical insights into this regulatory network. In a multi-center clinical study, Fernandez-Sanchez et al. found that the depletion of Lachnospiraceae and a decrease in Lachnospiraceae-derived metabolites, such as citrulline, 4-ureidobutyrate, 2′-deoxyuridine, and N-acetylglucosamine were associated with antibiotic-induced neutropenia, thereby demonstrating that these metabolites are essential for maintaining steady-state hematopoiesis20. Further studies in mouse models have elucidated the underlying mechanisms: antibiotic-induced alterations in gut microbiota disrupt the homeostasis of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs)21,22, while microbially derived metabolites, such as phosphatidylcholine and γ-glutamylalanine, have the potential to contribute to the maintenance of hematopoiesis and ameliorate antibiotic-induced hematopoietic impairments23. Mechanistically, these metabolites activate type I interferon (IFN) signaling, which induces STAT1 activation in non-myeloid hematopoietic cells, thereby supporting hematopoiesis23. In addition, the gut microbiota modulates hematopoiesis through another mechanism. Using a microbiota-depleted mouse model, Zhang et al. demonstrated that gut microbiota influences red blood cell (RBC) recycling by BM macrophages24. Specifically, the microbial metabolite butyrate, a short-chain fatty acid (SCFA), acts as a signaling molecule that regulates the erythrophagocytosis of BM macrophages by enhancing global histone H3 acetylation (H3ac) levels, thereby increasing the expression of RBC recycling genes such as Lrp1, Hmox1 and Mertk24. This process facilitates rapid iron availability in the BM and enhances HSC self-renewal.

Butyrate has also been shown to regulate neutrophil production and modulate their functions25. Dang et al. demonstrated that butyrate directly influences neutrophil maturation in the BM, leading to the accumulation of immature neutrophils in preclinical models26. Mechanistically, butyrate modifies the transcriptional landscape of both immature and mature BM neutrophils, significantly reducing transcript levels of Camp, Ltf, and Ngp that are essential for neutrophil degranulation and the activities of neutrophil granule enzymes26. Beyond their role in neutrophil function, butyrate also enhances the expression of erythroid-related genes. IKUTA et al. showed that butyrate serves as a promising inducer of γ-globin, stimulating both γ-globin mRNA and protein expression in patients with beta globin disorders, which offers a potential therapeutic strategy to enhance hematopoietic cell survival and proliferation27. In addition to its direct effects on myeloid and erythroid lineages, butyrate and other SCFAs can modulate the immunological functions of dendritic cells, regulatory T cells (Tregs), and Th-related hematopoiesis in the BM28. Tregs are critical for maintaining HSC homeostasis29. Using an antibiotic-treated mouse model, Han et al. found that the gut microbiota can enhance the development of Tregs, which subsequently supports hematopoietic homeostasis30.

Lee et al. investigated the role of another gut microbial metabolite, lactate, in hematopoiesis by utilizing Gpr81-deficient (Gpr81 − /−) mice31. Their results showed that lactate stimulates the secretion of stem cell factor (SCF) from leptin receptor-expressing (LepR+) mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) in BM, thereby activating both hematopoiesis and erythropoiesis31. The differentiation of lymphoid, myeloid, and erythroid lineages, as well as the proliferation of HSCs, is influenced by SCF expression from LepR+ MSCs32. Mechanistically, lactate enters the systemic circulation, reaches the BM, and activates SCF expression through lactate-Gpr81 signaling31. Functional studies further support this pathway: oral administration of lactate or lactate-producing bacteria, such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus spp., enhances erythropoiesis and hematopoiesis in mouse BM and promotes the self-renewal of HSCs31.

In addition to its role in SCF-mediated regulation, lactate also contributes to the mobilization of HSPCs33. Using preclinical murine models, Ordonez-Moreno et al. found that lactate plays a critical role in granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF)-induced mobilization of HSPCs33. G-CSF stimulates the proliferation, differentiation, and activation of neutrophils, leading to the generation of highly glycolytic cells that actively produce and secrete lactate into the extracellular space34,35. The uptake of extracellular lactate is crucial for the efficient mobilization of HSPCs33. Mechanistically, lactate disrupts the CXCR4/CXCL12 axis by upregulating CXCR4 expression and enhancing cellular proliferation, while concurrently reducing extracellular CXCL12 levels, thereby facilitating the mobilization of HSPC33. Despite these advances, the roles of other metabolites, including acetic acid, crotonic acid, caproic acid, valeric acid, and propionic acid, in hematopoiesis remain unclear.

Microbe-associated molecular patterns

Lipopolysaccharides (LPS) are the primary components of the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria, including Escherichia coli, Salmonellae, and other members of the Enterobacteriaceae family36. Using in vitro co-cultures of LPS-stimulated human bone marrow stromal cells (BMSCs), Ziegler et al. demonstrated that acute LPS exposure stimulates human BMSCs, thereby promoting the development of myeloid cells and the maintenance of progenitor cells37. BMSCs, as essential components of the bone marrow niche, express Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and can respond to TLR4 agonists, such as LPS37. Boettcher et al. found that acute LPS challenge induces the production of G-CSF and GM-CSF, significantly enhancing the secretion of M-CSF, IL-6, and IL-11 in vitro38. Following LPS-TLR4 signaling, full activation of HSCs in vivo requires the coordinated action of multiple proinflammatory cytokines, including IFN-α, IFN-γ, tumor necrosis factor-α, IL-1α, IL-1β, and others39.

However, chronic LPS stimulation adversely affects the division and differentiation potential of primitive hematopoietic cells, impairing the self-renewal capacity and functional integrity of HSC40. Using a humanized mouse model in which mice were engrafted with human CD34⁺ cord blood cells, Liu et al. demonstrated that persistent LPS exposure increases the frequency of Ki-67+ HSCs while severely depleting common lymphoid progenitors and B cell precursors41. Mechanistically, Takizawa et al. further reported that prolonged exposure to LPS ultimately impairs the self-renewal and competitive repopulation capabilities of HSCs via the TLR4-TRIF-ROS-p38 pathway42.

In a mechanism distinct from that of LPS in regulating hematopoiesis, NOD1, an intracellular sensor for both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, exerts broad effects on multiple progenitors and specifically targets bone marrow mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) as cytokine-producing intermediates43. In vitro, NOD1 ligand stimulated MSCs to produce hematopoietic cytokines, including interleukin-7, Flt3L, SCF, ThPO, and interleukin-6. In vivo, it restored bone marrow HSPC counts and serum cytokine levels in germ-free mice to control levels43. NOD1 signaling in MSCs acts as a critical pathway that mediates the microbiota-dependent regulation of steady-state hematopoiesis44. Oral administration of a commensal bacterial product, a NOD1 ligand, has been shown to rescue antibiotic-induced hematopoietic defects in mice23.

In general, the gut microbiome, microbial metabolites, and microbe-associated molecular patterns play a pivotal role in modulating the progression of hematopoiesis. The microbial metabolites butyrate and lactate, along with the microbe-associated molecular patterns LPS and NOD1, serve as primary regulators. However, despite the well-studied effects of microbial signals, the direct role of the gut microbiota in regulating hematopoiesis remains poorly understood.

Anemia, along with decreased neutrophil and platelet counts, is a common hematologic toxicity induced by myelosuppressive chemotherapy. In this section, we will discuss the direct and indirect relationships between these toxicities and gut microbiota. While the direct role of gut microbiota in CIM has yet to be fully elucidated, existing findings point to a potential connection. Chemotherapy-induced anemia

Nearly 90% of patients develop chemotherapy-induced anemia (CIA) during chemotherapy treatment45. In a prospective cohort study of 81 breast cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy, Hsu et al. investigated the alterations in gut microbiota between patients experiencing mild CIA (grades 0 and 1) and those with severe CIA (grades 2 and 3)46. The findings indicated that, at the family level, Eubacteriaceae and Enterobacteriaceae were significantly enriched in cases of severe anemia, while at the genus level, Anaerofilum and Eubacterium showed the strongest associations with this condition46. These microbiota are characterized as pro-inflammatory microorganisms. Their baseline presence may render individuals susceptible to cytokine-mediated anemia through two interconnected mechanisms: the suppression of erythropoiesis by IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-8, and functional iron deficiency resulting from iron sequestration in macrophages and enterocytes46,47. In a prospective cohort study involving 329 patients with locally advanced rectal cancer who received neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, researchers explored the potential role of gut microbiota in mediating sex differences in hematologic toxicities, including anemia, neutropenia, and thrombocytopenia48. They found that altered gut microbial composition and metabolites correlated with these hematologic toxicities48. Furthermore, they identified that sex-biased bacterial species (Parasutterella excrementihominis and Bifidobacterium adolescentis) and microbiota-mediated arginine metabolism were associated with hematologic toxicities48. Specifically, higher levels of Parasutterella excrementihominis, Parabacteroides johnsonii, fecal L-arginine, and Gly-Arg were observed in female patients, which predicted a higher risk of CIA48.

The pathogenesis of CIA is multifactorial, encompassing: (1) the direct myelosuppressive effects of chemotherapy, (2) functional iron deficiency, and (3) impaired bone marrow response to erythropoietin (EPO)49,50. Iron is an essential substrate for erythropoiesis. Insufficient dietary iron intake, along with reduced iron bioavailability, is the leading cause of the high prevalence of iron deficiency anemia51. In the intestinal lumen, ferric iron (Fe3+) is converted to ferrous iron (Fe2+) by duodenal cytochrome b (DCYTB)52; subsequently, hypoxia-inducible transcription factors-2α (HIF-2α) directly targets three crucial intestinal iron transporters—divalent metal transporter 1 (DMT1), DCYTB, and ferroportin—to facilitate the uptake of Fe2+ into enterocytes53. Gut microbiota and metabolites can regulate iron absorption. Bouglé et al. reported that Propionibacteria and their fermentation products, namely acetic and propionic acids, enhance iron absorption from the proximal colon of rats54. In contrast, Das and colleagues discovered that the gut microbiota metabolites 1,3-diaminopropane (DAP) and reuterin inhibit the activity of HIF-2α, leading to decreased intestinal iron absorption in mice55. Therefore, maintaining a balanced intestinal microbiota is crucial for systemic iron homeostasis56.

Human EPO is a crucial factor for the viability and proliferation of erythrocytic progenitors, primarily produced by the kidneys and regulated by HIFs57. Chemotherapy can induce nephrotoxicity and renal insufficiency, which may lead to decreased EPO production and renal anemia58. Recent studies indicate that gut microbiota may influence renal anemia through the regulation of HIF signaling pathways59. Chiang and colleagues found that indoxyl sulfate, a toxin derived from gut microbiota, inhibits hypoxia-induced HIF activation, subsequently reducing EPO production in mice60. Mechanistically, indoxyl sulfate significantly activates the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), which interferes with the formation of the HIF-AhR nuclear translocator (ARNT) complex and enhances the interaction between AhR and ARNT in the nucleus61. Additionally, indoxyl sulfate disrupts the function of transcriptional coactivators, thereby inhibiting the hypoxic induction of HIF target genes62.

In summary, these findings point to a high abundance of pro-inflammatory microbiota as a risk factor for CIA. Mechanistically, gut microbiota may contribute to the development of CIA by modulating HIF signaling and impairing iron absorption. Future research should explore whether specific microbial metabolites can directly regulate the proliferation and differentiation of erythroid progenitor cells, as this question warrants further investigation.

Chemotherapy-induced neutropenia

Chemotherapy-induced neutropenia (CIN) is a common and potentially life-threatening complication of myelosuppressive chemotherapy regimens63. Hsu Jiun-Wei and colleagues reported a marked depletion of both the Eubacterium hallii group (at the genus level) and Intestinimonas butyriciproducens (at the species level) in cases of severe neutropenia (grade 4)46. Eubacterium hallii is known to be a butyrate-producing bacterium. These results indicate that altered SCFA metabolism may contribute to the severity of CIN46. Nguyen et al. analyzed the relationship between gut microbiota and CIN in breast cancer and reported that elevated prechemotherapy gut microbiota α-diversity is inversely associated with the risk of CIN64. Specifically, a higher baseline abundance of the Synergistota phylum correlates with decreased susceptibility to severe neutropenia, while an increased abundance of the Firmicutes phylum is positively associated with the risk of severe neutropenia64. Sui et al. identified that Parasutterella excrementihominis, Eggerthella lenta and the activity of gut microbial dimethylarginine dimethylaminohydrolase were enriched in female patients with CIN, correlating with an increased risk of CIN48.

Gut microbiota signatures also serve as predictive biomarkers for febrile neutropenia (FN). In a prospective cohort study of children with acute lymphoblastic leukemia, Hakim et al. reported that patients who developed FN had significantly higher baseline levels of Proteobacteria, thereby identifying this phylum as an independent predictor of FN during chemotherapy65. Patients exhibiting dominance of Enterococcaceae or Streptococcaceae also showed a strong trend toward predicting FN induced by chemotherapy65. In another prospective cohort study, Yoon et al. similarly demonstrated that Enterobacteriaceae were significantly enriched in patients who developed FN after chemotherapy, with their abundance correlating with the occurrence of FN66. A parent randomized controlled trial revealed that among patients with esophageal cancer, those who developed FN following neoadjuvant chemotherapy exhibited significantly lower abundance of Anaerostipes hadrus compared to those who did not, both before and after therapy67. The reduced baseline abundance of Anaerostipes hadrus was significantly associated with an increased risk of FN, indicating its potential as a predictive biomarker for chemotherapy-induced FN67. Yuang et al. demonstrated that elevated prechemotherapy Enterococcus abundance is associated with shorter neutropenia duration (≤7 days) and could serve as a predictive biomarker for CIN duration in leukemia patients68. However, Enterococcus overgrowth is associated with delayed neutrophil recovery in childhood acute lymphoblastic leukemia69.

The neutrophil production and function are regulated by intestinal microbiota. In a mouse model of 5-fluorouracil (5-FU)-induced neutropenia, Chen et al. observed that prolonged neutropenia following 5-FU administration led to significant alterations in the composition of the gut microbiota70. These changes were characterized by a marked increase in the Ruminococcaceae family and an uncultured genus-level group within this family, indicating that extended neutropenia promotes the expansion of specific bacterial taxa at both the family and genus levels70. In addition, the authors observed that delayed neutrophil recovery was associated with elevated plasma concentrations of IL-17A and G-CSF70. Deshmukh et al. demonstrated that microbiota-derived signals promote the production of IL-17A by group 3 innate lymphoid cells (ILC3s) through a mechanism that is dependent on TLR4 and myeloid differentiation factor 88 (MyD88)71. This process results in increased levels of plasma G-CSF and enhances neutrophil populations in both circulation and BM of mice71. Additionally, Zhang et al. reported that neutrophil aging is also driven by the microbiota via TLR4- and MyD88-mediated signaling pathways, and the depletion of gut microbiota in mice significantly reduces the population of aged neutrophils in circulation72.

Collectively, these findings indicate a strong association between gut microbiota and CIN, highlighting its potential as a predictive biomarker. Additionally, the results reinforce the concept that an imbalance in gut microbiota may contribute to the prolongation of neutropenia and the exacerbation of CIN severity. Nevertheless, despite the identification of numerous potential predictive markers, there remains a notable absence of markers that exhibit high specificity and strong representativeness, an aspect that warrants further investigation. Moreover, utilizing gut microbiota and microbial signals as non-invasive biomarkers introduces a novel paradigm for the dynamic monitoring of bone marrow suppression in clinical settings.

Chemotherapy-induced thrombocytopenia

Chemotherapy-induced thrombocytopenia (CIT) can lead to bleeding complications and other adverse oncologic outcomes73. In a retrospective cross-sectional study, Yoon et al. investigated the relationship between gut microbiota and platelet count by analyzing fecal 16S RNA gene sequencing data from 1,268 subjects74. Their findings indicated that an increased platelet count was associated with reduced gut microbiota diversity and lower abundances of Faecalibacterium, including the beneficial species Faecalibacterium prausnitzii. This suggests that elevated platelet levels may adversely affect gut microbial diversity and composition74. Hsu et al. conducted a comparative analysis of the gut microbiota in patients with grade 1 and grade 2 CIT against those without CIT (grade 0)46. They found that patients with CIT exhibited a significant depletion of Eubacterium limosum, showing a 53-fold reduction (d = −1.60)46. Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) analysis identified the genus Eubacterium and the species Eubacterium limosum as significant biomarkers associated with CIT46.

Although research on the topic of CIT and gut microbiota is limited, there is a close association between gut microbiota and immune thrombocytopenia (ITP)75. It is well established that chemotherapy regimens can induce ITP75. Significant alterations in gut microbiome and metabolome profiles have been strongly correlated with platelet counts in adults with primary ITP76. Zhang et al. identified that gut bacteria such as Blautia, Streptococcus, and Lactobacillus are enriched in ITP patients, while Bacteroides are depleted76. Furthermore, combinations of Weissella and Streptococcus anginosus, along with 13-hydroxyoctadecanoic acid, could serve as promising diagnostic biomarkers for ITP76. Rui et al. demonstrated that Helicobacter abundance was significantly elevated in ITP patients compared to healthy controls, suggesting it may also serve as a potential diagnostic biomarker for ITP77. Liu et al. conducted a prospective clinical study that revealed an increased proportion of Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, and a higher Bacteroidetes/Firmicutes ratio in patients with ITP compared to healthy individuals78. Conversely, the proportion of Firmicutes was found to be decreased in ITP patients. Additionally, Anaerorhabdus, Sutterella, Peptostreptococcaceae, Clostridium XI, and Carnobacteriaceae have been identified as potential biomarkers for ITP78.

These findings indicate that various bacteria are associated with CIT and ITP, suggesting that these microorganisms may significantly contribute to the development of thrombocytopenia. Further investigation is necessary to determine whether specific microbial populations and their signals can directly influence thrombopoiesis, as well as to elucidate the mechanisms involved in this process.

Alleviating CIM via Gut microbiota modulation

Accumulating evidence has revealed a close relationship between gut microbiota and CIM, suggesting that therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating gut microbiota hold significant clinical promise for alleviating CIM. In this section, we discuss recent advances in the treatment and prevention of CIM through the modulation of gut microbiota. The strategies for microbiota modulation covered include probiotics, synbiotics, traditional Chinese medicine, and lactoferrin.

Probiotics

Probiotics are defined as “live microorganisms that, when administered in adequate amounts, confer health benefits to the host.” Upon consumption, probiotics can positively modulate gut microbiota composition and produce critical bioactive metabolites, notably SCFAs, which play an instrumental role in regulating inflammation, enhancing immune function, maintaining the intestinal barrier, and modulating hematopoietic processes79. One preclinical investigation revealed that administering a probiotics blend to gemcitabine-treated pancreatic cancer mice increased gut microbiota richness and composition, notably increasing the abundance of butyrate- and other SCFA-producing bacteria, including Eubacteriaceae, Ruthenibacterium, Faecalicatena, Pseudobutyrivibrio, and Roseburia80. Importantly, probiotic supplementation counteracted gemcitabine-induced hematological toxicity, restoring platelet counts and red blood cell levels80. A randomized controlled trial evaluating Bacteroides fragilis 839 (BF839), a next-generation probiotic, indicated that its administration significantly reduced the incidence of CIM in breast cancer patients compared to the placebo group81. Specifically, patients receiving BF839 exhibited smaller reductions from baseline in both white blood cell and neutrophil counts81. Probiotic lactic acid bacteria (LAB), including viable and non-viable Lactobacillus rhamnosus CRL1505 and Lactobacillus plantarum CRL1506 (referred to as Lr05 and Lr05NV, respectively), also demonstrated a promising effect in minimizing CIM in mice82. Andrés Gramajo Lopez and colleagues reported that treatments with Lr05NV and Lr05V promoted the early recovery of long-term hematopoietic stem cells (LSK), multipotent progenitors, and myeloid cells compared to the untreated cyclophosphamide control group82. Mechanistically, these treatments facilitated hematopoietic recovery and alleviated CIM by increasing serum IL-17A levels and GM-CSF expression while reducing CXCL12 levels in the bone marrow, thereby activating the IL-17A/GM-CSF axis to enhance the production of myeloid precursors82.

Synbiotics

Synbiotics are defined as a mixture of live microorganisms and substrates that are selectively utilized by host microorganisms, which confer health benefits to the host. This definition highlights the potential of synbiotics to promote human health and provide therapeutic advantages83. Motoori et al. found that administering synbiotics during neoadjuvant chemotherapy in esophageal cancer patients significantly reduced the frequency of severe lymphopenia and FN84. The levels of Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, and SCFAs such as acetate and propionate were significantly higher in the synbiotics group84. In a multicenter randomized study, the researchers demonstrated that combining synbiotics with enteral nutrition (EN) significantly decreased the incidence of grade 4 neutropenia while enhancing chemotherapy tolerability85. The abundance of Bifidobacterium, Lacticaseibacillus, and Enterobacteriaceae, along with acetate concentration, was significantly increased following the administration of synbiotics combined with EN85. The occurrence of FN was significantly associated with higher levels of Clostridioides difficile and inversely correlated with acetate levels post-chemotherapy85. Sugimoto and colleagues reported that their synbiotics, which include Lacticaseibacillus paracasei strain Shirota, Bifidobacterium breve strain Yakult, and galacto-oligosaccharides (LBG), combined with EN, help mitigate FN in patients with esophageal cancer undergoing neoadjuvant chemotherapy. The inhibition of bacterial translocation, resulting from the recovery of Anaerostipes hadrus and the enhancement of intestinal butyrate, may contribute to the suppression of FN by the combination of LBG and EN67.

Traditional Chinese medicinal

Traditional Chinese medicine represents a comprehensive healthcare system grounded in centuries of clinical experience. This system encompasses various therapeutic practices, including Chinese herbal medicine, dietetics, moxibustion, acupuncture, and other non-pharmacological therapies86. In a recent study, Xie et al. developed Epimedium-derived nanovesicles (ENVs) from the traditional Chinese medicinal herb Epimedium87. They demonstrated that both preventive and therapeutic administration of ENVs effectively ameliorated myelosuppression in a cyclophosphamide-induced mouse model. Mechanistically, ENVs operate through a gut-lactate-bone marrow axis, enhancing hematopoiesis and promoting the regeneration of HSCs. Specifically, ENVs facilitated the expansion of Lactobacillus spp. and increased lactate production. This microbiota-driven lactate signaling subsequently activated LepR+ MSCs in the bone marrow niche, leading to an increased secretion of stromal cell-derived factor-1 and SCF, thereby supporting HSC expansion and mediating hematopoietic recovery87. Using CIT murine models, Li et al. demonstrated that Si Xian Formula (SXF) alleviated carboplatin-induced damage to the BM microenvironment and facilitated recovery from thrombocytopenia88. These effects were mediated through the regulation of gut microbiota and BM metabolites88. Specifically, SXF treatment reduced Paraprevotella and Bacteroides, while enriching Lachnospiraceae_NK4A13688. Spearman analysis further showed that gut microbiota such as Parabacteroides, Prevotellaceae_UCG-001, and Bacillus were positively correlated with upregulated metabolites, and negatively correlated with downregulated metabolites in the BM88. Collectively, these findings suggest a dual mechanism by which SXF modulates gut microbiota and reprograms BM metabolic networks to alleviate CIT. Additionally, Hu et al. found that moxibustion improved bone marrow histology, restored hematopoietic cell function, and increased peripheral blood cell counts and hemoglobin concentrations in a mouse model of myelosuppression-induced aplastic anemia89. The underlying mechanism may involve the downregulation of Faecalibaculum abundance to maintain gut microbial stability, as well as the promotion of SCFAs-mediated immunomodulation89.

Lactoferrin

Findings from a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial indicated that oral lactoferrin supplementation significantly reduced the occurrence of FN in pediatric cancer patients undergoing induction chemotherapy90. Lactoferrin supplementation appears to promote gut microbiota homeostasis by maintaining microbial diversity and preventing the overgrowth of pathobionts90. These effects are characterized by an increased relative abundance of the Streptococcaceae and Veillonellaceae families, alongside decreased levels of Verrucomicrobiaceae, Ruminococcaceae, and Akkermansia90. From the results of another prospective cohort study, Rashidi et al. discovered that Akkermansia abundance predicts a higher risk for FN in acute leukemia patients during chemotherapy91.

Collectively, evidence from both preclinical and clinical studies indicates that probiotics, synbiotics, Chinese herbal medicine, moxibustion, and lactoferrin have therapeutic or preventive effects that reduce both the severity and incidence of CIM. Regarding clinical phenotypes, these therapeutic strategies have been shown to ameliorate CIN, CIA, and CIT. However, strategies specifically targeting the gut microbiota to improve CIA and CIT remain relatively limited, likely due to a poor understanding of the relationship between the gut microbiota and these conditions. Given the pronounced heterogeneity and interindividual variability of the gut microbiota among patients, this presents a significant challenge for the clinical translation of therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating the gut microbiota. In the future, the application of novel bioengineering technologies and personalized treatment strategies holds promise for enhancing the clinical translation of microbiota-modulating therapies.

Conclusion and future perspectives

CIM represents a significant risk factor that can lead to treatment delays, increased mortality risk, and reduced efficacy of chemotherapy. Growing evidence demonstrates that gut microbiota and microbial signals play a crucial role in host hematopoietic function, including the maintenance of HSPC homeostasis and the stimulation of BMSC activity. Under the stress of chemotherapy, both the intestinal microbiome and medullary hematopoiesis experience considerable impairment. Dysregulated gut microbiota is linked to disordered medullary hematopoiesis and may serve as a biomarker for predicting CIM. Mechanistically, the intestinal microbiome and its metabolites modulate CIM by regulating iron absorption and metabolism, HIF signaling pathways, TLR4/MyD88-dependent signaling pathways, and alterations in specific microbial abundances.

Microbiota modulation strategies, including probiotics, synbiotics, traditional Chinese medicine, and lactoferrin, have been employed to alleviate the severity and incidence of CIM in both clinical and preclinical settings. The therapeutic effects of these interventions are primarily mediated by metabolites, particularly SCFAs. The application of bioengineering approaches, such as natural nanovesicles, to modulate SCFA-producing gut microbiota represents a promising and innovative strategy. However, these interventions are still far from clinical application. Currently, the primary measures for treating and preventing CIM in clinical practice include G-CSF, GM-CSF, erythropoiesis-stimulating agents, and thrombopoietin receptor agonists. Strategies aimed at targeting the gut microbiota for CIM management require extensive clinical studies involving broadly representative cohorts to establish their safety and efficacy. Ultimately, addressing the heterogeneity and interindividual variability of the gut microbiota, as well as elucidating the underlying mechanisms linking gut microbiota to CIM, are essential for advancing the development and clinical translation of novel therapeutic approaches.

In recent years, emerging evidence has demonstrated that intratumoral bacteria can modulate the tumor’s response to chemotherapy. For example, Fusobacterium nucleatum has been shown to activate autophagy through the TLR-4/MyD88 signaling pathway, resulting in the selective downregulation of miR-18a and miR-4802, which confers resistance to 5-FU92. Additionally, intratumoral Gamma-Proteobacteria contribute to chemoresistance in pancreatic cancer by enzymatically converting gemcitabine into its inactive form via the bacterial enzyme cytidine deaminase93. However, the potential role of intratumoral bacteria in chemotherapy-related toxicity remains unexplored. Establishing this relationship is challenging due to their anatomical separation and diverse physiological functions. Microbiota can colonize tumor tissues through mucosal disruption, migration from adjacent tissues, and hematogenic invasion, acting as critical modulators within the tumor microenvironment94. Robles-Vera et al. discovered that gut barrier disruption induces the translocation of Enterococcus faecalis to the BM following dextran sulfate sodium treatment. The C-type lectin receptor Mincle (Clec4e) senses Enterococcus faecalis, mediating the trained immunity of BM progenitors95. This study provides direct evidence that the disruption of the intestinal barrier facilitates the translocation of gut bacteria to the BM.

Chemotherapy induces dysbiosis of the gut microbiota and compromises the intestinal barrier, resulting in increased intestinal permeability. This condition facilitates the translocation of luminal bacteria and their byproducts into the mucosa and systemic circulation96. Previous studies have primarily concentrated on the relationship between gut microbial byproducts and hematopoietic function or CIM, as well as the compositional shifts in gut microbiota associated with CIM. However, the direct influence of gut microbiota on hematopoiesis remains an underexplored area. To date, no study has directly demonstrated whether chemotherapy promotes bacterial translocation to the bone marrow. Nevertheless, the evidence discussed above provides strong indirect support for this possibility, raising several critical questions for future investigation: (1) Does chemotherapy induce the translocation of gut bacteria to the bone marrow? (2) What is the relationship between ectopic bone marrow microbiota and the development and progression of CIM, and how do these bacteria affect hematopoietic function? Addressing these questions is of great clinical significance for advancing our understanding of the gut microbiota–bone marrow axis.
