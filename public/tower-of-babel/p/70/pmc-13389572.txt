Endosomal TPC1–mediated Ca2+ nanodomains regulate transferrin receptor trafficking and iron homeostasis
Authors: William J. Burton, Alice Lin, Anthony J. Morgan, Lianne C. Davis, Cheng-Chang Chen, Antony Galione
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceHow the ubiquitous iron uptake via the endocytosis of transferrin receptors (TfRs) is regulated is unclear. Here, we reveal a paradigm whereby endosomes themselves generate their own “autocrine” local Ca2+ signals to drive TfR trafficking and iron homeostasis. Endosomes act as unique Ca2+ stores that are mobilized by Ca2+-permeable two-pore channel 1 (TPC1) which generates local Ca2+ domains. Other Ca2+ sources (and channels) cannot substitute for TPC1, attesting to its specificity. Such is its importance that TPC1 deletion reduces TfR trafficking and severely impairs intracellular iron homeostasis, causing anemic phenotypes. We suggest a critical and specific role for TPC1 for cell-surface membrane trafficking and iron homeostasis.

Membrane and vesicular trafficking, the process by which proteins, other macromolecules, and pathogens are transported and distributed throughout the cell, is a fundamental pathway maintaining cellular homeostasis and secretion (1, 2). Recently, endolysosomal channels, namely two-pore channels (TPCs) and transient receptor potential mucolipin channels (TRPMLs), have been identified as integral regulators of membrane trafficking pathways, including but not limited to the transport of pathogens (3, 4), toxins (5, 6), growth factor- and LDL-receptors (7–9). Acidic endolysosomal organelles, where TPC/TRPML channels reside, are Ca2+- and H+-rich stores, whose autonomous local Ca2+ domains couple to downstream pathways that other Ca2+ sources cannot, driving membrane-trafficking pathways selectively (4, 10). While the lysosomal TPC2 exhibits plastic ion-permeabilities (11, 12), there is uncertainty whether the endosomal TPC1 does the same (13, 14). TPC1 is Na+-permeant (15–17), activated either by the second messenger nicotinic acid adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NAADP) (13, 18, 19), or phosphoinositide phosphatidylinositol 3,5-bisphosphate (PI(3,5)P2) (15, 20, 21). TPC1 shows a lower, yet quantitatively uncertain, Ca2+ selectivity compared to TPC2 (14, 15, 22). Although trafficking between the plasma membrane, endolysosomes, and the trans-Golgi network is controlled by local Ca2+, the contribution of early endosomal Ca2+ and TPC1 is unclear.

The transferrin cycle is a constitutive homeostatic process which regulates the cellular uptake and transport of ferric iron (Fe3+) (23). Briefly, this involves the internalization of Fe3+-loaded transferrin upon binding to the transferrin receptor (TfR) at the plasma membrane, followed by endocytosis and trafficking through mildly acidic vesicles. The acidity of endosomes triggers Fe3+ dissociation and subsequent reduction to Fe2+. The apotransferrin–receptor complex is then recycled back to the cell surface for subsequent cycles of Fe3+ uptake (SI Appendix, Fig. S1) (24, 25). The transferrin cycle is a multiphasic pathway regulated by Ca2+ and endolysosomal proteins; clathrin-mediated endocytosis, endosomal trafficking, and exocytosis are all Ca2+-dependent. Local Ca2+ drives transferrin uptake and recycling, likely via Ca2+-dependent kinases such as CaMKK2 (26, 27), with the possibility that the TfR itself may evoke Ca2+ signals (28). Due to the prominence of TPC1 on TfR-positive endosomes (5, 29), we investigated the role of TPC1 within the transferrin cycle and which ionic fluxes are required.

Here, we establish endosomes as Ca2+ stores that drive transferrin/TfR trafficking. We show that phosphoinositide-induced TPC1-mediated Ca2+ fluxes regulate the distribution of cell-surface and internalized TfR levels. A consequence of altered transferrin trafficking is reduced cytosolic iron, resulting in decreased heme synthesis and iron-deficiency anemia in cells and mice. Our data strongly indicate that TPC1 drives endogenous transferrin/iron trafficking through local endosomal Ca2+ nanodomains.

Results

TPC1 Drives the Transferrin Cycle.

To investigate the significance of TPC1 in the transferrin cycle, we monitored fluorescently labeled transferrin in wild-type (WT) and TPCN1 knockout cells. HeLa cells were chosen as an extensively studied model of TfR/transferrin trafficking (23, 30, 31). TPC-deficient HeLa cells were generated using CRISPR-Cas9, with TPC deletion verified by RT-PCR (32) (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). We first quantified transferrin uptake in WT and TPCN1 knockout HeLa cells and found it was slower and maximally lower in the TPC1-null cells (Fig. 1A). A reduction in the number of transferrin/Rab5-positive early endosomes confirmed that this trafficking defect is associated with a reduced uptake and transport of transferrin (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). Similarly, deletion of TPC1 slowed the reverse process of transferrin recycling back to the cell surface because transferrin remaining intracellularly was increased in TPCN1 knockout compared to WT (Fig. 1B). To complement the genetic approach, inhibition of TPCs pharmacologically using tetrandrine [a nonselective TPC inhibitor (3, 33)] but not Ned-19 [an NAADP antagonist (34)], resulted in a similar transferrin trafficking defect (Fig. 1C). Therefore, this suggests that TPC1 deletion reduces the overall kinetics and efficiency of the transferrin cycle.

Next, we tested the specificity for TPC1 in the transferrin cycle, by comparing the role of other endolysosomal channels. Although deletion of lysosomal TPC2 did result in a ~27% reduction in transferrin uptake, deletion of TPC1 resulted in a greater ~45% trafficking defect (Fig. 1D). When TPC1 or TPC2 were reconstituted by transfection in either TPCN1 or TPCN2 knockout cells respectively, the transferrin-uptake defect was restored (Fig. 1D). The requirement of TPCs for transferrin internalization and trafficking was not peculiar to HeLa cells, because a trafficking defect was also observed in TPC1- and TPC2-deficient mouse embryonic fibroblast (MEF) cells (Fig. 1E). Transferrin trafficking was impaired further in Tpcn1/Tpcn2−/− double-knockout MEF cells, suggesting that both TPC1 and TPC2 are required for the efficient trafficking of the transferrin–TfR complex (Fig. 1E). We tested whether other types of channels could rescue the TPC1 defect by overexpressing different endolysosomal channels in TPC1-deficient cells, including the endosomal channel TRPML2, lysosomal channels TPC2 and TRPML1, as well as the pore-dead mutant channel (13) TPC1L273P (Fig. 1F). Remarkably, of all these channels, only the WT TPC1 could rescue the TPCN1 knockout defect, with other channels failing to restore trafficking (Fig. 1F and SI Appendix, Fig. S3B). These findings establish TPCs, notably TPC1, as mediators of the transferrin cycle, and reinforce a preferential requirement for TPC1 over other endolysosomal channels. Our next steps were to probe possible mechanisms for reduced TfR trafficking.

It has previously been suggested that for cell-membrane receptor-trafficking, including EGFR trafficking (7, 8), TPC deletion affects the expression levels of cell-membrane receptors. By analogy, a reduction in TfR levels could offer a trivial explanation for reduced transferrin uptake. Therefore, we assessed TfR expression by quantitative Western blotting for the TfR in both WT and TPCN1 knockout HeLa cell lysates. Reassuringly, we observed no alteration in the total levels of TfR in TPC1-deficient cells compared to WT (Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). However, this does not exclude the possibility that TPC1 regulates the fraction of TfR at the cell surface. To quantify cell-surface TfR, plasma-membrane proteins were surface-biotinylated, solubilized, and then isolated using streptavidin beads. Western blotting of streptavidin purified proteins revealed that the levels of biotin-tagged cell-surface TfR were substantially reduced in TPCN1 knockout cells relative to WT cells (Figs. 2B and 4B). Additionally, these findings were supported by an in-cell Western assay performed in nonpermeabilized cells: although total TfR levels were comparable, cell-surface levels of TfR were reduced in TPC1-deficient cells compared to WT (Figs. 2C and 4C). Taken together, these findings suggest that TPC1 does not regulate total TfR expression but rather controls the levels of cell-surface TfR through regulation of TfR recycling back to the cell surface.

Ca2+ Influx and ER Ca2+ Do Not Support TfR Trafficking.

We next began to address how, mechanistically, TPC1 might be signaling and driving transferrin trafficking. Previous reports proposed that intracellular Ca2+ signals are important for the transferrin cycle by affecting the rate of transferrin internalization and recycling (26, 28). We therefore investigated whether Ca2+ is required for TfR trafficking and recycling, and if so, which Ca2+ source(s) provide these signals, as this has not previously been defined.

To confirm that TfR trafficking is Ca2+-dependent, we first loaded the cytosol with chelators of either “global Ca2+” (EGTA) or “global plus local Ca2+” (BAPTA), by preincubating cells with their respective AM esters (Fig. 3A). Their differential ability to chelate local Ca2+ domains is a function of their different Ca2+-binding kinetics (35). To verify that these chelators were active, we quantified cytosolic Ca2+ responses to histamine-H1 receptors that couple to IP3Rs, following the addition of histamine in the presence or absence of the chelators. Confirming their activity, both EGTA and BAPTA almost completely ablated the Ca2+ response to histamine (Fig. 3B). If TfR trafficking is Ca2+-dependent, then these Ca2+ chelators should impair trafficking. Although EGTA significantly buffered global cytosolic Ca2+ (Fig. 3B), it surprisingly had no effect on transferrin trafficking (Fig. 3C). In contrast, BAPTA significantly impaired transferrin trafficking by 49% compared to DMSO control cells (Fig. 3C). This impairment was due to local Ca2+ chelation rather than off-target effects, since trafficking was unaffected by the low Ca2+ affinity BAPTA variant, tetrafluoro (TF)-BAPTA-AM (Kd ~ 65 μM). These data indicate that transferrin uptake and trafficking is indeed regulated by Ca2+, but at the level of a local Ca2+ domain and not a global Ca2+ signal.

The BAPTA effect suggests that local Ca2+ is important, but it does not indicate where the local Ca2+ is generated. A series of targeted approaches were required to establish which Ca2+ source(s) drive TfR trafficking. The largest Ca2+ sources available to the cell are the extracellular milieu (as Ca2+ influx) and the largest intracellular Ca2+ store, the ER. To exclude the influence of ER Ca2+ in transferrin trafficking, we emptied the ER Ca2+ using the SERCA inhibitors thapsigargin (Tg) and cyclopiazonic acid (CPA) (36). Experiments were conducted in Ca2+-free medium (supplemented with 100 μM EGTA) to prevent the complications of store-operated Ca2+ entry (37). Successful emptying of the ER by Tg or CPA was confirmed with the Ca2+ ionophore ionomycin [which mobilizes the ER independently of channels (38)]: its response was essentially eliminated by Tg or CPA (Fig. 4A). Crucially, ER Ca2+ depletion by Tg or CPA had no impact upon transferrin trafficking (Fig. 4B). Similarly, we found no role for Ca2+ influx across the plasma membrane, because removal of extracellular Ca2+ had no impact on trafficking either. These results confirm that neither extracellular Ca2+ influx, nor release from the ER is required, but that another smaller intracellular Ca2+ store must be involved.

While ER depletion failed to affect TfR trafficking, suggesting it did not physiologically support the process, we tested whether a large, experimentally induced ER Ca2+ release could nonetheless rescue the transferrin trafficking defect in TPCN1 knockout cells (Fig. 1). ER Ca2+ release was induced in four different ways: through the activation of IP3 receptors using histamine or through mobilization of Ca2+ using Tg, CPA, or ionomycin. Regardless of the stimulus, a substantial Ca2+ mobilization from the ER failed to rescue the TPCN1 knockout trafficking defect (Fig. 4C) and further excludes the ER as a major trafficking driver.

Having excluded separate Ca2+ sources, we investigated whether activation of neighboring endosomal channels could rescue the defect. TRPML2 is found on recycling endosomes and Ca2+ release can be evoked by its selective agonist ML2-SA1 (39) (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Despite residing on recycling endosomes similar to TPC1 (6, 39), Ca2+ release from TRPML2 did not rescue transferrin trafficking in TPC1-deficient cells (Fig. 4D). Finally, even a supraphysiological Ca2+ response to ionomycin only partially restored transferrin trafficking (Fig. 4D). Taken together, these results suggest that a) ER Ca2+ does not support transferrin trafficking, b) Ca2+ signals are compartmentalized even between channels on the same vesicle (10, 39), and c) only TPC1 Ca2+ signals can regulate transferrin trafficking–the latter we investigated further.

Mildly Acidic Endosomes Provide Ca2+ for Transferrin Trafficking.

Our data imply that local Ca2+ is important for regulating transferrin trafficking and given the importance of TPC1, we hypothesized that mildly acidic endosomal Ca2+ stores where TPC1 resides are the source of Ca2+ required for mediating transferrin trafficking.

To test our hypothesis, we first depleted acidic endolysosomal Ca2+ stores in two ways. We first impaired Ca2+ refilling of endolysosomes, by preventing H+-dependent Ca2+ influx using the vacuolar H+-ATPase inhibitor bafilomycin-A1 (Baf-A1) (40, 41). Following Baf-A1 preincubation, transferrin trafficking was significantly impaired compared to control treated cells (Fig. 5A). This is consistent with either the luminal pH or Ca2+ of endolysosomes being important for transferrin trafficking. To distinguish, we chelated Ca2+ within the lumen of endosomes using a Ca2+-binding dextran (Cal-520 dextran) which we chased to TPC1-positive compartments (SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). Following Cal-520 dextran loading, transferrin trafficking was significantly impaired and the number of transferrin-positive vesicles was reduced (Fig. 5B). Importantly, we did not observe any changes in transferrin trafficking with a non-Ca2+-binding dextran (CF, cyanine-based fluorescent dextran), implying that the inhibition of transferrin trafficking was attributed to the depletion of endosomal Ca2+.

The inhibitory effect of BAPTA suggested that local Ca2+ domains are important for the transferrin cycle (Fig. 3C), but it does not reveal the location of the Ca2+ domain. Therefore, we adapted our previous strategy (4) to selectively chelate Ca2+ in a targeted locus using a genetically encoded Ca2+-buffering system. To implicate local periendosomal Ca2+ in transferrin trafficking, a chemical-induced dimerization system acutely translocated Ca2+-buffering proteins to sites of endosomal Ca2+ nanodomains, analogous to that used at lysosomes (4). We used the FKBP12/FRB*-dimerization system to acutely translocate two tandem copies of the Ca2+-binding protein calbindin-2 (CBwt) to TPC1 upon application of the dimerizer rapalog AP21967 (Fig. 5C) (4). Unlike rapamycin, AP21967 does not inhibit mTOR, which excludes mTOR effects on TPC activity (17, 42). Rapalog induced a translocation of cytosolic FKBP12-CB to TPC1-FRB* puncta as quantified by an increased Pearson’s correlation (Fig. 5D). As a control for steric effects, a non-Ca2+-binding EF-hand mutant of CB (CBmut) was also compared. Physiologically, transferrin trafficking was unaffected when CBwt was in the cytosol (without rapalog). However, when CBwt was translocated to early/recycling endosomes by AP21967 pretreatment, transferrin trafficking was significantly inhibited (Fig. 5E and SI Appendix, Fig. S6B). Conversely, translocation of inactive CBmut to TPC1 compartments revealed no detectable effect in transferrin trafficking (Fig. 5E). Together, these data strongly suggest that local endosomal Ca2+ nanodomains generated by TPC1-mediated Ca2+ efflux from the endosome, are crucial for transferrin trafficking.

TPC1 Endosomal Ca2+ Release Drives Transferrin Trafficking.

Until now, our results have implicated Ca2+ as a mediator of the TPC1-dependent transferrin cycle. However, this does not address whether the predominant Na+ flux through TPC1 also contributes to the regulation of trafficking. Therefore, to more directly assess whether Ca2+, Na+, or both are required, we engineered a TPC1 Na+-deficient (but Ca2+-permeable) mutant channel. By analogy with a TPC2 Na+-deficient mutant channel (43), we hypothesized that the largely conserved filter in TPC1 (Fig. 6A) could be similarly rendered Na+-deficient by the equivalent mutations (TPC1A280S/V646M/N647G; SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). We assessed the ionic properties of this mutant channel “TPC1-mut3” using endosomal organelle electrophysiology.

As predicted, bi-ionic patch-clamping of vacuolin-enlarged (44) endosomes containing TPC1-mut3 revealed a significantly enhanced Ca2+ permeability ratio (PCa/Na) ~500 times greater than WT TPC1 (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, TPC1-mut3 displayed a large reduction in Na+ currents and a ~100 mV shift of the reversal potential toward more positive values (ERev approximately+53.3 mV; Fig. 6C), both indicative of a near complete loss of Na+ permeability, yet retaining its Ca2+ fluxes (14, 15). PI(3,5)P2-evoked TPC1-mut3 Ca2+ currents were suppressed by the TPC inhibitor tetrandrine (SI Appendix, Fig. S8A), suggesting that the small residual currents may reflect Ca2+ conductance rather than endogenous background activity (SI Appendix, Fig. S8B). To directly and quantitatively confirm an absolute loss of Na+ permeability, reversal potential-shifts were measured across varying [Na+]cytosolic gradients, in order to estimate the Nernst slope for WT TPC1 versus TPC1-mut3. As anticipated, TPC1-mut3 channels exhibited negligible Na+-dependent shifts, in comparison to WT TPC1 which showed a slope of −58 mV per decade (Fig. 6D). Taken together, these findings strongly establish TPC1-mut3 as a Na+-deficient yet Ca2+-permeable channel.

Applied to intact cells, remarkably the expression of TPC1-mut3 fully restored transferrin trafficking in TPCN1 knockout cells to the same degree as reexpressed WT TPC1 (Fig. 6D). These findings strongly suggest that the dominant Na+-flux component of TPC1 does not contribute to driving transferrin trafficking and it is instead the smaller Ca2+ flux that is the important ionic signal.

To exclude the possibility that the transferrin trafficking defect observed in TPC1-deficient cells was attributed to a possible change in endosomal pH, the luminal pH of WT and TPCN1 knockout early endosomes was quantified by transferrin conjugated to the pH probe pHrodo-Red (SI Appendix, Fig. S9A). Measurements were calibrated as absolute pH and performed ratiometrically against the pH-insensitive Alexa 488 transferrin-conjugate, to mitigate against misinterpreting altered pHrodo signals simply as a function of reduced transferrin uptake and trafficking in TPC1-deficient cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S9B). Deletion of TPC1 had no effect on the luminal pH of early endosomes relative to WT cells (pH ~ 6.7; Fig. 6F and SI Appendix, Fig. S9C), indicating that the TPC1-dependent TfR trafficking defect was unlikely a consequence of early endosomal pH imbalance.

Phosphoinositide Regulation of TPC1-Mediated Ca2+ Fluxes.

Our data suggest that Ca2+ release from TPC1 is essential for the trafficking of the TfR complex, but how TPC1 channels are regulated in this pathway and which second messengers might be gating the channel is unknown. Intriguingly, phosphoinositide lipids are known to regulate both TPC activity (15, 16, 20) and endosomal trafficking (45, 46). Given this tantalizing convergence, we tested whether specific phosphoinositides promote transferrin trafficking via TPC1 using both pharmacological and genetic approaches.

First, we depleted the endolysosomal phospholipids, PI(3)P and PI(3,5)P2, by inhibiting their respective synthetic enzymes PI3K or PIKfyve (Fig. 7A). As confirmed using multiple PI3K (Wortmannin and VPS34-IN1; Fig. 7B) and PIKfyve inhibitors (Vacuolin-1, YM201636, and Apilimod; Fig. 7C), transferrin trafficking was reduced across all treatment conditions. Furthermore, these agents likely act upon a TPC1-dependent pathway because their trafficking effects were reduced ≤50% in TPC1-deficient cells (they inhibited 15 to 17% in TPC1-null cells compared to 25 to 30% in WT cells). The data are therefore consistent with a requirement of phosphoinositide levels for TPC1 activity, and that PI(3)P and PI(3,5)P2 gate TPC1 (15, 20, 21). To more directly address this, we engineered a lipid-insensitive human TPC1 equivalent to the Lys331 mutation in the IS6 transmembrane domain of mouse TPC1 (20). The IS6 amino acid sequence is near-identical between human and mouse TPC1 (20), so we mutated the equivalent Lys330 of human TPC1 with the aim of abolishing PI(3,5)P2-induced activation (TPC1K330A; SI Appendix, Fig. S7B). Whole-endosome patch-clamp recordings confirmed that mutation of Lys330 to Ala (K330A) rendered TPC1 almost completely insensitive to PI(3,5)P2 activation compared to WT TPC1 (Fig. 7 D–F). When expressed in TPCN1 knockout cells, this lipid-binding mutant failed to restore TfR compared to WT TPC1 channel (Fig. 7G). Collectively, these data show that phosphoinositides drive endosomal trafficking by virtue of activating TPC1-mediated local endosomal Ca2+ efflux.

TPC1 Is Required for Hematopoiesis.

As TPC1 is required for receptor-mediated endocytosis of iron-bound transferrin, we conjectured that the downstream corollary of reduced iron uptake would be an iron-deficiency phenotype. To more directly monitor cellular Fe2+ levels, we loaded HeLa WT and TPCN1 knockout cells with an iron-selective probe [RhoNox-1 (47)] whose fluorescence changes proportionally with the cellular free Fe2+ (SI Appendix, Fig. S10A). Consistent with a reduction in iron-bound transferrin internalization, we observed a 39.7 ± 2.0% (mean ± SEM, n = 131 cells, P < 0.0001) decrease in the cytosolic RhoNox-1 fluorescence within TPCN1 knockout cells relative to WT.

However, alongside the anticipated reduction in cytoplasmic Fe2+, there was an additional unexpected alteration in the pattern of RhoNox-1 labeling. TPCN1 knockout cells exhibited a striking increase in punctate fluorescence: in contrast to WT cells which contained only a few puncta per cell, TPCN1 knockout greatly enhanced the number of puncta (Fig. 8A). These puncta were indeed Fe2+-loaded vesicles, since chelation of cellular iron with 2,2’-bipyridyl (2,2’-Bpy) reduced the number of puncta in TPCN1 knockout cells back to that of WT cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S10B). Furthermore, this was specific to RhoNox-1 labeling because another cytosolic fluorophore (the Ca2+-sensitive dye Fluo-8H), did not manifest puncta formation in cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S10C). The accumulation of puncta was demonstrably TPC1-dependent because reexpression of TPC1 in TPCN1 knockout cells reversed puncta formation (Fig. 8A). What was highly revealing was that each punctum represented a lysosome, as judged by the colocalization of the RhoNox-1 puncta and Lysotracker Green (Fig. 8A). Moreover, it was not just the number, but also the fluorescent intensity of the RhoNox-1 in lysosomes that was enhanced in TPCN1 knockout compared to those RhoNox-1-positive lysosomes in WT cells. Just as TPC1 reexpression normalized the vesicle number in knockout cells, it likewise restored the lysosomal RhoNox-1 fluorescence intensity (Fig. 8A).

Although the decrease in cytosolic Fe2+ could be explained by a reduction in transferrin-bound iron internalization, the paradoxical increase in RhoNox-1 Fe2+ vesicles observed in TPC1-deficient cells remains unexplained. Therefore, we turned our attention to the iron homeostatic process ferritinophagy, as a possible explanation. Initiated when intracellular labile Fe2+ levels become low, ferritinophagy scavenges Fe2+ from the iron-binding protein ferritin by selectively targeting its degradation. When cytosolic Fe2+ drops, the nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4) binds to ferritin and the resultant NCOA4–ferritin complex is encapsulated into an autophagosome which then fuses with lysosomes, ultimately leading to ferritin degradation and the release of Fe2+ ions to restore homeostatic balance (48, 49). As the distribution of NCOA4 is dynamic and stringently regulated by cytosolic labile Fe2+ levels (49), we hypothesized that the excessive lysosomal Fe2+ puncta observed in TPCN1 knockout cells stemmed from ferritin-NCOA4 lysosomal fusion. To determine if ferritin was targeted for lysosomal degradation, we assessed the colocalization of ferritin or NCOA4 with lysosomal-associated membrane protein 1 (LAMP1). TPC1-deficient cells exhibited enhanced redistribution of both ferritin and NCOA4 to LAMP1-positive lysosomes (Fig. 8B). Furthermore, within TPCN1 knockout cells the number of ferritin, or NCOA4 LAMP1-positive vesicles was the same (Fig. 8B), consistent with NCOA4 and ferritin being in a complex together. Together with our RhoNox-1 data, we suggest that TPC1 plays a major role in mediating iron homeostasis in two ways: a) it regulates TfR (and iron) uptake across the plasma membrane; b) consequently, TPC1 controls intracellular Fe2+ levels by indirectly promoting ferritin-NCOA4 lysosomal fusion, a finding consistent with increased ferritinophagy (48, 49). This would explain why the RhoNox-1 fluorescence becomes more punctate and intense, owing to enhanced ferritin degradation and subsequent Fe2+ accumulation/storage in lysosomes.

Having identified a defective iron trafficking phenotype in TPC1-deficient cells, we investigated if this resulted in a disease phenotype within cellular and animal models. One of the most prominent disorders of iron homeostasis is the iron-deficiency disorder anemia, characterized by insufficient iron to support hemoglobin synthesis and red blood cell production (50). In view of this, we tested if the hemoglobin precursor, heme, was reduced in TPC1-deficient cells. We quantified heme in HeLa cells by monitoring its conversion into fluorescent protoporphyrin IX (PPIX) (51) and found that heme levels were lower in TPCN1 knockout cells compared to WT (Fig. 8C). Extending this heme-deficiency to animal models using the International Mouse Phenotyping Consortium (IMPC) (52) (www.mousephenotype.org), we noted that the hematopoietic system within TPC1-deficient mice is severely affected. Hematological assessments revealed a reduction in both mean corpuscular volume and hemoglobin within early adult Tpcn1−/− mice (Fig. 8D), two common phenotypes associated with microcytic iron-deficiency anemia (50). In contrast, the hematopoietic system of Tpcn2−/− mice appears unaffected (Fig. 8D), supporting the specificity of TPC1 for maintaining iron homeostasis. These findings correlate with our cellular Fe2+ and heme experiments, consistent with TPC1 dysfunction resulting in an iron-deficiency phenotype. We speculate that TPC1 indirectly influences red blood cell formation by regulating the trafficking of iron-bound transferrin and the availability of cytosolic labile Fe2+ available for heme synthesis and hemoglobin production.

Discussion

Dysfunction of TPC1 can drive disease, including viral and bacterial toxin infectivity (3, 5, 6), fatty liver disease (7), and cancer (53), via an impairment of membrane- and protein-trafficking normally regulated by TPC1. Despite a clear involvement, the mechanism and cation modality of TPC1 in the context of cell-membrane trafficking pathways is lacking. This study characterizes TPC1 within TfR cell-membrane trafficking, establishing the precise molecular circuitry of the messenger and permeant ions involved, as well as the downstream effect of TPC1 dysfunction, iron-deficiency anemia.

Within the multiphasic transferrin cycle, TPC1 appears to reduce the rate of TfR recycling back to the cell surface (Fig. 1B), thereby decreasing TfR levels on the cell surface available to internalize iron-bound transferrin (Fig. 2 B and C). We found no alteration of total TfR expression which contrasts with TPCs affecting the expression of other receptors such as EGFR (7, 8). The ionic/molecular signals driving this were investigated.

Endosomal Ca2+ Is Discrete from Other Ca2+ Sources.

Here, we show that phosphoinositide-induced endosomal Ca2+ release from TPC1 is required for protein trafficking. Our data builds upon preexisting research implicating local Ca2+ signals as drivers of the vesicular transport of the TfR complex (26–28, 54). Although acidic lysosomal compartments are recognized as major Ca2+ stores, early/recycling endosomal compartments have received less attention as Ca2+ stores. Estimating the endosomal free Ca2+ concentration is challenging, and some reports detail a considerably lower endosomal luminal [Ca2+] (~2 to 50 μM) (55–57) compared to lysosomes (~500 μM) (58). Our own estimate of early endosomal [Ca2+] is in a similar range (~10 to 12 μM; SI Appendix, Fig. S11) but still represents a ~100-fold Ca2+ gradient relative to the cytosol (~100 nM). Therefore, we propose that Ca2+ efflux from endosomes can more than adequately support localized TPC1 Ca2+ nanodomains to drive transferrin trafficking.

Thereby, we established endosomes as Ca2+ stores that promote TfR trafficking by Ca2+ release, as evidenced from Ca2+ buffering on either the luminal or cytosolic face (Fig. 5 B and E). Other major Ca2+ sources (Ca2+ influx, the ER) cannot substitute for endosomal Ca2+ stores (Fig. 4), attesting to the uniqueness of the local endosomal Ca2+ signals in this pathway. Even other intracellular channels on the same vesicle population (TRPML2) cannot drive TfR trafficking (Fig. 4D); this mirrors our “extreme compartmentalization” of lysosomal Ca2+ signaling during phagocytosis, where the Ca2+ nanodomains of TPC2 and TRPML1 are segregated from one another to drive different biologies (10). We hypothesize that a similar model holds for TPC1 and endosomes.

TPC1 Ca2+, But Not Na+ Release, Modulates Membrane Trafficking.

Since loss of TPC1 did not change the pH of early endosomes (Fig. 6F), we tested if other ions were responsible for trafficking. TPC1 and its lysosomal counterpart, TPC2, are permeable to both Ca2+ and Na+ ions (13, 15, 16, 18). For TPC2, the relative permeability ratio of Ca2+/Na+ is not fixed but unusually depends upon the activating ligands (PI(3,5)P2 and NAADP) which exhibit “biased agonism” (11, 14). Our data reaffirm WT TPC1 as a Na+-selective channel (PNa/Ca = 16.2; Fig. 6B), but with some Ca2+ permeability. Our estimate of the PNa/Ca for TPC1 following PI(3,5)P2 activation broadly agrees with others (PNa/Ca ~ 10 to 20) (22) and is similar to that for TPC2 (PNa/Ca ~ 16.8) upon lipid activation (16, 43). In Fig. 6D, the relationship between the reversal potential of TPC1 and [Na+]cytoplasmic exhibited a slope of −58 mV per decade, in good agreement with the predicted Nernstian behavior and comparable to previous slopes for TPC1 (−56 mV) (15) and TPC2 (−57 mV) (16).

By engineering a newly characterized Na+-deficient, but Ca2+-permeable TPC1 channel (TPC1-mut3; Fig. 6), we confirmed that Ca2+ (not Na+) fluxes regulate TfR trafficking. Indeed, TPC1 was originally identified as a major endolysosomal Ca2+-permeable channel (13, 18, 59). Even though other Ca2+-permeable channels are present on endolysosomes, neither overexpression/activation of endosomal channels (TRPML2), nor lysosomal channels (TPC2 and TRPML1) could substitute for TPC1 (Figs. 1F and 4D), affirming the specificity of TPC1 within the transferrin cycle. The requirement of autonomous local Ca2+ mirrors phagocytosis, where discrete Ca2+ nanodomains are essential for downstream Ca2+-decoding (4, 10). How these TPC1-mediated local Ca2+ nanodomains are decoded to drive trafficking remains unknown. However, previous TPC interactomes identified several candidate Ca2+-dependent proteins, including syntaxins/SNAREs, CaMKK2, and Rab-GTPases, all integral for membrane trafficking (6, 27, 60, 61).

TPC1 Gating during Trafficking.

If endosomal Ca2+ fluxes through TPC1 are essential for vesicle trafficking and fusion during the transferrin cycle, how are these Ca2+ fluxes regulated? NAADP and PI(3,5)P2 are recognized to activate both human TPCs and additionally, these messengers differentially determine the Ca2+ or Na+-selectivity of TPC2 (11, 12, 19). Whether this is true for TPC1 is less clear, as only modest changes in the TPC1 PNa/Ca were observed with different agonists (14). Regardless, our evidence suggests that TPC1-mediated Ca2+ release occurs in response to PI(3,5)P2 (Fig. 6 B and C), similar to the small TPC2-mediated Ca2+ release observed with the PI(3,5)P2 mimetic, TPC2-A1-P (11, 12). The NAADP antagonist, Ned-19, had no impact upon trafficking (Fig. 1C), suggesting that TPC1 is not gated by NAADP during trafficking. Conversely, several lines of evidence point to gating by PI(3,5)P2: first, pharmacological inhibition of lipid synthesis reduced TPC1-dependent trafficking, and second, a TPC1 lipid-insensitive mutant (TPC1K330A) failed to support trafficking (Fig. 7). A limitation of this manuscript is that TPC1K330A was not assessed directly as to whether this mutation secondarily affected activation by NAADP. Due to technical difficulties, reliably measuring NAADP-evoked currents using patch-clamp is challenging, possibly due to the loss of cytosolic NAADP accessory proteins during endosome isolation (44). Measuring TPC1 Ca2+ responses in vitro might have been an alternative approach, but the instability and availability of membrane-permeant NAADP analogues [NAADP-AM and MASTER-NAADP (62, 63)] preclude these experiments. Collectively, our data predominantly support TPC1 gating by lipid, but we cannot formally exclude a minor role for NAADP in TfR trafficking.

Phosphoinositides are implicated in various aspects of membrane trafficking, including protein recruitment and endo/exocytosis, whereby they recruit proteins harboring PI-binding domains (45, 46). Our findings add TPC1 to the list of their potential targets. Previously, it has been suggested that the TfR acts as more than just an endocytic receptor, but also as a signal transduction receptor coupled to downstream Ca2+ signaling pathways, as provisionally shown by increased intracellular [Ca2+] following TfR binding (27, 28). However, we were unable to detect any reproducible Ca2+ signals using either cytosolic Ca2+ dyes, or the genetically encoded Ca2+ indicator G-GECO1.2 tethered to TPC1. Therefore, we tentatively speculate that the resting levels of phosphoinositides and constitutive endosomal Ca2+ efflux through TPC1 is sufficient.

TPC1 and Iron Homeostasis.

Given that endosomal TPC1–mediated Ca2+ fluxes regulate the internalization and trafficking of the iron-bound transferrin complex, we finally addressed whether a lack of iron uptake and transport culminates in a disease phenotype in TPC1-deficient cells and animal models. Enhanced TPC activity has been linked to dysregulation of iron homeostasis, particularly through cytosolic labile iron overload (64). Complementary to this, our findings indicate that TPC1 deletion reduces the cytoplasmic labile Fe2+ available for heme synthesis (Fig. 8C) and potentially enhances ferritinophagy (Fig. 8B). These cellular findings correlate with mouse models, which reinforce the importance of TPC1 within iron-dependent hematopoietic pathways, where Tpcn1−/− mice display clinical characteristics of anemic conditions such as microcytic anemia (50). Intriguingly, deletion of Tpcn2 does not appear to affect the hematopoietic system of mice (Fig. 8D), broadly consistent with our own observation that TPCN2 knockout does not affect transferrin trafficking as robustly as does TPC1 ablation (Fig. 1 D and E).

Surprisingly, the reduction in TfR uptake (Fig. 1) and cytosolic Fe2+ in TPCN1 knockout cells paradoxically increased lysosomal Fe2+-loaded vesicles (Fig. 9A and SI Appendix, Fig. S10 B and C). Importantly, RhoNox-1 fluorescence is not enhanced by other metal ions, reductants, or reactive oxygen species (47). Our current working hypothesis is that deletion of TPC1 diminishes intracellular Fe2+, resulting in the redistribution and fusion of the NCOA4–ferritin complex with lysosomes, consistent with the process of ferritinophagy (48, 49). Whether TPC1 is like TRPML1 and TRPML2, an additional Fe2+ egress pathway itself (65), is beyond the scope of this current manuscript. The clinical iron-deficient phenotype observed in TPC1-deficient cells and mice can be explained by a decrease in the internalization of iron-bound transferrin, in turn, reducing the amount of labile Fe2+ available for heme and hemoglobin synthesis (Fig. 9). Finally, in an attempt to restore intracellular Fe2+ to normal levels, NCOA4 binds to ferritin and fuses with lysosomes to degrade ferritin and release Fe2+ (Fig. 9). In relation to diminished intracellular Fe2+, we would expect to see dysfunctional changes regarding heme synthesis and ferritinophagy, such as decreased ferrochelatase enzyme activity, and increased lysosomal reactive oxygen species production (66, 67).

Regulatory mechanism of TPC1 within iron homeostasis. Schematic of the hypothesized role of TPC1 in transferrin/transferrin receptor trafficking (Tf/TfR) and iron homeostasis. Endosomal phosphoinositide-induced TPC1 Ca2+ nanodomains are required for TfR uptake and recycling. TPC1 dysfunction disrupts iron-bound transferrin internalization, reducing the cytosolic labile Fe2+ pool and consequently, the availability of Fe2+ for heme synthesis. In response to reduced cytosolic Fe2+, NCOA4 binds to ferritin, tagging ferritin for lysosomal degradation and Fe3+ reduction in an attempt to restore cellular Fe2+ homeostatic balance.

Conclusions

Together, our study of endosomal trafficking in HeLa TPCN1 and TPCN2 knockout cells indicates that TPC1 is critical for cell-surface protein/receptor trafficking, in particular trafficking and recycling of the TfR. Our findings present compelling evidence that endosomes are Ca2+ storage organelles that provide Ca2+ signals essential for membrane trafficking. In particular, we have confirmed the importance of endosomal Ca2+ release explicitly through TPC1, which generates its own local Ca2+ signaling domains. These local Ca2+ signals are probably the result of TPC1 gating by the phosphoinositide PI(3,5)P2, and this in turn drives TfR trafficking. The corollary of the dysfunction of endosomal membrane trafficking in TPC1-deficient cells is a disease-like anemic iron-deficiency phenotype in cells and animals. This research not only offers valuable insights into the role and mechanistic regulation of TPC1 within a cellular trafficking model but also underlines a vital requirement for TPC1 within iron homeostasis, whose expression likely governs numerous physiological processes.

Methods Summary

A fully detailed description of the methodology used in this study is provided in the SI Appendix, Supporting Materials and Methods.

Generation of Knockout Cell Lines.

HeLa TPCN1 and TPCN2 knockout cells were generated by CRISPR/Cas9 using double sgRNA (32) (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 and Table S1). The generation of Tpcn1−/−, Tpcn2−/−, and Tpcn1/2−/− MEF cells has been detailed previously (5, 68).

Plasmids.

All constructs were generated in-house by Gibson Assembly cloning or site-directed mutagenesis. Accession numbers and information regarding individual constructs are described in SI Appendix, Table S2.

Transferrin Trafficking Assays.

HeLa WT, TPC1- or TPC2-deficient cells were synchronized on ice for 10 min, and unless otherwise stated, followed by incubating with 10 μg/mL Alexa Fluor 647 transferrin for 2.5 min at 37 °C. Transferrin trafficking was arrested with ice-cold medium before fixation in 4% PFA for 15 min. For transferrin recycling experiments, cells were prelabeled with fluorescent transferrin for 10 min and then washed to remove the transferrin and fluorescence subsequently tracked for different times. To manipulate channel levels, TPC1-mScarlet-I was transfected in WT or TPC1 knockout HeLa cells. Multicolor images were collected by confocal laser-scanning microscopy and fluorescence quantified using ImageJ/FiJi.

The individual drugs, concentrations, and incubation timings, as well as the analysis of transferrin uptake/trafficking are detailed in the SI Appendix, Supporting Materials and Methods.

Chelation of Endosomal Luminal or Periendosomal Ca2+.

The Ca2+-binding dextran Cal-520 dextran (10 mg/mL) was chased to TPC1-positive endosomal compartments (SI Appendix, Fig. S6A) to chelate endosomal luminal Ca2+. Periendosomal Ca2+-nanodomains were chelated by modifying and acutely translocating the Ca2+-binding protein, calbindin-2 (or its nonbinding mutant), to TPC1 itself using AP21967 and the FKBP12 and FRB* dimerization system (4).

Immunological Techniques.

For Western blotting, proteins from HeLa WT or TPCN1 knockout cells were solubilized and denatured, then separated on an mPAGE gel and transferred onto a PVDF membrane. Membranes were blocked and labeled with an anti-TfR (H68.4) primary antibody, then visualized using a LiCOR scanner. TfR protein signal was normalized to total protein content. For cell-surface TfR analysis, surface proteins were biotinylated at 4 °C prior to solubilization and purified using streptavidin beads prior to Western blotting.

For in-cell Western assays, cell surface TfR was labeled in fixed nonpermeabilized cells using a primary antibody (G-8) that recognizes the extracellular C-terminus of the TfR. Signals were recorded on a LiCOR scanner and normalized to cell number via cytosolic staining (CellTag 520 stain).

Immunofluorescence staining was performed in fixed saponin-permeabilized cells using anti-FTH1, anti-NCOA4, anti-LAMP1, and anti-Rab5 primary antibodies.

Electrophysiology.

Manual whole-endolysosomal patch-clamp recordings were performed as previously detailed using Giga-ohm-seal glass pipettes (44, 69). HeLa cells were transfected with mNeonGreen-tagged plasmids of TPC1, TPC1A280S/V646M/N647G, or TPC1K330A. Cells were treated overnight with 2 μM vacuolin-A1 to enlarge endolysosomal vesicles. Cytoplasmic (bath) and luminal (pipette) solutions used for patch-clamp recordings and permeability calculations are detailed in the SI Appendix, Supporting Materials and Methods.

Ca2+ Imaging.

Global cytosolic Ca2+ was measured using the Ca2+ dye Fluo-8H-AM in the presence or absence of the cytosolic Ca2+ chelators EGTA-AM or BAPTA-AM. Perichannel Ca2+ nanodomains evoked by agonist ML2-SA1 were measured by the transfected fusion protein TRPML2-G-GECO1.2 (with cytosolic G-GECO1.2 as a control). Luminal endosomal Ca2+ was quantified ratiometrically by coloading cells with Ca2+-sensitive Cal-520 dextran and Ca2+-insensitive Texas-Red dextran for 5, 10, or 20 min. The Ca2+ affinity of Cal-520 dextran was determined in situ at endosomal pH 6.7 by clamping the free Ca2+ concentration to a range of values using different ratios of total Ca2+ and HEDTA (SI Appendix, Fig. S11A) and fluorescence measured on a plate reader.

Endosomal pH Measurements.

The pH of WT and TPCN1 knockout early endosomes was quantified ratiometrically by colabeling vesicles with transferrins conjugated to either pH-sensitive pHrodo-Red, or pH-insensitive Alexa 488 (SI Appendix, Fig. S9A). To generate a pH calibration curve, endosomal pH was clamped at given pH values by combining high-K+ extracellular media at different pH with nigericin/valinomycin ionophores (SI Appendix, Fig. S9B). Fluorescence was recorded by single-cell microscopy.

Iron Measurements.

The N-oxide-based fluorescent iron (Fe2+) probe RhoNox-1 (47), was used to detect cytosolic labile and vesicular Fe2+ levels in HeLa WT and TPCN1 knockout cells and imaged by fluorescence microscopy.

Heme Quantification.

Total heme in HeLa WT or TPCN1 knockout cell lysates was assessed by converting heme into its fluorescent precursor PPIX (51). Fluorescence was recorded on a plate reader.
