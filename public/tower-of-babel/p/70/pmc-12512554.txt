Functional dyspepsia: from human to dog, a retrospective study of 29 cases illustrating a complex entity
Authors: Hugo Kaufmann, Henri Duboc, Valérie Freiche
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFunctional gastrointestinal disorders, including mostly functional dyspepsia (FD) and irritable bowel syndrome, are highly prevalent in human patients. FD is a complex condition in human gastroenterology and is characterized by abdominal discomfort, epigastric pain or burning, postprandial fullness, or early satiety. To our knowledge, such a syndrome, in the absence of organic, metabolic, or systemic causes, has not been reported in dogs. We aimed to provide a comprehensive description of the presentation of suspected canine functional dyspepsia in a retrospective case series. All records of dogs suspected of having dyspeptic clinical signs were studied. Laboratory data, imaging results and gastroscopic findings unlikely to explain the intensity of clinical signs were mandatory for inclusion.ResultsTwenty-nine dogs were retrospectively enrolled. All presented with signs of gastrointestinal discomfort but results from bloodwork, abdominal ultrasonography, endoscopy, and histopathology did not fully account for the severity of the clinical signs observed. FD was found to predominantly affect females (66%) and was most associated with vomiting (97%), abdominal pain (58%), intermittent diarrhoea (52%), pica (52%), compulsive chewing behaviours (48%), and belching or excessive yawning (41%), along with other signs of upper gastrointestinal discomfort. The median duration of clinical signs was 1.5 years, with a median age of onset also at 1.5 years. Various treatments were attempted with varying success, including dietary changes, antacids, prokinetics, and steroids. Follow-up data were available for 21 dogs, of which 76% demonstrated clinical improvement over a median follow-up period of 12 months. Interestingly, anxiety disorder of the owner was commonly reported.ConclusionsFunctional dyspepsia is a clinically emerging functional gastrointestinal disorder in dogs, especially in toy and small breed, middle-aged, female dogs, characterized by chronic gastrointestinal discomfort and vomiting in the absence of confirmed organic disease. The clinical picture of intense abdominal crisis with highly involved and anxious owners is out of step with the results of investigations. Overlap with food-responsive enteropathies, immunosuppressant-responsive enteropathies, or gastroduodenal dysmotility disorders is still of concern, potentially triggered by emotional disorders. Canine FD could reflect a gut-brain axis disturbance and should be included in the differential diagnosis of refractory canine upper digestive disorders. Tailored therapeutic approaches, including dietary modifications, antacids, and prokinetic agents, may offer clinical benefit.Keywords: Canine, Chronic vomiting, Motility disorder, Functional gastrointestinal disorder, Anxiety, Gut-brain axis, Endoscopy

Background

Functional dyspepsia (FD) represents a significant health concern in human patients and is the most prevalent gastroduodenal disorder, with a pooled prevalence rate ranging from 5 to 20%, albeit with some regional variations [1, 2]. The ROME IV consensus defines FD as the presence of recurrent or chronic epigastric symptoms in the absence of structural disease that is likely to explain the clinical signs [3, 4]. To diagnose FD, one or more of the following criteria (the cardinal dyspeptic diagnostic criteria) must be present in the absence of an organic condition: (1) early satiation, (2) postprandial fullness, (3) epigastric pain, and (4) epigastric burning [4]. Additional clinical signs often present with FD: bloating in the upper abdomen, nausea, belching, heartburn, and regurgitation [5]. Clinical signs must persist for at least 3 Days a week over a span of 3 months to diagnose FD. The disease is also frequently associated with other conditions or risk factors, irritable bowel syndrome, or gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) [4]. Dietary factors play a significant role in triggering FD clinical signs [6]. Finally, a strong correlation still exists between psychiatric comorbidities and FD, which may lead to refractory disease [7]. Despite extensive research, the underlying pathophysiology of FD remains unclear in humans and is believed to be multifactorial. Disturbances in gastric motility (inadequate fundic accommodation or delayed gastric emptying), altered gastric sensation (hypersensitivity to duodenal acid or lipids, or gastric distension), and compromised gastroduodenal integrity (inflammation, impaired barrier function) could be linked with the onset of FD [8].

In canine medicine, emesis is a clearly defined symptom with a systematic diagnostic approach [9]. Once non-gastrointestinal causes, obstructive causes, and common causes of gastric disorders (infectious diseases or toxic origins) are excluded, the occurrence of chronic inflammatory enteropathy (including food-responsive enteropathy) or gastric motility disorders should be questioned. Food-responsive enteropathy (FRE) is the most common form of chronic inflammatory enteropathy in dogs, accounting for 50–65% of cases [10]. FRE can be associated with chronic vomiting, and its diagnosis is primarily based on the successful management of clinical signs through dietary management. Altered gastric motility is often associated with chronic vomiting, abdominal distension, abdominal discomfort, or bloating, which significantly impacts the quality of life of affected dogs [11]. Gastric motility has a complex physiology that depends on both the autonomic nervous system (including sympathetic and parasympathetic innervation) and the enteric nervous system [12]. Retrograde transit with gastroesophageal reflux, potentially leading to associated aerodigestive diseases, is also well documented as a possible consequence of delayed gastric emptying [13, 14].

In our experience, some dogs exhibit chronic vomiting and abdominal discomfort (compulsive licking, ptyalism, chewing, abdominal pain, belching, scraping, gagging, or retching) akin to dyspeptic clinical signs, but diagnostic examinations often fail to identify a specific underlying disease. Notably, there is a marked discrepancy between the abdominal discomfort reported by owners—often absent during clinical examination—and the results of clinical, biochemical, imaging, and gastroscopic evaluations. Diagnostic methods for assessing gastric motility disorders, such as gastric emptying scintigraphy or wireless motility capsules, are often considered too invasive and of limited diagnostic value, particularly given the intermittent nature of clinical signs [15]. To date, FD has not been clearly characterized in canine medicine, although its existence is plausible. The condition may significantly impair the quality of life of both dogs and their owners. Recent studies have shown a negative correlation between quality of life and the severity of chronic enteropathies, and suggest that, as in human FD, anxiety may play a contributing role in the development of functional gastrointestinal disorders (FGIDs) in dogs [16, 17].

The aim of this retrospective study was to describe cases of suspected functional dyspepsia in dogs, with particular emphasis on signalment, clinical presentation, diagnostic findings, and therapeutic approaches. Additionally, we aimed to retrospectively report anxiety and quality of life in these dogs and their owners as a possible cause of the development of canine FD.

Methods

Selection of cases

Medical records from the Alfort Veterinary University Hospital (Maisons-Alfort, France) spanning from 2012 to 2023 were retrospectively searched to identify dogs with gastroduodenal discomfort and clinical signs potentially linked to FD. We defined FD in dogs as the presence of chronic gastrointestinal discomfort in the absence of structural disease that is likely to explain the clinical signs. Accordingly, any dog with chronic vomiting or regurgitations for more than 3 weeks, together with signs of abdominal discomfort (e.g. licking, ptyalism, chewing, yawning, pica, abdominal pain, belching, scraping, rumbling, gagging, or retching), was included. Dogs with significant abnormal physical examinations, haematological or biochemical results, likely to explain the clinical signs, were excluded. Dogs presenting with clear mucosal damage, ulceration, or mass lesions on ultrasound, endoscopic or histologic examination of the upper gastrointestinal tract were excluded. As mild to severe gastroduodenal inflammation with impaired epithelial barrier function is a recognized feature of the pathophysiology of human functional dyspepsia, the presence of ultrasonographic or endoscopic evidence of gastroduodenitis, or a gastroduodenal lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate, was not considered an exclusion criterion. Cases were also excluded if their medical records concerning laboratory results, sonographic examinations, and endoscopic or histologic reports were incomplete. When present, diarrhoea was required to be a secondary manifestation, occurring in association with vomiting. Food-responsive enteropathy was ruled out by at least two prior dietary trials, each conducted for a minimum duration of one month. Immunosuppressant-responsive enteropathy was ruled out based on a prior failure to respond to corticosteroids and/or the absence of definitive remission with immunosuppressive therapy alone during follow-up. In all cases, the clinical signs could not be attributed to any identifiable structural or metabolic disorder, supporting a presumptive diagnosis of functional dyspepsia.

Data collection

Data collected for each case included breed, age, weight, sex, clinical signs, duration of gastrointestinal disorders, deworming habits, diet, previous medical history including treatments, physical examination findings, haematobiochemical results at presentation, ultrasonographic findings, endoscopic findings, histological results, and follow-up including duration of follow-up and response to treatment. A complete serum haematobiochemical profile was required for inclusion, including measurements of creatinine, urea, ALT, ALP, serum total protein, hemogram, and, if clinically relevant, CRP, cobalamin, lipase (canine serum pancreatic lipase immunoreactivity or DGGR lipase), and resting cortisol. All dogs had to undergo complete abdominal ultrasound and upper digestive tract endoscopy to be included. Gastric and duodenal biopsies were collected by the same experimented clinician under endoscopic guidance for histologic evaluation (Olympus GIF 190 videoendoscopy). Endoscopy scoring was evaluated according to the WSAVA guidelines [18].

Quality of life

Dogs’ quality of life (dQoL) was retrospectively assessed based on answers obtained from a visual 1–10 scale [17]. dQoL had been published in previous study from Marchetti et al. and demonstrated good reliability [17]. Anxiety levels of the owners were assessed using a visual 1–10 scale, with the question: “How do you evaluate your level of anxiety for your dog?” (Fig. 1) [17]. This retrospective study was entirely non-interventional, and no procedures, treatments, or experiments were conducted on the dogs. All owners provided informed consent prior to completing the questionnaire and agreed to the anonymous use of their data. No personally identifiable information was collected, and all responses were anonymised in accordance with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR, EU 2016/679). In line with French regulations and the European Directive 2010/63/EU, ethical approval was not required, as the study did not involve any prospective experimentation, handling, or treatment of animals, and relied solely on retrospective, owner-reported data. Furthermore, as the research did not involve human participants within the meaning of the French Public Health Code (Code de la santé publique), it was not subject to review by a Comité de Protection des Personnes (CPP). A control group was defined to compare dQoL and owner anxiety between dyspeptic dogs and healthy controls. Control dogs were presented for routine vaccination and were deemed clinically healthy by the attending veterinarian. Owners of control dogs completed the same questionnaire during the consultation. All owners provided informed consent prior to completing the questionnaire and agreed to the anonymous use of their data. Dogs under one year of age were excluded to avoid potential confounding due to age-related behavioural variability.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to analyse the results of the study. Continuous variables with a normal distribution are presented as means with 95% confidence intervals, while non-normally distributed variables are reported as medians with ranges. The two groups (healthy dogs and dyspeptic dogs) were compared for age, weight and difference in dQoL and anxiety using the Kruskal–Walli’s test, with pairwise comparisons with the Bonferroni correction test. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed via R v.4.4.0 software.

Results

Selection of cases

Initially, a total of 65 dogs were enrolled with suspicion of FD, but 23 dogs lacked endoscopic examination, 5 dogs rapidly responded to food management (classified as food responsive enteropathy), 5 dogs were suspected to present autonomic digestive focal seizures (classified as responsive to antiepileptic), and 3 dogs had incomplete medical records (Fig. 2). Finally, 29 dogs met the inclusion criteria, all presenting a history of marked gastrointestinal discomfort. Among these 29 dogs, 19 (66%) were females, including 18 spayed females, while 10 were males, including 5 neutered males. Statistical analysis revealed no significant difference between females and males (adjusted Chi-Square test, p-value = 0.09). The ages of the affected dogs ranged from 1 year to 10 year (median, 5 years). Dogs weighed between 2 kg and 32 kg (median, 9 kg). Breeds included 4 Bichons (14%), 3 Chihuahuas (10%), 3 Boxers (10%), 2 Yorkshire Terriers (7%), 2 Brittany Spaniels (7%), 2 West Highland White Terriers (7%), 2 Shih Tzus (7%) and 11 other dogs of various breeds.

History and clinical signs

Most dogs had vomiting (28 dogs, 97%), sometimes regurgitations (6 dogs, 21%) (Fig. 3). Owners frequently reported abdominal pain as the most common concurrent complaint (17 dogs, 58%), sometimes characterized by a prior position. Intermittent diarrhoea was also commonly reported (15 dogs, 52%) and pica was frequent (15 dogs, 52%). Chewing was well reported in 14 dogs (48%). Belching or yawning was observed in 12 dogs (41%), and rumbling was noted in 8 dogs (28%). Compulsive licking (floor, objects) was reported in 6 dogs (21%), and frequent deglutition or retching was noted in 7 dogs (24%). Additionally, scratching was observed in 3 dogs (10%). Intermittent loss of appetite and food inappetence were reported in 5 dogs (17%). Four dogs (14%) were fed a homemade diet, 3 dogs (10%) were fed a mixed diet (combination of homemade diet and commercial diet) and 21 dogs (72%) were solely on a commercial diet (9 dogs with gastrointestinal diet, 10 dogs with a hydrolysed diet and 2 dogs with a classic diet). The diet was unknown for 1 dog. Gastrointestinal disorders were chronic and intermittent but progressed to crisis in 14 dogs (48%), with clinical signs more frequently reported at the end of the night or early morning in 9 dogs (31%) and disturbed the owners. The duration of clinical signs between onset and initial consultation at our hospital ranged from 1 month to 8 years (median, 1.5 years) (Fig. 4). The median age at the onset of clinical signs was 1.5 years (range 3 months to 7.5 years). Most dogs (25 dogs, 86%) had received previously corticosteroid therapy from the attending veterinarian without significant clinical improvement. The median number of clinical signs observed per dog was 4 (range 1 to 9). Only one dog exhibited only vomiting without any other gastrointestinal discomfort, but the owner was highly concerned with the dog’s condition. The physical examination findings were unremarkable in 22 (76%) of the 29 dogs, whereas 7 dogs (24%) had cranial abdominal pain.

Clinicopathologic and laboratory data

One dog presented a marked increase in ALT (980 U/L) secondary to corticosteroid therapy, while another dog had a mild increase in ALP (296 U/L) without hepatic ultrasound involvement (Table 1). Specific lipase assays performed in 22 dogs revealed slightly elevated pancreatic lipase immunoreactivity in 2 dogs (417 and 441 µg/L) without clinical signs or ultrasound evidence of acute pancreatitis. These levels of lipasemia, in the absence of other unequivocal signs, were not considered significant enough by the clinician to support a diagnosis of pancreatitis. Cobalamin assessment was performed in 16 dogs, with 2 dogs displaying hypocobalaminemia requiring supplementation (< 150 and 236 ng/L, respectively). Nineteen dogs had a basal cortisol assay, with 16 dogs having basal cortisol levels > 55 nmol/L. Three dogs had basal cortisol levels < 55 nmol/L, but an ACTH stimulation test was performed in only 1 dog to exclude Addison’s disease. The two other dogs had already received steroids in the last weeks and could have affected the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis despite evidence of a reduced size in adrenal size. Other available laboratory data were unremarkable. Fecal flotation was performed in 5 dogs (17%), with all cases returning negative results; the remaining dogs had a recent history of deworming with milbemycin oxime and/or fenbendazole.

Imaging

Abdominal ultrasonography was conducted in all dogs, showing thickening of the gastric mucosa in 10 dogs (34%; mild in 5 dogs, moderate in 4 dogs and marked in 1 dog), of the duodenal mucosa in 4 dogs (14%; mild in 3 dogs and moderate in 1 dog), of the jejunal mucosa in 2 dogs (7%; mild in all dogs) and of the ileal mucosa in 1 dog (3%; mild) (Table 1). One dog had marked thickening of colonic mucosa along with concomitant diarrhoea. No dogs presented with abdominal Lymphadenopathy or effusion. In 4 dogs, subjective hepatomegaly with hyperechoic parenchymal background consistent with benign vacuolar hepatopathy was noted. None of the dogs presented with sonographic signs indicative of acute pancreatitis or pancreatic abnormalities. Additionally, a moderate decrease in intestinal peristalsis was observed in 2 dogs.

Gastroscopy and histology

All dogs underwent endoscopy of the upper gastrointestinal tract, revealing subjective signs of macroscopic gastritis and duodenitis in 24 dogs (83%; mild in 18 dogs and moderate in 6 dogs) and 19 dogs (66%; mild in 13 dogs, moderate in 5 dogs, marked in 1 dog), respectively (Table 1). This subjective assessment was based on the presence of mucosal hyperemia and graded from normal to severe using the endoscopic examination standard form developed by the WSAVA Gastrointestinal Standardization Group, although definitive characterization of inflammation required histologic evaluation [18]. Four dogs (14%) presented evidence of delayed gastric emptying, characterized by the persistence of a food bolus in the stomach during endoscopy despite > 12 h of fasting. No other notable abnormalities were observed during endoscopic examination, particularly no signs of gastric erosions or ulcerations. No oesophageal lesion was found in dogs experiencing regurgitations. The pylorus was easily catheterized in 26 dogs (90%). Histopathological examination of the gastric and duodenal mucosa revealed Lymphoplasmacytic infiltration in 25 dogs (86%; mild infiltration in 20 dogs, moderate infiltration in 4 dogs, and marked infiltration in 1 dog) and 15 dogs (52%; mild infiltration in 10 dogs, and moderate infiltration in 5 dogs), respectively. Spiral-shaped gastric bacteria (Helicobacter sp.) were detected in 18 dogs (62%).

Treatment

Multiple treatments were pursued with varying success. These treatments included switching to a novel protein diet or an hydrolysed diet in all dogs (n = 29; 100%), adding a gastric acid reducer (omeprazole, esomeprazole, cimetidine or ranitidine) (n = 27; 93%), metoclopramide (n = 20; 69%), steroids (prednisolone) (n = 22; 76%), deworming (milbemycin oxime and/or fenbendazole) (n = 22; 76%), gastric protectant (sucralfate or aluminum phosphate gel) (n = 24; 83%), dimethicone (n = 23; 79%), probiotics (n = 9; 31%), maropitant (n = 20; 69%). A dietary trial was attempted in all dogs, with a median course of 3 different diets (ranging from 2 to 8 different diets). As Helicobacter-associated gastritis is a minor concern in dogs, with little pathogenic significance compared to humans, no eradication treatment, including proton pump inhibitors and combination antimicrobial therapy, has been pursued.

Follow-up

Follow-up information was available for 21 dogs (72%), with a median follow-up period of 12 months (range 1 to 80 months). Among these, sixteen dogs (76%) improved with therapy, characterized by at least a 50% reduction in the frequency of chronic vomiting and an improvement in abdominal discomfort reported by the owner. No specific therapy emerged as consistently more effective in alleviating clinical signs. Only 14 of the 18 dogs (78%) treated with omeprazole therapy, 13 of the 17 dogs (76%) treated with steroids, and 11 of the 15 dogs treated with metoclopramide therapy (73%) showed improvement as defined above. There was no significant difference between gastric histologic inflammation (no or mild lymphoplasmacytic infiltration versus moderate or marked lymphoplasmacytic infiltration) and response to treatment (improvement versus stable or progressive disease) (p-value = 1.00). Similarly, no significant association was found between the presence of duodenal histologic inflammation and treatment response (p = 0.72). Eight dogs were lost to follow-up.

Quality of life

Anxiety and dQoL were assessed in 16 dyspeptic dogs (55%) and in 22 healthy control dogs (Fig. 5). The control group consisted of 10 females (6 neutered) and 12 males (8 neutered), with a median age of 4 years (ranging from 1 to 11 years) and a median weight of 20 kg (ranging from 5.5 to 39.5 kg) across various breeds. There were no significant differences in age (p-value = 0.92) or weight (p-value = 0.31) between the dyspeptic and healthy dogs. When comparing the two groups, there was a statistically significant difference in terms of general dQoL (p-value < 0.001), health dQoL (p-value < 0.001), activity dQoL (p-value = 0.025), interaction dQoL (p-value = 0.006), and owner’s anxiety (p-value = < 0.001) (Table 2). Owners of dyspeptic dogs were more likely anxious, and these dogs exhibited a decreased quality of life compared to healthy dogs. No significant correlation was found between general dQoL and anxiety in dyspeptic dogs (rs=−0.09 and p = 0.74).

Discussion

In our current study, FD in dogs was defined based on the ROME IV consensus for humans as chronic gastrointestinal discomfort in the absence of structural disease that could explain the clinical signs. Given this definition, only dogs with a complete haematological and biochemical workup, abdominal ultrasonography, and gastroscopic examination with biopsy sampling, were included to rule out any organic disease. In human primary care, such thorough investigations are not mandatory for diagnosing FD. Instead, FD is diagnosed on the basis of cardinal dyspeptic criteria, epidemiology, presence of H. pylori infection, and patient history [4, 8, 19]. Specifically, upper gastrointestinal endoscopy is not required to diagnose FD in humans unless alarm signs are present (e.g. advanced age, overt gastrointestinal bleeding, dysphagia, unintentional weight loss, painful swallowing, abdominal mass, persistent vomiting, or family history of oesophageal or gastric cancer) [8]. Excluding dogs that did not undergo gastroscopic examination significantly reduced the number of cases in our study: 23/65 (35%) cases exhibited clinical signs consistent with FD but were excluded due to the absence of upper digestive endoscopy. As reported in humans, the typical signs or cardinal signs of FD seem to be present in our case series, characterized by recurrent or chronic upper digestive disorders. These clinical signs observed in dogs included: (1) chronic vomiting (97%) or regurgitation (21%); (2) the presence of at least 2 other signs of digestive discomfort (97%), such as abdominal pain, mild diarrhoea, pica, chewing, belching, yawning, rumbling, retching, licking, scratching; (3) a typical evolution of FD with an intense clinical picture, progression over several months, and more pronounced and repetitive clinical signs at the end of the night or early morning; and (4) an absence of abnormalities during physical examination (76%) or reported abdominal pain (24%). FD may also be more commonly diagnosed in toy and small breed, middle-aged, female dogs. In humans, meta-analyses on risk factors for FD demonstrated a slightly higher prevalence in women compared with men, especially in North America and Europe [1, 2]. Even not completely understood, the overrepresentation of females in FD could be due to sex-specific biological differences in gastrointestinal function (sex hormone-driven alterations in intestinal motility) or sex-specific behaviour. Female predisposition is also observed in other FGIDs, reinforcing such hypothesis. Age also plays a significant risk factor in humans, as FD incidence seems to disappear with increasing age. The peak incidence of FD occurs in middle-aged individuals (forties or fifties) in humans, which is consistent with our canine series, where the median age was 5 years [4].

In dogs, functional dyspepsia should be regarded as a clinical entity characterized by chronic gastrointestinal discomfort—often compulsive in nature and distressing to the owners—and vomiting in the absence of identifiable organic disease, rather than as a well-defined, specific condition. The overlap with other canine diseases, such as food-responsive enteropathy (FRE), immunosuppressant-responsive enteropathy (IRE), non-responsive enteropathy (NRE), or gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), is a significant concern. These conditions can fit the dyspeptic criteria, suggesting that FD might encompass them to some degree (Fig. 6). Chronic vomiting and abdominal discomfort are part of the spectrum of chronic inflammatory enteropathies, along with diarrhoea, borborygmus, loss of appetite, abdominal pain, nausea, or weight loss [20, 21]. Diagnosis relies on clinical signs after excluding extra-intestinal diseases (renal, hepatic, pancreatic, Addison’s disease, or hypercalcemia), infectious or parasitic diseases, digestive neoplasia or mechanical obstruction [21]. Exclusion of these diseases involves blood workups, imaging examinations, and gastroscopy if conventional therapy fails to improve the condition. Most of these dogs would meet the criteria for dyspepsia, as diarrhoea was transient or minimal, thereby ensuring the inclusion of cases primarily involving upper gastrointestinal disorders. Nevertheless, diet should be the first-line therapy, using a hydrolysed diet or a novel antigen diet, as most of the canine chronic enteropathies (50 to 65%) are responsive to food management [10, 22–24]. Even if dogs suspected of FRE were excluded by at least two previous diet trial, FRE could not be entirely ruled out, even in cases of repeated non-responding trials. Based on the author’s experience within the same hospital over the same period, no dog showed such an atypical clinical presentation with intense and recurrent crisis. Diet has been shown to reduce immunogenicity, optimize gastrointestinal motility, modulate the intestinal microbiota, and favorably affect the biotransformation of bile salts in FRE dogs [10]. Meal ingestion and food composition are also triggering factors for clinical signs in dyspeptic patients. A low FODMAP diet (low in fermentable oligosaccharides, disaccharides, monosaccharides, and polyols), as well as a low-fat diet, can reduce poorly absorbable and highly fermentable substances, and decrease gastric emptying rate, which may alleviate dyspeptic signs [6]. A dietary trial was attempted in all dyspeptic dogs (with a median course of 3 different diets) and could have improved clinical signs. A proportion of FD cases responded, at least partially, to dietary changes and could be subcategorized as food-responsive FD (FR-FD), suggesting FD could be part of the FRE spectrum, especially in anxious dogs. The role of intestinal microbiota remains contradictory in human FD [8]. Based on human management of FD, no fecal microbiota transplantation or cholestyramine therapy were implemented in this canine cohort, though they might have been beneficial in these dogs by decreasing disease activity or correcting bile acid dysmetabolism [25, 26]. Nevertheless, no dog showed a refractory diarrhoea in this cohort. Overlap with bilious vomiting syndrome is also a concern, as a third of dyspeptic dogs (31%) had clinical signs more frequently reported at the end of the night or early morning. These dogs still experienced other abdominal discomfort and might respond to dietary management and therapy, suggesting the involvement of other disease mechanisms. Impaired gastroduodenal motility, characterized by altered gastric emptying and intestinal reflex activity observed in FD, might exhibit similar features to those reported in canine bilious vomiting syndrome [8, 27].

Holistic approach for canine functional dyspepsia combined with other gastrointestinal disorders

Overlap with IRE could still be a concern due to the histologic evidence of inflammation; however, the prevalence of true immunosuppressant-responsive enteropathies is limited in the general population, and in our study, no significant association was found between gastric histologic inflammation and response to treatment, including corticosteroids [10]. Nevertheless, most of the dogs had previously received corticosteroid treatment from the attending veterinarian without notable improvement, and none exhibited a response to immunosuppressive therapy alone. Given this lack of response, they were not classified as having IRE, although a response to other immunosuppressive agents could not be entirely excluded. Gastroduodenal dysmotility might also lead to GERD with clinical signs such as regurgitation, retching, gagging, eructation, lip licking, or abdominal pain, misleading diagnosis of FD [14, 28]. Diagnosis of GERD often relies on clinical signs, as telemetry device monitoring pH or pressure are scarce outside tertiary care centres. Although none of the dogs had oesophageal mucosal lesions compatible with GERD, endoscopy is poorly sensitive and could fail to detect GERD [29]. In humans, studies have showed that up to one-third of dyspeptic patients experience typical signs of GERD [30, 31]. In cases of uncertainty, a GERD diagnosis is more likely to be given than a FD diagnosis [32]. The overlap between these two conditions may be due to a shared pathophysiology, such as delayed gastric emptying and disturbed gastric accommodation. Similarities could be present in dogs, and FD might be part of the GERD spectrum. Two dogs also experienced a slight increase in pancreatic lipase immunoreactivity without ultrasound evidence of pancreatitis. False positive results, in the absence of clinical or imaging suspicion, can occur, as up to 40% of dogs with acute abdomen may show falsely elevated pancreatic lipase immunoreactivity [33]. Furthermore, in the author’s experience, increased pancreatic lipase immunoreactivity is common in dogs with chronic duodenal inflammation. FD also shares common clinical signs with gastroparesis (up to a third of dyspeptic patients have delayed gastric emptying), and could be considered part of the same spectrum of gastroduodenal discomfort in humans [34, 35]. Four dogs (14%) had evidence of slow gastric emptying rate during gastroscopy despite > 12 h fasting, suggesting gastric delay in those dogs, which might have therapeutic implications.

When the digestive tract was evaluated via ultrasonography, wall thickening, especially in the stomach (up to 34%), was a common finding. This may represent a characteristic feature in dogs, where small intestinal wall thickening is neither sensitive nor specific for enteritis, and normal bowel wall thickness does not exclude inflammatory disease [36]. In cases of chronic vomiting, the diagnostic utility of abdominal ultrasonography is rather low. One study reported that ultrasonography was deemed essential or beneficial for diagnosis in only 22.5% of dogs [37]. Although inflammatory cell infiltration of the gastric mucosa is a frequent finding in canine chronic vomiting, the inflammation is seldom limited to the stomach and frequently involves the small intestine, potentially explaining associated ultrasonographic changes. Histopathological examination of the gastric and duodenal mucosa revealed lymphoplasmacytic infiltration in most dyspeptic dogs (86% and 52%, respectively). In the pathophysiology of FD, impaired epithelial barrier function and increased mucosal permeability are thought to contribute to the development of gastroduodenal inflammation [4]. These findings are similar to those in dyspeptic people, two-thirds of whom exhibited mild gastric Lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate, and up to 6% might have marked gastric inflammation without any association with clinical signs [38, 39]. Mild lymphoplasmacytic gastritis is therefore a common histopathological finding and does not necessarily indicate an underlying disorder, as it may be reported in half of dogs without any clinical signs [40]. These results could partly be strengthened by the dog’s behaviour outside, frequently eating indigestible material. The WSAVA histopathological cell counts for diagnosing gastric inflammation might be too restrictive, relying on histological samples from only eight dogs that showed considerable inter-animal variation in cell counts [18, 41]. In human patients, H. pylori is a major concern in the management of FD [4, 19]. Despite limited understanding of the exact mechanisms by which H. pylori contributes to gastroduodenal dysfunction, therapeutic eradication may lead to clinical improvement in a subset of functional dyspepsia patients. The Kyoto consensus, adopted by the Rome IV consensus, suggests the creation of a new entity termed “H. pylori-associated dyspepsia” [42]. Therefore, dyspeptic patients should be tested for H. pylori, either non-invasively or through gastroscopy, and those with H. pylori-positive FD should receive eradication therapy [4]. Gastric spiral-shaped bacteria were detected in 18 dyspeptic dogs (62%) during histologic examination. This prevalence is similar to that found in clinically healthy dogs (67 to 100%) or in dogs presented for the investigation of recurrent vomiting (61 to 95%) [43, 44]. Some studies suggest that the presence of Helicobacter species within the surface epithelium and gastric pits in dogs is not strongly associated with clinically significant disease [43, 44]. To date, the association between Helicobacter-like organisms and gastric inflammation in dogs remains unclear, as mucosal inflammation, glandular degeneration, and lymphoid follicle hyperplasia are present in some infected animals but not in others [43]. Gastric inflammation can persist even with clearance of visible organisms in gastric biopsies [45]. Considering these results, Helicobacter eradication treatment is rarely advocated in dogs, which could represent a major discrepancy between canine and human FD management.

Quality of life was lower in dyspeptic dogs compared to healthy controls, despite the absence of physical abnormalities in most cases during clinical examination. FD may impact not only health-related dQoL, but also activity levels, interactions, and stimulation with the owner. Although dQoL assessment relies on the subjective evaluation by owners, this reinforces the necessity of implementing therapeutic measures to improve clinical signs and enhance owner satisfaction. A decrease in dQoL has also been reported in cases of canine chronic enteropathies, which negatively affect dQoL through persistent clinical signs, frequent veterinary visits, and the need for long-term treatment [17, 46]. dQoL at follow-up is significantly associated with the outcome and is more likely to improve with a complete response to therapy [17, 46]. However, due to the retrospective nature of the study, we were unable to determine such improvement in FD after treatment. In human patients, there is evidence that FD is associated with a significant decrease in quality of life, loss of work productivity, and elevated health expenses [4, 47, 48]. More importantly, the gut-brain axis described in human gastroenterology highlights the complexity of the bidirectional interactions between the central nervous system and the enteric nervous system, connecting the brain’s emotional and cognitive centres with peripheral intestinal functions [49, 50]. The gut microbiota plays an important role in these interactions between the gut and the nervous system, and is a subject of intense research to further understand this microbiota-gut-brain axis, even in dogs [51, 52]. Functional gastrointestinal disorders (FGIDs), including FD, are characterized as complex bidirectional dysregulations of gut-brain interaction without any structural abnormalities. Emotional disorders, including anxiety and depression, are strongly associated with dyspeptic clinical signs, the severity of FD, and impaired gastric accommodation [7, 53–56]. In half of the cases, FGIDs begin with psychological distress, followed by gastrointestinal symptoms, while in the other half, gut dysfunction occurs first, with psychological disorders developing subsequently [57]. Although the exact pathophysiology of FD is unknown, the disruption of the gut-brain axis with altered gut-brain signalling may lead to disturbances of gastric accommodation, gastric emptying, gastroduodenal motility, or visceral hypersensitivity [4, 8]. The gut-brain axis is far less studied in dogs, despite increasing evidence of its impact on canine anxiety disorders [58]. A recent research on 35 chronic enteropathy dogs suggested a link between chronic enteropathy and emotional disorder in dogs, with a higher frequency of displacement behaviours and lower scores for energy and interest [59]. Owner anxiety and high-to-intense involvement might play a significant role in onset of dyspeptic clinical signs in dogs, and owners often reported a marked clinical picture that is out of step with the results of investigations. Due to their ability to interpret human non-verbal cues, particularly through their superior olfactory processing network, dogs can accurately detect anxiety and stress in their owners, which can, in turn, influence the dogs’ own behaviour and decision-making processes [60–62]. Prior research has shown that exposure to human odours associated with fear or anxiety can induce stress-related behaviours and increased heart rate in dogs [63]. This response highlights the close emotional relation between dogs and humans and emphasizes the impact of human emotional states on canine behaviour. In our study, anxiety levels were higher in FD dogs compared to healthy dogs, similar to findings in chronic enteropathies, with a higher level of attachment between the owner and the dog [17]. Many of those dogs spent a long time a day in the arms of their owners. As in humans, anxiety may act as a triggering factor for dyspeptic signs in dogs and could contribute to the development of abdominal discomfort, particularly in toy breeds.

The management of functional dyspepsia is particularly challenging due to the lack of a clearly identifiable organic lesion, necessitating prolonged symptomatic treatment and regular clinical monitoring [64]. In human patients, achieving satisfactory relief of clinical signs is an important objective in the treatment of FD, and maintaining a confident patient-physician relationship is essential [19]. Helicobacter pylori testing and eradication therapy are recommended as first-line interventions to diagnose H. pylori-associated functional dyspepsia and to reduce the risk of preneoplastic lesions and peptic ulcer disease. H. pylori-positive FD patients should receive eradication therapy, based on the combination of a potent acid suppressant (e.g. proton pump inhibitors) and antibiotics (e.g. clarithromycin and amoxicillin or, alternatively, metronidazole) [4, 42, 65]. For patients who test negative for H. pylori, or those with functional dyspepsia who remain symptomatic following eradication therapy, first-line management includes lifestyle and dietary modifications combined with a daily dose of proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) for 4 to 8 weeks [4, 66]. Dietary modifications typically include frequent, small meals and the avoidance of high-fat foods, which are known to delay gastric emptying. Additionally, a low-FODMAP diet may help alleviate dyspeptic symptoms, as may adherence to a Mediterranean diet [6]. The rationale for prokinetic therapy in functional dyspepsia remains controversial; however, it may be considered as a second-line treatment, particularly in cases where gastroduodenal dysmotility is suspected or in patients who are unresponsive to PPIs [4]. These drugs include D2 antagonists, acetylcholinesterase inhibitors, or 5-HT4 receptor agonists [19, 66]. Based on these recommendations, treatment of dyspeptic dogs in our study encompassed diet management with the introduction of a novel protein or a hydrolysed diet (100%), addition of antacids (93%), and prokinetics (69%). Most dogs (78%) receiving omeprazole, regardless of concomitant therapy, showed improvement (reduction of clinical signs), possibly reflecting similar pathophysiological mechanisms to those in humans. The use of metoclopramide, beneficial for delayed gastric emptying or impaired gastric accommodation, is also highly effective in alleviating dyspeptic signs in both dogs and humans. Steroids (prednisolone) have been widely used in dogs (76%), due to the presence of gastric inflammatory infiltration, but their use was not significantly associated with clinical improvement, and previous use of steroids did not provide additional benefit of their use. Furthermore, the severity of inflammation was not correlated with treatment outcome. Despite the theoretical benefit of steroids in chronic inflammatory conditions, our results do not justify their use in these cases. Consequently, considering data from human patients and the lack of evidence in this population, the use of steroids in canine FD remains questionable. Antifoaming agents, commonly used to reduce bloating in general practice, have demonstrated efficacy in reducing overall gastrointestinal discomfort in FD patients [67]. Their use in canine dyspepsia (79%) as adjunct therapy may also be beneficial, according to the owners’ observations. Finally, probiotics, administered in 31% of cases, may be beneficial in certain dogs, possibly by exerting effects on the intestinal microbiota composition and function [10]. Its application in humans is also gaining increasing importance [68]. Currently, while evidence to recommend specific treatments for canine FD in everyday clinical practice is currently lacking, none of these therapies has proven superior to others. Chronic vomiting and abdominal discomfort improved in three-quarters of the dogs, indicating that an individual approach may be more effective than the general guidelines are. These therapies, to some extent, encompass the management of FRE (with diet trials), IRE (with corticosteroids) and GERD (with prokinetics and antacids), highlighting the overlap with these diseases. Place of anxiety and emotional disorders in both humans and canine FDs raises the question of antipsychotic drugs. While such medications were not widely used in our case series, tricyclic antidepressants and anxiolytics are considered second-line therapies for human FD [4, 8, 66].

The limitations of our study include its retrospective design, reliance on hospital records, the small sample size, and the lack of standardized dietary and therapeutic options. This pilot study aimed to provide a comprehensive diagnostic approach by integrating clinical signs with the absence of major abnormalities through thorough investigations (including biochemical panels, ultrasonography, gastroscopy, and histology). However, our diagnostic strategy may have missed cases of FRE, microbiota-responsive enteropathy, IRE, or gastric motility issues, including GERD. Future prospective studies could benefit from assessing dysbiosis index or oesophageal and gastric pH with telemetric device, which could be complicated to interpret as clinical signs may be intermittent. Additionally, our study may have selected more severely affected dogs, with highly involved owners, due to inclusion of dogs with gastroscopic results within our referral population. Drawing from human data, future prospective studies with larger canine cohorts could consider omitting routine gastroscopy in cases lacking alarm features, thereby facilitating the inclusion of milder clinical presentations. Considering the significant role of anxiety in the onset of FD, it is crucial to incorporate prospectively a standardized evaluation of anxiety, behavioural issues, relational factors, and quality of life using validated questionnaires and scores. A comparative analysis with a control group of dogs affected by FRE and IRE could help elucidate differing clinical expressions of analogous gastrointestinal disorders. Furthermore, extensive follow-up of FD dogs in a prospective study could offer valuable insight into the management and tailored treatment approaches for FD, improving our understanding and therapeutic strategies for canine FD.

Conclusion

FD is an increasingly recognized functional gastrointestinal disorder in dogs, predominantly affecting middle-aged, small and toy-breed females. It is marked by chronic vomiting and abdominal discomfort without structural or metabolic abnormalities likely to fully explain the clinical signs. The clinical presentation often includes severe episodes of gastrointestinal distress and anxious owners, despite largely unremarkable diagnostic evaluations. Anxiety in the owner and a decline in the dog’s quality of life have been linked to the development of FD, potentially acting as triggers for dyspeptic clinical signs. These associations may reflect an underlying disruption of the gut–brain axis, which has been implicated in impaired gastric accommodation, delayed emptying, and visceral hypersensitivity. In contrast to human medicine, Helicobacter species do not appear to be central to the pathogenesis of canine functional dyspepsia. Instead, FD in dogs likely overlaps pathophysiologically with conditions such as chronic inflammatory enteropathies and gastroduodenal dysmotility, and may be further influenced by emotional or behavioural comorbidities. When managed with tailored therapeutic approaches, the long-term outcome is generally favorable in most canine FD cases. However, prospective studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to advance our understanding of the condition, elucidate the role of gut dysbiosis, and optimize treatment protocols.
