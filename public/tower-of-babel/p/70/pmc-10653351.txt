To “Trim” or to “Wedge”?
Authors: Michael P Goodman
Published: 2023

I read with appreciation the Herculean effort successfully undertaken by Zahedi et al1 and Dr Nugent's perceptive commentary.2

Being equally comfortable with both linear and wedge techniques for labial reductive and reconstruction procedures is advantageous for the consummate female genital cosmetic and reconstructive surgeon. Both are reproducible, viable techniques. Often the difficulty, especially for the female genital cosmetic surgeon early in her or his career, is the decision as to which technique to utilize for a given patient with her unique anatomy, outcome desires, and recovery realities.

Toward these ends, I offer the following additions to Zahedi et al's useful algorithm:

As noted, wedges “tighten”; they “lift” and tend to correct ptosis and therefore may be the technique of choice for patients with significant redundancy, ptosis, and “stretchy” labia if recovery realities can be met (see below). Lateral wedge lines may be “fluid” as the anatomy and ptosis demand.

As any experienced female genital cosmetic and reconstructive surgeon knows, “anatomy” is all over the map, and frequently contralateral sides do not match. For patients with very robust prepucial folds, especially if these anchor laterally into the minora near the leading edge, a linear incision may cross this fold, and hood decompression may result in a “T’d” incision. Therefore, a wedge may be a good choice for this frequently encountered anatomic variant.

I agree that the eventual outcome of a labiaplasty is not entirely dependent on the surgeon's expertise. With its propensity to dehisce if not adequately “scaffolded,” a wedge recovery is more dependent on patient compliance compared with a linear-type technique. Initial care and rest, and proscription of certain activities such as squatting, brisk ambulation, stairs, and other vigorous activities, are more important for a wedge recovery than for a linear approach. Therefore, as an additional tenet of the “trim vs wedge” algorithm, the surgeon might be advised to take into account each individual patient's ability to follow the stricter recovery protocol that a wedge demands.

Not strictly protocol related, but related to Dr Nugent's comment regarding the increased edema noted in a wedge approach, I have begun utilizing low-dose corticosteroids (dexamethasone 2 mg/day beginning immediately preoperatively and continuing for 4 additional days) and anecdotally have noted less postoperative edema with this protocol.
