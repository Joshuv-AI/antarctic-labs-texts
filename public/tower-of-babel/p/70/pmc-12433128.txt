Debunking a one-size-fits-all in acute respiratory failure: lessons from the RENOVATE trial
Authors: Dominic C. Marshall, Malcolm Lemyze
Published: 2025

Acute respiratory failure (ARF) frequently necessitates escalation beyond conventional oxygen therapy (1). Non-invasive ventilation (NIV) is well-established, particularly for acute hypercapnic respiratory failure. Yet its use, particularly outside the intensive care unit (ICU), can be constrained by mask intolerance, air leaks, and the need for staff experienced in titrating pressures (2). There is an expanding use of high-flow oxygen via nasal cannula (HFNC) because it is quick to set up, better tolerated, and allows patients to speak, eat, and clear secretions (3). HFNC delivers warmed, humidified gas at up to 60 L/min⁻1, matching inspiratory demand, flushing nasopharyngeal dead space and stabilising FiO2. At these flows the pharyngeal pressure rises by 2–3 cmH2O, lower than the 5–10 cmH2O positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) typically produced by NIV (4). Yet several studies demonstrate that even this modest rise elevates pharyngeal and oesophageal pressures throughout the respiratory cycle, potentially offsetting auto-PEEP in obstructive disease, increasing end-expiratory lung volume and reducing inspiratory effort, thereby fulfilling the physiological definition of applied PEEP (4-7).

What do we know about HFNC so far?

Since 2015 several randomised and pragmatic studies have shown that in selected population HFNC, compared with conventional oxygen therapy, reduces intubation in moderate-to-severe AHRF (8-10). Meta-analyses published in 2023–2024 confirm these benefits without evidence of increasing mortality (3,11). Evidence directly comparing HFNC with NIV, however, is sparse and heterogeneous, leaving clinicians uncertain how to choose first-line support.

The RENOVATE study, a large multicentre randomized control trial recently published in JAMA, sheds light on the ongoing debate surrounding HFNC and NIV. Brazilian investigators randomized (in a 1:1 ratio) 1,800 patients from 33 institutions either to receive NIV or HFNC to treat ARF of different aetiologies (12). Patients were recruited mainly in the emergency department or in the ICU, and categorised into five different phenotypes: acute hypercapnic decompensation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (n=77), coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) (n=882), type I (hypoxaemic) ARF from cardiogenic pulmonary oedema (ACPE) (n=272), immunocompromised patients in type I ARF (n=50), and non-immunocompromised with other causes of type I ARF (n=485). The trial used an adaptive Bayesian design with dynamic borrowing across groups: essentially allowing the analysis to share information between patient subgroups if their outcomes appeared similar, while also permitting early stopping for futility or success within any group. The primary outcome was a composite of intubation or death within 7 days. RENOVATE assessed NIV exclusively as face-mask bilevel positive airway pressure, omitting other modes such as continuous positive airway pressure, average volume-assured pressure support, and helmet interfaces. Randomised trials report lower intubation with helmet NIV vs. HFNC (HENIVOT) and lower intubation and mortality with helmet vs. facemask NIV in acute respiratory distress syndrome (13,14).

Overall, HFNC was found to be non-inferior to NIV for the 7-day intubation/death outcome in the primary analysis of combined subgroups (12). One thousand seven hundred and sixty-six patients completed the study with the primary outcome occurring in ~39% of the HFNC group vs. ~38% of the NIV group. In four of the five subgroups, HFNC met the pre-specified criteria for non-inferiority compared to NIV. In the immunocompromised subgroup, which comprised only 50 patients, the non-inferiority threshold was not met, with the primary endpoint occurring in 57.1% and 36.4% of the HFNC and NIV groups, respectively. Although the analytic framework formally allowed for a superiority claim, the study was not powered to achieve it. No subgroup reached the prespecified posterior-probability threshold of 0.992; the ACPE cohort (n=272) came closest [odds ratio (OR) 0.52, posterior P=0.97], a signal more consistent with limited sample size than with a definitive treatment advantage. There were also notable secondary outcomes with patients receiving HFNC reporting lower levels of discomfort and fewer mask-related complications. The large number of patients enrolled across a variety of centres, supports the generalisability of the study’s findings. However, despite a rigorous design, we have three primary comments. Firstly, the heterogeneity of the population, particularly with respect to hypercapnic COPD exacerbations. Second, but related to this is the statistical approach and the use of dynamic borrowing. Third, the exclusion of important subgroups of patients commonly admitted for ARF in the emergency setting.

Uncertain interpretation regarding hypercapnic COPD exacerbations

COPD with hypercapnic respiratory failure constitutes a unique phenotype in which ventilatory support rather than oxygenation is required. In these patients NIV is lifesaving, rapidly correcting acidaemia and halving intubations (15). By contrast, HFNC offers only modest dead-space washout and pharyngeal pressure and may provide insufficient ventilatory unloading in moderate–severe acidosis. A 2022 systematic review judged the evidence inconclusive (16), and a recent randomised controlled trial (RCT) in COPD with pH 7.25–7.35 found HFNC inferior to NIV, doubling the intubation rate (14% vs. 5%) (17). RENOVATE, however, enrolled just 77 COPD patients, reported an unexpectedly high NIV failure rate of 26%, and on average delivered more than twice as many hours of HFNC as NIV by day 3 (median duration by day 3: 22 vs. 3 h). Twenty-two percent of HFNC-assigned COPD patients ultimately crossed over to NIV. Together, the small sample, unequal “dose”, and cross-over mean the COPD signal in RENOVATE is exploratory at best and should not overturn the current NIV-first standard without larger, phenotype-specific trials.

Is the cohort too heterogenous for Bayesian borrowing?

The Bayesian hierarchical model with dynamic borrowing is both a strength and a weakness of RENOVATE. By allowing information to “flow” between subgroups assumed to be biologically similar, it delivered the statistical power to test non-inferiority with a feasible overall sample. The trade-off is shrinkage: subgroup estimates are pulled toward the trial-wide mean. When the investigators repeated the analysis without borrowing, odds-ratio estimates in COPD, immunocompromised patients and ACPE shifted materially, sometimes crossing the non-inferiority margin (12). This divergence signals that the key modelling assumption (exchangeability) may be breached (18); in simpler terms, one size may not fit all forms of ARF. As the COPD (n=77) and immunocompromised (n=50) strata were small, the borrowing model may have over-smoothed genuine between-phenotype differences, whereas the no-borrowing model was under-powered and likely imprecise. Clinicians should therefore treat subgroup findings as hypothesis-generating, not practice-changing, until phenotype-specific trials or large real-world registries replicate them. A further key point of interpretation is that the non-inferiority relied on a 10% absolute margin (OR 1.55), a relatively wide threshold for an endpoint as consequential as intubation.

Who wasn’t included?

The RENOVATE study encompasses many phenotypes of patients admitted for ARF and at first glance, the results may be considered as being extrapolated to all patients needing a non-invasive ventilatory support. However, hypercapnic patients were only represented by COPD patients and patients with a do not intubate order were excluded from this trial.

In contemporary UK audit data, obesity hypoventilation syndrome (OHS) is the second most common indication for acute NIV after COPD (67% vs. 8%), exceeding cardiogenic pulmonary oedema (7%) (19). Hypercapnic obese patients were not studied in the RENOVATE trial, perhaps because they were considered to need higher PEEP than can be delivered by HFNC. NIV is still the first-line ventilatory support intervention recommended for the obese hypercapnic patients, where it is possibly more effective than in COPD (2,20). Reported levels of pressures required for NIV to reverse the ARF episode of OHS reached a median expiratory positive airway pressure of 9 cmH2O and a median inspiratory positive airway pressure of 18 cmH2O, a level of support unachievable by HFNC (20,21).

The exclusion of patients with treatment limitation decisions especially those with a do-not-intubate order further limits the generalisability of this study. These patients are an important group in whom the use of NIV and HFNC is ever increasing. In clinical practice, once NIV is started in the acute care setting, the British Thoracic Society recommends rapidly determining if NIV is the ceiling of care through a shared decision-making process (22). If a treatment limitation decision is made, then NIV failure would not be defined anymore as the need to resort to intubation, but rather as the need to transition to palliative care. This could have been considered in the composite primary endpoint of the RENOVATE study, if do-not-intubate patients had not been excluded from the trial. Future studies should consider these frail patients with chronic respiratory failure or multiple comorbidities, with do-not-intubate orders, as they represent up to 25% of those treated by non-invasive ventilatory support in the ICU, and with many surviving to hospital discharge (23,24).

Based on the RENOVATE trial, is HFNC a suitable first line option for all patients in respiratory distress?

RENOVATE demonstrates that for most patients with de-novo hypoxaemic ARF, high-flow nasal oxygen can be started safely without worsening intubation or 7-day mortality. Evidence is weaker for phenotypes that require ventilatory unloading, acute-on-chronic hypercapnia (COPD, obesity hypoventilation) or cases managed under do-not-intubate goals, because these groups were small or excluded. Until dedicated trials address them, NIV should remain first-line where hypercapnia or treatment-limitation plans apply. Future studies should consider stratifying by ventilatory vs. oxygenation failure and report device “dose,” crossover, and patient-centred endpoints to clarify when HFNC can truly replace NIV.
