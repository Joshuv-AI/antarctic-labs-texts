Panvascular Changes Under Intensive Medical Therapy in Intracranial Atherosclerotic Steno‐Occlusive Disease
Authors: WeiZhuang Yuan, Ming‐Li Li, Yue‐Lun Zhang, Zhongrui Yan, Wei‐Hai Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile intensive medical therapy improves culprit plaque characteristics in intracranial atherosclerotic steno‐occlusive disease, its broader effects on the intracranial arterial tree, venous pathways, and meningeal lymphatic system remain unclear. We explored longitudinal panvascular changes in patients with intracranial atherosclerotic steno‐occlusive disease under intensive therapy.MethodsWe included patients with a first‐ever intracranial atherosclerotic steno‐occlusive disease–related stroke who received intensive medical therapy and underwent multimodal magnetic resonance imaging at baseline and 6‐month follow‐up. Quantitative assessments included plaque burden and lumen area at the culprit artery, global arterial tree morphology, internal jugular vein area, and meningeal lymphatic vessel volume. Associations between low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol reduction and vascular metrics were analyzed using adjusted generalized additive models.ResultsAmong 24 enrolled patients, plaque burden at the site of maximal stenosis in the culprit artery decreased significantly (mean difference, −5.24% [95% CI, −9.25% to −1.23%]). Global arterial tree length (mean difference, 278.89 mm [95% CI, 26.60–531.18 mm]) and volume (mean difference, 411.38 mm3 [95% CI, 36.9–785.87 mm3]) increased, while the mean arterial diameter decreased (mean difference, −0.05 mm [95% CI, −0.08 to −0.01 mm]). Meningeal lymphatic vessel volume also expanded (mean difference, 337.42 mm3 [95% CI, 73.08–601.75 mm3]). No significant changes were observed in the cross‐sectional area of the internal jugular vein. Greater reduction in low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol was associated with more pronounced improvements among these panvascular metrics.ConclusionsThese findings reveal coordinated arterial and lymphatic changes beyond local plaque regression. Future studies are warranted to further explore these systemic changes and their clinical implications.

Intracranial atherosclerotic steno‐occlusive disease (ICAS) is a common cause of ischemic stroke worldwide. 1 Despite advances in acute reperfusion strategies and secondary prevention, ICAS continues to present therapeutic challenges, with high rates of stroke recurrence and long‐term disability. 2 Conventional understanding of ICAS has largely centered on focal arterial pathology, including culprit plaque formation, progressive luminal stenosis, and the subsequent risk of thromboembolic or hemodynamic stroke. 3 However, emerging evidence suggests that this lesion‐centric view may underestimate the systemic nature of intracranial vascular disease. 4 , 5

The brain's vasculature constitutes an integrated network of arteries, collaterals, veins, and meningeal lymphatic vessels that collectively maintain cerebrovascular homeostasis. 6 Beyond supplying and draining blood, these compartments dynamically interact to regulate perfusion, waste clearance, and immune surveillance. 6 Impairment in any component, such as inadequate collateralization, venous congestion, or lymphatic dysfunction, can exacerbate ischemic injury or hinder recovery. 4 , 5 Thus, ICAS may represent not merely a focal arterial lesion but a manifestation of global vascular dysregulation within the intracranial environment.

Intensive medical therapy has been shown to induce regression of culprit plaques, vessel wall remodeling, and improved perfusion, which are all associated with reduced stroke recurrence. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 However, whether such therapy triggers adaptive responses among the broader intracranial vascular‐lymphatic network remains unclear. In this prospective cohort study, we utilized multimodal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to characterize panvascular changes under intensive medical therapy in ICAS longitudinally. We quantified changes in plaque burden, lumen area, arterial tree morphology, internal jugular vein area, and meningeal lymphatic volume, and explored their associations with low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C) reduction. By adopting a panvascular framework, this study aims to move beyond the lesion‐centric paradigm and elucidate how systemic therapy reshapes intracranial vascular and lymphatic architecture to support vascular homeostasis and recovery.

METHODS

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Study Design and Participants

Patients in the current study were selected from a multicenter, prospective, cohort study conducted at 15 tertiary‐level centers between November 1, 2018, and June 30, 2021, named the SIPS‐ICAS (Stroke Imaging Package Study of Intracranial Atherosclerosis). 7 , 13 SIPS‐ICAS recruited patients with first‐ever acute ischemic stroke attributed to ICAS within 7 days after onset. Patients were recommended to receive high‐dose statin therapy with rosuvastatin 20 mg for more than 6 months, dual‐antiplatelet therapy for 90 days, and risk factor management in accordance with clinical guidelines. 14 The study was approved by the institutional review board at Peking Union Medical College Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (JS‐1699), and the local participating centers' ethics boards. All patients or their legal representatives provided informed consent. The detailed study design was previously described. 7 , 13

Patients were included in this study if they: (1) underwent both baseline and follow‐up multimodal MRI; (2) had 3‐dimensional (3D) T1‐weighted black‐blood MRI scans that were contrast‐enhanced and covered the whole brain; and (3) received intensive medical therapy, including high‐dose statin treatment with rosuvastatin for at least 6 months and dual antiplatelet therapy for at least 3 weeks.

Imaging Analysis

Patients from the SIPS‐ICAS underwent a stroke imaging package including conventional cranial MRI, 3D time‐of‐flight magnetic resonance angiography (TOF‐MRA), susceptibility‐weighted imaging or T2*‐weighted imaging, and 3D T1‐weighted black‐blood MRI. Arterial spin labeling MRI, 2‐dimensional T2‐weighted black‐blood MRI of intracranial arteries, and contrast‐enhanced 3D T1‐weighted black‐blood MRI were optional if available. Repeat black‐blood MRI was performed at 6 months (median interval: 26 weeks; interquartile range, 25–27 weeks) on the same machine to ensure comparability. Standardized protocols of imaging were previously described. 7 , 13 Traditional imaging parameters, meningeal lymphatics, vascular tree, and venous structures were evaluated using multimodal imaging, as described in the supplementary material, with representative patient images shown in Figure 1.

Assessment of Traditional Imaging Parameters

The culprit intracranial artery was assessed using TOF‐MRA. If >1 stenosis was present within a culprit segment, the narrowest portion was selected. Stenosis was graded as moderate (51–69%), severe (70–99%), or occlusion (100% with absent TOF‐MRA signal). 15 Symptomatic ICAS was then assessed on 3D T1‐weighted black‐blood MRI. Quantitative measurements of lumen and vessel areas at the maximal stenotic site (in the absence of thrombotic occlusion) were obtained from 3D T1‐weighted black‐blood MRI. Plaque burden was further calculated as: (vessel area−lumen area)/vessel area×100%.

The total cerebral small vessel disease burden was evaluated using a validated 0 to 4 scale, with 1 point assigned for each of the following: lacunes, cerebral microbleeds, moderate to severe enlarged perivascular spaces in the basal ganglia (grades 2–4), and severe white matter hyperintensities (periventricular grade 3 or deep white matter grade 2 or 3). 16

Meningeal Lymphatics, Vascular Tree, and Venous Assessment

Meningeal lymphatic vessels were identified as enhancing structures adjacent to the superior sagittal sinus, visible on coronal contrast‐enhanced 3D T1‐weighted black‐blood images, as previously described. 17 , 18 After rigid registration, the precontrast black‐blood images were subtracted from the postcontrast black‐blood images. For postprocessing of the subtracted black‐blood images, pixels within an inverted triangle‐shaped region of interest (drawn from the midportion at the eyeball level to include the parasagittal dural space) were sorted according to their signal intensity. Initially, pixels with intensities >50% of the maximum intensity within the large region of interest were defined as meningeal lymphatic vessels. The initial threshold ratio of 50% was manually adjusted based on the resulting segmentation, with adjustments not exceeding 10%. 17 To ensure longitudinal consistency and minimize bias, several methodological improvements were implemented. First, rigid registration was applied to align baseline and follow‐up postcontrast black‐blood images, allowing the use of a single region of interest for both scans. Second, the same patient‐specific threshold was applied to both the baseline and follow‐up images. The measurement procedure is illustrated in Figure S1.

For the intracranial vascular tree, an automated segmentation algorithm, FFCM‐MRF (Fast Fuzzy C‐Means Clustering With Markov Random Field Refinement), was applied to extract the vascular network. 19 Briefly, TOF‐MRA images were first preprocessed with skull stripping, denoising, and bias‐field correction. Then, a novel method integrating FFCM‐MRF optimization by vessel shape priors and spatial constraints was employed for vascular segmentation. Finally, quantitative parameters including mean vessel diameter, total vascular length, and vascular volume were calculated across the whole brain (Figure S2).

The use of 3D T1‐weighted black‐blood MRI for the visualization and measurement of intracranial veins has been previously reported. 20 , 21 We applied this technique to measure venous structures by adapting the analytical methods previously established in magnetic resonance venography studies. 22 The cross‐sectional area of bilateral internal jugular veins was measured at the jugular foramen level. Rigid registration was applied to align baseline and follow‐up scans, ensuring measurements at identical anatomical levels. Left, right, and total venous areas were calculated.

Quantification of the global arterial tree was performed in MATLAB (version 2024a). The cross‐sectional area of the bilateral internal jugular veins and the meningeal lymphatic volume were measured using 3D Slicer (version 5.8.1). Conventional imaging parameters were evaluated in OsiriX MD, version 9.02 (Pixmeo SARL). Two independent readers, a neurologist (W.Y., 8 years of experience) and a radiologist (M.L., 17 years of experience), analyzed the images and were blinded to the clinical information and other measurements. The optimal segmentation threshold for the meningeal lymphatic vessels was jointly determined by W.Y. and M.L. to ensure the most accurate visualization of the lymphatic structures. Interobserver agreement for the cross‐sectional area of the internal jugular veins was excellent (right side: ICC=0.99 [95% CI, 0.97–1.00]; left side: ICC=0.92 [95% CI, 0.78–0.97]; overall: ICC=0.98 [95% CI, 0.95–0.99]), as determined from 15 independently assessed slices. The interobserver reliability for traditional imaging parameters has been previously established and considered excellent in our prior studies. 7 , 13

Statistical Analysis

Longitudinal changes were assessed using paired t tests. To further explore sequential associations between medical therapy and panvasculature changes, generalized additive models (GAMs) with thin plate regression splines as the smoothing basis were used to visualize the relationships between changes in vascular metrics and changes in LDL‐C at 6 months, which was used as a proxy for treatment response. GAMs were adjusted for age and sex and were conducted for vascular metrics that showed significant pre–post differences. All statistical tests were 2‐sided, with P<0.05 considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed in R version 4.03 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing; http://www.r‐project.org).

RESULTS

Patient Characteristics

From an initial cohort of 255 patients in the SIPS‐ICAS, our analysis included only those from centers using contrast‐enhanced, whole‐brain black‐blood MRI protocols. This criterion excluded 104 patients from 8 centers (noncontrast MRI) and 124 patients from 6 centers (incomplete brain coverage). Of the remaining 27 patients, 3 were lost to follow‐up at 6 months. Therefore, the final study population comprised 24 patients (Figure S3).

The mean age of study participants was 57.0 ± 12.1 years, and 62.5% were men. Additional baseline characteristics are shown in Table S1. All patients completed 6 months of intensive statin therapy and at least 3 weeks of dual antiplatelet therapy (with 11 patients continuing up to 90 days). Patients with hypertension or diabetes received appropriate treatments according to guideline recommendations. During the 12‐month clinical follow‐up period, 1 patient experienced a recurrent stroke.

Significant improvements were observed in lipid control and plaque characteristics after medical therapy (Table1Table). LDL‐C levels decreased from 2.58 ± 1.16 mmol/L at baseline to 2.03 ± 0.85 mmol/L at 6 months (P=0.045). Changes in Meningeal Lymphatics, Arterial Tree, and Venous Structures

As detailed in the Table 1, quantitative analysis of the intracranial vascular tree showed a significant increase in total vascular length (mean difference, 278.89 mm [95% CI, 26.60−531.18 mm]; P=0.032) and vascular volume (mean difference, 411.38 mm3 [95% CI, 36.9−785.87 mm3]; P=0.033), while mean vessel diameter decreased significantly (mean difference, −0.05 mm [95% CI, −0.08 to −0.01 mm]; P=0.024). Meningeal lymphatic volume also increased significantly (mean difference, 337.42 mm3 [95% CI, 73.08−601.75 mm3]; P=0.015). No significant changes were observed in venous cross‐sectional area (mean difference, 2.94 mm2 [95% CI, −0.47 to 6.35 mm2]; P=0.088).

Association Between LDL‐C Reduction and Panvascular Percentage Changes

To further investigate the relationship between treatment response and panvascular structure, we modeled the percentage changes in vascular metrics using LDL‐C reduction at 6 months as a proxy of therapeutic effect (Figure 2). After adjusting for age and sex, greater reductions in LDL‐C were associated with larger percentage decreases in plaque burden and greater percentage increases in vascular length, vascular volume, and lymphatic volume. Mean vessel diameter showed no early change but exhibited a delayed percentage decrease with greater LDL‐C reduction. No consistent trend was observed for lumen area.

DISCUSSION

This prospective cohort study revealed that intensive medical therapy in patients with ICAS was accompanied by structural changes among multiple vascular compartments. We observed regression of culprit plaques with corresponding lumen enlargement, remodeling of the global arterial tree, expansion of meningeal lymphatic vessels, and a relatively stable venous drainage pattern. Moreover, the extent of LDL‐C reduction was associated with the magnitude of panvascular improvements, highlighting that therapeutic effects extend beyond culprit lesions and involve systemic vascular and lymphatic remodeling.

Plaque regression and lumen enlargement are consistent with prior studies showing that lipid‐lowering therapy can induce favorable changes in culprit arteries. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 Beyond culprit lesions, our study demonstrated structural changes of the distal intracranial arterial tree, characterized by increased vascular length and volume, together with a subtle reduction in mean vessel diameter. The observed arterial tree changes likely reflect 2 complementary processes. Regression of the culprit plaque with lumen enlargement restores distal perfusion pressure and wall shear stress, promoting arteriogenesis through shear stress–mediated endothelial remodeling and recruitment of preexisting collateral channels, thereby rendering previously underperfused distal vessels detectable on TOF‐MRA and increasing measurable arterial length and volume. 23 , 24 In parallel, high‐dose statins may further facilitate distal vascular remodeling via lipid‐independent, endothelium‐protective effects, such as enhanced nitric oxide bioavailability and reduced vascular inflammation, creating a microenvironment conducive to neovascularization. 25 , 26 These findings may suggest a redistribution of flow and potential recruitment or remodeling of distal small arteries, which may enhance collateral capacity and improve microcirculatory reserve.

Another novel finding was the expansion of meningeal lymphatic vessel volume following therapy. The meningeal lymphatic system has been increasingly recognized as a pathway for waste clearance and immune regulation. 5 , 27 Enlargement of these vessels may reflect improved clearance capacity and attenuation of vascular inflammation, processes that could contribute to more favorable long‐term outcomes. 5 The association with LDL‐C reduction raises the possibility that lipid‐lowering exerts anti‐inflammatory effects not only within arterial walls but also in the lymphatic system, providing a novel possible mechanistic link between systemic therapy and intracranial homeostasis. 5 , 27

We did not detect significant changes in the internal jugular veins during the study period. This negative finding may reflect the relative insensitivity of jugular vein cross‐sectional measurements to subtle hemodynamic shifts, or the fact that venous structures are less responsive to short‐term pharmacological interventions compared with arteries and lymphatics. These considerations suggest that more advanced venous imaging modalities and longer follow‐up may be necessary to fully characterize venous responses in ICAS.

Taken together, these findings expand the understanding of ICAS from a lesion‐centric disorder to a condition involving systemic vascular and lymphatic changes. The clinical significance of these panvascular findings warrant emphasis. Conventional monitoring of therapeutic response in ICAS relies primarily on serial assessment of stenosis severity and plaque burden at the culprit site. However, clinical outcomes do not always parallel local plaque changes, potentially due to persistent dysfunction in the downstream vascular network or impaired cerebrovascular reserve. Our findings raise the possibility that quantitative metrics of global arterial tree morphology and meningeal lymphatic volume may serve as complementary monitoring tools, capturing the full breadth of therapeutic effects that extend beyond the culprit lesion. Integrating these panvascular biomarkers into future clinical trials and routine follow‐up imaging frameworks may offer a more comprehensive and mechanistically informative assessment of therapeutic efficacy than culprit lesion metrics alone.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was small, which limits statistical power. For example, the nonsignificant venous changes may reflect true biological stability or insufficient power to detect subtle changes. The small sample also precluded subgroup analyses of potential clinical interest, such as whether panvascular changes differ according to stenosis location (anterior versus posterior circulation), degree of baseline stenosis (moderate versus severe), among other factors. Second, venous assessment was limited to internal jugular veins and dedicated magnetic resonance venography was not performed. Subtle or region‐specific venous changes may have been overlooked. Third, arterial tree reconstruction relied on 3T TOF‐MRA, which may underestimate distal branches compared with 7T or contrast‐enhanced imaging. Further, selection bias may exist, as the requirement for contrast‐enhanced, whole‐brain MRI protocols may preclude patients with greater disease severity who are unable to tolerate more comprehensive imaging procedures, potentially limiting the generalizability of our findings. Last, the absence of a healthy control group represents an important limitation. We therefore cannot definitively exclude the contribution of natural temporal variability to the observed panvascular changes. Future studies with larger multicenter cohorts, standardized imaging protocols among all participating centers, and age‐ and sex‐matched healthy control groups are needed to validate these panvascular metrics as imaging biomarkers. Longer follow‐up periods will also be required to characterize the temporal trajectory of arterial tree and lymphatic changes, and prospective linkage of these imaging metrics with clinical outcomes, including stroke recurrence, functional recovery, and cognitive trajectory, will be essential to establish their prognostic relevance in ICAS.
