An Innovative Multifaceted Approach to Enhance the Sensitivity of a Target Engagement ELISA
Authors: Nahong Qiu, Alexandra Nitsche, Isabelle Beley, Alessio Maiolica, Jennifer Cunliffe, Julie De Gagné
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTarget engagement (TE) assessments often come with bioanalytical challenges, particularly with low baseline level targets like cytokines and chemokines. Commercial immunoassay kits often cannot meet the requirements of sensitivity and drug interference. We present a novel methodology that significantly enhances the sensitivity of a conventional sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for TE assessment. To increase sensitivity, various strategies were applied, including the combined use of U-bottomed assay plates and Intelligent Multifunctional Analytical Plates (IMAPlate™). Lowering volumes of enzyme substrate and stop solution in U-bottomed plate led to a concentrated color solution. The unique bottom-less "well" of the IMAPlate™ was utilized to measure the intensified color solution, resulting in a significantly magnified signal. The use of Pluronic® F-127 detergent into the assay buffer system helped reduce assay background while improving sensitivity. The biotin labeling process of the detection antibody was optimized and combined with using poly horseradish-peroxidase (HRP)-streptavidin to further enhance sensitivity. To evaluate this approach, we developed a sandwich ELISA to quantify total (free and drug bound) serum human interleukin-13 (IL-13). A pair of non-competing for target binding antibodies was used. Due to IL-13's small size, steric hindrance was observed in the presence of NVS-0031 (anti-IL-13 mAb) and a saturation method to mitigate this interference was implemented. Despite an initial 40% sensitivity loss, we eventually improved sensitivity approximately 200-fold to achieve a limit of quantitation of 0.05 pg/mL. This methodology has been effectively employed in other ELISA-based bioanalytical assays that require heightened sensitivity (data not shown), demonstrating its wide-ranging applicability.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

TE assessment is key in drug development. It offers valuable insights on target occupancy and accumulation, drug efficacy level, dose determination, physiological effects, and potential toxicity. The data are instrumental in constructing mechanism-based Pharmacokinetic/Target Engagement/Pharmacodynamic (PK/TE/PD) models [1–4], which elucidate the drug exposure and subsequent pharmacodynamic effects [5–7].

The primary bioanalytical challenges for TE assays are sensitivity and drug interference, particularly when a drug’s targets are low in abundance such as cytokines and chemokines. Commercial immunoassay kits often fail to meet the sensitivity requirements. Following therapeutic mAb drug administration, a portion of target binds to the drug (bound), while the remainder exists in its free form (free). Development of an assay to detect the free target is not always feasible, due to the sensitivity required and the potential risk to perturb physiological target-drug equilibrium in vitro assays. For these reasons, researchers prefer to measure and use total concentrations of the target (free and bound) to build mathematical models to support drug development [8–10]. The TE assay discussed herein aims to determine total levels of IL-13 in human serum to mathematically investigate mechanisms of drug-target interaction.

Conventional sandwich ELISA [11–13] is still widely used in various applications today due to its numerous advantages [14, 15], such as: 1) the capability to achieve excellent specificity through the non-competitive binding of two different antibodies used for capturing and detection, 2) its ease of implementation in executing protocols; and 3) the simple technology and wide availability of consumables. However, ELISAs may sometimes have insufficient sensitivity to quantify low abundant proteins, especially in the sub pg/mL range when present in a complex matrix such as human serum and plasma [16, 17].

To overcome the ELISA drawbacks, the assay presented here was revised. First, the classical F-bottomed MaxiSorp Nunc-immuno assay plate normally used for classic ELISA was replaced with a combination of U-bottomed MaxiSorp Nunc-immuno plate and IMAPlate™. The U-bottomed plate was chosen for its robust and reliable performance, allowing various lower volumes of reaction solutions such as substrate and stop solution. This is an advantage compared to the F-bottomed plate, which usually requires a minimum working volume of 50 µL that dilutes signal intensity. IMAPlate™ was then employed to read reaction signals [18]. The extended light path-length of the wells in the IMAPlate™ significantly boosts the signal with consequent increase of signal-to-noise ratio. Second, Tween-20, a traditionally used detergent in ELISA buffer systems, was substituted with Pluronic® F-127. Inspired by recent publications demonstrating the capability of Pluronic® F-127 to prevent non-specific protein binding in microfluidic devices [19], its potential was explored for an ELISA. While both Tween-20 and Pluronic® F-127 markedly reduced the assay background, Pluronic® F-127 unexpectedly improved the assay sensitivity by approximately 1.6-fold in comparison to Tween-20. Third, the biotinylation protocol of the detection antibody was refined to enhance performance. Coupled with the use of poly HRP-streptavidin, this led to optimal binding of poly HRP to the specific immuno complex formed in the assay. Thus, it could reach a similar effect as the enzyme-catalyzed signal amplification step in highly sensitive ELISA [20] while reducing the number of assay steps.

Interference from drug or other elements in the test sample often pose a significant challenge for a TE assay. The selection of suitable assay tools is an essential strategy to resolve this issue. A common method to mitigate drug interference is to use a pair of antibodies that do not compete with the drug for target molecule binding [8]. However, when three antibodies (capture, detection, and antibody drug) simultaneously bind to a smaller target molecule, such as cytokines and chemokines, there is less availability of exposed epitopes for binding and steric hindrances might occur. These hindrances can potentially suppress the assay signal, resulting in an underestimation of the total target concentration. To overcome steric hindrance in the human IL-13 TE ELISA, an excess of NVS-0031 was added to fully saturate to IL-13’s binding sites, forming a target-NVS-0031 complex [1, 3, 21]. Thus, only the bound target was left available for the capture and detection antibodies in the TE assay. Apart from this interference, potential interferences from other soluble target binding proteins like IL-13Rα2 were evaluated during assay development.

In this article, we present a unique and innovative multifaceted approach to enhance the sensitivity of a sandwich ELISA-based TE assay for quantifying total human serum IL-13. This strategy encompasses: 1) Employing U-bottomed MaxiSorp Nunc-immuno plate and IMAPlate™, 2) Adding detergent Pluronic® F-127 into the assay buffer system, and 3) Optimizing the detection antibody's biotinylation, in conjunction with the use of poly HRP-streptavidin. In addition, a method for mitigating drug interference is showcased. The total IL-13 TE ELISA, as illustrated in Fig. 1, employed a pair of anti-IL-13 antibodies for capture and detection in the presence of NVS-0031, NVS-0031 saturation was utilized to minimize the interference. The results demonstrated that this TE ELISA can quantify low sub pg/mL levels of human serum IL-13, indicating its broad application potential for any ELISA where increased sensitivity is required.Fig. 1Total human IL-13 TE ELISA setting

Materials and Methods

Materials

The laboratory supplies and reagents used in the experiment were obtained from different suppliers. 96-well U-bottomed MaxiSorp and F-bottomed MaxiSorp Nunc-immuno plates were purchased from Thermo scientific (Reinach, Switzerland); White IMAPlate™ and plate adapter were purchased from NCL New Concept Lab (Möhlin, Switzerland). Anti-human IL-13 antibodies for capture and detection were obtained from Abcam (Cambridge, UK) and MyBioSource (San Diego, US) respectively, while recombinant human IL-13 used for calibrator and spiked samples,was acquired from ImmunoTools (Friesoythe, Germany). 3,3',5,5'-Tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) solution and Poly-HRP streptavidin were from Merck (Buchs, Switzerland) and Thermo Scientific respectively. Detergents and buffers included BioRad’s (Cressier, Switzerland) Coating buffer, Blocking buffer, Assay diluent, Thermo Scientific’s Poly-HRP dilution buffer and Merck’s washing buffer Phosphate Buffer Saline (PBS) with 0.05% (v/v) Tween-20, Pluronic® F-127 and Tween-20. Surrogate matrix was prepared with equal volumes of New Zealand White Rabbit serum pool from BioIVT (West Sussex, UK) and GIBCO® heat inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) from Thermo Scientific. The assay buffer was made of 10% surrogate matrix, 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) from Merck, and 0.05% Pluronic® F-127 in PBS, pH 7.4. NVS-0031 was produced by Novartis internally. BioIVT supplied all individual human sera and human serum pools. Recombinant human IL-13 receptor subunit alpha-2 (IL-13Rα2) was obtained from Sino Biological (Eschborn, Germany). For detection antibody biotinylation and determination of biotin-protein incorporation, EZ-Link™ NHS-PEG4-Biotin, EZ-Link™ Sulfo-NHS-Biotin and Pierce™ Biotin Quantitation Kit from Thermo Scientific were used. Zeba™ Spin Desalting Columns with 7 K MWCO and 0.5 mL size from Thermo Scientific were used for buffer exchange before and after biotinylation. The biotinylation quench reagent Monoethanolamide (MEA) was acquired from Merck. The SpectraMax ABS plus plate reader with the software SoftMax Pro version 7.1 from Molecular Devices (San Jose, US) was used for assay measurement and results calculation.

Detection Antibody Biotinylation

To prepare the biotinylated antibody, the antibody buffer was first exchanged to PBS pH8.0 via Zeba™ Spin desalting columns, then the antibody was mixed with freshly prepared 20 mM NHS-PEG4-Biotin or Sulfo-NHS-Biotin stock solution. Following a 30-min room temperature (RT) incubation, the biotinylation reaction was stopped with Quencher reagent MEA, and any unreacted NHS-PEG4-Biotin or Sulfo-NHS-Biotin was removed by Zeba™ Spin desalting columns. The final product was kept at 4 °C for future use.

Determination of Biotin to Protein Incorporation Ratio

To economize on biotinylated detection antibody, the biotin to protein incorporation ratio was determined using an IMAPlate™ based miniaturized method [22]. The protocol, which modified the Pierce™ Biotin Quantitation kit instructions to reduce sample and reagent volume by eightfold, involved a freshly prepared working solution of 4'-hydroxyazobenzene-2-carboxylic acid (HABA)/Avidin Premix in PBS. To start the reaction in an IMAPlate™, of which the bottom was sealed with parafilm, 2.5 μL of PBS (blank), positive control (biotinylated HRP from kit) and the test samples were added to the upper chambers. Then, 22.5 µL/well of working solution was added and mixed thoroughly. The parafilm was removed, and the IMAPlate™ was placed on an empty 96-deep well plate and shaken at 600 rpm for 1–2 min to allow liquid flow into the lower part of the IMAPlate™. Finally, the IMAPlate™ was placed in the adaptor for measurement. Absorbance was read at 500 nm with a reference wavelength of 650 nm. The optical density (OD) values of HABA/avidin/biotin (H/A/B) for blank, sample, and positive control were recorded once stable for 15 s.

The moles of biotin per mole of antibody were calculated using Beer’s Law, with a correction factor for dilution effect by the biotinylated protein in the H/A mixture. The positive control from the kit was used as the standard to determine the correction factor.

Human IL-13 TE ELISA Method

The procedure used a U-bottomed ELISA plate for reactions and an IMAPlate™ for measurement. Briefly, the U-bottomed MaxiSorp Nunc-immuno plate was coated with 50 µL/well of 3 µg/mL capture antibody, sealed, and incubated for 5 h at RT. It was washed with 250 µL/well of PBST 3 times, blocked with 200 µL/well of blocking buffer, sealed, and incubated for 45 min at RT. After washing with PBST 250 µL/well 3 times, 100 µL of calibrators, QCs and test samples were loaded, and the plate was sealed and incubated overnight on a Thermomixer at 15 °C, 300 rpm. The plate was again washed with PBST 3 times, then 50 µL of 0.5 µg/mL biotinylated detection antibody in assay buffer was added, sealed, and incubated for 1 h at RT. After washing with PBST 3 times, 50 µL/well of 1:20,000 (v/v) diluted Poly-HRP was added, sealed and incubated for 20 min at RT. The plate was washed with PBST 6 times, 25 µL/well of TMB solution was added, and incubated for 15 min at RT with shaking at 600 rpm on Thermomixer. The reaction was stopped by adding 10 µL/well of 1 N H2SO4 and mixing at 600 rpm. Finally, 25 µL/well of the colored solution was transferred to an IMAPlate™ and measured absorbance at 450 nm with a reference wavelength of 700 nm. Data were analyzed using SoftMax Pro version 7.1 software, and a calibration curve was established using a four-parameter curve fitting model with a weighting factor of 1/y2.

For comparison, the assay was also performed using a F-bottomed MaxiSorp Nunc-immuno plate in parallel under identical conditions, except for adding 50 µL/well of TMB and 50 µL/well of 1 N H2SO4 solution for the color reaction.

Calibrators, QCs and Test Human Serum Sample Preparation

Two-fold concentrated calibrators were freshly prepared by serially diluting recombinant human IL-13 in surrogate matrix. A minimum required dilution (MRD) of 2 was applied by diluting calibrators, QCs and test samples 1:1 (v/v) in assay buffer with 100 µg/mL of NVS-0031. All prepared samples including calibrators and QCs were incubated for 1 h at RT with shaking at 400 rpm on a thermomixer to form IL-13—NVS-0031 complex.

Assay Interference Assessment

mAb NVS-0031 Interference

Based on PK prediction using an in-silico model, three concentrations of NVS-0031 (100 µg/mL, 300 µg/mL, and 1,000 µg/mL—roughly 0.66 µM, 2 µM and 6.6 µM) were selected to assess interference in surrogate matrix and in eight individual commercial healthy human serum samples. The preparation procedure for surrogate matrix was: 1) Two-fold concentrated calibrators were prepared. 2) Each calibrator was mixed 1:1 (v/v) with assay buffer containing different concentrations of NVS-0031 (0, 100 µg/mL, 300 µg/mL, and 1,000 µg/mL) for MRD2. 3) The mixture was incubated for 1 h at RT, shaking at 400 rpm, to allow immune complex formation between IL-13 and NVS-0031. Meanwhile, eight individual human serum samples were mixed 1:1 (v/v) with assay buffer containing 100 µg/mL, 300 µg/mL, and 1,000 µg/mL of NVS-0031 for MRD2. After 1 h incubation at RT with shaking, the serum IL-13 value was determined for both calibrators and serum samples. Using 100 µg/mL of NVS-0031 as a benchmark, the percentage bias between various NVS-0031 concentration calibrators and individual human serum samples were calculated.

Recombinant Human IL-13Rα2 Interference

Six commercial individual healthy human serum samples were spiked with recombinant human IL-13Rα2 at final concentrations of 125 pg/mL and 250 pg/mL. Control samples without recombinant human IL-13Rα2 were also prepared. All IL-13Rα2 spiked and their corresponding control samples were subjected to MRD 2 in assay buffer with 100 µg/mL NVS-0031 and incubated for 1 h at RT with shaking at 400 rpm to ensure proper binding and interaction between the IL-13Rα2, NVS-0031, and serum IL-13. After IL-13 measurement, the percentage bias of IL-13 in spiked samples was calculated and compared to control samples.

Assay selectivity assessment

Three levels of recombinant human IL-13 (6 pg/mL, 3 pg/mL, and 1.5 pg/mL) were spiked in 10 individual human sera, including five hemolyzed and five lipemic samples. Additionally, two levels of IL-13 (1.25 pg/mL and 0.625 pg/mL) were spiked in five normal human sera. Prior to the assay, all samples, including the unspiked serum controls, were diluted at MRD 2 in assay buffer with 100 µg/mL NVS-0031 and incubated for 1 h with shaking at 400 rpm at RT. After measuring IL-13, the percentage bias for each sample was calculated.

Parallelism and Sample Dilution Linearity Assessment

Eight samples, including four individual human sera unspiked and four spiked with 7 pg/mL recombinant human IL-13, were prepared. A two-fold serial dilution was carried out in surrogate matrix. Prior to the assay, all samples were diluted at MRD 2 in assay buffer with 100 µg/mL NVS-0031 and incubated for 1 h with shaking at 400 rpm at RT. After determining the serum IL-13 values, the percentage bias for each dilution was calculated.

Endogenous IL-13 Concentration Measurement

Three commercial human serum pools and thirteen individual human sera were used to estimate the endogenous levels of IL-13. All samples were diluted at MRD 2 in assay buffer with 100 µg/mL NVS-0031 and incubated for 1 h with shaking at 400 rpm at RT before the assay.

Results

Comparison of TE ELISA Performance: U-bottomed Assay Plate with IMAPlate™ vs Conventional F-bottomed ELISA Plate

Due to its well shape, the U-bottomed MaxiSorp plate allows for reliable performance with lower volumes, unlike the F-bottomed plate, which typically requires a minimum of 50 µL working volume. Because of the low volume liquid in F-bottomed plate, it cannot cover the entire bottom surface, tending to flow to the edge of the well. The human IL-13 TE ELISA performed in a combination of U-bottomed assay plate and IMAPlate™ was compared to a conventional F-bottomed MaxiSorp Nunc-immuno plate. The signal was developed in U-bottomed plate and was transferred to an IMAPlate™ for measurement, whereas the solution from the F-bottomed plate was measured directly.

The OD value from the IMAPlate™ yielded a notably higher result in comparison to that of the F-bottomed plate, with a 6 pg/mL calibrator reporting an OD of around 3, at the plate reader's upper detection limit. This calibrator concentration, along with six additional twofold serial dilutions, formed a standard curve with seven concentrations (Fig. 2A). The readout from the IMAPlate™ were 6 to 8 times higher than those from the F-bottomed plate, demonstrating approximately eightfold improved sensitivity. The combination of a U-bottomed plate and IMAPlate™ reduced the detection limit to 0.05 pg/mL, calculated as 5 times the standard deviation (SD) of zero standard. Effect of Detergent on Assay Background and Sensitivity

During the initial phases of assay set-up, the use of IMAPlate™ resulted in an amplification of the assay background signal that could potentially narrow the detection range. Therefore, the use of detergent was evaluated to decrease the assay background.

Tween-20 or Pluronic® F-127 was added to all buffer solutions, including blocking buffer, assay buffer and poly HRP-streptavidin dilution buffer. Figure 2B illustrates three standard curves: without detergent, with 0.05% (v/v) Tween-20, and with 0.05% (v/v) Pluronic® F-127. Detergents reduced the assay background by nearly half and improved assay sensitivity. The addition of 0.05% Tween-20 and 0.05% Pluronic® F-127, compared to the absence of detergent, improved the assay sensitivity by 1.8-fold and 2.8-fold, respectively. Superior performance was observed by addition of 0.05% Pluronic® F-127 when compared to Tween-20.

Optimized Detection Antibody Labeling: Biotin Spacer Arm Length and Incorporation Ratio

The extra-large size of poly HRP-streptavidin may cause steric hindrance, which could potentially affect its binding to a multi-biotinylated detection antibody. To investigate the impact of biotin incorporation ratio and spacer arm length on the detection antibody biotinylation and subsequent poly HRP-streptavidin binding, two biotin labeling agents were compared: EZ-Link™ NHS-PEG4-Biotin (spacer arm 29 Å) and EZ-Link™ Sulfo-NHS-Biotin (spacer arm 13.5 Å).

The detection antibody was labeled with biotin under various conditions to achieve different degrees of biotin incorporation. The level of biotin incorporation was determined using an IMAPlate™ based miniaturized biotin quantitation method. A correction factor of 0.92, derived from the kit-provided positive control, was used to calculate the biotin-to-protein ratio.

Figure 2C presents data from different anti-IL-13 detection antibodies with varying biotin incorporation ratios and spacer arm lengths. Antibodies labeled with a spacer arm 29 Å demonstrated increased sensitivity with higher biotin incorporation. The antibody with a biotin ratio of 10.0 had the highest sensitivity, about 2.8 times higher than the one with a ratio of 4.8 and marginally higher than the one with a ratio of 8.9. Additionally, antibodies with similar biotin ratios (~ 10) showed 3.8-fold better sensitivity with a spacer arm 29 Å compared to a spacer arm 13.5 Å.

Mitigation of NVS-0031 Interference

Effect of NVS-0031 Interference on the Assay Standard Curve

It is essential to assess potential interference with the TE ELISA, which quantifies IL-13 in the presence of NVS-0031. The addition of NVS-0031 resulted in a parallel downward shift of the curves on a log–log scale compared to the one without NVS-0031, indicating significant interference likely caused by steric hindrances of tool antibodies and NVS-0031 in binding of the target. Effect of NVS-0031 Interference on Human Serum Sample

The assessment of NVS-0031 interference was conducted using human sera from eight individuals, each spiked with 100 µg/mL, 300 µg/mL, or 1,000 µg/mL of NVS-0031. The IL-13 values from 100 µg/mL samples served as a baseline to calculate the bias in the other groups. Table 1A presents the results, with the bias percentage ranging from −20% to 14%.

To summarize, the results demonstrated no substantial impact on the assay when NVS-0031 concentration was ≥ 100 µg/mL. Therefore, an assay buffer containing 100 µg/mL of NVS-0031 is designated as the MRD buffer for preparing calibrators, QC samples, and test samples in the human IL-13 TE ELISA.

Discussion

Previous studies indicated that IL-13 levels in healthy human serum generally fall within a range of 0 to 2 pg/mL [26–28]. Developing a simple and sensitive TE ELISA to detect low pg/mL of IL-13 in presence of NVS-0031 is challenging due to the required sensitivity and potential interference. Peter Spies et al. introduced a two-step ELISA method enhancing conventional ELISA sensitivity for human IL-2 [18]. This method involved reducing the reaction volume of TMB and stop solution and transferring 5 µL of the resulting solution to an IMAPlate™ for measurement. Leveraging this strategy, the sensitivity of IL-13 TE assay was enhanced 8 to 10-fold with the combined use of U-bottomed MaxiSorp plate and IMAPlate™. The substitution of Tween-20 with Pluronic® F-127 facilitated a doubling of sensitivity. Optimizing the detection antibody biotinylation heightened the sensitivity 3 to 4-fold, and incorporating poly HRP-streptavidin resulted in a further 3 to 4-fold improvement. Overall, employing the same antibody reagents and standard ELISA method resulted in a cumulative 200-fold enhancement in sensitivity.

Improving readout signal stability and simplifying handling in the human IL-13 TE ELISA was achieved by reducing the TMB reaction volume and stop solution to 35 µL and transferring 25 µL for measurement in an IMAPlate™. The funnel-like “well” of IMAPlate™ (Fig. 5) offers a longer light path-length, creating an "optical zoom" effect. A sample volume reduction from 5 µL to 4 µL leads to a significant 20% decrease in OD, but with a 25 µL sample volume, a 1 µL reduction only causes a 1% change in OD. This modification not only increased the assay sensitivity but also improved the measurement precision.Fig. 5Drawing of the IMAPlate™'s “well” structure and sample loading.

While the IMAPlate™'s long path-length provides advantages, it can also increase the background from non-specific protein binding and interferences. To mitigate this, Tween-20 and Pluronic® F-127 were incorporated into various buffers in the assay. Tween-20 is commonly used in ELISA, while Pluronic® F-127 is increasingly utilized for preventing non-specific protein adsorption in microfluidic systems [19]. These detergents were added to the blocking buffer, assay buffer, and poly-HRP dilution buffer during the preparation of calibrators, biotinylated detection antibody solution, and poly HRP-streptavidin solution to reduce the background. Both detergents could effectively decrease the assay background. However, Pluronic® F-127 outperformed Tween-20 in enhancing assay sensitivity. At 0.05% (v/v), Pluronic® F-127 achieved approximately 1.6 times higher sensitivity than Tween-20 (Fig. 2B). Further investigation demonstrated similar effects on background reduction and sensitivity increasing across Pluronic® F-127 concentrations (v/v) of 0.025%, 0.05%, 0.1%, and 0.2% (data not shown). To deepen our understanding of how polyethylene glycol (PEG) and polypropylene glycol (PPG) chain lengths affect assay background and sensitivity, future explorations will focus on the effects of variable chain lengths in alternative Pluronic® surfactants.

The effects of biotin spacer arm length and biotin to protein incorporation ratio were also investigated to further enhance assay sensitivity. Given the detection antibody size is relatively small compared to poly HRP-streptavidin, the amount of poly HRP-streptavidin that can bind to the detection antibody is highly dependent on the available space around the molecule. To examine the impact of biotin spacer arm length, the detection antibody was labeled using two types of biotin molecules: EZ-Link™ NHS-PEG4-Biotin (long spacer arm: 29 Å) and EZ-Link™ Sulfo-NHS-Biotin (short spacer arm: 13.5 Å). For comparison, both biotin conjugated detection antibodies had a similar biotin to antibody ratio of 10. Interestingly, the antibody labeled with the long spacer arm biotin yielded a standard curve slope four times steeper than that with shorter spacer arm biotin. Even with less biotin incorporated, a steeper slope calibration curve (i.e. higher sensitivity) resulted using the detection antibody biotinylated with long spacer arm (Fig. 2C). These results support the hypothesis that the long spacer arm biotin provides more space, improving poly HRP-streptavidin binding, especially when steric hindrance could obstruct the binding.

Given that the human IL-13 TE ELISA was specifically developed for clinical studies, it was crucial to investigate potential drug interference with the assay. The presence of NVS-0031 did affect the assay standard curves: when the concentrations were 100 µg/mL (0.66 µM) or above, the curves nearly overlapped. To put this into perspective, assuming a serum IL-13 concentration of 10 pg/mL, the mole ratio between 100 µg/mL of NVS-0031 and IL-13 in the sample is approximately 8.4X105. This suggests a saturation state for NVS-0031-target binding, indicating that interference can be minimized by applying a saturation method [26]. However, a notable drawback of this approach is the decreased assay sensitivity, as evidenced by the downward shift in the standard curves with NVS-0031 (Fig. 3).

During the development of a TE ELISA, it is important to consider the matrix effect and the presence of endogenous target protein in sample matrix. A common approach is to use a surrogate matrix devoid of endogenous target for calibrator preparation instead of the sample matrix. Therefore, the IL-13 calibrators were prepared in a surrogate matrix derived from a mixture of animal sera. When the calibrator and test serum samples were diluted with MRD 2 in the assay buffer containing NVS-0031, they reached the same serum concentration prior to the assay. Using an animal serum mixture for assay buffer preparation, instead of the commercial ELISA assay diluent from BioRad, significantly decreased the assay background. This improvement is likely due to the synergistic effects of serum proteins and the detergent Pluronic® F-127.

During assay qualification, various aspects were assessed, including sample dilution linearity and selectivity. The outcomes showed satisfactory dilution linearity and selectivity. Potential interference from soluble form of IL-13Rα2 was evaluated as a part of assay interference assessment. The results indicated that the measurement of serum IL-13 was unlikely to be affected by samples containing IL-13Rα2 at physiological concentrations. Additionally, serum IL-13 was successfully detected in thirteen individual and three pooled human sera, reaffirming the sensitivity of the IL-13 TE ELISA even in the presence of NVS-0031 (Fig. 4). The median serum IL-13 level from all sixteen sera (0.90 pg/mL) was approximately 3 times higher than the value reported by Dimitrova's lab for healthy donors [27], and the IQR (0.63 to 1.06 pg/mL) was narrower than Dimitrova's (0 to 1.20 pg/mL). The discrepancy in results may be due to the lower sensitivity assay used by Dimitrova et al., as the sample with zero IL-13 could be below the detection limit in their assay. Gotfredsen’s lab reported slightly higher IL-13 values [28] in intermediate age-related macular degeneration patients; the median serum IL-13 value was 1.31 pg/mL, with IQR ranging from 0.97 to 1.86 pg/mL. This difference could be due to chronic inflammation. Meanwhile, Cai et al. found a wide serum IL-13 range in 50 healthy individuals, from 0.11 to 2.25 pg/ml with a median of 0.36 pg/mL [29]. However, lacking a standard IL-13 reference and differing antibodies used across labs, it was not feasible to determine the IL-13 value with absolute accuracy. Nevertheless, our data aligns with reported serum IL-13 range, demonstrating the assay's relative accuracy in quantifying serum IL-13 in the presence of NVS-0031.

Conclusion

A novel, multifaceted method to improve the sensitivity of ELISA-based TE assays for human serum IL-13 quantification has been developed. Sensitivity gains were achieved synergistically using U-bottomed MaxiSorp Nunc-immuno plates and IMAPlate™, incorporating Pluronic® F-127 detergent, optimizing detection antibody biotinylation, and using poly HRP-streptavidin. The results distinctly exhibited a progressive increase in assay sensitivity. Moreover, this methodology can be adapted to various ELISA-based bioanalytical assays, showcasing its versatility and wide applicability.
