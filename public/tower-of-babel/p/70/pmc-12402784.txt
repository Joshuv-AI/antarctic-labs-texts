Hematopoietic Stem Cell Hierarchies as Novel Biomarkers of Drug Response in Myelodysplastic Syndromes and Acute Myeloid Leukemia
Authors: Teresa Ezponda, Ana Aldaz, Ana Alfonso-Pierola, Irene Ganan-Gomez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMyelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) and acute myeloid leukemia (AML) are clonal hematopoietic stem cell malignancies characterized by high clinical and molecular heterogeneity and poor outcomes. Effective treatments for these disorders remain unmet clinical needs, particularly after the failure of first-line therapies. Recent studies have uncovered that the hierarchical organization of MDS and AML stem cells and their progeny, which sustain and propagate the disease, follows specific patterns that reflect the founding stem cells’ differentiation capacity and transcriptional states. These cell identity traits determine signaling dependencies, thereby dictating drug sensitivity. The association between hematopoietic hierarchies and drug response has been best described for venetoclax, a proapoptotic agent that has emerged as a powerful therapeutic tool for the treatment of AML and, possibly in the near future, high-risk MDS. The finding that hematopoietic hierarchies define biologically distinct disease subtypes has important translational and clinical implications. It will not only be critical for the selection, follow-up, and post-failure management of patients with AML and high-risk MDS treated with venetoclax, but it should also be considered in drug discovery, therapy selection, and patient stratification in clinical trials. This review provides an overview of the current understanding of healthy hematopoiesis and abnormal hematopoietic stem cell hierarchies in MDS and AML and summarizes the growing body of evidence that these hierarchies determine sensitivity to venetoclax and other agents. Lastly, we address the translational and clinical implications of these findings and offer a critical discussion on how they may be used to improve patient outcomes.

Introduction

Myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) and acute myeloid leukemia (AML) comprise a group of heterogeneous myeloid malignancies that mainly affect an older population, displaying complex pathogenesis and diverse natural histories (1). Patients with MDS and AML have limited curative therapeutic options and very poor outcomes (2, 3). In recent years, an outburst of novel investigational approaches has yielded new therapeutic targets for both disorders. Nevertheless, most drugs have systematically failed phase III trials, particularly those for MDS with a high risk (HR) of progression to AML. Failure can be attributed to multiple factors (4), one of the primary causes being lax trial selection and eligibility criteria and the inherent heterogeneity of these diseases, as it is challenging to effectively incorporate diverse morphologic, phenotypic, cytogenetic, and molecular profiles into therapeutic decisions. This poses a question about the need for stratification systems to better select or follow up patients enrolled in clinical trials on the basis of their disease biology.

MDS and AML arise through the acquisition of genetic lesions (5, 6) by multi- or oligopotent hematopoietic precursors (7, 8). In recent years, research studies powered by single-cell transcriptomics have provided mounting evidence that the hierarchical organization of those MDS and AML stem cells and their cellular progeny follows defined patterns, which are patient-specific and highly recurring (9–13). Such hierarchies are not only defining of the biology of the disease but have also unfolded as powerful biomarkers of drug response that predict sensitivity to the BCL2 inhibitor venetoclax, one of the drugs most recently incorporated into the treatment of HR-MDS and AML (10, 12–15).

The combination of venetoclax with the hypomethylating agent (HMA) 5-azacitidine has transformed the treatment landscape for elderly or unfit patients with AML and is being evaluated as the new standard treatment for patients with HR-MDS. Where HMAs such as 5-azacitidine or decitabine provided a complete response rate of 20% to 30% and a median survival duration of 10 months in unfit patients with AML (16), the venetoclax–5-azacitidine regimen has demonstrated a response rate of approximately 65% and a median overall survival (OS) of 14.7 months in this challenging population, according to the pivotal VIALE-A study (17). An updated long-term analysis from this study reported a 2-year OS of 37.5%, representing a 20.6% improvement compared with 5-azacitidine monotherapy (18). In this line, the results of a phase II study with 5-azacitidine and venetoclax in patients with HR-MDS were highly promising, showing an 80% overall response rate and a median survival of 26 months in patients treated with the combination (19). However, a recent press release (20) revealed disappointing preliminary results of the phase III trial, the VERONA study (NCT04401748), which argues in favor of the implementation of biology-based selection and stratification criteria to unmask responder groups in very heterogeneous patient cohorts. The full results report is therefore eagerly expected. Nevertheless, primary resistance to venetoclax and 5-azacitidine combinations occurs in approximately one third of treated patients with AML and 20% of patients with HR-MDS (17–19), and the treatment of HR-MDS and AML remains an unmet medical need.

The discovery that hematopoietic hierarchies are determinants of response to venetoclax in MDS and AML entails significant progress in the understanding of the biology underlying drug resistance in these disorders and has a tremendous translational impact, as this knowledge may be employed to improve current trial design and decision-making in real-world clinical settings. Further, the same principle could be applied to the study of other therapies and aid in the personalized selection of optimal treatments, as well as in identifying suitable alternatives in cases of resistance. This review summarizes the biological findings supporting this discovery and discusses its potential translational and clinical implications.

Hematopoiesis and Stem Cells

Hematopoiesis is the process of continuously replenishing blood cells from an undifferentiated common precursor, the hematopoietic stem cell (HSC). HSCs, immunophenotypically defined as CD34+CD38−CD90+CD45RA−, are characterized by two key properties: (i) multipotency, which is the ability to form all differentiated blood cells, and (ii) long-term self-renewal, defined as the lifelong ability to give rise to progeny identical to the parent through cell division. The choice between self-renewal and differentiation must be tightly regulated to enable both the generation of differentiated cells and the accurate maintenance of the right HSC number (21). This regulation is partly mediated by bursts of norepinephrine, TNF-α, and melatonin in the bone marrow (BM), triggered by the onset of light and darkness cycles (22). Under normal conditions, HSCs undergo asymmetric cell division, resulting in the production of two distinct cell types. One cell is an identical copy of the original HSC, whereas the other is a multipotent progenitor (MPP) cell (23). Further downstream, MPPs give rise to oligopotent progenitors: (i) the lymphoid-primed multipotent progenitors (LMPP), which preserve stem cell properties (24, 25), and (ii) the common myeloid progenitors (CMP). The myeloid lineage further restricts into granulocyte–monocyte progenitors (GMP) and megakaryocyte–erythrocyte progenitors (26). The former give rise to terminally differentiated basophils, eosinophils, neutrophils, and macrophages, and the latter terminally differentiate into erythrocytes or megakaryocytes (23) although there is evidence that megakaryocytes may arise directly from stem cells (Fig. 1A; ref. 27). The differentiation route that they follow, either stepwise or directly from HSCs, results in functionally distinct megakaryocyte subpopulations (28). LMPPs, in turn, produce common lymphoid progenitors but retain myeloid differentiation capacity, being also able to differentiate into GMPs (25, 29, 30).

Although the aforementioned hematopoietic stem and progenitor cell (HSPC) populations are commonly referred to in order to define the differentiation states of cells throughout hematopoiesis, single-cell transcriptomics data have revealed that hematopoiesis may be far more of a continuous process than originally modeled (31). Moreover, functional analysis of hematopoietic cell states has led to the concept of the punctuated continuum hematopoietic model (32). This model proposes the existence of discrete cell states within the continuous transcriptomic landscape, in which each state is formed by a heterogeneous pool of cells that are variably primed to give rise to different hematopoietic lineages. Indeed, HSCs demonstrate functional heterogeneity and can differ in their contributions to the lymphoid and myeloid cell lineages. A recent work using DNA barcoding in endogenous adult HSCs in mice to trace the HSC contribution to major hematopoietic cell lineages demonstrated that, although the majority of HSCs give rise to a single lineage, many HSCs also clonally contribute to multiple lineages, specifically lymphoid and myeloid cells. These results suggested a close developmental relationship between the myeloid and lymphoid lineages (33).

Specific conditions, such as aging or injury, can change the differentiation potential of HSCs. In this sense, HSCs with a balanced output of lymphoid and myeloid cells predominate during youth (34), whereas aging is associated with increased proportions of HSCs with myeloid-biased output, resulting in decreased lymphopoiesis and increased myelopoiesis (35–37). Furthermore, it was recently demonstrated that depleting such myeloid-biased HSCs can restore the characteristic features of a youthful immune system (38). Changes in differentiation potential can also be triggered by emergency hematopoiesis associated with acute infection, promoting a transcriptional rewiring of HSCs that changes their identity and cellular output from lymphoid-biased toward myeloid-biased in order to supply elevated numbers of granulocytes (39, 40). Although HSPCs are predominantly quiescent, specific conditions such as BM injury or an inflammatory environment trigger their proliferation, promoting HSPC regeneration. In regenerating conditions, rare myeloid-biased MPPs are transiently overproduced by HSCs, supporting myeloid amplification (40). In order to regulate HSPCs’ regenerative response to such conditions, their stemness and proliferation are controlled by both non-cell autonomous [i.e., TNF-α (41) or the niche-secreted RNase angiogenin (42)] and cell-autonomous [i.e., transcription factor ETV2 (43) or calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase 2 (44) regulation] mechanisms. In summary, the tight regulation of the differentiation potential of HSCs adapts to specific conditions and is a key determinant of the hematopoietic output; thus, the dominance of aberrant HSCs with altered differentiation is associated with disease-specific abnormal hematopoiesis.

Stem Cell Hierarchies in MDS and AML

Genetic studies have demonstrated that MDS arise from HSCs that have differentiation deficiencies (7, 11, 45) and persist even during clinical remission to conventional therapies, such as lenalidomide and HMAs (12, 46, 47). Clonotypic and transcriptional studies support that normal immunophenotypically defined HSPC hierarchies (Fig. 1A) are preserved by MDS cells (11, 12, 45, 48), which has facilitated their study and isolation from genetically and clinically heterogeneous groups of patients.

MDS HSCs differentiate through myeloid-biased trajectories, giving rise to hematopoietic hierarchies that are enriched in either CMPs or GMPs (11, 12, 45, 48). A phenotypic study in a cohort of more than 100 genetically diverse patients, representative of most molecular and prognostic groups (12), revealed that each CMP- or GMP-enriched hierarchy could be detected in roughly half of all patients with MDS and persisted throughout therapy with HMAs. This strongly suggested that abnormal HSPC hierarchies were not arbitrary snapshots of transient stages of hematopoiesis but the result of stable differentiation trajectories sustaining the disease. Indeed, genomic and single-cell transcriptomic studies of the HSPC compartment revealed that each hierarchy arose from HSCs in distinct states [the most primitive, long-term HSCs (LT-HSC) or granulomonocytic-biased LMPPs, respectively] that followed separate differentiation trajectories (Fig. 1B; ref. 12). To standardize the nomenclature across studies, we will refer to these as “primitive” (LT-HSCs to CMPs) or “granulomonocytic” (LMPPs to GMPs) hierarchies.

Mechanistically, each MDS hierarchy was associated with the presence of specific genetic and cytogenetic aberrations, such as 5q deletions (11, 48) and TP53 mutations in the primitive hierarchy and RUNX1 or STAG2 mutations in the granulomonocytic hierarchy (12). In agreement with the novel concept of mutation-driven HSC fate bias (49), this indicated that certain genetic lesions can redirect HSC differentiation trajectories toward specific lineages and shape distinct MDS hierarchies. Yet associations between hierarchies and specific genotypes were only found in a fraction of the MDS cases, which may suggest that other factors, such as combinatorial effects or the presence of uncharacterized mutations, may be at play. Intriguingly, driver mutations were identified in the primitive LT-HSC population in the vast majority (if not all) of the MDS cases (11, 12), indicating that these cells are always either leukemic or preleukemic, whereas LMPPs were found to bear a higher mutational burden in the granulomonocytic trajectory (12). This suggested that the phenotype of the cell of origin harboring the driving genetic events may be a decisive factor in the emergence of each hierarchy. More HSC-focused genetic and mechanistic studies will be needed to fully understand the underlying HSC biology of the disease.

In contrast with MDS, the tumoral mass in AML comprises mainly lineage-committed precursors with arrested differentiation, blasts, but it also includes a subset of undifferentiated cells with stem cell properties that sustain the disease throughout therapy, termed leukemia stem cells (LSC; ref. 8). LSCs are myeloid-biased but show considerable intratumoral phenotypic heterogeneity and do not always follow a classic HSC developmental trajectory (50), in opposition to MDS HSCs, as they can exhibit more differentiated phenotypes (51–53). Moreover, although they almost always encompass cells with HSPC-like gene expression signatures, AML cellular hierarchies are remarkably variable across patients (9, 54).

Independent transcriptomic studies have described different proportions of HSC-like (primitive) versus GMP-like (monocytic) LSCs across patients, mirroring the two proposed MDS hierarchies (Fig. 1C). Pei and colleagues (13) found that LSCs, functionally defined by their low levels of reactive oxygen species (55), from patients with phenotypically primitive AML subtypes according to the French–American–British (FAB) classification (M0/M1/M2; ref. 56) were metabolically and transcriptionally different from LSCs from phenotypically monocytic AML subtypes (FAB M5). Specifically, functionally defined AML LSCs from monocytic AML subtypes upregulated myelomonocytic gene sets compared with primitive AML LSCs, which suggested that a monocytic-skewed LSC differentiation hierarchy drives M5 AMLs. In a phenotype-agnostic single-cell transcriptomic study of a large cohort of 179 unstratified AMLs from The Cancer Genome Atlas (57), Van Galen and colleagues (9) observed that primitive and granulomonocytic LSC signatures were largely mutually exclusive and that each signature was enriched in approximately half of the patients. Remarkably, they also detected a correlation between each signature and specific genotypes, in which mutations in TP53 or NPM1 and FLT3 internal-tandem duplications associated with primitive AML and RUNX1-RUNX1T1 fusions overlapped with monocytic AML. In this line, a study by Zeng and colleagues (10) deconvoluting bulk RNA sequencing data from more than 1,000 AML cases reported four leukemic cell hierarchies, including a primitive hierarchy enriched in HSC-like cells, a granulomonocytic hierarchy enriched in GMP-like cells, an intermediate (mixed) hierarchy, and a mature monocyte–like hierarchy. In agreement with Van Galen and colleagues, the authors found a correlation between the hierarchies and the genomic characteristics of the patients, including associations between AMLs with complex karyotypes and TP53 mutations with the primitive group and RUNX1-RUNX1T1 fusions with the granulomonocytic one, among others. This, again, suggested that specific genetic drivers can co-opt differentiation trajectories and shape leukemic cell hierarchies. However, as in MDS, the role of the cell of origin’s phenotype in this process was not clear. Sango and colleagues (58) shed light on this issue by showing that GMP-like cells are the specific soil for NRAS-driven AML, demonstrating for the first time that a genetic driver can restrict the cell of origin in leukemia and, therefore, define the disease’s hierarchy. In this line, an elegant lineage-tracing study in gene-edited murine cells recently revealed that mutations in Dnmt3a and Npm1 can reprogram HSCs’ fitness and clonal output in a driver gene-dependent manner, but the HSC state preceding the genetic hit dictates the phenotype of the resulting mutant clone (59).

The association between MDS and AML hierarchies with the immunophenotype of the blast population and clinical characteristics of the disease is not clear as different groups have reported opposed correlations or lack thereof (11–13, 45, 48, 52, 60). However, we believe that the translational relevance of the existence of MDS/AML hierarchies with stem cells in different states resides in these cells’ distinct epigenetic (61, 62), transcriptional (63, 64), metabolic (65), and cell-cycle (66, 67) regulation. That is, the mechanisms governing cell survival, proliferation, and differentiation depend on cellular identity.

In MDS, this concept is best illustrated in the setting of disease progression. Preexisting MDS and AML stem cells are the source of relapse after failure of therapy (60, 68, 69) and expand upon morphologic relapse (45, 47, 69, 70). The emergence of therapy-resistant leukemia-regenerating stem cells is accompanied by the acquisition of novel transcriptional signatures upregulating proinflammatory and prosurvival signaling (12, 71). Notably, the gene expression programs deregulated by the stem cells atop each of the two MDS hierarchies upon morphologic relapse are markedly different (12), which indicates that the mechanisms driving disease regeneration and/or malignification are differentiation state-specific.

Collectively, mounting evidence strongly suggests that primitive or granulo/monocytic-biased hematopoietic hierarchies are key distinctive biological features of MDS and AML and that stem cell type–specific oncogenic dependencies rule therapy relapse and disease progression in each of them.

Stem Cell Hierarchies and Sensitivity to Venetoclax

The differential signaling pathway dependence of distinct stem cells of origin in MDS and AML has allowed the identification of hierarchy-specific vulnerabilities that are key determinants of drug response. Owing to the growing clinical interest in venetoclax in the AML and MDS fields and its widespread study as an LSC/HSC-eradicating agent, this drug has become the best-known example of hierarchy-dependent drug sensitivity.

After failure of therapy with HMAs and a subsequent blast expansion, HSCs from patients with MDS with a primitive trajectory (LT-HSCs) acquired BCL2 dependence, whereas those from patients with granulomonocytic trajectories (LMPPs) upregulated NF-κB-dependent proinflammatory signaling pathways (12). Targeting either pathway effectively depleted the corresponding MDS HSC populations in vitro and in vivo, providing compelling evidence that MDS hierarchies are biomarkers of drug response. Furthermore, patients with HR-MDS and secondary AML enrolled in clinical trials of venetoclax in combination with HMAs who had primitive hierarchies underwent efficient MDS HSC depletion and exhibited earlier and more prolonged responses to therapy than patients with granulomonocytic hierarchies (12, 14). Remarkably, some of those were therapy-naïve, which suggests that prior failure to HMAs may not be a requirement for increased venetoclax sensitivity by primitive MDS HSCs. Instead, increased BCL2 dependence may be associated with the degree of malignization of the disease (e.g., progression to secondary AML).

Further evidence that stem cell architecture can predict response to venetoclax was provided by Zeng and colleagues (10), who revealed that the degree of differentiation of the AML hierarchy is negatively correlated with sensitivity to this drug in an ex vivo drug screening (5), with primitive hierarchies enriched in quiescent LSCs being significantly more sensitive than monocytic hierarchies. In agreement with this, three other studies showed that LSCs (13, 15) or bulk leukemic cells (72) from newly diagnosed, untreated patients with primitive bulk AML phenotypes were more sensitive to therapy with venetoclax–5-azacitidine than those from patients with monocytic AML bulk phenotypes, who also exhibited shorter remission latencies and OS in clinical trials of venetoclax–5-azacitidine (13, 15). In this line, Sango and colleagues recently demonstrated that NRAS-mutant monocytic LSCs are intrinsically resistant to venetoclax, as well as all NRAS-mutant myeloid progenitors from samples with monocytic hierarchies (58). Intriguingly, this group could not reproduce the association between clinical response and the monocytic phenotype alone (NRAS wild type) in clinical settings. Meanwhile, Pei and colleagues (13) found that both the FAB M5 classification and mutations in RAS pathway genes were associated with venetoclax resistance in univariate analyses, but only the M5 maturation state remained significant in a multivariate analysis. We believe that the discrepancies between these studies lie in their stratification criteria, as Sango and colleagues (58) segregated all M4 patients in the monocytic hierarchy group, whereas M4 leukemias from The Cancer Genome Atlas exhibited intermediate or mixed phenotypes in the study of Pei and colleagues (13).

Primary resistance to venetoclax in monocytic leukemias was associated with lower BCL2 expression and higher dependence on the antiapoptotic protein MCL1 for survival and stem cell function (13), consistent with a shift in mitochondrial apoptotic priming (73). Indeed, decreased expression of BCL2 and increased expression of MCL1 was also reported by Sango and colleagues (58) in LSC and progenitor cells from monocytic AMLs. Importantly, MCL1 was also one of the genes most significantly upregulated in LMPPs from patients with MDS with a granulomonocytic hierarchy expanded after relapse to HMAs (12), thereby suggesting that granulomonocytic MDS hierarchies and monocytic-biased AMLs rely on MCL1, rather than BCL2, for survival. Increased dependence on NF-κB signaling and MCL1 for survival by leukemic LMPPs was also reported in relapsed chronic myelomonocytic leukemia with a monocytic bias (74), which further supports a role for MCL1 in monocytic HSC/LSC survival and strongly indicates that hierarchy-specific oncogenic dependencies may be common to a broader spectrum of myeloid stem cell malignancies.

In further support that granulo/monocytic hierarchies are resistant to venetoclax, secondary resistance to this drug in patients with MDS and AML with primitive hierarchies has been associated with the emergence of granulo/monocytic stem cells and a shift in the hierarchy of the disease. In a follow-up study of patients treated in clinical trials of venetoclax and HMAs (75), Rodriguez-Sevilla et al. showed that all the patients with a primitive hierarchy who relapsed and did not have preexisting TP53 mutations [which confer cell-autonomous resistance to venetoclax (76)] underwent a granulo/monocytic shift in their differentiation trajectories and a replacement of the primitive HSC population with granulomonocytic HSCs (14). Consistent with a change in the identities of the cells of origin and their survival mechanisms, these granulo/monocytic HSCs significantly upregulated genes involved in NF-κB signaling, as well as MCL1, at the time of relapse (14). Importantly, the phenotypic switch was accompanied by the clonotypic expansion of preexisting BM cells harboring mutations in RUNX1 or STAG2, known to drive granulo/monocytic hierarchies in MDS (12). These findings suggest that venetoclax induces selective pressure in the HSC pool, granting preexisting granulo/monocytic HSCs a survival advantage that allows them to repopulate the BM with a new, venetoclax-resistant hematopoietic hierarchy. A similar observation has been reported in patients with AML with primitive (15) or mixed (primitive and monocytic; ref. 13) LSC populations treated with venetoclax–5-azacitidine, which selected for monocytic cells at the time of relapse. Further, the induction of NRAS mutations, which drive monocytic AML phenotypes, in aberrant induced pluripotent stem cell–derived HSPCs with baseline primitive hierarchies (77), conferred resistance to venetoclax (58). In both MDS and AML, the extent to which the resistance is derived from the lineage shift of the LSC or from the mutation itself remains to be further clarified.

Collectively, these studies demonstrated that MDS and AML hierarchies are determinants of sensitivity to venetoclax although they preserve certain phenotypic or clonal plasticity that may be leveraged as a drug resistance mechanism. In patients with primitive hierarchies, venetoclax therapy induces selective pressure toward the expansion of underlying HSC clones harboring genetic alterations that predispose to granulo/monocytic hierarchies, such as mutations in RUNX1 or STAG2. This may also be the case for NRAS mutations. Therefore, the presence of clones harboring those abnormalities could be predictive of shorter response durations and granulo/monocytic relapse in venetoclax-treated patients. Further investigations are needed to understand whether this mechanism feeds on preexisting granulo/monocytic subclones or can also be driven by the acquisition of new mutations or genomic aberrations by primitive HSCs. Intriguingly, in Rodriguez-Sevilla and colleagues (14) cohort, most of the patients with MDS with primitive hierarchies who relapsed after venetoclax therapy and underwent a trajectory rewiring had an isolated chromosome 8 trisomy (+8). Isolated +8 has been associated with a higher incidence of mutations in RUNX1 and STAG2 (78) and could, therefore, be considered an independent predisposing event for secondary venetoclax failure. Indeed, a correlative study of patient data pooled from three prospective clinical trials of venetoclax–HMA combinations (NCT04160052, NCT04550442, NCT04655755) found isolated +8 to be an adverse risk factor for OS by univariate analysis (75). Therefore, we posit that large genomic studies in MDS and AML should take hematopoietic hierarchies into account in order to identify additional associations with genetic drivers that may be valuable to understand and predict refractoriness to venetoclax.

Discussion

Mounting evidence is bringing to light the translational and clinical relevance of HSC/LSC biology in MDS and AML. We believe that the finding that hematopoietic hierarchies in these disorders reflect the dependence of the founding cells on specific survival mechanisms has great potential to open new avenues for precision medicine (Fig. 2).

Primitive and granulo/monocytic stem cells exhibit different signaling dependencies and, therefore, different drug sensitivities (bottom). Thus, hematopoietic hierarchies in patients with MDS and AML or genetic alterations correlated with such hierarchies should be used as biomarkers of drug sensitivity or resistance for clinical decision-making and response monitoring. Hematopoietic hierarchies may be altered by therapies that directly affect the viability of the stem cell compartment and generate selective pressure; therefore, hierarchy switching is a biomarker of acquired drug resistance. Although BCL2 inhibition with venetoclax is a paradigm for HSC hierarchy-dependent drug response, there is evidence that this phenomenon may be relevant to other drugs. Indeed, past analyses of LSC phenotypes in patients with AML treated with conventional chemotherapy showed that the LSC phenotype affects drug sensitivity (52) and that LSCs undergo immunophenotypic changes and an enrichment in primitive phenotypes at relapse (13, 70), suggesting that primitive hierarchies are more chemoresistant. An ex vivo drug screening in AML cells from 34 patients with different FAB subtypes reported distinct sensitivities to MEK and JAK inhibitors (trametinib and ruxolitinib, respectively) in monocytic cell subsets (79). Furthermore, leukemias originating in primitive LSCs have been shown to be more sensitive to inhibitors of lysine-specific demethylase 1 (80). Therefore, studies aimed at identifying other associations between MDS or AML hematopoietic hierarchies and drug response should be carried out in the future. Further, the existence of a similar phenomenon in other malignancies should be explored. LSC identity-specific drug sensitivity was also reported in chronic myelomonocytic leukemia (74), but it may not be limited to myeloid disorders. Indeed, T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) subtypes display differential expression of BH3 family molecules based on the differentiation stage of their cells of origin despite those being lineage-committed T-cell progenitors. Early T-cell progenitor ALL, in which the leukemic clone originates in a more primitive multipotent progenitor, exhibits BCL-2 dependency for survival, whereas the more mature T-cell ALL cells rely on BCL-XL (81). Thus, early T-cell progenitor ALL primary cells and lines have increased sensitivity to venetoclax in vitro and in vivo (81–83). Differentiation stage-specific addiction to survival mechanisms could, therefore, be a generalized phenomenon in which selective BH3 family protein expression may play a key role.

Lastly, in light of the causality between certain genetic aberrations and HSC hierarchies, it is tempting to speculate that yet-to-be-uncovered associations between hematopoietic hierarchies and specific genotypes may underlie otherwise unexplained correlations between clonal composition and drug sensitivity (84). The growing variety of novel single-cell technologies, such as combined DNA sequencing and protein detection and multiomic analysis methods, should be used to further delve into the genetic and regulatory mechanisms that sustain primitive and granulo/monocytic hierarchies at the HSC level in order to uncover other hierarchy-specific vulnerabilities.

Future Directions

From the clinical standpoint, it is imperative that we translate this quickly growing body of evidence into practice. First, we propose that MDS/AML hierarchies should be used as response biomarkers to better select and stratify patients in clinical trials, thus improving patient outcomes and decreasing the number of trials that fail due to high heterogeneity masking small subsets of responders.

Ideally, hierarchies should be defined on the basis of the presence of a marked granulo/monocytic transcriptomic signature in the stem cell compartment; however, transcriptomic analyses are still far from being implemented in routine clinical work-up. Other techniques such as flow cytometry, genomic screening, and morphology evaluations could serve as proxies in real-world practice (Fig. 3). In MDS, hierarchy assignment could be easily implemented in routine clinical immunophenotypic analyses by incorporating a few key classic HSPC markers in preexisting flow cytometry panels. In this study, we propose the combination of antibodies against CD34, CD38, and CD45RA, along with any available mature lineage (Lin) markers, as the minimal requirement to detect granulo/monocytic stem and progenitor cells, including LMPPs (Lin−CD34+CD38−CD45RA+) and GMPs (Lin−CD34+CD38+CD45RA+). After the assessment of the frequencies of those populations in live BM cells, the logarithmic regression classifier included in the “Methods” section of Ganan-Gomez and colleagues (12) could be used to confidently assign samples to the corresponding group, in which %LMPP would be the percentage of CD45RA+ cells among the Lin−CD34+CD38−, and %GMP would be the percentage of CD45RA+ cells among the Lin−CD34+CD38+. Although evidence of genetic associations is not strong enough for genetic data to be used as a classifier on its own, results from next-generation sequencing could be used to improve stratification in cases with unclear/mixed hierarchies and the presence of mutations in TP53, RUNX1, or STAG2. The stratification of patients with AML poses a different challenge, as there are no canonical LSC surface markers expressed in every patient. Pei and colleagues (15) used CD45dim, CD34+, and/or CD117+ as primitive markers and CD45bright, CD11b+, CD14+, CD36+, and/or CD64+ as monocytic markers. Phenotyping results could be combined with morphologic information (specifically, FAB categories M0–2 would be indicative of primitive hierarchies, vs. M5 as monocytic hierarchies) and genetic data (mutations in TP53 and NPM1 or FLT3-ITD as primitive AML and RUNX1-RUNX1T1 as monocytic AML) to strengthen the stratification. Caution is advised with other FAB categories, in which improved phenotyping strategies will be of help. In some of these cases, the more specific monocytic immunophenotype CD117−CD244−GPR56−CD64+ proposed by Pei and colleagues (15) may prove useful. Those markers could be implemented in routine measurable residual disease testing, as they could be useful in the detection of a phenotypic switch preceding relapse. Likewise, increasing the set of variants assessed by next-generation sequencing during measurable residual disease monitoring could help spot expanding clones harboring granulo/monocytic-predisposing mutations and, therefore, enable the early detection of acquired resistance.

Proposed implementation of hematopoietic hierarchy assessment in clinical practice in the setting of clinical trials. Minor adjustments could be implemented in routine clinical testing to allow patient stratification into primitive or granulo/monocytic hierarchies. Phenotypic studies, such as flow cytometry analysis, could be adapted to allow the quantification of Lin−CD34+CD38−CD45RA+ and Lin−CD34+CD38+CD45RA+ cells in MDS or CD45dim CD34+/CD117+ and CD45hi CD11b+/CD14+/CD36+/CD64+ or CD45hi CD64+CD244−GPR56− in AML. The FAB classification of AML could also be informative in this step. Genetic information from next-generation sequencing and cytogenetic analysis could be incorporated into the criteria, particularly in inconclusive cases. Once stratified, patients could be enrolled in clinical trials, prospectively followed for response monitoring and included in separate cohorts in correlative studies. The MDS and AML fields have witnessed numerous drugs fail phase III clinical trials despite having had fantastic results in phase II, without knowing the biological reasons. Prospective stratification prior to trial inclusion would improve the interpretation of correlative studies in the different response groups, enable biology-driven drug discovery efforts in cohorts of refractory patients, and guide clinical decisions about alternative lines of therapy. Furthermore, previously disregarded evidence-based therapeutic options could be reevaluated in those who fail trials, on the basis of their hierarchy. For example, according to the aforementioned studies, MCL1 inhibitors could be an attractive alternative in venetoclax-refractory patients with granulo/monocytic trajectories. Although the efficacy of MCL1 inhibitors in combination with venetoclax was demonstrated in vitro and in vivo (85–88), their elevated toxicity in clinical trials hindered their immediate clinical application (89). Yet lower, and therefore safer, doses may be effective in patients with granulo/monocytic hierarchies, particularly after relapse, or in patients with primitive hierarchies and evidence of minor clones with mutations in RUNX1, STAG2, or RAS family genes, in whom the depletion of granulo/monocytic LSCs (or LMPPs) may prolong the response to venetoclax. Additionally, we believe that any novel therapeutic targets uncovered by the deeper study of MDS/AML hierarchies in the near future should be promptly tested in preclinical trials and considered for clinical evaluation. In summary, we propose to utilize recent advancements in biology, specifically the understanding of stem cell hierarchies, as essential tools to assess differences in efficacy in future clinical trials and to translate the lessons learned in the clinic back into the laboratory to turn this knowledge into truly tailored medicine for patients with MDS and AML in the near future.
