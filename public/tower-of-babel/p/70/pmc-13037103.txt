Fear of disease progression among caregivers of outpatient children with perianal abscess: a cross-sectional survey
Authors: Ping Li, Yongping Wu, Liangfang Cheng, Yajuan Xu, Xiaohong Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPerianal abscess is a common pediatric anorectal infectious disease, and caregivers of affected children often experience fear of disease progression. This survey aimed to evaluate the fear of disease progression among caregivers of outpatient children with perianal abscess, to provide insights for clinical nursing practice.MethodThe primary caregivers of children with perianal abscess who attended the Anorectal Surgery Outpatient Clinic of hospital from January 2025 to July 2025 were included. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire (including general information and a validated Fear of Disease Progression Scale with Cronbach’s α = 0.894). Clinical trial number: not applicable.ResultsA total of 214 primary caregivers were included. The caregivers showed a moderately high level of fear of disease progression (46.18 ± 9.20), with the highest score in the Emotional Response dimension. Children’s age(r = -0.590), only-child status(r = 0.588), caregivers’ age (r = -0.585), educational background (r = -0.601) and household residence(r = 0.615) were correlated with fear scores (all P < 0.05). Multiple linear regression (R2 = 0.596, P < 0.001) identified those key influencing factors: Positive predictors included household residence (P = 0.001), child’s only-child status (P = 0.017), and younger caregiver age (P = 0.024). Negative predictors included higher caregiver educational background (P = 0.008) and older child age (P = 0.014).ConclusionFear of disease progression in these caregivers is jointly affected by child-related, caregiver-related, and environmental factors. Introduction

Perianal abscess is a common anorectal infectious disease in pediatrics, characterized by a clinical course of "high recurrence tendency, requirement for long-term outpatient follow-up, and potential need for surgical intervention" [1]. For young children (especially those under 3 years old), disease-related symptoms such as pain and defecation discomfort, combined with treatment procedures (e.g., local dressing changes, antibiotic administration), often trigger intense crying [2, 3]. This not only reflects the child’s physical distress but also amplifies the psychological burden on caregivers. As the core participants in disease management, pediatric caregivers (primarily parents) are tasked with continuous monitoring of the child’s symptom changes, coordinating outpatient treatment with family life, and bearing the pressure of anticipating disease progression—including concerns about complications such as abscess rupture or anal fistula formation, as well as potential impacts of long-term treatment on the child’s growth and development [4, 5]. Such fears stem not only from the inherent uncertainty of the disease but also from the unique care context: young children have limited ability to express their discomfort, forcing caregivers to independently interpret disease-related signals. Collectively, caregiver fear of disease progression (FoP) emerges as a critical factor influencing both their mental health and the quality of family care for children with perianal abscess.

Current research on pediatric perianal abscess primarily focuses on clinical treatment strategies (e.g., timing of antibiotic use, selection of surgical methods) and efficacy evaluation, while attention to "caregivers’ psychological experiences" remains in the preliminary stage [6]. Notably, existing literature lacks in-depth analysis of the associations between FoP intensity and caregivers’ demographic characteristics (e.g., age, educational level) or the child’s disease status. This research gap prevents clinical staff from accurately identifying the core concerns of this specific caregiver population, thereby hindering the delivery of targeted psychological support and care guidance.

With the transition of pediatric nursing toward the bio-psycho-social holistic care model, addressing caregivers’ psychological status and optimizing family care support have become key priorities for improving pediatric care quality. Children with perianal abscess receiving outpatient care require prolonged treatment cycles, with caregivers assuming responsibility for most home-based care tasks (e.g., local wound care, dietary adjustments). FoP intensity directly affects the standardization of caregivers’ care behaviors: excessive fear may lead to unnecessary frequent hospital visits or unauthorized care plan modifications, while insufficient fear may result in delayed monitoring of disease deterioration. To fill the aforementioned research gap, this study aims to three key objectives: (1) systematically investigate the current status and dimensional characteristics of FoP among caregivers of outpatients with perianal abscess; (2) identify child-related, caregiver-related, and environmental predictors of FoP; and (3) propose a targeted, three-pillar nursing intervention framework (hierarchical psychological counseling, precision information support, family function collaboration) tailored to this population. This investigation will not only alleviate caregivers’ psychological stress and improve family care efficiency but also provide empirical evidence for pediatric medical staff to formulate integrated outpatient care plans combining disease treatment, psychological support, and family care.

Methods

Study design

This study adopted a cross-sectional survey design, with "caregivers of outpatient children with perianal abscess" as the research subjects. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire, aiming to describe the current level of caregivers’ fear of disease progression, analyze its influencing factors, and propose targeted nursing strategies based on clinical practice. The study adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and all research procedures were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Nanjing Children’s Hospital (Ethics Approval No.: 202510075–1) to ensure the scientificity and ethical compliance of the research process. All participants signed an informed consent form. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Research subjects

Convenience sampling was used to select the primary caregivers of children with perianal abscess who attended the Anorectal Surgery Outpatient Clinic of hospital from January 2025 to July 2025. A "primary caregiver" was defined as an individual who lived with the child, assumed primary care responsibilities (e.g., daily care, accompanying medical consultations, participation in decision-making), and provided continuous care for ≥ 1 month (predominantly parents, with consideration for other relatives such as grandparents).

The inclusion and exclusion criteria for this study were as follows:

Inclusion criteria: ① The child was diagnosed with perianal abscess via clinical symptoms, physical signs, and ultrasound examination, and was in the outpatient treatment or follow-up stage; ② The caregiver was ≥ 18 years old and had full capacity for civil conduct; ③ The caregiver could understand the questionnaire content, communicate in Chinese, and complete the questionnaire independently or with the assistance of researchers; ④ The caregiver provided informed consent and voluntarily participated in the study.

Exclusion criteria: ① The child had concurrent severe organic diseases (e.g., congenital heart disease, malignant tumors) or intellectual and developmental disorders; ② The caregiver refused to participate in the survey.

Sample size calculation

The minimum sample size was determined using the standard formula for estimating proportions in cross-sectional studies [7], combined with findings from previous research [8] on caregiver psychological distress in pediatric populations and a pre-survey of 30 caregivers conducted in this study. Key parameters were defined as follows: two-tailed significance level (α) = 0.05, expected proportion of caregivers with moderate-to-severe fear of disease progression (P) = 0.5 (a conservative value that maximizes sample size representativeness), and allowable error (δ) = 0.08. A 20% correction coefficient was added to account for potential questionnaire loss and invalid responses. The basic sample size was calculated as n₀ = Z2α/₂ × P(1-P)/δ2 ≈ 150, and the final corrected sample size was determined to be n = 150 × 1.2 = 180 cases.

Research instruments

A self-designed structured questionnaire was used for data collection in this study. The questionnaire included 2 parts, both of which underwent rigorous reliability and validity testing:

General Information Questionnaire: Self-designed based on the research objectives, covering two dimensions: ① Basic information of the child (gender, age, weight, disease duration, disease severity [classified by abscess size: mild: ≤ 2 cm, moderate: 2–5 cm, severe: > 5 cm], medical expense payment method, and whether the child was an only child); ② Basic information of the caregiver (relationship with the child, age, educational background, occupation type, employment status, religious belief, average monthly household income per person, and household residence).

Fear of Progression Questionnaire (FoP-Q): A previously validated FoP-Q [9, 10] was adopted. This questionnaire consists of 12 items, including 3 dimensions: Emotional Response, Family Life, and Learning Process. A 5-point Likert scale was used, ranging from "1 point (Never)" to "5 points (Always)", with a total score ranging from 12 to 60. A higher score indicates a stronger fear of the child’s disease progression among caregivers. In the pre-survey of this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of the questionnaire was 0.894, and the Content Validity Index (CVI) was 0.96, indicating good reliability and validity.

Data collection

All researchers involved in the survey received unified training, covering the research objectives, interpretation of questionnaire content, survey procedures, standards for signing informed consent forms, and key points of quality control, to ensure consistent operational standards among all researchers. A pre-survey was conducted with 30 caregivers, and questionnaire expressions (e.g., simplification of professional terminology) were adjusted based on feedback to optimize the survey process. In the outpatient waiting area, after the child’s medical consultation, researchers explained the research objectives, content, risks, and rights to eligible caregivers in detail. After obtaining consent and having the caregivers sign the informed consent form, the questionnaire was distributed. For caregivers with poor eyesight or low educational background, researchers provided one-on-one verbal assistance to complete the questionnaire (strictly adhering to the "neutrality principle" without guiding responses). After the questionnaire was completed, researchers checked its completeness on-site, promptly reminded caregivers to supplement missing items, and further verified items with logical contradictions to ensure questionnaire validity.

Data analysis

First, data from all valid questionnaires were double-entered by two independent researchers using EpiData 3.1 software to control for entry errors. After data entry, a consistency test was conducted (Kappa coefficient > 0.95) to ensure data accuracy. Subsequently, SPSS 26.0 statistical software was used for data analysis, with specific methods as follows: For descriptive statistics, frequencies and composition ratios were used to describe categorical data (e.g., caregivers’ educational background), and mean ± standard deviation was used to describe continuous data (e.g., caregivers’ age, total score of the FoP-Q). For univariate analysis, the chi-square (χ2) test was used for categorical data to compare differences in FoP-Q scores among caregivers with different demographic characteristics, while the t-test or analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for continuous data; if variance was heterogeneous, Welch’s corrected ANOVA was adopted. For multivariate analysis, the total score of the FoP-Q was used as the dependent variable, and variables with P < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were used as independent variables. Multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to identify the independent influencing factors of caregivers’ fear of disease progression. For correlation analysis, Pearson correlation analysis or Spearman correlation analysis was selected based on data distribution characteristics to explore the association between caregivers’ FoP-Q scores and relevant characteristics. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with a significance level set at α = 0.05. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 214 primary caregivers of outpatient children with perianal abscess were included in this study (Table 1). Assessment using the FoP-Q (Table 2) showed that the mean total score of caregivers’ fear of disease progression was 46.18 ± 9.20, representing a moderately high level. Among the dimensions, the Emotional Response dimension had the highest score (24.02 ± 4.70), followed by the Family Life dimension (14.48 ± 3.54), indicating that caregivers’ core concerns were concentrated on emotional experiences. As illustrated in Supplementary Figure S1, caregivers demonstrated a moderately high overall level of fear of disease progression, with the Emotional Response dimension contributing the largest proportion to the total FoP-Q score.

Univariate analysis of the association between children’s and caregivers’ characteristics and fear scores (Table 1) revealed statistically significant differences in the following variables: For children’s characteristics, age (P = 0.012) and only-child status (P = 0.024) were significant—caregivers of children < 1 year old had a higher fear score (49.08 ± 8.87) than those of children aged 1–3 years (45.13 ± 9.07) and > 3 years (43.27 ± 9.15); caregivers of only children had a higher score (48.44 ± 9.15) than those of non-only children (42.01 ± 8.82). No significant effects were observed for children’s gender, weight, or medical expense payment method (P > 0.05). For caregivers’ characteristics, age (P = 0.031) and educational background (P = 0.009) were significant—caregivers < 30 years old had a higher score (48.18 ± 9.35) than those ≥ 30 years old (44.23 ± 8.46); scores decreased gradually with increasing educational background (from 49.96 ± 9.19 for junior high school or below to 41.06 ± 8.87 for bachelor’s degree or above). Household residence approached statistical significance (P = 0.034), while other characteristics (e.g., relationship with the child, occupation) had no significant effects (P > 0.05).

Correlation analysis (Table 3) showed that children’s age was significantly negatively correlated with caregivers’ fear scores (r = −0.590, P = 0.016), and only-child status was significantly positively correlated with fear scores (r = 0.588, P = 0.015). For caregivers, age was significantly negatively correlated with fear scores (r = −0.585, P = 0.027), educational background was significantly negatively correlated with fear scores (r = −0.601, P = 0.021), and caregivers residing in rural areas had a significantly positive correlation in fear scores compared with those in urban areas (r = 0.615, P = 0.017). No significant correlations were found for other characteristics (P > 0.05).

Multiple linear regression analysis was conducted with the total fear score as the dependent variable and variables with P < 0.05 in the univariate analysis as independent variables (Table 4). The model showed good fit (R2 = 0.596, F = 34.166, P < 0.001) and explained 59.6% of the variance in fear scores. Positive predictors included household residence (P = 0.001), child’s only-child status (P = 0.017), and younger caregiver age (P = 0.024). Negative predictors included higher caregiver educational background (P = 0.008) and older child age (P = 0.014). Specifically, caregivers had a higher level of fear if they resided in rural areas, cared for an only child, or were younger; conversely, caregivers with higher educational background or caring for older children had a lower level of fear. Discussion

This study found that the level of fear of disease progression among caregivers of outpatient children with perianal abscess was moderately high, with the highest score in the Emotional Response dimension. This is consistent with the general characteristics of psychological burden among caregivers of children with chronic diseases. Previous studies on caregivers of children with asthma or leukemia have pointed out that the "infectious nature," "recurrence tendency," and "potential need for surgery" of the disease are core triggers for caregivers’ anxiety. As an anorectal infectious disease, perianal abscess often causes local pain and defecation discomfort in children, leading to crying. Young children, however, cannot clearly express their symptoms, which further intensifies caregivers’ fear of "inaccurate judgment of the condition" and ultimately results in a prominent score in the Emotional Response dimension. In addition, the score in the Family Life dimension was the second highest, reflecting that caregivers not only focus on the child’s physical condition but also worry about the impact of the disease on daily family operations (such as the allocation of care time and financial expenditure). The lowest score was in the Learning Process dimension, which may be related to the fact that most children in this study were under 3 years old and had not yet entered the formal learning stage.

A post-hoc subgroup analysis was conducted to explore differences in FoP scores across children stratified by abscess severity (mild: ≤ 2 cm, moderate: 2–5 cm, severe: > 5 cm). Results showed no significant differences in total FoP scores (F = 2.13, P = 0.121) or scores across the three dimensions between subgroups. This finding suggests that caregivers’ fear in outpatient settings is primarily driven by the inherent uncertainty of the disease (e.g., recurrence risk, potential complications) rather than objective indicators of abscess severity. Collectively, these results indicate that clinical nursing should prioritize addressing caregivers’ emotional regulation needs while also providing targeted support for family functional adjustments.

Univariate and correlation analyses revealed that caregivers of younger children or only children reported significantly higher fear of disease progression (FoP) scores; child age was negatively correlated with FoP scores, while only-child status was positively correlated. This pattern can be explained by two interrelated mechanisms: disease cognition difficulty and family psychological expectations. For younger children, immature immune systems increase susceptibility to perianal abscess recurrence [11], and limited verbal expression abilities force caregivers to rely solely on behavioral cues (e.g., crying, feeding refusal) to assess condition severity. This information asymmetry amplifies uncertainty and excessive fear [12], whereas older children’s capacity to communicate discomfort, combined with more stable disease prognosis, reduces caregiver anxiety [13]. For only-child families, concentrated parental health expectations and lack of multi-child care experience trigger a distinct risk amplification effect [14]. Caregivers often direct all attention to the single child, intensifying concerns about long-term sequelae (e.g., anal fistula formation) and their impact on future quality of life— a psychological tendency less pronounced in multi-child families due to dispersed caregiving focus [15]. These findings highlight the need for differentiated communication strategies targeting families with young children or only children [16].

Caregiver-related factors (age and educational background) were also key correlates of FoP, with younger age and lower educational attainment linked to higher fear levels— a pattern rooted in care experience accumulation and health information literacy disparities. Younger caregivers, typically first-time parents, lack practical experience managing pediatric diseases like perianal abscess; their unfamiliarity with symptom changes and treatment protocols easily translates to anxiety [17]. In contrast, older caregivers often draw on prior care experiences or social networks to make more rational assessments of prognosis, mitigating fear [18]. Educational background further modulates FoP: caregivers with higher education demonstrate stronger abilities to access and interpret professional medical information (e.g., treatment guidelines, prognosis data), reducing fear of the unknown [19]. Conversely, those with lower education may struggle to understand specialized content (e.g., antibiotic risks, surgical indications) and are more susceptible to misleading non-professional information, exacerbating anxiety [20]. These observations underscore the importance of hierarchical information support tailored to caregivers’ age and educational profiles to optimize clinical communication.

The multiple linear regression model exhibited good fit (R2 = 0.596, P < 0.001) and high clinical utility, identifying household residence (rural), child’s only-child status, and younger caregiver age as positive FoP predictors, with higher caregiver education and older child age as negative predictors. Rural caregivers’ elevated fear merits specific attention, as it stems primarily from disparities in specialized medical resource access and health literacy. Rural medical institutions often lack expertise in perianal abscess management, requiring caregivers to undertake long-distance travel for urban tertiary care— a burden that increases financial and time costs while fostering concerns about delayed treatment [21]. Compounded by limited access to evidence-based disease information, rural caregivers face amplified uncertainty, driving higher FoP levels. This gap highlights the urgency of targeted interventions, such as medical resource allocation tilts toward rural areas and remote nursing guidance systems, to address regional disparities in care support [22].

Combined with the above research findings, clinical nursing strategies can be structured around three interconnected pillars: hierarchical psychological intervention, precision information support, and family function collaboration—with detailed implementation protocols and target populations summarized in Supplementary Table 1. First, for high-risk groups exhibiting elevated fear—including caregivers residing in rural areas, families with young children, only-child families, younger caregivers, and those with lower educational backgrounds—one-on-one psychological counseling should be prioritized [23]. Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) emerges as a valuable tool in this context, as it can help caregivers identify and reframe irrational cognitions associated with excessive fear [24]; for instance, sharing clinical cases to illustrate the prognostic patterns of perianal abscess can effectively mitigate catastrophic thinking about the child’s condition [25]. Second, precision information support should be tailored to address the varying needs of caregivers. For those with lower educational backgrounds, illustrated care manuals are recommended, with content focused on simplifying key practices such as symptom observation protocols and step-by-step local care procedures [26]. In contrast, younger caregivers may benefit more from interactive online education courses, which can integrate video demonstrations and real-time Q&A functions to enhance accessibility and engagement—addressing the preference of this demographic for digital learning and ensuring timely access to accurate disease-related information [27]. Third, strengthening family function collaboration involves targeted interventions to alleviate care-related burdens. For only-child families, linking them to family support groups can facilitate peer-to-peer experience sharing, reducing feelings of isolation and equipping caregivers with practical strategies from others in similar situations [28]. For rural families, establishing a remote follow-up mechanism is critical: specialist nurses can provide regular condition monitoring guidance via phone or video calls, minimizing the need for long-distance travel to medical facilities [29]. This approach should also include coordinating community medical resources to deliver basic nursing support locally, further easing the logistical and emotional strain on rural caregivers [30]. In addition, given that the Emotional Response dimension represents the core of caregivers’ fear, integrating emotional assessment into the routine outpatient nursing workflow for children with perianal abscess is essential [31]. This proactive measure allows for dynamic monitoring of caregivers’ psychological status, enabling early identification of escalating anxiety and timely intervention.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings and guiding future work. First, its cross-sectional design, while effective for capturing variable correlations at a single time point, prevents the establishment of causal relationships between identified factors (e.g., caregiver age, child’s only-child status) and fear levels—for example, the observed negative correlation between caregiver education and fear cannot rule out confounding influences like health information access or social support, which may covary with education. Second, convenience sampling from a single tertiary children’s hospital in China limits generalizability: the sample overrepresents caregivers of young children and those accessing urban specialized care, excluding groups like caregivers from grassroots facilities or underdeveloped rural regions outside Jiangsu Province, introducing potential selection bias. Third, the study focused on describing fear status and identifying factors but did not explore underlying mechanisms (e.g., whether rural caregivers’ higher fear stems from limited medical resources, low health literacy, or cultural beliefs) or empirically test proposed nursing strategies—gaps that could be addressed via mixed-methods research (quantitative surveys + qualitative interviews) and randomized controlled trials for intervention validation in future studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the caregivers exhibited a moderately high overall level of fear of disease progression: the Emotional Response dimension was their primary concern. From the perspective of influencing factors, child-related characteristics (younger age and only-child status), caregiver-related factors (younger age and lower educational background), and environmental conditions (rural residence) were associated with increased fear of disease progression. In contrast, older child age and higher caregiver educational background were linked to reduced fear of disease progression; notably, caregivers in rural areas reported significantly higher fear of disease progression than those in urban areas, a difference closely tied to disparities in medical resource accessibility and health information acquisition channels. These findings confirm that fear of disease progression among caregivers of outpatient children with perianal abscess arises from the combined effects of child-related, caregiver-related, and environmental factors. Accordingly, targeted nursing interventions should be developed for high-risk groups in clinical practice, including rural caregivers, younger caregivers, those with lower educational backgrounds, and families with young or only children. Specifically, hierarchical psychological interventions can be implemented to correct irrational cognitions; precision information support (e.g., illustrated care manuals, online educational courses) can enhance the accuracy of caregivers’ disease-related knowledge; and family function collaboration (e.g., remote follow-up services, peer support groups) can alleviate care-related burdens. Additionally, integrating emotional assessment into the routine outpatient nursing workflow for children with perianal abscess may enable the provision of integrated services that combine physical care for the child with psychological support for the caregiver.
