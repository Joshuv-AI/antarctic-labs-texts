Challenges in redirecting rumen hydrogen flow in vitro: insights from new hydrogenotrophic isolates
Authors: Samantha Joan Noel, Ole Højberg, Mirka Thorsteinsson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReducing methane (CH4) released from the rumen of cattle is essential for lowering greenhouse gas emissions from livestock. Methanogens, the archaea responsible for producing CH4, are the dominant hydrogen (H2) sink in the rumen. Hydrogen is a by-product of normal rumen fermentation, and increased H2 levels, resulting from inhibiting methanogenesis, may have negative effects on certain rumen fermentation processes. However, increased H2 levels may also open the possibility for H2 to be redirected into alternative pathways, harbored by H2-utilizing (hydrogenotrophic) bacteria that would normally not be competitive with methanogens. This study aimed to isolate hydrogen-utilizing bacteria, characterize their hydrogen metabolism in pure culture, and evaluate whether selected strains could reduce hydrogen accumulation in an in vitro rumen system where methanogenesis was chemically inhibited.ResultsA culture collection of 182 bacterial isolates was generated. When grown in selective medium with H2 and CO2 as substrates, 83 isolates produced acetate and 32 isolates significantly lowered dissolved H2 (by 7.7 to 84.6%.) compared with controls. Twelve isolates with the highest hydrogen-utilizing potential were tested in an in vitro rumen batch system (rumen fluid amended with corn silage), where methanogenesis was inhibited with iodoform. Iodoform reduced CH4 formation by 66.5% and increased H2 accumulation by 1400%. However, none of the twelve isolates reduced H2 or CH4 in the in vitro system, nor did they alter total gas production, fermentation acid profiles, or feed degradability. Remaining dissolved H2 measured in pure culture indicated that the isolates required substantially higher dissolved H2 concentrations than what is theorised to be in the in vitro bottles, where headspace gas was continuously released.ConclusionsAlthough many newly isolated bacteria were capable of utilizing H2 under controlled pure-culture conditions, they did not reduce H2 accumulation in an in vitro rumen system with inhibited methanogenesis under the conditions tested. The predicted low dissolved H2 concentrations in the system likely prevented the isolates from growing autotrophically. These findings indicate that alternative hydrogen-utilizing bacteria may require different in vitro conditions or in vivo environments to effectively act as H2 sinks when methanogenesis is suppressed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12866-026-05399-5.Keywords: Culturomics, Acetogens, Hydrogen dynamics, Iodoform, Methane

Introduction

Hydrogenotrophs are microorganisms that consume H2 as part of their metabolism and are integral for maintaining normal functioning of the gut microbiota [1–3]. The dominant methanogen group in the rumen are the hydrogenotrophic methanogens that form CH4 from H2 and CO2 [4]. Enteric fermentation in dairy cattle is a major source of CH4 emissions globally and represent a potential loss of feed energy [5, 6]. Although CH4 production is negative from an environmental and host energy perspective, methanogens function to remove H2 from the rumen, keeping dissolved H2 low and promoting rumen fermentation. The rumen is also inhabited by hydrogenotrophic bacteria that utilize H2; these include acetogens that can metabolize H2 via the reductive acetogenesis (Wood-Ljungdahl) pathway, sulfate reducing bacteria (SRB) as well as some bacteria reduce H2 via fumarate or nitrate reduction (e.g. Selenomonas). However, these bacteria are not the dominant utilizers of H2 in the mature rumen and in the case of acetogens, this is due to methanogens’ ability to outcompete them for H2. In the same niche, there would be competition among methanogens, SRB and acetogens for H2 as a substrate and if H2 could be directed to the reductive acetogenesis pathway, this would result in reduced CH4 production and increased acetate production, thus having the twofold benefit of reducing greenhouse gas emissions from ruminants as well as more efficiently utilizing the ingested feed matter for ruminant energy. However, theoretically, considering thermodynamics and H2 thresholds, the most successful hydrogenotrophs are the SRB, followed by methanogens while acetogens are the least competitive of the three [1, 7]. Thermodynamically, the calculated Gibbs free energy (ΔG°’) for oxidation of H2 by SRB (ΔG°’= -152.2 kJ/mol) is favored over methanogens (ΔG°’= -131 kJ/mol) and, methanogens are thermodynamically favored over reductive acetogens (ΔG°’= -95 kJ/mol) [7]. The H2 thresholds further supports this order of favorability, where microorganisms with the highest substrate (H2) affinity will compete more successfully at lower H2 concentrations. Thus, the order for lower substrate thresholds is SRB (10–20 ppm), methanogens (30–100 ppm) and reductive acetogens (400–950 ppm) [1]. This means that SRB and methanogens could keep the H2 concentration below the threshold for reductive acetogens to be able utilize it. However, within the rumen, the relative favorability of each pathway is not determined by H₂ concentration alone, but is also influenced by end-product accumulation and the availability of suitable electron acceptors. SRB and for that matter fumarate and nitrate reducers are usually low abundant and do not contribute substantially to the H2 utilization pathways, likely due to limited availability of the terminal electron acceptors (sulfate, nitrate and fumarate). In addition to hydrogenotrophic pathways, alternative hydrogen sinks in the rumen include propionate formation—particularly via the fumarate/succinate pathway—which competes with methanogenesis for H2 [8]; accordingly, reduction of methanogenesis is often associated with increased propionate production as H2 is redirected toward this pathway [9].

The question remains, if the methanogens are inhibited, can the unused H2 then be utilized by hydrogenotrophic bacteria, thereby reducing negative effects on rumen fermentation and animal productivity? Sterile lambs inoculated with a functional microbiota lacking methanogens have shown the potential for reductive acetogens to sustain a functional rumen and replace methanogens as rumen H2 sink; when acetogens were inoculated at birth, they rapidly established to high cell densities (109-1010 cells g-1 in a few weeks) in the absence of methanogens [10]. Further, metagenomic analysis have revealed alternative H2 uptake pathways to be significantly upregulated in low CH4 yield sheep [11]. Moreover, the acetogenic strains Eubacterium limosum DSM 20,543 and Blautia producta DSM 2950 have both been shown to reduce H2 under in vitro fermentation of rumen fluid [12, 13]. Accordingly, reductive acetogens could potentially play an important role in maintaining H2 balance in the rumen when methanogens are inhibited.

In the present study, we aimed to identify hydrogenotrophic bacteria, particularly focusing on reductive acetogens, with the potential to become the dominant H2 sink when inoculated into the rumen in combination with a methanogenesis inhibitor. A collection of potentially hydrogenotrophic bacteria was isolated and their ability to utilize H2 and their metabolite profiles were assessed, resulting in the selection of 12 promising bacterial strains; it was hypothesized that inoculating these strains into in vitro rumen fluid batch system would alleviate the H2 build up when methanogenesis was inhibited.

Materials and methods

Media

Anaerobic media were made using the Hungate technique in glass tubes sealed with butyl rubber stoppers and perforated caps.

Isolation media

Two media formulations were used in the isolation of potential acetogens, a selective medium for acetogens, medium AC: B1 [14] (final pH of 6.8) with the addition of 2-bromoethanesulfonic acid (BES, final concentration 2.5 mM) and RM02 with the addition of NoSubRFV [15]. Medium AC: B1 contained per L; 0.28 g KH2PO4, 0.94 g K2HPO4, 0.14 g NaCl, 0.16 g KCl, 0.2 g MgSO4, 0.5 g NH4Cl, 0.001 g CaCl2.H2O, 0.5 g yeast extract, 1 mL of each trace element solution SL10 [15] and selenite/tungstate solution (per L 0.5 g NaOH, 3 mg Na2SeO3.5H2O, 4 mg Na2WO4.2H2O), 100 mL of clarified rumen fluid and 0.001 g resazurin. Components were boiled and allowed to cool under 80:20 N2:CO2 gas (minimum 40 min). Once cooled, 6 g NaHCO3, 1 mL of vitamin 10 solution [15] and 10 ml of reducing agent (2.5% wt/vol each L-cysteine.HCl.H2O and Na2S.9H2O, pH 10) were added. Final pH was adjusted to 6.8 and 10 mL dispensed into Hungate tubes before autoclaving at 121 °C for 15 min. After inoculation, the tube headspace was replaced with 80:20 H2:CO2 gas mixture at an initial pressure of 150 kPa.

RM02 base medium contained per L, 1.4 g KH2PO4, 0.6 g (NH4)2SO4, 1.5 g KCl, 1 mL of each trace element solution SL10 and selenite/tungstate solution, 50 µL 0.1% (w/v) resazurin solution, 4.2 g NaHCO3, 0.5 g L-cysteine.HCl.H2O and 950 mL distilled water. Medium components apart from the NaHCO3 and L-cysteine were brought to the boil and cooled under O2-free 100% CO2 gas. Once cool, NaHCO3 and L-cysteine were added. Final pH was adjusted to 6.5, and 9.5 mL of medium was dispensed into Hungate tubes before autoclaving at 121 °C for 15 min. After sterilization, 0.5 mL of filter sterilized NoSubRFV (containing per 100 ml of clarified rumen fluid, 1 g yeast extract, 2 mL Vitamin 10 concentrate, 1.63 g MgCL2.6H2O, 1.18 g CaCl2.2H2O) was added to each tube. Once diluted in the base RM02 medium, NoSubRFV had concentrations of 5% rumen fluid, 4 mM each Ca2+ and Mg2+, and 0.5 g of yeast extract per liter. Tubes were stored in the dark for at least 24 h before use. After inoculation with a strain, the tube headspace was replaced with 80:20 H2:CO2 gas mixture at an initial pressure of approx. 150 kPa.

Solid medium was made with the addition of 1.5% agar to the respective AC.B1 or RM02 media. Agar plates were poured in an anaerobic chamber with a gas environment of 100% CO2 and allowed to dry for one day before use. Plates were transferred to anaerobic jars where the gas was replaced with 80:20 H2:CO2 gas mixture. Gas was topped up in anaerobic jars every second day.

Bacterial strains Bac PC1 and Bac PC8 were isolated from a methylotrophic methanogen enrichment culture as described in [16] on RM02 + NoSubSASF medium which is RM02 base medium supplemented with NoSubSASF. Addition of NoSubSASF results in final concentrations of 5% rumen fluid, 4 mM each Ca2+ and Mg2+, 20 mM sodium acetate, 60mM sodium formate, 15 mM trimethylamine and 40mM methanol. The isolation was performed on RM02 + NoSubSASF plates as described above. To include a potential fumarate reducing hydrogenotroph, two isolates (ss180 and ss191) were isolated from rumen content on Selenomonas selective medium as described by [17].

Medium for VFA and H2 determinations

A modified version of RM02 medium (RM02 + rumen) was used when VFA determinations were made and when hydrogen utilization was tested. RM02 + rumen differs from RM02 + NoSubRFV only in when the rumen fluid was added and results in the same final composition. In this version per L of medium, 50 mL of twice spun rumen fluid (18,000 g for 20 min) replaced 50 mL of water in the base medium. The NoSub-Mix was made replacing the clarified rumen fluid in NoSubRFV with water. This allowed an even distribution of rumen fluid in the tubes and gave uniform background VFA concentrations.

Routine growth medium

For routine growth, isolates were grown in RM02 medium with abundant substrates, supplemented with GenRFV. GenRFV contains per 100 mL of clarified rumen fluid, 0.36 g D-glucose, 0.34 g D-cellobiose, 0.30 g of each D-xylose and L-arabinose, 0.88 mL Na L-lactate 50% syrup, 2 g of each yeast extract, casamino acids and Bacto-Peptone, 2 mL Vitamin 10 concentrate, 1.63 g MgCL2.6H2O, 1.18 g CaCl2.2H2O. GenRFV was filter sterilized and kept under 100% N2 gas. To get final concentrations of 5% (vol/vol) rumen fluid, 0.5 mM cellobiose, 1 mM each of glucose, xylose and arabinose, 5 mM lactate, 4 mM each Ca2+ and Mg2+, and 1 g each of yeast extract, casamino acids and Bacto-Peptone per liter, 0.5 mL of GenRFV was added to 9.5 mL of RM02 base medium.

Generation of a culture collection of hydrogenotrophic bacteria

Samples were obtained from animal experiments that were conducted at Aarhus University, AU Viborg – Research Centre Foulum, Denmark, and complied with the guidelines set out by the Danish Ministry of Environment and Food. All animals were owned by Aarhus University. Bovine rumen content for isolation was obtained either via a flora scoop (stomach tube) collection device or directly by taking grab samples from rumen cannulated cows and collecting the fluid by hand squeezing the solids. Rumen fluid was collected from 15 different Holstein dairy cows at different times, fed a variety of diet formulations based on the Nordic Feed Evaluation system [18]. Details on the animals’ housing and care have been previously reported [19, 20]. Pig digesta were collected from the mid colon of two Danish Landrace × Yorkshire × Duroc crossbred pigs from the study of Panah et al. [21]. The pigs were healthy control animals, fed a basal diet containing 17.5% protein and were sacrificed at 64-days old by a bolt pistol and bled from the jugular vein for post-mortem sampling. Rumen liquid or pig digesta (1 mL) was immediately injected into a tube containing anaerobic RM02 base medium to minimize exposure to oxygen. The initially inoculated tubes were serially diluted 10 times in AC.B1 medium with 10-fold dilutions. Presumptive acetogens were enriched on AC.B1 liquid medium with 80:20 H2:CO2 gas mixture headspace and then isolated on AC.B1 or RM02 + NoSubRFV plates in anaerobic jars, also with the H2:CO2 gas mixture. The serially diluted enrichment cultures were sub-cultured 1–3 times in AC.B1 medium and grown for a week before 10µL of grown culture from dilutions (range 10 4 to 1010) was streaked onto agar plates. Single colonies were streaked twice onto fresh plates and microscopically examined before being considered pure and then transferred to liquid RM02 + NoSubRFV medium. Isolates were placed in a shaking incubator at 39 °C and given a minimum of 7 days to grow. All isolates were assigned a unique name code and stored anaerobically in glycerol (25% final conc) at -80 °C.

Control strains

Two acetogen strains, Blautia producta DSM 2950 and Eubacterium limosum DSM 20,543, and one SRB strain, Desulfovibrio piger DSM 749, were purchased from the DSMZ culture collection.

Isolate taxonomic identification

DNA was extracted from a well grown culture using the NucleoSpin DNA stool kit (Machery-Nagel, Düren, Germany) following the manufacturers methods. Full length 16 S rRNA gene was amplified with primers 27f and 1492r [22]. The PCR conditions were 1 µL of 10 pmol/µL of forward and reverse primer, 12.5 µL of TEMPase hot start master mix (Ampliqon, Denmark), 2µL of isolate DNA made up to 25µL with dH2O and cycled for 95 °C 15 min followed by 30 cycles of 95 °C for 30s, 52 °C for 30s and 72 °C for 40s with a final elongation of 72 °C for 10 min. PCR product was sent for sanger sequencing at Macrogen Korea using the 785 F (GGATTAGATACCCTGGTA) and 907R (CCGTCAATTCMTTTRAGTTT ) primers. Sequences were imported into codon code aligner v12.0.1 (CodonCode Corporation, MA, USA), trimmed, and assembled. For those sequences that would not assemble into one contig, the longest unassembled fragment was used. Near full length and partial 16 S rRNA genes were submitted to BLAST excluding environmental clones and the closest strain hit was recorded. The sequences for isolates reported in this study (56 isolates, Table S1) have been deposited in GenBank (accession numbers PZ338159-PZ338214).

PCR of the acsB gene

Isolates were checked for presence of the reductive acetogenesis marker gene acsB with primers ACS_f (CTBTGYGGDGCIGTIWSMTGG) and ACS_r (AARCAWCCRCADGADGTCATIGG) [23]. DNA obtained from the acetogenic strains B. producta and E. limosum were used as positive controls. The reaction mix contained 0.5 µL of 10 pmol/µL of forward and reverse primer, 6.25 µL of TEMPase hot start master mix (Ampiqon, Denmark), 1 µL of isolate DNA made up to 12.5 µL with dH2O and cycled for 95 °C 15 min followed by 35 cycles of 95 °C for 30s, 60 °C for 30s and 72 °C for 30s with a final elongation of 72 °C for 5 min. PCR products were mixed with loading buffer and ran on a 1% agarose gel containing gel red stain at 100 V for 35 min.

Growth of isolates for in vitro tests

To increase the number of bacterial cells added to the in vitro bottles without introducing large volumes of medium with abundant substrates, cells from a 10 mL of isolate culture grown in medium containing carbohydrates substrates (RM02 + GenRFV) were concentrated by centrifugation and added to a culture grown in 10 mL of selective medium RM02 + NoSubRFV.

Isolates (0.2 mL of overnight culture) were inoculated into 10mL tube of RM02 + GenRFV medium and into 10 mL of RM02 + NoSubRFV medium. After inoculation, the tube headspace was replaced with 80:20 H2:CO2 gas mixture at an initial pressure of 150 kPa. Tubes were incubated for 2 days at 39 °C with shaking. Cultures grown in RM02 + GenRFV medium were centrifuged at 15,000 g for 10 min to pellet the cells. Cell pellets were resuspended in 0.5 mL of RM02 base medium and added to the corresponding culture grown in RM02 + NoSubRFV before 2 mL was added to the in vitro bottles. The optical density of the grown cultures was measured (WPA colourwave CO7500 colorimeter). The number of bacterial cells added to the in vitro bottles from the 1st of the replicates was calculated by counting specific dilutions of the grown culture using a counting chamber (Burker-Turk) with a phase contrast microscope at 40x magnification (Olympus BH2). The total number of cells per mL was calculated from the average of 5 counted squares, the volume of the counted area and multiplied by the dilution factor of the counted suspension.

Hydrogen utilization measurements

The ability of a strain to utilize dissolved hydrogen was tested in RM02 + rumen medium. Overnight cultures of each isolate (0.2 mL) were inoculated into triplicate tubes of RM02 + rumen + NoSub-Mix medium. 2x triplicate uninoculated tubes (n = 6) were included as negative controls. Headspace gas was replaced with 80:20 H2:CO2 gas mixture and tubes were pumped for 10 s reaching an initial pressure of 150 kPa. Strains were incubated for 5 days at 39 °C in a shaking incubator. The optical density of the grown cultures was measured (WPA colourwave CO7500 colorimeter) and gas pressure in the tube measured with a digital pressure gauge (RAZE®).

Dissolved hydrogen concentration in liquid medium was measured with a H2-X sensor (Unisense, Aarhus, Denmark) coupled to a H2 UniAmp (Unisense, Aarhus, Denmark). The sensor signal was allowed to stabilize for 1 h before readings were taken. For each experiment, a new calibration curve was made with distilled water for the 0 value and with 80:20% H2:CO2 gas mix vigorously bubbled through distilled water for 5 min until readings stabilized for the high value. The salinity of the medium was measured to be 5.92 ppt and the calculated concentration of the dissolved hydrogen in the high reading were included in the calibration curve. Tubes were opened and readings were immediately taken for an average of 1 min until values stabilized. Results were recorded with the Unisense logger (Application version 3.4.200.33185 D-V. 1.13) and then further processed in Microsoft excel. The average from 15 data points (data recorded every second) across the period where the readings stabilized were extracted for each measurement. After dissolved hydrogen was measured 1 mL of culture or control from each tube was stored at -20 °C until analyzed for VFA content.

VFA determinations from grown cultures

Concentrations of VFA and lactate were analysed according to [24] and [25], respectively, using a Hewlett Packard gas chromatograph model 6890 (Agilent Technologies Inc., Wilmington, DE, USA) with a flame ionization detector and a 30-m SGE BP1 column (Scientific Instrument Services Inc., NJ, USA). Results are reported as VFA levels corrected for the background levels from the controls.

In vitro rumen batch culture tests

The H2 utilization ability of 12 selected bacteria was tested using the ANKOM (ANKOM Technology, Macedon, USA) in vitro system, by inoculating them into buffered rumen fluid amended with corn silage (0.5 ± 0.02 g DM) and using iodoform as methanogenesis inhibitor. Treatments included (a) inhibitor + bacterial culture, (b) inhibitor + medium control, (c) inhibitor + water control and (d) a no-inhibitor control, while buffered rumen fluid incubated alone (no corn silage) with 0.1 mL of 99% ethanol served as blank samples. A fixed volume (2 mL) of bacterial cultures, uninoculated medium or water were added to Duran® bottles (capacity: 132 ± 1.1 mL) together with corn silage (ash: 3.54%; CP: 7.77%; NDF: 32.9%; starch: 35.1% on a DM basis). Iodoform dissolved in 99% ethanol (0.525 mg/mL) was used as an anti-methanogenic feed additive to increase the amount of available H2 [26]. Hence, 0.1 mL of the iodoform solution were added to the fermentation bottles. The effect of the isolate-medium alone was investigated by incubating corn silage with 2 mL of medium and 0.1 mL iodoform. Additionally, corn silage with the addition of 0.1 mL of 99% ethanol was included as no-inhibitor control. MilliQ water (2 mL) was added to the treatments without isolates or medium to compensate for the reduced headspace. Thus, in total 15 different treatments were compared. Each treatment was included as triplicates in 2 separate runs. To minimize possible confounding effects of bottle position and inoculation, the runs were divided into three blocks with one bottle per treatment in each block. The treatments were randomly assigned to a bottle within block.

Prior to feeding, rumen liquid and particles were sampled from 3 rumen-cannulated non-lactating Danish Holstein cows (BW 805 ± 28.1 kg and BCS 3.17 ± 0.29 on a scale 1–5). The cows were fed two equal-sized meals of a diet consisting of grass/clover hay, barley straw, and a pelleted concentrate mixture (39.6% barley grain, 39.9% oat grain, 10% soybean meal, 3% rapeseed meal, 3% sugar beet molasses, and 4% of a commercial mineral mixture per kg fresh mixture) at maintenance level, which amounted to 7.7 kg DM daily (with 13.7% starch, 13.9% crude protein, 24.1% NDF, and forage to concentrate ratio of 69:31 on a DM basis). At collection, the rumen fluid and particles were filtered through a nylon cloth (250 microns) into preheated vacuum flasks. Buffer, redox indicator, reducing agent, macro and micro mineral solutions were prepared as described by Menke and Steingass [27]. Rumen fluid was added in a 2:1 ratio (buffer solution: rumen fluid). The final solution (pH: 7.89 ± 0.20) was kept under anaerobic conditions by continuously flushing with N2. To each bottle, 90 mL of inoculum and the respective treatment were added, whereafter a wireless fully automated ANKOMRF module (ANKOM Technology, Macedon, USA) was mounted for measurement of in vitro gas production. The ANKOMRF was operated as described by Thorsteinsson, Weisbjerg et al. [28]. Accumulated gas in the bottle headspace was released via the automated ANKOM cap (for 250 ms) when the pressure inside the bottle reached 1 psi (approx. 6.9 kPA) above ambient pressure. For the continuous collection of released gas, a gas-tight FlexFoil bag (1 L; SKC Ltd, Dorset, United Kingdom) was attached to each sensor module. The bottles were incubated for 48 h at 38.5 °C in a controlled incubator shaker (New Brunswick TM Excella® E25R, Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) with the oscillation set at 50 rpm. After 24 h of incubation, 10 mL of gas was extracted from each gasbag using a gastight syringe with a twist valve (Hamilton Bonaduz AG, Bonaduz, Switzerland). Following, the gas was transferred into Exetainer® vials (Labco Limited, Ceredigion, United Kingdom) for later gas analyses. After 48 h of incubation, the bottle content was filtered through F57 fiber bags (ANKOM Technology, Macedon, New York, USA) for the collection of undegraded feed residues. A sample of the filtrate was collected for later analysis of VFA.

The standard feed, corn silage, was freeze-dried and ground on a 2-mm screen (Ultra Centrifugal Mill ZM 200, Verder Scientific, Hann, Germany). Nutrient content was analyzed using near-infrared spectroscopy at a commercial feed testing laboratory (Eurofins Agro Testing A/S, Vejen, Denmark). After incubation, the content of DM was determined by oven-drying the samples at 103 °C for 24 h, while OM in the residues was determined by combustion at 525 °C for 6 h. The collected inoculum was stabilized with 1 mL of 25% metaphosphoric acid (MPA) to reach 5% MPA in the stabilized sample. The concentrations of VFA were determined as described by Thorsteinsson and Nielsen [29]. Similarly, concentrations of CH4 and H2 in gas samples were determined using a GC (Trace 1310, Thermo Scientific, Germany) as described by Thorsteinsson and Nielsen [29]. Quantification was performed using standard curves with standard gases in concentrations ranging from 500 − 250,000 ppm for CH4 and from 250 − 50,000 ppm for H2.

Afterward, the total gas production was blank-corrected. Methane and H2 production (mL) were calculated from the total gas production and concentration of the individual gas.

Results

Culture collection

A culture collection of 182 bacteria was obtained on selective media using enrichment and direct plating (Table S1). They were isolated predominantly from bovine rumen fluid (n = 145), but also from pig mid colon content (n = 25) and from rumen enrichment cultures of methylotrophic methanogens (n = 12). Isolation of potential acetogenic bacteria was targeted by using selective medium AC.B1 that contained BES to inhibit growth of methanogens. The enrichment and isolation media contained H2 and CO2 as the main energy and carbon source, respectively but also contained rumen fluid and yeast extract which can support growth of some non-acetogens. Some isolates were able to grow to visible densities within 1 or 2 days; however, more isolates were obtained when the enrichments or isolation plates were incubated for longer time (2 weeks or more). Many cultures did not grow to a high density on the isolation medium. Included in the culture collection, 2 strains (ss180 and ss191) were isolated from rumen content on Selenomonas-selective medium as described by [17]. Phylogenetic identity of the isolates was inferred from 105 near full length 16 S rRNA gene sequences plus 71 partial 16 S rRNA gene sequences (no sequence was obtained for 6 isolates). They represented 7 phyla, 14 classes, 27 orders, 41 families and 58 genera. The most represented genera were Bacillus with 27 isolates, followed by Escherichia/Shigella (9 isolates), Eubacterium (8 isolates), Propionibacterium (8 isolates) and Clostridium sensu stricto with 7 isolates (data not shown).

The isolate collection was characterized by assessing the VFAs produced when grown on the selection medium, and by determining their identity by 16 S rRNA gene sequencing (Table S1). Around 46% of the isolates produced acetate (83 isolates). The culture collection was also screened for the presence of reductive acetogenesis marker gene ascB. Positive bands of the correct size were found for 17 isolates, 5 from cow rumen, 10 from pig colon and 2 from enrichment culture of methylotrophic methanogens. This, however, gave inconclusive results as a strong band of the correct size was found for the acetogen E. limosum but not for B. producta. Spurious bands of the incorrect size were also noted for many isolates; increasing the recommended annealing temperature of the PCR from 52 °C to 60 °C did not eliminate the production of spurious bands.

Hydrogen utilization of bacterial isolates

From the initial culture collection, 56 new isolates plus 3 known hydrogenotrophs (B. producta, E. limosum and D. piger) were screened for their ability to utilize hydrogen in pure culture (Fig. 1 and Table S2). Isolates were selected to represent the phylogenetic diversity of the culture collection and priority was given to those that produced acetate when grown in selective acetogen medium (potential acetogens), isolates producing propionate (a hydrogen utilizing pathway) or the presence of reductive acetogen marker gene acsB (Table S2). The two additional isolates from Selenomonas selective medium were included to represent potential fumarate or nitrate reducing hydrogenotrophs. Neither of the two isolates were identified as Selenomonas species by 16 S rRNA gene sequencing but were closely related to Megasphaera hexanoica and Mitsuokella jalaludinii, respectively (Table S2). Growth of the isolates was generally low in the selective medium, reaching an average OD of only 0.15 (min − 0.01, max 0.65) (Figure S1). Remaining pressure in the tube gives an indication of hydrogen utilization; cultures with a high hydrogen affinity, like methanogens, can reduce tube pressure to 0. Pressure readings (Figure S2 and Table S2) show 4 cultures significantly reduce the gas pressure in the culture tube when compared with the uninoculated controls. Thirty-two isolates were able to significantly reduce the dissolved hydrogen ranging from 7.7 to 84.6% reduction from the control (Fig. 1). All three control hydrogenotrophs were among the bacteria that could utilize hydrogen, with D. piger reducing hydrogen most (84.6%). Seven of the newly isolated bacteria were able to reduce dissolved hydrogen by more than 50% which was greater than for acetogens E. limosum at 17.9% and B. producta at 14.2% under these conditions. The bacterial cultures produced a range of VFAs when grown in the selective RM02 + rumen medium (Fig. 2). The 3 control hydrogenotrophs and 39 isolates produced acetic acid as a byproduct of growth (Table S2 and Figure S3). Of the VFAs produced in growth medium after acetic acid, formic acid, lactic acid and succinic acid were the most predominant. Rationale for choice of isolates for in vitro rumen simulation tests

From a culture collection of 182 isolates, 12 bacteria could be included in a single in vitro fermentation experiment. From the isolates that significantly reduced dissolved hydrogen in culture, the strains with the lowest remaining dissolved H2 were chosen. The amount of acetate produced from growth in RM02 + NoSubRFV medium was also a contributing factor in the isolate selection. The SRB D. piger had the lowest remaining dissolved H2 in the hydrogen utilization test and was selected as a positive control of the known hydrogenotrophs in the in vitro experiment. Two isolates (7842 029 and P161) that showed a numerical decrease and a tendency to reduce hydrogen from a previous in vitro fermentation were included [33]. The selected isolates were related to Citrobacter sp. (7842 030sw a), Clostridium sartagoforme (7842 030sw b), Caldibacillus sp. (7482 031sw a), Proteiniphilum sp. (7842 036), Actinomyces ruminicola (7842 042), Paenibacillus sp. (7690 051b), Enterococcus gallinarum (7690 059), Chordicoccus furentiruminis (Bac PC1), Mitsuokella jalaludinii (ss191), Streptococcus gallolyticus (P161) and Paenibacillus sp. (7842 029) (Table S2).

Effect of the addition of live bacteria cultures to rumen fermentation in vitro

In this study, the volume of the inoculum was kept constant to ensure the same headspace in the in vitro system. Culture growth was monitored with OD and as a check, the actual cell numbers were counted for the first run (Table 1). The isolates had more dense growth in the RM02 + GenRFV medium and the number of cells in the selective medium was increased when the cell pellet was added to RM02 + NoSubRFV grown culture (Table 1). All manipulations were performed under anaerobic conditions, and these were successfully maintained as the oxygen indicator present in the medium did not react and change color. As expected, the addition of iodoform reduced CH4 production and increased H2 accumulation (Table 2). The inclusion level of iodoform used was chosen to result in around a theoretical 50% reduction in methane production; ~66.5% reduction (mL/g DM) when compared to the medium control was obtained. This resulted in 1400% increase in H2 (mL/g DM). Addition of iodoform numerically decreased total VFA production, and significantly decreased acetate, isovalerate, valerate and caproate production and it increased propionate and butyrate (Table 3). Iodoform did not significantly alter the pH or degradability of DM or OM. There was no effect of the growth medium alone on the gas production, degradability or VFA production. Total gas production after 24 h CH4 production and H2 production were not affected by the addition of any of the 12 bacterial cultures compared to the medium control (Table 2). Likewise, total gas production after 48 h was also unaffected by the 12 bacterial cultures. Compared with the growth medium alone, the largest numerical decrease in CH4/dOM (mL/g) of 10.5% was seen with isolate ss191 and there was a small numerical decrease of 10% in H2/dOM (mL/g) as well. This isolate was identified as Mitsuokella jalaludinii and is in the same family as Selenomonas ruminantium (Selenomonadaceae) and was included as a potential fumarate reducer [34]. Several of the isolates numerically increased the H2 level over the medium control levels including the isolate (P161) that otherwise showed a tendency to reduce H2 in a previous in vitro test. The addition of any of the 12 isolates tested did not significantly alter the VFA production in combination with iodoform.

Discussion

To maintain food production from ruminant agriculture while reducing greenhouse gas emissions, methods to reduce CH4 production are sought. Methane mitigating feed additives such as Bovaer® are now available on the market; however when rumen methanogenesis is reduced by the addition of inhibitors that – like Bovaer - directly target methanogens, an increase in H2 accumulation is observed [35, 36]. In the rumen H2 plays a central role as an intermediate that regulates microbial metabolism and increased H2 may have a negative effect on rumen fermentation processes but also opens the possibility for it to be redirected into alternative H2-utlizing pathways that would normally not be competitive with methanogenesis [37]. Reductive acetogenesis has been proposed as such an alternative pathway for H2 removal [38–40]. It has been demonstrated that the addition of an acetogenic bacteria to an in vitro rumen fluid system, where methanogenesis had been inhibited, promoted reductive acetogenesis [12, 13]. Additionally, methanogen-free lambs were demonstrated to maintain a functional rumen where acetogens were able to contribute to H2 removal [10]. Thus, these studies provide evidence that reductive acetogenesis can at least partially replace methanogenesis as rumen H2 sink.

In this study, we sought to isolate new acetogens from the rumen for potential use as direct fed microbial treatments in combination with a chemical methanogenesis inhibitor. Acetogens are present in the mature rumen, but the acetogen population is known to vary depending on host species [41, 42]. Therefore, to increase the diversity of isolates, acetogens were also isolated from pig colon content. An acetogen selective medium AC-B1, which was modified from AC-11 medium, described by Greening and Leedle [43] to isolate Acetitomaculum ruminis, was used to enrich for and isolate potential acetogens. A resulting culture collection of 182 isolates was obtained. Similar strategies have previously been used successfully to enrich for and isolate acetogens from ruminants [44–46]. Likewise, acetogens from buffaloes were isolated on the acetogen agar medium of Boccazzi and Patterson [47] using a roll tube technique [48] and by using H2 limited continuous culture [49]. The AC-11 medium, like the AC-B1 medium, contains rumen fluid and was used to isolates acetogens [10, 50]. The inclusion of rumen fluid and yeast extract provides various important minerals, vitamins and co-factors, however they may further provide enough nutrients to support the growth of some non-hydrogenotrophic bacteria. The isolates obtained in this study were of very diverse phylogenetic affiliations, and some were not from previously known acetogenic lineages, suggesting that non-acetogenic bacteria could have been isolated on the AC-B1 medium. The metabolite production of the new isolates, when grown with H2:CO2 headspace, were determined, and 83 isolates produced acetate as at least one of their products. However, growth and metabolite production of the culture collection were not determined in parallel with an N2:CO2 headspace; thus, the possibility of fermentative production of acetate cannot be ruled out. Isolates P171, P169, P166, P148 (E. coli from pig colon) and 7842 030sw (Citrobacter sp.) produced similar amounts of acetate as the know acetogens E. limosum and B. producta. E. coli has not previously been identified as a reductive acetogens; they are able to produce various acids, incl. acetate, via mixed acid fermentation, however they do not harbor the Wood-Ljungdahl pathway to produce acetate from CO2 and H2. The E. coli isolates were presumably able to grow on the small amounts of substrates provided by the 0.5 g/L of yeast extract and the rumen fluid in the medium.

Acetogenic bacteria are not monophylogenic and can be found in many different genera [51]. As outlined above, reductive acetogens are characterized by harboring the Wood-Ljungdahl (acetyl-CoA) pathway. Acetogens can grow autotrophically by using CO₂ and H₂ to produce acetate through the Wood–Ljungdahl pathway. They can also grow heterotrophically by fermenting various organic substrates. Under mixotrophic growth, acetogens can use both inorganic and organic substrates simultaneously as sources of carbon and energy [52]. To identify reductive acetogens from the culture collection, we employed a PCR assay based on targeting the marker gene acsB, encoding the acetyl-CoA synthase (ACS) only found in the Wood-Ljungdahl pathway, and therefore used as a marker gene for reductive acetogens [23]. Targeting the acsB gene has the advantage over the other commonly used acetogen marker, targeting the formyltetrahydrofolate synthase gene (fhs), as formyltetrahydrofolate synthase (FTHFS) may be involved in other pathways [53]. The acsB PCR assay, however, gave inconclusive results as a strong band of the correct size was found for the known acetogen E. limosum but not for B. producta. Moreover, applying the acsB assay to the isolate collection also resulted in various bands of incorrect sizes among the tested isolates. However, many isolates identified as Eubacterium sp. gave PCR products of the correct size, leading to the hypothesis that the acsB primers may work better for Eubacterium and related reductive acetogens than for members of other taxa. Also, the incorrect bands could be misread as false positives if they were a similar size to the correct band, wherefore the marker gene screening was given low weight when choosing representatives for further testing. However, finding 17 out of 182 isolates (9%), with a correct sized band, is comparable to the 4 out of 49 isolates (8%) that were identified with the fhs acetogenesis marker gene in the study of Kim et al. [44], isolated on the same selective AC-B1 medium as used in the present study. Ruminants are estimated to have 7.9 × 105 to 3.2 × 106 cells/mL of acetogens in 20 h old lambs and 2.5 × 102 cells/mL in adult sheep as determined by most probable number (MPN) [54], 75 cells/mL in finishing steers and 2.5 × 105 cells/mL (determined by MPN) in beef cows [14] compared to pig colon that has 3.4 × 104 to 6.0 × 105 cells/mL as determined by MPN [55]. Including pig digesta as a source material for isolation more than doubled the number of isolates with acsB gene (7 from cows vs. 10 from pigs) despite there being only 25 isolates from pig digesta. This shows it was much easier to isolate acetogens from pigs digesta, however, most of the pig isolates were closely related to Eubacterium sp. and did not show the diversity seen with the rumen isolates. Future cultivation attempts from ruminants should include isolation from the hindgut as it has recently been demonstrated that there is a higher prevalence of distinct populations of acetogens in the ruminant cecum [56].

To find hydrogenotrophic bacteria with the best ability to use H2, roughly a third of the isolates were selected to test their ability to utilize H2 when grown in selective medium with H2:CO2 as the energy source. These isolates included known acetogens, a known SRB and potential new acetogens, fumarate and nitrate reducers from the culture collection. The aim of this screening was to find isolates that could utilize H2 and to see which isolates were the most likely to be competitive by having the lowest remaining dissolved H2 after 5 days growth. Thirty-two isolates were able to significantly reduce the dissolved hydrogen ranging from 7.7 to 84.6%, showing the isolation strategy was successful in enriching for and isolating hydrogenotrophic bacteria.

Eleven new isolates and one SRB were assessed for their ability to reduce H2 accumulation in vitro rumen simulation when methanogenesis was inhibited. The total number of isolate cells added to the in vitro bottles was around 0.03 to 0.7% of the total cells, meaning they would likely not be in the top 20 most prevalent species (typically over 0.5%) but only among the top 50–100 prevalent species from what is typically seen in the rumen microbiome. We chose not to add an overabundance of cells to the in vitro system since, if these are to work as direct fed microbial supplements, they would need to be fed at rather low amounts to be an economically feasible solution. There was a clear effect of adding iodoform to the in vitro fermentation system. Iodoform is a halomethane that is thought to block the function of enzymes in the methanogenesis pathway [26]. Iodoform addition reduced total gas production after 24 h and 48 h, reduced CH4 production, reduced acetate, valerate, isovalerate and caproate, increased propionate and butyrate, whereas total VFA and DM and OM degradability were not significantly changed. When comparing the no inhibitor control with the medium + iodoform control, CH4 (mL/g DM) was reduced by 66.5% and H2 (mL/g DM) increased by 1400% in vitro which is in line with what was seen in vivo when dairy cows were fed iodoform in rations and CH4 (g CH4/kg DMI) was reduced by 46% and H2 (g H2/kg DMI) increased by 1127% with the highest dose of 20 mg/kg DM [57]. The increase in H2 is however less than when iodoform was pulse dosed into the rumen of dairy cows, where CH4 (g CH4/kg DMI) was reduced by 66% and H2 (g H2/kg DMI) increased by 12,500% with the highest dose of 800 mg/day [26]. Even with the increase in H2 seen in the present study, H2 (mL/g DM) only accounted for 0.2% of the total gas captured after 24 h in the control (medium + iodoform). The reduction of CH4 in the control (medium + iodoform) was 9.05 mL/g DM and the increase in H2 was 0.14 mL/g DM, based on stoichiometry if 4 mol of H2 is used in the formation of 1 mol of CH4 then an increase in H2 of 36.2 mL/g DM could be expected. This leaves 99.6% of the theoretical increase in H2 unaccounted for. Some of the unaccounted H2 can be explained by the redirection into the production of propionate, a process that uses hydrogen, however there is also an increase in butyrate which produces H2 and shift in VFA production of the magnitudes seen here could only explain a small fraction of the missing H2 seen in vitro. In vitro experiments, using the same ANKOM system, with Asparagopsis taxiformis as the CH4 inhibitor showed that only 2.5% of the theoretical excess of H2 was accounted for [58]. This is not the case seen in vivo where a decrease in CH4 emissions is often accompanied by a dramatic increase in H2 emissions and 19–28% of the theoretical excess H2 is was accounted for when 3-NOP (Bovaer®) was the methane inhibitor in low and high fat diets [59]. It has recently been demonstrated that the headspace and emitted H2 had weak correlations with dissolved H2 in the rumen [60], highlighting the difficulties faced when trying to use these proxy measures to make inferences about dissolved H2 levels.

Addition of the isolates were not able to significantly reduce CH4 or H2 or change VFA production in the in vitro rumen fermentation when supplemented with iodoform. Acetogenic bacteria have metabolic flexibility, many with the ability to switch between autotrophic, heterotrophic or mixotrophic growth and some are able to grow utilizing non-acetogenic pathways [61, 62]. This may explain why even though these isolates are potentially acetogens or other hydrogenotrophs (SRB or fumarate reducers), they have no effect on the in vitro fermentation. The SRB D. piger should be able to compete with methanogens for the H2 substrate if it has sufficient sulfate availability, however the in vitro results showed total gas, CH4 and H2 production to be remarkable similar to the water + iodoform control. Gagen et al. [63] proposed that acetogens may be more prone to grow autotrophically in young animals, where they are more prevalent and the number of competitive methanogen are lower, and shift to heterotrophic growth in mature animals, as the acetogen diversity was similar in young and adult ruminants but acetogens were not a major H2 sink in adults. Further to this, evidence from metagenome assembled genomes show rumen acetogens are highly metabolically versatile, adapted to use both carbohydrates and H₂ as electron donors, therefore are not limited to competing for H2 and heterotrophic acetogenesis is an important process in the rumen [62]. Regardless of whether growing autotrophically or heterotrophically, the isolates added to the in vitro fermentation showed no measurable influence on fermentation outcomes in this study.

An important difference between the hydrogen utilization in pure culture versus that in the in vitro system is that in pure culture, the H2 was provided in excess (pumped to 150 kPA) and the cultures allowed a longer time to use the substrates, whereas the in vitro was run for 48 h only and released the gas from the culture bottles as the pressure built up; accumulated gas in the bottle headspace was thus released via the automated ANKOM cap (for 250 ms) when the pressure inside the bottle reached 1 psi (approx. 6.9 kPA) above ambient pressure. Using Henry’s law calculations and some standard assumptions (Henry’s constant (KH) at 25 °C. (L*atm/mol)) if the H2 made up approx. 0.2% (calculated from the medium + iodoform control) of the total gas at 1 psi over atmospheric pressure (assuming a constant rate of H2 evolution), then the dissolved H2 would be around 1.6 µmol/L at 25 °C. The bottles contained buffered rumen fluid, not water and at 39 °C not 25 °C, but it is hypothesised that the release of gas from the culture bottles did not allow the dissolved H2 to accumulate to a level where acetogenesis was thermodynamically favorable. The actual level of dissolved H2 was not measured in the ANKOM in vitro bottles, and the level for autotrophic growth to be thermodynamically favorable over heterotrophic growth is not known for these isolates. However, the minimum dissolved H2 remaining after 5 days growth measured in the H2 utilization assay for these isolates ranged from 179 to 1015 µMol/L which is ~ 100–600 fold above the calculated level of dissolved H2 in the in vitro rumen simulation system. This supports the hypothesis that the H2 concentration did not reach a level where reductive acetogenesis would occur. The kinetics of H2 consumption by acetogenic bacteria has recently been proposed to follow first-order kinetics (linear increasing rate with increasing substrate levels) rather than Monod kinetics (increasing rate at low concentrations and then a shift to no increase in rate at high levels) [64]; this implies that the level of H2 is a critical factor in the rate of acetogenesis.

Taken together, the unexpectedly low H2 emission found when CH4 was inhibited in vitro, and the pressure in the in vitro bottle not building up enough, the limits of the ANKOM in vitro system make it unsuitable for determining H2 dynamics. It is still possible that addition of hydrogenotrophs could have an effect in vivo or other in vitro systems, simulating rumen conditions. Hence, future work should employ a different in vitro model system like closed batch incubations and look at the effect of addition of hydrogenotrophs in ruminants.

In conclusion, new putative hydrogenotrophic bacteria from bovine rumen and pig digesta were isolated using selective medium. Characterization of the resulting culture collection revealed 83 isolates that produced acetate and 32 thereof that were able to utilize H2 during their growth. Addition of the isolates to the in vitro rumen system under the conditions used here, supplemented with iodoform and corn silage, was neither able to significantly reduce CH4 or H2 nor change VFA production, possibly due to H2 concentrations not reaching high enough levels. Iodoform is a potent inhibitor of methanogenesis, but the expected increase in H2 accumulation, caused by the decrease in CH4 production, was not observed in vitro.
