Techno-economic analysis of bioactive-rich oil production from Nannochloropsis oculata
Authors: Henrique Leivas Fuentes, Pedro Minasi Brandão, Viviane de Carvalho Arabidian, Filipe Echart de Oliveira, Fabio Roselet, Dariano Krummenauer, Bibiana Porto da Silva, Débora Pez Jaeschke, Nauro da Silveira, Luiz Antonio de Almeida Pinto, Tito Roberto Sant’Anna Cadaval, Rafael Lipinski Paes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCarotenoids and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) from microalgae have applications in food, pharmaceuticals, cosmetics, and aquaculture. However, the large-scale use of these compounds is limited by high production costs associated with microalgae harvest and extraction. Microalgae such as Nannochloropsis oculata are also recognized for their capacity to capture CO₂ and remove nutrients from wastewater, positioning them as promising tools for pollution mitigation within circular bioeconomy models. In this article, a techno-economic analysis was performed for three plant sizes (720 kg h-1, 360 kg h-1, 180 kg h-1), considering the downstream processing of cultivated biomass for oil extraction using ethanol as solvent. All unit operations of the process were studied, and mass and energy balances were performed using experimental and literature data. The estimated net present value indicated positive economic feasibility for all plant sizes, and the plant with the highest capacity demonstrated the most favorable profitability metrics. Sensitivity analysis revealed that the net present value of cash flow after tax remained positive for minimum oil selling prices ranging from US$ 90.45 kg⁻¹ to US$ 90.73 kg⁻¹, highlighting the high added value of bioactive compound-rich oil. By demonstrating economically viable production routes, this study supports the deployment of microalgae-based systems that simultaneously valorize biomass and contribute to pollution mitigation, aligning with the goals of sustainable water management and climate action.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50785-8.Keywords: Microalgae, Polyunsaturated fatty acids, Carotenoids, Biomass valorization, Ethanol extraction, Minimum selling priceSubject terms: Biotechnology, Environmental sciences, Plant sciences

Introduction

Microalgae comprise a taxonomically and functionally diverse group of unicellular photosynthetic microorganisms that inhabit nearly all aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems. These microorganisms convert carbon dioxide into high-value-added compounds, such as proteins, lipids, carbohydrates, and pigments. These compounds have several applications in the food, pharmaceutical, and energy sectors, and many of them are considered high-value-added products, such as pigments and PUFAs1,2. In recent decades, the prospection of microalgae has diversified due to their wide range of applications, for example carotenoids have gained attention due to the increasing demand for natural and sustainable alternatives to synthetic dyes in food and cosmetic products. These compounds are typically aliphatic polyene molecules, insoluble in water, and biosynthesized through the condensation of isoprene units, which are also referred to as isoprenoids3. Beyond their role as colorants, these pigments also exhibit antioxidant activity, protecting cells from oxidative damage and decreasing the risk of cancer and other degenerative diseases4,5. Due to their natural coloring and functional benefits, carotenoids are commonly added to various food products, including beverages, soups, dairy items, and infant formula. In the pharmaceutical industry, they are applied in drugs and therapeutic treatments. In cosmetics, they are utilized as natural pigments in skincare products such as anti-aging creams, sunscreens, and UV protection formulations. Additionally, in aquaculture, carotenoids enhance fish pigmentation, improving their coloration and overall market value6.

Regarding PUFAs, many microalgae species are important sources of essential ω−3 fatty acids, including α-linolenic acid (ALA), eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA), docosapentaenoic acid (DPA), and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA). DHA and EPA support cell membrane integrity, regulate inflammation and play crucial roles in brain function, cardiovascular health, and eye development. While fish and fish oil supplements are common sources of these compounds, concerns related to overfishing, environmental impact, and contamination have led to growing interest in alternative sources like microalgae. Plant-based diets, though rich in ω−6 fatty acids, often lack sufficient ω−3 fatty acids, potentially increasing inflammation and risk of diseases. Therefore, microalgae-derived oils provide a sustainable alternative to fish-derived ω−3 products7. As the feasibility of microalgae biofuels remains limited due to high costs, research is shifting towards its use in the food and nutraceutical industries8.

However, the industrial utilization of carotenoids and PUFAs remains limited due to challenges in microalgae cultivation, harvesting, and intracellular compound extraction, which increase production costs. Moreover, only a few species, including Chlorella vulgaris, Dunaliella salina, Arthrospira platensis, and Haematococcus pluvialis, are commercially cultivated. Nevertheless, research continues to explore other promising strains for future applications9. One of the main constraints is related to the low cell concentration in the culture medium (< 0.5 g L− 1 in open systems), requiring high cultivation volumes to yield substantial amounts of biomass. Concerning harvest, it may be performed by centrifugation, flocculation, filtration or flotation. The main limitations are related to the negative surface charge and the small size of the cells, which make it difficult to separate microalgal cells from the cultivation medium1,10.

Moreover, the extraction of intracellular compounds presents several challenges, including the need to dry the biomass and effectively disrupt the cell membrane. Drying facilitates the diffusion of non-polar compounds and is estimated to account for 20–30% of the total production cost10. Cell disruption is commonly achieved through chemical methods, which involve the use of organic solvents, such as ethanol and hexane. These solvents enable the extraction of non-polar internal compounds, such as lipids and carotenoids11–13.

To better understand the economic viability of microalgae products, a techno-economic analysis (TEA) can enable investors and design specialists to establish more competitive processes for products that may have previously been considered unsuitable for commercial applications. This analysis facilitates the identification of target markets where biomass and microalgae products can be utilized and enables the estimation of minimum selling prices14,15. Additionally, microalgae have also been widely investigated for pollution mitigation due to their capacity to uptake nitrogen, phosphorus, heavy metals, and CO₂ from wastewater and industrial effluents16,17. As a result, algal-based bioremediation systems generate biomass as a valuable by-product, which can be subsequently converted into high-value compounds within circular bioeconomy frameworks18. Strengthening the economic feasibility of downstream processing, particularly oil extraction enriched in carotenoids and PUFAs, is therefore essential for enabling integrated systems that simultaneously support pollution control and biomass valorization, contributing to Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) 6 (Clean Water and Sanitation), 13 (Climate Action), and 14 (Life Below Water).

Although several techno-economic studies have assessed microalgae for biodiesel production, most of them focus on bulk biofuel pathways and assume large-scale fuel markets as the primary revenue stream19,20. Limited attention has been given to integrated biorefinery strategies targeting high-value bioactive-rich oils under realistic biomass cost scenarios. In addition, many studies consider only the cultivation stage, without integrating downstream processing and product valorization21,22. Moreover, the viability of a production process using ethanol as solvent for extracting polar lipids and associated carotenoids into a bioactive-rich oil fraction from microalgae has not been found in the literature.

In this context, the present work evaluates the techno-economic feasibility of producing bioactive-rich oil from Nannochloropsis oculata within a biorefinery framework, including the valorization of defatted biomass. The analysis performed in this work assessed all unit operations of the process for three different plant sizes (720, 360 and 180 kg h− 1), with lipid extraction performed using ethanol as the solvent. Mass and energy balances calculations were supported by literature and experimental results. The economical evaluation included capital investment estimation, product cost determination, and assessment of the overall economic viability of the proposed system.

Materials and methods

Microalgae cultivation

N. oculata was cultivated at the Marine Aquaculture Station, Institute of Oceanography, Federal University of Rio Grande, Brazil, according to Couto et al.15. The microalgae, obtained from the Laboratory of Microalgae Production (strain NANN OCUL-1), were grown in f/2 medium using natural seawater at 28 g L− 1. Figure 1 presents the cultivation system used in the present work: 330 L plexiglass bubble column photobioreactors, in a greenhouse under natural environmental conditions (temperature, irradiance and photoperiod) and with controlled aeration (filtered air, 4.8 L min− 1). Biomass concentration was monitored daily by measuring absorbance at 750 nm, reaching a biomass concentration of 0.5 g L− 1 after 18 days. The bioreactor content was then centrifuged in a continuous disk centrifuge (FJ 130 EPR, Janschitz Gmbh, Althofen, Austria) with a flow rate of 20 L h− 1 and rotation of 9500 rpm. The biomass was freeze-dried and stored at −18 °C until further analyses.

Cultivation system: plexiglass photobioreactors placed in a greenhouse under natural environmental conditions with controlled aeration using filtered air.

Biomass characterization

Proximal composition

N. oculata biomass was characterized regarding its total lipid, protein, carbohydrates, and ashes content according to AOAC methods23. All analyses were performed using dried biomass, and results were expressed on a dry weight basis (d.b.). Although freeze-drying may retain a small amount of residual moisture, this fraction was considered negligible for compositional analysis. Protein concentration was determined by Kjeldahl method using a value of 6.25 for the N factor. The ash content was evaluated in a muffle at 550 °C for 6 h, and the carbohydrate concentration was determined by difference. Total lipid content was determined by Bligh & Dyer method24. The polar lipid content was determined by Soxhlet extraction with ethanol (99%) unti the refluxing solvent became colorless25.

Total carotenoid content

The total carotenoid content of the biomass was determined using the methodology described by Rodriguez-Amaya26 with some modification. Dried biomass (0.02 g) was subjected to sequential extraction using acetone (4 cycles of 2 mL each). After acetone extraction, the procedure was repeated using ethyl acetate (2 mL per cycle) until the extract became colorless (4 cycles). All extraction steps were performed at room temperature under reduced light conditions to minimize carotenoid degradation. The combined extracts were subjected to liquid–liquid partition using diethyl ether and petroleum ether (1:1, v/v). Saponification was carried out with 10% (w/w) methanolic KOH solution overnight at room temperature in the dark. After saponification, samples were washed with distilled water until neutral pH, and the organic phase was concentrated using a rotary evaporator under reduced pressure. The residue was diluted in ethanol and analyzed spectrophotometrically at 440 nm. Results were expressed as milligrams of violaxanthin equivalents per gram of dried biomass (mg g⁻¹).

Fatty acids methyl esters (FAMEs) analyses

Lipids were esterified using 500 µL of KOH (0.1 M), following the method described by Hartman et al.27. The mixture was vortexed and incubated at 60 °C in a water bath for 1.5 h. Subsequently, 1.5 mL of H2SO4 (1 M) was added to the flasks, and the mixture was kept at 60 °C for another 1.5 h. After cooling, samples were mixed with 2 mL of n-hexane and allowed to separate into phases. The n-hexane phase was then subjected to analysis in a gas chromatograph (GC-FID, Shimadzu, 2010AF, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a capillary column of fused silica SP-2560 (100 m × 0.25 mm × 0.2 μm) and a flame ionization detector (FID). Carrier gas was nitrogen (1 mL min− 1), and hydrogen and synthetic air were used as flame gases. The column temperature was initially set to 100 °C for 15 min and then ramped up to 250 °C at a rate of 4 °C min− 1, holding at this temperature for 45 min. The sample split ratio was set at 1:100. The injector and detector temperatures were maintained at 250 °C and 255 °C, respectively. For fatty acid identification, retention times were compared with those of a methyl ester standard mixture (Supelco 37 Component FAME Mix) analyzed under identical chromatographic conditions and using the same SP-2560 capillary column.

Structure and morphology of the microalgae cells

The dried biomass was analyzed by optical microscopy (Jeol JEM-1400) and by SEM (Scanning Electron Microscopy) to evaluate its structure and morphology. For SEM, the biomass was sputter-coated with gold and analyzed using a Jeol, JSM − 6610LV microscope28,29.

Process flowchart: mass and energy balance

Figure 2 presents the process flowchart detailing the unit operations involved in the downstream production of bioactive-rich oil from N. oculata biomass. The flowchart includes the following unit operations: separation of microalgae from the cultivation medium through flocculation, biomass water removal using a dewatering press and tray dryer, oil extraction with ethanol, solvent evaporation to obtain a concentrated oil extract, and biomass desolventization. Although cultivation was experimentally carried out to obtain biomass for compositional characterization and extraction yield determination, its capital and operating expenditures were not included in the techno-economic model. Therefore, the system boundary of the economic assessment encompasses only the downstream processing stages, from harvested biomass to the production of bioactive-rich oil and defatted biomass. Mass and energy balances were established for all downstream processing steps based on experimental data and complementary literature information. Mass balance

Material balance calculations were employed to determine raw material costs and equipment sizing, following the methodology of Richard M. Felder et al.30. Physical constraints, outflows, inflows, unknown and known variables were identified, and an analysis of the degrees of freedom was performed, as described elsewhere31,32. The application of the mass conservation law was conducted for each control volume, considering the following assumptions: the decanter was projected to separate a suspension containing 0.5% (w/v) total solids, using a flocculant dosage of 10 g m−³, and assuming a recovery of 90% of the iitial amount of biomass33; the dewatering press and tray dryers were designed assuming complete recovery of solids, resulting in a final moisture content of 10% (dry basis); biomass characterization served as a basis for determining the key parameters of the extractor, which was designed to extract 98% of total polar lipids and associated carotenoids into a bioactive-rich oil fraction; finally, the desolventizer was designed to remove 99.9% of the solvet.

Energy balance

A simplified energy balance was applied for each unit operation of the process, assuming steady-state operation and considering only thermal energy requirements. Mechanical and electrical energy demands were not included in the analysis, except for the dewatering press, for which power consumption data were available. This approach follows the methodology proposed by Peter et al.34 for preliminary techno-economic evaluations. In addition, the kinetic and potential energy values were considered negligible in relation to the other terms of the energy balance for all control volumes. Moreover, the following assumptions were applied: the decanter and the extractor were assumed to operate at room temperature; the dewatering press was considered to have an electric power of 2.2 kW per module; the dryer was designed based on experimental results of a previous work35, with an air temperature of 50 °C, a sample temperature of 30 °C, a solid input of 4 kg m−², and a drying time of 4.5 h; the evaporator was assumed to operate at 40 °C and 5 mmHg to prevent lipid oxidation. Equipment sizing

The size of the process equipment was determined according to Geankoplis36 using the results of the mass balance and the mass flow rate of commercially available equipment. Information regarding the capacity of commercial equipment was acquired from local manufacturers. The decanter was projected considering a laminar flow of liquid solution and the area was calculated using water properties at 25 °C and 1 atm. The dewatering press was designed considering the flow rate and the hydraulic capacity per module (5,000 L h− 1) of commercially available equipment. The dryer was projected by calculating the drying area, considering a solid load of 4 kg/m² and that a single tray dryer can have a maximum drying area of up to 100 m². The thermal demand for drying was determined by the relationship between the drying time and the energy required to heat the air. The operational parameters were obtained in previous work35. The extractor was designed as a counter-flow system, considering a residence time of 1 h and a solid load of 4 kg m−². The desolventizer was projected considering the physical properties of the solvent and the biomass to calculate the heat required for the solvent to change phase. Based on this calculation and considering a minimum thermal flow for the worst possible scenario (450 kJ m−² °C− 1 h− 1), the total area needed for the desolventizer was calculated. The evaporator was calculated considering a global heat coefficient of 200 W m−²K− 1 and the energy required to evaporate the solvent.

Once the sizing was established, the equipment costs were calculated using the vendor cost or the ‘Equipment costs’ abacus by Peter et al.34 with prices data of 2002. The Marshall and Swift Cost Index (M&S) (2020) indexer was employed for the price readjustment37.

Economic feasibility evaluation

Table S1 summarizes the main techno-economic assumptions adopted for the economic assessment, including operational parameters, process-related costs, and financial criteria. Based on these assumptions, the total capital investment (TCI) and total product cost (TPC) were estimated for the three processing scales previously defined. Initial capital investment and total product costs were estimated using the cost structure and parameters given by Peter et al.34, Brown38 and Couper39. The methodology used was detailed in previous studies32. The total capital investment was categorized as direct fixed capital investment (equipment, machinery, office furniture, fittings, installed service facilities, and buildings); indirect fixed capital investment (land engineering, construction expenses, legal fees, contractors’ fees, and contingency costs); working capital; and startup expenses. The total product cost was segmented into manufacturing costs (direct production expenses, fixed charges, and plant overhead) and general expenses (administrative expenses, selling and distribution expenses, and research and development).

The percentage method was applied to estimate the costs of all products except for raw materials, reagents, operating labor, and utilities. The base price of microalgal biomass was determined based on commercial prices of comparable products available in the local market, pilot-scale operational data from southern Brazil, and literature reports indicating a wide cost variation ranging from €2.90 to €290 kg⁻¹22. The base selling price of bioactive-rich oil were determined based on market trading values for specialty microalgal oils enriched in ω−3 PUFAs and natural carotenoids40,41. Market data indicate that bulk algal oils (30–60% DHA/EPA) typically range from US$ 20–50 kg⁻¹, while high-purity pharmaceutical grades (> 70–90% PUFA) may reach US$ 200–800 kg⁻¹42,43. Therefore, the adopted value represents an intermediate purified grade consistent with documented market segments.

The costs of chemicals (ethanol and coagulant) and utilities (thermal energy, water, electric energy, and waste disposal) were obtained from literature and vendor quotations44–48. The estimation for operating labor was based on the methodology by Peter et al.34. The economic viability of investment was evaluated using the financial indices Net Present Value (NPV), Internal Rate of Return (IRR), Payback Period, Earnings Before Interest and Taxes (EBIT), and Return on Sales (ROS), derived from equations taken from conventional economic models and data obtained from anticipated cash flows49,50, as described elsewhere32.

Results and discussion

Biomass characterization

Proximal composition and total carotenoid content

N. oculata biomass composition was 42.74 ± 2.56% of proteins, 43.50 ± 0.01% of lipids, 4.95 ± 0.06% of ashes, and 8.81% of carbohydrates (dry basis). Other authors reported similar values, with protein content varying from 14 to 47%, lipids from 6 to 40%, carbohydrates from 6 to 36%, and ash from 7 to 12% for different Nannochloropsis strains51. The relatively high lipid content observed in this strain is advantageous compared to other microalgae species, such as Chlorella (20–30%), as it directly enhances oil yield per unit of biomass and positively impacts overall process profitability52. The total polar lipid content of the biomass was 27.8 ± 0.05%, indicating that ethanol extracts approximately 64% of th total lipid content.

Regarding carotenoid content, the biomass presented a total concentration of 1.40 ± 0.02 mg g− 1, which falls within the range reported in the literature (from 0.4 to 2.9 mg g− 1)53. It is important to note that in Nannochloropsis, carotenoids such as violaxanthin are predominantly present as oxygenated xanthophylls associated with membrane polar lipids. Due to their partial polarity and structural association with glycolipids and phospholipids, a significant fraction of these carotenoids is expected to be recovered in the ethanol extract during polar lipid extraction. Previous studies have reported carotenoid extraction yields ranging from 33 to 65%, depending on the extraction conditions54,55. Therefore, carotenoids were assumed to be co-extracted with ethanol and incorporated into the bioactive-rich oil.

Fatty acids methyl esters (FAMEs) analyses

Table S2 shows the FAMEs profile of the N. oculata oil, revealing a lipid composition rich in PUFAs, followed by saturated fatty acids (SFAs), and monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs). The major fatty acids present are eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) (ω−3) (25.6%), palmitic acid (24.6%), and palmitoleic acid (22.2%). Smaller amounts of other fatty acids, such as γ-linolenic acid (ALA) (ω−6), oleic acid (ω−9), linoleic acid (ω−6), were also identified. Similar results were found by Patil et al.56 for N. oceanica. The researchers reported 37.8% o PUFAs, 37.1% o SFAs, and 22.8% o MUFAs, and also observed high amounts of EPA (23.4%).

The FAME profile confirms that the N. oculata oil is naturally rich in PUFAs, particularly EPA (25.6%). The omega-3 fatty acids content in the biomass suggest that these products would have a high market value in comparison to other lipid products57. This compositional profile supports the economic assumption of a US$100 kg⁻¹ selling price, consistent with high-value omega-3 concentrates.

Structure and morphology of the microalgae cells

Figure 3 shows microscopy images of microalgae cells after freeze-drying. Most cells appear intact, maintaining their round structure with no visible damage. Additionally, the small cell size of N. oculata is evident, highlighting the challenge of separating these microalgae.

Mass and energy balance: equipment sizing and process yields

Table S3 presents the mass balance for all streams depicted in Fig. 2, based on a processing capacity of 720 kg h⁻¹ of biomass. As the scaling relationships are linear, the material flow rates corresponding to the 360 and 180 kg h⁻¹ scales are not shown. Results of the mass balance showed that from the total biomass introduced into the process, 28% would be oil (rich in PUFAs and carotenoids), while 72% would remain as solid (named as “defatted biomass”).

Table S4 presents the equipment capacities and energy demand for the three processing scenarios evaluated for producing bioactive-rich oil from N. oculata. As described in Sect. 2.3.2, the reported energy demand values predominantly reflect thermal energy requirements, with the exception of the dewatering press, for which electrical energy consumption was considered. For the dewatering press and tray dryers, the flow values exceeded the maximum capacity of the individual equipment, necessitating the use of multiple units connected in parallel. The energy demand values obtained for each equipment were used to estimate the costs of utilities. The evaporator accounted for the highest energy demand in the process, primarily due to ethanol recovery through phase change, which requires substantial latent heat of vaporization. In solvent-based extraction systems, solvent regeneration is commonly the dominant contributor to thermal energy consumption, particularly when high solvent-to-feed ratios are employed34,58. In the present configuration, the evaporator was the most thermal energy-intensive unit, followed by the desolventizer, responsible for removing residual solvent from the extracted solids, and the tray dryer, which requires continuous heat input to reduce biomass moisture prior to extraction.

Cost analysis

Figure 4 and Table S5 present the annual production costs for the three plant sizes. The raw materials and general expenses (administrative, selling, distribution, and research and development) contribute to approximately 98% of total costs. The most considerable contribution to total product costs came from the N. oculata biomass cost (77.67–77.61%). The biorefinery of this study does not consider the cultivation stage, and cultivation costs were included in the cost of N. oculata biomass (25 US$ kg− 1). Shimi et al.59 evaluated the costs of biodiesel production process from microalgae oil and found that the raw material and utilities account for 70 to 75% o the total production costs. In the present study, general expenses represented 19.60–19.59% o the total product cost and were distributed as follows: administrative expenses (6.43%), selling and distribution expenses accounted for (10.97–10.96%),and research and development expenses (2.19%).

Cost analysis of microalgae bioactive-rich oil and defatted biomass production.

Revenue estimates were based on the output obtained from processing a unit of raw material. The utility costs ranged from US$ 285.316 million for the large plant to US$ 73.913 million for the small plant. Fixed charges decreased from US$ 0.241 million for the highest capacity to US$ 0.075 million for the smallest capacity (a decrease of approximately US$ 0.165 million). Moreover, the results showed that, due to economies of scale, the percentages of raw materials, general expenses, and operating labor increase relative to the total cost. On the other hand, direct production costs, fixed charges, and plant overhead decrease. Economies of scale occur when an increase in production results in a reduction in average costs per unit. This is particularly relevant for fixed costs, which tend to decrease as production volume increases. Fixed costs and plant overhead, which are less sensitive to production volume, decrease proportionally relative to the total cost. Conversely, variable costs, such as raw materials and overheads, can represent a larger share of total costs in large-scale production since they increase directly with the production level60.

Financial indices were computed using a minimum acceptable rate of return (MARR) of 20%, and income tax was applied at 15% of profit before tax. The small plant showed an EBIT of US$ 4.42 million year-1, ROS of 8.77%, profit after tax of US$ 3.75 million year-1, NPV of US$11.42 million, and an IRR of 138,01%. The project can be considered attractive because the expected return is greater than the opportunity cost (IRR > MAR). The high IRR values observed (ranging from 138.01% to 160.59%) are primarily attributed to the high market value of the bioactive-rich oil coupled with a relatively low total capital investment. While the ROS remains moderate at approximately 8–9%, reflecting substantial annual operating costs, particularly the raw biomass price, the rapid capital recovery is driven by the significant annual net profits relative to the initial plant cost. This discrepancy between high IRR and moderate ROS is characteristic of processes where high-value specialty chemicals are produced from expensive raw materials in relatively compact industrial facilities.

To further validate the economic superiority of this biorefinery model, a direct comparison was performed against a standard biodiesel production scenario (transesterification) using the same 720 kg h⁻¹ scale and biomass cost (US$ 25 kg⁻¹). In the biodiesel scenario, even assuming a 100% conversion yield, the raw material cost alone (contributing US$ 65.78 per kg of oil produced) far exceeds the market value of biodiesel (approximately US$ 1.50–2.50 kg⁻¹)14,61. Consequently, the biodiesel route results in a negative NPV and proves unfeasible under these conditions, whereas the bioactive-rich oil route remains highly profitable due to its high added value (US$ 100 kg⁻¹).

Although economies of scale contributed to reducing the minimum selling price (MSP), scale effects alone do not represent the main contribution of this study. Microalgal biomass alone represents approximately 77–78% of the total product cost across all evaluated capacities (Table S5), whereas utilities account for less than 0.2%. This indicates that the economic feasibility of the proposed process is primarily sensitive to feedstock price rather than to energy demand or equipment scaling. The sensitivity analysis also indicates that NPV remains positive for microalgae biomass costs ranging from US$ 27.67 kg− 1 to US$ 27.59 kg− 1. It is important to emphasize that biomass production costs exhibit considerable variability due different productivities (different strains present different productivity values), geographic locations, cultivation technologies, and dewatering efficiencies, resulting in a wide range of cost estimates64. According to Vázquez-Romero et al.22, establishing a single production cost is not straightforward, as reflected by the variability of microalgae costs obtained by different authors, ranging from € 2.90 kg− 1 to € 290 kg− 1.

Overall, this study advances the economic assessment of microalgal systems by demonstrating that profitability is primarily driven by product value rather than scale alone. Considering a conservative biomass cost scenario (US$ 25 kg⁻¹), the results provide evidence that high-value bioactive oil production within a biorefinery framework can overcome the economic limitations typically associated with microalgae-based biodiesel. Comparative analysis highlights the structural advantage of value-added product strategies and quantitatively demonstrates their impact on NPV, IRR, and payback period, contributing practical decision-support information for future industrial implementation.

Conclusions

This study presented a techno-economic assessment of a N. oculata biorefinery for the production of bioactive-rich oil and defatted biomass under three processing capacities (720, 360, and 180 kg h⁻¹). All evaluated scenarios demonstrated economic feasibility, with positive NPVs and high IRRs (138–160%), and payback periods ranging from 1.28 to 1.63 years, indicating strong investment attractiveness even under a biomass cost of US$ 25 kg⁻¹. Direct production costs, general expenses, and total product costs decrease as plant capacity increases. Although larger plants exhibited improved profitability due to economies of scale and dilution of fixed costs, the results demonstrate that product value plays a more decisive role than scale alone in ensuring economic viability. The high contribution of biomass cost to total production cost (77–78%) highlights that upstream optimization remains a critical factor for further improving competitiveness. Furthermore, the proposed biorefinery configuration offers a valorization pathway that can be integrated into algal systems designed for nutrient recovery or wastewater treatment, supporting circular economy strategies. Overall, this work provides quantitative evidence that shifting from bulk biofuel production toward high-value bioactive compounds significantly enhances the commercial prospects of microalgae-based processes and offers a structured framework for future industrial implementation.
