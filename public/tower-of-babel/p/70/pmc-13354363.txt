Motor neuropathy following inhalational exposure to aluminum phosphide: a case report and literature review
Authors: Alisha Kusatha, Surya Raj Nishad, Roshan Raj Nishad, Aashutosh Jha, Ujwal Sah, Sarala Koju, Rajkishan Kushwaha, Krishna Lawaju, Bamdev Parajuli, Rajan Basnet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Aluminum phosphide (AlP) poisoning is a common means of self-harm in South Asia, while accidental inhalational exposure remains underreported despite its severe toxicity. This case reports a unique instance of acute neurotoxicity following inhalational AlP exposure, thereby expanding the current knowledge of phosphine-related neurological sequelae.Case presentation:A previously healthy 21-year-old female developed acute shortness of breath, vomiting, tingling sensations, and symmetrical limb weakness after inhaling AlP fumes. Examination revealed respiratory distress with bilateral limb weakness but preserved consciousness. Investigations showed respiratory alkalosis, elevated lactate, and transient hyperglycemia. She required ICU admission, mechanical ventilation, high-flow oxygen, and supportive care. Although clinically stabilized, persistent bilateral lower-limb weakness remained. Neuroimaging excluded central causes, suggesting peripheral motor neuropathy secondary to phosphine toxicity. Nerve conduction studies were planned during follow-up; however, the patient declined further evaluation because of financial constraints.Conclusion:This case highlights inhalational AlP as a dangerous route of exposure, capable of inducing early and significant neurological deficits even in the absence of altered mental status. It underscores the importance of heightened clinical vigilance, early neuro-monitoring, and research into neuroprotective strategies for phosphine toxicity. It also emphasizes the need for public education on the dangers of pesticide misuse, particularly in unventilated environments.

Introduction

Aluminum phosphide (AlP) is a widely used pesticide in South Asia[1], known for its high toxicity and rapid release of phosphine gas upon contact with moisture[2]. Although most cases of AlP poisoning result from intentional ingestion in suicidal attempts, less common routes, such as occupational and accidental inhalational exposures, have also been documented. While the cardiopulmonary manifestations of AlP toxicity are well recognized[1], its neurological effects, particularly in cases of inhalational exposure, remain poorly understood and sparsely reported in the medical literature. The present case is among the few reports documenting early and persistent neurological sequelae following inhalational AlP exposure. This case aims to expand the clinical understanding of phosphine-related neurotoxicity and highlights the need for comprehensive neurological monitoring, even in conscious, oriented patients, and early intervention strategies in AlP poisoning scenarios.

This case has been reported in accordance with the CARE guidelines[4] and the TITAN guidelines[5].

Case presentation

A 21-year-old female was exposed to AlP pellets (Fig. 1), which were used for bedbug control in her room. The pellets were bought from a nearby shop, where she was falsely assured that it was safe to sleep in the treated room. She used five AlP pellets (~60 g) in her room under her mattress at around 5 p.m. Although the room was initially ventilated, it was closed at around 10 p.m. before she went to sleep. At around 1 a.m., she woke up with breathing difficulty, a tingling sensation in her limbs, and nausea. She went outside briefly for fresh air but returned to the same room. Similar symptoms recurred twice during the night. She also vomited multiple times (non-bloody, odorless, food contents) before her arrival at the hospital. By 9 a.m., she presented to the Emergency Department with worsening symptoms, bilateral limb weakness, and chest tightness. She had no significant past medical, surgical, or psychiatric history. There was no history of substance use, genetic disorders, or notable family illness.

On examination, she was conscious (GCS 15/15), anxious, hyperventilating, and tachypneic, with a respiratory rate (RR) of 52/min, a pulse rate of 120 bpm, SpO2 of 97% on room air, and GRBS of 194 mg/dL. Pupils were bilaterally dilated and reactive (5 mm). Neurological examination revealed profound generalized weakness with motor power 1/5 in both upper and lower limbs, while sensory function and reflexes were preserved. The chest examination revealed equal air entry that was clear to auscultation with no retractions, and head, neck, and abdominal examinations were unremarkable. Capillary refill time was >3 seconds, with cool distal extremities.

She was kept nil per oral, managed with a reservoir mask with 10 liters of O2, antiemetics, proton pump inhibitors, anti-anxiety medication, intravenous fluids, and corticosteroids (to reduce airway inflammation and acute respiratory compromise). Immediate medical consultation was conducted, and the patient was admitted to the Medicine Intensive Care Unit (MICU).

In the MICU, the patient initially required high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) at 40% FiO2 for tachypnea and remained hemodynamically stable for 10 hours before developing acute respiratory distress with tachypnea (RR 60/min), tachycardia (HR 124 bpm), anxiety, chest tightness, abdominal pain, blurred vision, and persistent muscle weakness. FiO2 was increased to 50%, and IV calcium gluconate and magnesium sulfate were administered for membrane stabilization and anti-arrhythmic effects. Progressive hypoxemia (SpO2 86%) with transient apnea (>30 sec) prompted endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation for impending respiratory failure. A generalized seizure occurred post-intubation and was aborted with IV midazolam. The following day, hypotension developed, requiring noradrenaline support, which was tapered off by day 4. She was weaned from ventilation and extubated on day 5. Neurologically, generalized muscle weakness (initial motor power 1/5) improved to 4/5 without flaccidity, with normalization of electrolytes. At discharge, she was hemodynamically stable with SpO2 > 96% on room air, with residual bilateral lower-limb weakness and mild chest discomfort.

Follow-up

At 2 months post-exposure, the patient reported persistent chest discomfort and bilateral lower-limb weakness with flaccidity (motor power 3/5), preserved sensation, and intact deep tendon reflexes; she required assistance for standing and walking but could move her legs while seated. A screening of brain and whole-spine magnetic resonance imaging showed no acute pathology, excluding central causes, while alternative etiologies, including critical illness neuropathy, electrolyte imbalance, myositis, and nutritional deficiencies, were ruled out through serial biochemical and hematologic evaluations. High resolution computed tomography chest demonstrated resolving post-toxic inflammatory changes without fibrosis or active infection, and chest X-ray and ECG were normal. She was advised physiotherapy, incentive spirometry, multivitamins, and follow-up with nerve conduction study (NCS) and pulmonary function testing (PFT) at 3 months/SOS.

However, she did not attend the scheduled visit and presented only at 6 months with improved motor strength (4/5) and resolution of flaccidity, though she remained unable to walk independently, with persistent vague chest discomfort. Despite counseling, she declined NCS and PFT and opted for continued conservative management with physiotherapy and oral supplements (vitamin B12 and calcium), with advice for follow-up in 3 months or earlier if symptoms worsened.

Discussion

Aluminum phosphide is widely used as a fumigant for grain storage, but domestic misuse for pest control, especially in poorly ventilated spaces, poses a serious risk of accidental inhalational toxicity, as in this case. Upon contact with moisture, regardless of quantity, AlP liberates highly toxic phosphine gas[2]. This toxic action is not only harmful to insects and rodents but also markedly hazardous to human health[6].

Inhalational exposure is particularly dangerous due to the rapid absorption of PH3 across the moist respiratory epithelium, bypassing gastrointestinal detoxification. Importantly, phosphine gas reacts with hydroxyl radicals (HOₓ) in the atmosphere and is eliminated via photochemical degradation, with a half-life of approximately 5 hours in daylight and 28 hours in darkness[2]. Additionally, PH3 is heavier than air, so proximity to a source of phosphine is not required to be at risk of toxicity[7]. These properties make inhalational exposure particularly hazardous, especially in poorly ventilated indoor settings, where the gas can persist and accumulate to lethal levels, making domestic misuse dangerous.

The National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) designates an Immediately Dangerous to Life or Health (IDLH) level for phosphine at 50 ppm[8]. Experimental lethality has been reported at 11 ppm over 4 hours and 200 ppm hours for cumulative exposure, indicating the narrow safety margin of this compound[2,7]. Similarly, the studies by the inhalation route indicate that both the concentration and duration of exposure are important determinants of acute lethality[7]. The patient was exposed to approximately 60 grams of 15% aluminum phosphide, packed into five tablets, and released in a single room enclosure over ~8 hours. Despite the generation of a large quantity of phosphine, the patient perceived no odor, consistent with the fact that pure PH3 is colorless and odorless and that the characteristic “garlicky” or “fishy” odor is due to impurities like diphosphine (P2H4)[2]. Although the phosphine levels in the patient’s room were not quantified, the symptom onset, exposure duration, and the quantity released suggest near-lethal inhalational toxicity.

The acute presentation of this patient is consistent with early mild to moderate systemic toxicity from phosphine inhalation[2]. The neurological involvement in this case is of particular clinical importance due to its persistence beyond the resolution of acute cardiorespiratory instability. The characteristic persistence of bilateral lower limb weakness with flaccidity, plantar flexion, and intact sensation points to a probable motor-dominant neuropathy, especially a lower motor neuron lesion rather than central pathology or toxic myopathy, as supported by neuroimaging, although the patient denied NCS. The heterogeneity in neurological outcomes across published cases likely reflects differences in exposure route, intensity, and duration. While acute high-dose exposures have more commonly been associated with transient or reversible neurological dysfunction and delayed presentation, prolonged or repeated inhalational exposure has been linked to sustained neurological deficits documented at follow-up, possibly related to cumulative mitochondrial and axonal injury[9–14]. Despite the correction of acid-base status and cardiorespiratory parameters, the neurological deficit persisted, (bilaterally or bilateral) lower limb motor weakness with preserved sensation following initial stabilization, reinforcing the probable role of neurotoxic mechanisms of phosphine.

The mechanisms of phosphine-induced neurotoxicity remain incompletely understood but appear multifactorial: Primarily mediated through mitochondrial dysfunction and histotoxic hypoxia, resulting from the inhibition of cytochrome c oxidase and the disruption of oxidative phosphorylation, as demonstrated in experimental models and supported by clinical observations[7,15,16].This mitochondrial inhibition is accompanied by increased oxidative stress, with excess generation of superoxide and peroxide radicals, leading to lipid peroxidation and protein denaturation[16].Disturbances in glucose metabolism, including transient hypo- or hyperglycemia (also observed in this case), may reflect a stress response mediated by catecholamines and cortisol, compounded by mitochondrial inhibition and oxidative stress impairing glucose utilization, have also been reported and may influence neurological outcomes[7,17].

Management of AlP poisoning remains largely supportive. The absence of a specific antidote necessitates[2,7] early ICU admission, hemodynamic support, and respiratory support to prevent hypoxic injury. A GIK infusion was administered as adjunctive treatment to counteract myocardial depression and metabolic derangements, based on clinical and mechanistic rationale[18]. Subsequent pilot studies and institutional studies have suggested potential hemodynamic benefits, although evidence remains limited, and its impact on neurological outcomes is unclear[19,20].

Although prognostic scoring systems such as the PGI score[21], SAPS II (Simplified Acute Physiology Score)[22], APACHE II (Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation)[23], and PSS (Poison Severity Score)[24] are valuable tools for predicting early mortality and systemic toxicity in AlP poisoning, their utility is restricted to the acute phase of illness. In our case, the SAPS II score was 8 and the PGI score was <1, both indicating a low risk of mortality and limited systemic toxicity. However, these scoring systems are limited to the acute phase and do not capture the risk of long-term complications, as highlighted by our patient’s persistent neurological and respiratory sequelae. This discrepancy highlights a critical knowledge gap and underscores the need for follow-up assessments aimed at detecting chronic sequelae and guiding rehabilitation.

Limitation

The main limitation of this report is the lack of electrophysiological confirmation due to patient refusal, which restricts precise localization and classification of the lesion. Additionally, quantitative phosphine levels were not measured, which is common in resource-limited settings. Despite these constraints, the comprehensive clinical documentation and exclusion of alternate causes provide a reasonable basis for causal inference. The case should, therefore, be interpreted as probable rather than definite toxic motor neuropathy secondary to aluminum phosphide inhalation.

Conclusion

This case highlights the importance of vigilant neurological monitoring and comprehensive neurological assessment during the management of AlP poisoning, as preserved mental status may not exclude evolving neurotoxicity. It also underscores the need for structured follow-up protocols or scoring tools to predict long-term neurological morbidity, along with public education on safe pesticide handling and further research into neurophysiological assessment, neuroprotective strategies, and rehabilitation in phosphine poisoning survivors.
