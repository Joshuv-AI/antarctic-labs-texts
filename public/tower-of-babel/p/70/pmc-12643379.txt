Barium Meal and Follow-up in the Diagnosis of Chronic Appendicitis in Children with Recurrent Abdominal Pain: Is the Old War Horse Still Effective?
Authors: Amar Shah, Ravi Gajjar, Ria Sharma, Palak Raninga, Anirudh Shah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Recurrent abdominal pain (RAP) in children presents a complex diagnostic challenge, with causes ranging from organic to functional and psychological. While most cases are benign, chronic appendicitis remains a rare but important consideration. Its atypical presentation and overlap with other gastrointestinal disorders make diagnosis difficult. Among the various diagnostic tools, barium meal studies, though not primarily designed to assess the appendix, can provide useful insights when combined with small bowel follow-through, potentially revealing subtle indicators of appendiceal pathology in children with RAP.Methods:A retrospective review was conducted on 20 children with RAP lasting 3–18 months, all previously evaluated for both functional and organic causes without a diagnosis. Ultrasound was unremarkable in all, and 17 had normal computed tomography scans. After clinical evaluation, all underwent barium meal and follow-through studies. Findings suggestive of chronic appendicitis, such as nonvisualization, mucosal irregularities, or filling defects, prompted laparoscopic appendectomy. All patients underwent regular postoperative follow-ups.Results:All children demonstrated imaging features consistent with chronic appendicitis and underwent laparoscopic appendectomy. Intraoperative findings were unremarkable aside from the appendix. Histopathology confirmed chronic inflammation, with mucosal fibrosis, lymphoid hyperplasia, and inflammatory infiltrates. Postsurgery, all patients reported complete symptom resolution.Conclusion:Though largely superseded by modern imaging, the barium meal with follow-through can still serve as a valuable adjunct in pediatric RAP cases, especially when conventional modalities are inconclusive or inaccessible. While no longer central in diagnosis, it remains a potentially useful tool in the broader diagnostic arsenal.

INTRODUCTION

Recurrent abdominal pain (RAP) in children is one of the most perplexing challenges in pediatric medicine. Often functional, sometimes psychological, and occasionally organic, the causes are varied and elusive. While most cases are benign, a subset of children may suffer from chronic appendicitis, which is a rare but significant cause of persistent or intermittent abdominal pain. Diagnosing chronic appendicitis can be difficult due to its atypical presentation and overlap with other gastrointestinal (GI) disorders. Among various diagnostic tools, barium meal studies have historically been employed as an adjunct to help identify abnormalities in the GI tract, including the appendix. Although not designed specifically to examine the appendix, this imaging modality, when combined with small bowel follow-through, can offer subtle but telling clues to diagnose appendiceal pathology in children with RAP.

METHODS

This is a retrospective study of 20 children who were referred to us with RAP. The pain was located over the central and right lower abdomen. All the children had been extensively investigated and treated for organic and functional causes by their respective pediatricians. Three children had also undergone counseling by a pediatric psychologist. All children had undergone multiple ultrasound scans, which were unremarkable. Seventeen of the 20 children had also undergone a computed tomography (CT) scan of the abdomen, which was reported normal. After a detailed history and thorough clinical examination, all 20 children underwent a barium meal and follow-up. Following a plain X-ray, all children were given 150–180 ml of barium sulfate orally, and serial X-rays were taken as it passed through the esophagus, stomach, small, and large bowel. Postevacuation or delayed X-rays were taken as deemed necessary by the radiologist. Chronic appendicitis was suggested by either nonvisualization of the appendix, mucosal irregularities, partial filling of the lumen, or filling defects in the lumen suggestive of fecoliths [Figures 1–4]. These children underwent laparoscopic appendicectomy. All patients were given three doses of intravenous antibiotics (third-generation cephalosporin), starting with the preinduction dose. Children were discharged when they were in satisfactory condition, afebrile, free of pain, and feeding well. Paracetamol was used for analgesia when necessary. Appendices were sent for histology. All children were followed after 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months of discharge.

RESULTS

Twenty children with RAP were evaluated. The mean age was 10.5 years (7–14 years). There were 14 males and 6 females. They had symptoms of RAP for a mean period of 8.9 months (3–18 months). The pain was predominantly over the central and/or over the right lower abdomen. Clinical examination showed no palpable lumps or any particular area of pinpoint tenderness over the abdomen. All children had undergone extensive serological investigations, ultrasound scans, and CT scans with contrast (17 of 20). All these investigations were nonsuggestive of a particular cause. All the children were adequately treated medically for organic and functional causes by their treating pediatricians. Three children had also undergone counseling by a child psychologist. Barium meal and follow-up in these children were suggestive of chronic appendicitis. Further to this, a laparoscopic appendicectomy was done. There were no other additional pathologies found at laparoscopy. Macroscopic appearance showed scarring of the appendiceal wall with mural thickening. The lumen of the appendix was shrunken, irregular, or obliterated from chronic inflammation, fibrosis, or fecoliths. Microscopic examination showed mucosal and submucosal fibrosis, prominent lymphoid follicles in the submucosa, and infiltration of the lamina propria by lymphocytes, plasma cells, and macrophages. All children recovered well and were relieved of their symptoms on follow-up.

DISCUSSION

RAP is defined as three or more episodes of significant abdominal pain and discomfort over at least 3 months, disrupting a child’s normal activities. First described by Dr. John Apley in the 1950s, this definition remains widely accepted, though modern approaches may also assess for identifiable organic causes. Although not the most common form of appendiceal illness, chronic appendicitis is well recognized as a cause of RAP in children. Although the definition is not completely consensual, chronic appendicitis is marked by persistent, low-grade inflammation of the appendix leading to recurring or prolonged abdominal pain or discomfort.[12] Though its existence appears to be controversial among many physicians, chronic appendicitis does pose a diagnostic and therapeutic dilemma as the majority of the children present with atypical symptoms.[3] In the past few decades, numerous case reports and case series have emerged, offering substantiating evidence that chronic appendicitis although uncommon, does occur, and should be considered in the differential diagnosis of RAP.[4] A recent study even advocates that laparoscopic appendectomy should be considered early for patients with unremarkable workup with chronic right lower quadrant abdominal pain of unclear etiology.[5] Another study showed that three-quarters of all patients with pain in the right lower quadrant but no serious signs of inflammation showed histologic criteria for chronic appendicitis.[6] It has been estimated that approximately 14%–30% of patients undergoing an appendectomy will have findings consistent with chronic appendicitis.[78]

Chronic appendicitis does not cause fever or acute tenderness. Rather, it lingers, causing vague, intermittent right lower quadrant pain, mild GI disturbances, and a general sense of unease. It’s frequently misattributed to irritable bowel syndrome, mesenteric lymphadenitis, or even growing pains. Because symptoms often wax and wane and laboratory results are frequently normal or only mildly abnormal, imaging becomes essential to find the potential cause of this problem. The pathophysiology of chronic appendicitis is thought to be related to a partial or chronic appendiceal obstruction, and diagnosis is frequently made following an appendectomy based on histologic findings of chronic inflammatory changes.[9] Recognition of this pathology is important in children, as careful investigation and treatment can alleviate symptoms and reduce complications.

Ultrasound is the first imaging modality used in children due to its safety and its ability to visualize the appendix. However, it has several limitations in diagnosing chronic appendicitis. The image quality and interpretation can vary widely depending on the radiologist’s skill. Chronic appendicitis often presents with only mild changes, which are difficult to detect by an inexperienced radiologist. Factors such as overlying bowel gas, obesity, or a deeply located retrocecal appendix can obscure visualization. Moreover, findings such as mild wall thickening or lymphadenopathy may mimic other conditions, making the diagnosis even more challenging.[10] CT scan with contrast is typically used when ultrasound findings are equivocal or inconclusive, or when the child is older, obese, or with associated GI symptoms. CT scan has more than 90% sensitivity and specificity in diagnosing acute appendicitis. However, it has limited sensitivity in diagnosing chronic appendicitis because the findings are often subtle and can lead to misdiagnosis or overlap with other conditions, making the diagnosis unclear. In cases of chronic appendicitis, CT may show only mild appendiceal wall thickening without signs of severe inflammation. The appendix is also typically less distended than in acute cases. In addition, fat stranding or surrounding inflammation is less pronounced, fluid collections are often absent or minimal, and there may be only slight enlargement of nearby lymph nodes, all of which can make distinguishing chronic appendicitis from other abdominal conditions more difficult.[11]

Hence, in the world where high-resolution ultrasound and CT dominate, one might wonder: does barium meal and follow through, that old war horse of GI imaging, still have a role to play? Although primarily used to assess the esophagus, stomach, and small intestine, barium meal studies can occasionally provide clues to appendiceal pathology when combined with a small bowel follow-through. Schey was the first to outline the criteria for the diagnosis of appendicitis on barium enema.[12] These were nonfilling of the appendix with a local impression on the cecum, partial filling of the appendix, nonfilling of the appendix, and irregular mucosal pattern of the appendix or a “cut-off” sign, indicating a nontapered abrupt halt to the flow of barium in the appendix. Barium enema however, entails some amount of discomfort to the child. In contrast, barium meal and follow-through can be used as a simplified radiographic adjunct.[13] The procedure of performing a barium meal and follow-up was no different from the routine study. In certain cases, particularly when barium progresses through the terminal ileum into the cecum, it may enter and outline the appendiceal lumen. If the appendix is mildly inflamed or partially obstructed, the study can reveal indirect signs such as nonfilling or delayed filling of the appendix, partial filling with a narrowed lumen, displacement due to mass effect or fibrosis, and localized spasm or thickening of the terminal ileum or cecum. While these findings are not pathognomonic, they can support the diagnosis of chronic appendicitis when interpreted in conjunction with the patient’s clinical symptoms. Since filling of the appendix may be difficult, a second attempt spot filming on postevacuation and delayed X-rays may be necessary [Figure 5]. Patients with normal retrocecal appendix may also be difficult to fill. In these cases, delayed X-rays up to 6 h after the initial study or postevacuation films are essential before establishing a “no fill” situation. For the barium meal to be effective, appendiceal filling should be expected as often as with a barium enema. Goldman[14] showed that 80% of normal appendices will fill when barium is administered orally. Sakover and Del Fava[15] have also reported similar numbers with barium enema examinations in unobstructed colon. Thus, an 80% appendiceal filling rate can be expected in normal asymptomatic patients, regardless of the route by which barium is introduced. Recurrent appendicular infections could result in submucosal lymphoid hyperplasia, resulting in nonvisualization or partial filling of the appendix on a barium study. A common concern is regarding the radiation exposure caused by this study. The approximate Radiation exposure to a child during a CT scan of the abdomen with contrast is about 5–10 millisieverts (mSv). On the other hand, radiation exposure for a barium meal and follow-up study, along with X-ray films with intermittent bursts of Image Intensifier Television and Visualization screening, is 0.1–3.0 mSv.[16]

Although limited in contemporary pediatric practice, barium meal and follow-through studies may still provide diagnostic value for chronic appendicitis in select cases of RAP. While not routinely recommended for diagnosing chronic appendicitis, it can still play a role in specific clinical scenarios when advanced imaging is unavailable, other diagnostic modalities are inconclusive or as a part of a comprehensive GI workup in children with unexplained RAP.

CONCLUSION

A common challenge in pediatrics is addressing parental concerns about RAP in their children. Parents are often hesitant to consent for diagnostic laparoscopy and/or appendectomy without a definitive diagnosis. In the ever-evolving field of pediatric diagnostics, barium meal and follow-up remains a nostalgic yet occasionally useful tool which can offer valuable insights. While newer imaging modalities have largely taken over, there are still scenarios where the old war horse proves its worth. Its effectiveness lies not in pinpoint accuracy, but in its ability to support a larger clinical picture. In the right hands and the right setting, it still rides strong. The barium meal and follow-up is no longer the frontline in diagnosing chronic appendicitis, especially in well-resourced settings. However, in clinically ambiguous cases, in regions with limited access to modern imaging, or as part of a broader GI workup, it can still uncover subtle hints that direct clinicians toward the diagnosis and help reassure parents by providing a clearer understanding of the cause of their child’s persistent pain. It may not be the star of the show anymore, but it has not completely exited the stage either.
