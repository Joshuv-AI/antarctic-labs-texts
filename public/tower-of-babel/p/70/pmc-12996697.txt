Risk factors and prediction model for early high-output stoma after colorectal cancer surgery: A retrospective study
Authors: Haiyan Chen, Qunmin Wang, Yehong Chen, Lu Li, Feixia Wang, Chaoxu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis retrospective cohort study aimed to identify independent risk factors for early high-output stoma (HOS) following colorectal cancer (CRC) surgery and to construct and validate a predictive nomogram.MethodsConsecutive patients who underwent ileostomy for CRC at a tertiary hospital were enrolled. The cohort was divided into a training set (n = 470; January–December 2024) and a temporal validation set (n = 187; January–May 2025). Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify independent predictors and construct the nomogram. Model performance was evaluated in terms of discrimination (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [AUC]), calibration, and clinical utility using decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsThe overall incidence of early HOS was 27.7%. Independent predictors included sex, age, alcohol consumption, use of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs) or angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs), intestinal obstruction, serum albumin level, leukocytosis, and serum calcium level (all P < 0.05). The nomogram demonstrated excellent discrimination, with AUCs of 0.906 in the training set and 0.888 in the validation set. Calibration and DCA indicated good agreement and favorable clinical utility.ConclusionsThe proposed nomogram shows strong predictive performance and potential clinical applicability. It enables early risk stratification, thereby supporting targeted surveillance and individualized postoperative management for patients at high risk of HOS.Keywords: High-output stoma, Ileostomy, Colorectal cancer, Prediction model

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the second most prevalent cancer in China, with over 100,000 new patients requiring stoma formation each year.1 Recently, diverting ileostomy has been widely used in anal preservation procedures for CRC to reduce the risk of anastomotic leakage and improve surgical safety.2 It has been reported that the proportion of ileostomies among all stoma procedures has increased from 57% to 70%.3 However, the creation of an ileostomy alters bowel function and may lead to some stoma-related complications, such as dermatitis, stoma prolapse, parastomal hernia, high-output, and dehydration,4 with high-output stoma (HOS) being particularly prevalent in patients with an ileostomy. HOS is defined as a condition in which fluid loss through the stoma exceeds the body's compensatory capacity.5 It is primarily characterized by excessive stoma output and significant losses of fluids and electrolytes.6 Since the colon is an important site for water absorption, intestinal contents are discharged directly after surgery without reabsorption by the colon, which can easily lead to excessive fluid loss.4 Previous studies indicate that HOS most frequently occurs in the early postoperative period, affecting approximately 16%–50% of patients.7

As advances in medical care enable more high-risk patients to undergo ileostomy,8 the need for effective prediction and management of HOS has become increasingly important. Screening for risk factors and developing a predictive model can identify high-risk patients for prompt intervention. However, limited research is currently available on the development of a prediction model specifically for HOS. Fujino et al. analyzed 68 ileostomy patients and developed a decision tree model for HOS, identifying female sex, younger age, and elevated white blood cell (WBC) count as predictors.9 However, the model was limited by its small sample size and narrow range of variables. Similarly, Chen et al. established a prediction model for HOS in a cohort of 140 patients with ileostomy, incorporating hypertension, alcohol consumption, and elevated WBC count.10 Nevertheless, the model demonstrated only moderate predictive performance and lacked external validation. Despite the increasing prevalence of HOS, its risk factors remain complex and have not been fully defined. Seifarth et al. identified older age, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), and small bowel resection as risk factors for HOS,5 while Pencovich et al. indicated that older age, high body mass index (BMI), hypertension, elevated creatinine levels, and decreased albumin (Alb) and hemoglobin (Hb) levels are associated with HOS.11 Some recognized factors include residual small bowel length < 200 cm, infection, IBD, total colectomy, and the use of prokinetic drugs.12 Previous research indicates that HOS is associated with various factors, including demographics, underlying diseases, nutrition, medications, surgical procedures, and complications. However, some factors show inconsistent results across studies and a comprehensive predictive model for CRC patients with HOS is currently lacking.

The reported incidence of HOS varies from 23.7% to 31.1%,13 with variations likely due to the absence of a standardized definition. Currently, there is no consistent diagnostic criterion for HOS. It is often defined by the stoma output volume and duration within a specific time range, such as ≥ 1000 mL/24 h for three consecutive days,5,9,10 ≥ 1500 mL/24 h,14, 15, 16 ≥ 2000 mL/24 h,17 or ≥ 2000 mL/24 h for three consecutive days.18 HOS is considered one of the leading causes of unplanned readmission in patients with ileostomy, accounting for about 42% of cases.19 Notably, nearly half of patients with HOS experience malnutrition, and up to 51% need continuous medication.20, 21, 22 Additionally, HOS may cause renal impairment,19,23 potentially impairing the tolerability of subsequent treatments, such as adjuvant chemotherapy.

Given the limitations of existing research, it is necessary to develop a comprehensive and effective risk prediction model to reduce the incidence of HOS and improve patient outcomes. Therefore, this study seeks to fill the existing gap by developing a predictive model that incorporates various factors to identify early postoperative HOS risk in CRC patients. The aim is to establish a nomogram to help identify high-risk patients early, thereby improving perioperative management and reducing the incidence of HOS.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective cohort study employed convenience sampling to analyze patients who were hospitalized for CRC surgery and ileostomy at a tertiary hospital in China between January 2024 and May 2025. The patients were divided into two groups using a time-split approach: the modeling group and the validation group. Specifically, all eligible CRC patients who were consecutively enrolled from January 2024 to December 2024 (n = 470) were assigned to the modeling group, while those enrolled from January 2025 to May 2025 (n = 187) were assigned to the validation group. Both groups received the same preoperative stoma marking, bowel preparation, and postoperative care. They all underwent the same surgical procedure performed by the same surgical team, with ileostomies created approximately 15–25 cm from the ileocecal valve. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) confirmed diagnosis of CRC based on clinicopathologic findings; (3) availability of complete clinical data, including stoma output records, laboratory test results, and surgical records. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with severe organ dysfunction or critical illness; (2) patients with other gastrointestinal disorders (such as IBD, familial polyposis, or diverticulitis). This study was reported in accordance with the STROBE checklist for case–control studies (Supplementary File 1).

Sample size

This study included 26 potential risk factor variables. The sample size estimation was based on established guidelines for predictive modeling, which recommends a ratio of 5–10 times the number of variables.24 For this study, we selected 10 times the number of variables, resulting in a target sample size of 260 cases. Considering potential incomplete cases, the sample size was increased by 20%, bringing the total to at least 325 cases in the modeling group. Following the recommended sample size ratio of 7:3 between the modeling and validation groups for predictive modeling,25 the study initially included 739 patients. Based on the exclusion criteria, 82 patients were excluded and the final analysis included 657 patients. Among these, 470 patients were assigned to the modeling group, and 187 patients to the validation group. Fig. 1 shows the flow chart of the study design.

Research tools

Based on previous literature review and expert meetings, we designed a data collection form for HOS risk factors, which includes the following categories:

Patient-related data

Patient-related information included sex, age, BMI, hypertension, diabetes, drinking history (defined as an average daily intake of ≥ 50 g for more than 6 months), and smoking history (defined as smoking ≥ 1 cigarette per day for more than 1 year).

Disease and treatment-related data

Disease and treatment-related information included Tumor Node Metastasis (TNM) stage, history of previous abdominal surgery, preoperative neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, medication use [including diuretics, steroids, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs) and angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs)], American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification, surgical procedure, perioperative blood transfusion, postoperative intestinal obstruction, and the initiation time of enteral nutrition [postoperative day (POD)]. The use of diuretics and steroids refers to postoperative administration. The use of ACEIs/ARBs is defined as administration during the entire perioperative period.

Laboratory parameters

Postoperative laboratory examinations included postoperative creatinine, albumin (Alb), hemoglobin (Hb), white blood cell (WBC) count, C-reactive protein (CRP), and serum electrolyte levels (including potassium, sodium, and calcium).

Data collection

All variables were collected by two trained researchers from the hospital's electronic medical record system. BMI was measured preoperatively with standardized equipment and all laboratory indicators were analyzed using consistent devices at our hospital. Laboratory measurements, including creatinine, Alb, Hb, WBC count, CRP, and serum electrolyte levels, were collected 3–5 days prior to the day of maximum stoma output. Stoma output data were extracted from nursing records to determine the occurrence of HOS. In this study, HOS was defined as a maximum stoma output of ≥ 1500 mL/24 h during hospitalization. This cutoff aligns with the guidelines of the UK Association of Stoma Care Nurses26 and is widely adopted in current literature.14, 15, 16 The data were entered into Microsoft Excel and cross-verified to ensure accuracy.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were performed via SPSS 27.0 and RStudio 4.4.2. When processing missing data, cases with a missing data rate greater than 20% were initially excluded from the analysis. To address the remaining missing values, we employed predictive mean matching (PMM) as a method for multiple imputation. The missing values were replaced by the average of the five imputed values generated through multiple imputations. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using the t test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as medians and interquartile ranges and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher's exact test as appropriate. Logistic regression analysis was employed to identify independent predictors. A nomogram model was established using the ‘rms’ package. The model's predictive accuracy was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC). The calibration curves were used to evaluate the precision of the model predictions. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was conducted to assess the net benefit to patients. Additionally, internal validation was performed using the bootstrap resampling (1000 repetitions). A P-value of < 0.05 was considered significant. Statistical details are provided in Supplementary File 2.

Results

Data preprocessing

The overall rate of missing data across all samples was less than 20%. No missing values were observed in categorical variables. Continuous variables had an aggregate missing rate of 6.7%, specifically associated with BMI, serum sodium level, serum calcium level, and C-reactive protein, which had individual missing rates of 0.9%, 0.6%, 3.3%, and 1.8%, respectively. To address the missing continuous data, PMM was employed for multiple imputation.

Univariate analysis of risk factors for high-output stoma

A total of 470 patients were included in the modeling group, among whom 130 (27.7%) developed HOS. Among the continuous variables, only serum Alb, serum potassium, and serum calcium were normally distributed, while the others were not. The modeling group consisted of 311 males (66.2%) and 159 females (33.8%), with a median age of 65 years. Preoperative chemoradiotherapy was administered to 23.8% of the cohort, and 57.9% of patients had a tumor TNM stage of 0–2. Laparoscopic surgery was performed in 93.4% of cases. Postoperative intestinal obstruction occurred in 6.6% of patients. HOS occurred on average 5.8 days after surgery. The univariate analysis of factors associated with HOS is presented in Table 1. Significant differences between HOS and non-HOS groups were observed in sex, age, drinking history, smoking history, use of steroids and ACEIs/ARBs, intestinal obstruction, and postoperative creatinine, Alb, WBC, and calcium levels (P < 0.05).

Multivariate analysis of risk factors for high-output stoma

Forward stepwise logistic regression analysis was performed with variables that were statistically significant in the univariate analysis. Variables were selected using forward stepwise regression based on likelihood ratio tests, with an entry criterion of P < 0.05 and a removal criterion of P > 0.01. The results indicated that sex, age, drinking history, use of ACEIs/ARBs, intestinal obstruction, postoperative Alb, WBC count, and calcium level were independent risk factors for early HOS development in patients with ileostomy (P < 0.05), as presented in Table 2. The final multivariable model incorporated 8 predictors for 130 events of early postoperative HOS, yielding an events-per-variable ratio of 130/8 = 16.25, which is above the commonly recommended threshold of 10, indicating a robust model.

Establishment of a risk prediction model for early postoperative high-output stoma

Based on logistic regression, a risk prediction model was constructed with the following formula: logit (P) = 17.928 + 1.484 × (sex) − 0.035 × (age) + 0.892 × (drinking history) + 1.899 × (use of ACEIs/ARBs) + 1.774 × (intestinal obstruction) − 0.336 × (Alb)+ 0.384 × (WBC count) − 4.695 × (calcium). A visualized nomogram was established, as shown in Fig. 2A. Each variable is represented by the corresponding score in the nomogram (e.g., age = 60 years, corresponding to 10 points). All risk factors for HOS are added to calculate the total score, from which the corresponding risk probability can be determined. For example, as shown in Fig. 2B, a 90-year-old female patient with no history of alcohol consumption or use of ACEI/ARB developed postoperative intestinal obstruction. Her current laboratory results indicate an Alb level of 30 g/L, a WBC count of 13 × 109/L, and a serum calcium level of 1.9 mmol/L. As shown in the figure, the total score is approximately 160, corresponding to a predicted probability of > 0.9, indicating a high risk of early postoperative HOS.

The model demonstrated excellent discriminatory performance (AUC = 0.906, 95% CI: 0.875–0.936), with a Youden index of 0.680, accuracy of 85.1%, sensitivity of 81.5%, and specificity of 86.5% (Fig. 3A). The optimal cutoff value was 0.322. Calibration curve analysis revealed that the mean absolute error between the predicted and observed values was 0.013, with the curve closely aligning with the ideal line, further confirming model accuracy (Fig. 4A).

Model validation

Internal validation was performed via 1000 bootstrap resamples, which yielded an AUC of 0.912 (95% CI: 0.875–0.945), sensitivity of 80.0%, and specificity of 86.5%. Temporal validation was performed on a cohort of 187 patients with CRC, among whom 54 (28.9%) were HOS patients. No significant differences in baseline characteristics were observed between the modeling and temporal validation cohorts. The AUC for the validation set was 0.888 (95% CI: 0.839–0.937), with an accuracy of 80.2%, sensitivity of 88.9%, and specificity of 78.2% (Fig. 3B). Additionally, the results of the calibration curve for the validation group indicated that the calibration curve of the predictive model closely matched the ideal curve (Fig. 4B). The DCA curves showed good clinical benefit in both the modeling group (Fig. 5A) and validation group (Fig. 5B).

Discussion

Main findings

Higher incidence of early postoperative HOS in patients with CRC

This study found that early postoperative HOS occurred in 27.7% of the modeling cohort and 28.9% of the validation cohort. The incidence was consistent with previously reported rates ranging from 23.7% to 31.1%.13 The absence of a standardized definition for HOS may contribute to considerable variation in reported incidence rates. Future research should aim to establish definitive diagnostic criteria for HOS. Additionally, this study only included patients with CRC and excluded those with IBD and other intestinal disorders to enhance cohort homogeneity. However, earlier studies have shown a higher incidence of HOS in patients with IBD, which may be related to chronic inflammation, extensive bowel resection, and immunosuppressive treatments.27,28 As a prevalent complication following ileostomy, HOS poses significant clinical challenges that warrant more attention from healthcare providers. However, research indicates that many colorectal surgical nurses have insufficient knowledge and practice regarding HOS.13 In practice, standardized monitoring of stoma output is often overlooked, relying instead on subjective reports of patients and caregivers. Consequently, many cases of HOS remain undiagnosed until severe symptoms arise, such as dehydration, vomiting, dizziness, electrolyte imbalance, or acute kidney injury. This highlights the need to identify risk factors and high-risk patients, as well as develop preventive strategies for HOS.

Risk factors for early postoperative HOS in patients with CRC

In this study, we have identified the key variables that significantly contribute to the HOS, including sex, age, drinking history, ACEIs and ARBs use, intestinal obstruction, Alb, WBC count, and serum calcium. This study identified male sex and a history of alcohol consumption as risk factors for early postoperative HOS, aligning with the findings of Li Y and Gray et al.,29,30 which reported that males generally exhibit poorer bowel function following ileostomy due to physiological and lifestyle differences. Compared with females, males tend to have higher rates of smoking, alcohol consumption, and poor dietary habits. In this study, smoking (17.0% vs. 0%) and alcohol consumption (14.5% vs. 0.6%) were significantly higher in males than in females. Nicotine in tobacco can stimulate the sympathetic nervous system and promote intestinal motility,31 while excessive alcohol has been shown to inhibit the absorption of water and sodium and is often linked to diarrhea,32,33 potentially increasing stoma output. Some recent studies support our speculation.10,34 Therefore, for male patients with alcohol consumption, it is necessary for nurses to provide structured educational interventions and behavioral support on promoting a healthy lifestyle and dietary practices, particularly regarding smoking and alcohol cessation. Patients should receive explicit guidance on recognizing symptoms of dehydration (e.g., excessive thirst, dizziness, reduced urine output) and when to seek immediate medical care.

Moreover, this study revealed that younger age is associated with early HOS, which was similar to the findings of Fujino et al.9 Younger patients generally begin oral intake and physical activity earlier following surgery, which can promote intestinal motility and subsequently lead to early HOS.30 In contrast, older patients are often less physically active, which has been shown to be associated with constipation.30 Therefore, postoperative education on diet and activity is essential. Younger patients should avoid large meals and high-fiber or high-sugar foods initially which may exacerbate intestinal motility. Dietary changes should be gradual, and prophylactic use of low-dose loperamide or other antimotility agents may be considered when necessary. Nurses should evaluate patients’ activity levels and provide stepwise rehabilitation guidance to balance activity and prevent overexertion. In contrast to our findings, studies by Chun LJ and Pencovich N have identified older age as a risk factor for HOS,11,35 suggesting that elderly patients are more susceptible to developing HOS due to decreased ability to tolerate rapid fluid shifts. These conflicting findings suggest that age may influence the development of HOS through different physiological and behavioral mechanisms. Future research should further explore the association between age and HOS.

Besides demographic factors, ACEIs and ARBs, commonly prescribed for hypertension, have also been identified as contributing factors for early postoperative HOS. In the intestinal tract, the local renin‒angiotensin system (RAS) plays an pivotal role in maintaining fluid and electrolyte homeostasis and nutrient absorption.36 However, the administration of ACEIs and ARBs can inhibit the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), thereby impairing intestinal absorption.37,38 This is consistent with the findings of Charak G.39 Additionally, Slagelse noted that ACEIs/ARBs may cause renal impairment following CRC surgery, further affecting water and sodium regulation.40 Therefore, for patients who must continue taking ACEIs and ARBs, it is essential to closely monitor the stoma output, electrolyte levels, and serum creatinine to identify any potential renal function deterioration and electrolyte imbalances.

Consistent with recent studies,10,11,41 intestinal obstruction, hypoalbuminemia, and high WBC count were identified as key risk factors for early HOS in this study. All three factors are associated with postoperative inflammatory response. High WBC counts indicate excessive inflammation, potentially impairing intestinal barrier function and causing abnormal cytokine secretion, which can lead to intestinal dysfunction and HOS.10,41 Intestinal inflammation and edema are primary mechanisms through which obstruction causes HOS.41 HOS often occurs during recovery from intestinal obstruction.42 The accumulation of luminal contents in the proximal bowel promotes bacterial overgrowth, while the obstructed segment's inability to absorb fluids results in fluids loss through the stoma.7,17 Additionally, serum Alb not only reflects nutritional status but also plays a crucial role in inflammation and immune regulation.43 Due to systemic inflammatory response to the tumor, CRC patients often experience malnutrition and hypoalbuminemia,44,45 which can reduce plasma colloid osmotic pressure, causing intestinal and stoma edema, leading to stoma outlet obstruction and HOS.42 Additionally, Pak et al. noted that patients with hypoalbuminemia are more susceptible to diarrhea, especially when Alb levels drop below 2 g/dL.46 In the early postoperative period, it is essential to monitor inflammatory markers dynamically and administer prophylactic antibiotics appropriately. Meanwhile, prophylactic use of probiotics may be beneficial, which has been shown to lower proinflammatory cytokines and improve inflammation following CRC surgery.47 Additionally, nurses should closely monitor postoperative bowel function and abdominal signs to enable the prompt identification, diagnosis, and management of intestinal obstruction. Finally, it is important to manage hypoalbuminemia to prevent early HOS. Regular nutritional monitoring should be incorporated into routine stoma care, post-discharge follow-up, and transitional care. Diversified nutritional support, including nutritional supplements, fluid management, and specialized dietary interventions, should be developed based on patient needs. A multidisciplinary team of healthcare professionals should collaborate to develop and continuously refine nutrition management plans.

The most common symptoms of HOS are dehydration and electrolyte imbalance.27 In this study, patients with HOS had significantly lower levels of sodium and calcium, aligning with previous studies, which reported a high prevalence of electrolyte disturbances in patients with HOS.12,48 Although only serum calcium was significant in the final model, abnormalities in other electrolytes, such as potassium and sodium, may still hold predictive value for HOS. This finding underscores the need for close monitoring of electrolyte levels in patients with HOS. Once electrolyte abnormalities are detected, prompt interventions, such as fluid and electrolyte replacement or dietary and medication adjustments, should be implemented to reduce the risk of other complications, such as arrhythmias and neuromuscular dysfunction.49

Implications for nursing practice and research

HOS is a preventable complication following ileostomy. It would be helpful for healthcare professionals to predict which patients are at risk for developing HOS. This study developed a nomogram to predict early HOS, showing good predictive performance. The predictors are easily accessible from medical records or self-reports, making it convenient for use in clinical practice. Based on these predictors, nurses can assess the risk of early HOS and visually present risk probabilities, allowing for preventive measures. Such an approach offers valuable guidance for clinical decision-making and resource allocation. However, clinical applications should incorporate healthcare providers' clinical judgment and individual patient presentations to further enhance predictive accuracy. In addition, current clinical practice provides insufficient education on the early prevention and management of HOS. Health education mainly focuses on ostomy care knowledge and skills, resulting in patients’ poor awareness of HOS and increasing the risk of readmission due to fluid and electrolyte imbalances. Therefore, it is essential to strengthen patient education on the methods and importance of monitoring stoma output, enabling them to adopt measures to prevent dehydration and to recognize symptoms of fluid and electrolyte disturbances at an early stage. Patients should be encouraged to use intake–output diaries to record their daily stoma output and oral intake.50 Resources permitting, regular telephone follow-up after discharge can further reinforce education on early identification of HOS, which may be highly beneficial.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, this study was conducted at a single center and lacked validation in independent cohorts from other institutions. The retrospective design, relatively small sample size, and the use of temporal validation may limit the generalizability of the model and increase the risk of model overfitting. Second, the relatively short follow-up period may have underestimated the incidence of HOS, and the model was applied only to predict early postoperative HOS. Finally, this model serves as a valuable tool for early identification and prompts closer monitoring of high-risk individuals. Although certain demographic factors are non-modifiable, they are crucial for risk stratification. Future research should include large, multicenter prospective cohort studies that enroll patients from diverse clinical settings to evaluate the external validity of the model and optimize its clinical utility, and, on this basis, further explore preventive strategies for HOS.

Conclusions

In conclusion, male sex, younger age, drinking history, the use of ACEIs and ARBs, intestinal obstruction, hypoalbuminemia, high WBC count, and low calcium are identified as risk factors for early postoperative HOS. These factors interact through mechanisms involving nutrition, inflammation, and intestinal dysfunction. The nomogram model demonstrates good predictive performance. This practical tool supports healthcare professionals, particularly nurses, in early risk assessment and identification of high-risk patients after CRC surgery with ileostomy. Future multicenter prospective studies are warranted to validate its external applicability and refine clinical utility.
