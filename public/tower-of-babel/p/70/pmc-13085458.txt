Exceptional long-term survival in a patient with recurrent pancreatic carcinoma on adjuvant tegafur–gimeracil–oteracil potassium: a case report
Authors: Naomi Enyi Sim, Gwo Fuang Ho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPancreatic carcinoma is a highly malignant tumor, and it is aggressive even in its early stage. It has a very poor prognosis and responds poorly to systemic therapy. It is common for resected patients to undergo postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy. However, survival remains low, with an average 5-year survival of 10–25% and median survival between 10 and 20 months.Case presentationA 50-year-old Malaysian Chinese gentleman with no past medical history was diagnosed with stage IIb pancreatic cancer. A computed tomography scan of the abdomen showed a tumor at the uncinate lobe of the pancreatic head. He underwent a Whipple pancreaticoduodenectomy followed by postoperative chemotherapy with gemcitabine combined with 5-fluorouracil for 6 months. However, a rising carbohydrate antigen 19-9 level and a positron emission tomography–computed tomography scan showing a 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose avid focus above the resected pancreas led to a repeat laparotomy 10 months after the first surgery. Even though an omental tissue biopsy at the area of the hepatic flexure was normal, the CA19-9 level continued to increase, indicating tumor recurrence. Subsequently, the patient was treated with 25 fractions of radiotherapy to the peripancreatic lymph nodes followed by tegafur–gimeracil–oteracil potassium maintenance. He went on to achieve a progression-free survival of more than 5 years with good tolerability and no adverse drug induced reaction. More than 9 years into treatment, he continues to enjoy good health and quality of life and is tolerating the treatment well.ConclusionThis case shows an exceptional long-term survival of a patient with pancreatic cancer with early recurrence after adjuvant chemotherapy treated with maintenance tegafur–gimeracil–oteracil potassium.Keywords: Tegafur–gimeracil–oteracil potassium (TS-1), Gemcitabine, 5-Fluorouracil, CA19-9, Recurrent pancreatic carcinoma, Case report

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer is one of the most lethal malignancies, characterized by a poor prognosis and low survival rates. In Malaysia, according to the National Cancer Registry Report 2018 [1], the overall 5-year relative survival for patients with pancreatic cancer is 14%, with a median survival time of 6.5 months. This report also revealed that cancer survival estimates in Malaysia are comparable to those in other Asian countries, but lower compared with Australia, New Zealand, the USA, and most European countries. Survival outcome prediction is influenced by several factors, including the tumor marker carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA19-9) levels, presence of peripancreatic invasion, the number of distant organ metastases, and postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy. Herein, we report a case of a patient with recurrent pancreatic cancer who has survived for more than 9 years since his initial diagnosis. In this report, we present our experience in managing the disease and compare the survival outcome with the survival expectancy in Malaysia, as well as survival findings from tegafur–gimeracil–oteracil potassium (TS-1)-related studies, given that our patient was on TS-1 maintenance therapy. TS-1 is an oral fluoropyrimidine-based chemotherapy combining tegafur (a prodrug of 5-fluorouracil [5-FU]), gimeracil (which inhibits 5-FU degradation), and oteracil (which reduces gastrointestinal toxicity), originally developed for gastric cancer but now used for various solid tumors, including pancreatic cancer.

Case presentation

In January 2014, a 50-year-old Malaysian Chinese gentleman with no past medical history presented with a 6-month history of steatorrhea associated with episodes of loose stool and unintentional weight loss of 15 kg in 6 months. Clinically, he was not jaundiced, and there was no abdominal mass on palpation. He underwent a computed tomography (CT) scan of the abdomen in January 2014, which showed a soft tissue tumor at the uncinate lobe of the pancreatic head. There were no distant organ metastases. His baseline tumor marker, CA19-9 was raised at 80.4 U/mL and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) was 4.9 ng/mL. Consequently, he underwent a Whipple pancreaticoduodenectomy on 17 January 2014. Intraoperatively, the tumor was at the uncinate lobe and mildly adhered to the portal and superior mesenteric veins. The pancreatic duct was dilated, and the regional nodes were enlarged. The tumor specimen showed irregular infiltrating glandular structures with desmoplastic stroma, and was immunohistochemically positive for expression of CK7, CK19, MUC1, and S100P. Histopathology confirmed moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma of pancreas, with four out of seven peripancreatic lymph nodes showing tumor metastases. The size of the tumor was 55 mm × 45 mm and very close to the resection margin of the pancreas. However, molecular sequencing on the sample was not performed.

Postoperatively, he received six cycles of gemcitabine 1000 mg/m2 on day (D) 1, D8, and D15, and 5-fluorouracil 370 mg/m2 on D1, D8, and D15 with folinic acid 20 mg/m2 D1, D8, and D15 every 4 weeks for 6 months. His tumor markers normalized during chemotherapy, and an interval CT of the abdomen during chemotherapy in June 2014 showed no tumor in the remnant pancreas. Then, 3 weeks after completing chemotherapy, CA19-9 increased from 19 U/mL to 40 U/mL. A subsequent positron emission tomography (PET)–CT scan showed a small 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) avid focus above the resected pancreas and at the hepatic flexure of the ascending colon (Fig. 1: PET–CT scan dated 28 August 2014). The patient remained asymptomatic during that time, while baseline colonoscopy revealed normal findings and histopathology examination.

However, CA19-9 showed slowly increasing levels from 40 U/mL to 59 U/mL, leading to a repeat laparotomy in November 2014. Even though an omental tissue biopsy at the area of the hepatic flexure was normal, CA19-9 levels continued to increase to 108 U/mL, indicative of tumor recurrence and incomplete resection. Following this, the patient was treated with 25 fractions of radiotherapy to the peripancreatic lymph nodes. His CA19-9 subsequently returned to a normal level of 24.9 U/mL (0–37) at the end of radiotherapy in April 2015. Post radiotherapy, his blood CEA levels were used to guide TS-1 dose modification. Beginning April 2015, he was placed on maintenance chemotherapy TS-1 25 mg twice daily (BD) (2 weeks on 1 week off). Following 3-weekly monitoring with full blood count, renal profile, liver function tests, and tumor markers, the dose of TS-1 was titrated to 45 mg in the morning (OM) and 40 mg at night (ON) for 2 weeks, with 1 week rest before settling on a stable dose of 40 mg BD. A PET–CT reassessment in April 2016 (Fig. 1: PET–CT scan dated 27 April 2016) did not reveal any suspicious FDG-avid activity in the hepatic flexure and remaining pancreas, indicating treatment response to radiotherapy and TS-1.

The patient then continued maintenance therapy with TS-1 with blood test CA19-9 and CEA monitoring and PET–CT scan twice a year to assess the soft tissue focus at the pancreatic bed. Serial imaging from 2018 to 2023 (Fig. 2) of the pancreatic bed (site of recurrence) revealed similar metabolic activity compared with the earlier scans in 2014. The size of the focus remained similar in each serial scan performed, while CA19-9 remained within normal limits after TS-1 (Fig. 3). His blood CEA levels remained mildly elevated but stable and guided dose adjustment of TS-1 (Fig. 4). His full blood count and other organ function tests remained within normal limits, and no drug-induced adverse effects were reported. Currently, he is maintained on TS-1 40 mg twice daily (2 weeks on, 1 week off).

Discussion

In 2018, the Malaysian Study on Cancer Survival reported that pancreatic cancer has a low survival outcome compared with 15 common cancers in Malaysia, with an overall 5-year relative survival of 14% and a median survival time of 6.5 months [1]. The study used population-based data from the National Cancer Registry, involving patients diagnosed from 2007 until 2011 with follow-up to 2016. This report revealed that cancer survival estimates in Malaysia were comparable to other Asian countries, and the patterns were similar to other studies carried out for the same type of cancer. However, survival rates were lower compared with Australia, New Zealand, the USA, and most European countries. Our patient survived more than 9 years since he was first diagnosed with cancer, showing a significantly long-term survival compared with the survival expectancy in Malaysia.

Gemcitabine and 5-fluorouracil have been shown to be effective agents in the treatment of pancreatic cancer. Between the two agents, there was no significant difference in either progression-free survival or global quality-of-life scores [2]. The median survival was 23.6 months for patients treated with gemcitabine and 23.0 months for patients treated with fluorouracil plus folinic acid [2]. Although there were no significant differences between the two agents, the ESPAC-3 trial showed that gemcitabine did not result in improved overall survival compared with the use of fluorouracil plus folinic acid in patients with completely resected pancreatic cancer [2]. Following that, the ESPAC-4 trial comparing adjuvant gemcitabine and capecitabine with gemcitabine monotherapy proved that the use of combination chemotherapy was superior to a single-agent regime, with the median overall survival of 28.0 months for patients in the gemcitabine plus capecitabine group compared with 25.5 months in the gemcitabine group [3]. The estimated 5-year survival was 28.8% for patients randomized to gemcitabine plus capecitabine and 16.3% for patients randomized to gemcitabine [3], showing that a combination regimen favored longer survival. Our patient used a combination of gemcitabine with 5-fluorouracil in the initial treatment. While ESPAC-3 and ESPAC-4 provided important survival benchmarks for gemcitabine- and capecitabine-based adjuvant therapy in resected pancreatic cancer, they both excluded radiotherapy and did not evaluate TS-1, thus limiting their relevance to our case. However, our patient’s survival following recurrence and salvage radiotherapy and TS-1 maintenance far exceeded the survival benchmarks of both trails.

In the clinical practice guideline for pancreatic cancer published by the Japan Pancreas Society, TS-1 has been approved for use in the treatment of pancreatic cancer as first-line chemotherapy in the adjuvant and metastatic setting [4]. This was following a few key studies that compared the benefit of TS-1 with other chemotherapy agents. The GEST study concluded the noninferiority of TS-1 to gemcitabine with respect to overall survival with good tolerability and TS-1 as a convenient oral alternative for locally advanced and metastatic pancreatic cancer [5]. The median overall survival was 8.8 months in the gemcitabine group, 9.7 months in the TS-1 group, and 10.1 months in the gemcitabine plus TS-1 group [5]. Following that, the JASPAC-1 pancreas cancer adjuvant trial demonstrated higher overall 5-year survival of 44.1% in the TS-1 group and 24.4% in the gemcitabine group [6]. The median overall survival was 25.5 months with gemcitabine and 46.5 months with TS-1 [6]. Relapse was also less frequent in the TS-1 group [6]. Since the initiation of TS-1 maintenance, our patient tolerated the treatment well and did not suffer any relapse. Although the GEST and JASPAC-1 trials provided insight into TS-1 efficacy, they differ significantly from our case owing to exclusion of radiotherapy, differing intents of therapy, and absence of standardized post-recurrence management, making direct comparison on overall survival outcomes less applicable. However, the 9.9-year survival outcome of our patient significantly surpassed the expected survival benchmarks reported in these studies.

A key limitation of this case is the absence of molecular profiling on the original tumor specimen, which restricts interpretation of whether long-term survival and sensitivity to TS-1 may have been driven by favorable underlying tumor biology. In our case, despite absence of histological confirmation of tumor recurrence from second surgery, a clinically driven decision was made to initiate radiotherapy on the basis of rising CA19-9 levels, prior nodal positivity, and radiologic suspicion, reflecting high probability of a locoregional recurrence. The exceptionally long-term survival likely reflected the synergistic impact of multimodal therapy, including salvage radiotherapy and TS-1.

Conclusion

This case encourages more extensive clinical research to better understand the factors contributing to prolonged survival. Studying factors such as genetic predispositions, tumor biology, and patient-specific responses to treatment is important so that more effective and personalized treatment plans could be developed for better overall survival.
