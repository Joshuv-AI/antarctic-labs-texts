AI-assisted ultrasound for trauma: a scoping review
Authors: Mümin Murat Yazıcı, İsmail Ataş, Enes Hamdioğlu, Cengiz Kazdal, Ali Çelik, Özcan Yavaşi, Özlem Bilir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveArtificial intelligence (AI) has become a disruptive technology in medicine, with rapidly developing implications for education, diagnosis, image enhancement and assessment, prognosis, and treatment. The literature on AI-assisted ultrasound is still developing. Therefore, we aimed to investigate how AI is employed in ultrasound for trauma and how AI-assisted ultrasound could be deployed in clinical practice.Materials and methodsThe review was conducted according to JBI’s scoping review methodology. An appropriate search strategy was initially formulated for PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science (WoS), and Google Scholar (backward and forward citation tracking). The concepts of “artificial intelligence”, “ultrasound”, and “trauma” were searched across these databases using their respective Medical Subject Headings (MeSH). The core concepts were AI applications integrated with ultrasound in trauma. In this context, studies focusing on the following categories relating to the use of AI-assisted ultrasound in the field of trauma have been included in the review: (1) diagnostic accuracy, (2) differential diagnosis and confirmation of diagnosis, (3) quality and standardization, (4) operator support, and (5) training and modelling. Data extraction and analysis were performed using Microsoft Excel and were verified.ResultsTwenty-one studies were included. Targeted AI tasks primarily comprised diagnostic abnormality detection and segmentation/localization. Predominantly focused assessment with sonography for trauma (FAST), thoracic trauma evaluation, and extremity trauma. Although high apparent diagnostic accuracy was reported across development-stage retrospective cohorts, independent external validation was executed in only one study, with the remainder bounded by single-center data or synthetic phantoms. Crucially, none of the studies assessed subsequent patient outcomes such as mortality, time to intervention, or resource utilization.ConclusionThis scoping review confirms that while deep learning frameworks show promising retrospective diagnostic performance and image-quality automation, their translation into acute trauma care faces critical technical and clinical bottlenecks. Key current limitations include restricted model interpretability, missing regulatory pathways, a lack of clinician trust, inherent decision-making biases, poor algorithmic transparency, and the lack of patient-outcome tracking. To address these barriers, future studies should be designed with stronger methodological rigor, including prospective, multicenter, randomized controlled, and externally validated approaches.Trial registrationNo clinical trial number is available.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12880-026-02603-w.

Introduction

Trauma is a leading cause of death and disability worldwide, particularly among young adults. Rapid, accurate diagnosis can significantly improve survival rates. Computed tomography (CT) is considered the most accurate imaging method and is routinely recommended by leading medical organizations, including the American College of Radiology [1, 2]. However, ultrasound in trauma offers real-time imaging, bedside availability, and affordability, making it a valuable first-line tool, particularly for hemodynamically unstable patients [3, 4].

Artificial intelligence (AI) has become a disruptive technology in medicine, with rapidly evolving implications for education, diagnosis, image enhancement and assessment, prognosis, and treatment [5, 6]. AI enables AI-assisted ultrasound in trauma. AI-assisted ultrasound improves the speed and accuracy of Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma (FAST) and Extended Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma (E-FAST) interpretation during trauma evaluation, providing real-time image guidance that reduces operator dependence and enhances scan quality [7]. Portable AI-enabled systems support rapid triage in prehospital and disaster settings, enabling early identification of life-threatening conditions even in resource-limited environments. In addition, deep learning applications facilitate detection of organ-specific traumatic injuries [8], and AI platforms support training and standardized clinical decision support, promoting greater consistency and reliability in trauma ultrasound practice.

The literature on AI-assisted ultrasound is still developing. Therefore, we aimed to map and collate current AI applications in ultrasound for trauma using a reproducible systematic search strategy, to explore how AI is employed in ultrasound for trauma, and to examine how AI-assisted ultrasound for trauma could be deployed in clinical practice.

Methods

The scoping review was conducted in accordance with JBI’s scoping review methodology [9, 10] and reported in line with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis–Scoping Review extension (PRISMA-ScR).

Search strategy

The PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science (WoS) databases were used to identify peer-reviewed journal articles published between 1 January 2010 and 12 December 2025; articles identified via Google Scholar (using a backward and forward citation tracking strategy) were exported. An appropriate search strategy was initially formulated for PubMed, Scopus, WoS, and Google Scholar (backward and forward citation tracking). The concepts of “artificial intelligence”, “ultrasound”, “sonography”, and “trauma” were searched across these databases using their respective Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms. Synonyms for these concepts were searched for within keywords, titles, and abstracts. To ensure a comprehensive mapping of the entire trauma landscape without restricting the focus to standard abdominal protocols, broad root terms with wildcards (e.g., ultraso*, sonogra*) were utilized to inherently capture all point-of-care protocols (such as point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) and FAST) and specific traumatic pathologies (such as pneumothorax and fractures). Finally, all terms were combined using the “AND” operator. The detailed search strategy covers studies published up to 12 December 2025. The search strategy is detailed in Appendix S1.

Screening and selection criteria

Two independent reviewers (İA, EH) screened titles and abstracts according to the predefined eligibility criteria. Full-text articles of potentially eligible studies were then assessed in detail. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion, and a third reviewer (MMY) was consulted when necessary. Screening was performed manually, with no automated software used.

This review includes studies involving human trauma patients (adults or children), as well as technical validation studies using high-fidelity tissue phantoms or simulation models designed to mimic trauma scenarios. Studies focusing on non-trauma settings was excluded. Also, studies utilizing animal models (e.g., porcine or canine trauma frameworks) were excluded. This exclusion was applied because deep learning layers are highly sensitive to geometric organ contours and tissue acoustic impedance boundaries. To inclusion was limited to ‘human-centric’ diagnostic spaces, encompassing only real human clinical data or synthetic tissue phantoms and simulation frameworks explicitly engineered to mimic human anatomical dimensions and human operator kinematics. The core concepts evaluated were AI applications—including machine learning, deep learning, and computer vision models—integrated with ultrasound in trauma. In this context, studies focusing on the following categories relating to the use of AI-assisted ultrasound in the field of trauma have been included in the review: (1) diagnostic accuracy, (2) differential diagnosis and confirmation of diagnosis, (3) quality and standardization, (4) operator support, and (5) training and modelling. Also, Studies conducted in emergency departments, trauma centers, prehospital settings, intensive care units, or simulation-based laboratories focusing on acute trauma resuscitation, triage, or operator training were eligible. Studies outside this scope were excluded. Prospective, retrospective, observational, technical validation, and simulation studies published in peer-reviewed journals were included. Review articles, case reports, case series, letters to the editor, book chapters, and conference abstracts were excluded to maintain a high standard of peer-reviewed data. Studies without an English abstract or full text available were excluded due to translation limitations.

Data extraction and analysis

The data were independently extracted from the included studies by two reviewers (IA, EH). Data from each evidence source were entered into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. Findings were categorized and then quantified. Collected variables included title, first author, year, country, study design, study population, image/view, AI model, trauma category, and AI function category. Additionally, the advantages and limitations of the studies were categorized and documented. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus, and a third reviewer (MMY) was consulted when necessary.

After common concepts were categorized for easy identification and recall, the study within each category was presented according to its frequency.

Results

Search results

The initial search yielded 883 studies, of which 214 were excluded as duplicates. Title and abstract screening of the remaining 669 studies identified 56 potentially relevant studies. After full-text screening, 21 studies were included in the final review. Figure 1 illustrates the literature search and study selection process.

Study characteristics

Of the studies, thirteen were published in the United States [11–13, 15, 16, 19–23, 29–31], three in Canada [14, 26, 28], and two in China [24, 25]. In addition, one study each came from South Korea [17], Taiwan [18], and Austria [27]. Two studies were prospective, single-center designs [22, 28]; fourteen were retrospective, single-center designs [11–16, 18, 20, 21, 24–27, 29]; one was a retrospective, multicenter design [17]; and four were phantom validation-technical designs [19, 23, 30, 31]. The study population was adults in 12 studies [11–13, 16–18, 20–22, 24, 25, 29], simulated (motion or US) in 6 studies [14, 19, 23, 26, 30, 31], and children in three studies [15, 27, 28]. Although the FAST/E-FAST protocol was predominantly used in the studies (imaging/view), lung ultrasound, skull ultrasound, and wrist ultrasound were also employed. The AI models used in the studies have been evaluated. The studies utilized VGG-16 ResNet-50, DenseNet121, Inception v3, vgg11_bn, YOLOv3, CNN (time series), ResNet-152, YOLOv3 (Darknet-53), AutoML, ResNet50-V2, YOLOv8, Support Vector Machine, CNN (ShrapML), U-Net, Attention U-Net, DeepMind (I3D), EfficientNet (B0 to B7), YOLOv11, ResNet34, YOLOv7, SqueezeNet, and the other CNN models were used.

A cross-examination of the 21 included studies reveals a profound divergence in dataset dimensions and engineering configurations. Dataset volumes ranged from small clinical cohorts analyzed under restricted parameters—such as Leo et al. [13] (n = 94 cases) and Yıldız Potter et al. [16] (n = 76 cases) to expansive image repositories constructed by extracting sequential frames from stored video loops, notably Kornblith et al. [15] (1,062,612 frames) and Taye et al. [11] (∼525,000 frames). Crucially, the establishing mechanisms for reference standards demonstrated varying risks of verification bias. Technical validation tools mapping extremity ballistic data or thoracic configurations relied exclusively on pre-programmed phantom spatial dimensions or geometric physical layouts [19, 30, 31], restricting their current utility to controlled, pre-clinical proof-of-concept spaces. Within human clinical subsets, several workflow-automation or image-QA models utilized internal expert consensus loops or single-faculty ultrasound annotations to determine diagnostic pass/fail boundaries [11, 21]. In contrast, true pathology-driven diagnostic classifiers cross-referenced exploratory POCUS findings with objective external gold standards, incorporating definitive CT tracking, or formal echocardiography registries to secure true clinical ground truth verification [13, 16, 17, 24].

Advantages of AI-assisted ultrasound for trauma

The advantages of AI-assisted ultrasound for trauma have been categorized into distinct technical and clinical domains. Specifically, eleven studies reported satisfactory performance restricted to native test-set partitions [12, 13, 16–18, 20, 24, 25, 27–29], while nine focused on providing visual feedback via automated localization or segmentation masks [13, 16, 20, 24, 27–31]. Additionally, five studies standardization and workflow efficiency through quality automation or consistent interpretation [11, 12, 15, 21, 22]. Discussion

The aim of our study was to determine how AI is used in trauma ultrasound, how it is applied, and the advantages of AI in trauma ultrasound. This study is one of the first to compile and summarise the current clinical applications of AI in trauma ultrasound. AI performs and simplifies functions and tasks in trauma ultrasound, thereby facilitating the repeatability of partial, limited ultrasonographic scans in various point-of-care settings. Automated features such as abnormality detection, anatomical differentiation, and probe guidance reduce the learning curve and operator dependency, providing an advantage for minimally trained, novice ultrasound users. Once clinically disseminated and used by novices, AI-assisted ultrasound can accelerate clinical decision-making and care processes in trauma, reduce the burden on ultrasound users, and improve resource allocation. In our review, FAST/E-FAST applications were observed to feature prominently in studies [12, 13, 17, 18, 20, 22]. This may be because FAST/E-FAST applications are used in trauma ultrasound for rapid, bedside diagnosis and exclusion. Although most studies focus on diagnostic performance, areas of AI-assisted trauma ultrasound include improving image quality, education, and simulation-based concepts. In the following sections, we will discuss the advantages, challenges, and context of AI-assisted ultrasound in trauma.

Advantages of AI-assisted ultrasound for trauma

The most significant advantage of AI-assisted ultrasound was its satisfactory performance restricted to native test-set partitions [12, 13, 16–18, 20, 24, 25, 27–29]. Although this performance was primarily focused on abdominal evaluation, results for the lungs, heart, skull, and extremities were also noteworthy. These results are of sufficient quality to serve as a basis for subsequent research. Interpretability [13, 16, 20, 24, 27–31] and the standardization of image quality [11, 12, 15, 21, 22] also stand out as advantages. Other advantages include trainability [14, 15, 19, 22, 23, 26], real-time or practical adaptability [13, 16, 22, 25, 29], simulation-based development, and control mechanisms [19, 23, 26, 30, 31]. These advantages are fundamental to the creation of a training model in trauma ultrasound. Finally, AI-assisted ultrasound may be a good candidate for use as a triage tool in trauma [30, 31].

Diagnostic accuracy

Eleven studies have reported that AI-assisted ultrasound use in trauma has apparent diagnostic accuracy [12, 13, 16–18, 20, 24, 25, 27–29]. Levy et al. demonstrated that convolutional neural networks could identify FAST view adequacy and fluid positivity with up to 89.6% sensitivity and 100% specificity, indicating apparent performance even in heterogeneous clinical datasets [12]. Leo et al. showed that a YOLOv3-based deep learning model for right upper quadrant FAST imaging reached 95% sensitivity, 94% specificity, and an Area Under the Curve (AUC) of 0.97 for hemoperitoneum detection, supporting real-time bedside applicability [13]. Yıldız-Potter et al. employed a YOLO-based model for pericardial effusion detection, reporting 89% sensitivity and 92% specificity, supporting automated cardiac component analysis within E-FAST [16]. Jeong et al. validated a Google AutoML model across institutions and reported 94–95% sensitivity, 99% specificity, and an AUC of 0.97 for free fluid detection in Morrison’s pouch, highlighting strong external generalizability [17]. Cheng et al. utilized a ResNet-based model for Morison’s pouch free fluid detection and achieved sensitivities above 90% with AUC values exceeding 0.95, confirming high diagnostic accuracy in abdominal trauma [18]. Sjogren et al. reported that an automated machine-learning system for FAST examinations achieved 100% sensitivity and 90% specificity for detecting abdominal free fluid, demonstrating early feasibility of AI-based interpretation in trauma ultrasound [20]. Huang et al. developed an Attention U-Net for pleural effusion detection, achieving 97% sensitivity, 91% specificity, and a Dice coefficient of 0.86, showing apparent segmentation and classification in thoracic ultrasound [24]. Qiang et al. applied video-based temporal deep learning for pneumothorax diagnosis and reported 99.2% sensitivity, 89.2% specificity, and an AUC of 0.97, emphasizing the added value of spatiotemporal modeling [25]. Hankel et al. showed that AI-assisted pediatric skull ultrasound differentiation between fractures and sutures achieved an F1 score of 0.84 and PR-AUC of 0.91, outperforming unassisted human readers [27]. Knight et al. demonstrated that AI-based interpretation of pediatric distal radius ultrasound reached sensitivities of 0.91–1.00 and AUROC values above 0.90, comparable to expert radiologists, reinforcing the reliability of AI across musculoskeletal trauma imaging [28]. Finally, Montgomery et al. used limited-data neural networks for pneumothorax detection and achieved 86% sensitivity and 75% specificity, demonstrating feasibility even with small training datasets [29]. High diagnostic performance is noteworthy for the abdomen, lungs, heart, skull and extremities. This supports the use of AI-assisted ultrasound in trauma. The findings support the relationship between point-of-care ultrasound and AI in the literature [32–34].

While the baseline frequency tallies are overwhelmingly dominated by retrospective, in-distribution test-set configurations, the single prospective randomized controlled framework by Hartline et al. represents the unique and closest available signal of actual clinical translation within active trauma workflows [22]. Unlike the remaining body of literature that reports static, overoptimistic classifier metrics from pristine image banks, Hartline et al. subjected their real-time AI quality-guidance architecture to live handling by non-expert military medics. This prospective deployment revealed that while AI-assisted guidance significantly increases the baseline rate of adequate image acquisition, the actual downstream diagnostic interpretation accuracy did not experience an automatic, parallel surge. This critical divergence underscores that phantom-derived accuracy or retrospective partition bounds heavily overstate real-world bedside diagnostic value.

A granular analysis of the reported diagnostic accuracies reveals a critical translational pivot regarding data source selection. Across the 11 studies investigating diagnostic abnormality detection [12, 13, 17], the reported sensitivities (86%−100%) and specificities (75%−99%) were achieved almost exclusively using static images or curated video loops pre-acquired by fellowship-trained, expert POCUS faculty or dedicated ultrasound directors. This introduces a major contextual limitation to the technology’s clinical maturity. While these algorithms show highly satisfactory retrospective performance in classifying positive versus negative pathologies, they evaluate an artificial baseline where optimal acoustic windows and perfect anatomical orientations are already secured. In a real-world end-to-end trauma workflow (FAST/E-FAST), these applications are primarily intended to support non-trauma specialists, rotating residents, or prehospital medics who cannot reliably capture pristine views. Consequently, current diagnostic accuracy metrics fail to reflect the high rate of clinical artifacts, off-axis views, and acoustic dropouts generated by novice operators—representing an unaddressed bottleneck in the end-to-end diagnostic pipeline.

Image quality and standardization

Five studies reported that AI-assisted ultrasound use in trauma has apparent standardized image quality [11, 12, 15, 21, 22]. Taye et al. developed a deep learning autoencoder-based model for FAST quality assessment and reported 99% sensitivity and 98% specificity in distinguishing good- versus poor-quality FAST examinations, demonstrating that unsupervised feature compression is highly effective for automated ultrasound quality control [11]. Levy et al. evaluated convolutional neural networks for FAST view adequacy and showed that AI achieved 88.7% accuracy, 83.3% sensitivity, and 93.6% specificity for classifying adequate views, indicating reliable identification of diagnostically sufficient imaging frames [12]. Kornblith et al. focused on automated pediatric FAST view classification and demonstrated 97.8% accuracy for video clips and 93.4% accuracy for still frames, confirming that deep learning models can apparently recognize correct FAST views across heterogeneous real-world datasets [15]. Cull et al. proposed a standardized FAST image quality grading system and an AI-based assessment tool, achieving substantial human–AI agreement and enabling a fivefold reduction in expert review workload while preserving 85% correct pass/fail identification, highlighting the operational utility of AI in quality assurance workflows [21]. Finally, Hartline et al. conducted a prospective randomized trial in military medics and found that AI-assisted ultrasound significantly improved image adequacy in left upper quadrant and pelvic views (P = 0.008 and P = 0.004) and increased user confidence, demonstrating that AI support can enhance acquisition quality in minimally trained operators [22]. Collectively, these studies indicate that artificial intelligence–based systems can reliably and objectively assess ultrasound image quality and view adequacy with high accuracy, thereby addressing one of the major limitations of point-of-care ultrasound, namely operator dependency and variability in image acquisition.

Within the included literature, current models successfully execute isolated view checking but completely fail to provide dynamic multi-view integration. In an emergency trauma resuscitation, a standard FAST/E-FAST protocol demands sequential, synchronized logic across four distinct anatomical windows (abdominal, pelvic, pericardial, and thoracic). To date, zero papers in the surveyed literature examine an integrated pipeline that synthesizes real-time quality feedback with continuous spatial transducer-tracking guidance for novice users. Current quality automation remains locked in post-acquisition retrospective filtering, leaving the critical upstream phase of live, interactive operator guidance largely unexamined.

Other advantages

Mejia et al. developed a modular, full-torso E-FAST tissue phantom designed specifically for training and AI development, demonstrating that the model generates anatomically realistic ultrasound images and enables exposure to variable injury severities, thereby supporting both novice skill acquisition and AI-guided instructional workflows [19]. Hernandez-Torres et al. reported that an ultrasound tissue-mimicking E-FAST phantom successfully trained deep learning models with accuracies exceeding 97% across scan sites, highlighting its dual educational value for standardizing trainee image acquisition and facilitating automated feedback during ultrasound training [23]. Tyrrell et al. demonstrated that deep learning–based video analysis using modified I3D networks could automatically assess FAST operator skill level with an overall accuracy of 82.6%, indicating strong potential for objective, scalable, and expert-independent assessment of trainee competency during ultrasound education [26]. Furthermore, Holland et al. demonstrated that AI-based segmentation models (YOLOv7 and U-Net) can apparently detect foreign bodies in ultrasound images and generate objective distance-based triage metrics, enabling quantification of shrapnel proximity to critical neurovascular structures and thereby supporting injury severity stratification and evacuation prioritization in austere trauma settings [30]. Similarly, Snider et al. developed an object detection framework (YOLOv3) that achieved high detection performance and implemented a real-time proximity-based triage tool to automatically flag high-risk injuries based on shrapnel–neurovascular distance, highlighting the potential of AI-assisted ultrasound to enhance rapid clinical decision-making and resource allocation in emergency and military medicine [31]. These studies demonstrate the advantages of creating a training model for AI in trauma ultrasound. Consequently, current baseline data regarding automated fragmentation and foreign body localization demonstrate promising pre-clinical, phantom-stage feasibility. These algorithmic configurations cannot be framed as functional clinical triage tools at present, as prospective human validation tracking downstream patient outcomes within live, chaotic emergency resuscitations remains strictly required to transition these models from laboratory feasibility to actual bedside triaging.

Limitations and challenges of the AI-assisted ultrasound for trauma

There are numerous limitations to AI applications in healthcare, including AI-assisted ultrasound studies in trauma. Firstly, the most notable limitation in the studies was their retrospective design [11–21, 23–27, 29–31]. Secondly, almost none of the studies included external validation [11–16, 18–31]. Finally, ethical issues such as the fact that most AI models are still in their infancy, their limited interpretability, the lack of consolidated legal regulations, clinicians’ lack of confidence, the risk of bias in the decision-making process, and the lack of transparency are limitations that emerge in the studies [11, 17, 25, 32, 35, 36]. Due to these challenges, AI applications cannot be sufficiently implemented clinically despite their potential [36–38].

The most significant and clinically relevant structural shortcoming identified across all the data in this review is that none of the 21 included studies reported any data on patient outcomes. At present, the literature remains confined entirely to the technical aspects of diagnosis and isolation. None of the existing convolutional neural networks or object detection frameworks have been evaluated in terms of concrete clinical endpoints, such as a reduction in the time to surgery, the impact on survival/mortality rates, or emergency department resource utilisation metrics. This complete lack of prospective validation in humans represents a fundamental evidence gap in the field. This situation highlights that, although computer vision can reliably segment pixels in a laboratory setting, its operational value within the high-risk, time-critical workflows of live acute trauma resuscitation remains entirely unproven. This gap will guide future research.

Despite the high apparent performance metrics demonstrated across the reviewed literature, several profound clinical and structural challenges restrict the immediate translation of these AI models into routine trauma care. The most significant overarching limitation identified across the entire baseline is the completely retrospective or synthetic nature of 20 out of the 21 included studies. Crucially, 100% of the included studies failed to report any downstream patient-outcome evaluation. None of the current models have been assessed against operational endpoints such as reductions in time-to-intervention, impact on overall mortality/morbidity, resource utilization, or concrete clinical decision-making within the emergency department workflows. Furthermore, current models exhibit geographic and demographic constraints, with the vast majority of human data originating from single-center environments in high-resource quaternary settings. This presents a major operational hurdle for deployment in prehospital, military, or mass-casualty triage environments where image quality significantly degrades due to austere scanning conditions and operator exhaustion. Additionally, because these models primarily target static frames or localized regions (e.g., restricted Morison’s pouch views), integrating them into the fluid, multi-view sequential logic of the Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) pathway remains an open clinical challenge.

Furthermore, integrating these algorithms into standard ATLS pathways remains an unaddressed workflow. Current models primarily target static frames or isolated regions (e.g., restricted Morison’s pouch views), failing to adapt to the multi-view sequential logic required by trauma clinicians. This implementation challenge extends directly to prehospital care and triage settings. In austere or field environments, image quality significantly degrades due to tissue clutter, respiratory motion artifacts, and operator movement. Because current diagnostic and triage scoring networks have not been rigorously validated against these noisy, real-world clinical failure modes, their deployability by frontline paramedics or military medics remains limited to bench-stage feasibility.

Technical vulnerabilities and risk of bias in AI-assisted ultrasound for trauma

Beyond basic diagnostic metrics, the structural validation architecture within the reviewed literature exposes critical systemic vulnerabilities. Approximately 95% of current trauma-AI ultrasound studies utilize retrospective in-distribution test frameworks or internal partition loops (such as cross-validation or split-sample configurations drawn from a single device ecosystem or specific institutional database). Jeong et al. represents the only multi-center retrospective framework that successfully subjected its core algorithm to a true independent external validation cohort across separate institutions, confirming model robustness against dataset shift [17]. The near-total absence of independent external clinical testing across the remaining 20 papers signifies a high risk of overfitting to native machine properties and local operator techniques. Furthermore, because the vast majority of studies are bound by retrospective designs or synthetic benchmarks, current algorithms remain vulnerable to real-world clinical failure modes—such as rib-shadowing artifacts, acoustic dropouts, or swift patient motion—and are severely limited in their technical explainability, operating as definitive “black-box” systems.

Translational framework, regulatory pathways, and human factors engineering

Transitioning an AI algorithm, retrospective test partitions into live, chaotic trauma resuscitations requires addressing severe translational, regulatory, and cognitive challenges. Chief among the technical hurdles is dataset, which severely limits algorithmic robustness. An static image repositories acquired experiences a collapse in performance when deployed on ultra-portable, handheld transducers in austere or prehospital trauma settings. This vulnerability is compounded by POCUS. Unlike static imaging modalities like CT, ultrasound is highly vulnerable to real-time artifacts; severe rib shadowing, respiratory motion blur, subcutaneous emphysema, and acoustic dropouts act as adversarial noise that can easily confuse deep neural network layers, leading to false-negative or anomalous outputs. These technical vulnerabilities directly influence the regulatory pathways required for safe clinical translation. The vast majority of diagnostic AI-ultrasound in trauma frameworks covered in current research operate strictly within pre-clinical or internal proof-of-concept spaces, relying on standard in-distribution splits. This must generalizability. Finally, the integration of automated guidance into acute trauma resuscitations introduces critical human factors that remain completely unaddressed in current validation models. Novice clinicians or military medics relying on real-time AI guidance are highly susceptible to automation bias—the tendency to over-rely on automated decision-making prompts. In a fast-paced trauma scenario, if an algorithm erroneously flags a view as negative for free fluid due to an acoustic dropout, a less-experienced operator may experience automation complacency, accepting the false-negative output and delaying life-saving surgical intervention or definitive CT imaging. Designing apparent human-in-the-loop interfaces that present AI outputs purely as probabilistic decision-support tokens rather than absolute diagnostic truths is an essential milestone for future human factors engineering in ED.

Next steps for implementation and research

Future research should focus on the use of AI-assisted ultrasound in trauma settings beyond FAST/E-FAST. This will enable better assessment of diagnostic value outside of FAST/E-FAST. Again, focusing solely on diagnostic accuracy may misrepresent the clinical benefit of a diagnostic test. A test may be less apparent than the current gold standard, but it may have distinct advantages such as low cost, accessibility, and low invasiveness. Therefore, future research should focus on evaluating the clinical benefit of AI-assisted ultrasound in trauma. AI-assisted ultrasound studies in trauma are also limited in the paediatric age group. To overcome this limitation, more studies should be planned in the paediatric trauma group. AI model developers warn researchers about bias, risks, and applicability issues, which cause bias in the clinical use of AI models [39, 40]. To overcome these biases, methodologically apparent studies (prospective, multicenter, randomized controlled, externally validated, etc.) should be planned. Finally, in trauma, it is essential to focus not only on diagnostic tests but also on numerous other areas, such as education and standardization.

Expanding the horizon: AI-assisted ultrasound in trauma

Whilst much of the literature on AI-assisted ultrasound in trauma is limited to diagnostic tasks such as free fluid scoring or effusion segmentation, an emerging new paradigm is shifting the clinical focus towards automated interventional guidance and acute pain management. In severe trauma resuscitation, rapid peripheral and central vascular access is one of the key determinants of survival; however, this process is often complicated by severe haemorrhagic shock, vascular collapse or serious soft tissue injuries. Recent advances in surgical data science have successfully integrated deep learning-based computer vision with robotic manipulation to enable autonomous vascular access. It has demonstrated the clinical feasibility of using real-time ultrasound models to guide handheld robotic devices for femoral and peripheral venous catheterisation [41, 42]. Furthermore, by dynamically tracking neurovascular targets despite severe physiological instability and vascular collapse, it has successfully validated these automated vascular cannulation frameworks [43].

In addition to vascular interventions, AI-assisted “interventional procedures” also form part of the acute trauma workflow. In this context, acute pain management in cases of complex limb fractures or chest wall injuries can be optimised through peripheral nerve blocks using AI-assisted interventional ultrasound. Evidence suggests that interventional artificial intelligence can safely compensate for a lack of expertise among frontline clinicians. Many platforms developing artificial intelligence technologies are seeking to demonstrate the safety and generalisability of ‘interventional guidance’ systems in multicentre prospective clinical trials. This evidence will shed light on future developments in the field by taking AI-assisted ultrasound research in trauma beyond standard diagnostic frameworks and directing such research towards integrated robotic and interventional ultrasound networks.

Strengths and limitations of this review

This scope review possesses multiple strengths. Firstly, the literature review and data extraction were conducted methodologically, enabling the quantification of common trends in AI-assisted ultrasound research in trauma. Furthermore, including simulation-based studies in the inclusion criteria ensured that AI-assisted ultrasound was not limited to clinical use. Finally, the establishment of no age limit in the studies has ensured that both adult and pediatric age groups are included.

Our study has several limitations. Firstly, a distinct structural limitation of this scoping review is the deliberate exclusion of high-fidelity animal trauma models (such as porcine shock frameworks). While algorithm testing typically transitions sequentially from synthetic tissue phantoms to animal models before human bedside deployment, our selection criteria restricted the search space exclusively to models utilizing human data or phantoms explicitly cast from human anatomical silhouettes. Secondly, we did not distinguish between different types of AI, including machine learning and deep learning. Therefore, all literature containing the term ‘AI’ was included in the study. Thirdly, we only included English articles, which may have contributed to selection bias as we may have overlooked articles published in other countries and languages. Fourthly, our inclusion of simulation-based studies led us to report on studies that used phantom or experimental models instead of real patient data. This may have created limitations in terms of realism. Fifthly, transparency in model development and reporting was limited in many studies (e.g., pre-processing steps, hyperparameter tuning), which hinders reproducibility and increases the risk of bias. Finally, we acknowledge that results may have been excluded based on our inclusion criteria and that we may have overlooked studies that explicitly reported patient outcomes.

Conclusion

This scoping review maps the evolving landscape of AI integrated with trauma-related US, presenting evidence that deep learning frameworks possess promising apparent diagnostic performance, view classification accuracy, and image-quality automation capabilities within controlled, exploratory, and retrospective domains. However, the current translation of these algorithms into routine acute trauma care remains severely restricted by critical technical and clinical bottlenecks. Also, the limited interpretability of AI models, the lack of legal regulations, clinicians’ lack of trust, the risk of bias in decision-making, the lack of transparency, and the lack of patient-outcome tracking are the main limitations of AI in research. Leaving the actual clinical utility of these tools unproven. To overcome these limitations, methodologically studies (prospective, multicenter, randomized controlled, externally validated, etc.) should be planned for the future.
