Long-term history of continental weathering and particle transport to the sea
Authors: Don E. Canfield, Shuichang Zhang, Ross N. Mitchell, Xiaomei Wang, Amin Naemi, Christopher J. Spencer, Pengyaun Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceContinental weathering delivers particles from the land to the sea. Released elements enrich ecosystems, while particles bury elements and organic matter, regulating ocean chemistry and atmospheric oxygen content. Here, we explore the long-term evolution of continental weathering, finding that weathering dynamics is defined by two main episodes. From about 2000 to 650 Mya, weathering was controlled by physical erosion with transport of particles from land to sea in braided rivers. After this, deep-subduction plate tectonics emerged, and weathering became controlled by tectono–mantle geodynamic processes such as mountain building. Our results show how the weathering process has been impacted by evolving tectonics, with implications for the evolution of elemental cycling over geologic time.

Weathering occurs on land by both physical and chemical processes (e.g., ref. 1). Physical weathering occurs as processes such as wind abrasion, glacial plucking, temperature changes, and freeze/thaw cycles break rocks into smaller fragments with little chemical alteration of the rock matrix. Chemical weathering occurs as fluids (e.g., rainwater, groundwater with associated carbonic and organic acids) interact with rocks. As rocks break down from chemical weathering, solutes are liberated along with solid products. Solid products include clays that form from minerals susceptible to chemical alteration like feldspars, amphiboles, and biotite (2, 3). Also liberated from the rock matrix with minimal alteration are refractory minerals like quartz, zircon, and spinels (4). Chemical weathering consumes atmospheric CO2 (e.g., ref. 1), and solutes liberated during chemical weathering flow from land to the sea enriching both terrestrial and marine ecosystems. The particles liberated during physical weathering become transported, mostly by rivers and streams, to deposit as floodplain and alluvial deposits on land and as sediments in the ocean (5, 6). Particle deposition in the oceans can impact rates of organic carbon burial and hence oxygen liberation to the atmosphere (7), while also influencing the marine mass balance of particle-associated elements, including nutrients (N, P), and many trace metals whose removal rate may depend on rates of sediment deposition (e.g., ref. 8).

The balance between physical and chemical weathering, as well as the fate of particles after erosion from soils, can be addressed in modern river systems through the analysis and comparison of solute and particle chemistry and fluxes (e.g., ref. 9). Indeed elevated rates of chemical weathering have been linked to higher surface temperature (10–12) and higher runoff rate (13), while the balance between physical and chemical erosion is often linked to elevation and topographic slope (5, 14), where steeper topography promotes proportionally higher rates of physical erosion.

Eroded particles, however, do not necessarily make it to the sea. Indeed, after erosion, particle transport downstream is often sluggish, with considerable retention of particles in river drainage basins on time scales from years to millennia to even longer (15). Indeed, recent long-term continental particle storage may be substantial, with estimates that some 30 to 60% of the pre-Anthropogenic Holocene erosion flux is deposited on the continents (16, 17). In addition, continents may have been an important sink for eroded particles (via depositional storage) at other times during the Phanerozoic Eon (18, 19), as well as during the Precambrian (20).

Many studies have addressed the long-term history of chemical weathering, with no real consensus on this history. For example, compilations of the chemical index of alteration (CIA) (21–23) show little directional change over the last 3 billion years, suggesting a steady baseline of chemical weathering intensity through Earth history. In contrast, the mineralogy of shales from 775 to 530 Mya shows a consistent increase in clay production (24), indicating an increased chemical weathering intensity. This increase was linked to an increase in the abundance of biotic soils, although the observations themselves cover a relatively short period of time. In another approach, the Rb/Al ratio has been compiled in mudrocks over geologic time (11) showing an apparent decrease in values sometime around 500 to 700 Mya. This decrease was interpreted as an increase in Rb loss due to intensified chemical weathering, although the temporal sampling resolution of this record is rather low.

Overall, we view the history of chemical and physical weathering through time as uncertain, as well as the history of particle transport to the sea. There is, however, an emerging consensus that episodicity, or even cyclicity, in tectono–mantle geodynamics may control important Earth-surface processes such as mountain building, sedimentation, subduction rate, and sea level. For example, the ~500 to 800 My cycles associated with supercontinent assembly and breakup, achieved by multiple shorter ~200 to 350 My Wilson cycles of ocean closing and opening, have long been associated with changes in sea level, sedimentation, and sediment recycling (e.g., refs. 25–27). In addition to Wilson cycles and supercontinent cycles, numerous records of crust–mantle interaction, such as zircon abundance, zircon εHf(t), large igneous provenance ages, and the relative abundance of igneous rocks, show cyclic oscillations with periodicities of ~95 and ~185 My (28). The exact cause(s) of these harmonics are still uncertain, but they are broadly linked to mantle dynamics and crust–mantle interactions including the periodicity of arc magmatism overlain by step-wise changes in plate tectonic style (e.g., ref. 29). Changes in plate tectonic styles include major developments such as the switch from potentially single-lid tectonics to subduction tectonics and the further development of modern-like deep subduction (29–31). Indeed, periodicities in tectono–magmatic drivers like plate speed and subduction rate could be related to the evolution of the tectono–mantle geodynamic system as it impacts the thermochemical structure and circulation dynamics of the mantle (32). Such periodicities in tectono–mantle geodynamics, with their expression in Earth-surface processes like mountain building and sea level, could impact the history of weathering and particle transport.

Our study will address the long-term history of weathering including both the balance of physical/chemical processes during the breakdown of surficial materials from weathering and the long-term history of particle transport from the land to the sea. We will highlight episodes of enhanced particle storage on land as well as periods of enhanced particle erosion. We will explore the potential periodicities of these processes and attempt to link them with periodicity in crust–mantle interactions and the evolution of plate tectonic style. Overall, we find a rich history in the evolution of continental weathering and particle transport dynamics over time, with a strong link to the evolution of plate tectonics and to mantle dynamics.

The Database.

We leverage the Sedimentary Geochemistry and Paleoenvironments (SGP) database (33), supplemented with ~2,000 new analyses, mostly from our own samples. The SGP database includes data from a variety of sources including contributions from individual scientists, literature data, as well as large databases from the United States Geological Survey National Geochemical Database (USGS-NGDB) and the United States Geological Survey Global Geochemical Database for Critical Metals in Black Shales (USGS-CMIBS). The USGS databases have not been curated for deposition environment. Therefore, we first filtered the SGP data to include samples with analyses of both Zr and Al; most of these samples also had K, Rb, and Na data, elements also explored in the present study. After this, the depositional environment for each remaining formation was determined using literature and internet sources with the provision that each formation contain at least three samples. Environments were categorized as terrestrial (lake, fluvial, alluvial, aeolian), marine (all subaqueous marine sediments), transitional (environments at the continental margin that fluctuate between marine and terrestrial sedimentation depending on changes in relative sea level), and other environments (typically heavily volcanic, containing only small intervals of marine sedimentation).

All the formations surveyed, including their depositional characterization, as well as interpreted or average age are presented in Dataset S1. Of all the SGP data surveyed, 59% were assigned to marine sedimentary environments, 28% to terrestrial environments, 11% to transitional environments, and 2% to other environments. In what follows, we consider only results from formations determined to be subaqueous marine. Of these, there is a strong North American bias to the data (Fig. 1), largely because of the extensive USGS databases used in the compilation. Other recent data compilations expand on the SGP and USGS databases (34), but this database was not analyzed in the current contribution.

Discussion

Background.

Our results will be discussed in the context of the evolving Earth system, as this evolution likely exercised critical control on the dynamics of continental weathering and particle transport to the sea. Our analysis begins at 2000 Mya, and there is broad, although not universal (e.g., refs. 35 and 36), agreement that by this time, plate tectonics involved the convergence and subduction of ocean lithosphere promoting the assembly and breakup of supercontinents (e.g., refs. 37–41). The origin of plate tectonics should have impacted weathering through the construction of mountain belts, through the assembly and rifting of large landmasses with internal drainage, through sea-level change, and through the migration of continental mass into and out of zones of climate-induced enhanced weathering [additionally affected by continental latitude changes due to true polar wander; (42)]. However, “modern”-style plate tectonics with deep subduction may have first emerged in the late Neoproterozoic Era (30, 38). With this development, subduction rates likely increased (43), with episodes of mountain building more vigorous and more frequent (29, 44).

In addition, the severe “Snowball Earth” glaciations of the Neoproterozoic Cryogenian Period may have significantly impacted the weathering environment through subglacial weathering, the grinding of rock into fine glacial flower and the massive delivery of particles to the ocean during periods of glacial melt (as discussed below).

We also highlight the later evolution of rooted land plants that acted to stabilize soils. This stabilization altered river dynamics while also introducing weathering acids into the soil environment (45–47). Of particular importance to river dynamics is the stabilization of soils and riverbanks leading to a transition from predominantly braided rivers before the spread of rooted land plants, to meandering rivers afterward (20, 45, 46, 48). Each of these consequences of rooted plant evolution could have had a substantial impact on chemical weathering and particle transport to the sea.

Finally, we highlight continental breakup and assembly as a potential indicator of tectonic activity and long-term sea level. We do this through the compilation of continental fragment counts over time (49, 50), where a higher number of continental fragments represents more disassembled continents (Fig. 3). Long-Term Trends.

In what follows, we will use the records of Zr/Al, Rb/Al, and Na/Al to explore long-term trends in chemical weathering, the balance between physical and chemical weathering during erosion, and the transport of particles from continents to the ocean.

Chemical weathering.

We begin with the record of chemical weathering. As noted in the Introduction, previous studies have explored long-term trends in the CIA, finding relatively stable average values of CIA through time (21–23). While CIA is sensitive to temperature (51) and can certainly respond to climate change (52, 53), these studies suggest little long-term evolution in the intensity of weathering through time. These conclusions based on CIA seem to contrast with other studies suggesting that chemical weathering was less efficient during the Proterozoic Eon when compared to the Phanerozoic. One such study, as noted above, shows an increase in clay formation, indicating increased chemical weathering rates, through the end of the Proterozoic Eon (24). However, as also noted, this study is not a long-term analysis, as it only explores a relatively short time interval of about 250 My.

In another study, also mentioned above, the Rb/Al of the clay fraction of sediments was developed as an indicator of weathering intensity. In this study, Bayon et al. (11) found that the Rb/Al of modern river sediments responded to environmental temperature, where higher temperatures led to more Rb loss and thus lower Rb/Al values. Rb is found substituted for K (and thus can be viewed as a surrogate for K) in a variety of sheet silicates and in potassium feldspar. These minerals are relatively resistant to weathering (e.g., refs. 54 and 55), where K (and likewise Rb) is the least mobile during weathering of the soluble major cations (Na, Ca, Mg, K) during weathering (13, 56, 57).

These insights were applied to a record of Rb/Al in ancient shales, showing a decrease in Rb/Al starting sometime near the late Neoproterozoic Era (11). This decrease in Rb/Al was taken to indicate an increase in chemical weathering intensity at this time. The study of Bayon et al. (11), however, has a rather low data density, so we also explore Rb/Al over time with our expanded data compilation.

We recognize first, however, that Rb can be added to sediments through late diagenetic processes including the transformation of smectite to illite (e.g., ref. 58). Indeed, in a study of shales ranging from Ediacaran to Miocene in age, Środoń et al. (58) found that, typically, 30 to 50% of the Rb in the shales was bound in 2:1 clays (illite and smectite). In these shales, K/Ar dating clearly indicated postdepositional K addition, and by extension, Rb addition to the shales. It was not possible to quantify the magnitude of postdepositional addition of Rb to the samples of this study, but it was likely of importance, as has long been known for K (e.g., ref. 14).

Thus, in using Rb/Al as a long-term indicator of weathering intensity, we must consider the possible impact of postdeposition alteration on Rb concentration. We cannot, unfortunately, quantify postdepositional addition of Rb to the samples in our database, but Rb/Al might still be a useful proxy for ancient weathering intensity. For one, if postdepositional alteration impacted all ages similarly, then its impact over time would average out. Counter to this idea, Garrels and Mackenzie (14) found a significant increase in the smectite/illite ratio moving forward in time from ca. 300 Mya, suggesting that the smectite to illite transition took hundreds of millions of years to complete. However, in another compilation of shale analysis, the smectite to illite transition was, on average, complete in only 100 My (59). Furthermore, and focusing on Rb, Środoń et al. (58) found no discernable difference in the Rb content of 2:1 clays when comparing rocks younger than the late Triassic Period to those of the Ediacaran Period. Overall, the time dependence of Rb incorporation into sediments and sedimentary rocks is still unclear, but it may not be of great concern in the interpretation of our Rb/Al results. We also note that our compilation includes rock types ranging from shales to sandstones, and the coarser-grained rocks should be less impacted by postdepositional Rb addition. So, we cautiously analyze the history of Rb/Al as an indicator of weathering intensity, recognizing potential pitfalls with this analysis.

Our database for Rb/Al (Fig. 2B) is consistent with Bayon et al. (11) showing relatively high Rb/Al through most of the Proterozoic Eon. After this, there are swings in the ratio (discussed below), but a monotonic drop in the ratio begins around 300 to 400 Mya. Thus, our higher-resolution data show that the long-term decrease in Rb/Al occurred much later than suggested by Bayon et al. (11). Overall, the apparent increase in chemical weathering around 300 to 400 Mya is timed with the evolution and expansion of rooted plants (Fig. 3) (47). As noted above, land plants likely impacted chemical weathering intensity through the stabilization of soils and the release of weathering acids through their root systems (60). We cautiously favor this argument over a decrease in Rb/Al related to incomplete smectite to illite transition as discussed above.

The other major soluble elements like Na, Ca, and Mg are typically found in more easily weatherable minerals like olivine, pyroxene, amphiboles, and plagioclase feldspars. We focus on the evolution of Na/Al, as Ca and Mg can be added to marine sediments (and thus sedimentary rocks) by carbonate formation, compromising any primary information about weathering intensity. Thus, the Na/Al record (Fig. 2C) shows extensive Na loss, with Na/Al well below crustal average (CA) values already at 2000 Mya, meaning that the minerals containing Na were highly chemically weathered by this time. Therefore, unlike the more resistant K-bearing minerals, there was extensive weathering of the more easily weatherable minerals already at 2000 Mya.

There is a rather curious increase in the Na/Al ratio roughly coincident with the decrease in Rb/Al around 300 Mya (Fig. 2 B and C), trending to very high average values approaching, and even exceeding, the CA of 0.4 in the youngest rocks. The highest Na/Al ratios in the youngest rocks are much greater than found in river suspended matter (0.05) or river bottom sediment (0.14) (17), so these high values do not likely reflect weathering products. We suggest they reflect trapped sediment pore water in immature samples. The earlier increase in Na/Al starting around 300 Mya, however, likely reflects real rock values. Indeed, this increase is counterintuitive as one would not expect Na/Al to increase in response to an increase in weathering intensity as revealed through the Rb/Al ratio. One possible explanation for this rise in the Na/Al ratio would be the increasingly significant late diagenetic conversion of Na-bearing smectite to illite moving back in time (14), with the subsequent release of Na to solution. As noted above, however, this conversion may be nearly complete, on average, over a time span of 100 My, and thus of too short a time scale to account for our Na/Al trends. Another possibility is enhanced trapping of clays in meandering rivers that developed after the evolution and spread of land plants (61). As noted above, the Na/Al of the suspended river load, comprising fine particles, is much lower than the Na/Al of coarser-grained bottom sediment (17). Therefore, the accelerated trapping of this fine-grained Na-depleted sediment in alluvial systems may have allowed the transport of more Na-enriched coarser sediment to the oceans. The trend of increasing Na/Al at around 300 Mya may also be part of natural variability in particle delivery to the oceans as explored below.

As our database is heavily weighted toward N America (Fig. 1), we attempted a declustering analysis to test whether broader regional trends are consistent with those observed in our raw dataset. We applied LOcally Estimated Scatterplot Smoothing (LOESS) to both the full dataset and to spatially declustered subsets, using three spatial windows (5°, 10°, and 20°) which correspond to data occupying approximately 5%, 10%, and 20% of grid cells on the planet’s surface, or about 18%, 35%, and 70% of the land-area grids. LOESS trends of the raw data (SI Appendix, Fig. S5) resemble the running averages (Fig. 2). Declustered trends, especially at the 5° scale (which retains the most data), closely resemble the raw data trends for both Rb/Al and Na/Al. This suggests that, despite a North America bias, our observed geochemical trends likely reflect broader regional signals.

Particle delivery and storage.

General principles.

We have hinted at aspects of continental particle storage above, but we will now leverage our Zr/Al compilation to more fully explore the dynamics of continental particle storage and particle delivery to the oceans. To do so, we follow an interpretive framework derived from a previous study of the Zr/Al dynamics in modern rivers, soils, and marine sediments (17). In this study, Canfield and Naemi (17) explored the chemistry of river particulates (suspended and bottom sediments), surface soils, and marine sediments to i) look for chemical signals that best differentiate between river suspended and bottom sediments, ii) to use these signals to determine the main source of particles to the modern marine sediments, and iii) to quantify the storage of weathered particles on land during the pre-Anthropogenic Holocene. Of all the elements analyzed, Zr/Al proved the best at differentiating between river suspended and bottom sediments (including continental floodplain deposits), while being free of diagenetic effects that could impact the concentration of many elements in marine sediments (reproduced in SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Thus, the Zr/Al ratio of marine sediments records the continental particle source (river suspended or bottom sediment) to the sediment.

In compiling these ratios, Canfield and Naemi (17) found that the frequency distribution of Zr/Al for river suspended matter generally peaked well below the CA Zr/Al (SI Appendix, Fig. S3), although rivers draining high relief areas with rapid physical erosion, such as in Taiwan for example, had suspended sediment Zr/Al ratios closer to the CA value. Suspended material transported by rivers is of smaller grain size and of lower density than river bottom sediment (17). Thus, histograms of river bottom sediment and continental floodplain deposits have Zr/Al peaking well above the CA (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). This is mainly because they accumulate dense zircons, that are the primary carrier of Zr. Modern marine sediments have a similar Zr/Al frequency distribution to river suspended sediment showing that during the pre-Anthropogenic Holocene, river suspended matter dominated the source of particles to ocean sediments. Canfield and Naemi (17) also calculated that about ~50% of the particles liberated from weathering were stored on land, before the added particle storage from dam construction.

From these observations of Zr/Al in modern river particulates, soils, and marine sediments, we generate basic principles (Fig. 4) from which we interpret the ancient record of Zr/Al, as recorded in sedimentary rocks. We propose that the Zr/Al values will likely depend on three dominant factors: i) When physical weathering dominates, the original rock remains more intact, with rock fragments likely transporting a Zr/Al ratio closer to the original rock value. As the rock becomes more heavily dissolved and disaggregated, zircons are liberated from the rock matrix and these zircons can become sorted based on size, density, and flow rates, allowing for a wider range in Zr/Al in both suspended sediment and bottom sediment as these particles are transported from land to the sea. ii) even if chemical weathering was extensive, high rates of erosion could efficiently transport all particles to the sea, potentially reducing the spread in Zr/Al in marine sediments. iii) terrestrial deposits accumulate particles with Zr/Al ratios above the CA. Thus, the storage of particles on land skews marine sediments to lower Zr/Al ratios. Provided our database represents marine sediments in general, periods when Zr/Al is below the CA should represent periods of net terrestrial particle accumulation on the continents, while periods of high Zr/Al represent periods of net particle release from the continents to the oceans, including previously stored terrestrial deposits.

Main principles outlining our interpretation of the Zr/Al ratio in sediments over geologic time. Both high ratios of physical to chemical weathering and high erosion rates will favor smaller spreads in the Zr/Al ratio in marine sediments. In addition, continents store particles with relatively high Zr/Al ratios, so continental storage of particles will tend to deliver particles with low Zr/Al to the oceans. The net erosion of particles previously stored on the continents will tend to deliver particles with high Zr/Al to marine sediments. See main text for details.

We also conducted a declustered data analysis for the Zr/Al database. As before, the LOESS on the raw data (SI Appendix, Fig. S5) resembles the running average (Fig. 2). The declustered data results for the 20° and 10° analyses diverge noticeably from the raw data. This is perhaps not surprising as the declustered database is small, and regional differences in particle sorting and transport dynamics, as they control Zr/Al, are expected to be large. However, the 5° analysis preserves the small Proterozoic range in values and the more dynamic Zr/Al changes in the Phanerozoic. Also preserved are some of the Phanerozoic peaks and troughs in Zr/Al, especially from ca. 500 Mya onward. These trends are discussed in more detail below. Overall, the declustered data analysis reveals some similarities in regional trends in Zr/Al but also differences. As with Rb/Al and Na/Al, the regional coverage of the database is relatively small, and a more robust declustered data result would emerge from a more complete global dataset.

Pre-cryogenian.

Long-term trends in Zr/Al display a relatively low spread in values through most of the Proterozoic Eon with an apparent increase in spread around 720 to 630 Mya (Fig. 2A). The reduced spread is seen in a reduced density of both high and low Zr/Al values, as also seen in histograms showing the frequency distribution of Zr/Al in specific time windows (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). In these frequency distributions, especially for the time windows 630 to 720 Mya and 1100 to 1600 Mya, the range of values is relatively narrow and peak in Zr/Al is near the CA value of 24. In the context of the interpretive framework presented above, the relatively narrow range in Zr/Al values, and the tendency for these values to cluster near the CA, would indicate either a relatively high ratio of physical to chemical weathering during much of this time and/or a high rate of physical erosion and efficient transport of particles to the ocean.

There are several factors unique to the Proterozoic Eon that might have contributed to both high degrees of physical weathering and efficient particle transport to the sea. As noted above, through this time, braided rivers dominated drainage from the land to the sea (20, 45, 46, 48). Meandering rivers, with their more complex flow path and a greater ability to trap particles, first came to dominance in the Devonian Period with the stabilization of soils accompanying the evolution and expansion of rooted land plants (Fig. 3) (e.g., refs. 45 and 46). Thus, with less stable soils and rapid flow, the dominance of braided rivers might have led to both relatively high rates of physical erosion and relatively efficient transfer of particles to the sea with limited particle storage on land.

Another possibility, also as described above, is that chemical weathering was less efficient during the Proterozoic Eon. Indeed, the record of Rb/Al is compatible with less efficient weathering of the more resistant K-bearing mineral phases during this time (Fig. 2B). Still, as also noted above, the Na/Al record suggests that the weathering of the more easily weatherable phases, including many important rock-forming minerals, was likely quite efficient through the Proterozoic Eon back to at least 2000 Mya. Therefore, the extent to which low chemical weathering intensity contributed to a less efficient breakdown of rocks in the Proterozoic weathering environment is uncertain. Finally, Rb/Al evidence does not support overall enhanced chemical weathering of the more resistant K-containing minerals until ca. 400 Mya. Taken together, high rates of physical erosion and efficient particle transport due to river geomorphology likely contributed to the relatively low spread in Zr/Al values during much of the Proterozoic Eon. Reduced efficiency of chemical weathering may have contributed as well, but this is less certain. As chemical weathering efficiency and the development of meandering rivers did not occur until ca. 400 Mya with the evolution and expansion of rooted land plants, the change in Zr/Al dynamics at ca. 600 to 700 Mya is still unresolved and will be considered in more detail below.

Cryogenian period.

The change to a larger spread in Zr/Al values begins during/after the Cryogenian Period (Fig. 2A). During the severe Snowball Earth glaciations, moving glaciers would have ground bedrock into fine particles, allowing for particle sorting during transport, potentially yielding a larger spread in Zr/Al values. Indeed, this type of particle sorting is seen in the transition from the Sturtian-aged Sturt Tillite to the overlying Tapley Hill Formation from South Australia (SI Appendix, Fig. S4), where high Zr/Al ratios are observed in the coarse-grained tillite representing an interval of glacial discharge. The complementary fine-grained low Zr/Al particles that would have also discharged during glacial melting are not apparent in these units and likely deposited in contemporaneous low-energy environments not sampled in this stratigraphy.

In addition, a main staple of the Snowball Earth hypothesis (62, 63) is more efficient chemical weathering due to high atmospheric CO2 levels at the termination of Cryogenian glacial episodes. Enhanced chemical weathering efficiency could have accelerated the breakdown of continental rocks, allowing for greater particle separation during transport, as argued above for the physical breakdown of rocks by glaciers. Indeed, various types of isotope evidence indicate an elevated efficiency of chemical weathering at the end of both the Sturtian (658.8 Mya) (64) and Marinoan (ca. 635 Mya) (65) glaciations. However, these episodes of elevated chemical weathering efficiency are relatively short-lived (a few million years at most). Furthermore, Rb/Al ratios do not provide evidence for enhanced chemical weathering efficiency during the Cryogenian Period (Fig. 2B). Therefore, with available data, both the physical and chemical consequences of glaciation on particle weathering and transport were relatively short-lived. If true, the apparent post-Cryogenian increase in Zr/Al, as controlled by the dynamics of continental weathering and particle transport, was not directly a result of Snowball Earth conditions.

Post-cryogenian.

In post-Cryogenian times, Zr/Al behavior emerges with much greater swings in values than before (Fig. 2). This new type of Zr/Al behavior continues through the Phanerozoic Eon (as discussed in more detail below) and thus represents a new style of weathering/particle transport dynamics. There are two major geological events associated with the start of this new Zr/Al behavior. One is the “The Great Unconformity,” where, focusing on Laurentia, there was an apparent loss of 3 to 5 km of surficial rocks from ca. 700 to 500 Mya (66). Another is the initiation modern deep-subduction plate tectonics, as evidenced by the history of high pressure (HP) and ultra-HP metamorphic rocks. These rocks are associated with deep subduction and are mainly found in the Phanerozoic Eon, while mostly absent from older rocks (e.g., refs. 30, 31, 38, 67, and 68).

The evolution of modern tectonics is thought to reflect relatively recent manifestations of secular change due to mantle cooling (69). This is corroborated by intraplate continental basalts indicating rapid mantle cooling from the Proterozoic to the Phanerozoic (70) and an associated transition in the convective balance of Earth’s mantle from once bottom–up to now top–down-dominated since the late Neoproterozoic Era (71). A relationship between this new style of tectonics and The Great Unconformity is possible, but the details are uncertain. Thus, while The Great Unconformity reflects an important source of particles to the oceans from ca 700 to 500 Mya, we highlight the role of changing tectonic style as a primary driver of Post-Cryogenian weathering and erosion.

As described above, this new modern style of deep-subduction plate tectonics may have been much more vigorous than what preceded (72–74). Evidence for more vigorous tectonics includes an increase in subduction flux (43) and observations that mountain-building episodes became more intense and more frequent (29, 44). Therefore, we hypothesize that there began a different type of control on physical and chemical weathering processes after the Cryogenian Period, and that tectonics began to pace the dynamics.

To understand these dynamics, we focus on our Zr/Al results since 650 Mya (Figs. 2A and 5 and SI Appendix, Fig. S2). From 650 Mya and forward in time, we see periods with Zr/Al alternating between values that tend much greater than the CA value and periods with values tending to less than the CA. Periods with values tending to >CA are most apparent in the time windows of approximately 590 to 630 Mya, 450 to 530 Mya, 250 to 350 Mya, and 50 to 130 Mya. Conversely, periods when Zr/Al tend toward values < CA are found in the time windows approximately 530 to 590 Mya, 410 to 450 Mya, 370 to 390 Mya, 200 to 250 Mya, and 20 to 50 Mya. We note that the data density in the time window 120 to 250 Mya is especially low (Figs. 2A and 5), and greater data density may alter these dynamics. We also note that, especially in time windows of generally low CA, that we see frequent short-lived swings to high Zr/Al as well.

If we follow the interpretive framework outlined above (Fig. 4), times when Zr/Al was mostly < CA represent net particle accumulation on the continents. Conversely, times when Zr/Al was mostly > CA represent net particle erosion from the continents. However, the frequent swings in Zr/Al may suggest that shorter periods of net erosion may be interspaced with broad periods of continental particle accumulation. Similarly, the longer periods of net particle erosion may be interspersed with shorter periods of particle accumulation.

There are few available studies from which to compare our assessment of the history of continental particle dynamics. However, in one study, the whole history of Phanerozoic sedimentation was reconstructed (18), and the percent of sediment preserved as terrestrial deposits is reproduced in Fig. 5B. These results are broadly compatible with ours showing low percentages of terrestrial sediment accumulation when we proposed periods of net erosion in the time widows of ca. 450 to 520 Mya, 280 to 400 Mya. Also broadly compatible, ref. 18 reconstructs high percentages of terrestrial sedimentation, indicating net particle storage from ca. 380 to 420 Mya, 200 to 300 Mya, and during the past 50 Mya, similar to our results. In contrast, ref. 18 does not show obvious evidence for reduced terrestrial storage in the time window of ca. 50 to 130 Mya, where we suggest net erosion of particles from the continent.

In another study, marine and continental sediment deposition rates through the Phanerozoic Eon were modeled (19). Like the present study, this model produced an important episode of continental particle deposition in the time window between 130 to 260 Mya. This model, however, differs from the present study, and also from ref. 18, in showing no terrestrial particle deposition between 370 to 450 Mya, and little particle deposition from 20 to 50 Mya.

Cyclicity of particle dynamics.

Our data suggest alternating periods of net continental sediment accumulation and net sediment erosion in post-Cryogenian times. We argue that these dynamics have a tectonic control. To test this hypothesis, and to reveal possible mechanisms controlling these dynamics, we explore for cyclicity in our geochemical records and compare these results with cyclicity in processes linked to tectonic control. Starting with our data, spectral analysis of the Zr/Al data yields several cycles with high statistical (>99%) significance (Fig. 6). We highlight cycles in the range of 160 to 240 My, 80 to 120 My, ~50 My, and ~35 My. Spectral analysis of our Rb/Al results generate similar cyclicity in the 140 to 210 My range, with a small peak at ~100 My and with additional cyclicity at ~50 My and ~35 My. We have also analyzed cyclicity in zircon ages over time (75) producing similar periods of ~200 My and 85 My, as also found in previous analyses of zircon age frequencies (e.g., ref. 28; see below). We also tested for cyclicity in Phanerozoic sea level using the record of (76). Our spectral analysis agrees with previous analysis of this record (77), and with the analyses of independent sea level records (78), showing dominant peaks in cyclicity in the range of 150 to 250 My and 36 to 37 My, with a possible peak at ~100 My. Other peaks in the range of 50 to 70 My and 28 My are also revealed. Besides generating cyclicities, our results also reveal in-phase relationships for these records, especially for the ~200 My cycles (Fig. 6).

Similar cyclicity is observed in many other geological and geochemical indicators of surficial processes that may be related to tectono–mantle geodynamics, with periods of ~90 My and ~190 My being particularly well represented (e.g., refs. 28, 29, 44, 71, and 79). In addition to the periodicity of U/Pb zircon ages (as noted above), there is similar periodicity in the Lu/Hf isotopic composition of zircons (εHf(t)). Both the zircon age periodicity and εHf(t) are likely indicators of subduction intensity and/or arc dynamics. Thus, increases in zircon abundance likely reflect enhanced production through subduction-related magmatism (e.g., refs. 28, 29, 44, and 80; although see ref. 81), while negative deviations in εHf(t) reflect a high input of reworked continental crust to subduction-related magmas. In addition, the emplacement of large igneous provinces displays a dominant period of ~93 My (28). Strong periodicities of ~90 and 180 My, respectively, resemble the time scales for upper and whole mantle convection (79) and have been directly linked to periodicity in mantle circulation, and specifically mantle plume events (28, 29).

In addition, broad changes in eustatic sea level are linked to seafloor spreading rate, where higher rates yield warmer, less dense, shallower ocean crust, and sea-level rise (e.g. refs. 82 and 83). Spreading rate, in turn, is likely coupled to subduction rate intensity, which can be evidenced in the distribution of zircon ages (e.g., refs. 44 and 78). All in all, numerous Earth surface processes display periods of ~90 My and ~180 to 200 My, and these are likely driven by tectono–mantle geodynamics, and many have been linked in ways to drive dominant cyclicity in weathering patterns through the Phanerozoic Eon.

Synthesis.

Our analysis begins at 2000 Mya, and from here, until around the Cryogenian Period, particle transport to the oceans was relatively efficient. This efficiency, we argue, resulted from rapid and efficient particle transport in predominantly braided river systems. We also find evidence for reduced efficiency of chemical weathering during this time. Reduced chemical weathering efficiency could have limited the disaggregation and breakup of the rock matrix during weathering even though the most easily weathered rock constituents, like sodium feldspar, were extensively weathered back to 2000 Mya. Overall, from 2000 Mya to the Cryogenian Period, physical weathering was important with efficient particle transport to the oceans in braided river systems. During this time, weathering intensity was governed by geomorphology.

There was a dramatic shift in the processes regulating particle transport to the oceans beginning in post-Cryogenian times, where swings to both high and low values of Zr/Al become exaggerated. In addition, we also recognize periods of both excess particle storage on the continents and excess particle transport from the continents to the oceans. We argue that with the late Proterozoic origin of deep-subduction, tectonics began to control the dynamics of particle erosion and transport. With the origin of deep subduction, rates of subduction accelerated as well as the frequency and intensity of mountain building. Indeed, within the Phanerozoic Eon, numerous geochemical indicators begin to show periodicities compatible with the influence of tectono–mantle dynamics.

Over the last 500 to 600 My, the weathering indicators Zr/Al and Rb/Al are in-sync with zircon age distribution, a likely indicator of subduction intensity. They are also in sync with sea level, likely also driven by spreading rate with a link to subduction rate. Overall, when subduction rates are high, zircon generation is high, mountain building is accelerated and sea level is high. We suggest that high rates of mountain building generate more effective erosion, delivering newly eroded, as well as stored terrestrial particles, to the oceans as evidenced by high Zr/Al. High sea level, especially during transgressions, may also accelerate coastal erosion delivering stored particles to the sea. We also note that some periods of net particle erosion from the continents (40 to 120 Mya, 260 to 360 Mya, 560 to 530 Mya, and 600 to 650 Mya) are times of high continent fragmentation (Figs. 3 and 5), and increased coastline length where particles might be more efficiently transferred to the sea. In addition, during times of accelerated particle erosion, chemical weathering is reduced as seen by elevated Rb/Al ratios (Fig. 2).

When subduction is less intense, mountain building is less intense, sea level is lower and particle storage on the continents is more likely. These times also seem to correlate with less continental fragmentation and thus more internal drainage for particle storage (Fig. 5). These circumstances generate marine sediments with lower Zr/Al. In addition, because physical erosion is less intense, chemical weathering is more intense, generating particles with lower Rb/Al ratios.

There is a clear trend toward more intense chemical weathering, at least for the more refractory rock components, around 400 Mya as indicated by a reduction in the Rb/Al ratio. Although there could be a diagenetic component to this trend, we believe it largely reflects an acceleration of weathering coinciding with the Paleozoic evolution and expansion of land plants. Indeed, land plants stabilized soils and produced corrosive root exudates, leading to a more aggressive chemical weathering environment. The stabilization of soils led to the dominance of meandering river systems that may have enhanced the trapping of fine particles as evidenced by increasing values of Na/Al since ca. 400 Mya. Other than this, however, the expansion of land plants had no obvious impact on the overall dynamics of particle transport to the oceans as evidenced by no obvious changes in Zr/Al dynamics since 400 Mya.

We wish to highlight some caveats. First, we believe that the late Proterozoic transition in weathering and particle dynamics is likely robust, but data are sparse, and more data might reveal evidence for some tectonic control on particle dynamics earlier in the Proterozoic. Indeed, peaks in zircon abundances correlate with the assembly of supercontinents Nuna/Columbia and Rodinia (44), and these major continent-building episodes could have impacted weathering and particle transport dynamics. In addition, our data are heavily biased toward North America and not very abundant in some time windows. A declustered data analysis suggests that there might be some regional differences in Zr/Al dynamics over time, but a more extensive and more global dataset could answer the extent of such variability. A more extensive dataset might also alter some of the patterns of particle accumulation and storage observed here. We believe however, that the tectonic control on weathering and particle transport dynamics as outlined here is also likely robust. Overall, we present a comprehensive view on the history of weathering and particle transport over time; a view that may well evolve as more data become available.
