Associations of gestational diabetes mellitus with the risk of cardiometabolic diseases: results from NHANES and mendelian randomization analyses
Authors: Libi Zhang, Yiwen Qiu, Ye Huang, Diliyaer Ainiwan, Haibo Zhou, Haoyue Cheng, Xialidan Alifu, Yan Zhuang, Peihan Chi, Danqing Chen, Hui Liu, Yunxian Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is associated with an increased risk of long-term cardiometabolic diseases beyond the perinatal period. This study aimed to investigate the association and the potential causality between GDM and subsequent risk of cardiometabolic diseases, as well as to evaluate the effect of GDM onset age on the diseases.MethodsData were from 10,251 women with at least one live birth in the NHANES 2007-2018. Survey-weighted logistic regression analysis was performed to investigate the association. Subsequently, the inverse variance weighted (IVW) and other supplementary approaches of two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) were used to verify the causal association.ResultsWomen with a history of GDM had an increased risk of obesity (OR: 1.67, 95%CI: 1.38–2.01), hyperlipidemia(OR: 1.28, 95%CI: 1.02 – 1.61), type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) (OR: 5.39, 95%CI: 4.25–6.85), metabolic syndrome (MetS) (OR: 1.62, 95%CI: 1.28–2.05), cardiovascular diseases (OR: 1.61, 95%CI: 1.04–2.52) and coronary heart disease (CHD) (OR: 1.89, 95%CI: 1.17–3.06). Besides, participants diagnosed with GDM at a young age were more prone to developing cardiometabolic diseases. Furthermore, a causal link between GDM and the risk of T2DM (OR: 1.54, 95%CI: 1.09–2.17) and CHD (OR: 1.08, 95%CI: 1.04–1.12) was supported by the IVW method of the MR analysis. Mediation analyses of observational and MR study consistently revealed that T2DM partially mediated the association between GDM and incident CHD.ConclusionsGDM was significantly associated with an increased risk of cardiometabolic diseases, and a younger age of GDM onset indicated a higher risk. Moreover, a causal association of GDM with the risk of T2DM and CHD was identified.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-025-01958-4.

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) was defined as any degree of glucose intolerance with onset or first recognition during pregnancy according to The International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups (IADPSG) [1]. The development of GDM was associated with mechanisms including the exacerbation of insulin resistance and pancreatic β-cell dysfunction. With the prevalence of obesity and the growing number of older pregnant women, the incidence of GDM has increased dramatically, ranging from 7% to 10% in Europe and the United States [2]. Increasing evidence suggests that GDM was associated not only with poor obstetric outcomes including caesarean section and macrosomia, but also with an increased risk of long-term health issues. Previous studies have shown that women with a history of GDM had an increased risk of developing type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), hypertension, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), metabolic syndrome (MetS), and cardiovascular diseases (CVD) [3–6].

However, there is some room for improvement of previous studies. Firstly, although cardiovascular and metabolic diseases are often intertwined, few studies have investigated the potential mediating role of chronic metabolic diseases in the association between a history of GDM and CVD risk, and the available evidence is inconsistent. Two studies have reported that GDM was associated with an increased risk of CVD independent of T2DM [7, 8], but another two studies have yielded the opposite conclusion [9, 10]. In addition, it is worth noting that the effects of β-cell dysfunction and insulin resistance may persist although GDM resolves postpartum. A retrospective cohort study reported a higher incidence of T2DM and hypertension in younger women with GDM [11]. This suggests that earlier GDM onset may extend exposure to detrimental metabolic states, potentially accelerating cardiometabolic decline and elevating disease risk. However, it remains unclear whether younger age at GDM diagnosis independently predicts higher cardiometabolic morbidity. Thirdly, the current literature is limited to observational studies, which are susceptible to confounding factors and reverse causality, and it is difficult to predict the causal relationship between GDM history and cardiometabolic diseases. Mendelian randomization (MR) studies employ single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as instrumental variables (IVs) to assess causality between exposure and outcome, which is a feasible alternative to randomized controlled trials. Liang et al. used a single-sample MR analysis to assess the causal association between GDM and CVD, however, only 4 cardiometabolic diseases were considered in that study [12].

Therefore, the purpose of our study was to investigate the association of a history of GDM and the onset age of GDM with subsequent risk of cardiometabolic diseases based on a population study. We also evaluated whether the association between a history of GDM and CVD was influenced by metabolic diseases. Furthermore, a causal association between GDM and cardiometabolic diseases was confirmed by the MR analysis.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) is an ongoing cross-sectional study designed to assess the health and nutrition status of adults and children in the United States. The survey uses a complex multistage probability sampling, which enrolls a nationally representative sample of about 5,000 people each year. All data were obtained from standard questionnaires, physical examinations, and laboratory tests. The study has been approved by the institutional review board of the National Center for Health Statistics, and all participants provided written informed consent.

We included participants from NHANES 2007-2018 who underwent an assessment of a history of GDM and cardiometabolic diseases and had at least one live birth. Of these, we excluded participants who were currently pregnant. Additionally, those diagnosed with T2DM, hypertension, or CVD prior to GDM were also excluded to ensure temporal sequence and avoid confounding by pre-existing cardiometabolic diseases. Women with other underlying causes of hepatic steatosis, including those who consumed more than 14 drinks/week or tested positive for the hepatitis virus were further ruled out.

Exposures and covariates

Information on GDM was obtained from the reproductive health questionnaire. A history of GDM was determined by self-reporting whether they had diabetes during pregnancy. Participants with a history of GDM were further divided into early-onset (< 30 years) and late-onset (≥ 30 years) according to the median age of first onset of GDM.

Baseline demographic data, physical activity, smoking status, alcohol consumption, dietary intake, and pregnancy-labor history were evaluated through structured questionnaires. Smoking status was divided into never smokers (< 100 cigarettes in a lifetime), former smokers (≥ 100 cigarettes in a lifetime, but recently quit smoking), and current smokers (persistent smoking habits). Weekly alcohol consumption was assessed based on the number of drinking days per week, and the average daily alcohol consumption. Recreational physical activity was created as a binary binge indicator, with active recreational physical activity defined as walking or cycling trips, or moderate or vigorous recreational activities lasting at least 10 minutes per week. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated from the height and weight derived from physical examinations.

Definition of outcome

The primary outcome of the study was cardiovascular and metabolic morbidity. CVD included coronary heart disease (CHD), heart failure and stroke, which were obtained from the medical health questionnaire. Participants were identified as having CHD if they self-reported a diagnosis of major coronary heart disease events, angina, or heart attack.

Metabolic diseases including obesity, hyperlipidemia, T2DM, hypertension, MetS, and NAFLD were the outcomes of interest. Obesity was defined as a BMI greater than 30 kg/m2. Hyperlipidemia was assessed based on the ATP 3-NCEP criteria, which includes total cholesterol (TC) ≥ 200 mg/dL, triglycerides (TG) ≥ 150 mg/dL, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) < 50 mg/dL, or low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) ≥ 130 mg/dL [13]. Participants were considered to have T2DM if they self-reported a diagnosis of diabetes, were currently taking insulin or glucose-lowering medication, had hemoglobin A1C >6.5%, fasting blood glucose (FBG) >125 mg/dL, or 2-h blood glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL. Hypertension was defined as a self-reported diagnosis of high blood pressure or a mean systolic/diastolic blood pressure of ≥ 140/90 mmHg at physical examination. MetS was defined according to the NCEP ATP III-2005 criteria for participants who met three or more of the following criteria: (1) elevated waist circumference (≥ 88 cm); (2) elevated blood pressure (≥ 130/85 mmHg or previously diagnosed hypertension); (3) decreased HDL-C (< 50 mg/dL); (4) elevated TG (≥ 150 mg/dL); (5) elevated FBG (≥ 100 mg/L or diagnosis of T2DM) [14]. Fatty liver index (FLI) ≥ 60 was used to diagnose NAFLD [15]. The FLI was calculated using physical measurements including BMI and waist circumference, as well as laboratory test results including TG and gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT). The specific calculation method of FLI was described in the previous study [16].

Data sources for MR analysis

Summary statistics of GDM were extracted from the ninth release of the FinnGen study including 13,039 cases and 197,831 controls of the European population (Table S1). Strict selection criteria were used to obtain qualified IVs. Firstly, the significant SNPs strongly associated with GDM with a P value less than 5×10−8 were extracted as candidate IVs. Secondly, SNPs with linkage disequilibrium (r2 = 0.01 and kb = 10,000) or palindromic SNPs with intermediate allele frequencies were eliminated. Furthermore, we excluded SNPs that were not obtained in the outcome genome-wide association study (GWAS). F-statistic was used to assess the strength of IVs, and only strong IVs with F-statistic >10 were included in further analysis. Detailed information on genetic variations including SNP IDs, effect alleles, effect sizes, and F-statistics is displayed in Table S2. 17 SNPs strongly associated with GDM were used as IVs. Based on F-statistics (range 30 to 766), our findings were unlikely to be influenced by weak instrumental variables.

Genetic instruments for obesity (4,688 cases and 458,322 controls), hyperlipidemia (3,439 cases and 459,010 controls), and hypertension (119,731 cases and 343,202 controls) were extracted from the largest publicly available GWAS conducted by Elswort et al. T2DM were obtained from a meta-analysis of GWAS (62,892 cases and 596,424 controls) [17]. MetS was extracted from the most comprehensive collection of GWAS in the UK Biobank (UKB), comprising 59,677 cases and 231,430 controls of European ancestry [18]. The summary statistics for NAFLD were extracted from a genome-wide meta-analysis of 4 European ancestry cohorts, including 8,434 cases and 770,180 controls [19]. In addition, the summary statistics for CVD were obtained from the UKB consortium, comprising 177,923 cases and 306,675 controls [20]. The summary statistics data for CHD were obtained from the Coronary Artery Disease Genome-wide Replication and Meta-analysis (CARDIoGRAM) plus the Coronary Artery Disease (C4D) Genetics Consortium (CARDIoGRAMplusC4D), comprising 60,801cases and 123,504 controls [21]. The data on heart failure were extracted from the Heart Failure Molecular Epidemiology for Therapeutic Targets Consortium (HERMES), which consisted of 47,309 cases and 930,014 controls from 26 cohorts [22]. Summary-level data of stroke were obtained from MEGASTROKE consortium, including 40,585 cases and 406,111 controls [23]. The sources of datasets are shown in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

Sample weights, strata, and primary sampling units were incorporated in the analysis due to the complex sample design of NHANES. Continuous variables were presented in the form of means and standard deviations (SD) or medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) depending on the distribution, while categorical parameters were presented as number (N) and percentage (%). For descriptive statistics, t-tests or Mann-Whitney tests were used for continuous variables and Chi-square tests were used for categorical variables. Survey-weighted logistic regression analysis was performed to estimate the odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) to investigate the association between the history of GDM, the onset age of GDM and the risk of cardiometabolic diseases. A multinomial logistic regression analysis was used to investigate the relationship between the history of GDM and cardiometabolic disease comorbidities. We also conducted a sensitivity analysis by excluding those women with BMI >40 kg/m2. Model 1 was adjusted for age, and race; Model 2 was further adjusted for BMI, educational level, income, parity, alcohol consumption, smoking status, physical activity, energy intake, and age at first birth. For obesity, the covariates adjusted in model 2 did not include BMI. For CVD, we also accounted for additional adjustments for hyperlipidemia, T2DM, hypertension, MetS, and NAFLD in model 2. In addition, mediation analysis was used to explore the mediating role of metabolic diseases (including obesity, hyperlipidemia, T2DM, hypertension, MetS, and NAFLD) in the association of the history of GDM with CVD risk. In this analysis, we adjusted for the same covariates as the multivariable regression models. A parameter regression approach was used to estimate the total effect, including both the indirect effect and the direct effect of GDM on CVD risk. The coefficients were estimated from 1000-replication bootstrap [24]. The mediation analysis was conducted using the R package "mediation".

The two-sample MR analysis was performed to evaluate the potential causal effects of GDM on cardiometabolic events. Inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method that provides unbiased causal estimates in the absence of horizontal pleiotropy and heterogeneity was used as the primary method [25]. In addition, three complementary MR methods, including the weighted median method, MR Egger method and MR-pleiotropy residual sum and outlier (MR-PRESSO) method were employed as sensitivity analyses [26–28]. The weighted median method could provide robust estimates even when most IVs were invalid. Heterogeneity among genetic variants was assessed using Cochran’s Q statistic. Potential directional pleiotropy was evaluated with MR-Egger regression, where a non-zero intercept indicates pleiotropic bias and the slope offers a pleiotropy-adjusted causal estimate. Furthermore, the MR-PRESSO method was employed to detect and correct for horizontal pleiotropy through a global test, with outlier variants removed to derive refined causal estimates. Considering that BMI is strongly linked to the development of outcomes, we conducted multivariate MR (MVMR) analyses to assess the causal effect of GDM on cardiometabolic diseases independent of BMI [29]. Metabolic diseases with a significant causal relationship were included as mediators in subsequent mediation analyses. In this study, we used a two-step MR method to analyze the mediating role of T2DM in the association of GDM and CHD. All analyses were performed using R software version 4.2.1, with a P value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Table 2 shows the association of a history of GDM with the risk of cardiometabolic diseases. After full adjustment in Model 2, women with a history of GDM had an increased risk of obesity (OR: 1.67, 95%CI: 1.38–2.01), hyperlipidemia (OR: 1.28, 95%CI: 1.02–1.61), T2DM (OR: 5.39, 95%CI: 4.25–6.85), and MetS (OR: 1.62, 95%CI: 1.28–2.05) compared to those without a history of GDM. The association between a history of GDM and NAFLD was not statistically significant after adjusting for covariates. Moreover, individuals with a history of GDM were associated with a 1.61-fold (95%CI: 1.04–2.52) and 1.89-fold (95%CI: 1.17–3.06) increased risk of CVD and CHD, respectively. However, there was no significant association of a history of GDM with the risk of hypertension, heart failure and stroke. Moreover, women with a history of GDM were more likely to have multiple cardiometabolic diseases simultaneously (Data was not shown). Furthermore, the associations of the onset age of GDM with the risk of cardiometabolic diseases are presented in Table 3. Participants with early-onset GDM were more susceptible to developing cardiometabolic diseases in reference to those without a history of GDM. The odds ratio of T2DM increased from 4.71 (95%CI: 3.14–7.04) in participants with late-onset GDM to 5.71 (95%CI: 4.04–8.07) in those with early-onset GDM. Similarly, the risk of obesity and Mets was heightened in individuals with early-onset GDM. In addition, early-onset GDM was associated with a 1.42-fold (95%CI: 1.02–1.99) increased risk of hypertension, whereas late-onset GDM indicated null association (OR: 0.85, 95%CI: 0.60–1.21). Women with early-onset GDM had a 1.81-fold (95%CI: 1.19–2.74) higher risk of NAFLD, but no significant association was observed in those with late-onset GDM (OR: 1.01, 95%CI: 0.60–1.70). The results for CVD and CHD were similar. In contrast, women with late-onset GDM were associated with a higher risk of hyperlipidemia (OR: 1.66, 95%CI: 1.15–2.40), whereas early-onset GDM showed null association (OR: 1.09, 95%CI: 0.79–1.49). In addition, stratified analysis by median age at first birth (< 22 years and ≥ 22 years) showed generally consistent results (Table S4). After matching, follow-up duration was well-balanced (14.89 ± 9.89 years each, P = 1.000). Figure 1 presents the causal link between GDM and the risk of cardiometabolic diseases. The IVW method indicated that genetically predicted GDM was significantly associated with the risk of T2DM (OR: 1.54, 95%CI: 1.09–2.17). Despite initial heterogeneity in T2DM analyses (Phet < 0.001), MR-PRESSO analyses supported robust causal inference after outlier removal (OR: 1.58, 95%CI: 1.17–2.12), with no evidence of directional pleiotropy (Pintercept = 0.497). In addition, the causal relationship between GDM and CHD risk was confirmed by the IVW method (OR: 1.08, 95%CI: 1.04–1.12), and this association was not affected by heterogeneity or horizontal pleiotropy (Phet = 0.485, Pintercept = 0.820). Moreover, the supplementary approaches yielded similar findings. MVMR showed that the causal effect of GDM on T2DM and CHD remained robust after adjusting for BMI (OR for T2DM: 1.48, 95%CI: 1.37–1.60; OR for CHD: 1.05, 95%CI: 1.01–1.09). However, we did not observe a significant causal relationship between GDM and other cardiometabolic diseases. In addition, the mediated analysis indicated that T2DM was responsible for approximately 90% of the association between GDM and CHD risk (mediation effect β [95% CI]: 0.070 [0.010–0.129]). Discussion

In the current study, we found that women with a history of GDM were significantly associated with an increased risk of multiple cardiometabolic diseases. Furthermore, MR analysis supported a causal association of GDM with the risk of T2DM and CHD. Mediation analyses consistently revealed that T2DM partially mediated the association between GDM history and incident CHD.

The positive association we observed between GDM history and increased T2DM risk is robustly supported by a meta-analysis of 20 studies, which found that women with a history of GDM had a nearly 10-fold increased risk of developing T2DM [30]. Our MR analysis also confirmed a causal link between GDM and T2DM despite the presence of heterogeneity. The consistency of the result across methods, the lack of evidence for directional pleiotropy, and the stability of the estimate after outlier removal all support a causal relationship between GDM and T2DM. Consistent with our observational and MR study, in a prospective cohort study of the UK Biobank women with GDM were followed up for 10.3 years after delivery and had a 1.31-fold increased risk of CHD [31]. Although MR study did not reveal such an association, our observational study found that GDM was significantly associated with postpartum obesity and hyperlipidemia, which was in line with Ajala et al.’s study [32]. In agreement with our results, a prospective study in Finland women with previous GDM exhibited a higher risk of MetS in midlife [33]. However, another prospective cohort study involving 426 women indicated a null association between GDM and MetS, which was consistent with our findings of MR analysis [34]. The inconsistent results between the MR analysis and the observational study may be due to fundamental differences in study design, population characteristics, and phenotype measurement. The MR analysis used limited European-ancestry genetic instruments and strict GDM diagnoses, while the observational study included diverse populations with self-reported GDM that may be misclassified. Although we adjusted for confounders such as diet and exercise in the observational study, residual confounding still existed and may lead to the overestimation of causal effects. Additionally, observational metabolic measurements may reflect intermittent states, whereas MR captures stable genetic risks unaffected by postnatal environment.

In addition, we did not find a positive association between GDM and heart failure or stroke in either the observational or MR study, while previous studies have shown inconsistent conclusions. Similar to our study, Liang et al. employed MR analysis in the Finnish population to confirm no association between GDM and heart failure, stroke, or hypertension [12]. Another study from the United States did not show a significant association between GDM and heart failure [35], and several recent large cohort studies also indicated a null association between GDM and stroke [8, 36]. However, a cohort study in Denmark reported a 65% increased risk of heart failure and a 120% increased risk of stroke in women with GDM [37], which might be attributed to an older study population, extended follow-up duration, and inclusion of more severe clinical cases. Contrary to previous observational studies, our study did not identify associations between a history of GDM and hypertension or NAFLD [4, 38]. This divergence may be attributed to inconsistent diagnostic criteria: our cases were identified through self-reporting or parameter measurements, whereas their studies relied on antihypertensive medication use and ultrasound confirmation, potentially capturing more severe cases.

Although GDM was strongly associated with a higher risk of T2DM (a recognized risk factor for CHD), mediated analysis of our observational study and MR analysis consistently suggests that an increased risk of CHD is only partially attributable to the development of T2DM in women with a history of GDM. Previous studies have extensively investigated the potential role of T2DM in the association of GDM with CHD risk, but have reached inconsistent conclusions. Similar to our findings, a prospective cohort study of 21,353 women with a history of GDM found that subsequent development of T2DM explained 25.0% of the association between GDM and CHD risk [37]. Another study conducted in Canada reported an elevated risk of CHD was observed in women with GDM regardless of the presence or absence of T2DM [7]. However, the Nurses’ Health Study II indicated the association between GDM and CVD became no longer statistically significant among women without progression to T2DM [10], which may be related to misclassification bias of self-reported GDM and various covariate adjustments in models. Progressive decline in β-cell function and persistent insulin resistance in postpartum patients with GDM can lead to the development of T2DM. Hyperglycemia and β-cell dysfunction may further contribute to elevated systolic blood pressure, abnormal lipid metabolism, increased oxidative stress, and activation of the renin-angiotensin system, thereby accelerating atherosclerosis and increasing the risk of cardiovascular diseases [39–41]. In addition to diabetic pathways, our observational study also found that obesity and MetS partially mediated the GDM and CHD association by less than 10%. A substantial proportion of women with a history of GDM would progress to T2DM and even develop some cardiovascular risk factors in the early postpartum period [30]. Increasing evidence indicated that women with a history of GDM had more cardiovascular risk factors, including elevated levels of weight gain, C-reactive protein, HbA1c and low adiponectin levels[10, 42–44]. Risk factors such as chronic inflammation and adipokine imbalance can promote endothelial dysfunction and instability of atherosclerotic plaques, thereby increasing the risk of CHD. Longitudinal cohort studies have identified persistent subclinical cardiac dysfunction in women with prior GDM, characterized by increased left ventricular mass and impaired left ventricular relaxation and systolic function, which may mediate increased CHD risk independent of T2DM [45, 46].

Furthermore, we found that participants diagnosed with early-onset GDM may have a higher risk of developing cardiometabolic diseases and the association was less susceptible to the influence of age at first birth. Our findings were supported by Hwu et al., who reported that the incidence of T2DM decreased with age in women with a history of GDM, with a prevalence rate of 49.1% in 15- to 29-year-old women and 34.2% in 40- to 44-year-old women [11]. It may reflect that GDM patients have a poorer metabolic and vascular environment, thus leading to a more aggressive progression of vascular complications. Several studies have reported that adverse cardiovascular risk factors were present prior to pregnancy and worsened over time in women who went on to develop GDM [42, 47]. GDM may synergize with these accumulating risks, exacerbating cardiovascular impairment. However, our results should be interpreted considering unequal follow-up time. While sensitivity analysis with balanced follow-up showed generally consistent effects, cautious interpretation remains warranted.

The strength of this study was that we investigated the association between a history of GDM and multiple cardiometabolic diseases in a nationally representative survey cohort with a relatively large sample size, and further employed MR studies to confirm the causal associations. However, there are several limitations in our study. Firstly, GDM and some cardiometabolic diseases were identified through self-reporting in standard structured questionnaires, which may lead to misclassification bias and hamper the accurate estimation of associations. Even so, NHANES is widely regarded as a valid and high-quality study that can provide reliable results on the prevalence and risk factors of major diseases. Secondly, although we adjusted for some confounding factors, residual confounding still requires attention due to some unavailable variables, such as other pregnancy complications and metabolic status during pregnancy. Thirdly, due to the inherent limitations of cross-sectional studies, especially the potential reverse causality and the unmeasurable confounding factors, the findings from NHANES data should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than causal inference. Consequently, we performed the MR analysis, which relies on the random allocation of alleles at conception, to obtain a causal link between GDM and cardiometabolic diseases. Fourthly, our cross-sectional and MR analyses were not drawn from the same sample. In order to eliminate the potential effect of population heterogeneity, further studies are required in the same racial. Lastly,the study population of NHANES and MR analysis exhibited a lack of racial diversity, and participants with pre-existing comorbidities were excluded in NHANES study, which may limit the generalisability of the study conclusions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that GDM was significantly associated with an increased risk of multiple cardiometabolic diseases. Yet, the MR analysis only confirmed causal associations of GDM with the risk of T2DM and CHD. Notably, a younger age of GDM onset may be associated with a higher risk of cardiometabolic diseases. It implies that the importance of postpartum follow-up and screening of cardiovascular risk factors in women with GDM, especially for those diagnosed at a younger age. Implementing targeted screening and intervention strategies for early-onset GDM cases could reduce the long-term disease burden, enhance life expectancy and alleviate the social healthcare burden.
