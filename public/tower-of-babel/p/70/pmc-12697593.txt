Mechanical Thrombectomy for Patients with Large Core Ischemic Strokes—An International Cost‐Effectiveness Analysis
Authors: Cathal McCarthy, Victor Durieux, Matthew Crockett, Sarah Power, Alan O'Hare, Karl Boyle, John Thornton, Fiona Boland, Thomas Barthe, Anne‐Laure Bocquet, Patrick Nicholson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDRecent trials demonstrate mechanical thrombectomy (MT) benefits patients with large core infarcts. Mechanical thrombectomy (MT) has revolutionized the treatment of acute ischemic stroke caused by large vessel occlusion, with multiple trials demonstrating significant benefit in carefully selected patients. Following publication of the initial swathe of positive randomized clinical trials for MT, 1 patients with large infarct cores, traditionally defined by an Alberta Stroke Program Early CT [Computed Tomography] Score (ASPECTS) <6 or core volume >70 mL on perfusion imaging, were typically excluded from MT based on concerns about poor outcomes and potentially increased risk of hemorrhagic complications. 2

However, 6 recent randomized controlled trials have challenged this paradigm. The RESCUE‐Japan LIMIT (Recovery by Endovascular Salvage for Cerebral Ultra‐Acute Embolism–Japan Large Ischemic Core Trial), 3 SELECT 2 (Randomized Controlled Trial to Optimize Patient's Selection for Endovascular Treatment in Acute Ischemic Stroke), 4 ANGEL‐ASPECT (Endovascular Therapy in Acute Anterior Circulation Large Vessel Occlusive Patients with a Large Infarct Core), 5 TENSION (The Efficacy and Safety of Thrombectomy in Stroke with Extended Lesion and Extended Time Window), 6 TESLA (Thrombectomy for Emergent Salvage of Large Anterior Circulation Ischemic Stroke), 7 and LASTE (Large Stroke Therapy Evaluation) 8 trials demonstrated that MT can significantly improve functional outcomes in patients with large infarct cores. A recent meta‐analysis of these trials 9 showed that MT more than doubled the odds of good functional outcome (odds ratio 2.90) compared with medical management alone, albeit with a modest increase in symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage.

This expansion of MT indications to include patients with large infarct cores has important implications for health care systems globally. Although the clinical benefit has been established, the economic impact of providing MT to this broader patient population remains unclear. Health care systems must balance the substantial upfront costs of establishing and maintaining 24/7 MT capability against long‐term cost savings from reduced disability. This analysis is particularly critical given that stroke remains a leading cause of disability worldwide, with associated long‐term care costs representing a significant burden on health care systems and society. Although recent trials have demonstrated the clinical benefit of MT in this population, it is important to consider that increased survival in patients with large core strokes may be accompanied by greater disability, potentially leading to substantial long‐term care costs and affecting the overall cost‐effectiveness. 10

Previous cost‐effectiveness analyses of EVT have focused primarily on patients with favorable imaging characteristics. 11 Although these studies consistently demonstrated cost‐effectiveness across different health care systems, their findings cannot be automatically extrapolated to the large core population, who may have different patterns of recovery, complications, and long‐term outcomes.

Therefore, we conducted an international cost‐effectiveness analysis of MT for patients with large infarct cores across 11 diverse health care systems. Using data from recent randomized trials and country‐specific cost inputs, we aimed to determine whether MT represents good value for money in this expanded patient population and how this varies across different health care contexts. Our findings will help inform clinical guidelines, resource allocation decisions, and health care policy as systems adapt to provide EVT to this previously excluded patient group, potentially benefiting the estimated 20%–30% of patients with acute stroke who present with large infarct cores.

METHODS

The authors declare that all supporting data are available within the article (and its online Supplementary Files).

Model Structure

The analytical framework consisted of a dual‐temporal decision model: a 90‐day acute phase decision tree coupled with a 20‐year state transition Markov model, designed to evaluate the comparative economics and clinical efficacy of mechanical thrombectomy (MT) versus medical management in patients with acute ischemic stroke presenting with extensive infarct volumes. The model architecture was implemented using Microsoft Excel (version 2408).

The short‐term decision tree evaluated both cost and clinical efficacy. The acute phase component quantified both financial burden and therapeutic outcomes. Patient stratification for low ASPECTS cases was modeled via a decision tree incorporating projected 3‐month modified Rankin Scale (mRS) outcomes, synthesized from a meta‐analysis encompassing six randomized controlled trials focused on low ASPECTS cohorts. 9 Although the mRS provides a 7‐tier functional assessment metric (spanning from 0 [complete functional independence] to 6 [mortality]), it is important to note that the scale exhibits nonuniform intervals between ordinal levels, and the correlation between disability severity (and associated costs) and mRS scoring demonstrates nonlinear characteristics. 12

The model stratifies patients based on their mRS score at 90 days post ‐stroke. This stratification implicitly incorporates the impact of any stroke recurrences that may occur within the 90‐day acute phase, as these recurrences would influence a patient's 90‐day mRS outcome. Therefore, the model does not explicitly model a separate probability of recurrence during the acute 90‐day period but rather captures its effect through the 90‐day mRS distribution. The longitudinal disease progression was simulated through a lifetime Markov model utilizing 3‐month cycle intervals to project clinical trajectory and associated economic burden. The model architecture incorporated distinct temporal phases: midterm (90 days to 1 year) and long‐term phases (>1 year), implementing tunnel states to accommodate time‐dependent recurrence probabilities. Within each cycle iteration, patients could maintain their current health status, experience a subsequent stroke event, or transition to mortality. In cases of stroke recurrence, patients were redirected to the initial 3‐month cycle corresponding to their 90‐day mRS classification, subsequently reentering the Markov process at the 3 to6 month interval, unless mortality occurred. For recurrent events resulting in mRS scores exceeding 2, therapeutic intervention was limited to conventional medical management, excluding additional mechanical thrombectomy. To account for the continuous nature of state transitions within discrete cycles, a half‐cycle correction methodology was implemented. Both costs and health outcomes were subject to an annual discount rate of 3% to reflect time preferences. Country‐specific cost data for each of the 11 health care systems were derived from the most recently published studies within each country (references listed in Supplementary Materials). These studies provided cost data at 90 days post stroke, stratified by mRS score. To account for inherent variability in cost components, deterministic sensitivity analyses were conducted, varying cost inputs by ±20%. This range was selected to reflect heterogeneity in medical practices, price negotiations, and potential inflation. Clinical outcome data for the model, specifically the comparative effectiveness of MT versus medical management in patients with large core infarcts, were sourced directly from the study‐level meta‐analysis by Jhou et al. 9 This meta‐analysis provides pooled estimates from trials focusing on this specific patient population.

Long‐term projections were carried out using a state transition Markov model, which considered quarterly stroke recurrence risk, 13 , 14 death from other causes linked to poststroke mRS scores, 12 and transitions between mRS states toward a poorer health state. 15 Mortality data were obtained from the Human Mortality Database (https://www.mortality.org/), covering the 11 countries to reflect death likelihood by specific mRS scores. Additional details on long‐term analysis parameters are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Costs

The analysis adopted a health care system perspective, with direct medical expenditures calculated using country‐specific cost data encompassing procedural interventions, acute care services, and both midterm and long‐term medical management. All monetary values were standardized to 2023 US dollars to facilitate cross‐market comparison.

Quality of Life

Utility measures based on mRS score categories were sourced from a prospectively validated cohort study assessing EuroQol (EQ‐5D) in poststroke patients. 16 Detailed utility scores are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Cost‐Effectiveness Analysis

The comparative economic value was quantified using the incremental cost‐effectiveness ratio (ICER), calculated as the differential cost between MT and medical management interventions divided by the corresponding incremental gain in quality‐adjusted life years (QALYs) derived from the model simulations.

The model's structural integrity and predictive reliability underwent comprehensive sensitivity testing through multiple analytical approaches. Univariate and bivariate deterministic sensitivity analyses identified critical model parameters and evaluated the impact of parameter uncertainty on outcome measures. These deterministic analyses encompassed systematic ±20% variations across all input variables. Furthermore, probabilistic sensitivity analysis was executed through Monte Carlo simulation methodology (10 000 iterations), with input parameters varied stochastically according to their predetermined probability distributions. The uncertainty analysis results were visualized through cost‐effectiveness scatter plots and acceptability curves to illustrate the robustness of the base‐case findings across varying willingness‐to‐pay (WTP) thresholds.

Our analysis demonstrated that MT was cost effective across all 11 health care systems evaluated. Quality of life improvements, measured in QALYs, were consistent across all health care systems. The incremental QALY gain ranged from 1.16 in Australia to 1.98 in France, with most countries achieving improvements between 1.7 and 2.0 QALYs. These substantial quality of life improvements were observed across all health care settings, regardless of the underlying cost structure.

The ICER calculations revealed favorable outcomes across all systems studied. Belgium achieved a negative ICER of −$422 per QALY, indicating both cost savings and improved outcomes. In other countries, ICERs ranged from $1175 per QALY in Spain to $10 005 per QALY in Australia. Notably, all ICERs remained well below the WTP threshold of $20 000 per QALY, supporting the economic viability of MT across diverse health care settings.

To test the robustness of our findings, we conducted a deterministic sensitivity analysis by systematically varying each model input parameter by ±20% from its base value. Results were most influenced by variations in acute care costs for patients experiencing death or severe disability (mRS scores 4–6). The sensitivity of cost‐effectiveness to acute care costs for severely disabled patients (mRS scores 4–6) is likely driven by the substantial long‐term care requirements associated with disability in these states. Our cost‐effectiveness model accounted for survival and death through the use of a lifetime horizon Markov model. The model projected costs and QALYs based on transitions between mRS states, including transitions to mortality (mRS score 6). Costs associated with each mRS state, including mRS score 6, were considered for both acute and long‐term periods. The costs for mRS scores 4–6 are elevated due to intensive medical needs and long‐term disability care, whereas costs associated with mRS 6, although potentially acutely high, do not accrue over a lifetime horizon. (Further details on the model structure and cost data, including cost inputs for each mRS state and time horizon, are available in the Supplementary Materials).

This finding is clinically intuitive, as these patients require the most intensive medical care and have the highest associated health care costs. Despite this sensitivity to acute care costs, MT remained cost effective even when these costs were varied by 20% in either direction. When acute care costs for severely disabled patients were increased by 20%, the highest ICER observed was $12 006 per QALY (Australia), still well below our WTP threshold of $20 000 per QALY. Conversely, when these costs were decreased by 20%, the lowest ICER was −$506 per QALY (Belgium), indicating maintained cost savings. Other model parameters, including procedural costs, rehabilitation costs, and long‐term care expenses, showed less influence on the overall cost‐effectiveness results. These findings demonstrate that although acute care costs for severe disability have a significant impact on the magnitude of cost‐effectiveness, they do not alter the fundamental conclusion that MT provides good value for money across all health care systems studied.

The probabilistic analysis revealed 3 distinct tiers of cost‐effectiveness thresholds. In 5 countries (Belgium, France, Germany, Italy, and Spain), MT demonstrated 100% probability of cost‐effectiveness at a WTP threshold of $10 000/QALY, representing the most economically favorable outcomes. A second group comprising China, the Netherlands, Sweden, the United Kingdom, and the United States achieved 100% probability of cost‐effectiveness at a threshold of $15 000/QALY. Australia, although showing the highest incremental costs in the base case analysis, still maintained 100% probability of cost‐effectiveness at $20 000/QALY. Notably, all simulated scenarios across all health care systems remained cost effective at the conventional WTP threshold of $20 000/QALY, reinforcing the robust economic value of MT in this patient population.

DISCUSSION

This international cost‐effectiveness analysis demonstrates that MT for large‐core ischemic stroke (ASPECTS ≤5) consistently achieves cost‐effectiveness across 11 distinct health care systems, including those in Australia, China, Belgium, France, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, Spain, Sweden, the United Kingdom, and the United States. MT showed improved QALYs compared with medical management alone, even with significant differences in health care delivery models and underlying cost structures. Although incremental costs varied considerably among countries, ranging from cost savings in Belgium to increased costs in Australia and China, all ICERs were well within conventional WTP thresholds, even under extensive sensitivity testing. These findings highlight the robust economic value of MT for this patient group.

The interpretation of our cost‐effectiveness findings necessitates careful consideration of economic context. Although our base‐case ICERs remained well below the $20 000/QALY threshold across all countries, the perceived “acceptability” of these values is inherently linked to country‐specific WTP benchmarks and health care priorities. As highlighted by World Health Organization guidelines suggesting thresholds of 1–3 times gross domestic product per capita, our ICERs are robustly cost effective across diverse economic settings. Furthermore, the observed variation in incremental costs across the 11 countries reflects the complex interplay of health care system factors, including differing hospital financing models, labor costs, material pricing, and variations in healthcare organization and infrastructure. These system‐specific factors underscore the need for nuanced interpretation and highlight the heterogeneous economic landscape for implementing MT globally.

Beyond individual patient costs, the successful implementation of expanded MT access for patients with large‐core stroke necessitates significant system‐level investments. This includes substantial upfront costs for infrastructure development, such as angiography suites and specialized critical care units, as well as workforce expansion through training and recruitment of specialized personnel. These capacity‐building costs are crucial for realizing the potential cost‐effectiveness we have demonstrated. Moreover, our findings should be viewed within the broader context of stroke care optimization. Although expanding MT is vital, achieving optimal stroke outcomes requires a comprehensive approach encompassing improvements across the entire stroke pathway, from prehospital care and thrombolysis rates to timely rehabilitation. Particularly in resource‐constrained settings beyond the scope of our study, these system‐level challenges and broader stroke care priorities become even more pronounced, requiring careful tailoring of implementation strategies to local contexts.

Our analysis extends previous cost‐effectiveness studies, which primarily focused on patients with favorable imaging profiles (higher ASPECTS), such as the work by Kunz et al., which assessed proximal occlusions within a similar framework. 11 Although these prior studies showed economic benefits, our results broaden the scope to include patients previously deemed ineligible for MT due to large core infarcts. Our multinational approach offers broader insights than single‐country studies by confirming MT's consistent cost‐effectiveness across diverse health care environments, in parallel with the previous findings regarding more proximal occlusions. 17

The implications for clinical practice and health policy are significant. Our results support broadening MT patient selection criteria to include those with low ASPECTS, in line with the recent positive randomized controlled trials showing clinical benefit in this group. This expansion necessitates adjustments within stroke systems to ensure timely MT access for all eligible patients. health care systems should incorporate these findings into treatment guidelines, resource allocation strategies, and service organization to maximize patient benefit and promote equitable access. Our analysis offers strong economic evidence for guideline committees to recommend MT for low ASPECTS patients.

Implementing expanded MT access poses economic and logistical challenges. Although expanding the indications for MT to include patients with large‐core stroke does involve substantial investment, our findings suggest the potential for long‐term cost‐effectiveness through reduced disability and associated care needs. Expanded MT programs require thoughtful planning around workforce development and infrastructure. Health care systems with varying funding models will need tailored strategies to ensure financial viability and equitable access.

The strengths of our study include its international scope, comprehensive sensitivity analyses, reliance on high‐quality randomized clinical trial data, and detailed economic modelling that integrates country‐specific cost inputs. Our analysis, although robust, is subject to certain limitations. The simplification of acute stroke recurrence probability within the 90‐day model phase, though implicitly captured by 90‐day mRS score, represents a necessary modeling choice. Furthermore, the health care cost data, although derived from the best available sources, are inherently estimates and may not fully capture the complexities and potential variability in real‐world hospital costs, particularly for the resource‐intensive care of patients with large core stroke. Clinically, it is essential to acknowledge the high mortality and morbidity associated with large core stroke, even with MT intervention. Although MT improves outcomes, a significant proportion of patients will still face severe disability or death, underscoring the continued importance of clinical judgment in patient selection and the need for realistic expectations regarding outcomes in this challenging population. Future research should prioritize prospective validation of cost estimates and explore the broader societal impact by incorporating indirect costs into analyses. Other potential limitations include assumptions in the same long‐term economic modelling, possible limits in generalizing findings to nonstudied health care systems, and variations in treatment protocols and outcomes across different settings. In addition, data collection of country‐specific cost inputs may not consider exactly the same scope and could lead to slight differences in comparisons.

The extrapolation of lifetime costs and health outcomes from 90‐day endpoints introduces inherent methodological uncertainty, 18 as the trajectory of functional recovery and rehabilitation effectiveness can significantly influence long‐term resource use patterns and health‐related quality of life. 19 , 20 , 21 Studies have shown that poststroke recovery patterns can extend well beyond the acute phase, with some patients experiencing long‐term functional improvements post intervention. 22

Our analysis, constrained to direct health care expenditures from a payer perspective, likely understates the comprehensive economic value of mechanical thrombectomy. 23 The exclusion of indirect costs—including productivity losses from disability and informal caregiver burden—omits substantial societal impacts. 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 Research indicates that indirect costs can represent >60% of total stroke‐related economic burden. 28 Integration of these productivity losses would presumably generate more favorable incremental cost‐effectiveness ratios.

In conclusion, this analysis provides strong evidence supporting the cost‐effectiveness of MT for patients with large core stroke across a range of health care settings. These findings offer a robust economic rationale for policymakers, guideline developers, and health care systems to expand MT access, enabling more patients to benefit from this established intervention. Although this expansion requires thoughtful resource allocation and reorganization within stroke systems, the potential for long‐term cost‐effectiveness combined with meaningful improvements in patient outcomes and quality of life makes this investment worthwhile.

CONCLUSION

Mechanical thrombectomy for patients with large core strokes secondary to large‐vessel occlusion is cost effective across diverse healthcare systems, supporting expanded access to improve patient outcomes and quality of life. This necessitates adjustments in stroke systems and resource allocation to ensure timely and equitable delivery of this beneficial intervention.
