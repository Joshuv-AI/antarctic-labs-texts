PPISHES—an enhanced physicochemical approach for predicting protein interaction sites using graph neural networks
Authors: Darpan Khanna, Abdullah Abdul Sattar Shaikh, Luis Rueda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate prediction of protein interaction sites is crucial for understanding biological processes. Many existing methods capture structural, evolutionary, and sequence features; however, they overlook important physicochemical properties, limiting their performance. We propose Prediction of protein interaction sites based on solvent accessible surface area, hydrogen‐bonding propensity, and electrostatic potential sites, an enhanced model that incorporates the three physicochemical features to improve site prediction for both obligate and non‐obligate complexes. Feature ablation and other analyses identified key features to improve model performance. The model achieved up to 42.8% and 29.3% improvements in Area Under the Precision‐Recall Curve for Test_315 and Test_71, respectively. The model also outperforms current state‐of‐the‐art methods across other key metrics, such as Recall, Area Under the Curve, and Matthews Correlation Coefficient.Keywords: electrostatic potential, hydrogen‐bonding propensity, physicochemical features, protein interaction site prediction, residual graph convolutional network, solvent‐accessible surface area

INTRODUCTION

Prediction of protein–protein interaction (PPI) sites is crucial for understanding interaction mechanisms, constructing PPI networks, and understanding protein functions (De Las Rivas et al., 2012). Experimental identification of PPI complexes and the relevant sites is often challenging, costly, and time‐consuming, highlighting the need for efficient computational methods. On the other hand, PPIs can be categorized into obligate and non‐obligate complexes. Obligate complexes, or obligomers (Julian Mintseris & Weng, 2005), involve protomers that are structurally and functionally dependent, unable to exist independently. In contrast, non‐obligate complexes are transient and stable as independent structures outside interactions.

Computational methods for PPI site prediction include sequence‐based approaches, which are less accurate, and structure‐based approaches, which leverage resolved protein structures for higher accuracy. However, structural determination remains experimentally challenging, with only about 50% of Protein Data Bank (PDB) (Berman, 2000) entries representing protein complexes.

Over the past two decades, many traditional techniques were employed to predict PPI sites. Experimental approaches such as (Murakami & Mizuguchi, 2010; Neuvirth et al., 2004) and more methods mentioned in (Zhong et al., n.d.) have laid the foundations for PPI site prediction, although factors such as high cost and time consumption pose some limitations. Several algorithms have been developed for protein structure prediction, evolving from early models like DELPHI (Li et al., 2021), which combines convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and recurrent neural networks (RNNs), to more advanced approaches. Later models integrated contextual and global features (DeepPPISP (Zeng et al., 2020)), employed graph‐based classification (GraphPPIS (Yuan et al., 2022)), and utilized hybrid architectures (HN‐PPISP (Kang et al., 2023)). Geometric learning techniques (ScanNet (Tubiana et al., 2022)) and ensemble strategies (EDLMPPI (Hou et al., 2023)) further improved prediction accuracy. More recent approaches, including GNNGL‐PPI (Zeng et al., 2024), which leverage global graphs and local subgraphs, and DSSGNN‐PPI (Zhang et al., 2024), employ Double Structure and Sequence graph neural networks and have significantly advanced protein representation and predictive performance.

GraphPPIS focuses on global features, although it lacks local spatial detail. RGCNPPIS (Zhong et al., n.d.) addresses this by integrating seven node features and using GraphSAGE (Hamilton et al., 2017) for enhanced local feature extraction. Residual connections fuse initial node representations, local features, and previous GCN layers, improving information flow and mitigating over‐smoothing. Although the model effectively captures local, global, evolutionary, and sequence‐based features, it overlooks all physicochemical properties for protein interactions. Furthermore, it does not deal with feature redundancy and does not clearly prioritize or evaluate the importance of individual features, making it difficult to optimize its performance effectively.

Transformer‐based architectures and large language models (LLMs) are enhancing PPI site prediction by improving feature representation and generalizability. TransformerGO (Ieremie et al., 2022) captures semantic similarities between Gene Ontology annotations, while TUnA (Ko et al., 2024) integrates ESM‐2 embeddings with uncertainty quantification for more reliable predictions. PLM‐Interact (Liu et al., 2024) extends protein language models to PPI prediction, outperforming previous methods. Hybrid approaches like KSGPPI (Hu et al., 2024) and xCAPT5 (Thanh Hai Dang & Tien Anh Vu, 2024) combine ESM‐2 embeddings with CNNs and T5‐XL‐UniRef50 for richer amino acid embeddings. Additionally, ProLLM, a protein‐specific LLM, utilizes a chain‐of‐thought reasoning approach to improve the interpretability and accuracy of PPI predictions (Jin et al., 2024).

Lastly, while AlphaFold‐Multimer (Evans et al., 2021) excels in predicting the global structure of protein complexes, it does not explicitly focus on identifying interaction sites at the residue level or the specific features governing these interactions, but rather predicting the interaction of protein subunits as a whole. As models continue to evolve, integrating Transformer‐based architectures with graph‐based learning approaches could provide a more robust framework for PPI site prediction by capturing both local and global structural information, reducing redundancy, and improving interpretability.

PROPOSED METHOD

We propose an enhanced model, prediction of protein interaction sites based on solvent accessible surface area, hydrogen‐bonding propensity, and electrostatic potential sites (PPISHES) for structure‐based PPI site prediction, aimed at improving prediction accuracy by addressing limitations in RGCNPPIS. Our research integrates these three new features to enhance feature representation and better capture protein interactions.

This relationship provides insights into the electrostatic forces that govern PPIs.

Hydrogen bonding propensity

Hydrogen‐bonding is a shared non‐covalent interaction between H‐donor and H‐acceptor. A large amount of structural information for hydrogen‐bond interactions can be used in a statistical model to provide valuable insights about existing hydrogen‐bond interactions. The hydrogen‐bond propensity (HBP) is the probability of formation of an interaction based on the defined functional groups and fitting the data accordingly (Macrae et al., 2008). HBP quantifies the likelihood of hydrogen bond formation between donor and acceptor atoms in proteins (Macrae et al., 2008).

Solvent accessible surface area

Solvent accessible surface area (SASA) is the atomic surface area of a molecule accessible to a solvent (Liu & Hu, 2011). Usually expressed in square Ångstroms (Å2), constitutes an important structural parameter that measures a protein residue exposure or a buried status of its residues. For a given protein's three‐dimensional structure, the SASA is typically computed by rolling a probe sphere over the protein's van der Waals surface (Sanner et al., 1996).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

An enhanced model of PPI site prediction is presented here, which takes into consideration the graph representation of structural features and the most relevant physicochemical properties of proteins. Our model, PPISHES, extends previous work by incorporating all major physicochemical features, such as electrostatic potential, hydrogen‐bonding propensity, and SASA, within a graph‐based learning framework. We use GCN and GraphSAGE modules to integrate global and local structural information of proteins to create robust feature representations for PPI site prediction.

Datasets

In this study, we use the main three benchmark datasets used by Zhong et al. (n.d.): Dset_186, Dset_72 (Murakami & Mizuguchi, 2010), and Dset_164 (Dhole et al., 2014), named after their protein counts. Dset_186 was created from known protein–protein complexes in PDB and refined through six filtering steps, including removing structures with over 30% missing residues, identical UniprotKB/SwissProt accessions, transmembrane proteins, oligomeric structures, and proteins with low surface accessibility or interface polarity (Yuan et al., 2022). Dset_72 was based on the protein–protein docking benchmark set (Hwang et al., 2008), and Dset_164 was constructed from newly annotated protein complexes in PDB (June 2010–November 2013). Both Dset_72 and Dset_164 underwent the same filtering criteria as Dset_186. Eventually, these datasets were filtered using BLASTCLUST (Altschul & Altschul, 1990) with thresholds of resolution<3 Å, sequence similarity>5%, sequence homology<25%, and overlap>30%.

We constructed Train_324 by combining 169 chains from Dset_186 and 155 chains from Dset_164. For testing, we used two independent sets: Test_71 (71 chains from Dset_72) and Test_315 (315 chains from PDB complexes). Performance differences between Test_71 and Test_315 reflect their biological distinctions—Test_71 contains transient complexes with smaller interfaces, while Test_315 comprises obligate complexes with larger, stable binding sites.

This dataset split ensures temporal separation (Test_315 contains newer structures) and functional diversity (Test_71 focuses on docking scenarios), providing comprehensive evaluation of model performance across different complex types and time periods.

The more intense the red, the stronger the electrostatic potential. Distinct patches of deep red suggest regions with significantly high potential, which may play a key role in molecular interactions. The long‐range nature of electrostatic interactions results in a large interface area. It is a one‐dimensional feature vector in this EP setup; we use software packages PDB2PQR v3.7.1 (Pdb2pqr documentation, n.d.) and APBS v3.4.1 (Apbs documentation, n.d.) to calculate electrostatic energies. PDB2PQR is a tool that automates common tasks for preparing structures for electrostatic calculations. Its main purpose is to add missing heavy atoms, place missing hydrogen atoms, and assign charges and radii to PDB files. The output of this package is a PQR structure file, which is the input for APBS. APBS is a software package that is used to solve the Poisson‐Boltzmann equation numerically and evaluate electrostatic calculations (Vasudev et al., 2012). The Poisson‐Boltzmann equation is solved using the linear form with boundary conditions. The model incorporates ions with charges of +1 and −1, each with a concentration of 0.150 M and respective radii. The dielectric constants for the solute and solvent are set, and the surface model has a molecular structure. Additional parameters include the solvent radius, surface density, and temperature. Finally, outputs such as potential, charge density, and kappa are written in the files for further analysis. These electrostatic properties provide critical insights for feature engineering in molecular studies.

Hydrogen‐bonding propensity

Hydrogen bonding is a one‐dimensional feature vector and is critical for maintaining protein structure and stability. In our model, we calculate hydrogen‐bonding propensity by identifying donor‐acceptor atoms for each amino acid residue. The donors and acceptors are defined based on known chemical properties, such as the nitrogen atoms in arginine (ARG) acting as donors and the oxygen atoms in asparagine (ASN) serving as acceptors. The calculation proceeds by evaluating the distance between donor and acceptor atoms within the protein structure, using a threshold of 3.5 Å. If the distance between a donor and an acceptor is below this threshold, a hydrogen bond is considered to form. The number of hydrogen bonds formed by each residue is then recorded, providing a quantitative measure of hydrogen‐bonding propensity. This feature is critical for modeling protein stability and interactions, particularly in the prediction of PPI sites. By incorporating HBP, the model captures local structural information that enhances its ability to predict interaction sites and assess the effects of mutations on protein stability.

Solvent accessible surface area

SASA is a crucial physicochemical property that reflects how much amino acid residues are exposed to the solvent. It is a one‐dimensional feature vector and its values are calculated at the atomic level and aggregated for each residue. The result is normalized using predefined maximum SASA values for each amino acid, producing relative solvent accessibility (RSA). RSA values range between 0 and 1, where 1 indicates maximum exposure to the solvent and 0 means the residue is fully buried. Residues with unknown maximum SASA values are assigned an RSA of 0. This feature provides insights into the exposure of residues, which is critical for understanding protein stability, interaction potential, and PPI site prediction. By including SASA in the model, we capture important structural information that contributes to the accuracy of predictions of protein behavior in various molecular environments. Graph construction

A protein is modeled as an undirected graph G=V,E, where V represents amino acids and E represents the distances between pairs of amino acids. The protein graph G is defined using an initial node representation matrix Hinit∈ℝn×m and an adjacency matrix A∈ℝn×n, where n is the length of the protein chain and m=72, the number of features.

Initial node representation: Each amino acid node is represented by a 72‐dimensional feature vector combining 10 distinct feature types, all normalized to a [0,1] scale for model consistency. Evolutionary features include a 20‐dimensional position‐specific scoring matrix (PSSM) and 20‐dimensional hidden Markov model profiles (Zhong et al., n.d.). Structural features comprise 14‐dimensional one‐hot encoded DSSP secondary structure states and 11‐dimensional PSAIA interface descriptors. Physicochemical properties incorporate SASA, EP, and HBP as one‐dimensional features. Sequence‐based elements include one‐dimensional ProtVec1D embeddings (Li et al., 2021) and relative amino acid propensity (Vacic et al., 2007), complemented by one‐dimensional functional annotations for disordered binding regions and anchor binding scores (Mészáros et al., 2018). The complete representation concatenates these features in fixed order after ensuring all values fall within the [0,1] range through method‐specific normalization protocols.

Model validation

To ensure optimal performance, the trained model was saved at key epochs based on cross‐validation results. During testing, an independent dataset was used to evaluate the generalization power of the model. Predictions were generated using a Softmax function and compared with true labels to compute metrics such as binary accuracy, precision, recall, Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), F1 score, area under the curve (AUC), and area under the precision‐recall curve (AUPRC). The decision threshold was optimized to maximize the F1 score, ensuring reliable predictions.

We adopted a five‐fold cross‐validation strategy, dividing the dataset into five subsets, training on 80% and validating on 20% per fold. This approach balances performance consistency and computational efficiency and was chosen based on previous research demonstrating its effectiveness for similar tasks. For each fold, the best epoch, AUC, and AUPRC were recorded, and the best‐performing model was reloaded for subsequent folds.

In post‐validation, the average AUC, AUPRC, and best epoch were calculated to summarize generalization performance. Finally, the model was trained on the entire dataset, with key metrics such as loss, accuracy, AUC, and AUPRC tracked across epochs.

EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS

We configured our model architecture and hyperparameters through systematic optimization to ensure robust performance and generalizability. The final model employs an 8‐layer graph network with a 256‐dimensional hidden state, a configuration selected from comprehensive searches (layers: 4, 6, 8, 10, 12; hidden dimension: 64, 128, 192, 256, 320, 384, 512) to balance representational capacity and computational efficiency. Key hyperparameters include a 14 Å cutoff distance for contact maps (optimized from 10 to 20 Å), a dropout rate of 0.1 (tested from 0.0–0.5), an alpha value of 0.7 (tuned from 0.5 to 0.9), and a learning rate of 0.001 (selected from 1e‐4 to 1e‐2). Input features consisted of either 34‐dimensional BLOSUM62 or 72‐dimensional evolutionary embeddings. To prevent overfitting in this high‐dimensional feature space, we incorporated strong regularization strategies including L2 weight decay and dropout. The model's high accuracy and AUPRC on both independent test sets, Test_71 and Test_315, confirms its robustness and effective learning without significant feature redundancy or overfitting.

The model was designed for binary classification, distinguishing between binding and non‐binding sites, and trained over 50 epochs. Experiments were conducted on Kaggle using a stable long‐term support version, leveraging an Nvidia GeForce RTX 1650 GPU for efficient computation.

Evaluation metrics

PPI site prediction is characterized by a significant class imbalance, where non‐binding residues vastly outnumber binding sites. Our test sets reflect this challenge: Test_71 contains 1635 positive residues (binding sites) versus 12,635 negative residues (non‐binding sites), resulting in a positive‐to‐negative ratio of 1:7.7. Similarly, Test_315 shows 8378 positive residues against 50,490 negative residues, yielding a ratio of 1:6.0. This substantial imbalance underscores the importance of using robust evaluation metrics like AUPRC and recall, which are less sensitive to class distribution than accuracy alone.

We evaluated our model using several performance metrics, including accuracy, AUC, precision, recall, MCC, AUPRC, and F1 score. Accuracy represents the proportion of correctly identified samples out of all samples. Recall describes the proportion of correctly identified positive samples. Precision represents the probability of correct prediction among all predicted positives. MCC takes into account true positive (TP), true negative (TN), false positive (FP), and false negative (FN), making it a more balanced evaluation metric. AUC and AUPRC are threshold‐independent metrics that provide an overall performance measure for a given method.

Biological insight on saliency

Saliency analysis measures the contribution of each feature to the model's predictions. We also conducted experiments on Test_71 by normalizing saliency on each feature across all amino acids. A higher saliency score indicates greater influence on the model's decision‐making process. As shown in Figure 3, the pro2Vec_feature exhibits the highest normalized saliency, highlighting its crucial role in the model's predictive capabilities. This suggests that it provides rich sequence‐based information that the model heavily relies on. Following closely, the psaia_feature, hmm_feature, and pssm_feature features also demonstrate high saliency values. These features likely capture essential structural or evolutionary patterns, further contributing to the model's ability to accurately predict protein interaction sites. Features such as EP and RAA_feature show moderate importance, indicating that while they are useful, they are not as central to the model's decision‐making as the top features. The dssp_feature, anchor_feature, SASA, and HBP features have lower saliency scores. Though they contribute less compared to the top‐ranking features, they still provide complementary information that supports model performance. Their lower influence may be attributed to the nature of the dataset or the specific interaction sites being modeled. For instance, features like SASA and HBP may have weaker direct correlations with interaction site prediction but still hold biological relevance.

We evaluated the model's performance across different metrics to compare its effectiveness in predicting interaction sites in obligate and non‐obligate protein complexes (Ray & Bera, 2009). As illustrated in Figure 5, the model shows a slightly higher accuracy on non‐obligate sites (0.92) compared to obligate sites (0.91), suggesting that the model identifies more non‐obligate instances overall. This may be due to a class imbalance in the dataset, with a greater number of non‐obligate examples influencing the model's learning process.

However, a shift is observed in the precision, recall, and F1 score, where the model performs better on obligate sites (0.81, 0.75, and 0.75, respectively) than on non‐obligate ones (0.77, 0.71, and 0.72). The lower precision and recall for non‐obligate sites suggest the model struggles to consistently capture true interaction sites in this category, despite identifying non‐obligate regions more frequently.

Feature ablation study

Further, we carried out an ablation study to investigate the relevance of each feature through AUPRC values derived by individually masking various features. The contribution of each feature is investigated by masking one feature at a time and observing the effect that this has on the model's performance. It follows from Figure 4 and Table 1 (refer to Supplementary Material) that masking the EP feature results in the highest AUPRC of 0.5753, increasing by 0.0756 compared with the average. AUPRC evaluates the model's performance in terms of precision and recall for the binding sites. This tells us how well the model can identify positive cases when the data is imbalanced. The higher the AUPRC, the better the model is at distinguishing the binding sites from non‐binding sites. The minimal performance impact when excluding this feature suggests it contributes limited predictive value to the model, indicating potential redundancy in the feature set. Similarly, masking the RAA feature and using the baseline model (no features masked) resulted in AUPRC values of 0.5656 and 0.5650, respectively, which are modest increases from the average and further suggest that these features are relatively unimportant. In contrast, masking Anchor, HBP, and Pro2Vec results in a moderate decrease in AUPRC. These drops of around 0.0643, 0.0644, and 0.0608 respectively, from the average represent that while these features are informative to a small extent, they are not critical. In contrast, much larger decreases in AUPRC are observed when masking PSAIA, PSSM, and DSSP features, with AUPRC values dropping to 0.5383, 0.5323, and 0.2761, respectively, which suggests that these features are much more important for the model's performance. Finally, masking SASA led to the lowest AUPRC of 0.2039, marking a significant decrease of 0.2958 from the average, suggesting that this feature is one of the most critical for achieving high model accuracy and predictive capability. This particular behavior, namely that —SASA and RAA showed low saliency and negative correlation in linear analysis, proved to be critical in ablation testing—highlights their non‐linear and contextual importance within the graph neural network. The substantial performance drop when masking SASA suggests these features contribute through complex interactions that linear correlation measures cannot capture, emphasizing the limitations of traditional feature importance metrics when interpreting GCN models. In a similar fashion, we repeated the masking process in such a way that only one feature was unmasked, yielding some important observations (refer to Table 2 and Figure 2, Supplementary Material). As part of the detailed analysis, a correlation analysis has been conducted, with the discussion provided (refer to Figure 1, Supplementary Material).

AUPRC values are calculated by removing individual features from the initial node representation on Test_71, highlighting the impact of each feature on model performance and identifying key features for maintaining accuracy.

CONCLUSION

This study introduces PPISHES, a novel protein interaction site prediction model that integrates electrostatic potential, hydrogen‐bonding propensity, and SASA into a GCN framework. By incorporating these physicochemical features, PPISHES outperforms existing methods, achieving superior predictive accuracy, sensitivity, and precision‐recall performance on benchmark datasets. Feature ablation analysis confirms the importance of these properties in model optimization and interpretability. PPISHES sets a new state‐of‐the‐art, excelling in predicting both obligate and non‐obligate interaction sites. While obligate complexes provide stable structures with fewer false positives, non‐obligate complexes achieve higher accuracy but lower precision and recall. Future work should focus on incorporating dynamic structural features to improve predictions for transient interactions. In conclusion, PPISHES represents a significant advance in protein interaction site prediction, offering unmatched accuracy and interpretability, and establishing a new benchmark for the field.
