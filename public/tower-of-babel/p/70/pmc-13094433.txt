Baseline characteristics, perioperative complications, and 1-year outcomes in cervical OPLL versus cervical spondylotic myelopathy: a multicenter retrospective cohort study
Authors: Yasushi Oshima, Toru Doi, Hideki Nakamoto, Hiroyuki Nakarai, Yusuke Sato, Katsuyuki Sasaki, Naohiro Tachibana, Fumihiko Oguchi, Fumiko Saiki, Masayoshi Fukushima, Chiaki Horii, Naoki Okamoto, Shima Hirai, Masahito Oshina, Junya Miyahara, Kazuhiro Masuda, So Kato, Yuki Taniguchi, Sakae Tanaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCervical ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL) is an important cause of degenerative cervical myelopathy. Unlike cervical spondylotic myelopathy (CSM), OPLL has been increasingly recognized as a distinct phenotype with potential systemic metabolic and mineral-regulatory associations. The purpose of this study was to compare baseline characteristics, perioperative complications, and patient-reported outcomes (PROs) between cervical OPLL and CSM.MethodsThis retrospective multicenter cohort study included patients undergoing surgery for cervical myelopathy due to OPLL or CSM at 13 high-volume spine centers. Baseline characteristics, comorbidities, serum phosphate levels, perioperative complications, and outcomes were assessed. PROs comprised numerical rating scales for neck and arm pain, the Neck Disability Index, the EuroQol 5-Dimension, the 12-Item Short Form Survey, and the Core Outcome Measures Index. Outcomes were evaluated preoperatively and at 1 year. Inverse probability of treatment weighting was used to adjust for baseline differences.ResultsA total of 1,824 patients were included (OPLL, n = 317; CSM, n = 1,507). Patients with OPLL were younger, more frequently male, and had higher body mass index. Diabetes was more prevalent and serum phosphate levels were lower in OPLL, and these differences persisted after IPW adjustment. Crude perioperative neurologic complications were more frequent in OPLL, but the difference was attenuated after weighting. Postoperative PROs at 1 year did not differ significantly between groups.ConclusionsCervical OPLL and CSM demonstrate distinct baseline profiles, particularly with respect to metabolic comorbidities, body habitus, and serum phosphate levels. Although crude perioperative complication rates were higher in OPLL, these differences were largely explained by baseline characteristics after adjustment.Keywords: OPLL, CSM, Degenerative cervical myelopathy, Diabetes mellitus, Body mass index, Phosphate, Inverse probability weighting, Complications, Patient-reported outcomes

Introduction

Degenerative cervical myelopathy (DCM) is the leading cause of nontraumatic spinal cord dysfunction in adults and encompasses etiologies that converge on chronic cervical cord compression and progressive neurologic decline [1]. Cervical spondylotic myelopathy (CSM) is common worldwide, whereas ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL) is particularly prevalent in East Asian populations and is increasingly recognized globally with wider use of computed tomography (CT) [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. Although both disorders cause myelopathy through static and dynamic compromise of the spinal canal, patients with OPLL often show a distinct baseline phenotype compared with those with CSM, including male predominance, higher body mass index (BMI), and a higher prevalence of metabolic comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. These factors may influence perioperative risk through microvascular dysfunction, impaired wound healing, and technical challenges related to exposure and instrumentation [[10], [11], [12]]. Beyond cardiometabolic traits, emerging data implicate mineral-regulatory pathways in OPLL: elevated fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23) with lower serum inorganic phosphate (Pi) has been reported, suggesting that endocrine phosphate regulation may be relevant [13,14].

Prior comparisons have reported broadly similar postoperative recovery between OPLL and CSM/other DCM etiologies, whereas perioperative complication rates may appear higher in OPLL; however, interpretation is complicated by systematic baseline differences and variation in surgical approach [12,[15], [16], [17]]. Therefore, using a large multicenter cohort of surgically treated DCM, we compared baseline characteristics between cervical OPLL and CSM, with particular attention to metabolic factors and serum phosphate. We further described perioperative complications and patient-reported outcomes assessed preoperatively and at 1 year and examined whether between-group differences attenuated after inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPW) to adjust for baseline differences.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study compared patients undergoing surgery for cervical myelopathy due to OPLL versus CSM. We used prospectively collected registry data from 2,916 patients treated at 13 high-volume spine centers in the Greater Tokyo metropolitan area between April 2017 and March 2022. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Clinical Research Support Center at the authors’ institution (IRB No. 10335).

Eligible patients were adults (≥18 years) who underwent elective cervical spine surgery for cervical myelopathy due to CSM or OPLL. Patients with radiculopathy without myelopathy, cervical disc herniation, trauma, tumors, infection, or hardware removal were excluded. After applying these criteria, a total of 1,824 patients were enrolled. The decision to proceed with surgery was based on the judgment of spine specialists at each participating center. Diagnostic classification was based on standard radiographic criteria, and OPLL was confirmed by CT. Surgical procedure was categorized as laminoplasty, laminectomy with fusion, anterior decompression and fusion, and combined approaches.

Clinical data included age, sex, BMI, comorbidities (diabetes, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia), smoking status, and serum calcium and phosphate levels. Postoperative complications occurring within 30 days were documented by the treating spine surgeons using standardized charting. Patient-reported outcomes were assessed preoperatively and at 1 year postoperatively using numerical rating scales for neck pain or arm pain, the EuroQol 5-Dimension, the 12-Item Short Form Survey, and the Core Outcome Measures Index.

Statistical analysis

We compared unadjusted baseline characteristics and outcomes between groups. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Variables demonstrating approximate normal distribution were analyzed using Student’s t tests, whereas non-normally distributed variables were evaluated using appropriate nonparametric tests (e.g., Mann-Whitney U test). To address baseline differences, we applied inverse IPW based on propensity scores to balance observed covariates between the OPLL and CSM cohorts. Stabilized weights were derived from propensity scores estimated using logistic regression. Propensity scores were estimated separately for baseline description (Table 1: age, sex, BMI, and surgical procedure) and for perioperative complication and patient-reported outcome analyzes (Table 2, Table 3: age, sex, BMI, surgical procedure, diabetes, and smoking). Covariates were selected a priori based on established clinical relevance and prior literature identifying these factors as potential confounders in cervical spine surgery outcomes. Age and sex were included as fundamental demographic variables; BMI and diabetes were selected given their established associations with metabolic comorbidity and surgical risk; and surgical procedure was included to account for differences in operative complexity that may influence complication rates. Additional adjustment for smoking was incorporated in outcome analyzes given its known association with perioperative morbidity.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Among 1,824 patients (OPLL, n = 317; CSM, n = 1,507), patients with OPLL were younger, were more likely to be male, and had a higher BMI than those with CSM. Operative time and intraoperative blood loss were greater in the OPLL group. OPLL was also associated with a higher prevalence of diabetes and lower serum phosphate (Pi) levels. After IPW adjustment for age, sex, BMI, and surgical procedure, differences in diabetes prevalence and serum phosphate remained statistically significant (Table 1). Prespecified sensitivity and robustness analyzes for laboratory variables, including weight truncation and doubly robust modeling, yielded consistent effect estimates, indicating stability across alternative model specifications (Supplementary Tables S1–S3).

Perioperative complications

In unadjusted comparisons, perioperative complication rates, including neurologic complications, were higher in the OPLL group. After IPW adjustment for age, sex, BMI, surgical procedure, diabetes, and smoking, between-group differences in complication rates were attenuated, suggesting that the higher unadjusted risk is at least partly attributable to baseline differences and correlated operative factors rather than OPLL diagnosis alone (Table 2). Given that laminoplasty comprised 76% of procedures overall, statistical comparisons of complication rates across individual surgical techniques were likely underpowered.

Patient-reported outcomes

Table 3 compares patient-reported outcomes between the OPLL and CSM groups. Preoperative outcomes did not differ significantly between groups. One-year postoperative outcomes were available for 1,342 patients (73.6%). Follow-up completeness was similar between groups (73.8% for OPLL and 73.5% for CSM). In unadjusted analyzes, postoperative outcomes did not differ significantly between groups. After IPW adjustment for age, sex, BMI, surgical procedure, diabetes, and smoking, no significant between-group differences were observed in postoperative outcomes (Fig. 1).

Discussion

This study demonstrates 3 clinically relevant points regarding the comparison of cervical OPLL and CSM in a contemporary surgical cohort.

First, OPLL was associated with a distinct baseline phenotype characterized by higher BMI and a higher prevalence of diabetes mellitus. This clustering of OPLL with metabolic comorbidity has been reported across multiple study designs [[4], [5], [6], [7]], including genetic analyzes demonstrating a positive genetic correlation between BMI and OPLL risk [[18], [19], [20]]. These observations are consistent with the possibility that OPLL may reflect not only a localized degenerative process but also a broader systemic susceptibility in which inherited and metabolic factors interact. Genetic and molecular studies of ectopic ossification pathways, including regulators of mineralization and pyrophosphate metabolism, have suggested biologic mechanisms that may contribute to ligament ossification in subsets of patients with OPLL [19,[21], [22], [23]]. Classic clinical work also linked glucose metabolism to ossification burden; Akune and colleagues reported that indices of insulin secretion and related measures of glucose metabolism correlated with the extent of OPLL [18]. In addition, several studies have suggested that patients with OPLL may exhibit higher bone mass and/or altered bone quality [11,14], potentially reflecting a systemic osteogenic tendency rather than a purely focal process.

Importantly, our study does not directly evaluate genetic or molecular mechanisms; therefore, these references provide context rather than evidence of causality in the present cohort.

Second, OPLL surgery is often perceived as higher risk, because of severe anterior compression, dural adhesion, and multilevel involvement [24,25]. Prior comparative studies support this concern in crude analyzes, yet also suggest that outcomes depend on baseline profiles and surgical approach. For example, in a large multicenter cohort limited to posterior decompression, Nori and colleagues studied 814 patients (CSM, n = 636; OPLL, n = 178) and reported comparable perioperative complication rates after propensity-score matching (98 matched pairs) [17]. Similarly, in the prospective AOSpine CSM-International study (OPLL, n = 135; other forms of DCM, n = 344), perioperative complications tended to be more frequent in OPLL, whereas neurologic complication rates did not differ significantly between groups [16].

In our cohort, perioperative complication rates were higher in the OPLL group in unadjusted analyzes, but the between-group differences were substantially attenuated after IPW adjustment. Importantly, our inference regarding perioperative risk is based on the IPW-adjusted analzses rather than the unadjusted comparisons. This pattern suggests that the observed excess risk is at least partly attributable to differences in baseline characteristics and operative factors rather than the OPLL diagnosis itself. Accordingly, perioperative risk reduction in OPLL may benefit from optimization of modifiable comorbidities (e.g., glycemic control and weight-related factors where feasible), careful preoperative planning of surgical strategy, and heightened intraoperative attention to technically demanding steps, rather than assuming that diagnosis alone dictates unavoidable excess risk.

Taken together, these results suggest that the higher crude complication burden in OPLL is driven largely by the phenotype and technical demands that accompany OPLL, rather than the diagnosis itself—supporting risk mitigation strategies centered on comorbidity optimization and procedurespecific planning.

Third, lower serum phosphate in OPLL may represent a biologically informative signal consistent with phosphate/pyrophosphate dysregulation in a subset of patients. While BMI and diabetes are clinically actionable factors, the phosphate finding is mechanistically intriguing, particularly because it persisted after adjustment in our cohort. Emerging genetic and molecular work increasingly frames OPLL as a disorder influenced by osteogenic and mineralization pathways, including Wnt signaling and genes implicated in ectopic ossification [26]. Within this framework, phosphate and pyrophosphate biology is especially relevant: ENPP1 is central to extracellular pyrophosphate generation, and ENPP1-related pathways have been linked to ectopic calcification phenotypes [23,27,28].

Clinical observations also support the plausibility of phosphate-related mechanisms; for example, OPLL has been described in hypophosphatemic disorders (including rickets/osteomalacia) in which FGF23-mediated phosphate handling is abnormal [29]. At the same time, we do not interpret our findings to mean that low phosphate “causes” OPLL in all patients. Rather, these data are consistent with a model in which altered phosphate and pyrophosphate homeostasis characterizes a subset of OPLL and may interact with metabolic comorbidities and mechanical stress to promote ectopic ossification [13,23].

However, our findings represent a clinical association rather than mechanistic proof. These data may be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and warrant further investigation integrating metabolic and molecular approaches.

The key strength of this study is the large OPLL sample size; to our knowledge, it represents 1 of the largest cohorts used for a direct comparison of baseline characteristics and outcomes between OPLL and CSM with risk adjustment. Large sample size is particularly valuable in OPLL research, where heterogeneity in morphology, extent, and surgical approach complicates inference, and where complications are relatively infrequent events that require scale for stable estimation.

On the other hand, several limitations warrant emphasis. First, the retrospective observational design limits causal inference; even with IPW, residual confounding from unmeasured variables may remain. In particular, detailed radiographic severity parameters—such as canal-occupying ratio, number of involved levels, OPLL morphology, cord signal changes on MRI, and standardized neurologic severity grading—were not uniformly available across the 13 participating centers and study period. Because of this heterogeneity, incorporation of these variables into the IPW model was not feasible without introducing substantial selection bias. Accordingly, the present study should be interpreted as a large-scale baseline phenotyping and risk-adjusted comparative analysis rather than a severity-stratified outcome study. Although detailed radiographic severity metrics were unavailable, operative time and intraoperative blood loss remained significantly greater in OPLL even after weighting, indirectly suggesting increased technical complexity associated with this diagnosis. Second, laboratory variables, particularly serum phosphate and calcium, were available only in a subset of patients. Although prespecified sensitivity analyzes yielded findings consistent with the primary results, the possibility of selection related to laboratory testing cannot be excluded. Laboratory evaluation may have been influenced by institutional practice patterns or patient comorbidity profiles, which could introduce unmeasured bias. Therefore, the phosphate findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of systemic mineral dysregulation in all patients with OPLL. Third, surgical strategy selection was not randomized; procedure choice (e.g., decompression alone vs. fusion) may differ between diagnoses and correlate with both baseline phenotype and outcomes. Nevertheless, future prospective multicenter studies incorporating harmonized imaging measures and standardized neurologic grading are warranted to refine risk stratification further. Fourth, generalizability may be limited by regional epidemiology and practice patterns, although whole-spine CT-based studies and international reviews indicate growing recognition of OPLL beyond East Asia [30]. Fifth, postoperative follow-up was limited to 1 year. Although 1-year PROs are commonly used and may capture early recovery [31], longer-term follow-up is needed to evaluate durability of symptom relief and functional improvement beyond the first postoperative year. Finally, while we focused on diabetes and BMI as key phenotype markers, other cardiometabolic factors (e.g., dyslipidemia and atherosclerosis burden) and bone/mineral metabolism–related factors (e.g., FGF23 and bone turnover markers) may also be relevant.

Conclusions

Patients with cervical OPLL differ from those with CSM in baseline characteristics, including higher BMI, greater prevalence of diabetes mellitus, and lower serum phosphate levels. Although crude perioperative complication rates were higher in OPLL, these differences were substantially attenuated after IPW adjustment, indicating that baseline characteristics and operative factors account for much of the apparent excess risk rather than the diagnosis itself. Lower serum phosphate may reflect underlying biological differences between OPLL and CSM and warrants further investigation.
