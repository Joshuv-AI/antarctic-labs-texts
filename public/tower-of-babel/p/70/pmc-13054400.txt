Postictal electroencephalographic suppression in generalized tonic-clonic seizures: clinical correlates and predictive factors from a cross-sectional observational study
Authors: Zahra Hamidiya, Amir Reza Bahadori, Hamed Amirifard, Ali Namazian, Abbas Tafakhori, Sara Ranji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights:•Gradual clonic termination was the strongest independent PGES predictor.•Secondary seizures linked to prolonged PGES and widespread cortical suppression.•Higher BMI linked to prolonged PGES and postictal respiratory risk.•PGES may help identify patients at elevated SUDEP risk.

Introduction

Postictal generalized electroencephalographic suppression (PGES) is a temporary flattening or near-complete absence of cortical electrical activity seen on EEG immediately after a generalized tonic–clonic seizure (GTCS) [1]. It is defined by a generalized EEG attenuation below 10 µV within 30 s of seizure end, typically lasting from a few seconds to over a minute, though occasionally longer [2]. PGES is most often seen after nocturnal GTCS and is commonly linked to postictal unresponsiveness and immobility [3]. Clinically, PGES is significant due to its proposed role as a biomarker for sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP). Studies suggest that prolonged PGES, especially over 50 s, may be associated with increased SUDEP risk [4], [5]. PGES has also been connected to periictal brainstem dysfunction, indicating transient impairment of arousal and cardiorespiratory centers [6], [7]. It often co-occurs with cardiorespiratory issues such as central apnea, bradycardia, and hypoventilation, reinforcing its clinical importance [8], [9].

Various risk factors for PGES have been reported, though none are universally confirmed. PGES is more likely in prolonged seizures—particularly when the tonic–clonic phase exceeds 60 s—in nocturnal seizures, and in patients with recurrent generalized seizures [10], [11]. Demographic and clinical associations include older age, use of multiple antiseizure medications, and extended postictal paresis [12]. Among seizure features, longer tonic phase duration is the most consistent predictor of PGES [10], [13]. However, PGES occurrence can vary even within the same patient, suggesting a multifactorial and heterogeneous pathophysiology [14].

Postictal generalized EEG suppression (PGES) has been described as an abrupt flattening of cortical activity immediately after a generalized tonic–clonic seizure (GTCS), with amplitude <10 μV [5], [15]. The mechanisms behind PGES remain debated. One leading theory involves activation of inhibitory reticulo-thalamo-cortical networks that help terminate seizures but also suppress cortical reactivation [6] Other models point to subcortical involvement, especially brainstem dysfunction affecting arousal and respiration [5], [16]. Animal studies support this, showing that spreading depolarization in the medulla can cause apnea followed by EEG suppression [17]. Some researchers propose a passive mechanism, where PGES reflects neuronal exhaustion and metabolic depletion after intense neuronal firing [18]. The consistent link between tonic phase duration and PGES, rather than total seizure length, supports the idea that active inhibitory processes are more critical than simple fatigue [13]. Overall, PGES likely results from complex interactions among cortical inhibition, brainstem dysfunction, and seizure-induced metabolic changes.

Given PGES’s potential as a SUDEP biomarker, identifying its predictors and correlates is a key clinical and research priority. This study aims to examine the clinical and electrophysiological factors associated with PGES in patients with GTCS, focusing on demographic variables (e.g., age, sex, comorbidities, smoking), seizure characteristics (e.g., duration, tonic and clonic phases, semiology), and periictal physiological changes (e.g., heart rate variability, apnea, postictal paresis). A clearer understanding of PGES risk factors may improve individualized seizure management and contribute to SUDEP prevention strategies.

Method and material

Study design and setting

This study used a cross-sectional observational design to assess associations between clinical characteristics and the occurrence of postictal generalized EEG suppression (PGES) in patients with GTCs. The design allowed for the evaluation of various clinical, electrographic, and physiological variables without altering exposures or outcomes. It was chosen due to the practical constraints of real-time seizure capture in the long-term video EEG monitoring (LTM) unit. The study was conducted at a regional hospital in Tehran, Iran from March 2023 to March 2024.

Participants and eligibility criteria

The study population included patients admitted for long-term video-EEG monitoring in whom at least one GTC was recorded. Eligible participants were required to be over nine years of age and provide informed consent, either directly or through a legal guardian in the case of minors. Patients were excluded if they had a known history of chronic kidney disease, uncontrolled hypertension, cardiac arrhythmias, structural cardiac abnormalities, or if they were pregnant at the time of admission.

Data collection and variables

Data were retrospectively extracted from medical records and EEG reports. Demographic variables included age, sex, weight, height, and body mass index (BMI). Clinical background such as comorbidities and smoking history was also recorded.

Seizure semiology was analyzed using EEG-video review, including aura, versive movements, focal impaired awareness with motor manifestations, focal impaired awareness seizure, motor sequencing, and end-jerking. Motor sequencing was defined as stereotyped sequential motor patterns observed during the ictal phase, while end–jerking referred to isolated, irregular jerks occurring after the main clonic phase had ceased, distinct from terminal cloni. Each generalized tonic–clonic seizure (GTCS) was classified as either a GTCS or a focal to bilateral tonic–clonic seizure (FBTCS), in accordance with ILAE terminology.

Seizure features included the duration of tonic and clonic phases, total seizure duration, and the type of clonic phase termination (gradual or abrupt). Clonic phase duration was measured from the first rhythmic jerk to the last, and seizure duration was measured from EEG onset to EEG offset of the generalized phase, excluding aura. Gradual termination was defined as a progressive reduction in clonic amplitude and frequency over ≥3 beats before cessation, whereas abrupt termination was defined as sudden cessation without tapering.

Heart rate variability (HRV) during the peri–ictal period was categorized as tachycardia or bradycardia based on ECG. Peri–ictal heart rate changes were defined from seizure onset until 2 min postictally. Tachycardia was HR > 100 bpm sustained for ≥10 s, and bradycardia was HR < 60 bpm sustained for ≥10 s.

The state of arousal at seizure onset (sleep or wakefulness) and the presence of postictal paresis (Todd’s paralysis) were also documented. Postictal generalized EEG suppression (PGES) was defined as generalized EEG suppression below 10 µV for at least one second within 30 s after seizure termination. We used a ≥1–second threshold to capture all suppression events, including brief episodes. Sensitivity analyses using a ≥10–second threshold were also performed to ensure robustness of findings.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 20 and SAS version 9.1. Continuous variables were expressed as means and standard deviations, while categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons between PGES-positive and PGES-negative patients were conducted using independent t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, depending on normality assumptions. Categorical variables were compared using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. Correlations between continuous variables were assessed using Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients.

To determine independent predictors of PGES, variables with a p-value < 0.10 in univariate analysis were entered into a multivariate logistic regression model. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Missing data were handled using listwise deletion.

Interrater reliability for PGES detection was assessed using Cohen’s kappa statistic (κ = 0.82), indicating substantial agreement between raters. Sensitivity analyses were additionally performed using a ≥ 10–second PGES threshold to ensure robustness of findings.

Ethics

This study has been registered in the office of the Institute of Board Review of Tehran University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran and approved by the Ethics Committee of Research under the number: IR.TUMS.IKHC.REC.1403.033. All patients provided written informed consent prior to participation. Patient anonymity and data confidentiality were strictly maintained throughout the study, and participants were assured that refusal or withdrawal from the study would not affect their clinical care.

Result

Demographic characteristics of the patients

A total of 142 patients with generalized tonic–clonic seizures (GTCs) were included. The mean age of the cohort was 32.0 years, with an age range of 9–60 years. Of these, 22 patients (15.5%) were younger than 20 years (pediatric/adolescent group), and 120 patients (84.5%) were 20 years or older (adult group). Eighty–three patients (58.5%) were male and 59 (41.5%) female. Pre–existing medical conditions were present in 51 patients (35.9%), while only 2 patients (1.4%) had a history of smoking. The mean weight was 70.73 ± 16.20 kg, mean height 168.78 ± 11.78 cm, and mean BMI 24.51 ± 3.72 kg/m2.

Seizure characteristics of the patients

Seizure semiology revealed aura in 33 patients (23.2%), versive movements in 81 (57.0%), focal impaired awareness with motor manifestations in 44 (31.0%), focal impaired awareness seizure in 68 (47.9%), motor sequencing in 28 (19.7%), and end jerking in 31 (21.8%). Motor sequencing was defined as stereotyped sequential motor patterns observed during the ictal phase, while end–jerking referred to isolated, irregular jerks occurring after the main clonic phase had ceased, distinct from terminal cloni.

Gradual termination of the clonic phase was observed in 86 patients (60.6%), while 56 (39.4%) had sudden termination. The mean duration of the clonic phase was 31.12 ± 1.92 s, and the tonic phase averaged 22.54 ± 10.85 s. Clonic phase duration was measured from the first rhythmic jerk to the last, and seizure duration was measured from EEG onset to EEG offset of the generalized phase, excluding aura.

Regarding seizure type, 21 patients (14.8%) had generalised tonic-clonic seizure and 121 (85.2%) had focal to bilateral tonic–clonic seizures. The mean total duration of GTCs was 53.54 ± 18.23 s. Heart rate variability showed tachycardia in 140 patients (98.6%) and bradycardia in 2 (1.4%). Seizures occurred equally during sleep and wakefulness (71 cases each). Postictal paresis (Todd’s paralysis) was noted in 15 patients (10.6%). The average duration of PGES was 25.42 ± 21.77 s.

Among focal to bilateral tonic-clonic seizure cases, 72 had temporal lobe epilepsy, 34 frontal lobe epilepsy, and 15 other focal syndromes. Generalised tonic-clonic seizure cases were consistent with generalized epilepsy syndromes.

Frequency of PGES and associated factors

PGES was detected in 101 out of 142 patients (71.1%). No significant differences were found between PGES-positive and PGES-negative groups in terms of gender (P = 0.697), age (P = 0.141), pre-existing conditions (P = 0.206), or smoking history (P = 0.496). However, patients with PGES had significantly higher mean weight (72.70 ± 13.66 kg vs. 65.88 ± 20.62 kg, P = 0.011) and BMI (24.91 ± 3.42 vs. 23.51 ± 3.26 kg/m2, P = 0.042).

Sensitivity analyses using a ≥ 10–second PGES threshold yielded similar associations with BMI and clonic phase termination, confirming the robustness of these findings. Specifically, when applying the ≥ 10–second definition, PGES prevalence was 64.1%, and patients with prolonged PGES continued to show significantly higher BMI values and a greater frequency of gradual clonic phase termination compared to those without PGES.

Seizure characteristics and PGES

No significant differences were found between PGES and non-PGES groups in terms of aura, versive movements, focal impaired awareness with motor manifestations, focal impaired awareness seizure, motor sequencing, or end jerking. However, gradual clonic phase termination was significantly more common in the PGES group (68.3% vs. 41.5%, P = 0.003). Although clonic phase duration was slightly longer in PGES patients (32.10 ± 10.01 vs. 28.54 ± 12.00 s), the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.147). Total seizure duration was significantly longer in the PGES group (55.64 ± 24.95 vs. 24.95 ± 6.48 s, P = 0.040).

Logistic regression model for PGES factors

Multivariate logistic regression was used to identify independent predictors of PGES. Variables included in the model—BMI, clonic phase termination type, and total seizure duration—were selected based on univariate analysis (p < 0.10). Among these, only gradual termination of the clonic phase emerged as a significant predictor (OR = 2.32, 95% CI: 1.02–5.89, P = 0.045). Association between seizure characteristics and prolonged and short-duration PGES.

Gradual termination of the clonic phase was significantly more frequent in patients with prolonged PGES (73.2% vs. 52.3%, P = 0.013), while sudden termination was more common in the short PGES group. No significant differences were found in clonic phase duration (P = 0.891), tonic phase duration (P = 0.276), or total seizure duration (P = 0.583).

Seizure type showed a significant association with PGES duration. Focal to bilateral tonic–clonic seizures were more common in the prolonged PGES group (92.9% vs. 80.2%), while generalised tonic-clonic seizure were more frequent in the short PGES group (19.8% vs. 7.1%, P = 0.038).

Heart rate variability showed no significant differences: tachycardia was present in 98.2% of prolonged PGES cases and 98.8% of short PGES cases, while bradycardia occurred in 1.8% and 1.2%, respectively (P = 0.758).

Seizure timing was also similar between groups, with 44.6% of prolonged PGES seizures occurring during wakefulness and 55.4% during sleep, compared to 53.5% and 46.5% in the short PGES group (P = 0.303).

Discussion

This study adds to the growing evidence that PGES is a common and clinically relevant phenomenon following generalized tonic-clonic seizures. Our findings show that PGES occurred in over 70% of patients, reinforcing its importance in postictal brain dysfunction. Given its link to autonomic instability and immobility, PGES may serve as a practical marker for identifying patients at higher risk of SUDEP. These results support the need for closer postictal monitoring and individualized seizure management.

PGES prevalence and predictive seizure features

The observed PGES prevalence of 71.1% aligns with newer studies reporting higher rates than previously assumed. Earlier reports showed wide variability—from 1% to 81%—likely due to differences in methodology, patient selection, and EEG monitoring duration [12]. Our use of continuous long-term video-EEG likely contributed to the higher detection rate, capturing subtle postictal changes that routine EEG might miss. Although PGES duration varied, our average of 25 s is shorter than some previous studies, which reported mean durations between 38 and 128 s [19], [20]. This difference may reflect variations in seizure types, age groups, and monitoring protocols.

Gradual clonic phase termination was the strongest independent predictor of PGES (OR = 2.32, P = 0.045), which agrees with earlier studies [5], [13]. Longer tonic and clonic phases were more common in PGES cases, though not all differences were significant. Total seizure duration was significantly longer in PGES patients, supporting the idea that longer seizures may lead to more cortical suppression. Our findings are consistent with Bauer PR et al. [21], who demonstrated that clonic phase characteristics influence PGES occurrence, and Vlachou et al. [22], who further highlighted the role of clonic phase termination patterns in PGES duration. Bilateral tonic extension has also been linked to PGES. Nocturnal seizures are strongly associated with PGES, and PGES often coincides with unresponsiveness. Respiratory and autonomic dysfunction are also common, though some studies found no consistent link to cardiac instability [8].

BMI and PGES: potential implications for clinical management

A notable finding was the association between higher BMI and PGES, especially prolonged PGES. This supports earlier research suggesting that obesity and related conditions like sleep apnea can worsen postictal respiratory compromise [23]. Patients with elevated BMI may benefit from targeted interventions, such as enhanced respiratory monitoring and early oxygen support after seizures. Our findings differ from Pavlova M et al. [24], who did not find BMI associated with PGES. This discrepancy may reflect differences in cohort composition (adult versus pediatric populations), PGES definitions, and methodological approaches. In particular, our cohort was predominantly adult, whereas Pavlova et al. focused on children and adolescents, among whom PGES prevalence and respiratory compromise are lower. These strategies could help reduce PGES severity and improve postictal recovery. Elevated BMI may contribute to PGES by increasing risk of sleep–disordered breathing, hypoxemia, and impaired postictal recovery.

Autonomic dysfunction and postictal paresis

We found no significant difference in HRV between PGES and non-PGES patients, unlike earlier studies linking reduced HRV to PGES and SUDEP [19]. Although HRV reflects autonomic control, our results suggest it may not be a reliable predictor of PGES in GTCs. Autonomic dysfunction might be a secondary effect of PGES rather than a cause. Other factors like oxygen levels and immobility may be more important.

Postictal paresis was more frequent in PGES patients, but the difference did not reach statistical significance. Immobility is often seen as a sign of severe postictal dysfunction. In our study, it didn’t predict PGES on its own, but it still deserves attention during monitoring.

PGES, SUDEP Risk, and seizure type

PGES has been proposed as a SUDEP marker. The MORTEMUS study showed prolonged PGES often followed fatal cardiorespiratory arrests [25]. Autopsy studies also found PGES-like suppression in SUDEP cases [26]. However, some researchers argue PGES reflects seizure severity, not a direct cause of death [27]. Others warn against using PGES alone as a biomarker [28]. Combining PGES with other periictal signs like hypoxemia and immobility may be more useful. Early oxygen and nursing care can shorten PGES and improve recovery [8], [29]. Emerging technologies, such as automated EEG analysis, may also enhance detection accuracy and provide objective measures for clinical monitoring [18].

Focal to bilateral tonic–clonic seizures were more common in prolonged PGES cases. Studies show secondary seizures involve more brain network disruption and longer PGES [30]. Bilateral electrocorticographic (ECoG) suppression in these patients is linked to PGES and SUDEP [31]. Changes in aperiodic brain activity also relate to seizure severity and age at onset [32]. These findings suggest secondary seizures may increase the chance of prolonged PGES due to widespread brain changes.

Our findings support PGES as a marker of seizure severity and postictal dysfunction, rather than a direct predictor of SUDEP. PGES should therefore be interpreted in the context of other peri–ictal features, including respiratory compromise and immobility, when considering SUDEP risk stratification.

Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations. First, its cross–sectional design restricts causal inference, preventing definitive conclusions about the temporal relationship between seizure features and PGES. Although PGES was carefully documented using continuous video–EEG, we did not collect long–term follow–up data, including SUDEP outcomes, which limits our ability to assess the real–world impact of PGES. In addition, body position during and after seizures was not systematically recorded, despite its known association with SUDEP risk. Peri–ictal respiratory changes, such as ictal or postictal apnea, were also not systematically collected, which restricts interpretation of PGES mechanisms.

Future studies should focus on combining clinical data, EEG findings, and physiological markers to build stronger risk models. The role of BMI and autonomic changes in PGES also needs more investigation, especially in relation to breathing and recovery after seizures. Including oxygen saturation, sleep-related factors, and postictal care in future research could help clarify how PGES develops and how to manage it better.

Conclusion

PGES is common in patients with GTCs. We found longer seizure duration, higher BMI, and gradual clonic phase termination were linked to PGES, with termination type being the strongest predictor. These results show seizure dynamics and systemic health matter in postictal suppression. PGES may help identify patients at risk for SUDEP and guide better monitoring and care. These insights may contribute to improved SUDEP risk stratification and inform targeted postictal care strategies.
