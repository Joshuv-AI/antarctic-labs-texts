Identification of barriers to patient education and awareness following dialysis-requiring AKI
Authors: Svati Pazhyanur, Andrew Zhang, Elizabeth Spranger, Michael Heung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSurvivors of acute kidney injury (AKI) requiring dialysis remain at high risk for adverse outcomes, including both recurrent AKI and mortality. Patient awareness and education are prerequisites to pursuing kidney-protective behaviors as a potential approach to improve post-AKI outcomes. The goals of this study were to assess post-AKI awareness and follow-up among patients who suffered AKI-D, and to identify barriers and opportunities to improve post-AKI patient education.MethodsWe conducted a single-center, retrospective study of patients hospitalized in 2021 who survived AKI-D and no longer required dialysis upon discharge. Patients with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), kidney transplant recipients, and hospice-bound patients were excluded. Charts were reviewed to identify documentation of AKI patient education and follow-up instructions. Patient AKI knowledge was assessed using a survey administered via text or phone call.ResultsA total of 91 eligible patients were identified. At one year, post-discharge mortality was 11%, and 34% of survivors had received nephrology follow-up. A diagnosis of AKI was documented on the patient after-visit summary for 54% of patients. Among surveyed patients, 20% were unaware of their AKI. No patients described themselves as very knowledgeable about their AKI or current kidney health. Patient-reported barriers to nephrology follow-up included lack of knowledge regarding how to set up an appointment and competing priorities including other healthcare appointments and non-healthcare obligations.ConclusionsOne in five AKI-D patients were unaware of their AKI diagnosis. We identified several potential opportunities to improve post-AKI education, including more consistent education and follow-up processes.Keywords: Acute kidney injury, Patient awareness, Patient education

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a common complication among hospitalized patients and is associated with important downstream sequelae. AKI requiring dialysis (AKI-D) represents the most severe form of AKI and contributes to high short and longer-term mortality [1]. Survivors of AKI remain at increased risk for poor outcomes including recurrent AKI, re-hospitalization, and decreased quality of life [2].

Recent studies have shown that lack of follow-up and poor patient awareness may be two important factors contributing toward the poor long-term outcomes associated with AKI-D. The 2012 KDIGO guidelines recommend repeat evaluation 3 months following any AKI to assess for resolution or progression to CKD [3]. Low rates of nephrology follow-up have been observed both generally among the AKI population and among those at high-risk for recurrent AKI [4]. While not all AKI patients require follow-up with a nephrologist specifically, patients who incur AKI-D are at higher risk for adverse outcomes than the general AKI population and therefore should generally receive timely follow-up with a nephrologist [5]. Regarding lack of patient awareness, studies have documented poor self-perceived knowledge among AKI survivors, with one in five patients unaware of their AKI diagnosis [6]. Thus, lack of awareness represents an additional challenge for secondary prevention of complications.

Expert committees including the Acute Disease Quality Initiative (ADQI) have highlighted the need to quantify post-AKI follow-up for all AKI patients as part of quality improvement efforts in this area [7]. We sought to assess the current state of post-AKI care processes at a large academic medical center, focusing on the AKI-D subpopulation who should receive nephrology follow-up, and to identify factors that may be associated with patient awareness of their AKI episode. By focusing on potentially modifiable factors, including delivery of patient education and follow-up, the goal of this study was to set the stage for future interventions aimed at improving post-AKI outcomes.

Methods

This study was conducted within a quality improvement framework beginning with stakeholder engagement and process mapping. Nephrology faculty, trainees, and staff participated in departmental discussions to delineate the existing workflow for inpatient AKI education, referral placement, and post-discharge follow-up. Following this structured review of the care pathway, potential points of fragmentation and variability were identified, which informed the selection of process and outcome measures evaluated in this study. The goal of this evaluation was to establish baseline performance and identify actionable gaps to inform subsequent quality improvement interventions.

We conducted a retrospective review of patients with AKI-D during calendar year 2021 at Michigan Medicine, a large (~ 650 bed) academic medical center. A preliminary list of patients was created through electronic health record queries using International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) diagnosis codes for acute kidney injury in combination with inpatient dialysis procedure codes during calendar year 2021. This preliminary cohort was subsequently manually reviewed by two investigators to confirm eligibility based on pre-specified inclusion and exclusion criteria. Following eligibility confirmation, the same investigators conducted structured chart abstraction using a standardized data collection template developed for this quality improvement initiative.

The inclusion criteria were adult patients (≥ 18 years) who initiated acute dialysis during hospitalization and who recovered to dialysis-independence by the time of discharge. Exclusion criteria were discharge to hospice, having a diagnosis of end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) on admission or discharge, or prior history of kidney transplantation. This project was reviewed by the Michigan Medicine Institutional Review Board and approved with a waiver for informed consent for the retrospective data collection (HUM 00029545).

Aligned with the Donabedian model of healthcare quality, we collected data spanning structural components, process measures, and patient-centered outcomes. Data collection included patient demographics and baseline kidney function. Regarding details of hospitalization, we collected length of stay, type and duration of dialysis received, and discharge disposition. To assess AKI-related processes of care during hospitalization, we looked for documentation of patient education and follow-up recommendations based on nephrologist consult notes, discharge summaries, and the after-visit summary (AVS) patient handout. For evidence of patient education by nephrologists, we specifically looked for statements in the nephrology note that documented direct communication to the patient (e.g. phrases such as “counseled patient on …” or “discussed … with patient.”). Patient AKI education in the AVS and at discharge were recorded separately and based on presence of similar statements to the above or explanatory language for the patient about their AKI. Regarding follow-up recommendations, the nephrologist note and discharge summary were searched for explicit mention of a nephrology follow-up plan. Examples of this would include “follow-up in 3 months” in the nephrology note, or documentation of an existing relationship with an outpatient nephrologist that the patient follows with in the discharge summary.

Post-hospitalization outcomes were assessed up to 12 months post-discharge from the AKI-D hospitalization. The primary outcome of interest was whether patients completed a follow-up visit with their nephrologist and/or primary care physician. Secondary outcomes included any subsequent re-hospitalizations, recurrence of AKI (requiring or not requiring dialysis), and mortality. 12-month eGFR was computed using the available serum creatinine closest to 12 months post-discharge. If no serum creatinine values fell within 2 months of the 12-month post-discharge date, the value was considered missing.

To assess patient knowledge and awareness regarding AKI, all living patients were asked to complete a voluntary 10-question patient survey (Appendix 1) via text message and phone call. Prior to survey administration, patients were informed of the aims of the survey, all questions were answered, and consent was obtained [8]. Questions included a variety of qualitative and quantitative fields to better understand their perspective and assess knowledge regarding their AKI. Six questions included a Likert scale, and four questions included an opportunity for open response.

Statistical analysis

Data are reported as either mean (SD) or median (IQR) for continuous variables, and as percentage for categorical variables.

One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare continuous variables across AKI severity groups. Chi-square analyses were conducted to compare categorical variables across groups.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 91 patients who incurred an AKI-D were included. The mean (± SD) age was 57 (± 16) years. 31% of patients were female, and 29% of patients identified as non-White race (Table 1). Mean baseline eGFR was 67 (± 33) ml/min/1.73m2. Mean discharge eGFR was 61 (± 34) ml/min/1.73m2. Median hospital length of stay was 32 days (± 40), and median duration of dialysis was 7 days (± 14). A slight majority of patients were treated with continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) as the initial dialysis modality. Only 4% of patients had seen a nephrologist prior to their AKI-D hospitalization.

Patient characteristics

AKI-related processes of care

Completion rates of AKI-related processes of care are summarized in Fig. 1. Intention to arrange nephrology follow-up was mentioned in nephrology notes 46% of the time. Only 13% of patients had documentation of education in a nephrology note, and only 54% had a diagnosis of AKI listed on their AVS patient handout. Less than one third of patients had written education about their AKI on their AVS (Fig. 1).

Completion of AKI processes of care

Less than half of all patients had a documented recommendation to follow-up with nephrology. Among those, 41% successfully completed follow-up compared to 22% of patients without a documented follow-up recommendation. Median time to nephrology follow-up among patients who completed an appointment was 46 days (± 63). In contrast, 74% of all patients completed a follow-up appointment with their primary care provider with a median time to follow-up of 20 days (± 25).

12-Month post-hospitalization outcomes

The 12-month mortality was 11%. Among patients surviving to 12 months (n = 81), 1-year post-hospitalization eGFR was available for 40% of patients with a mean of 68 (± 31) mL/min/1.73m2. Following the index hospitalization, 27% of all patients incurred one or more recurrent AKI episodes during the subsequent 12 months.

Survey results

Of the 81 patients alive at one-year post-hospitalization, 59 (73%) completed the survey.

When asked how knowledgeable they were about their AKI episode, 12 (20%) selected “Unaware. I was not aware I had a problem with my kidneys.” 27 (46%) patients described themselves as “not knowledgeable” about their current kidney health, and 38 (64%) patients felt “very uncomfortable” or “uncomfortable” with future AKI prevention. (Fig. 2).

Patient responses to select survey questions

Regarding NSAID use, 14 (24%) of patients reported continuing to use NSAIDs at least on a weekly basis. Of the 4 (7%) patients who reported using NSAIDs daily or multiple times per day, all incurred recurrent AKI.

From the open-ended survey questions, several patterns emerged (Table 2). Barriers to follow-up included not knowing how to set up an appointment and competing priorities including other healthcare appointments and non-healthcare obligations. Some patients were able to recall general strategies for preventing future AKI, but others remained uncertain why they experienced an AKI and desired more detailed instructions in their discharge materials, including information regarding the importance of follow-up.

Discussion

This observational study focused on AKI-D, as these patients have the greatest risk for adverse outcomes and are recommended to receive nephrology follow-up. Only 34% of survivors at 1 year ultimately received nephrology follow-up, and one in five surviving patients were unaware of their AKI episode. We also identified overall low rates of documented patient education regarding AKI. These results shed light on awareness and follow-up as separate but related issues potentially contributing to adverse post-AKI outcomes, and identify key areas for quality improvement.

Prior literature has reported on the low awareness of AKI among survivors. Siew et al. surveyed survivors of moderate to severe AKI and found that 80% were unaware of their AKI episode [9]. In another study from Thailand, Thanapongsatorn et al. reported that 50% of AKI survivors were unaware of their AKI episode before attending a post-AKI clinic [10]. Conversely, Ortiz-Soriano noted that 21% of AKI survivors were unaware of their AKI diagnosis [6]. To our knowledge, our study is the first to assess post-AKI knowledge specifically in patients with AKI-D. The proportion of patients unaware of their AKI episode in our study is especially remarkable given that all patients included in this study received acute dialysis during their hospitalization.

There is limited evidence directly investigating the association between patient awareness of AKI and outcomes including recurrent AKI and progression to CKD, however, there is a well-established association between health literacy and poor outcomes for patients with CKD. A systematic review by Taylor et al. including 29 studies found that limited health literacy as measured by various standardized questionnaires was significantly and independently associated with hospitalizations, emergency department use, missed dialysis sessions, cardiovascular events and mortality [11]. Moreover, several quality improvement interventions focusing on bolstering AKI patient education have resulted in significant reductions in AKI recurrence and readmissions within individual health systems [12]. Thus, patient education and awareness does appear to be an important, modifiable driver of poor outcomes for AKI patients.

Our data suggest that avoidance of nephrotoxic medications and intention to pursue follow-up may be two mechanisms by which patient education can translate to lower risk of AKI recurrence and overall improved outcomes. Whereas some patients indicated that specific barriers prevented them from following through with scheduled appointments, others stated they did not understand the importance of following up with a nephrologist and in fact chose not to do so, despite nephrologist follow-up being associated with reduced risk of all-cause mortality in some studies [13]. A noteworthy fraction of AKI patients in our data also continued to take NSAIDs regularly, many of whom incurred recurrent AKI during the study period.

One potential contributor to poor patient awareness and knowledge may have been the relatively low frequency of documented patient education regarding AKI. Prior work suggests that chart audit methodologies may underestimate true care delivery due to inconsistent documentation practices [14]. Accordingly, our approach utilizing chart abstraction may not fully capture actual patient education delivery. However, even in direct-observation studies of inpatient settings, provider communication during the peri-discharge period focuses on addressing symptomatic concerns (hunger, need to use the restroom) and logistical issues of discharge (transportation, time of discharge), with less emphasis on patient education [15]. Moreover, our patient survey results demonstrate significant gaps in both AKI awareness and knowledge among surviving patients, with nearly 1 in 4 patients unable to recall if they received counseling from a nephrologist. Ambiguity regarding roles and responsibilities for patient education has been proposed as an explanation for under-delivery of education in inpatient settings [15].

The finding that nearly half of patient-facing discharge instructions did not explicitly mention AKI represents a potential missed opportunity to reinforce this diagnosis. Patients in our survey suggest that written materials may help fill this gap during a transition of care in which other concerns compete for attention with medical concerns.

As discussed above, one theme that emerged from the survey data was that patients perceived their AKI to be a relatively less important problem and did not fully comprehend the risks of foregoing their follow-up visit, suggesting that lack of awareness may be an important explanation for subsequent loss to follow-up. In addition to perceiving nephrology follow-up as relatively less critical, patients reported challenges accessing these appointments. Barriers mentioned in our survey included appointment wait time, lack of knowledge about how to schedule an appointment, and time and transportation constraints. These findings suggest that referral and appointment outreach workflows contain opportunities for further improvement. Many of these barriers have been previously described in the FUSION study, which found that nephrology follow up was often deemed over burdensome in post-AKI patients along with other competing demands [16]. Given the significant barriers to reliably ensuring completion of these appointments, alternative strategies for delivering AKI-related patient education may warrant consideration. PCP follow-up, which in our study occurred at nearly double the rate of nephrology follow-up, may be a particularly beneficial opportunity to promote renal recovery after AKI-D.

There are several limitations of our study. The survey was administered more than 1 year after patients’ initial AKI-D, leaving our data reliant on patient recall which is error-prone. However, the survey results should still accurately reflect patient knowledge and awareness at time of completion, and the findings remain highly relevant given the many long-terms risks associated with AKI. As with any other retrospective study, our analyses are limited to exploring correlations between patient and system-level factors and clinically relevant outcomes, but cannot establish causality. Furthermore, documentation may not fully reflect care delivered, as previously demonstrated in prospective standardized patient studies [14]. Finally, the sample size was limited to patients from a single large academic medical center and may not be completely generalizable to other health systems, especially at institutions where nephrology teams may have primary admitting and discharge responsibilities for patients. However, we believe that the findings from this study could be relevant to other centers, and encourage individual systems to pursue similar quality assessment to critically assess patient education delivery and follow-up rates.

In our study, self-reported awareness and knowledge regarding AKI was poor among a population of patients who incurred the severest form of AKI, and appeared to contribute toward low follow-up rates. We identified several areas for potential process improvement, including provision of written patient educational material and improved post-AKI follow-up visits [12]. As a result of this study, our institution is developing dedicated post-AKI follow-up slots in nephrology clinics to facilitate scheduling, creating standardized educational materials, and continuing to educate providers on the importance of this work. These findings may inform future efforts within individual health systems to evaluate and strengthen post-AKI care processes.
