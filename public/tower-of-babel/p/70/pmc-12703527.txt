Decoding the Unexpected: How Cell-Free DNA Sequencing Unveiled an Elusive Tularemia Case
Authors: Jack R Watson, Rehan R Syed, Victor Manuel Salazar, George Dahdouh, Jacqueline Bursalyan, Anya English, Hector Ponce-Sanchez, Fernanda Urrutia, Fiona Chan, Mokshitaa Dhamotharan, Natalie Gonzalez, Bethlehem Messele, Saahil Mohta, Jyoti Sah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTyphoidal tularemia is a rare form of Francisella tularensis infection with nonspecific systemic symptoms that pose diagnostic challenges. Microbial cell-free DNA (mcfDNA) sequencing offers promise for detecting difficult-to-diagnose pathogens, including tularemia.An 80-year-old woman presented with fever and acute encephalopathy, with in-hospital evaluation unrevealing of an identifiable source or pathogen. She was discharged with clinical improvement on empiric levofloxacin. Following her hospitalization, mcfDNA sequencing results detected Francisella tularensis, leading to diagnosis of typhoidal tularemia.This case demonstrated mcfDNA’s utility for diagnosing rare zoonotic infections that would likely remain undiagnosed using conventional methods. The authors propose molecular testing should be considered for updated tularemia case definitions for public health surveillance. Clinicians should maintain suspicion for zoonotic diseases in patients with undifferentiated fever in California.Keywords: Tularemia, Typhoidal, Francisella tularensis, microbial cell-free DNA, tick-borne, California, public health surveillance

Introduction

Tularemia is a zoonotic disease caused by Francisella tularensis, an aerobic and fastidious gram-negative bacterium that can be challenging to grow in routine cultures. 1 Human infections occur through several modes of infection, most commonly tick bites, but also through inhalation, handling of infected animals, and exposure to contaminated food and water. Tularemia is not transmitted from person to person and is often treated using fluoroquinolones, tetracyclines, and aminoglycosides. 2 The clinical manifestations of acquiring F tularensis range from asymptomatic illness to septic shock and death, in part depending on the virulence of the strain causing illness, the portal of entry, the inoculum quantity, and the immune status of the host. 3 The case fatality rate of tularemia is typically below 2% but has been reported as high as 24%. 4

As the clinical presentation can range from acute sepsis to a chronic febrile illness, it is important to recognize the spectrum of tularemia disease. 3 Persons who have acquired tularemia typically experience a rapid onset of nonspecific symptoms, including fevers, chills, anorexia, and malaise following an incubation period of 3–5 days (range 1–21 days) after exposure. Other nonspecific symptoms include headache, fatigue, myalgia, sore throat, cough, abdominal pain, emesis, or diarrhea. Fevers may remit after a few days, but frequently return. In some patients, these systemic symptoms may resolve by the time of medical evaluation. When patients come to medical attention, they typically exhibit specific clinical manifestations associated with one of the major clinical forms of tularemia (see Figure 1). 5

Tularemia clinical presentations. 5 The 5 clinical presentations of tularemia include ulceroglandular, oculoglandular, oropharyngeal, pneumonic, and typhoidal. Patients may exhibit some or all of the symptoms found within these presentations. Ulceroglandular tularemia presents with localized lymphadenopathy and a cutaneous ulcer at the infection site. Oculoglandular tularemia presents with photophobia, vision impairment or loss, conjunctivitis, and regional lymphadenopathy. Oropharyngeal tularemia presents with severe sore throat, exudative pharyngitis or tonsillitis, and regional lymphadenopathy. Pneumonic tularemia presents with nonproductive cough, substernal tightness, pleuritic chest pain, and hilar adenopathy. Typhoidal tularemia presents with generalized systemic symptoms without localization, which may include pulmonary infiltrates on chest radiograph in the absence of respiratory symptoms. As symptoms may be nonspecific, diagnosis can be challenging; this is particularly the case with the typhoidal form of tularemia. The differential diagnosis of subacute fever in the winter in northern California is broad, and patients presenting with culture-negative systemic illness without localized features require consideration of infections including from challenging-to-culture bacteria, viral illnesses, vector-borne illnesses, and zoonoses, depending on factors such as geography of residence and travel history. 6

Unlike in areas with a high prevalence of tick-borne disease (including the upper midwest and eastern United States), where transmission is enhanced in the summer, low prevalence can be seen year-round in California. 7,8 For perspective, in California, a state with almost 40 million residents, there were no confirmed tick-exposure–related cases of tularemia in 2023 using traditional testing methods. 9

Typhoidal disease typically occurs after inhalation of the organism but may result following inoculation from any portal of entry, with the source often unapparent at the time of presentation. Occasionally, localized abdominal symptoms may be observed, including “abdominal tularemia,” which presents as mesenteric adenopathy and is thought to be in the setting of pathogen ingestion. Enlargement of the liver and spleen is more likely to be detected following a longer duration of illness. Pulmonary involvement secondary to hematologic spread is seen in up to 45% of cases. Potential laboratory findings in severe typhoidal tularemia include elevated creatine phosphokinase, myoglobinuria, hyponatremia, and renal failure. 3

Of the 1163 cases of tularemia reported in the United States between 2006–2021 that were detected by traditional methods and reported to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, typhoidal disease was the fourth most common form of the disease, reported in 13% of cases. 10 In California, there were 19 tularemia cases from 2013–2019. 11

A high incidence of tularemia was observed from 2009–2013 in Arkansas, with over 60% of the 45 cases in persons in the 60–90 year age group presenting with typhoidal disease. Although the majority of individuals who acquired illness reported arthropod bites, clinical disease appeared to spread hematogenously and without localized signs in many cases. 12 A recent abstract by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention investigating the use of mcfDNA for the diagnosis of tularemia in the United States found the source is usually inapparent at the time of presentation. Among 15 patients in 6 states from 2018–2023, infection with F tularensis was not suspected in 13 (87%) patients prior to receipt of mcfDNA test results. 13

Case Patient Presentation

In January of 2025, an 80-year-old woman with a history of longstanding bronchiectasis and chronic spine and shoulder pain presented to a community hospital in Marin County, California, with 3 days of fever and 1 day of acute encephalopathy. Her son, with whom she lived, noted she had been complaining about neck stiffness, but reported an unchanged mild chronic cough and denied acute respiratory symptoms, including dyspnea and hemoptysis. She had recently celebrated her birthday 6 days prior to presentation and had visited multiple bars and restaurants for 4 consecutive nights. She lived in a suburban neighborhood and initially reported no history of recent travel, sick contacts, pets, or animal exposures. She enjoyed walks recreationally on paved roads in her neighborhood but had not spent time in regional parks or in the wilderness.

The physical examination was notable for an ill-appearing woman who was confused and oriented only to self. She was observed to be febrile to 102 °F. She endorsed left-sided neck discomfort with lateral rotation but none with neck flexion or extension. She denied severe pain. No additional focal findings were identified on examination. She was admitted to the hospital and started on empiric therapy for possible meningitis while undergoing a workup for infectious and noninfectious causes of her syndrome of fever, encephalopathy, and neck stiffness. Initial empiric coverage included intravenous vancomycin, ceftriaxone, and acyclovir. She also briefly received meropenem, given prior history of Pseudomonas aeruginosa on respiratory culture. Imaging performed included a head computed tomography scan and magnetic resonance imaging of the brain, with no acute abnormalities identified. On hospital day 1, a lumbar puncture was performed with cerebrospinal fluid findings not suggestive of meningitis. Antibiotics were stopped, and the patient was monitored clinically. She initially experienced resolution of fever but continued to experience fluctuating encephalopathy. Hospital day 4 was notable for a return of fever to 102 °F, however, clinically her encephalopathy and neck pain improved, and she was without focal signs or symptoms that might indicate a source of infection. In the setting of fever, empiric cefepime and metronidazole were started, while additional infectious workup was pursued, including collection of a blood sample for mcfDNA sequencing. Over the subsequent 2 days, she defervesced and continued to clinically improve, with resolution of all symptoms, including encephalopathy and neck discomfort. The patient was eager to return home, and in the context of clinical improvement, she was discharged on hospital day 6 with planned close outpatient follow-up. However, with a persisting leukocytosis and unclear disease etiology, she was recommended to continue levofloxacin and metronidazole as an outpatient, given concern for an occult source of infection.

Treatment Pathway

Two days after discharge, the mcfDNA sequencing results returned positive with detection of F tularensis molecules in blood. The patient’s clinical syndrome was felt to be most consistent with typhoidal disease, and her levofloxacin course was extended to a total duration of 10 days. Nine days after discharge, serum F tularensis qualitative enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) immunoglobulin M and immunoglobulin G was sent for corroboration and returned positive. Clinically, she experienced a full recovery without recurrence or sequelae of symptoms. Local and state public health departments did not recommend additional confirmatory testing.

The patient was interviewed for possible exposures. Retrospectively, the patient did not recall a tick bite but lived adjacent to a wooded open space frequented by deer. She commented that she “is not a wilderness person” and preferred to keep to paved streets in her neighborhood when walking recreationally. It was considered that the tick exposure may have been in the household and introduced by a visiting domestic animal. A daughter from Redding had visited with her dog for several days, leaving the patient’s residence 21 days prior to symptom onset. The dog spent time between the house and wooded backyard during the stay, also interacting with another local son’s dog (with which the patient had prior exposure), and notably frequents an open space where a dead squirrel with tularemia was found in 2024. 14 That son noted it was unusual that he had been regularly removing ticks from his dog during the winter.

Outcomes and Discussion

The diagnosis of typhoidal tularemia in this San Francisco Bay Area patient with undifferentiated fever and encephalopathy was made using mcfDNA testing. Given the rarity of this diagnosis in the region and the clinical improvement observed with partial therapy using an agent that incidentally treats the pathogen, a definitive diagnosis would likely have been challenging or impossible to make without the use of this test. This adds to the body of literature demonstrating the utility of mcfDNA for the diagnosis of tularemia. 13,15,16

A key benefit of unbiased mcfDNA sequencing is the ability to detect diagnostically challenging microbes, such as zoonotic and vector-borne pathogens. 17 Particular benefits from this approach include pneumonia in immunocompromised patients, fever of unknown origin, febrile neutropenia, invasive fungal infections, culture-negative endocarditis, and unresolving community-acquired pneumonia. Prompt identification of these traditionally difficult-to-diagnose pathogens is important for clinical management of individual patients and, frequently, for public health purposes (see Figure 2). 18,19

Currently, molecular laboratory methods such as mcfDNA may be supportive of a tularemia diagnosis but do not meet the criteria for a confirmatory test. As such, the described case of tularemia was considered a probable, not confirmed, case for public health surveillance purposes. Historically, the diagnosis of tularemia in the United States has been confirmed serologically by the detection of a 4-fold or greater change in F tularensis antibody titers in acute and convalescent serum specimens or by isolation in clinical culture. However, due to the diagnostic challenge this pathogen presents, and with increased use of nonquantitative methods such as current commercially available ELISA tests, confirmatory testing is not always feasible by the time it is considered (see Figure 3). 22

A limitation of the current mcfDNA technique is that it does not detect RNA viruses, which include influenza, severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS), Middle East respiratory syndrome (MERS), dengue virus, hepatitis C virus, hepatitis E virus, West Nile virus, Ebola virus, rabies virus, poliovirus, mumps virus, and measles virus, among others. 25 However, the use of microbial cell-free detection techniques for RNA is currently under investigation. 26

The potential exposure of the patient remains unclear. It was hypothesized that she may have been exposed via an unnoticed tick bite or an environmental exposure, such as aerosolized bacteria. Ticks that transmit F tularensis bacteria to humans include the American dog tick (Dermacentor variabilis) and the Pacific coast tick (Dermacentor occidentalis), which feed on deer. 9 In a survey conducted in 2023 by the California Department of Public Health Vector-Borne Disease Section, a total of 160 adult D variabilis and 9 adult D occidentalis ticks from 11 counties were tested for F tularensis. One adult D variabilis tick from China Camp State Park in Marin County tested positive for F tularensis by reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction, and the statewide infection prevalence was found to be 0.6%. 9

Conclusion

This case highlights the importance of inclusion of zoonotic diseases in the differential diagnosis of wintertime subacute fever in California. Given the relative rarity of these infections and the frequent requirements for multiassay specialty testing to comprehensively evaluate for these illnesses, mcfDNA sequencing represents a promising technology for prompt clinical detection of infections in immunocompetent patients with subacute fever in this region. The authors additionally propose that molecular testing, including mcfDNA testing, should be considered for confirmatory testing as part of an updated tularemia case definition for public health surveillance purposes.
