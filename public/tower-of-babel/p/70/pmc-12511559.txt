Co‐Existing ATTR Amyloidosis and Coronary Artery Disease: A Risky Combination
Authors: Arnaud Planchat, Damien Guijarro, Yazan Musayeb, Georgios Giannakopoulos, Raphaël Giraud, Nana K. Poku, Dre Agathe Py, Stéphane Noble
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe coexistence of cardiac amyloidosis with coronary artery disease is a complex diagnosis and a challenging clinical scenario. Amyloid fibril deposition contributes to arteriosclerosis, and amyloidosis is also recognized to be associated with intramural microvascular disease. The restrictive heart failure associated with amyloidosis worsened the complication of PCI. A multimodal imaging approach is necessary when cardiac amyloidosis is highly suspected.

Introduction

The combination of cardiac amyloidosis and coronary artery disease (CAD) creates a complex and high‐risk clinical scenario. We present the case of a 73‐year‐old woman with undiagnosed transthyretin cardiac amyloidosis (ATTR‐CM) who developed severe complications after percutaneous coronary intervention for a significant left circumflex artery (LCX) stenosis. Patients with amyloidosis have a higher risk of complications after percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) likely due to more advanced CAD and more compromised cardiac function [1]. They experience longer hospital stays, an increased risk of strokes due to thromboembolic events, cerebral amyloid deposition, and a higher incidence of acquired coagulation abnormalities and platelet dysfunction.

Case History

A 73‐year‐old woman presented with a history of NYHA II dyspnea but no typical chest pain. Her ECG showed a normal precordial R‐wave progression and no repolarization disorder. Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) performed after an episode of acute heart failure demonstrated no myocardial infiltrate but showed a perfusion defect at the basal and mid‐inferior territory. She had a history of polyneuropathy and carpal tunnel surgery as well as an endoscopic bladder resection for a superficial low‐grade carcinoma, with histopathology examination revealing bladder and subcutaneous fat amyloid deposits. Notably, she had refused previous investigations for cardiac amyloidosis despite a high index of suspicion due to suggestive findings on transthoracic echocardiography performed at an external facility.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

Coronary angiography demonstrated a single‐vessel coronary artery disease with an angiographically significant proximal LCX stenosis (Figure 1A). Optical coherence tomography (OCT) was performed to better understand the nature of the plaque. It showed a large thin‐cap fibroatheroma with a 180‐degree lipid pool with a pronounced reduction of the minimal lumen area of 1.7 mm2 (Figure 1B). The decision was taken to proceed with PCI, considering that the location of the stenosis was compatible with the ischemic territory on the CMR in a symptomatic patient. Pre‐dilatation was performed using a 2.5 mm × 15 mm semi‐compliant balloon. The angiographic control revealed no reflow secondary to distal embolization. OCT showed no dissection. A 3.5 × 30 mm ONYX FRONTIER (Medtronic, Minneapolis, Minnesota, USA) drug‐eluting stent was subsequently implanted (Figure 1C) with post‐dilatation. The final angiographic result showed a TIMI II flow.

The patient presented with an acute pulmonary edema towards the end of the procedure, first requiring non‐invasive ventilation and then rapid mechanical support after orotracheal intubation. The patient developed pulseless electrical activity during intubation with zero no‐flow and 7 min of slow flow. A second coronary angiography was performed via the right femoral artery, showing normalization of TIMI flow in the LCX. Left ventricular end‐diastolic pressure rose to 35 mmHg, which led to the insertion of an Impella CP (Abiomed Inc., Massachusetts, USA) through the right femoral artery using pre‐closure with Perclose (Abbott, Illinois, USA). Subsequently, considering the SCAI SHOCK stage C, veno‐arterial extra‐corporeal membrane oxygenation (VA‐ECMO) was inserted via the left femoral artery and the right femoral vein using pre‐closure with Perclose.

Transthoracic (TTE) and transesophageal echocardiography showed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 50% with hypokinesia of the inferolateral territory and moderate mitral regurgitation (MR). While weaning off the Impella CP after 48 h, the patient experienced acute pulmonary edema. This was due to the worsening of the functional eccentric MR directed posteriorly due to the restriction of the posterior leaflet. However, an underlying diastolic heart failure related to cardiac amyloidosis contributed to the worsening of the clinical picture. After a multidisciplinary Heart Team discussion, it was concluded that mitral transcatheter edge‐to‐edge repair (TEER) was the best option. An A2‐P2 XTW MitraClip (Abbott, Illinois, USA) reduced the MR to mild after removing the Impella CP and under minimal VA‐ECMO support. The final mitral mean gradient was 3 mmHg.

The clinical course was further complicated by a right common femoral artery thrombosis in the context of premature anticoagulation discontinuation after bleeding from the urinary tract. The right common femoral artery thrombosis was successfully treated by mechanical thrombectomy using the Rotarex system. Due to the overall good hemodynamic evolution, we proceeded to the percutaneous explantation of the arterial VA‐ECMO cannula using the pre‐closure with Perclose. After the final angiographic control by the contralateral approach, a significant dissection of the left common femoral and iliac extern arteries was treated by a 6 × 100 mm Propathen Viabahn endoprosthesis (Gore, Putzbrunn, Germany).

Conclusion and Follow‐Up

A follow‐up TTE revealed multiple signs of cardiac amyloidosis, namely biventricular thickening, valve thickening, and mildly reduced global longitudinal strain with apical sparing. Serum and urine protein electrophoresis with immunofixation, along with free light chain analysis, showed no abnormality, ruling out clonal dyscrasia. Immunohistochemistry of the previously resected bladder found no evidence of amyloid A (AA) and light chain (AL) amyloidosis. Technetium 99m Tc‐pyrophosphate bone scintigraphy discovered a Perugini grade 2 cardiac uptake compatible with senile or hereditary cardiac amyloidosis (Figure 2). Of note, the CT scan images, in addition to SPECT, were essential in our case, as the contrast on planar images did not make myocardial uptake visible.

Technetium 99 m Tc‐pyrophosphate bone scintigraphy showed a PERUGINI grade 2 cardiac uptake encompassing the basal segments and mid‐anteroseptal segment on SPECT–CT images, suggesting senile or hereditary cardiac amyloidosis. Contrast on planar images does not make myocardial uptake clearly visible, unlike SPECT.

Tafamidis therapy was initially started, then switched to patisiran, considering polyneuropathy, and after further genetic testing, confirmed a pathogenic variant (class 4) in the TTR gene: c.88T>C (p.Cys30Arg). Screening of first‐degree family members found the same mutation in the patient's daughter. At the two‐year follow‐up, the patient was in NYHA class II. Residual MR was assessed as mild, with two regurgitation jets and a mean gradient of 4 mmHg at a heart rate of 70 bpm.

Discussion

Restrictive cardiomyopathy, combined with ischemic heart disease, represents a complex clinical scenario that complicates both diagnosis and treatment strategies. An observational study found that while CAD is prevalent among patients with ATTR‐CM, CAD diagnosis typically precedes ATTR‐CM; therefore, an epicardial lesion such as a proximal LCx plaque should be considered non‐specific in this context [2]. Herein, the suspicion of cardiac amyloidosis was unknown to the interventional cardiologists at the time of the coronary angiography, and the final diagnosis was made later.

Amyloidosis has been postulated to result in CAD by depositing amyloid fibrils that contribute to atherosclerosis, affecting the arterial media more than the endothelium [3]. Histopathological analysis of a plaque in a Japanese patient undergoing directional coronary atherectomy resection found it to be infiltrated by amyloid deposits [4]. Amyloidosis is also recognized to be associated with intramural microvascular disease in the absence of obstructive epicardial CAD, as shown by histopathological studies of AL amyloidosis patients [5]. It is additionally hypothesized that amyloid deposition accelerates arteriosclerosis through amyloid‐induced inflammation, as suggested by elevated interleukin‐6 levels in ATTR‐CM patients and its role as an independent predictor of cardiovascular events [6].

A study comparing patterns of CAD found that heart failure patients with cardiac amyloidosis had fewer calcified lesions, less ostial involvement, and a reduced anterograde coronary blood flow [7]. However, the clinical characteristics of ATTR‐CM patients with CAD are similar to their counterparts without CAD, suggesting their prognosis is mainly linked to ATTR‐CM. This observation will be impacted by the improvement in the overall prognosis of cardiac amyloidosis with improvement in early diagnosis and the recent emergence of novel therapies [2].

Due to the overall poor prognosis in cardiac amyloidosis, with an estimated median survival time of less than 1 year for AL and 4 years for ATTR‐CM amyloidosis, percutaneous coronary revascularization is the preferred treatment option. Non‐urgent revascularizations in these patients require a tailored approach, balancing the risks and benefits of medication, chemotherapy, and revascularization procedures [3].

In a retrospective study between 2016 and 2020 including more than 2.3 million patients undergoing PCI, 7855 patients had underlying infiltrative cardiomyopathies (0.33%, also including sarcoidosis and hemochromatosis). PCI in amyloidosis patients (0.04%) was associated with worse composite post‐PCI outcomes (OR, 1.6; 95% CI, 1.1–2.44; p = 0.02), including higher in‐hospital mortality (OR, 1.9; 95% CI, 1.1–3.4; p = 0.04), potentially related to more advanced CAD and more compromised cardiac function [1]. The length of stay was also longer (8.2 vs. 3.9 days; p < 0.001). In amyloidosis patients, there was a higher risk of ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke (OR, 4.0; 95% CI, 1.1–16.0; p = 0.04), respectively, due to a higher risk of thromboembolic events and amyloid fibril deposition in cerebral vessels [1]. Other postulated mechanisms include acquired deficiency of coagulation and platelet dysfunction [1].

In our case, the clinical course of post‐PCI complications seemed disproportionate. Indeed, the restrictive heart failure secondary to amyloidosis may have worsened the complication of no reflow and contributed to the need for a ventricular assist device. Our case also emphasizes the critical role of multimodal imaging. Whereas CMR did not show myocardial infiltration, a comprehensive TTE, which was performed later in the clinical course, revealed suggestive characteristics of cardiac amyloidosis. The diagnosis was confirmed by Technetium 99m Tc‐pyrophosphate bone scintigraphy. In addition, the case also highlights the importance of having CT‐scan images in addition to SPECT, as the contrast on planar images did not make the myocardial uptake visible. Some forms of ATTR amyloidosis exhibit low uptake on bone scintigraphy and can sometimes even result in false negatives. However, data are lacking for rarer mutations, such as the one found in our patient [2].

The association between CAD and ATTR‐CM in the elderly population represents a challenging clinical scenario. PCI in patients with amyloidosis is associated with more complications, and the restrictive heart failure associated with amyloidosis worsened the complication of no reflow in our patient. There is a need for more studies to better understand the unique pathophysiology of coronary artery disease patterns in cardiac amyloidosis and management options.
