Atlantic multidecadal oscillation modulates the impact of precipitation extremes on crop yields in Africa
Authors: Haris Majeed, Carmen H. Logie, Daniyal Zuberi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Climate variability driven by North Atlantic sea surface temperatures variability through the Atlantic Multidecadal Oscillation (AMO) is known to influence climate conditions in Africa. Yet, there is a need to understand whether AMO’s interannual variability modulates the risks of elevated precipitation on agriculture yields in regional Africa. Here we investigate whether a positive or negative phase of the AMO modulates the risk of cereal, fruit, and vegetable yield during high precipitation events across African regions over the past six decades. Using region-specific annual yield data and climate indicators, we assess how AMO’s decadal phases interact with precipitation and affect agricultural yield in North, East, West, and Southern Africa. To our knowledge this study is unique in finding that during a negative phase of the AMO (pre-1995), high precipitation levels significantly increased the likelihood of increased cereal yield in almost all regions of Africa, yet not during the positive phase of AMO (post-1995). A negative AMO exhibited more favorable growing conditions only for cereal, not fruit or vegetable yield. These findings highlight the importance of integrating oceanic climate variability into agricultural risk assessment and planning in possibly other low-income nations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-59286-0.

Introduction

Cereal grain, fruits, and vegetables are essential part of the human diet, especially in regions experiencing malnutrition such as in some African regions. In fact, some regions of Africa are facing severe food insecurity and operating at less than 80% food self-sufficiency due to slow productivity gains1. Projected food demand is set to rise by 35–60% between 2020 and 2050, driven by an increasing population growth in Africa2. In addition to numerous agricultural, social, and political risk factors, climate conditions play an important role in agricultural productivity in various parts of Africa. Among these, climate related to surface air temperature and precipitation variability are known to be the primary determinants of yield outcomes, especially in Africa2–4.

For instance, in North Africa, cereal yields have shown modest increases over the past decades yet remain constrained by water scarcity stress5. East Africa has experienced fluctuating cereal and fruit production due to erratic rainfall and land degradation6. In West Africa, yield improvements in cereals and vegetables have been documented, but remain vulnerable to climate temperatures and rainfall extremes7. Southern Africa exhibits significant interannual yield variability, attributed to temperature stress and drought8. Overall, droughts and reduced rainfalls can reduce food yields9,10. The regional heterogeneity in precipitation response emphasizes the need to examine climate-yield interactions within a broader climatic context.

The Atlantic Multidecadal Oscillation (AMO), a long-term fluctuation in North Atlantic sea surface temperatures, exhibits warm (positive) and cool (negative) phases (i.e. periodicity) approximately every 60–80 years11,12. The AMO influences global climate patterns, including precipitation distribution across Africa. For instance, the AMO’s warm phase has been linked to droughts in the Sahel and wetter conditions in Southern Africa13. Conversely, the cool phase has coincided with more wet and humid conditions in West, Central, and East Africa11.

Thus, the influence of AMO phases on African precipitation patterns, and consequently on crop yields, is a timely and novel area of research. These climatological shifts suggest that AMO may modulate the effect of precipitation extremes on crop yield, but empirical evidence across crop types and regions remains limited.

In this study, we hypothesize that the AMO modifies the effects of high and moderate precipitation levels on cereal, fruit, and vegetable yields in Africa. Specifically, we expect that during the negative phase of the AMO, high precipitation will help elevate drought conditions and result in increase growth of yields throughout Africa. Our regional analyses utilize over six decades of data and aims to quantify AMO-precipitation-yield interactions to improve climate-informed agricultural planning.

Overall, these findings support the hypothesis that AMO phases modify the yield response to precipitation. The results suggest that during positive AMO periods, increased heat alongside rainfall irregularity may offset the benefits of high precipitation, particularly for water-sensitive cereal crops.

Discussion

This study is unique in utilizing six decades of data to understand the relationships between the AMO, precipitation, and agricultural yields throughout Africa. Our results provide new evidence that the AMO significantly modulates the relationship between precipitation and yields across African regions, particularly for cereal crops. These findings are consistent with previous studies showing that the positive AMO phase enhances heat stress and rainfall variability, particularly in the Sahel and East Africa14–16, leading to agricultural disruptions.

The diminished yield benefits of high precipitation during a positive (warm) phase of the AMO may be due to reduced rainfall efficiency, increased evapotranspiration, and more frequent intra-seasonal dry spells that disrupt growth phases of rainfed crops6,17. Our analysis also highlights region-specific crop sensitivities, while cereal yield are negatively affected by AMO-modulated precipitation extremes, vegetable yields in Southern Africa appear more resilient, likely due to differences in root systems, water needs, and irrigation access18,19. Temporal yield patterns from 1961-2023 further confirm that agricultural development has not uniformly offset climate variability impacts, particularly in East Africa, where land degradation and housing infrastructure deficits intersect with climatic stressors20. While West Africa showed a very strong association between high precipitation and cereal yield during the negative phase, other regions like East Africa face intersecting stressors such as land degradation and infrastructure deficits that may limit the yield benefits of favorable rainfall. Additionally, the resilience of certain crops in specific regions, such as vegetables in Southern Africa, may be due to differences in irrigation access and root systems rather than a lack of prevalence.

Research has demonstrated that for major African staples, the negative impacts of exceeding critical temperature thresholds, particularly during sensitive reproductive stages like anthesis and grain filling, often outweigh the potential physiological gains from increased rainfall21. By incorporating indicators for heat stress and maximum surface air temperature into our adjusted statistical models, we clarify that the interaction between these thermal extremes and precipitation variability is a primary driver of yield outcomes. This dominance of temperature over precipitation in determining African agricultural productivity is well supported by established literature, which highlights that thermal stress exerts a more robust and consistently negative influence on crop yields across the continent than variations in precipitation alone22.

Understanding the effects of large-scale climate patterns, in this case the AMO, on shaping agro-climatic risk can provide insight into critical yields. This is particularly salient under global warming scenarios, as climate models project increased frequency and intensity of AMO-like variability in the future23,24. Integrating decadal-scale ocean-atmosphere indices into crop forecasting systems could substantially improve early warning capacity and inform region-specific adaptation strategies in African agriculture3.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, while we leveraged long-term regional data from 1961 to 2023, the quality and completeness of agricultural yield records and climate observations vary by country and decade, potentially introducing measurement bias. Second, our analyses were conducted at the regional level, which may obscure important sub-national heterogeneities in crop response, soil characteristics, and local adaptation practices. Third, we focused primarily on a sea surface temperature driven climate variability (i.e. AMO), yet there could be other patterns (i.e. El Niño Southern Oscillation or the Indian Ocean Dipole) that potentially have a dominant effect on African yields in one year compared to another. Finally, while we identified statistical associations between AMO phase, precipitation, and yields, the underlying causal mechanisms particularly those involving soil moisture or plant physiological stress require further investigation using mechanistic or process-based models.

Future work should explore mechanisms through which AMO interacts with local land surface feedbacks, such as soil moisture dynamics and land cover changes, and how these interactions influence both precipitation efficiency and crop physiological stress. Overall, these results highlight the need for climate variability (i.e. through sea surface temperature) integrated into agricultural policies that consider both interannual and multidecadal variability, integrating historical and projected climate modes into national food security planning25.

Methods

Study regions and data sources

This is a cross-sectional study spanning more than six decades of agriculture and climate data, with 52 nations in Africa. These 52 nations were grouped into five regions; North Africa; West Africa; East Africa; Central Africa; Southern Africa, consistent with World Health Organisation (WHO) macro-regions.

Annual country-specific crop yield data in tonnes per hectare for cereal, fruit, and vegetable from 1961 to 2023 were obtained from the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO)26: https://www.foodsystemsdashboard.org/.

Cereals are the world’s largest crops, also called staple foods, which consist of rice, wheat, rye, oats, barley, millet, and maize (i.e. corn). Fruits comprised bananas, citrus, mangoes, and melons, while vegetables included tomatoes, onions, cabbages, and leafy greens26.

Population and population growth data (of all ages) were also ascertained from FAO.

In terms of exposure climatological data, the annual average maximum surface air temperature and total annual precipitation were retrieved from the ERA5 reanalysis dataset, which provides gridded meteorological data at high temporal resolution27. Additional climate indicators from ERA5 included the annual number of precipitation days exceeding 20 millimeters and the number of hot days above 35 °C, representing short-term extremes relevant to crop stress27.

Annual carbon dioxide emissions (MtCO2 per year) data was also ascertained using national-level estimates from the Global Carbon Atlas28. The low-pass 10-year Sahel precipitation index29 was regressed with the global SST field.

Descriptive analyses

To illustrate long-term changes, country-level crop yield and population were classified into low, medium, and high categories based on three equally weighted tertile cutoffs and visualized (Figure 2d-f). Precipitation was stratified within each country into three levels: low (≤20th percentile), moderate (>20h to 80th percentile), and high (≥80th percentile), based on the distribution over the 63-year period.

The Atlantic Multidecadal Oscillation (AMO) index used was the unsmoothed version developed by the Colorado State University30, which accounts for North Atlantic sea surface temperatures and sea level pressures anomalies, providing a holistic impression over the central North Atlantic Ocean. The AMO index was divided into two phases: a negative phase (1967-1994) and a positive phase (1995-2023), consistent with documented transitions in the AMO patterns31.

Statistical analyses

Initial diagnostic testing of annual yield time series data revealed first-order autocorrelation, indicating temporal dependence across years. Due to the skewed nature of yield distributions and the need to account for repeated measures across countries and time, we modeled the log-transformed crop yield using generalized least squares regression with a negative binomial link and an autoregressive AR(1) correlation structure32.

The main exposure of interest was precipitation (as categories), the AMO phases, and their interaction, with adjustment for covariates (i.e. adjusted associations): maximum temperature, population growth rate, CO2 emissions, precipitation frequency (days >20 mm), heat stress (days >35°C), and fixed effects for country and year (i.e. trends). Model coefficients were exponentiated to yield rate ratio (RR), representing the multiplicative change in yield associated with each predictor. A significant interaction between AMO phase and precipitation levels was found, which indicated that the AMO modifies the relationship between precipitation extremes and African yields3.

All analyses, figure, and data creation were conducted using a combination of R (version 4.3.1), STATA (version 16), and Microsoft Office 365.
