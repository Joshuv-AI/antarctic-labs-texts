The effect of Fogg model-based virtual education on health literacy and stages of behavior change in cervical cancer screening: study protocol for a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Saba Mousavi-Nejad, Elham Rezaei, Roghaiyeh Nourizadeh, Zeinab Mohammadzadeh, Esmat Mehrabi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCervical cancer imposes a significant burden on women in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where participation rates in screening programs are suboptimal. Identifying effective intervention strategies to increase screening participation is critical, particularly in these communities. This study aims to determine the effect of Fogg model-based virtual education on cervical cancer health literacy and stages of behavior change related to cervical cancer screening.MethodsThis is a two-arm, parallel-group, randomized controlled trial with blinded outcome assessor and data analyst. Eighty women aged 21–65 years will be recruited from one-fifth of Tabriz’s health centers, selected on the basis of socioeconomic diversity, and randomized at a 1:1 ratio via restricted block randomization and sealed opaque envelopes for allocation concealment. The intervention group will receive virtual education based on the Fogg behavior model for 4 weeks (twice weekly) via the Iranian messaging platform Eitaa, which uses multimedia content (videos, audio, infographics, text messages, and quizzes) about cervical cancer and its screening. The content includes spark messages (to increase motivation and reduce psychological barriers), facilitator messages (to simplify practical barriers), and trigger messages (to prompt action). Data analysis will use ANCOVA for continuous variables and ordinal regression for stages of change.DiscussionIf the results of this study demonstrate positive effects, the intervention could increase screening participation, reduce the disease burden, and be scalable and adaptable for other LMICs, serving as a foundation for national digital prevention programs for diseases such as cervical cancer.Trial registrationThis trial was prospectively registered on the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials. Introduction

Background and rationale {9a}

Cervical cancer, the fourth most common cancer among women globally, poses a significant health threat, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where approximately 90% of related deaths occur [1]. In 2020, the World Health Organization (WHO) launched a global strategy focusing on HPV vaccination, regular screening, and treatment of precancerous lesions to achieve a 42% reduction in the incidence of cervical cancer by 2045 [2]. However, participation in cervical cancer screening programs, particularly Pap smear tests, remains low in LMICs, with rates of approximately 44% [3].

Multiple barriers to cervical cancer screening participation have been identified, with three key obstacles: low health literacy, psychosocial barriers, and practical limitations. Low health literacy leads to misconceptions, inadequate risk perceptions, and a lack of awareness of screening benefits. Psychosocial barriers include fatalistic attitudes toward cancer, embarrassment, fear of test-related pain, and anxiety about potential cancer diagnosis. Additionally, time constraints and screening costs are significant practical barriers [4]. A study by Aghaei et al. (2020) revealed that low health literacy among Iranian women is directly associated with lower screening participation and delayed diagnosis [5].

According to the Transtheoretical Model (TTM), health behaviors such as screening progress through stages, i.e., precontemplation, contemplation, preparation, action, and maintenance. Individuals may remain in one stage for extended periods or fail to reach the action stage [6]. Targeted interventions are thus essential to facilitate progression. Previous studies on changes in cervical cancer screening behavior have focused on the Health Belief Model [7], decision-making tools [8], in-person education or counseling [9], and text messaging or telephone counseling [10]. While these interventions have positive effects on behavior change stages, they face limitations. In-person education and counseling encounter challenges such as transportation difficulties, limited access in underserved areas, time constraints, high costs, and cultural barriers to discussing women’s health issues. To address these issues, the WHO emphasizes the need for remote educational and counseling interventions to improve reproductive health behaviors [11]. Digital interventions, such as social media-based education, have high potential to overcome access barriers; however, few of them incorporate theoretical frameworks in their design, leading to limited evidence quality [12].

Among health education models, the Fogg behavior model (FBM) provides an effective framework for designing virtual interventions. The FBM focuses on three constructs: motivation, ability, and triggers. Motivation includes subconstructs of pain/pleasure, fear/hope, and social rejection/acceptance. Ability relates to the ease or difficulty of performing a behavior and is influenced by six factors: time, cost, physical effort, mental effort, social norms, and alignment with individual routines. Prompts encourage behavior adoption and are most effective when delivered at a moment when both motivation and ability are sufficient [13]. Despite the significant potential of the FBM as a theoretical framework for health education in virtual settings, few studies have explored the impact of the FBM on changes in cervical cancer screening behavior.

Although being preventable and treatable in early stages, cervical cancer continues to impose a significant global burden, particularly in LMICs. Low health literacy and screening participation contribute to late diagnosis and disease progression. Social media, as a key source of information and behavior shaping, holds high potential for educational interventions, but the lack of theory-driven, culturally tailored interventions creates a critical gap. This randomized controlled trial addresses the gap by designing a virtual FBM-based educational intervention targeting health literacy and behavior change stages, with secondary outcomes of decision self-efficacy and anxiety.

Objectives {10}

The primary objectives of this randomized controlled trial are to determine the effect of FBM-based virtual education on cervical cancer health literacy and stages of behavior change for screening among women aged 21–65 years. Trial design {12}

This is a two-arm, parallel-group, randomized controlled trial with a blinded outcome assessor and data analyst, and a 1:1 allocation ratio will be used.

Methods: participants, interventions, and outcomes

Trial setting {13}

The study will involve women aged 21–65 years residing in Tabriz, the capital of East Azerbaijan Province, northwest Iran. Tabriz has 88 health centers accessible to individuals of varying socioeconomic backgrounds, serving as the first level of referral for urban women in Iran’s healthcare system. Each center employs at least one general physician, midwife, nurse, health worker, and environmental/occupational/mental health specialist. Midwives provide care for women from birth to post-menopause. Health centers maintain family health records, including contact details, for women aged ≥ 21 years. One-fifth of these centers, selected on the basis of socioeconomic diversity, will be included.

Intervention and comparator

Intervention and comparator description {15a}

Following randomization, the intervention group will receive FBM-based virtual education via the Iranian messaging platform Eitaa, which is widely accessible and similar to Telegram. Participants not registered on Eitaa will be enrolled prior to the intervention. The multimedia content (videos, audios, text messages, quizzes, and infographics) will be uploaded to a dedicated channel named “Zan-e-Salem” (meaning “Healthy Woman”) twice weekly, on Mondays and Thursdays at 5:00 PM, for 4 weeks. The intervention content, prepared by SM (BSc in Midwifery, MSc student in Midwifery), is evidence-based and aligned with FBM constructs. It includes spark messages (to increase motivation and reduce psychological barriers, e.g., “Think it’s all up to ‘whatever fate has in store’? Fate’s put your health in your hands! Plus, screening’s already prevented 80% of cancers!”), facilitator messages (to simplify practical barriers such as cost and access, e.g., “Did you know the test is affordable with insurance coverage and discounted centers? Your health deserves this easy investment! Worried about travel? No issue – choose a local center; the whole process takes even less than an hour – super convenient! One simple test protects you and your family.”) and trigger messages (to prompt action, e.g., “Book your Pap smear appointment with one call today! Go with a friend, set a reminder, you’re stronger than your fears! One small step, a healthy life!”). The validity of the content was confirmed by five midwives with ≥ 5 years of experience, two gynecologists, and three midwifery faculty members.

The control group will receive no intervention. To prevent control group access, Eitaa’s channel will disable forwarding and screenshots, and intervention group participants will be asked not to share content until outcome assessments are complete. Furthermore, selection of one-fifth of the 88 health centers minimizes the likelihood of interaction between the intervention and control groups, as the number of participants recruited from each center remains limited. SM will maintain contact with both groups via phone and messaging to minimize dropout, with control group interactions limited to non-intervention-related topics. If the intervention is found effective, the control group will receive content post-study for ethical considerations (Fig. 1).

Strategies to improve adherence to intervention/comparator {15c}

The intervention group participants will receive SMS notifications after each upload and be asked to confirm content reviews by sending a “like” via private message to the researcher (SM); they can seek clarification as needed. If no response is received within 24 h, a reminder SMS will be sent, followed by a phone call after 48 h if still unresponsive.

Adherence will be documented by recording the percentage of participants completing all eight sessions and the number of reminders needed. These data will be reviewed and documented weekly by the researcher (SM) to ensure consistent and complete delivery of the intervention to all participants in the intervention group. Nonadherence (missing > 2 sessions) will be noted as a protocol deviation and reported in the final analysis.

Sample size {19}

The sample size was calculated via G*Power 3.1.9.7 software. For the primary outcome of screening behavior, assuming a baseline Pap smear frequency of 46.5% in the control group (P1) and a 20% increase post-intervention (P2 = 66.4%) according to Ahadinezhad et al. [15], with 80% power, α = 0.05 (two-sided), and accounting for 10% dropout, the final sample size is 80 (40 per group). For health literacy, a sample size of 15 per group was calculated on the basis of a mean (SD) of 97.88 (12.7) [16], assuming a 20% increase, with 80% power and α = 0.05 (two-sided). Thus, a final sample size of 80 was adopted.

Recruitment {20}

The first author (SM) will visit selected health centers, extract contact information for women aged 21–65, and contact them to explain the study’s aims and procedures, and assess inclusion criteria. Interested women will be invited to attend an in-person session at the health center, where SM will provide detailed study information and obtain written informed consent, emphasizing voluntary participation and the right to withdraw at any time.

Assignment of interventions: randomization

Sequence generation: who will generate the sequence {21a}, type of randomization {21b}

Following the provision of written informed consent and collection of baseline data, participants will be randomized to the intervention or control group. An individual not involved in recruitment (EM) will generate the random allocation sequence using RAS software, with restricted block randomization (block sizes of 4 and 6) and a 1:1 allocation ratio.

Allocation concealment mechanism {22}, Implementation {23}

Allocation concealment will be achieved using sequentially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes. During the allocation procedure, SM will receive these envelopes, inscribe the full names of the participants on each, and open them sequentially in the presence of the respective participant.

Assignment of interventions: blinding

Who will be blinded {24a}, How will be blinding be achieved {24b}

Baseline data will be collected by SM through in-person interviews. Since SM will deliver the intervention and interact with participants, outcome assessments will be conducted by a trained research assistant blinded to group allocation. Additionally, data analysis will be performed by an investigator blinded to group allocation, with no prior involvement in participant recruitment or intervention implementation.

Procedure for unblinding if needed {24c}

N/A, because neither the intervention deliverer nor the participants will be blinded.

Data collection and management

Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes {25a}

Baseline data collection will involve in-person interviews to complete the demographic-obstetric questionnaire, the Bazaz health literacy questionnaire, the stages of change checklist, the Decision Self-Efficacy (DSE) scale, and the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI Y-1). Four weeks post-intervention, the Bazaz health literacy, DSE, and STAI Y-1 questionnaires will be completed via in-person interviews. The stages of change checklist will be completed 8 weeks post-intervention to measure the frequency of intention, preparation, and action stages.

Demographic-obstetric questionnaire:

A 22-item researcher-designed questionnaire will collect demographic and obstetric data, including age, spouse’s age, age at marriage, weight, height, marital status, education level, spouse’s education, employment status, spouse’s employment, household income, insurance status, number of pregnancies, number of deliveries, age at first pregnancy, age at menarche, contraception method, age at menopause (if applicable), daily hours spent reading newspapers/magazines/books, daily hours watching health-related TV programs, internet searches for health-related issues, and discussions with family/friends about Pap smear importance. The questionnaire’s face and content validity will be assessed by 10 faculty members from the Faculty of Nursing and Midwifery, Tabriz University of Medical Sciences, with revisions applied as needed.

Cervical Cancer Health Literacy questionnaire (Bazaz):

The Bazaz et al. (2019) questionnaire, developed in Iran for women of reproductive age attending health centers in Khuzestan Province, will be used to measure cervical cancer health literacy. This 27-item, five-option questionnaire measures five domains: access skills (5 items, score range 5–25), reading skills (4 items, score range 4–20), comprehension skills (5 items, score range 5–25), evaluation skills (5 items, score range 5–25), and decision-making/application skills (8 items, score range 8–40). The total score ranges from 27–135, with health literacy categorized as low (< 99), moderate (99–106), or adequate (> 106). The questionnaire’s internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha) is 0.706 [16].

Stages of the Change checklist:

The stages of the change checklist, which is based on the TTM by Prochaska and DiClemente, include 3 items scored on a 5-point Likert scale: “I have not had a Pap smear/cervical examination and have no intention to do so in the next 6 months” (precontemplation, score 1); “I have not had a Pap smear/cervical examination but intend to do so within the next 6 months” (contemplation, score 2); “I have not had a Pap smear/cervical examination but intend to do so within the next month” (preparation, score 3); “I have had a Pap smear/cervical examination once and intend to do so again in the coming year” (action, score 4); “I have had a Pap smear/cervical examination two or more times and intend to continue as scheduled” (maintenance, score 5). Owing to time constraints, only stages up to “action” will be measured. The tool’s reliability (kappa coefficient) is 0.78 over two measurements taken two weeks apart [17].

Decision Self-Efficacy scale (DSE):

The 11-item DSE scale, developed by O’Connor (1995), will measure decision self-efficacy for Pap smear testing in this study. The participants will respond on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = strongly agree, 4 = strongly disagree), with scores ranging from 0 to 44. The scale’s internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha) is 0.92 [18]. It has been validated in Iran for women of reproductive age, with high reliability reported [8].

State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI Y-1):

The STAI Y-1 measures state anxiety with 20 items assessing feelings “at this moment.” Responses are scored from 1 to 4, with 4 indicating high anxiety. Half of the items are reverse-scored. Total scores range from 20–80, with anxiety levels categorized as mild (20–31), moderate-low (32–42), moderate-high (43–53), relatively severe (54–64), severe (65–75), and very severe (≥ 76). Cronbach’s α for the state anxiety scale of the Iranian version is 0.70, reflecting acceptable reliability [19] (only state anxiety related to screening will be measured).

Plans to promote participant retention and complete follow-up {25b}

SM will maintain contact with both groups via phone and messaging to minimize dropout, with control group interactions limited to non-intervention-related topics.

Data management {26}, Confidentiality {33}

Data will be anonymized during collection, storage, and management, with confidentiality maintained throughout; only the research team will have access to identifiable data. The data will be entered into SPSS version 26, with one quarter of the entries randomly checked for accuracy after one-tenth of the data is entered. The final data entry will undergo range checks and frequency analysis.

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {27a}

Data normality will be assessed using the Kolmogorov‒Smirnov test, with logarithmic transformation applied if needed. For normally distributed data, independent t tests will compare baseline scores for health literacy, decision self-efficacy, and anxiety, whereas ANCOVA (controlling for baseline scores) will be used at 4 weeks post-intervention. For non-normally distributed data, despite logarithmic transformation, the Mann‒Whitney test will be applied. Frequencies will describe stages of change, and ordinal regression will compare stages at baseline and 8 weeks post-intervention. Demographic-obstetric data will be analyzed using descriptive statistics. The significance level will be set at 0.05.

Who will be included in each analysis {27b}

Data will be analyzed using the intention-to-treat principle, including all randomized participants in their assigned groups.

How missing data will be handled in the analysis {27c}

Given that questionnaires will be completed via interviews, missing data is expected to be minimal. However, multiple imputation is planned for any instances of missing data.

Discussion

FBM-based virtual education intervention is expected to improve cervical cancer screening participation by increasing health literacy and decision self-efficacy, and reducing screening-related anxiety. This supports the WHO’s goal of achieving a 42% reduction in cervical cancer incidence by 2045 [2]. Few studies have evaluated FBM-based interventions for cervical cancer screening. For example, Lee et al. (2014) conducted a 7-day text-based FBM intervention among 30 Korean-American women aged 21–29 years with no Pap smear history and reported increased knowledge and positive attitudes [20]. However, limitations included a small sample size, short intervention duration, a focus on minority groups, limited use of digital technology, a lack of a control group, and a focus on knowledge rather than health literacy.

The RCT design, with block randomization, allocation concealment, and blinded assessor/analyst, offers methodological rigor. Based on our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive, simultaneously targeting four aforementioned outcomes. Validated tools (the Bazaz questionnaire, O’Connor’s DSE, the TTM checklist, and the STAI Y-1) will measure the outcomes. Adherence monitoring ensures the quality of intervention implementation, enhancing internal validity.

Despite its strengths, the study has several limitations. Focusing on women aged 21–65 in Tabriz may limit generalizability to rural areas, other Iranian cities, or culturally/economically diverse populations. Using Eitaa assumes smartphone and internet access, potentially introducing selection bias and excluding women with economic or technical barriers. Future studies could explore offline or alternative delivery methods. Adherence monitoring via “like” feedback relies on self-reporting, which may be inaccurate if participants provide false feedback or partially engage. Future studies could use objective digital interaction tracking. Time constraints (due to the first author’s master’s thesis) limit assessment to the “action” stage, excluding “maintenance,” potentially providing an incomplete picture of behavior sustainability. Longer follow-up studies could address this issue.

Conclusion

This study has multidimensional implications. Clinically, it aims to increase screening participation by addressing psychological mechanisms. Methodologically, it provides a model for FBM-based interventions in women’s health. Policy-wide, it can contribute to women’s empowerment by promoting equitable access to cervical cancer screening through digital education, offering evidence for scaling virtual education in Iran and other LMICs, potentially informing national screening guidelines and breaking the cycle of late diagnosis and preventable mortality.
