Genetic associations of cholelithiasis and biliary tract cancer
Authors: Yong Jiang, Huimou Chen, Qing Tian, Chonghui Hu, Zhengyu Wu, Yuan Yuan, Xueliang Ouyang, Kunlong Chang, Daixin Wu, Juncheng Chen, Junwei Huang, Rufu Chen, Shangyou Zheng, Li Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•First comprehensive genetic study integrating MR, MTAG, and colocalization to dissect BTC pathogenesis.•Identified actionable genetic variants with dual roles in gallstone formation and carcinogenesis.•Demonstrated independent BTC risk pathways unrelated to cholelithiasis.

Introduction

Malignant tumors of biliary tract are notorious for their highly aggressive nature and dismal prognosis, posing significant challenges in the field of oncology [1,2]. Cholelithiasis, a condition that affects approximately 10 % to 20 % of the global adult population, not only imposes a substantial socioeconomic burden but also emerges as a potentially crucial risk factor for hepatobiliary-pancreatic cancers (HPCs) [[3], [4], [5]]. In numerous studies, a strikingly elevated incidence of cancer has been observed within the cholelithiasis cohort, with biliary tract cancer (BTC) being especially prominent. However, the etiology of BTC involves multiple risk factors, including shared risk factors such as age, obesity, and diabetes, as well as factors like immune cell traits and gut microbiota [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12]]. These factors collectively constitute a confounding influence; thus, the extent to which cholelithiasis truly contributes to cancer development remains a subject of intense debate.

The ABCG5/8 transporter, when functionally impaired, disrupts the normal transport of bile acids, thereby predisposing to the formation of cholelithiasis [3]. Cholelithiasis, in turn, induces abnormal bile acid metabolism, leading to its accumulation within the bile duct and further promoting the occurrence and development of BTC through mechanisms such as damaging the bile duct epithelial cells and triggering inflammatory responses [13,14]. Both cholelithiasis and the occurrence of BTC are associated with abnormal bile acid metabolism and other alterations in the biliary environment. We speculate that these two conditions may share certain pathogenic mechanisms or pathways, thereby leading to a significantly increased risk of BTC in the cholelithiasis population.

This study integrated genomic data from multiple public databases and GWAS summary statistics, using MR methodology to minimize confounding effects and systematically evaluate the assocition. Multi-trait analysis of GWAS (MTAG) was further utilized to explore the shared genetic architecture, and colocalization analysis was performed to dissect potential regulatory relationships. Finally, the relevance of risk loci was validated in a cholelithiasis-free population to explore independent genetic pathways.

Method

Study population

Our cohort data were derived from the UK Biobank (UKB), a comprehensive biomedical database that integrates extensive population health information and genetic research resources [15]. The UKB's continuous updates and longitudinal follow-ups make it an invaluable asset for long-term epidemiological and genetic studies. Detailed methods for participant identification, disease diagnosis, and genotyping are described in the Supporting Materials. We employed diagnostic codes from ICD-9, ICD-10, and UKB self-reported data to identify and categorize cases. Tumors originating from the intrahepatic bile ducts, extrahepatic bile ducts, gallbladder, and ampulla of Vater were defined as BTC. Given the anatomical proximity of the liver, biliary tract, and pancreas, as well as the potential for metastatic spread, only the first primary cancer diagnosis or synchronous cancers were considered in the analysis. The follow-up period was calculated from the date of enrollment in the UKB to the date of the first diagnosis of HPCs, the date of death, or December 19, 2022, whichever occurred first.

The UKB received ethics approval from the North West Multicenter Research Ethics Committee. All participants provided informed written consent, and they had the option to withdraw their personal data from the study at any time. The research was carried out utilizing resources from the UKB, with application number 85,224.

Single and two-sample MR

The MR analysis was conducted to determine the causal relationship between gallstones and BCT while controlling for confounding factors, with liver cancer and pancreatic cancer serving as comparison diseases [[16], [17], [18]]. The IVs were derived from the gallstone meta-analysis GWAS conducted by Fairfield et al., which identified up to 75 gallstone risk-associated loci, representing the most comprehensive genetic dataset available to date.

To estimate the causal relationships between cholelithiasis and HPCs at the individual level within the UKB cohort, we implemented a single MR approach utilizing the two-stage least squares (2SLS) method [19,20]. This analytical framework comprises two sequential regression stages: the first stage assesses the association between genetic risk variants and the exposure (cholelithiasis), while the second stage predicts the outcome (cancer risk) based on the genetically predicted exposure. The analysis was performed using the "ivtools" package [21] in R, with the "ts" estimation method specified within the "ivglm" function.

Additionally, two-sample MR analyses were conducted using the R software package TwoSampleMR (Version 0.6.8) [22]. Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) included in the analysis were those reaching genome-wide significance (GWAS p < 5 × 10⁻⁸) with a minor allele frequency (MAF) > 0.01. The causal relationships were evaluated using exposure and outcome GWAS summary statistics obtained from the FinnGen R11 dataset [23].In evaluating heterogeneity, the Cochrane’s Q test was used, with P-values < 0.05 interpreted as signifying substantial heterogeneity. To examine horizontal pleiotropy, the Mendelian Randomization Pleiotropy Residual Sum and Outlier (MR-PRESSO) method was applied alongside the MR-Egger approach; P-values below 0.05 were taken as evidence for the existence of horizontal pleiotropy. Additionally, a leave-one-out analysis was conducted to assess the sensitivity of results, where each SNP was excluded sequentially to determine if estimates were affected by outliers or bias.

GWAS and MTAG

In addition to FinnGen data, we obtained genome-wide association study (GWAS) data from the GWAS Catalog (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/) for relevant phenotypes, including cholelithiasis and BTC, based on multiple large-scale biobanks, such as the UKB and BioBank Japan [24]. Due to the absence of GWAS data encompassing all anatomical sites of BTC within the UKB cohort, we performed GWAS analyses using a machine-learning-based approach known as REGENIE [25] analyze BTC associations, applying a whole-genome regression model that accounts for quantitative and binary phenotypes.

We conducted three distinct analyses to explore the genetic associations between cholelithiasis and BTC traits using summary statistics from three cohorts, employing the MTAG framework. MTAG implements a generalized inverse-variance-weighted (IVW) meta-analysis approach, which enhances statistical power for locus identification by leveraging shared genetic architecture across related traits [26]. Additionally, we analyzed the associations of cholelithiasis with intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma and extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma phenotypes derived from the UKB. The summary statistics generated by MTAG are interpretable in a manner analogous to those from a univariate single-trait GWAS, providing a robust foundation for genetic discovery. We retained MTAG loci only if the variants demonstrated association with the examined trait (P < 0.1) in the corresponding original GWAS, with a consistent direction of effect between the MTAG and original GWAS results. This stringent filtering approach minimizes false positives and strengthens the validity of the identified genetic associations.

Functional mapping and annotation

We employed the Functional Mapping and Annotation of GWAS (FUMA) platform to conduct a comprehensive functional analysis on the summary statistics derived from MTAG [27]. Initially, all GWAS SNPs (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸) were subjected to clumping (r² < 0.6) to identify genomic risk loci, followed by a secondary clumping step (r² < 0.1) to define independent genetic signals. SNPs exhibiting pairwise linkage disequilibrium (LD) within the range of 0.1 ≤ r² < 0.6 or located within a 500 kb genomic window were grouped into the same LD block. SNPs that remained after the second clumping step were classified as independent signals, with the top SNP in each LD block (i.e., the one with the smallest P-value) designated as the lead genomic risk locus.

Subsequently, we performed detailed annotation and gene prioritization analyses, incorporating all independent SNPs identified. Pairwise LD calculations were conducted using the European reference population from the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3. For positional gene mapping, SNPs were assigned to protein-coding genes if they fell within a 10 kb window upstream or downstream of any transcript, as defined in Ensembl build v92 [28]. To enhance functional relevance, we further prioritized exonic and splice-site variants with a CADD score ≥ 12.37, a stringent threshold indicating high deleteriousness. This systematic approach enhances the biological relevance and interpretability of the GWAS findings.

Colocalization

We employed the coloc software [29] to conduct colocalization analysis, a Bayesian divisive clustering algorithm designed to identify shared genetic associations between traits within a genomic region using GWAS summary statistics. For each top signal identified from MTAG, we applied this method to a genomic region spanning ±500 kb centered on the top SNP to detect colocalized loci between quantitative trait loci (QTL) or splicing quantitative trait loci (sQTL) and BTC traits in the liver and other relevant tissues from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project. A posterior probability threshold of 0.5 was set to define significant colocalization events, ensuring robust identification of shared genetic signals across traits.

Bulk RNA-seq data analysis and single-cell RNA-seq data analysis

The bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data of cholangiocarcinoma tissues were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) cohort (http://tcga.xenahubs.net) [30]. All gene expression values were pre-normalized for downstream analysis. For single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data, we utilized a publicly available dataset (GSE138709) from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, which profiles BTC tissues at single-cell resolution. Clustering analysis was performed using the Tumor Immune Single-cell Hub (TISCH) database [31] (http://tisch.comp-genomics.org/), a comprehensive resource for single-cell transcriptomic data in tumor microenvironments. Differential expression analyses were conducted across distinct cell types to identify cell type-specific transcriptional signatures and their potential roles in BTC pathogenesis.

Carcinogenesis and statistical analyses

We employed a hard-calling approach to convert imputed genotype dosages into discrete genotypes (0, 1, 2) by selecting the genotype with the highest posterior probability. For colocalized loci, cancer incidence analysis was performed by grouping individuals carrying one or two effect alleles (1/2) as one group and those without the effect allele as the control group. Variants with a MAF below 0.05 were excluded from the analysis. Cox proportional hazards models were utilized to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and their corresponding 95 % confidence interval (CI). In cases where the allele exhibited a protective effect, the reference allele was swapped, and Cox regression was repeated to ensure robustness. All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.3.3. To visualize cumulative incidence, we employed the R packages “survival” and “survminer.” A two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

MR results

After excluding SNPs that are related to confounding factors associated with cancer as previously reported, such as BMI, and those that cannot be obtained from the imputed data of the UKB, a total of 56 variants were retained as IVs for the single MR analysis. The 2SLS analysis (Fig. 1A) revealed that the risk of BTC significantly increased with each standard deviation rise in cholelithiasis, with the log odds increasing by 4.98 (95 % CI: 2.00–7.96, p < 0.001). In contrast, no statistically significant changes were observed in the risks for HCC (p > 0.05).Fig. 1Results of the single MR two-stage least squares method and the two-sample MR.Fig. 1

Results of the single MR two-stage least squares method and the two-sample MR.

In our two-sample MR analysis, after excluding IVs associated with confounding factors, weak correlations, and SNPs with significant heterogeneity, a causal and positive association between cholelithiasis and BTC was demonstrated using the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method, yielding an odds ratio (OR) of 1.383 (95 % CI: 1.098–1.742, P = 0.006) (Fig. 1B and Figure S2). However, the weighted median (WM) sensitivity analysis did not reach statistical significance, with an OR of 1.343 (95 % CI: 0.980–1.841, P = 0.067). For HCC and PC, no significant causal relationships were observed (p > 0.05) (Fig. 1B, Figure S3 and S4). Among the IVs, neither weak instrument bias nor significant heterogeneity indicators were detected (Table S3). Additionally, evaluation via the MR-PRESSO method revealed no distinguishable outliers (Table S3). Furthermore, the robustness of the dataset was further validated through leave-one-out analysis (Figure S2, Figure S3 and Figure S4).

Novel loci identified in GWAS and MTAG analysis

The results of the BTC GWAS in the UKB population are summarized in Figure S5. When integrating BTC with cholelithiasis in MTAG, no significant risk loci for BTC were identified (Figure S6). Similarly, no significant loci were detected in the Finnish or Japanese databases (Figures S7 and S8).

Within the UKB dataset, no significant loci were associated with intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (Figure S9), whereas hundreds of significant risk loci were identified for extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (Fig. 2). After filtering out loci with a P-value > 0.1 in the original GWAS, performing LD analysis, gene mapping, and functional annotation, we identified 20 independent signals and 5 novel lead genomic risk loci for BTC (Table 2). After performing LD analysis (r² < 0.1) using the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 data, we identified two independent loci, rs6741243 and rs7599981(Fig. 4C).Fig. 4Association between ABCG5, ABCG8, and LRPPRC loci with the risk of BTC, QTL, and cholelithiasis.Fig. 4

The HR for the effect allele of rs6741243 showed a 41 % increased risk compared to the reference group (95 % CI: 13 %–75 %), while the HR for rs7599981 indicated a 35 % increased risk (95 % CI: 12 %–63 %).The incidence of BTC in the effect alleles group and the control group is shown in Figure 6A and 6B, with significant differences observed in both groups Both rs6741243 and rs7599981 were significantly associated with cholelithiasis (Fig. 4B).

Further analysis revealed that rs6741243 exhibited a significant negative correlation with splicing quantitative trait loci (sQTL) of the liver gene ABCG5 (beta = −0.34, P = 0.0028) (Fig. 4A). Although rs7599981 lacked available QTL and sQTL data, it showed strong LD (r² = 0.91) with rs4953029, which was significantly associated with liver gene expression (beta = 0.64, P = 9.5 × 10–11).

Gene expression analysis

In cholangiocarcinoma tissues from TCGA dataset, the expression of LRPPRC was significantly higher compared to normal tissues (P < 0.001) (Fig. 5D). In contrast, ABCG5 exhibited significant downregulation in tumor tissues (P < 0.001) (Fig. 5C). ScRNA-seq analysis further revealed that LRPPRC expression was enriched in tumor cells, showing a significantly higher expression level compared to normal bile duct cells (log2FC = 1.0, P < 0.001) (Fig. 5F and 5I). Discussion

We conducted a large-scale, comprehensive study to explore the genetic relationship between cholelithiasis and BTC, we discovered multiple loci near the coding regions of ABCG5/8 and LRPPRC, particularly in the intermediate regions, that are associated with both cholelithiasis and BTC. Notably, rs6741243 and rs4953029 may simultaneously influence cholelithiasis and BTC by modulating the expression of genes such as ABCG5, ABCG8, and LRPPRC.

The current consensus is that cholelithiasis increases the risk of BTC. Previous studies, mainly relying on prospective cohorts or case-control designs, seek higher-level evidence [5]. While some meta-analyses support an increased risk of BTC and PC in gallstone patients, significant heterogeneity raises concerns about reliability [[32], [33], [34], [35]] This study used single-sample analysis in the UKB and two-sample analysis with a Finnish cohort, applying MR to reduce confounding. Both analyses consistently found a significant link between cholelithiasis and BTC risk, but no such link was observed for HCC or PC. Cross-cohort consistency strengthens the causal relationship, highlighting the complex role of cholelithiasis as a potential cancer risk factor.

Genetic factors play a significant role in the pathogenesis of cholelithiasis, with approximately 25 % of the risk attributed to genetic determinants [3]. Among these genetic factors, variants in the ABCG5/ABCG8 genes are particularly critical. When performing MTAG for cholangiocarcinoma across multiple loci using different databases, no new significant loci were identified. This may be due to high heterogeneity across multiple loci and the low incidence rate of cholangiocarcinoma, which reduces the statistical power of GWAS. However, when extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma and cholelithiasis were used as phenotypes for MTAG, five lead significant loci were identified. Notably, ABCG5/8 and LRPPRC and are located in close proximity within the coding region. Colocalization analysis revealed that the MTAG signals for extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma and ABCG5/LRPPRC colocalized in liver tissue.

Given the potential link between cholelithiasis and BTC, we excluded patients with cholelithiasis and used Cox regression to independently assess the impact of genetic loci on BTC risk. This analysis identified multiple loci within the coding regions of the chromosome associated with BTC risk. Following LD analysis, the risk genes were categorized into two groups: ABCG5-related and LRPPRC-related.

Our analysis identified that the rs6741243 downregulates the sQTL of the ABCG5 gene, which is known to contribute to cholelithiasis. ABCG5 forms a heterodimer with ABCG8 (ABCG5/G8), which functions as a cholesterol transporter playing a central role in maintaining systemic cholesterol homeostasis and promoting hepatic cholesterol excretion into bile. Dysfunction of this complex is a major genetic susceptibility factor for cholesterol gallstone formation [36]. Although previous studies have not reported a direct link between reduced ABCG5 expression and BTC, our validation in biliary tract tumor tissues suggests a potential association. Studies have shown that ABCG5 and ABCG8 contribute to cholelithiasis by regulating bile acid metabolism. Beyond its role in gallstone formation, dysfunction of ABCG5/G8 may lead to alterations in bile composition (such as cholesterol supersaturation) and persistent bile acid toxicity stress, thereby creating a microenvironment conducive to the malignant transformation of biliary epithelial cells [37]. Our study found that even in the absence of cholelithiasis, the loci associated to ABCG5 may also increase the incidence of cholangiocarcinoma, potentially due to its role in bile acid dysregulation. Notably, clinical studies have found significantly upregulated ABCG5 mRNA expression in the gallbladder epithelial cells of patients with cholesterol gallstones, suggesting a complex regulatory pattern in biliary tract diseases that may play different roles at various pathological stages [38]. This highlights an independent carcinogenic effect of ABCG5-mediated bile acid disorder, separate from its role in cholelithiasis.

Rs7599981 was associated with cancer risk, although its functional role remains unconfirmed. In contrast, rs4953029 showed a significant correlation with the sQTL of LRPPRC in the liver, and the high expression of LRPPRC in tumor tissues and tumor cells further supports this association. LRPPRC is a multifunctional protein localized to mitochondria that is essential for maintaining mitochondrial mRNA stability, polyadenylation, and coordinated translation [39]. Its deficiency leads to severe mitochondrial dysfunction [40]. Although there is no direct evidence linking the cancer risk loci to cholelithiasis, rs4953029 is located in the 3′ untranslated region variant of ABCG8, suggesting it may contribute to cholelithiasis by altering ABCG8 expression. Studies have shown that LRPPRC is overexpressed in multiple cancers [41]. Recent studies have also found that LRPPRC can promote tumorigenesis by regulating epigenetic mechanisms such as m6A RNA modification (e.g., in multiple myeloma), indicating that it may drive tumor progression by influencing post-transcriptional regulation across different cancer types [42]. LRPPRC may directly influence mRNA expression levels through its interacting PPR modules, thereby affecting tumor proliferation and invasion [43].

Limitations of th absence of QTL or sQTL evidence supporting the effect of rs4953029 and other loci in strong LD on ABCG8 complicates the identification of the specific mechanism responsible for gene expression changes associated with cholelithiasis. This lack of evidence makes it challenging to determine which variant influences ABCG8 expression and contributes to cholelithiasis susceptibility. Further research is needed to elucidate the precise genetic mechanisms underlying this association.

In conclusion, variants near rs6741243 and rs7599981 may simultaneously influence cholelithiasis and BTC by modulating the expression of genes such as ABCG5, ABCG8, and LRPPRC.
