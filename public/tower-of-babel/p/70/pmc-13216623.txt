Enhanced adsorption of nalidixic acid from aqueous solutions using molybdenum oxide-decorated graphene oxide
Authors: Samira Farnia, Masoumeh Ghahremani, Reza Rasuli, Hamed Bahrami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The persistence of antibiotic residues in aquatic environments poses significant ecological and public health challenges, necessitating the development of advanced remediation strategies. This study investigates the efficacy of graphene oxide (GO) and molybdenum oxide-decorated graphene oxide (GO/MoO3) adsorbents for the removal of nalidixic acid (NA), persistent antibiotic pollutant, from aqueous solutions. GO was synthesized via a modified Hummers’ method, while MoO3 nanoparticles, produced by electric arc discharge, were anchored onto GO sheets. Comprehensive characterization using FTIR, XRD, BET, SEM, and EDX, confirmed the structural integrity and enhanced surface functionality of the GO/MoO3 composite. Under optimized conditions (pH 7.2; 40 mg L− 1 initial NA concentration; 1 mg adsorbent dosage; 2 h contact time), GO achieved 87% removal efficiency and 130 mg g− 1 adsorption capacity. In comparison, GO/MoO3 reached 91% removal efficiency and an adsorption capacity of 135 mg g− 1 at an initial NA concentration of 80 mg L− 1. Adsorption followed pseudo-second-order kinetics and the Freundlich isotherm, indicating multilayer chemisorption with the rate-limiting step governed by synergistic interactions between MoO3’s redox-active sites and GO’s oxygenated functional groups. These results highlight the potential of GO/MoO3 as a scalable and efficient adsorbent for mitigating pharmaceutical pollutants in water treatment systems.

Introduction

Pharmaceutical pollutants, particularly antibiotics, present significant challenges to environmental and public health due to their widespread release into aquatic ecosystems through manufacturing effluents, improper disposal of expired medications, and metabolic excretion1–3. Their high aqueous solubility contributes to their persistence in water bodies, while conventional treatment methods often prove ineffective at removing these contaminants4–6. Antibiotics exhibit prolonged environmental half-lives and are associated with microbial resistance and bioaccumulation, further amplifying the risks to ecological and human health7–9. Despite these threats, global antibiotic consumption continues to rise, underscoring the urgent need for advanced remediation strategies10.

Nalidixic acid (NA), a first-generation quinolone antibiotic, inhibits bacterial DNA gyrase and is widely used to treat urinary tract infections and aquaculture-related bacterial diseases11. However, its persistence in aquatic environments raises concerns regarding antibiotic resistance, neurotoxicity, and gastrointestinal disturbances in humans, necessitating advanced removal strategies due to its low biodegradability and high solubility in water.

The remediation of pollutants in wastewater typically employs mechanical, chemical, or biological methods, The selection of an appropriate approach is guided by factors including contaminant type, initial and final concentrations, and economic feasibility12.

Adsorption-based techniques are widely employed for pharmaceutical removal due to their cost-effectiveness, simplicity, and high efficiency, leveraging the tunable surface chemistry and high porosity of materials, including activated carbon13, clay minerals14,15, zeolites16, layered double hydroxides (LDHs)17, MXenes18, and graphene-based nanomaterials19. These materials leverage tunable surface chemistry, high porosity, and selective binding mechanisms to effectively remove both organic and inorganic pollutants from water20,21.

Past studies have focused on single-material adsorbents such as activated carbon and zeolites, while current research emphasizes hybrid nanocomposites to enhance adsorption capacity and selectivity. Future developments aim to integrate these materials into scalable filtration systems and smart membranes for real-time pollutant monitoring and removal.

Graphene-based nanomaterials, particularly graphene oxide (GO), have garnered significant attention as advanced adsorbents for water remediation due to their moderate surface area, tunable oxygen functionalities, and strong affinity for organic contaminants22,23. GO consists of a two-dimensional carbon lattice with oxygen-containing groups (such as epoxy, hydroxyl, and carboxyl), enhancing its reactivity and water compatibility. GO’s hydrophilic nature, driven by oxygen-containing groups, facilitates the adsorption of polar organic compounds. Unlike pristine graphene, the oxygen-rich surface of GO confers superior biocompatibility and environmental compatibility, making it particularly effective for the sequestration of pharmaceutical pollutants24. Recent advances in GO synthesis and surface engineering, particularly in optimizing functional groups for water purification, have further solidified its role in wastewater treatment, emphasizing its scalability and efficiency in removing recalcitrant compounds such as antibiotics25,26.

GO has shown promising adsorption efficiency toward various quinolone antibiotics due to its high surface area, abundant oxygenated functional groups, and ability to engage in π–π stacking and hydrogen bonding. For example, sulfonated GO achieved high removal efficiencies (> 95%) and capacities up to ~ 1000 µmol g− 1 for ciprofloxacin under optimized conditions, outperforming pristine GO in several studies27. Similarly, modified GO systems demonstrate effective uptake of quinolones via heterogeneous multilayer mechanisms28.

Further enhancement of GO’s adsorptive properties is often achieved through hybridization with metal oxides to create synergistic composites. Among these, molybdenum trioxide (MoO3) is a promising candidate. MoO3, a wide-bandgap semiconductor (~ 3 eV), exhibits high ionization energy (9.7 eV) and work function (6.9 eV), enabling strong Lewis acid-base interactions with polar organic molecules29–31. These attributes, coupled with its robust thermal and chemical stability and intrinsic redox activity (Mo6+ ↔ Mo5+ transitions), have spurred innovations in hybrid material design for water treatment32. For instance, Kaur et al. demonstrated that MoO3-decorated starbon exhibits multifunctional efficacy, leveraging synergistic effects to enhanced adsorption and catalytic degradation32. Similarly, Meyer et al. established that MoO3-decorated graphene enhances charge transport in optoelectronic devices33, while hydrothermally synthesized rGO/MoO3 composites show exceptional electrocatalytic activity34. However, many of these synthesis routes, such as hydrothermal methods, can be complex or costly. In contrast, the arc-discharge method offers higher reproducibility and lower costs due to its semi-automated setup35, as demonstrated by Servati and Rasuli (2019), who developed efficient MoO3-decorated rGO via arc-discharge for electrochemical applications36.

Current strategies for NA removal employ adsorbents such as activated carbons, functionalized carbon nanotubes, and biodegradable materials such as chitosan-based composites, which offer eco-friendly alternatives for pollutant removal37,38. Advanced materials like NiZrAl-layered double hydroxide-graphene oxide-chitosan composites achieve an adsorption capacity of 277.79 mg g− 1 via chemisorption39, while biocompatible bovine serum albumin (BSA) nanoparticles exhibit 240 mg g− 1 capacity through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobicity40. These approaches are often modeled using Freundlich isotherms and pseudo-second-order kinetics, highlighting their multilayer adsorption and reaction-driven mechanisms.

The design of hybrid nanocomposites, particularly those combining graphene oxide with metal oxides, has emerged as a powerful strategy to enhance adsorption performance by leveraging synergistic effects. This approach is exemplified in several recent studies. For instance, Pytlakowska et al.41 demonstrated the exceptional adsorption capacity of a MoS2-reduced GO composite for heavy metal ions, attributing this performance to the abundant sulfur sites and the large surface area. Similarly, Al Nafiey et al.42 reported that a Co3O4-rGO nanocomposite exhibited high efficiency in adsorbing Cr(VI) and organic dyes, facilitated by its magnetic properties and enhanced surface functionality. In a distinct approach focusing on pharmaceutical pollutants, Akpotu and Moodley43,44 developed mesoporous silica (MCM-41/MCM-48) encapsulated with GO/rGO, achieving remarkable adsorption capacities for drugs like acetaminophen and caffeine. They highlighted the critical role of hydrophobicity and π-π interactions in the adsorption mechanism. Furthermore, Mohan et al.45 utilized MgO nanocube-decorated GO for Pb(II) removal in a fixed-bed column system, demonstrating the practical applicability and scalability of such nanocomposites for continuous water treatment. These studies collectively underscore the versatility and efficacy of GO-metal oxide hybrids.

Building upon this foundation, the present study addresses the critical need in scalable, cost-effective adsorbents for NA by developing a novel GO/MoO3 nanocomposite. The innovation of this study lies in the development of GO/MoO3 nanocomposite synthesized via a cost-effective electric arc discharge method for MoO3 nanoparticle production, followed by electrostatic anchoring onto GO sheets. Unlike conventional hydrothermal methods, this approach ensures high reproducibility and scalability while enhancing adsorption performance through synergistic interactions between MoO3’s redox-active sites and GO’s oxygenated functional groups. By targeting NA, a recalcitrant antibiotic pollutant, this work contributes to addressing the pressing need for efficient and scalable adsorbents for pharmaceutical remediation, offering a practical solution for water treatment applications with potential for industrial adoption.

Given the escalating concerns over antibiotic contamination and the limitations of conventional removal methods, this study aims to comprehensively evaluate and compare the efficacy of GO and GO/MoO3 nanoadsorbents for the removal of NA from aqueous solutions. The research encompasses the synthesis and detailed characterization of these nanomaterials, followed by a systematic investigation of their batch adsorption performance under varying conditions, including pH, adsorbent dosage, initial NA concentration, and contact time. The enhanced performance of GO/MoO3 is attributed to: (i) synergistic interactions between MoO3’s redox-active sites and GO’s oxygenated functional groups; (ii) facilitated adsorbent separation due to increased particle density; (iii) enhanced hydrophobicity resulting from partial GO reduction; and (iv) improved dispersion of GO sheets, mitigating aggregation challenges25. Furthermore, the study systematically examines adsorption kinetics and isotherm behavior, providing critical insights into the real-world applicability of these composites for pharmaceutical pollutant removal in water treatment systems.

Experimental section

Reagents and equipment

All chemicals were of analytical grade and used without further purification: graphite powder (99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich) for GO synthesis via the modified Hummers’ method, which employed potassium permanganate (KMnO4), sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 98%), and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, 30%); molybdenum trioxide (MoO3, 99.9%) and ethanol (C2H5OH, 99.8%) for the preparation of the GO/MoO3 adsorbent; and NA (C12H12N2O3, 98%, Merck) as the target adsorbate. Ultrapure water served as the solvent for all aqueous solutions. The concentration of NA in aqueous solutions was quantified using an ion mobility spectrometer (IMS300, TOF Tech Pars Co.). Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was performed on a Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS10 spectrometer (resolution: 4 cm− 1, spectral range: 400–4000 cm− 1) to analyze functional groups. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were acquired using a Philips PW 1730 diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å, 40 kV, 30 mA) over a 2θ range of 5°–80°. Morphological characterization was conducted via scanning electron microscopy (SEM) using a TESCAN VEGA-II instrument. BET (Brunauer-Emmett-Teller) surface area and pore characteristics were measured using nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms (Type IV, mesoporous), with standard deviations (SD) reflecting triplicate measurements (RSD < 5%).

Synthesis of GO

GO was synthesized via the modified Hummers’ method36. Briefly, 1.0 g of graphite powder was oxidized in 15 mL of concentrated sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 98%) under stirring, followed by the gradual addition of 3 g of potassium permanganate (KMnO4). The mixture was then diluted with deionized water, inducing a color transition from dark green to brown. Excess permanganate was neutralized with hydrogen peroxide, yielding a suspension with a characteristic absorbance peak at ~ 230 nm, indicative of GO formation. The product was centrifuged (10,000 rpm, 20 min), washed repeatedly with HCl (5%) and deionized water until a final pH of 6.5–7.0 was achieved, removing residual ions. Strong oxidants (KMnO4 and H2SO4) were used to introduce oxygen-containing functional groups, enhancing GO’s adsorption capacity, while sonication (1 h, 40 kHz) exfoliated graphite oxide into single or few-layer GO sheets. The GO slurry was dried at 60 °C for 24 h and annealed at 400 °C (2 h, air) to enhance the structural order of GO layers (as confirmed by XRD) and remove impurities while retaining oxygen functional groups. TGA studies indicate that GO undergoes significant weight loss (150–250 °C) due to the removal of oxygen-containing groups, with carbon decomposition occurring above 600°C46.

Synthesis of MoO3 nanoparticles

MoO3 nanoparticles were synthesized via a semi-automatic electric arc discharge method36,47. Two high-purity molybdenum electrodes (99.8%, 3 mm diameter) were positioned at a 45° angle within a water-filled glass reactor. A direct current of 10 A was applied across the electrodes, generating a stable electric arc. The high-temperature plasma (~ 5000 K) induced anode erosion, releasing molybdenum vapor into the aqueous medium. Simultaneous electrolysis of water produced reactive oxygen species, facilitating the oxidation of molybdenum to form MoO3 nanoparticles. Rapid quenching in the cold aqueous environment (≤ 25 °C) promoted the formation of spherical nanoparticle morphology and minimized agglomeration. The process was sustained for 30 min, yielding a dark crimson colloidal suspension of MoO3 nanoparticles.

Decoration of GO with MoO3 nanoparticles

The GO/MoO3 adsorbent was prepared by dispersing 3 mL of GO (0.5 mg mL− 1 in ultrapure water) into the as-synthesized MoO3 colloidal suspension (500 mL in ultrapure water). The mixture was stirred for 24 h to facilitate electrostatic anchoring of MoO3 nanoparticles onto GO sheets. Post-stirring, the composite was vacuum-dried at 60 °C and annealed at 400 °C (2 h, air) to enhance interfacial bonding via thermal oxidation, thereby preserving GO’s oxygen functional groups. This step ensured robust integration of MoO3 nanoparticles onto the GO matrix while maintaining crystallinity.

Adsorption experiments

A stock solution of NA (1000 mg L− 1) was prepared by dissolving 10 mg of NA in 10 mL of ultrapure water. Calibration standards (1–40 mg L− 1) were generated via serial dilution. Sample analysis was performed using IMS, with 1 µL aliquots injected via a microsyringe. Triplicate measurements were acquired for each concentration, with a relative standard deviation (RSD) of less than 3%, thereby ensuring high precision. The calibration curve was constructed by plotting signal intensity (nA) against NA concentration.

Adsorption studies were conducted by adding specified masses of GO or GO/MoO3 nanoadsorbent to 2 mL of NA solutions under controlled conditions (pH 7.2, 25 °C). All experiments were performed in triplicate, with results reported as mean ± SD and RSD below 3%. The mixtures were agitated on a magnetic stirrer at 500 rpm, followed by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 20 min to separate the adsorbent. Residual NA concentrations were quantified via IMS. Key parameters - initial pH (2–9), adsorbent dosage (0.001–0.006 g), initial NA concentration (30–80 mg L−1), and contact time (0.5–5 h) - were systematically evaluated. Data analysis, including regression fitting for isotherm and kinetic models and graphical representations, was performed using OriginPro 2019 (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA). All experiments were performed in triplicate, with mean values and SDs used for analysis.

Desorption and reusability experiments

GO and GO/MoO3 (dosage of 0.001 g in 2 mL solution) were equilibrated with 40 mg L− 1 NA solution at pH 7.2 and 25 °C for 2 h in a temperature-controlled shaker. After adsorption, the NA-loaded adsorbent was separated by centrifugation (4000 rpm for 20 min). Desorption was performed using 10 mL of ethanol 70% (v/v) containing 0.5 M HCl for 180 min under magnetic stirring (500 rpm) at 25 °C. The regenerated adsorbent was washed several times with ultrapure water, dried at 60 °C for 12 h, and reused for up to six consecutive cycles. The desorption solution was centrifuged, filtered, and analyzed using IMS to quantify the released NA.

Results and discussion

Adsorbents characterization

FTIR spectrum of GO and GO/MoO3

FTIR spectroscopy was employed to validate the successful synthesis of the GO/MoO3 nanoadsorbent and to characterize the functional groups present in both GO and the composite material.

Fig. 1FTIR spectra of GO and GO/MoO3 adsorbents.

FTIR spectra of GO and GO/MoO3 adsorbents.

The GO spectrum (Fig. 1, blue trace) displayed characteristic peaks at 3440 cm− 1 (O-H stretching), 2944 cm− 1 (C-H stretching), 1733 cm− 1 (C = O stretching), 1628 cm− 1 (C = C stretching), and 1077 cm− 1 (C-O-C stretching). The GO/MoO3 spectrum (Fig. 1, red trace) exhibits additional bands attributed to Mo-O-Mo (991 cm− 1) and Mo = O (857 cm− 1) stretching vibrations, along with Mo-O bending modes in the 460–480 cm− 1 region, confirming the integration of MoO3 into the GO matrix. A shoulder on the 591 cm− 1 band is attributed to overlapping Mo-O-Mo stretching modes. The partial reduction of GO during composite formation was further evidenced by attenuated intensities of O-H (3440 cm− 1) and C = O (1733 cm− 1) bands, alongside enhanced C = C (1628 cm− 1) signals. This signifies restoration of the graphitic sp2 network, consistent with Raman spectroscopy data from our prior study using identical arc-discharge synthesis36, in which reduced GO/MoO3 exhibited decreased ID/IG ratios (~ 1.22) relative to GO (~ 1.05), confirming sp2 domain regeneration. Deconvolution of FTIR spectra showed that Mo-O-Mo (991 cm− 1) and Mo = O (857 cm− 1) bands in GO/MoO3 contributed approximately 15% and 10% to the total peak area, respectively, confirming successful MoO3 integration. This quantitative analysis aligns with established protocols for nanohybrid characterization48. Collectively, these spectral alterations demonstrate the synergistic chemical interactions between MoO3 and oxygenated moieties on GO, underscoring the efficacy of the synthesis protocol.

To further elucidate the elemental composition and dispersion of MoO3, SEM-EDX elemental mapping and spectrum analysis were conducted (Fig. 5). The mapping illustrates the distribution of carbon (C), oxygen (O), and molybdenum (Mo) across the composite, with an atomic composition of approximately 0.1% C, 79.22% O, and 20.68% Mo, and a weight% of 61% Mo. This high Mo content confirms the successful integration of MoO3, while the elevated oxygen level reflects contributions from both GO’s oxygenated functional groups and the MoO3 phase. The low carbon signal is attributed to the high MoO3-to-GO ratio (500 mL of MoO3 suspension vs. 3 mL of GO at 0.5 mg mL− 1) and partial reduction of GO during the 400 °C annealing process, consistent with the synthesis procedure (Sect. 2.4) and supported by arc-discharge synthesis literature36. The uniform dispersion of Mo, despite some clustering, validates the electrostatic anchoring of MoO3 nanoparticles onto the GO matrix, aligning with FTIR (Mo-O-Mo and Mo = O bands at 991 cm− 1 and 857 cm− 1) and XRD (orthorhombic MoO3 phase) results. This distribution enhances surface heterogeneity, contributing to the improved adsorption capacity observed in batch experiments (135 mg g− 1 for GO/MoO3 vs. 130 mg g− 1 for GO, Sect. 3.3). Although EDX is surface-sensitive and may be biased toward MoO3-rich regions48, the results provide robust evidence of the composite’s composition and support the proposed adsorption mechanism (Sect. 3.2).

BET analysis and pore structure characteristics

This analysis is particularly important for supporting the claim regarding nanoparticle aggregation at higher adsorbent dosages (Sect. 3.4) and for understanding the multilayer adsorption mechanism (Sect. 3.6). The data were obtained from N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms (77 K, Type IV, mesoporous behavior), and the pore size distribution was evaluated using the Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) method. The BET results show that pristine GO exhibited a specific surface area of 150.0 ± 5.0 m2 g− 1, which is lower than the theoretical range reported for pure graphene oxide (200–800 m2/g)52. This reduction can be attributed to the partial restacking of GO nanosheets during the modified Hummers synthesis and subsequent annealing at 400 °C. This observation is consistent with the XRD patterns (Fig. 2, Sect. 3.1.2), where the (001) peak at 2θ ≈ 10.2° confirms the structural ordering and stacking of GO layers.

In contrast, the GO/MoO3 nanocomposite showed a significantly lower surface area (111.0 ± 4.0 m2 g− 1) and total pore volume (0.10 ± 0.01 cm3 g− 1) compared to GO (0.13 ± 0.01 cm3 g− 1). This decrease is attributed to the deposition and aggregation of MoO3 nanoparticles on the GO surface, which block some of the accessible surface area and pore channels. This phenomenon was further supported by SEM observations (Fig. 4, Sect. 3.1.3).

The average pore diameters were similar for both materials (3.52 ± 0.10 nm for GO and 3.41 ± 0.08 nm for GO/MoO3), indicating a mesoporous structure, which is in good agreement with the multilayer adsorption behavior described by the Freundlich isotherm (Sect. 3.6). The slight decrease in rp, peak from 2.5 ± 0.2 nm (GO) to 2.3 ± 0.2 nm (GO/MoO3) further suggests partial pore blockage caused by MoO3 nanoparticles.

These BET findings strongly support the hypothesis proposed in Sect. 3.4, which attributes the decrease in adsorption efficiency at higher adsorbent dosages (> 0.001 g) to nanoparticle aggregation. Such aggregation, particularly pronounced in the GO/MoO3 nanocomposite, reduces the effective surface area and limits access to active sites, including oxygen-containing functional groups on GO and Mo6+ Lewis acid centers. Similar behavior has been reported in previous studies on GO-based nanocomposites, where reduced surface area was linked to nanoparticle deposition and clustering53.

Effect of initial pH on NA adsorption

The pH of the aqueous medium plays a critical role in determining the ionization state of NA and the surface charge characteristics of GO and GO/MoO3 adsorbents, directly regulating their adsorption performance. Under standardized experimental conditions (initial NA concentration: 40 mg L− 1, adsorbent mass: 0.001 g, contact time: 2 h, temperature: 25 °C), maximum removal efficiencies of 90 ± 0.15% for GO and 94 ± 0.15% for GO/MoO3 were achieved within the pH range of 5–7, with optimal adsorption observed at pH 5, as demonstrated in Fig. 6. The initial NA concentration of 40 mg L− 1 was selected to optimize adsorption performance, as it yielded maximum removal efficiencies, enabling evaluation of adsorbent capacity under controlled, high-concentration conditions, though typical effluent concentrations may be lower.

The 4% increase in removal efficiency for GO/MoO3 compared to GO results from the synergistic effects of MoO3’s Lewis acid sites and enhanced surface heterogeneity, though GO’s oxygenated groups already provide substantial adsorption capacity for NA, limiting the relative improvement39,54.

Effect of initial pH on NA removal efficiency by GO and GO/MoO3 (40 mg L− 1 NA, 0.001 g adsorbent, 2 h, 25 °C). Error bars indicate ± SD from triplicate measurements (pH 2: GO, 29 ± 0.8%; GO/MoO3, 34 ± 0.11%; pH 4: GO, 74 ± 0.09%; GO/MoO3, 75 ± 1.5%; pH 8: GO, 75 ± 2%; GO/MoO3, 67 ± 1.2%).

At pH < 4.5, NA predominantly exists in its neutral form, characterized by a protonated carboxyl group (-COOH), while the surfaces of both GO and GO/MoO3 remain positively charged, resulting in diminished adsorption efficiency55. As the pH increases to 4.5–6.0, NA transitions to a zwitterionic state (-COO− and -NH3+), driven by its ionization equilibria (carboxylic acid pKa1 ≈ 5.95; pyridinyl N pKa2 ≈ 8.54)56. This facilitates electrostatic attraction between NA’s -COO− groups and Mo6+ Lewis acid sites in GO/MoO3, while simultaneously, the adsorbents develop a negative surface charge under these conditions. This shift facilitates electrostatic attraction between the deprotonated carboxyl groups (-COO−) of NA and the Lewis acid sites (Mo6+) in GO/MoO3, synergistically enhanced by hydrogen bonding and π-π interactions between NA’s aromatic rings and GO’s sp2-conjugated domains. At pH > 6.0, NA adopts a fully anionic form (-COO−), while the adsorbent surfaces retain their negative charge55,57. Despite weakened electrostatic forces, residual adsorption persists through Lewis acid-base coordination (Mo6+-COO−) and hydrophobic interactions, underscoring the multifunctional binding mechanisms governing the process.

While maximal removal occurred at pH 5, the marginal efficiency decline (< 5%) at neutral pH (7.2) justified its selection for subsequent experiments. NA transitions to zwitterionic or anionic forms at pH 7.2, while GO (PZC ~ 4.0) and GO/MoO3 (PZC ~ 4.5) are negatively charged, promoting electrostatic and Lewis acid-base interactions with NA’s carboxyl groups54,57. This choice aligns with practical water treatment scenarios, adjusting the pH to acidic conditions is often energetically and economically prohibitive.

Effect of initial NA concentration on adsorption performance

The influence of initial NA concentration (30–80 mg L− 1) on adsorption efficiency was systematically investigated under fixed experimental conditions (Contact time: 24 h, pH 7.2, adsorbent dosage: 0.001 g). Figure 7a illustrates the relationship between initial NA concentration and removal efficiency for GO and GO/MoO3. For both adsorbents, the removal percentage increased from 30 to 40 mg L− 1, reaching maximum values of 87 ± 0.09% (GO) and 91 ± 0.29% (GO/MoO3), respectively. Interestingly, removal efficiency at 30 mg L− 1 initial concentration was slightly lower than at 40 mg L− 1. This trend is attributed to enhanced mass transfer driving forces at moderate concentrations, promoting interactions between NA molecules and available adsorption sites. Also, this may be attributed to higher relative contribution of boundary layer resistance or incomplete site saturation at lower concentrations, leading to marginally reduced percentage removal despite similar absolute uptake. Such minor anomalies are occasionally reported in batch adsorption studies and do not alter the overall favorable trend.

Beyond 40 mg L− 1, removal efficiency declined progressively, likely due to saturation of surface-active sites and limited accessibility to binding moieties at higher NA loadings. The marginal improvement in GO/MoO3 (91 ± 0.29% vs. 87 ± 0.09% for GO) is due to NA’s strong affinity for GO’s functional groups, with MoO3 providing additional binding sites36,39. The superior performance of GO/MoO3 underscores the role of MoO3 incorporation, which introduces additional Lewis acid sites through Mo6+ centers - consistent with: (i) Characteristic Mo6+ IR vibrations (Mo-O-Mo, Mo = O) at 857–991 cm− 1 (ii) Optimal adsorption at pH 5–7 matching Lewis acidity window of Mo6+ (iii) Thermodynamic stability of Mo6+ in aqueous-synthesized α-MoO358, and enhances π-π interactions with NA’s aromatic framework. The RSD values for the removal percentages were below 1% (range: 0.11–0.98%) across all concentrations, indicating high experimental precision.

Effect of adsorbent dosage on NA adsorption

Adsorbent dosage is a critical parameter governing the efficiency of NA removal, as it directly influences the availability of active sites and mass transfer dynamics. To evaluate this effect, experiments were conducted using GO and GO/MoO3 dosages ranging from 0.001 to 0.006 g under experimental conditions (initial NA concentration: 40 mg L− 1, pH 7.2, contact time: 2 h, temperature: 25 °C).

As shown in Fig. 8a, the removal efficiency for GO remained stable (~ 87 ± 0.18%) between 0.001 and 0.002 g, followed by a gradual decline to ~ 86 ± 0.29% at 0.006 g. Similarly, GO/MoO3 exhibited consistent removal (~ 91 ± 0.10%) up to 0.003 g, after which efficiency decreased to ~ 90 ± 0.15% at 0.006 g. Notably, both adsorbents showed decreased performance at higher dosages, despite the increased quantity of material.

This counterintuitive trend is attributed to nanoparticle aggregation at elevated dosages, which diminishes the effective surface area and blocks access to active sites. Agglomeration reduces the number of functional groups (such as -OH, Mo6+) available for interactions with NA molecules, thereby lowering removal efficiency. Consequently, 0.001 g was identified as the optimal dosage for both adsorbents, as it offers the best balance between site accessibility and minimal particle crowding.

Effect of contact time on adsorption kinetics

The influence of contact time on NA adsorption was evaluated under experimental conditions (initial concentration: 40 mg L− 1, pH 7.2, adsorbent dosage: 0.001 g, temperature: 25 °C) over a range of 0.5–5 h (Fig. 8b). Equilibrium was attained at 2 h for both GO and GO/MoO3, beyond which no significant increase in removal efficiency was observed. Rapid adsorption occurred within the first 30 min, with GO/MoO3 achieving 91 ± 0.65% removal and GO reaching 87 ± 0.73% at equilibrium. The initial fast kinetics are attributed to the abundance of unoccupied active sites (such as oxygenated functional groups on GO, Mo6+ Lewis acid sites on MoO3), which facilitate rapid binding of NA molecules via π-π interactions, hydrogen bonding, and electrostatic attraction. As adsorption progressed, the gradual decline in rate reflects the saturation of accessible adsorption sites and increased diffusion resistance for NA molecules to reach internal pores or interlayer spaces. The superior performance of GO/MoO3 underscores the synergistic contribution of MoO3 nanostructures, which enhance surface heterogeneity and provide additional binding sites. However, the improvement remains modest because of NA’s inherent strong affinity for GO’s functional groups39. The optimal contact time of 2 h was therefore selected for subsequent experiments, ensuring equilibrium attainment while minimizing operational duration.

Nonlinear least-squares regression was adopted as the primary fitting method to ensure accurate and unbiased parameter estimation. Proposed adsorption mechanism

The adsorption behavior of NA can be rationalized by considering the heterogeneous nature of the adsorbent surfaces and the coexistence of multiple interaction modes. The Freundlich isotherm indicates a non-uniform distribution of adsorption energies, suggesting that adsorption does not proceed via a single mechanism but through a combination of surface processes.

For pristine GO, the extended π-conjugated domains enable strong π-π interactions with the aromatic quinolone ring of NA, which likely represent the dominant contribution. Simultaneously, oxygen-containing functional groups on GO form hydrogen bonds with the carboxyl and carbonyl functionalities of NA, enhancing adsorption stability. Electrostatic interactions depend on solution pH: at near-neutral conditions, partial deprotonation of NA and the negative surface charge of GO may limit adsorption due to repulsive forces, whereas protonation at lower pH favors attraction. Additional stabilization arises from dispersive and hydrophobic interactions between non-polar regions of NA and graphitic domains.

In GO/MoO3, the introduction of MoO3 nanoparticles substantially increases surface heterogeneity and the density of active sites. Besides retaining the graphene-based π system, MoO3 contributes polar surface groups that promote stronger hydrogen bonding. More importantly, electron-deficient Mo centers can interact with electron-rich functional groups of NA, particularly the carboxylate group, thereby increasing adsorption affinity. The modified surface charge environment further reduces electrostatic barriers under neutral conditions. Consequently, adsorption on GO/MoO3 results from a synergistic combination of aromatic stacking, hydrogen bonding, and surface interactions involving Mo centers, leading to significantly improved adsorption performance compared with bare GO. Desorption and reusability studies

To evaluate the technical and economic feasibility of the adsorption process and the long-term stability of the adsorbents, desorption and reusability experiments were conducted for GO and GO/MoO3 over six consecutive cycles. Desorption of NA was carried out using a mixture of 70% (v/v) ethanol containing 0.5 M HCl for 180 min at 25 °C under magnetic stirring (500 rpm).

The results indicate a gradual decrease in adsorption efficiency with increasing cycle number. As shown in Fig. 17a, for pristine GO, the removal efficiency decreased from 87% in the first cycle to approximately 54% in the sixth cycle. For GO/MoO3, it declined from 91% to about 62% over the same period (Fig. 17b). This progressive reduction is primarily attributed to partial and irreversible occupation of active sites by residual NA molecules, minor agglomeration of MoO3 nanoparticles or GO sheets, and slight changes in surface functional groups during repeated exposure to the acidic-alcoholic desorption medium.

Despite the observed decline, GO/MoO3 retained more than 60% of its initial removal efficiency after six cycles, indicating satisfactory stability and reusability for practical applications. The superior performance of GO/MoO3 compared to GO is attributed to the presence of redox-active and Lewis acid Mo6+ sites, which enhance chemical stability and facilitate regeneration.

This trend is consistent with reports in the literature on carbon-based and GO adsorbents for the removal of quinolone antibiotics, where a 30–40% efficiency loss after 5–7 cycles is commonly reported68,69. The use of an ethanol-acidic mixture in the present study also enabled effective adsorbent regeneration.

Overall, the results demonstrate that GO/MoO3 not only exhibits suitable adsorption capacity but also offers good reusability and economic viability, making it a promising candidate for the treatment of wastewater containing recalcitrant antibiotics. These findings confirm the industrial feasibility and potential for cost reduction in the adsorption process.

Comparative evaluation of GO/MoO3 for NA removal: contextualization with literature

The comparative analysis presented in Table 7 demonstrates that the GO/MoO3 nanocomposite exhibits highly competitive performance for NA removal. With a maximum adsorption capacity of 175.4 mg g− 1 and 92% removal efficiency under neutral pH conditions (pH 7.2), our material outperforms many conventional carbon-based adsorbents, including carbon nanotubes (186 mg g− 1)70, carbon nanofibers (67 mg g− 1)46, and various functionalized MWCNTs (79–111 mg g− 1)37. Although certain specialized nanocomposites such as NiZrAl-LDH-GO-CS show higher adsorption capacity (277.79 mg g− 1)39, they typically require complex synthesis procedures and operate under optimized conditions that may not be practical for real-world applications. The distinct advantage of GO/MoO3 lies in its cost-effective arc-discharge synthesis method, excellent chemical stability across a wide pH range, and maintained high efficiency at environmentally relevant neutral pH - a critical factor for practical water treatment. Furthermore, its adherence to the Freundlich isotherm model and pseudo-second-order kinetics is consistent with a heterogeneous, chemisorption-driven binding mechanism, in line with the synergistic interface between MoO3 nanoparticles and GO sheets.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates the superior performance of GO/MoO3 nanocomposites compared to GO alone for removing NA from aqueous solutions. Under optimized conditions (pH 7.2, 40 mg L− 1 initial concentration, 1 mg adsorbent dosage, 2 h contact time), GO/MoO3 achieved a removal efficiency of 91% and an adsorption capacity of 135 mg g− 1, compared to 87% and 130 mg g− 1 for GO at an initial concentration of 80 mg L− 1. The adsorption process followed pseudo-second-order kinetics and the Freundlich isotherm, indicating multilayer chemisorption. The adsorption mechanism involves π-π stacking between NA’s aromatic rings and GO’s sp2 domains, hydrogen bonding with GO’s oxygenated groups, and Lewis acid-base coordination between MoO3’s redox-active sites and NA’s carboxyl groups, as evidenced by FTIR and kinetic data. However, the modest increase in removal efficiency for GO/MoO3 (91% vs. 87% for GO) primarily results from NA’s inherent strong affinity for GO’s functional groups. Comprehensive characterization using FTIR, XRD, BET, SEM, and EDX confirmed the successful integration of MoO3 nanoparticles, enhancing surface reactivity. These findings highlight GO/MoO3 as a cost-effective and scalable adsorbent for mitigating antibiotic pollution in water treatment systems, with potential applications in industrial wastewater management and the removal of other emerging contaminants.
