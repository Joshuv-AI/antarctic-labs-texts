Toward an “accordion intensive care unit” model: A flexible, patient-centered approach to critical-care capacity
Authors: Kais Regaieg, Sabrina Chaouch, Ilef Alila, Toboure Zanze
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Intensive care units (ICUs) are designed to provide continuous monitoring and advanced support for critically ill patients. Yet patient flow is highly variable, driven by infectious outbreaks, seasonal variations, mass gatherings, or extraordinary events such as natural disasters or terrorist attacks. Traditional fixed-bed ICU models often fail to respond effectively to these fluctuations, resulting in either under-utilization during quiet periods or saturation during peaks. Several authors, including Halpern, have highlighted the structural limits of rigid ICU organizations and the need for adaptable models in modern critical care.[1] In this context, We propose the concept of an “accordion ICU,” a flexible structure in which ICU capacity–beds, staff, and equipment–can dynamically expand or contract according to real-time patient needs.

The accordion model is founded on the principle of dynamic adaptability. During high-demand periods such as epidemics, winter surges, or increased tourist activity, ICU capacity can be rapidly increased. Conversely, during low-activity phases–summer, holidays, or epidemiological plateaus–resources can be redistributed to other hospital services. This approach aligns with observations made by Vincent and Creteur, who emphasize that the hospital of the future must be both resilient and flexible to optimize care and resource utilization.[2] Maintaining unused ICU beds is costly from a workforce, material, and infrastructural standpoint; activation of beds only when necessary improves efficiency while ensuring readiness.

A central component of the accordion ICU is the integration of intermediate-care units. Even when not required for full-scale critical care, these units should be equipped and staffed to function as ICU beds when needed–echoing the international analyses by Wunsch, who noted that countries with adaptable intermediate-care structures respond more effectively to surges in critical-care demand.[3] Medical and nursing teams must therefore possess polyvalent competencies and critical-care training, enabling rapid deployment. During calm periods, these teams can reinforce emergency rooms, high-dependency units, or specialty wards while maintaining their skill level. Equipment, including ventilators, advanced monitors, and essential consumables, must be stored safely and remain ready for immediate activation with rigorous maintenance schedules.

The accordion ICU model offers several clinical and organizational benefits.

First, it promotes patient-centered care: patients can remain in the same bed as their severity fluctuates, reducing intrahospital transfers and improving continuity of care.

Second, it enhances clinical team engagement, reducing fragmentation of care and improving job satisfaction, as clinicians can accompany patients throughout their trajectory.

Third, it strengthens system resilience, a notion emphasized by Aziz et al., who demonstrated that ICUs with integrated surge-capacity protocols performed better during the COVID-19 pandemic.[4] Fourth, it optimizes economic and environmental efficiency, reducing the cost and carbon footprint associated with unused high-tech beds.[5]

Implementing an accordion ICU requires thoughtful planning regarding staffing models, architectural adaptability, supply-chain organization, and the development of a culture of flexibility within healthcare teams. Challenges include appropriate workforce management during peaks, maintaining uniform quality of care across multiple care sectors, and sustaining preparedness without overburdening personnel. Aziz et al. have recently argued that resilient ICUs must integrate such flexible surge-capacity strategies into routine planning–not only during pandemics but also for seasonal and intermittent activity peaks.[4]

This concept was implicitly applied during the COVID-19 pandemic, when hospitals expanded ICU capacity by repurposing intermediate-care units, postanesthesia recovery areas, and other clinical spaces. However, these adaptations were frequently reactive and unstructured. Formalizing this approach as a proactive, anticipatory model offers the potential for predictable, safe, and scalable responses in both crisis scenarios and routine fluctuations.

In conclusion, the accordion ICU represents a flexible, patient-centered, and economically efficient framework for the organization of modern critical care. By modulating beds, staff, and equipment according to actual needs, hospitals can ensure continuity and safety of care while maintaining readiness for sudden surges in patient numbers. Integrating this model into hospital planning reflects a broader evolution toward dynamic, adaptable, and resilient critical-care systems suited to the challenges of 21st-century medicine.
