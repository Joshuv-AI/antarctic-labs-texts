Cytogenomics and optical genome mapping approaches characterize a derivative interstitial monosomy 18p due to a maternal complex intrachromosomal rearrangement
Authors: Ludovico Graziani, Silvia Genovese, Maria Luce Genovesi, Cristiana Di Rosa, Rosalba Di Noi, Sara Loddo, Mario Bengala, Vito Luigi Colona, Antonio Novelli, Giuseppe Novelli, Viola Alesi, Anna Maria Nardone

AbstractBackgroundMonosomy 18p (MIM: 146390) is a well-known chromosomal disorder associated with intellectual disability, short stature, and non-specific craniofacial features resulting from partial or total deletion of the short arm of chromosome 18. Background

Monosomy 18p (MIM: 146390), also referred to as 18p deletion (18p-), is a chromosomal disorder caused by the deletion of all or part of the short arm of chromosome 18. The estimated incidence is approximately 1 in 50,000 live births, with most cases arising from de novo chromosomal rearrangements [1, 2]. To date, over 300 cases have been reported worldwide, the vast majority of which were diagnosed postnatally, while prenatal reports remain particularly rare [3]. The main clinical features in monosomy 18p are mild to moderate intellectual disability (ID), short stature, and non-specific craniofacial features, including a round face, ptosis, dysplastic ears, and short philtrum [1, 4]. Some patients may also present with holoprosencephaly or its milder forms [5]. Differential diagnosis for monosomy 18p includes several syndromes presenting with ID and short stature [6]. Given the overlap in phenotypic presentations, cytogenetic analysis is needed for a definitive diagnosis [1]. Recent advancements in Chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA) technology have enabled the precise identification of the deletion breakpoints and the genes implicated in the 18p- [7]. Nevertheless, a significant limitation of CMA is its inability to detect balanced structural variants (SVs), such as balanced chromosomal translocations, insertions, or inversions [8]. In contrast, Optical Genome Mapping (OGM) has recently emerged as a powerful tool for comprehensive analysis of large-scale structural genomic variants, offering a resolution that surpasses conventional cytogenetic techniques and enabling the characterization of chromosomal rearrangements [9].

Herein, the first reported case of an interstitial 18p deletion caused by a maternal complex intra-chromosome rearrangement. This diagnosis was achieved through an integrated approach combining standard cytogenetics, CMA, and OGM technologies, highlighting the diagnostic potential of these methods in uncovering cryptic chromosomal rearrangements.

Case presentation

Clinical description

The patient, an 8-year-old girl, is the first child of healthy, non-consanguineous parents with an unremarkable family history. She was born at 39 weeks of gestational age by cesarean section due to a breech presentation. The pregnancy had progressed normally until the seventh month of gestation, when intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) was observed.

Her birth weight, length, and occipitofrontal circumference (OFC) were 1980 g (− 3.8 SDS), 46 cm (− 2.2 SDS), and 30.5 cm (−3.5 SDS), respectively. Newborn metabolic screening and otoacoustic emissions tests at birth were normal, although hypotonia was noted. The neonatal echocardiogram demonstrated a patent foramen ovale (PFO) with left-to-right atrial shunt. A mild motor development delay was reported, as she was able to sit with no support at 6 months, and she could walk independently at 18 months. She also manifested a language delay: she could pronounce her first words at about 12 months, and she was able to complete simple sentences at about 4 years old after logopedic therapy. The audiometric evaluation was regular.

At a physical examination at 5 years and 4 months old, the patient’s weight, height, and OFC were 13.1 kg (−3.1 SDS), 100.5 cm (−2.3 SDS), and 46 cm (−4.9 SDS), respectively. Facial features included a round face, bilateral ptosis, mild hypertelorism, epicanthal folds, large protruding ears, flat and broad nasal bridge, bulbous nasal tip, short nasal philtrum, wide mouth with down-slanted corners, and thin upper lip (Fig. 1a, b). She presented with small hands and feet, and bilateral clinodactyly of the fifth finger (Fig. 1c, d). The neurological assessment at 6 years old demonstrated general clumsiness, generalized muscular hypotonia, and mild ID. At the last evaluation at 7 years, due to persistent growth delay, hormonal testing was conducted and showed normal values. Bone age was also delayed, corresponding to 5 years and 4 months old, according to Greulich and Pyle standards. Brain Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), abdominal ultrasound, and echocardiogram were unremarkable.

Cytogenetics

Informed consent was obtained from the parents of the proposita.

GTG-banding karyotype analysis was performed according to standard procedures for metaphases obtained from phytohemagglutinin (PHA) -stimulated lymphocytes. Chromosome analysis of the patient and her parents was carried out at the 550-band level. The karyotype description followed the recommendations of the International System for Human Cytogenomic Nomenclature (ISCN 2024) guidelines.

Chromosomal microarray analysis

CMA was performed on the patient’s DNA using Infinium CytoSNP-850 K BeadChip (Illumina, San Diego, CA), in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Array scanning data were generated on the Illumina NextSeq 550 system, and the quantitative results were produced by BluefuseMulti 4.4 software.

Maternal DNA was processed by the 180 K CGH-array platform (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Images, generated by the Agilent microarray Scanner, were processed by Cytogenomics 5.3 software for data production and copy number variant (CNV) calling.

Optical genome mapping

OGM analysis was performed on a peripheral blood sample collected from the patient’s mother in EDTA and stored at − 80 °C after sampling. Ultra-high molecular weight (UHMW) DNA was extracted according to the manufacturer’s instructions (SP Frozen Human Blood DNA Isolation Protocol, Bionano Genomics, San Diego, CA, USA), and enzymatically labeled by the DLE-1 Enzyme (Bionano Prep Direct Label and Stain Protocol). Labeled DNA was loaded on a Saphyr chip and scanned on the Saphyr instrument (Bionano Genomics, San Diego, CA, USA) with a minimum of 100X effective coverage. The de novo assembly and variant annotation pipeline was executed on Bionano Solve software V3.6 using the Human Genome Reference Consortium GRCh38 assembly as a reference for structural variant detection. Reporting and direct visualization of the SVs were performed on Bionano Access V1.6.

Results

Cytogenetic analysis demonstrated an interstitial deletion at the 18p11.32p11.21 chromosomal region in the proband, and an apparently balanced aberrant morphology of chromosome 18 in her mother, consisting in the insertion of 18p material within the long arm of the same chromosome.

CMA characterized the proband’s deletion, which was a 10.8 Mb in size, spanning from nucleotides 2,851,821 to 13,666,769 (GRCh38) and partially encompassing the critical region associated with 18p deletion [1]. CMA performed on maternal DNA detected 4 microduplications on chromosome 18, at 18p11.23 (134.1 kb), 18p11.22p11.21 (698 kb), 18p11.21 (912.5 kb), and 18q21.1 (558.3 kb), respectively.

The structure and morphology of the maternal chromosome 18, following the deletion identified in the proband, excluded the presence of a pericentric inversion. Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) analysis in the proband targeting the 18pter and 18qter regions confirmed their normal positioning, effectively ruling out a recombinant chromosome 18. OGM analysis of the mother’s genomic DNA revealed a complex intrachromosomal rearrangement on chromosome 18, which accounted for the observed CNVs and involved multiple breakpoints (Fig. 2).

Discussion and conclusions

Monosomy 18p is a well-characterized chromosomal disorder. However, establishing accurate genotype-phenotype correlations remains challenging, yet it plays a crucial role in prognostic evaluation. Phenotypic severity can vary significantly and is often correlated with the size of the deletion and the haploinsufficiency of specific genes involved within the 18p region [4, 10, 11]. Most deletions involve the pericentromeric region, with a potential breakpoint cluster at 18p11.1; other breakpoints are scattered along the short arm [5, 12]. In the present case, the patient carried an interstitial deletion spanning 18p11.32-p11.21. To the best of our knowledge, only one other case involving a large interstitial deletion of the short arm of chromosome 18 has been reported [13]. Our patient exhibited several characteristic features of monosomy 18p, including neurodevelopmental delay, short stature, hypotonia, round face, ptosis, and large, protruding ears. CMA revealed partial involvement of the 18p critical region, sparing the 18pter [1]. Nevertheless, the relatively mild ID and the Less pronounced facial features observed in this patient support the hypothesis that the phenotypic severity may be linked to the size and location of the breakpoints within the 18p region [14]. More than 75% of 18p deletions arise de novo, while the remainder are due to unbalanced translocations, malsegregation of a balanced parental translocation or inversion, ring chromosome 18, isochromosome 18q, or, more rarely, familial transmission [1, 2, 11, 15]. Identifying the underlying chromosomal mechanism is essential for assessing recurrence risk. CMA remains the first-tier diagnostic approach for detecting CNVs across the genome, yet characterization of complex or balanced SVs is limited [8]. OGM is emerging as a powerful complementary technique. OGM can reliably detect SVs that are beyond the standard karyotype resolution and those that can be difficult to identify with long-read sequencing (LRS), as it works directly with native DNA [16]. Although some long-read sequencing platforms may also use native DNA, the higher effective molecule length, absence of sequencing noise, and genome-wide uniformity provided by OGM allow for the detection of complex and large-scale structural variants that may remain unresolved by current LRS technologies. Furthermore, OGM often matches CMA in detecting aneuploidies and CNVs [17]. Compared to traditional techniques, such as FISH, OGM offers improved resolution for breakpoint detection, greater efficiency in terms of time and resources, and a genome-wide, unbiased approach to identifying complex chromosomal anomalies [18]. However, some region features can affect the sensitivity of the platform in detecting structural variants, leading to false negative results. In fact, when the rearrangement breakpoints fall within heterochromatin or large segmental duplication regions, the system may not be able to characterize them. These genomic regions are usually masked in order to avoid artefact calls, thus overlooking some structural events, especially when involving centromeres or highly repeated regions. Moreover, for complex chromosomal rearrangements as the one detected in our patient’s mother, the fine detection may not be sufficient to solve the chromosomal structure, and integration between OGM and cytogenetics data is often needed.

In the presented case, OGM enabled the full characterization of a complex maternal intrachromosomal rearrangement involving chromosome 18. While conventional chromosome analysis suggested a direct insertion of 18p11.32-p11.31 material within the long arm of the same chromosome 18, OGM revealed a multiple-breakage complex rearrangement, including insertions, duplications, and inversions that lead to a significant reshuffling of 18p sequences (i.e., chromothripsis). Similar examples have been described in the literature, where rearrangements initially detected by CMA were later clarified by OGM as complex structural variants such as insertions or inversions, thus explaining unexpected phenotypes [19–23].

Although carriers of balanced or nearly balanced rearrangements are often phenotypically normal, they remain at risk of generating unbalanced gametes due to meiotic recombination. Depending on the segregation pattern during meiosis, this can result in offspring with deletions or duplications. Reported recurrence risks for such rearrangements can range from 15% to 50% [24]. However, when the structural rearrangement is highly complex, as in this case, predicting meiotic outcomes and reproductive risk becomes more difficult. Microstructural variations, such as inverted duplications and ectopic insertions, may further promote non-allelic homologous recombination, increasing the unpredictability of meiotic segregation [25].

In conclusion, traditional cytogenetic methods, supported by standard techniques such as CMA and FISH analysis, remain essential in the diagnostic workflow for rare genetic syndromes, especially those with highly heterogeneous phenotypes and variable chromosomal aberrations, such as monosomy 18p. However, OGM has proven to be a valuable addition to the current diagnostic toolkit, offering deeper insight into complex structural rearrangements and expanding our understanding of the underlying mechanisms driving genomic disorders.
