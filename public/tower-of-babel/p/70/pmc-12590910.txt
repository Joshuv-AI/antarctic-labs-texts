Opportunistic AI-derived adiposity measures from coronary artery calcium scans predict new-onset type 2 diabetes in adults without obesity or hyperglycemia: insights from the AI-CVD study within MESA
Authors: Morteza Naghavi, Amir Azimi, Kyle Atlas, Anthony P Reeves, Chenyu Zhang, Jakob Wasserthal, Seyed Reza Mirjalili, Mohammadhossein Mozafarybazargany, Ali Hashemi, Thomas Atlas, Claudia I Henschke, David F Yankelevitz, Jeffrey I Mechanick, Andrea D Branch, Susan K Fried, Khurram Nasir, Zahi A Fayad, Michael V McConnell, Rozemarijn Vliegenthart, David J Maron, Jagat Narula, Kim A Williams, Prediman K Shah, Matthew J Budoff, Daniel Levy, Emelia J Benjamin, Robert A Kloner, Nathan D Wong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe AI-CVD initiative aims to maximize the value of coronary artery calcium (CAC) scans for cardiometabolic risk prediction by extracting opportunistic screening information. We investigated whether artificial intelligence (AI)-derived measures from CAC scans are associated with new-onset Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) in adults without obesity or hyperglycemia.MethodsBaseline CAC scans and up to 23 years of follow-up data were analyzed for participants without obesity (body mass index < 30 kg/m²) and hyperglycemia (fasting plasma glucose < 100 mg/dL) from the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA). AI-derived measures included liver attenuation index (LAI), subcutaneous fat index (SFI), total visceral fat index (TVFI), epicardial fat index (EFI), skeletal muscle index, and skeletal muscle mean density. Cox regression models compared highest vs. lowest quartiles of each AI-derived metric for T2DM risk. Multivariable models assessed adjusted predictive value using Wald chi-squared statistics. Subgroup analyses stratified participants by demographic and clinical factors.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 19.7 years among 2,993 participants (baseline mean age 61.9 ± 10.5 years, 53% women), 257 participants (8.6%) developed T2DM. Key predictors included LAI (HR: 3.13, 95% CI: 2.15–4.55), SFI (HR: 2.85, 95% CI: 1.93–4.21), TVFI (HR: 2.49, 95% CI: 1.72–3.60), and EFI (HR: 1.59, 95% CI: 1.09–2.32). LAI remained the most robust predictor after adjusting for all metrics (Wald χ² = 38.24). Subgroup analyses confirmed LAI’s consistent predictive performance.ConclusionAI-derived adiposity measures from CAC scans—especially liver fat—can identify adults without obesity or hyperglycemia at elevated risk for developing T2DM. These findings underscore the potential of AI-enabled opportunistic screening during CAC imaging to support early T2DM risk stratification in individuals not captured by current clinical guidelines.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-025-01970-8.Keywords: AI-CVD, Artificial intelligence, Coronary artery calcium (CAC) scans, Opportunistic screening, Type 2 diabetes mellitus

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a major global health burden, affecting over 400 million people worldwide and constituting one of the leading causes of morbidity [1]. Despite its prevalence, approximately 45% of adult T2DM cases remain undiagnosed globally [2, 3]. While obesity is a recognized risk factor, epidemiological studies reveal that a substantial portion of newly detected T2DM cases occur in people who are not obese, challenging traditional risk assessment paradigms [4, 5]. Significant racial and ethnic disparities exist in this phenomenon: in Asian and African countries, 24–66% of T2DM cases occur in normal-weight individuals, considerably higher than the 10% observed in the U.S [6]. Even within the U.S., disparities are evident: T2DM prevalence among normal-weight individuals is 13.5% in Black individuals, 13.0% in Hispanic individuals, and 18.0% in Hawaiian/Pacific Islander individuals—substantially higher than the 5.0% in non-Hispanic White individuals [7]. Since patients with T2DM have a shortened healthspan due to vascular complications, including diabetic retinopathy, nephropathy, and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), early identification of high-risk individuals for timely intervention through lifestyle modifications and pharmacotherapy is crucial, particularly for adults without obesity or hyperglycemia marginalized from guideline-directed screening or case finding protocols [8].

An emerging promising approach for early chronic disease detection and risk stratification is opportunistic screening, which is analyzing the robust data embedded within imaging studies performed for unrelated clinical indications [9]. The incidental imaging information within thoracic and abdominal computed tomography (CT) scans can provide valuable insights regarding body composition measures such as visceral/ectopic adiposity, subcutaneous/ectopic adiposity, and muscle mass, which have previously been shown to be associated with cardiometabolic-based chronic disease [10, 11]. The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) has revolutionized body composition analysis from these images, making it less labor-intensive and more systematic.

The AI-CVD initiative was established to maximize the value of routine CT scans, particularly coronary artery calcium (CAC) scans, by extracting opportunistic information to enhance cardiometabolic disease prediction. Our group has published several studies validating various AI-CVD components in the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA), including automated measurements of bone mineral density, cardiac chamber volumes and left ventricular mass, coronary artery calcification, and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) [12–17]. In our studies, AI-measured liver adiposity reported as liver attenuation index (LAI) by CAC scans predicted cardiovascular disease and mortality in adjusted analyses. Additionally, we demonstrated that individuals who fall in the top quartile of both LAI and CAC score had higher incidence of coronary heart disease than those who fell in either quartile alone [18]. These studies consistently showed that AI-enabled opportunistic screening can provide valuable prognostic information beyond the primary imaging indication.

While prior studies have largely focused on associations between abdominal CT-derived body composition measures and T2DM risk [2, 5, 19, 20], the specific utility of CAC scans for this purpose remains poorly understood. Although CAC imaging is increasingly used to assess ASCVD risk, its potential to concurrently stratify T2DM risk, particularly in normoglycemic, non-obese individuals considered low risk by current standards, has not been explored. This study seeks to fill that gap by evaluating whether AI-derived adiposity metrics from CAC scans can predict new-onset T2DM in those without obesity or hyperglycemia.

Methods

Study design and population

We analyzed MESA data for this analysis. MESA, initiated in July 2000, is a prospective cohort study investigating subclinical CVD progression in participants without baseline CVD. The study enrolled 6,814 participants aged 45–84 years from six U.S. communities: Baltimore (Maryland), Chicago (Illinois), Forsyth County (North Carolina), Los Angeles County (California), northern Manhattan (New York), and St. Paul (Minnesota). A comprehensive description of the MESA study design has been published previously [21].

Participant selection

From the initial MESA cohort, participants were systematically excluded based on several criteria. These exclusions included 771 participants who did not consent to commercial data use, 223 with incomplete CT scan data (missing slices), 244 lacking baseline T2DM data, 720 with baseline T2DM (treated or untreated) according to American Diabetes Association 2003 guideline [22], 144 with missing outcome, baseline body mass index (BMI), or fasting glucose data, and 1,719 with impaired fasting glucose (IFG) (fasting plasma glucose ≥ 100) or obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²)(Fig. 1).

Definition of incident DM

Incident T2DM was defined by any of the following criteria: fasting glucose levels ≥ 126 mg/dL, self-reported diagnosis of T2DM, or self-reported use of insulin or oral hypoglycemic medications. Incident T2DM was identified as any new case of DM (treated or untreated) documented during follow-up visits through examination seven. Time-to-event was calculated as the interval between examination one and the examination where T2DM was first detected. Participants who did not develop T2DM during the study period were censored at either their last attended examination or at examination seven, whichever came first.

CT scan protocol

Baseline cardiac CT scanning employed two technologies: Three centers (New York, Illinois, and California) utilized electron-beam CT (EBCT) with image acquisition gated at 50% of the R-R interval, while the remaining sites (Maryland, Minnesota, and North Carolina) used multidetector CT (MDCT) with gating at 80% of the R-R interval. Each scan was performed from the carina to below the apex of the heart, including images of the liver and spleen. The scans yielded an average of 44 images per participant. To ensure consistency across sites, image reconstruction parameters were standardized: 2.5 mm slice thickness, 512 × 512 matrix, 350 mm field of view (encompassing the entire chest width), and 0.68 mm in-plane pixel spacing. The complete scanning protocol, including technical parameters, quality control measures, and image acquisition methods, has been comprehensively documented in a previous publication [23].

The AI software for automated measurements

The AI-CVD™ software (HeartLung.AI, Houston, TX) was employed for extracting imaging biomarkers opportunistically from CAC scans. AI-CVD™ employs the TotalSegmentator anatomical model as an input framework for segmenting fat and muscle within the thoracic cavity, a validated approach developed independently from our research group [17, 24] (Supplementary Material 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1).

An independent 3D U-Net convolutional neural network, distinct from the base input model, was trained using the Medical Open Network for AI (MONAI) framework to segment liver, skeletal muscle, subcutaneous fat, and visceral fat from CAC scans [25]. The TotalSegmentator model was trained and validated on 1139 whole-body CT examination cases with 447 cases of CCTA randomly sampled from the University Hospital Basel picture archiving and communication system. The ground truth consisted of segmentation masks for the sternum, subcutaneous fat, skeletal muscle, and visceral torso fat. Image standardization included intensity scaling, foreground cropping, anatomical reorientation (left, posterior, superior), isotropic resampling, and spatial padding. Training data underwent randomized label cropping augmentation. The network architecture comprised a 5-level 3D U-Net trained using a Dice and Cross-Entropy composite loss function with the AdamW optimizer [26]. The Dice score measures overlap as twice the intersection divided by the sum of both sets, while the composite Cross-Entropy loss and the AdamW optimizer are used to update model weights during training. The optimal model demonstrated robust performance with mean Dice scores of 0.918 (validation) and 0.926 (test set).

For quality control, post-processing algorithms detected poor-quality segmentations affected by artifacts or missing regions, supplemented by connected component analysis to eliminate ectopic segmentation islands. Volumetric measurements for each region of interest were calculated by summing cross-sectional areas across all available slices in the CAC scans, multiplied by slice thickness. Skeletal muscle volume encompassed all thoracic cavity muscles, including pectoralis, paraspinal, and intercostal muscles. Myosteatosis was operationally defined as measurements in the lowest quartile of thoracic skeletal muscle mean density (SMD) in Hounsfield Units (HU). LAI, a surrogate for hepatic steatosis, was determined as the proportion of liver voxels below 40 HU within the range of −30 to 70 HU, minimizing artifact influence (Fig. 2) [18]. Multiple studies confirm that LAI on noncontrast CT accurately reflects hepatic fat content, and a liver attenuation threshold below 40 HU has demonstrated high accuracy for detecting moderate-to-severe steatosis [27–30].

Epicardial fat volumes were calculated using the fat HU range (−190 to −30 HU) within segmented heart regions, excluding cardiac chambers, myocardium, ascending aorta, and blood pools. Heart region segmentation utilized a separate 3D U-Net model trained on 197 manually annotated CT volumes from the University of Basel, divided into training (n = 178) and validation sets (n = 19). Images underwent similar preprocessing (intensity scaling, foreground cropping, isotropic resampling, and left-posterior-superior reorientation), with the network utilizing voxel patches centered around the heart. This heart segmentation model achieved a Dice score of 0.956 (Fig. 3).

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using R Studio and Python 3.10 software. All tests were two-tailed with statistical significance set at α = 0.05 and power at β = 0.20. Descriptive statistics were presented according to data distribution: normally distributed continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation, non-normally distributed continuous variables as median (25th–75th percentile), and categorical variables as frequencies (percentages). Between-group comparisons were performed using appropriate statistical tests: independent t-tests (two groups) or one-way ANOVA (multiple groups) for normally distributed variables; Wilcoxon rank-sum test (two groups) or Kruskal-Wallis test (multiple groups) for non-normally distributed variables; and chi-square tests for categorical variables. AI-derived CT measurements were normalized to body surface area (BSA) to account for differences in body size. All AI-derived CT volumetric measurements, including subcutaneous fat, visceral fat, epicardial fat, and skeletal muscle volumes, were normalized to body surface area (BSA) using the DuBois formula [BSA = 0.007184 × weight0.425 × height0.725]. These normalized values were reported as indices: subcutaneous fat index (SFI), total visceral fat index (TVFI), epicardial fat index (EFI), and skeletal muscle index (SMI).

The correlations between AI-derived CT measurements and baseline clinical parameters (age, BMI, waist-to-hip ratio, waist-to- height, triglycerides, Systolic blood pressure (SBP), Diastolic blood pressure (DBP), HDL cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, fasting plasma glucose, HbA1c, and homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR, calculated as fasting plasma glucose × insulin/405)) were analyzed using Pearson correlation analysis and illustrated using a symmetric correlation matrix.

The associations between AI-derived CT measurements and incident T2DM were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression models. We used penalized Cox regression with a weak L2 penalty to improve model stability in analyses with low sample size relative to the number of covariates, and to handle moderate-to-high collinearity among variables. AI-derived measurements were categorized into sex-specific quartiles, with hazard ratios calculated comparing the highest versus lowest quartiles. Additionally, hazard ratios per 1-SD increment were computed to assess the continuous relationship between these metrics and T2DM risk. Model assumptions were rigorously assessed using Schoenfeld residuals for the proportional hazard assumption and martingale residuals for linearity. A multivariable Cox proportional hazards model was constructed to estimate the association of each measurement, adjusted for all other metrics. We used the Wald chi‑square statistic (z²) to compare the strength of statistical evidence provided by each metric. For the primary multivariable Cox proportional hazards model, we simultaneously entered all six AI‑derived adiposity indices—liver attenuation index (LAI), subcutaneous fat index (SFI), total visceral fat index (TVFI), epicardial fat index (EFI), skeletal muscle index (SMI), and skeletal muscle mean density (SMD)—as predictors.

Subgroup analyses were conducted by stratifying participants according to age (≤ 60 years, > 60 years), sex (male, female), body mass index (< 25 kg/m², 25–30 kg/m²), race and ethnicity, hemoglobin A1C (HbA1c) (< 5.7%, ≥ 5.7%), waist-hip ratio (< median, ≥median), and waist- height ratio categories (< median, ≥median) and scanner type (MDCT, EBCT). It is important to note that baseline HbA1c data were not available in the initial MESA examination; therefore, HbA1c measurements from MESA examination 2, conducted approximately two years after baseline, were utilized.

We conducted a sensitivity analysis using an obesity threshold of BMI ≥ 27.5 kg/m², as recommended by previous literature specific to the Asian population [31].

We also conducted an analysis assessing the incremental improvement in discrimination when adding LAI and other adiposity measures to the established American Diabetes Association (ADA) diabetes risk score [32]. Also, the Category-free net reclassification index (NRI) was calculated as the sum of differences between upward and downward reclassifications for events and non-events.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Among the 2,993 participants included, the mean age was 61.9 ± 10.5 years, with males representing 47.0% of the sample. The cohort was ethnically and racially diverse, comprising White, (45.5%), Hispanic (20.7%), Chinese American (18.8%), and Black/African-American (14.9%) individuals. The mean BMI was 25.2 ± 2.9 kg/m². During the follow-up period of 23 years (median 19.7 [12.5–21.3] years), 257 participants (8.6%) developed T2DM, corresponding to an incidence rate of 0.5 per 100 person-years. Compared with those who remained T2DM-free, participants who developed T2DM were significantly younger and had higher BMI, fasting plasma glucose, HOMA-IR (a measure of insulin resistance), diastolic blood pressure, triglycerides, and waist-to-hip ratio, as well as lower HDL-cholesterol (all p < 0.001). Baseline clinical characteristics stratified by incident T2DM status are summarized in Table 1. Analysis of AI-derived CT measurements revealed significant differences between individuals who developed T2DM and those who did not. Participants with incident T2DM had higher LAI, SFI, EFI, TVFI, and SMD. Although SMI was also higher in the T2DM group, this difference was not statistically significant. The correlation between baseline clinical parameters and AI-derived CT measurements is illustrated in Fig. 4.

In Cox proportional hazards models comparing the highest to the lowest quartile of each AI-derived index, LAI had the strongest association with incident T2DM (HR: 3.13, 95% CI: 2.15–4.55, p < 0.001), followed by SFI (HR: 2.85, 95% CI: 1.93–4.21, p < 0.001), TVFI (HR: 2.49, 95% CI: 1.72–3.60, p < 0.001), and EFI (HR: 1.59, 95% CI: 1.09–2.32, p = 0.016) (Table 2). SMI (HR: 1.07, 95% CI: 0.75–1.53, p = 0.706), Agatston CAC score (HR: 0.96, 95% CI: 0.67–1.38, p = 829), and SMD (HR: 1.06, 95% CI: 0.74–1.50, p = 0.349) were not significantly associated with incident T2DM.

The per SD change analysis further supported these findings. LAI again showed the strongest association (HR: 1.62, 95% CI: 1.44–1.82, p < 0.001), followed by SFI (HR: 1.25, 95% CI: 1.11–1.40, p < 0.001), TVFI (HR: 1.27, 95% CI: 1.15–1.41, p < 0.001), and EFI (HR: 1.25, 95% CI: 1.10–1.41, p < 0.001). In multivariable analyses simultaneously incorporating all AI-derived CT measurements, LAI retained the highest predictive significance (Wald χ² = 38.27), followed by SFI (Wald χ² = 16.90) and SMD (Wald χ² = 3.43). EFI, SMI, and TVFI showed minimal predictive importance (Wald χ² = 0.75 and 0.02, respectively; Fig. 5).

Subgroup analyses revealed consistent associations of LAI with incident T2DM across all evaluated strata, including age groups (≤ 60 and > 60 years), sex, BMI categories (≤ 25 and 25–30 kg/m²), race and ethnicity (White, Hispanic, Chinese American, Black/African-American,), HbA1c levels (< 5.7%, ≥ 5.7%), waist-to-hip ratio, waist-to- height ratio, and scanner type categories. SFI demonstrated significant predictive associations in all subgroups except Hispanic and higher waist-to-height ratio, whereas TVFI showed significance in most subgroups except those aged > 60 years and Hispanic individuals. EFI, SMI, and SMD showed weaker and mostly non-significant predictive associations across subgroups (Table 3).

When redefining obesity as BMI ≥ 27.5 in Chinese American individuals, the results showed no significant differences. LAI and SFI remained the most predictive indices, confirming the robustness of our conclusions (Supplementary Tables 1, and Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10).

We evaluated whether incorporating AI-measured adiposity metrics enhanced discrimination for incident T2DM beyond the ADA risk score alone. The ADA score demonstrated modest discrimination for incident diabetes (AUC = 0.560). Incorporating LAI significantly improved performance (AUC = 0.632), while the addition of other adiposity measures provided only a marginal further improvement (AUC = 0.637) (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Adding LAI to the ADA score significantly improved net reclassification for incident T2DM (category-free NRI = 0.27; 95% CI, 0.12–0.42; p < 0.001). Adding the full adiposity panel to the ADA score also significantly improved reclassification (NRI = 0.31; 95% CI, 0.20–0.49; p < 0.001). However, adding other adiposity components beyond LAI did not provide additional improvement over LAI + ADA (NRI = 0.17; 95% CI, − 0.08–0.39; p = 0.166) (Supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the association between AI-quantified adiposity measures from CAC scans and incident T2DM in people without obesity or hyperglycemia. In this prospective analysis of 2,993 MESA participants followed for a median of 19.7 years, LAI emerged as the strongest predictor of T2DM, followed by SFI, TVFI, and EFI. In multivariable models, LAI retained the highest predictive value, with SFI contributing modestly, while other indices showed minimal independent associations. The predictive strength of LAI was consistent across demographic and clinical subgroups. These findings underscore the potential of opportunistic CAC scan-derived metrics—particularly LAI—for early T2DM risk stratification in populations not typically flagged by current screening guidelines.

The strong association between LAI and incident T2DM observed in our study aligns with growing evidence that MASLD is associated with increased risk of incident T2DM [33]. While the prior MESA study measured liver attenuation by averaging two 1 cm² regions in the right hepatic lobe on CAC scans, finding that the lowest quartile of hepatic attenuation (≤ 57 HU) indicated fatty liver and predicted significantly higher T2DM risk [34], our study employed a more comprehensive approach by assessing the entire visible liver area and evaluating all accessible adiposity measures in the CAC scan view. Supporting our findings, a retrospective longitudinal study with 1,798 participants assessing hepatic steatosis as liver attenuation value on non-contrast abdominal CT scans reported that moderate to severe hepatic steatosis was associated with a hazard ratio of 3.24 for T2DM development compared to individuals without steatosis [35], remarkably similar to our observed HR of 3.13 for LAI. Our results extend previous findings by demonstrating that even in the absence of obesity or IFG, excessive hepatic fat infiltration is significantly associated with T2DM risk or is associated with other factors that lead to incident T2DM. The pathophysiological mechanisms likely involve hepatic insulin resistance, altered glucose metabolism, and increased inflammatory cytokine production [35]. Furthermore, our previous study has shown that LAI derived from CAC scans can also predict cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality [18]. This suggests that hepatic fat accumulation represents a systemic metabolic disturbance with broad health implications beyond T2DM alone. LAI’s persistence as the strongest predictor in our multivariable model underscores its central role in metabolic health.

While visceral adiposity has traditionally been considered more metabolically harmful, our results suggest that subcutaneous fat accumulation may also be important in predicting T2DM, particularly in those without obesity. This finding aligns with the Framingham Heart Study’s analysis of 2,306 participants, which demonstrated that upper body subcutaneous adipose depots quantified on chest CT exams predicted adverse cardiometabolic risk factors in adjusted models, including elevated fasting glucose [36]. The metabolic relevance of subcutaneous fat distribution is further supported by the MESA Lung Chest CT study findings, in which higher subcutaneous adipose tissue index derived from thoracic scans showed significant associations with T2DM incidence [37].

In contrast to Korean cohort studies, in which abdominal visceral fat index outperformed other CT biomarkers in association with T2DM risk [20], our study found that TVFI in CAC scans contributed minimally to multivariable models. This discrepancy likely stems from anatomical differences in the adipose depots captured by different CT protocols. Abdominal CT primarily measures intra-abdominal visceral fat surrounding organs like the liver and intestines, which has consistently demonstrated strong associations with T2DM development [20, 38]. In contrast, chest CT evaluates mediastinal and pericardial visceral fat, which, while reflective of systemic adiposity patterns, may have less direct metabolic impact than abdominal visceral fat in T2DM pathogenesis. On the other hand, our study provides novel insights into the association between epicardial fat and incident T2DM. EFI showed a significant univariate association with incident T2DM risk, though only a modest association for most subgroups. Importantly, prior studies have demonstrated that epicardial adipose tissue is significantly increased in patients with T2DM and is associated with adverse cardiometabolic outcomes [39].

Contrary to our expectations based on our recent studies, in which we discovered strong associations between myosteatosis (defined as lowest quartile of SMD) in CAC scans and incident atrial fibrillation, heart failure, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [40, 41], the present study revealed that neither SMI nor SMD demonstrated significant associations with incident T2DM in our cohort without baseline obesity or hyperglycemia. This finding also contrasts with previous literature suggesting relationships between muscle parameters and T2DM risk, in which reduced muscle mass and increased myosteatosis have been linked to insulin resistance through impaired glucose disposal and inflammatory pathway alterations [42–44]. This discrepancy likely stems from the fact that our cohort was metabolically healthy without baseline obesity or hyperglycemia, where such associations appear less pronounced. Furthermore, myosteatosis in different muscle groups (upper body vs. lower body) may play different roles or have different cardiometabolic impacts. Han et al.‘s research in Japanese Americans demonstrated that thigh muscle area was inversely associated with future insulin resistance and T2DM incidence [45]. This fundamental difference in glucose metabolism by muscle group likely explains our non-significant findings, as our thoracic measurements may not capture the most metabolically relevant muscle tissues for glucose regulation, particularly in a metabolically healthy cohort.

Our subgroup analysis revealed the remarkable consistency of LAI and SFI associations with incident T2DM across diverse populations, supporting the robustness of our findings regardless of age, sex, BMI, most race/ethnicity groups, HbA1c levels, waist-to-hip, and waist-to-height categories. While some variation was noted — notably, SFI and TVFI lacked significant predictive value for T2DM development in the Hispanic subpopulation, and EFI demonstrated higher predictive importance than LAI in the Black/African-American population — these observations are limited by small sample sizes. These exploratory analyses highlight the need for further research to better understand potential differences in predictive value across populations.

Our findings have significant clinical implications for enhancing preventive strategies against T2DM through opportunistic screening. CAC scans for cardiovascular risk assessment contain valuable body composition data that can now be leveraged through AI technology to identify individuals at high risk for T2DM, especially among those who appear metabolically healthy by conventional criteria. This AI-enabled approach requires no additional radiation exposure, scanning cost, or patient and provider time, representing a significant advance in value-added radiology where maximum diagnostic information is extracted from existing imaging studies. In practice, preventive cardiology clinics could integrate automated AI-based adiposity measurements into routine CAC reporting, identifying metabolically at-risk patients despite normal BMI and glucose values, while endocrinology practices could establish referral pathways for patients with elevated LAI, implementing earlier preventive interventions. This approach is particularly valuable for addressing disparities in T2DM development among normal-weight individuals from diverse ethnic backgrounds, enabling earlier intervention in populations historically underserved by conventional screening methods.

AI-based body composition assessment has shown excellent reproducibility and accuracy compared with conventional methods, with studies demonstrating high intraclass correlation coefficients and minimal inter-observer variability. Specifically, AI-driven CT-based measurements exhibit superior precision compared to manual approaches [45]. The automated nature of AI analysis eliminates the subjective variability inherent in manual measurements, ensuring consistent results across different operators and timepoints. Regarding cost and practicality, CAC scanning represents a relatively affordable screening modality, with typical costs ranging from $50–250 with insurance coverage [46]. The radiation exposure is minimal (approximately 0.5–1.5 mSv), comparable to mammography and representing less than 50% of annual background radiation [47].

Practical implementation could be seamlessly integrated into existing radiology PACS infrastructure, with AI algorithms running in the background to automatically flag high-risk individuals based on opportunistic findings from CAC scans. This would enable clinicians to initiate earlier lifestyle interventions, adjust T2DM screening frequency, or consider preventive pharmacotherapy before glycemic abnormalities emerge. Such an approach is especially valuable for identifying the substantial number of future T2DM cases that arise in non-obese individuals who might otherwise go undetected by current preventive strategies. This approach aligns with the preventive cardiometabolic-based chronic disease model, emphasizing that early detection and intervention targeting cardiometabolic drivers—particularly abnormal adiposity and hyperglycemia—can significantly reduce the clinical and economic burdens of chronic disease [11].

When added to the established ADA diabetes risk score, LAI significantly improved both discrimination and risk reclassification for incident T2DM in this non-obese, normoglycemic cohort. The category-free net reclassification improvement enhanced both the event and non-event components, indicating that LAI effectively “up-classifies” individuals who will develop diabetes while simultaneously “down-classifying” those who will not, demonstrating clear potential to influence clinical management decisions.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has several notable strengths. First, it leverages the extensive MESA cohort with its long-term follow-up (median 19.7 years) and diverse ethnic representation, enhancing the generalizability of our findings. Second, our advanced AI technology enables automated, standardized quantification of body composition measures from CAC scans, providing more robust measurements by capturing the full volumetric extent and heterogeneity of tissue characteristics throughout the entire visible anatomy, unlike previous methods that relied on small regions of interest or single-slice assessments. Third, our focus on individuals without baseline obesity or hyperglycemia addresses a critical knowledge gap regarding early T2DM risk identification in apparently low-risk populations.

This study has several important limitations that warrant consideration. First, as an observational population-based investigation, our findings are constrained by the absence of interventions to control for potential residual confounders. Second, changes in CT-derived parameters not measured during follow-up may represent a source of unmeasured confounding. The trajectory of these biomarkers over time might provide deeper insights into their association with T2D incidence. Third, HbA1c—widely recognized as a strong predictor of T2DM incidence [46]—was not assessed at MESA baseline. For our subgroup analysis, we utilized HbA1c data from MESA exam 2, conducted approximately two years after baseline measurements. Fourth, oral glucose tolerance test and HbA1c were not available at the baseline exam, which may have led to inadvertent inclusion of individuals with undiagnosed T2DM, potentially attenuating or biasing the observed associations. Fifth, CAC scans in MESA were obtained using both EBCT and MDCT scanners, introducing potential scanner-related heterogeneity. To address this concern, we conducted scanner-stratified analyses demonstrating that although mean AI-derived adiposity measures differed significantly between scanner types (Supplementary Table 3), the associations with incident T2DM remained directionally consistent and of similar magnitude for both MDCT and EBCT scanners (Table 3). Because EBCT is no longer in routine use, generalizability to current practice may be limited; prospective validation on contemporary MDCT platforms with higher spatial resolution is warranted. Sixth, the MESA cohort was predominantly middle‑aged at enrollment, and included a relatively low proportion of non‑Western racial groups—particularly Asians—which may limit the applicability of our findings to younger populations and to underrepresented racial and ethnic groups. Finally, our analysis did not adjust for multiple comparisons, nor did we evaluate the calibration or reclassification performance of our findings compared to established T2D prediction models. Additionally, external validation in independent cohorts remains necessary to confirm the reproducibility and generalizability of these associations. It is important to note that our study intentionally did not adjust for traditional diabetes risk factors, as our objective was not to identify independent pathophysiological pathways but rather to develop an opportunistic screening tool. The AI-derived adiposity measures serve as composite risk markers that may capture the cumulative effect of various metabolic disturbances not individually detected by current screening guidelines in normoglycemic, non-obese individuals. This approach aligns with the opportunistic screening paradigm, where the goal is to flag high-risk individuals who would otherwise remain unidentified by conventional risk assessment protocols.

Future directions

Building on these findings, several key future directions warrant investigation. First, validation of our MESA findings in other cohorts with different imaging protocols and different ethnic compositions is essential to confirm the generalizability of these AI-derived adiposity measurements across diverse populations and healthcare settings. Second, developing multimodal risk prediction models that combine these imaging biomarkers with genetic profiles, metabolomic signatures, and traditional clinical risk factors could enable more personalized and precise T2DM risk stratification, potentially guiding targeted preventive interventions for those at highest risk. Third, longitudinal studies examining the temporal trajectories of these CT-derived measures and their relationship to metabolic deterioration may provide valuable insights into T2DM pathophysiology. Fourth, similar studies should be conducted using full chest CT scans to assess whether these AI-derived adiposity measurements remain robust and predictive across different scan types and anatomical coverage. Finally, multicenter studies should establish standardized, scanner-agnostic cutoffs and thresholds for these CT measurements to account for technical variations and ensure reliable clinical implementation across diverse healthcare settings.

Conclusion

In this comprehensive analysis of AI-CVD data from MESA, we observed that AI-derived adiposity measures from CAC scans predict incident T2DM in adults without obesity or hyperglycemia over a median follow-up of 19.7 years. Liver adiposity, assessed by the liver attenuation index (LAI), was the strongest predictor, followed by subcutaneous, visceral, and epicardial fat. These associations were consistent across diverse demographic and clinical subgroups. The findings highlight the clinical value of CAC scans beyond cardiovascular risk assessment, revealing metabolic risk markers that can be opportunistically extracted via AI without added radiation, scan time, or patient burden. Future studies should validate these results and define strategies for integrating AI-enabled opportunistic screening into personalized preventive care.
