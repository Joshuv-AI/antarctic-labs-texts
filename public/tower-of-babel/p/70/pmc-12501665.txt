Photocatalytic Co‐Reduction of CO2 and Nitrate over Porphyrin Metal–Organic Frameworks: Dual Atomic Active Site and Nanostructure Synergy Enhances C–N Coupling for Urea Production
Authors: Tong Bao, Chencheng Tang, Yunuo Wu, Yin Bi, Chaoqi Zhang, Yamin Xi, Yingying Zou, Chao Liu, Chengzhong Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe photocatalytic co‐reduction of NO3 − and CO2 (NitRR&CRR) offers a sustainable approach for urea synthesis and environment remediation. However, there is a lack of high‐performance photocatalysts. Herein, we report a nanoflower‐like bimetallic porphyrin metal–organic framework superstructure (Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF, TCPP = tetrakis(4‐carboxyphenyl)porphyrin) as an efficient NitRR&CRR photocatalyst for urea production. Systematical investigations have revealed that the Cu and Co centers serve as the active sites for driving NitRR and CRR, respectively. The high pore volumes of nanoflower architecture can improve the light adsorption, promote the reactant enrichment, and intermediate generation. Consequently, the C–N coupling process over Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF is facilitated with reduced energy barriers and inhibited side reactions, resulting in an unprecedent urea yield of 2459.8 µg h−1 gcat −1 in the absence of sacrificial agents. This study provides new insights into the design of advanced photocatalysts for urea synthesis.Keywords: Carbon dioxide reduction, Nitrate reduction, Photocatalysis, Porphyrin metal–organic framework, Urea synthesis

Introduction

Urea (CO(NH2)2) is an indispensable nitrogen fertilizer in modern agriculture with global demand surpassing 200 million tons per year.[ 1 , 2 , 3 ] The current industrial urea production predominately relies on the classic Bosch–Meiser process, i.e., the reaction between carbon dioxide (CO2) and liquid ammonia (NH3) at high temperature (150–200 °C) and pressure (150–250 bar), which consumes substantial fossil fuels and causes heavy CO2 emission.[ 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 ] The solar‐driven photocatalytic C–N coupling via co‐reduction of N2 and CO2 (NRR&CRR) is potentially an energy‐saving and sustainable approach for urea synthesis.[ 8 , 9 ] Various photocatalysts such as noble metal‐ and transition metal‐based materials have been reported for producing urea via photocatalytic NRR&CRR.[ 10 , 11 ] However, restricted by the high N≡N bond energy (941 kJ mol−1), low solubility of N2 in H2O, and strong competition with side reactions such as NRR, CRR, and hydrogen evolution reaction (HER), the overall performance is still unsatisfactory with relatively low urea production rates (< 1500 µg h−1 gcat −1) in pure water without sacrificial agents.[ 12 , 13 ]

Compared to N2, nitrate (NO3−) is a more active nitrogen feedstock with lower N─O bond energy (204 kJ mol−1), excellent water solubility and high abundance in wastewater.[ 14 , 15 ] At the same time, NO3 − is an environmental pollutant in water bodies and industrial / agricultural effluents, thus photocatalytic co‐reduction of NO3− and CO2 (NitRR&CRR) is expected to simultaneously achieve efficient urea synthesis and wastewater purification.[ 16 , 17 ] Recent progresses in electrocatalytic C–N coupling have highlighted the superiorities of NitRR&CRR route in urea synthesis, delivering urea yields an order of magnitude higher than NRR&CRR systems.[ 18 , 19 ] However, the research on photocatalytic NitRR&CRR is still in the infancy. To our knowledge, only one study has reported a Cs2CuBr4/TiO x composite photocatalyst with a low urea yield of 0.223 µg h−1 gcat −1.[ 20 ] There is huge room for developing high‐performance photocatalysts for efficient urea synthesis via the photocatalytic NitRR&CRR approach.

Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), as an important class of porous crystalline materials, have attracted extensive attention in the fields of catalysis, adsorption, separation, and energy storage.[ 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 ] Specifically, MOFs have been applied as electrocatalysts in NitRR&CRR for urea synthesis with excellent performance.[ 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 ] Recent studies have shown that the C–N coupling efficiency in electrocatalytic NitRR&CRR is restricted by the CRR step due to low solubility and insufficient supply of CO2 molecules.[ 31 ] Nevertheless, their applications as NitRR&CRR photocatalysts are rarely reported. To design MOF‐based photocatalysts for urea synthesis, porphyrin‐derived MOFs that combine the photosensitivity characteristics of porphyrin molecules and the structural advantages of MOFs are excellent candidates.[ 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 ] The structure of porphyrin MOFs can be adjusted by varying the ligands (e.g., tetrakis(4‐carboxyphenyl)porphyrin, TCPP) and the metal ions (M2+, M = Fe/Co/Ni/Cu/Zn, etc.), either as nodes in the framework or chelated in the porphyrin ring.[ 36 , 37 , 38 ] Despite these unique features, the use of porphyrin‐derived MOFs as photocatalysts in the NitRR&CRR reaction has not been reported; how to design such photocatalysts with high‐performance for urea synthesis remains challenging.

Herein, we report the synthesis of bimetallic porphyrin MOFs composed of nanosheets and a flower‐like architecture (Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF) as a high‐performance NitRR&CRR photocatalyst for urea production (Scheme 1). Experimental and computational calculation results have revealed that Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF undergoes ligand‐to‐metal electron transfer upon photoexcitation with Cu and Co centers as active sites for NitRR and CRR, respectively. Besides, the nanoflower architecture with high pore volumes enhances the light harvesting and promotes the reactant adsorption for accelerating the intermediate generation (e.g., *CO, *NO). The elaborate structural design at both atomic and nano‐scale levels synergistically facilitates the C–N coupling toward urea production with reduced energy barriers and inhibits the side reactions. Consequently, Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF exhibits a urea yield of 2459.8 µg h−1 gcat −1, superior than control samples (mono‐metallic materials, Cu‐TCPP(Co) nanosheet) and the reported photocatalysts in both NRR&CRR and NitRR&CRR systems.

Results and Discussion

Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF was synthesized by employing Cu(NO3)2·3H2O as the metal source and TCPP(Co) as the ligand according a literature protocol,[ 39 ] except a high‐concentration trifluoroacetic acid (TFA, 8.13 mM) was used. The morphology and microstructure of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF were investigated by field‐emission scanning electron microscope (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The SEM image of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF (Figure 1a) exhibits a flower‐like structure assembled by interconnected ultrathin nanosheets. The average particle diameter and thickness of nanosheets are estimated to be ≈ 6 µm and 20–30 nm, respectively. The nanosheets wrap into void space with open pores as large as hundreds of nanometers, but not uniform in size. Energy‐dispersive X‐ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping images of one typical particle (Figure 1b) indicate the homogeneous spatial distribution of C, N, O, Cu, and Co elements throughout the whole structure. Figure 1c,d show the TEM images, further showing the three‐dimensional (3D) flower‐like morphology.

a) and b) SEM images and corresponding elemental mapping images, c) and d) TEM images, e) HRTEM image, f) experimental and Pawley refined XRD patterns of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF; high resolution XPS spectra of g) Cu 2p and h) Co 2p of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF, Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NS, Cu‐TCPP‐NF, and TCPP(Co).

In the high‐resolution TEM (HRTEM) image (Figure 1e), the lattice fringes with an interplanar distance of 1.64 nm are ascribed to the (001) plane of Cu‐TCPP(Co) crystal.[ 39 ] Powder X‐ray diffraction (XRD) pattern refined by the Pawley fitting reveals a tetragonal 2D structure with an alternative AB stacking mode. The cell parameters of the simulated structure are a = b = 16.60 Å, c = 17.5 Å, α = β = γ = 90° (R wp = 4.47%, R p = 3.31%, Table S1), similar to the reported porphyrin MOFs.[ 40 ] The diffraction peaks at 2θ = 7.6°, 8.9°, 12.0°, 19.5°, and 30.6° are assigned to the (110), (001), (320), (330), and (440) crystallographic planes, respectively. The N2 sorption isotherm of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF (Figure S1a) shows type IV curves with a steep capillary condensation at lower relative pressure (P/P0) < 0.1, indicative of micropores. The pore size distribution curve (Figure S1b) shows a micropore diameter centered at ∼1.3 nm. A steep N2 uptake step is also observed at P/P0 > 0.9, indicating the existence of large meso‐macrospores, consistent with the SEM and TEM observations. The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) specific surface area, total pore volume and micropore volume were determined be 383.1 m2 g−1, 0.39 cm3 g−1, and 0.13 cm3 g−1, respectively.

The chemical composition of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF was studied by Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, with TCPP and TCPP(Co) ligands as comparison. As shown in the FTIR spectrum of TCPP (Figure S2a), the characteristic peaks of in‐plane N─H vibration and C ═ O stretching vibration are observed at 962 and 1683 cm−1, respectively.[ 41 ] Upon cobalt incorporation, the peak intensity of in‐plane N─H vibration in the spectrum of TCPP(Co) is significantly reduced with the generation of a new peak attributed to Co‐N stretching vibration at 1000 cm−1, suggesting the substitution of protons by cobalt ions (Figure S2b,c).[ 42 ] By reaction of TCPP(Co) with Cu2+, the peak of C ═ O stretching vibration almost disappears while a distinct peak of Cu─O vibration emerges at 713 cm−1 in the spectrum of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF, ascribed to the Cu─O bonds in Cu2(COO)4 paddlewheel clusters that are coordinated with the carboxyl groups in TCPP(Co) (Figure S2d).[ 43 ]

X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was conducted to explore the surface electronic states of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF. In addition to TCPP(Co), Cu‐TCPP‐NF was also prepared as a control sample via the similar synthesis protocol of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF, except TCPP was used as the ligand. SEM and TEM images together with XRD characterization of Cu‐TCPP‐NF (Figure S3a–d) reveal the similar flower‐like morphology composed of nanosheets and crystalline structure with Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF. The XPS survey spectrum of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF, Cu‐TCPP‐NF and TCPP(Co) are consistent with their difference in compositions (Figure S4a). The high‐resolution Cu 2p spectrum of Cu‐TCPP‐NF (Figure 1g) can be deconvoluted into seven peaks attributed to 2p3/2 and 2p1/2 states of Cu0/Cu+ (932.44 and 952.58 eV) and Cu2+ (934.46 and 954.46 eV), and three satellite peaks at 939.85, 943.81, and 962.77 eV.[ 44 ] Compared to Cu‐TCPP‐NF, the Cu0/Cu+ and Cu2⁺ peaks in Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF exhibit negative shifts of 0.52 and 0.43 eV, respectively. To further distinguish Cu+ and Cu0, the Cu LMM auger spectrum of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF was recorded (Figure S5). The two peaks at 570.25 and 572.89 eV are assigned to Cu2+ and Cu+ species, respectively, suggesting the presence of Cu+ rather than Cu0. The Co 2p spectrum of TCPP(Co) (Figure 1h) displays two peaks of 2p3/2 and 2p1/2 orbitals of Co2+ at 779.93 and 795.53 eV, respectively. The binding energies of Co peaks in the spectrum of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF are also 0.26 eV lower than those of TCPP(Co).

Figure S4b presents the O 1s spectrum of TCPP(Co), where the peak at 532.28 eV is assigned to C─O bond. After coordination with Cu2+, an additional peak corresponding to Cu─O bond is generated at 530.70 eV in the spectrum of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF. The substitution of TCPP(Co) with TCPP leads to a 0.58 eV negative shift of Cu─O peak for Cu‐TCPP‐NF. In the N 1s spectrum of TCPP(Co) (Figure S4c), the peaks located at 398.38 and 402.06 eV are ascribed to C ═ N─C and Co─N bond, respectively. Compared to TCPP(Co), the binding energies of C ═ N─C and Co─N peaks for Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF increase by 0.51 and 0.46 eV, respectively. For Cu‐TCPP‐NF without Co─N bond, the peak at 399.32 eV belongs to H─N─C bond and the binding energy of C ═ N─C peak is 0.92 eV lower than that of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF. The C 1s spectrum of TCPP(Co) (Figure S4d) can be fitted into three peaks of C─C group (284.81 eV), C─N group (285.94 eV), and O─C ═ O group (288.39 eV). Similar to the evaluated binding energy of O and N, the O─C ═ O peak of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF also positively shifts to 288.57 eV.

Compared to either TCPP(Co) (ligand chelated to the porphyrin ring) or Cu‐TCPP‐NF (ligand coordinated to the node), the opposite binding energy shift direction between metal centers (Cu/Co) and nonmetal atoms in ligand (O/N/C) in Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF is attributed to its unique ligand‐to‐bimetal electron transfer mode. Due to electronegativity difference, the ligand TCPP attracts less electron in the case of two coordinated metal sites than only one metal, leading to the formation of more electron‐sufficient Cu and Co sites in the bimetallic Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF.

The formation process of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF was investigated by monitoring the time‐dependent structure evolution during synthesis. The SEM image of the sample collected at 1 h shows individual nanosheet‐like morphology with the size approximately of 1–5 µm (Figure S6a). By prolonging the reaction time to 1.5 h, the curved nanosheets assembled into flower‐like rudiment with decreased amount of nanosheets (Figure S6b). After 2 h, the nanosheets almost disappear and the flower‐like morphology is dominated with gradually increased diameter (Figure S6c,d).

When only the TFA concentration was reduced to 0.813 mM, isolated nanosheets without flower‐like morphology were obtained with relatively flat surface (Figure S7a–c). The nanosheet diameter ranges from 8 to 18 µm, significantly larger than that of the nanosheet units in Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF. The obtained sample, named as Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NS, was characterized by XRD (Figure S7d) and XPS (Figure S8), showing almost identical crystalline and electronic structures with Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF. The N2 sorption isotherm and pore size distribution curve of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NS (Figure S9) suggest a micropore‐dominated structure. Compared to Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF, the BET specific surface area (354.4 m2 g−1) and micropore volume (0.12 cm3 g−1) of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NS are similar, but the total pore volume (0.233 cm3 g−1) is reduced by ∼ 40% (Table S2). When TFA was replaced with acetic acid (CH3COOH) and HCl, Cu‐TCPP(Co) with a flower‐like morphology was not obtained neither. Instead, only disordered nanoparticle is obtained by adding CH3COOH (Figure S10), while a nanosheet morphology with flat surface was observed with HCl (Figure S11). The above results have shown that the high acidity, high ionic strength, and the presence of ─CF3 groups arising from high‐concentration TFA is crucial for the formation of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF with a flower‐like morphology. It is postulated that the negative surface charge of nanosheets (zeta potential of –12.46 mV) can be efficiently neutralized via proton adsorption under highly acidic condition, reducing the electrostatic repulsion between nanosheets.[ 45 , 46 ] According to the Derjaguin–Landau–Verwey–Overbeek (DLVO) theory,[ 47 , 48 ] the high ionic strength promotes the nanosheet aggregation and assembly. Besides, the ─CF3 group in TFA can reduce the surface tension,[ 49 ] favoring the formation of smaller‐size nanosheets that undergo further assembly into a flower‐like morphology.

Ultraviolet–visible diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (UV–vis DRS) was employed to probe the optical properties of the samples. As illustrated in Figure S12a, Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF exhibits strong light absorption from the UV to vis region. The characteristic porphyrin absorption bands including Soret band (382 nm, S0→S2 transition) and Q‐band (530–540 nm, S0→S1 transition) are observed.[ 35 , 50 ] The optical bandgap (E g) of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF was determined to be 1.65 eV via the Kubelka–Munk equation (Figure S12b), narrower than that of Cu‐TCPP‐NF (1.80 eV). Moreover, the light harvesting ability of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF is slightly stronger than Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NS, presumably induced by the enhanced light scattering inside the hierarchical pores of flowers.[ 51 , 52 , 53 , 54 ] Mott–Schottky analysis (Figure S12c) further reveals the conduction band (CB) potentials of –0.66, –0.62, and –0.56 V versus NHE for Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF, Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NS, and Cu‐TCPP‐NF, respectively. Based on the E g and CB values, the valence band (VB) positions of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF, Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NS, and Cu‐TCPP‐NF were calculated as 0.99, 1.09, and 1.24 V, respectively. The derived band structures are summarized in Figure S12d, allowing for the photocatalytic reduction of CO and NO3 −. Moreover, band structure and density of state (DOS) have been calculated. As shown in Figure S13, the valance band maximum (VBM) and conduction band minimum (CBM) are predominantly contributed by C orbitals of porphyrin ligand and Cu/Co orbitals, respectively, suggesting the photogenerated electrons transfer from porphyrin ligands to metal sites upon excitation.[ 55 , 56 ]

The performance of photocatalytic urea synthesis was evaluated in a CO2‐saturated NO3− aqueous solution (1 M) under visible‐light irradiation (λ ≥ 420 nm) without sacrificial agents. In addition to TCPP(Co) and Cu‐TCPP‐NF, other two samples including Co‐TCPP‐NF and TCPP (Cu) were also prepared as comparison (Figures S14 and S15). The possible products during photocatalysis including urea, NH4 +, NO2 −, CO, and H2 were quantified by using diacetyl monoxime method, indophenol blue spectrophotometry, Griess reagent method and gas chromatography, respectively. The corresponding standard curves are presented in Figures S16–S19. The yield rates of urea, NH4 +, NO2 −, and CO for TCPP(Co), Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NS, Cu‐TCPP‐NF, and Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF are shown in Figure 2a. TCPP(Co) affords a urea production rate of only 55.2 µg h−1 gcat −1 but a relatively high yield of CO as the main product (158.6 µg h−1 gcat −1). For Cu‐TCPP‐NF, the urea production rate increases to 568.5 µg gcat −1 h−1 with the evolution of NH4 + (189.8 µg h−1 gcat −1) and NO2 − (278.4 µg h−1 gcat −1) as dominant byproducts. By incorporating Co, Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF delivers a significantly elevated urea production rate of 2459.8 µg h−1 gcat −1 with trace amounts of NH4 + (95.1 µg h−1 gcat −1), NO2 − (76.4 µg h−1 gcat −1), and CO (37.2 µg h−1 gcat −1). Compared to Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF, Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NS with the same composition but different morphology exhibits a lower urea yield (1025.5 µg h−1 gcat −1) but higher yields of NH4 + (510.1 µg h−1 gcat −1) and NO2 − (250.7 µg h−1 gcat −1). Besides, Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF exhibits a H2 yield of 5.7 µmol gcat −1 h−1, lower than that of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NS (12.3 µmol gcat −1 h−1), Cu‐TCPP (24.4 µmol gcat −1 h−1), and TCPP(Co) (36.8 µmol gcat −1 h−1) (Figure S20). For other two single‐metal samples, the urea yield rate of Co‐TCPP‐NF (30.1 µg gcat −1 h−1) and TCPP(Cu) (106.2 µg gcat −1 h−1) are also much inferior than that of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF (Figure S21).

To further present the role of Cu and Co sites, Na2S2O3 and KSCN as masking agents were used to block Cu and Co sites in Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF, respectively. The photocatalytic results show that the urea yield dramatically decreased to 227.3 µg gcat −1 h−1 and 60.3 µg gcat −1 h−1 with undesired NH4 + and CO as main products after blocking Cu and Co sites, respectively (Figure S22), verifying the synergistic effect of Cu/Co dual sites in promoting C─N coupling. In the presence of impurity ions (e.g., Cl− and SO4 2−) that widely exist in real wastewater, the urea production rate slightly decreases to 2327.6 µg gcat −1 h−1, suggesting the excellent ion‐resisting property of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF. When performed in the presence of sacrificial agents (e.g., NaHCO3), the urea yield Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF remarkably increases to 12280.3 µg h−1 gcat −1, further demonstrating its superior photocatalytic performance. These observations suggest the crucial roles of both bimetal composition and flower‐like morphology in promoting C─N coupling for urea production and suppressing side reactions including NitRR, CRR, and HER. Notably, the exceptional urea yield of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF surpasses all reported photocatalysts used in both NRR&CRR and NitRR&CRR systems (Figures 2b and S23 and Table S3). Except for the NitRR&CRR process, the coupling oxidation reaction was also explored. As shown in Figure S24, the yield of O2 as the oxidation product follows the order of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF (150.9 µmol gcat −1 h−1) > Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NS (113.6 µmol gcat −1 h−1) > Cu‐TCPP‐NF (60.2 µmol gcat −1 h−1) > TCPP(Co) (33.8 µmol gcat −1 h−1).

To further explore the photocatalytic efficiency of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF, the apparent quantum yield (AQY) was measured under monochromatic light irradiation with different wavelengths. The AQY values show a wavelength‐dependence feature, with a maximum value of 8.12% at 400 nm (Figure 2c). In addition, the solar‐to‐urea (STU) conversion efficiency of the Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF catalyst was determined as 0.194% under standard AM 1.5 G solar illumination. To identify the N source of produced urea, an isotopic labeling experiment was performed. The nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra (Figure 2d) display doublet and singlet peaks of CO(15NH2)2 and CO(14NH2)2 when using 15NO3 − and 14NO3 − as raw materials, respectively.[ 57 ] Upon changing the feeding gas to 13CO2, a typical singlet shift of 13CO(NH2)2 is observed in the C NMR spectrum (Figure S25).[ 58 ] In addition, negligible urea was generated when either or both reactants (CO2 and NO3 −) were excluded from the reaction (Figures 2e and S26). For the system containing only KNO3 or CO2, NH4 +, or CO was generated as the main product, respectively. These results verify the production of urea through photocatalytic C─N coupling between CO2 and NO3 −. Moreover, no product is detected in the absence of photocatalysts, demonstrating the indispensable role of photocatalyst for urea formation. Apart from the excellent activity, Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF also possesses high photocatalytic durability, with only an 6.88% decline of urea yield after 100 continuous reaction cycles (Figure 2f). Characterizations of used Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF by SEM, TEM, XRD, and XPS (Figure S27) and ICP results of the post‐test reaction solution demonstrate that the nanoflower‐like composition morphology, crystalline, and electronic structures are well preserved. Besides, almost no Cu and Co elements are detected in the supernatant after reaction, suggesting the structural integrality during photocatalysis.

To understand the enhanced performance of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF, the reactant adsorption and activation properties of the samples were systematically investigated. First, the distribution of CO2 and NO3 − within Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF and ‐NS was simulated by finite element analysis (FEA) method. As presented in Figure 3a,b, two models with distinct morphologies were constructed including a 3D flower assembled by interconnected ultrathin nanosheets and an individual 2D nanosheet. Then, the spatial distribution of NO3 − and CO2 concentrations was simulated by placing the nanoflower and nanosheet models in the aqueous solution containing NO3 − (0.1 M) and CO2 (0.01 M). In aqueous solution, the dissolved NO3 − and CO2 molecules trend to be adsorbed onto the surface of 2D nanosheet with high surface energy via van der Waals interactions to reduce total interfacial energy of the system.[ 59 , 60 ] By assembling the nanosheets into nanoflowers, the created pore channels can accelerate the adsorption kinetics and increase the adsorption capacity via capillary force and reinforced potential wells.[ 61 , 62 ]

Finite element simulation of the local concentration of a) CO2 and b) NO3 − around Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF and Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NS; c) CO2 TPD; d) CO2 adsorption isotherms; e) time‐dependent NO3 − adsorption tests of TCPP(Co), Cu‐TCPP‐NF, Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF, and Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NS; LSV curves f) under different conditions of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF; g) with CO2‐saturation and Na2SO4; and h) with Ar‐saturation and KNO3 of TCPP(Co), Cu‐TCPP‐NF, Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF, and Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NS.

To provide direct evidence, CO2 temperature‐programmed desorption (CO2‐TPD) measurement, CO2 and NO3 − adsorption tests were performed. The CO2‐TPD curves (Figure 3c) show two major adsorption peaks assigned to physisorption (90–190 °C) and chemisorption (300–350 °C). The physisorption temperature of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF is measured to be 193.6 °C, close to that of Cu‐TCPP‐NF but higher than TCPP(Co) (150.3 °C) and Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NS (151.5 °C), suggesting that the flower‐like morphology is advantageous for CO2 capture. In contrast, the chemisorption temperature of Cu‐TCPP‐NF (329.4 °C) is lower than TCPP(Co) (349.7 °C). For Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF, the temperature is slightly elevated to 355.3 °C, almost identical with Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NS (353.6 °C), indicating that the Co coordination is mainly responsible for CO2 chemisorption and the Cu coordination slightly improve the capability.

Figure 3d displays the CO2 adsorption isotherms. Among all the samples under test, Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF exhibits the highest CO2 uptake amount (53.0 cm3 g−1). In addition to CO2, the NO3 − adsorption test showed that the NO3 − adsorption ratio capability follows the order of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF (23.3%) > Cu‐TCPP‐NF (20.9%) > Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NS (13.1%) > TCPP(Co) (2.2%), demonstrating the strongest NO3 − adsorption capacity of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF (Figure 3e). The above results are consistent with the FEA observations.

Furthermore, the CO2/NO3 − activation capabilities of the samples were studied by linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) measurements. As shown in Figure 3f, Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF exhibits a higher current density in the presence of both CO2 and NO3 −, compared to that under other conditions in the absence of reactants or with individual reactants, implying the occurrence of photocatalytic co‐reduction of CO2 and NO3 −. By comparing the LSV curves of different samples collected in the electrolytes with only CO2 or NO3 −, TCPP(Co) delivers significantly higher CO2RR activity than Cu‐TCPP, whereas the activity trend is inverted for NitRR, suggesting that the Co and Cu sites predominantly account for CO2RR and NitRR, respectively. For Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF, the current densities of both CO2RR and NitRR are the highest among all samples. The electrochemical studies align well with the photocatalytic results, revealing the crucial roles of bimetal sites and flower‐like structure in facilitating CO2 and NO3 − activation.

To further study the superior photocatalytic performance of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF, the charge separation efficiency was investigated through photoelectrochemical and photoluminescence (PL) methods. The photocurrent response profiles in Figure 4a reveal that Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF exhibits the highest photocurrent density compared to other samples, demonstrating the promoted separation of photoinduced electron‐hole pairs. The electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS, Figure 4b) manifest the smallest arc radius of Cu‐TCPP(Co) NF in Nyquist plots, indicative of the lowest charge transfer resistance. Moreover, open‐circuit voltage decay (OCVD) measurement was carried out to assess the photocarrier dynamics (Figure 4c). Under illumination, the voltage response follows the sequence of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF > Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NS > Cu‐TCPP‐NF > TCPP(Co), suggesting the enhanced charge separation efficiency of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF. After switching off the light (Figure 4d), the slowest voltage decay is observed for Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF with prolonged carrier lifetime. In the steady‐state PL spectra (Figure 4e), Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF shows the lowest emission peak intensity among all samples, indicative of the lowest electron‐hole recombination efficiency. Additionally, time‐resolved PL (TRPL) spectra (Figure 4f) with biexponential fitting analysis yields an average carrier lifetime of 17.59 ns for Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF, longer than that of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NS (14.61 ns), Cu‐TCPP‐NF (14.64 ns) and TCPP(Co) (1.08 ns). The promoted charge separation with prolonged carrier lifetime of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF is conducive for improving the photocatalytic performance.

To monitor the C─N coupling process on Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF, in situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) was employed to track the intermediate evolution. The DRIFTS spectra and corresponding contour maps of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF, TCPP(Co), and Cu‐TCPP‐NF are illustrated in Figure 5a–f, respectively. In the spectrum of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF in darkness, the peak located at 3548 cm−1 corresponds to the adsorption of CO2 molecules. Under light irradiation for 20 min, the peak intensity of adsorbed CO2 decreases with the generation of two new peaks attributed to *CO and *NO at 1365 and 1540 cm−1, respectively, suggesting the concurrent reduction of CO2 and NO3−.[ 63 , 64 ] More importantly, the characteristic bands of *OCNO species and C─N stretching vibration are detected at 2077 and 1420 cm−1, respectively, suggesting the occurrence of C─N coupling between *CO and *NO intermediates.[ 11 ] The distinct peaks of stretching mode of ─NH (3651 cm−1) and bending mode of ─NH2 (1648 cm−1) further verify the formation of urea.[ 65 ] By prolonging the reaction time, the peak intensities of *OCNO, C─N, ─NH, and ─NH2 gradually increases, indicating the continuous urea production.

In situ DRIFTS spectra and corresponding contour map collected on a) and b) Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF, c) and d) TCPP(Co), and e) and f) Cu‐TCPP‐NF. g) 2D charge density distribution of Cu‐TCPP(Co). h) CO2 and NO3 − adsorption energy on the Cu and Co sites in different samples. i) Free energy diagrams for photosynthesis of urea on TCPP(Co), Cu‐TCPP, and Cu‐TCPP(Co).

For TCPP(Co), the peak intensity of *CO is sharply intensified with the irradiation time. However, the *NO intermediate is almost undetected with reduced peak intensities of *OCNO, C─N, ─NH, and ─NH2 than Cu‐TCPP(Co) NF, suggesting that the Co center favors CO2RR rather than NitRR. In sharp contrast, strong *NO signal is observed in the spectra of Cu‐TCPP‐NF, while the peaks relative to CO2RR are extremely weak (i.e., *CO2 and *CO), indicating the preferential NitRR activity over CO2RR (Figure 5e,f). Consequently, the urea generation over Cu‐TCPP‐NF is limited, as evidenced by the largely attenuated ─NH and ─NH2 signals.

To gain deeper insights into the reaction mechanism, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were carried out. First, differential charge density was analyzed to probe the spatial charge distribution within Cu‐TCPP(Co). The differential charge distribution diagram (Figure 5g) demonstrates the electron accumulation around both Co and Cu atoms while electron depletion from porphyrin ring, suggesting the ligand‐to‐metal electron transfer. Afterward, the adsorption energies of CO2 and NO3 − on TCPP(Co), Cu‐TCPP, and Cu‐TCPP(Co) were calculated to identify active sites. As shown in Figure 5h, the adsorption energy of CO2 on Co sites (–0.67 eV) in Cu‐TCPP(Co) is lower than that on Cu sites (–0.28 eV), indicating that the CO2 adsorption is thermodynamically more favorable on Co sites. Moreover, the more negative CO2 adsorption energy on Co sites in Cu‐TCPP(Co) than that for TCPP(Co) (–0.62 eV) and Cu‐TCPP (–0.25 eV) demonstrates the enhanced CO2 adsorption via bimetallic coordination. Conversely, NO3 − is preferentially adsorbed on the Cu sites in Cu‐TCPP(Co) with more negative adsorption energies (–1.53 eV for Cu, –0.34 eV for Co). Therefore, the Co and Cu sites in Cu‐TCPP(Co) are responsible for driving CO2RR and NitRR, respectively, consistent with the experimental observations (e.g., CO2‐TPD, LSV, in situ DRIFTS).

Moreover, the Gibbs free energy change (ΔG) and optimized adsorption configurations of each basic step during the urea synthesis process are presented in Figures 5i and S28–S30. In light of the different adsorption strength of CO2 and NO3 − over Cu‐TCPP(Co), Cu‐TCPP, and TCPP(Co), the reaction starts with NitRR for Cu‐TCPP(Co) and Cu‐TCPP while with CRR for TCPP(Co). The C─N bond formation step by coupling *NO and *CO intermediates (*NO + *CO → *OCNO) is the rate‐determining step (RDS) for Cu‐TCPP(Co) and Cu‐TCPP. As shown in Figure 5i, Cu‐TCPP(Co) requires an energy barrier of 0.38 eV for RDS, significantly lower than that of Cu‐TCPP (1.72 eV). As for TCPP(Co), an even higher energy cost of 2.74 eV is needed for the RDS of *CO + *NO3→ *CONO3. The observations suggest the thermodynamically more favorable C‐N coupling process over Cu‐TCPP(Co), in good agreement with the electrochemical results. Additionally, the HER activities of the samples were also assessed. As presented in Figure S31, the ΔG of H* to H2 for Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF is calculated to be 1.22 eV, higher than that of Cu‐TCPP (0.85 eV) and TCPP(Co) (0.53 eV), indicating suppressed HER over Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF. This result is in line with the H2 byproduct test.

Collectively, the experimental and DFT results reveal the vital roles of nanostructure and dual‐site engineering in promoting urea production. Within Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF, the Cu and Co sites are responsible for NitRR and CRR to produce *NO and *CO intermediates, respectively. The subsequent spillover of NO from Cu sites to adjacent Co sites induces the C─N coupling reaction for the formation of key *OCNO intermediate. The 3D flower‐like architecture enhances the reactant adsorption (i.e., NO3 − and CO2) and intermediate generation (i.e., *CO, *NO), facilitating the C─N coupling and inhibiting the side reactions including NitRR and CRR. Together with the improved light harvesting and charge carrier separation, the excellent performance of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF is achieved. For practical applications, developing large‐scale and cost‐effective preparation route of Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF is a critical challenge and requires further research.

Conclusion

In summary, we have designed a bimetallic porphyrin MOF with nanoflower‐like structure (Cu‐TCPP(Co)‐NF) for photocatalytic NitRR&CRR with a urea yield of 2459.8 µg h−1 gcat −1. It is demonstrated that the Cu and Co sites facilitate the formation of *NO and *CO intermediates via NitRR and CRR, respectively. The nanoflower architecture improves the light harvesting, promotes reactant adsorption and accelerates intermediate generation. Taken together, the C─N coupling process is boosted toward enhanced urea production. Our study paves the way for the development of high‐performance photocatalysts for urea synthesis.
