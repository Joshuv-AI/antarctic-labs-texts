ADAR-GPT: Learning the language of the epitranscriptome
Authors: Siqi Wang, Anna Fraser, Xinshu Xiao
Published: 2026

The Linguistic Complexity of the Epitranscriptome

Adenosine-to-inosine (A-to-I) RNA editing is a widespread posttranscriptional modification contributing to transcriptomic and proteomic diversity (1, 2). Catalyzed by the Adenosine Deaminase Acting on RNA (ADAR) family of enzymes, this reaction converts specific adenosines to inosines, which are interpreted as guanosine by the cellular splicing and translational machinery (3). Thereby, A-to-I editing has broad functional consequences, including protein-coding sequence alterations, splicing sites modifications, and the reshaping of miRNA target interactions, without altering the underlying genomic architecture. In this issue of PNAS, Rosenwasser et al. present a transformative step in deciphering the rules of RNA modification, introducing a computational framework that leverages Large Language Models (LLMs) to predict editing sites with unprecedented precision (4).

Biologically, A-to-I editing ensures immune homeostasis by masking endogenous double-stranded RNAs (dsRNAs) from cytosolic sensors like MDA5 (2, 5, 6). Loss-of-function mutations in ADAR1 are linked to autoimmune disorders, including Aicardi-Goutières Syndrome (7). In cancer, A-to-I editing is frequently dysregulated both globally and at disease-relevant loci, and drives malignancies (8), such as the recoding of AZIN1 in hepatocellular carcinoma (9).

Although millions of RNA editing sites exist, primarily residing within noncoding Alu repetitive elements (10, 11), the cis-regulatory grammar for ADAR selectivity (i.e., “RNA editing code”) remains poorly understood. Prediction is complicated by ADAR’s promiscuity and substrate specificity, the complex interplay of sequence and structure, as well as the tissue-specific expression patterns (12–15).

In PNAS, Rosenwasser et al. present a transformative step in deciphering the rules of RNA modification, introducing a computational framework that leverages Large Language Models (LLMs) to predict editing sites with unprecedented precision (4).

The core methodological breakthrough is the Continual Fine-Tuning (CFT) paradigm. Previous machine learning models in epitranscriptomics typically relied on static training, treating editing as a binary state (edited vs. non-edited). However, true biological editing exists on a gradient. Rosenwasser et al. addressed this gap by progressively refining ADAR-GPT across a sequential curriculum of increasing editing frequency thresholds: 1%, 5%, 10%, and 15%. This adaptation allows the model to incrementally distinguish biological signals from low-level background noise while preventing “catastrophic forgetting”, a phenomenon where training on new data overwrites the earlier knowledge learned from previous tasks.

ADAR-GPT processes RNA as a “language” by encoding the primary nucleotide sequence into a text-based prompt. This approach allows the Transformer’s attention mechanism to implicitly learn the spatial dependencies of the RNA duplex. The results are striking: At a 15% threshold, ADAR-GPT achieved approximately 0.759 accuracy and an AUPRC of 0.801, significantly outperforming both traditional convolutional neural network (CNN) baselines like EditPredict (16) and general-purpose RNA foundation models like RNA-FM (17).

However, these models largely relied on manually engineered features such as local nucleotide composition and thermodynamic parameters, limiting their generalizability to new contexts.2.Deep Learning: EditPredict (16), DeepRed (19), and ATTIC (20) utilized deep neural network architectures to automate feature extraction. While capturing general RNA “grammar”, they often suffer from low specificity and frequent “overprediction” when applied to tissue-specific datasets.4.Generative and Specialized LLMs: ADAR-GPT combines generative pretraining with threshold-progressive continual fine-tuning. It achieves high specificity by implicitly learning structural rules and cis-regulatory motifs (e.g., upstream guanosine depletion) directly from the sequence.

Advances, Contributions, and Importance

Rosenwasser et al. mark a distinct leap forward. First, the CFT paradigm offers a blueprint for tuning AI to handle biological data with gradient (editing frequency: 1%, 5%, 10%, and 15%). Standard binary classification struggles to capture the subtle distinctions between different editing frequencies. By teaching the model to identify “high-signal” sites, the authors develop a tool that is more reliable for drug discovery and therapeutic design. Second, the study addresses the critical need for therapeutic selectivity. As ADARs are leveraged for RNA engineering where guide RNAs (gRNAs) are designed to reverse genomic mutations (23, 24), ADAR-GPT’s high specificity helps predict preferred targets and reduce off-target risks. Finally, this approach demonstrates that LLMs can implicitly learn higher-order structural dependencies directly from the primary sequence. This suggests that transformers could infer the complex interplay between sequence and structure, effectively bypassing the need for explicit structural representations.

Future Outlook and Limitations

While ADAR-GPT represents a state-of-the-art model, several challenges remain for future investigation.

Tissue and Paralog Expansion.

The current model is optimized for liver tissue, which is ADAR1-dominated. A critical next step will be extending this framework to other tissues, such as the brain, where ADAR2 and ADAR3 are abundantly expressed and regulate neurological processes. Future models must address paralog-specific prediction, ADAR co-expression dynamics, and the confounding influence of non-ADAR RNA-binding proteins (RBPs).

Incorporating Explicit Structure.

Uniquely, ADAR-GPT achieves high performance using only primary sequences, likely by inferring structural motifs (such as Alu inverted repeats) via attention mechanisms. However, the true ADAR-RNA interaction occurs in 3D space and is highly context-specific, where tertiary contacts, pseudoknots, and higher-order folding may influence substrate recognition. Integrating experimentally determined or computationally predicted structures into LLM frameworks represents an important frontier.

Biological Interpretability.

The “black box” of LLMs remains a challenge. Future research could extract the specific sequence and structural features that distinguish highly edited adenosines, for instance, upstream guanosine depletion, A-to-C mismatches, or bulges and internal loops. In addition, ADAR-GPT has learned patterns hidden within its attention weights. Addressing these questions could validate the model’s biological relevance and inform the rational design of guide RNAs for therapeutic applications.

From Prediction to Design.

Ultimately, ADAR-GPT paves the way for generative systems that move beyond classification to actively design optimal guide RNAs for site-directed RNA editing. Such tools would accelerate the development of precise, tissue-specific RNA-editing therapeutics, bringing us closer to an era of safely and efficiently rewriting the human transcriptome for clinical benefit.

Conclusion

In summary, ADAR-GPT represents a significant step toward cracking the “RNA editing code”. The model frames posttranscriptional modifications as a linguistic problem within the nucleotide sequence, applying a threshold-based curriculum. Rosenwasser et al. have provided a robust tool for both basic research and clinical intervention. As we move toward an era of precise, tissue-specific RNA editing therapeutics, such models will provide essential guides to human transcriptome engineering.
