Contributions of genetic liability and the COVID-19 pandemic to rising psychopathology among youth in the United States
Authors: Lucy Shao, Jonathan Ahern, Robert Loughnan, Bohan Xu, Holly E Baker, Susan F Tapert, Fiona C Baker, Wesley K Thompson, Orsolya Kiss, Eva M Müller-Oehring, Marie Gombert-Labedens, Chun Chieh Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdolescent mental health concerns surged during the COVID-19 pandemic, but whether the pandemic amplified pre-existing genetic vulnerabilities remains unclear. This study examined how shared genetic liability for eight psychiatric disorders interacted with adolescent mental health during the pandemic. Using genomic structural equation modeling, we identified four latent genetic factors: Compulsive, Psychotic, Neurodevelopmental, and Internalizing. We computed corresponding polygenic risk scores (PRS) in 10,374 participants from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study© across five waves and modeled on 20 psychopathology outcomes using longitudinal mixed models, stratified by sex and ancestry. Among European-ancestry adolescents, each 1 standard deviation increase in neurodevelopmental or internal PRS was associated with a 3–21% increase in most psychopathologies. Pandemic periods were linked to significant increases in parent-reported withdrawn/depressed, rule-breaking, conduct, somatic, and ADHD symptoms, and in all youth-reported symptom scores, even after adjusting for PRS. In sex-stratified analyses, females showed a greater increase in parent reported affective problems during the early pandemic (25% increase; 95% CI: 16–34%) and stronger associations between internal PRS and symptom increases than males. Interactions between neurodevelopmental PRS and the early pandemic period were observed for conduct (16%; 95% CI 9–25%; P = 1.2 × 10⁻⁵) and aggressive behavior (9%; 95% CI 4–15%; P = 6.8 × 10⁻⁴) scores in females. Overall, genetic liability and pandemic period were independently associated with adolescent psychopathology, with pandemic-related increases occurring on top of elevated symptom burden among youth with higher PRS. These findings highlight the need for targeted mental health support during widespread stressors, especially for genetically at-risk adolescents.Subject terms: Psychiatric disorders, Genetics

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic brought unprecedented challenges worldwide, significantly impacting mental health across various demographics. Among youth, the risk of having depression in the first few months of pandemic was reported to be three times higher than during the pre-pandemic time [1]. Global estimates showed that the prevalence of depression and anxiety among children and adolescents increased by 2-fold, from 11–12 percent to 20–21 percent in the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic [2]. Social isolation, disrupted routines, academic pressure, fear of infection, and economic strain may all contribute to the deterioration of mental health among young individuals [3–6].

While evidence of poor mental health outcomes among youth in the first year of COVID-19 is comprehensive, it is unclear if these effects held into the second year of the pandemic, when vaccines began to be rolled-out, lockdown efforts became uneven, infection-incidence waxed and waned, and youths’ routines adapted [7–10]. Such adaptations and changing socio-environmental challenges from the pandemic may have resulted in less depressive and anxiety symptoms among youth. Conversely, mental health problems may have lingered, as social disruptions may have had accumulative impacts regardless of the changing pandemic landscape.

Critically, the rise of mental health issues among some youth may have been driven by pre-existing vulnerabilities toward psychiatric disorders [11, 12]. Most of the discussion regarding groups sensitive to the unique challenges of COVID-19 pandemic has focused on socio-economic disadvantages as risk factors [13–15]. Genetic liability for mental health problems among youth have been largely ignored despite that psychiatric disorders have a considerable heritable component [13]. How COVID-19 pandemic challenges interacted with genetic pre-dispositions remain unclear. The rising prevalence of the mental health issues may have resulted from independent effects of pandemic related socio-environmental challenges and genetic liabilities, or it may have resulted from disruptions accelerating disease processes among youth at high risk due to genetic and environmental liabilities, resulting in higher incidences for these individuals. Here, polygenic risk scores were used as etiologic and stratification-oriented indices of broad inherited psychiatric liability. Their purpose was to test whether genetic liability helps explain heterogeneity in youth symptom burden and pandemic-period change at the group level; they were not intended for individual prediction, diagnosis, or treatment selection.

Using longitudinal data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study®, we examined whether adolescent psychopathology changed during the COVID-19 pandemic and whether these changes differed by genetic liability. We defined pandemic exposure using calendar periods (pre-pandemic, early pandemic [March–December 2020], late pandemic [January 2021–January 2022]). We tested four prespecified hypotheses: (H1) psychopathology scores increased in the early pandemic relative to pre-pandemic; (H2) psychopathology scores increased further in the late pandemic relative to the early pandemic, consistent with persistent or cumulative pandemic-related stressors; (H3) higher psychiatric polygenic liability was associated with higher psychopathology across follow-up; and (H4) pandemic-period associations differed by genetic liability (PRS × period interactions) and by sex-at-birth, given known sex differences in adolescent psychopathology trajectories. Here, we refer to “early-pandemic” changes as short-term disruptions coinciding with widespread lockdowns and school closures, and “late-pandemic” changes as persistent or cumulative effects occurring after prolonged disruption and adaptation.

To answer these questions, we utilized longitudinal data from a large-scale adolescent cohort, the Adolescent Brain Cognitive DevelopmentSM (ABCD) study. The COVID-19 pandemic happened during the longitudinal follow-up of ABCD Study© when the participants of age range from 10–14 at 2020. Given the sample size, interlacing visits with pandemics, and the availability of the genomic data, we can investigate associations and interactions of different mental health challenges faced by adolescents during the COVID-19 pandemic overtime.

Method

Study cohort

This study uses ABCD Study data release 5.0. The ABCD Study–the largest in the U.S. assessing longitudinal brain development–was initiated by the United States National Institutes of Health (NIH) in 2015. Starting aged 9–10 years, children are being followed annually with comprehensive measures, including both self- and parent/caregiver-completed questionnaires [16, 17]. More details about the ABCD Study can be found at http://abcdstudy.org.

The source population was all ABCD participants in Data Release 5.0 (N = 11,380 at baseline). For the present analysis, participants were eligible if they had at least one completed Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL) and Youth Brief Problem Monitor (BPM-Y) assessment at any of the five scheduled assessment waves spanning 2016–2022. This yielded N = 11,378 participants contributing at least one outcome observation. Of these, N = 10,374 had baseline covariates and genomics data available. The final analytic dataset included repeated observations across 5 waves, with 41923 total observations, with 2–4 observations pre-pandemic, 1–2 in early pandemic, and 1–2 in late pandemic. A visualization of data distribution by time is presented in Appendix Figure S1, and the participant flow chart is presented in Appendix Figure S2. For the longitudinal analyses, we included participants with at least one eligible CBCL or BPM-Y assessment across the five study waves; thus, the analytic sample was an unbalanced repeated-measures cohort rather than a complete-case panel.

Given that we use polygenic risk scores (PRSs) throughout our analyses in this report, which are known to have significant challenges of portability between ancestral groups [18], we stratified eligible individuals into two sub-cohorts, each analyzed independently. EUR is our primary study cohort since their genetic background is matched with the study populations of the genome-wide association studies (GWAS) which the PRS are based upon. DIV is our secondary analytic group because the increased inferential uncertainties given mismatched study populations between the GWAS training samples and the PRS application sample [19].

Child behavior checklist (CBCL)

The CBCL [20] is based on the Achenbach System of Empirically Based Assessment (ASEBA), which measures behavioral and emotional problems among children and adolescents. CBCL was completed by parents/caregivers [21]. We used raw summary scores of CBCL/BPM-Y instead of thresholded T-scores (shown in Figure S3 for clinical interpretability) given the consideration of the model reliability. Eight syndrome scales (anxious/depressed, withdrawn/depressed or depressed, somatic complaints, social problems, thought problems, attention problems, rule-breaking behavior, and aggressive behavior) and six DSM-oriented (Figure S4) scales (affective problems, anxiety problems, somatic problems, attention deficit/hyperactivity problems, oppositional defiant problems, and conduct problems) were included. CBCL DSM affective problems is specifically designed to assess symptoms related to depressive disorders, so we refer it as DSM depress in this paper. Two additional higher-order summary scores were derived based on syndrome scales and raw scores, i.e. internalizing and externalizing problem scores. We did not include CBCL Total Problems as an additional primary outcome because it is a total sum of all the other scores and lack of specificity. Therefore, in total, we examined 16 different CBCL scores as the main outcomes in this study.

The BPM forms are developed from the longer corresponding versions of the ASEBA Child Behavior Checklist for those aged 6–18 years. To evaluate the subjective well-being of ABCD Study participants, we also used self-rated Youth Brief Problem Monitor BPM-Y [20] questionnaire. It contains four summary measures, based on children’s self-report, including Total Problems, Internalizing Problems, Externalizing Problems, and Attention Problems(Figure S5). In total, we examined 4 different BPM-Y scores as outcomes in this study.

Classification of early pandemic and late pandemic period

To examine patterns of change over the course of the pandemic, we used January 2021 as the demarcation between “early” and “late” pandemic periods. We defined the early pandemic period as starting from March 1, 2020, when lock-down orders in the U.S. were announced, to December 31, 2020, when the first COVID-19 vaccines appeared on the horizon. The late-pandemic period was defined as an indicator for observations from January 1, 2021, through January 1, 2022, the final date available in the dataset, during which multiple outbreak waves were ongoing and COVID-19 social distancing measures were implemented unevenly across the U.S. The pre-pandemic period before March 2020 served as the reference category. The reference is the pre-pandemic state (before March 2020). These pandemic-period variables were defined as calendar-based indicators of the broader pandemic context and were intended to capture average differences in symptom levels by assessment period. They were not designed to measure SARS-CoV-2 infection, persistent post-COVID symptoms, local policy restrictions, or any other single mechanism.

Genotype data and analysis

Genetic information was retrieved from saliva and blood sources and assayed using the Smokescreen™ Genotyping array [22]. Using HRC reference panel with TOPMED imputation server [23, 24], the genotypes were imputed in 280,850,795 imputed variants, aligned to genome build GRCh38. Genetic principal components were calculated using PC-Air [25] with default settings. Using PLINK (v2) [26], we filtered the input data to include only autosomal variants with a minor allele frequency of 1% or greater, which left us with 10,927,293 variants. The discovery GWAS used to derive these latent factor PRSs were based predominantly on adult psychiatric phenotypes. Accordingly, the resulting scores should be interpreted as indices of broad inherited psychiatric liability rather than as child-specific genetic predictors of symptom presentation in early adolescence. The PRSs were derived from external GWAS summary statistics and applied to the independent ABCD target sample, so the analyses do not involve fitting and testing the same phenotype in the same dataset. Because the latent-factor PRSs were designed to capture broad transdiagnostic liability, we did not exclude component disorders that were phenotypically related to specific symptom outcomes. Thus, associations between a factor PRS and a related symptom domain should be interpreted as evidence of shared genetic liability rather than outcome-specific prediction.

Genetic ancestry calculation

Genetic continental ancestry was estimated using SNP weights [27] and external genomic reference panels for African, East Asian, European[28], and Indigenous North and South American[29] ancestry populations. ABCD participants were separated into continental ancestry groups if they had an inferred genetic ancestry of at least 80% consistent with a continental reference panel; if not, they were assessed as having admixed ancestry. Using inferred genetic ancestries allowed us to more precisely define admixture in the ABCD cohort; previous work has shown that ABCD inferred ancestries closely align with genetic principal component analysis [30]. The genetically derived ancestral categories were used to define the study subsets, i.e. EUR and DIV.

Latent factor polygenic scores for psychiatric disorders

To maintain a comprehensive characterization of psychopathological liabilities using genetic information while reducing the burden of multiple testing, we used the framework developed by Grotzinger et al. [31]. Using the structural equation model formulation implemented in Genomic SEM, we fit confirmatory factor models based on summary statistics of GWAS of 8 psychiatric disorders: attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) [32], anorexia nervosa (AN) [33], autism spectrum disorder (AUT) [34], anxiety disorders (ANX) [35], bipolar disorder (BIP) [36], major Depressive disorder (MDD) [37], obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) [38], and schizophrenia (SCZ) [39], The best fitting model (Comparative Fit Index (CFI) of 0.984) resulted in four latent factors: Compulsive disorders (COM), Psychotic disorders (PSY), Neurodevelopmental disorders (ND), and Internalizing disorders (INT). The first factor, COM consists of disorders characterized by compulsive behaviors AN and OCD. The second factor, PSY factor characterizes disorders that may had psychotic features SCZ, BIP, and MDD. The third factor, ND factor represents disorders with developmental origins, i.e. ADHD and AUT. The fourth factor, INT factor characterizes ANX and MDD, which have internalizing symptoms. For the present study, we used the 8-disorder Genomic SEM framework because it provided broad coverage of compulsive, psychotic, neurodevelopmental, and internalizing liability relevant to our hypothesis-driven analyses. The four-factor model structure and coefficients are shown in Fig. 1. These PRSs were therefore treated as research variables indexing broad transdiagnostic liability at the population level rather than as clinically actionable measures for individual participants.

Structural equation model illustrating the hierarchical relationships among latent psychopathology factors and Polygenic risk scores (PRSs) of psychiatric disorders.

Four higher-order latent factors—Compulsive, Psychotic, Neurodevelopmental, and Internalizing—are modeled with covariance/correlation between them, reflecting their interrelations. Each latent factor loads onto a subset of PRSs of psychiatric disorders: Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (OCD) and Anorexia Nervosa (AN) load onto the Compulsive Factor; Schizophrenia (SCZ) and Bipolar Disorder (BIP) onto the Psychotic Factor; Autism Spectrum Disorder (AUT) and Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) onto the Neurodevelopmental Factor; and Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) and Anxiety Disorders (ANX) onto the Internalizing Factor. Curved double-headed arrows represent correlations or covariances between latent variables and residuals.

After deriving four latent factors based on eight psychiatric disorders, we performed latent factor GWAS on the summary statistics to derive the coefficient weights for factor specific PRSs. GenomicSEM was used to estimate the mean effect sizes of SNPs across disorders, given the latent factor model we specified, resulting in the beta weights for a given SNPs for each of the latent factor. We then deployed those weights to the genetic data of ABCD, generating four PRS for each participant, i.e. COM PRS, PSY PRS, ND PRS, and INT PRS.

Statistical analysis

Generalized linear mixed-effects models (GLMMs) were fitted with negative binomial link functions using the R package NBZIMM [40]. These mixed-effects models used all available repeated outcome observations from participants with at least one eligible assessment and therefore did not require complete data at all five time points. Participants contributed data to the estimation for whichever waves they completed. The raw scores of our outcomes (CBCL and BPM-Y) ranged from 0–64 and were zero-inflated; distributions of the scores are presented in Appendix Figure S3, S4 and S5; clinical cutoffs are based on t-scores. Therefore, we used a negative binomial linear mixed effects model with a log link function to model these as dependent variables. Repeated measures over time were modeled using random effects of participant and site ID nested, while the independent variables of interest and potential confounding variables were included as fix effects. The independent variables of interest were: 1) indicators for pandemic states (early or late), the reference group is the pre-pandemic state, and 2) the four genetic liability scores (COM PRS, PSY PRS, ND PRS, and INT PRS). The corresponding interactions between pandemic state and genetic liabilities were also included as fixed effects. Other covariates controlled in the fixed effect are baseline collection date, sex-at-birth, age at assessment, household income level, parents’ marriage status, and first ten genetic principal components (PCs) derived from GWAS data with the random effect to be site ID nested within ID. Given the staggered visit timing and the wide age range at pandemic onset (approximately 10–14 years in March 2020), there were substantial between person variations in the age-at-visit and age-at-pandemic. We therefore adjusted for time-varying age at assessment and evaluated sensitivity to nonlinear age trends and age × period interactions to control the natural developmental trend of CBCL and BPM-Y scores, as youth gets older. Note that age at assessment was included as a time-varying covariate to account for normative developmental change. Because visits were staggered and participants spanned a broad age range at pandemic onset, ages overlapped across calendar periods; therefore, pandemic-period coefficients were interpreted as age-adjusted associations with assessment period, rather than as effects fully separable from developmental stage. All PRSs and age are scaled and centered. Further stratified analysis on male and female were conducted on the European-like sample. Figure S7 presented the hypothesized causal relationships among pandemic period, PRS, baseline covariates, and outcomes, as a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG), clarifying which variables were included for adjustment versus evaluated as potential effect modifiers.

To improve the interpretability of the results given the negative binomial mixed-effects models, we report the association of the independent variable on the dependent variable via the rate ratio (RR). The RR quantifies the fold change in the rates of the outcome, given one-unit increase in the predictor. The corresponding 95% confidence intervals were calculated for RR and the significant thresholds for hypothesis testing are set at the 0.05 level after Bonferroni correction of 20 tests.

To assess sensitivity for PRS × pandemic interactions, we conducted a post hoc minimum detectable effect analysis based on the fitted negative-binomial mixed models. Calculations were performed on the log-rate-ratio scale using the Wald standard error of each interaction coefficient, with 80% power and a two-sided Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold of α = 0.0025α = 0.0025 across 20 outcomes. When needed, the standard error was back-calculated from the reported 95% confidence interval under the Wald approximation.

Trajectories of mental health during the COVID-19 pandemic

Figure 2 summarize the full pandemic by PRS associations with psychopathologies among youth, after controlling for age-at-visit as time-varying covariates, sex, family memberships, and genetic PCs, among participants whose genetic ancestries are closer to continental Europeans (EUR set). Because symptom trajectories change with development, all models adjusted for age at assessment as a time-varying covariate. Given overlap in age across pre-pandemic and pandemic assessments, the early- and late-pandemic coefficients should be interpreted as age-adjusted differences in symptom levels by assessment period. During the early pandemic, a broad range of youth psychopathologies significantly increased, with CBCL and BPM-Y scores rising by approximately 4–14% (Fig. 2). During the late pandemic, symptom levels remained elevated (ranging from 6–24% above early-pandemic levels) relative to the pre-pandemic period and, for several outcomes, were higher than in the early pandemic period, indicating persistent symptom burden across later pandemic assessments. The most evident late-pandemic increases were CBCL withdrawn/depressive symptoms (17% increase, 95% CI 10–24%, p = 2.9 × 10⁻7), CBCL rule-breaking behavior (21% increase, 95% CI 14–29%, p = 1.1 × 10⁻9), and self-reported BPM-Y internalizing problems (24% increase, 95% CI 18–30%, p = 1.4 × 10⁻19).

Associations with pre-disposing vulnerabilities among youth

We tested if our latent factor PRSs were significantly associated with mental health among youth and if the pandemic related worsening of psychopathologies were further accelerated among those who were vulnerable (Fig. 2). The COM PRS was negatively associated with CBCL rule-breaking problems (6% decrease, 95% CI 2–9% decrease, P = 3.133 × 10⁻3). The PSY PRS was significantly associated with BPM-Y internalizing problems (8% increase, 95% CI 4–12%, P = 7.7 × 10⁻5). The ND PRS was significantly associated with most of the psychopathologies except for the two-anxiety measures, with 5–21 percent more symptoms for every one standard deviation increases of the ND PRS. The INT PRS was significantly associated with all psychopathologies with 3–12% increment.

No significant multiplicative interactions were found between PRS factors and either pandemic period, after Bonferroni correction (all interaction terms: rate ratios ranging from 0.95–1.05, P > 0.05). Limited evidence for the multiplicative interactions suggests that pandemic effects on youth’s mental health were compounded uniformly across groups, regardless of their pre-existing vulnerabilities. In our post hoc sensitivity analysis, the female EUR models had 80% power at the Bonferroni-corrected threshold to detect interaction effects of approximately RR = 1.14 for conduct and RR = 1.104 for aggressive behavior. The observed conduct interaction (RR = 1.16, 95% CI 1.09–1.25) exceeded this threshold, whereas the aggressive-behavior interaction (RR = 1.09, 95% CI 1.04–1.15) was close to the study’s detection boundary.

Sex differences in mental health trajectories

We examined the difference in psychopathological changes during the pandemic between male and female participants by sex-stratified analyses. Figure 3 is the summary of the results, separated into domains of psychopathologies. Across psychopathologies, pandemics have stronger associations with the increasing rates of psychopathologies among females, as evident in the rightward shift of the effects in Fig. 3 (also see Appendix Figure S6). Depressive symptoms (CBCL DSM Depress) were higher (Fig. 3a) among female participants during the early pandemic state (25% increase, 95%CI 16–34%, P = 1.1 × 10⁻8), compared to males’ non-significant effect size (1.04, 95% CI 0.97–1.12, P > 0.05). The observed sex differences were even more prominent in late pandemic period. During the late pandemic, female participants had higher increment of CBCL Withdraw Depression (22% increase, 95% CI 12–33%, P = 5.5 × 10⁻6) and CBCL Rule-breaking (31% increase, 95% CI 19–44%, P = 4.7 × 10⁻8). The association of the late pandemic and self-reported BPM-Y scores of female participants also generally exhibited larger effect size than in the analogous male’s models. In contrast, the increase in symptoms among males during early pandemic period was diminished during the late pandemic state. Some mental health issues among males were even reported significantly less by parents than pre-pandemic period (Fig. 3).

Using the female EUR-stratified models (n = 3020) as a conservative benchmark for interaction sensitivity, the Bonferroni-corrected 80% minimum detectable interaction effects were approximately RR = 1.14 for the Early Pandemic × ND PRS association with conduct problems and RR = 1.10. Because the primary EUR analyses were conducted in the larger full EUR sample (n = 6349), these female-stratified estimates provide conservative reference points. Discussion

In this study, we found that the associations of the pandemic with the mental wellbeing of youth was persistent and lingering. In adolescent females, the associations were found stronger and longer lasting than in adolescent males. Genetic liabilities for neurodevelopmental and internalizing disorders were associated with increased levels of psychopathology among youth. Although no significant interaction was found, the non-linearity of the negative binomial model implies compounding effects of pandemic exposure and genetic risk by default.

Longitudinal studies have consistently demonstrated that mental health problems originating in childhood can persist and manifest as more severe disorders in adulthood [41]. In our analysis, we also found that age at collection time had significant positive associations with almost all psychopathologies. Notably, participants’ ages ranged from 10–14 years old in the year 2020. We adjusted for age at assessment to account for normative developmental change. Pandemic period is defined by calendar time, but the probability of contributing an assessment during each period may vary because of scheduling and attrition; therefore, we report follow-up patterns and conduct non-response analyses to evaluate potential selection bias. Because psychopathology levels were evidently varied during adolescence, this adjustment ensures that observed differences during pandemic are not simply due to participants getting older. Note that such adjustment reduce, but did not eliminate, confounding between developmental change and calendar time; accordingly, pandemic-period coefficients should be interpreted as age-adjusted associations with assessment period.

This design allowed us to compare symptom levels across pre-pandemic, early-pandemic, and later-pandemic assessment windows while adjusting for age and other covariates. The observed pattern was consistent with sustained elevation in several symptom domains across later pandemic assessments. We want to note that our pandemic-period estimates should be interpreted as associations with assessment timing during broader pandemic phases rather than as evidence for any single underlying mechanism. These period indicators may reflect multiple concurrent influences, including social disruption, family stress, changes in schooling and peer contact, infection-related experiences, and other contextual factors. While early-pandemic increases were substantial, the continued rise in symptoms during the late-pandemic period, particularly in withdrawn/depressive symptoms, rule-breaking behavior, and internalizing problems, points to a potential cumulative burden of prolonged stress and disruption.

Higher ND and INT PRS were associated with elevated symptom burden across follow-up. However, because primary PRS × pandemic interactions were not significant after correction, these findings do not support broad genetic moderation of pandemic-period symptom changes. Higher polygenic risk for neurodevelopmental (ND) and internalizing (INT) disorders was broadly associated with elevated symptom levels, suggesting that youth with these genetic predispositions may be especially sensitive to environmental stressors such as the pandemic. Notably, the PSY PRS was linked to greater internalizing problems, while the COM PRS was unexpectedly associated with lower rule-breaking behavior, raising questions about potential resilience or compensatory mechanisms within certain risk profiles.

In the primary EUR models, PRS × pandemic interaction terms were not significant after Bonferroni correction, whereas sex-stratified analyses identified two female-specific early-pandemic interactions involving ND PRS. This pattern should be interpreted cautiously because interaction analyses are less powered than main-effect analyses, particularly given the modest predictive ability of current PRS and the multiple-testing burden. Given our post-hoc sensitivity analysis on the detection limits of multiplicative interactions, our data argue against broad moderate-to-large PRS × pandemic interactions across outcomes. At the same time, the positive main effects of ND and INT PRS indicate that genetic vulnerability was associated with greater symptom burden across follow-up. genetic vulnerability compounded the mental health concerns during the COVID-19 pandemic. An example is given in Figure S8; just considering the main effects, the increments in BPM-Y Internalizing problem scores during the pandemic for those with 2 standard deviation higher ND PRS is 70% higher for those with 2 standard deviation lower ND PRS. In a multiplicative model, similar pandemic-related rate ratios can still translate into larger absolute symptom increases among youth with higher baseline symptom levels; this should not be interpreted as statistical interaction, but it helps explain why adolescents with greater genetic liability may still experience greater absolute burden during periods of stress. These findings highlight the importance of considering both genetic and environmental factors to better identify and support at-risk youth.

Loades et al. [4] found that the absence of social connections during adolescence can hinder emotional development and exacerbate mental health vulnerabilities. Their review suggests that loneliness is linked to increased depression in girls and social anxiety in boys, with girls being particularly sensitive to perceived social exclusion which can lead to lasting effects on well-being [42]. Our findings align with this literature: during the COVID-19 pandemic, adolescent females experienced a sustained decline in mental health, which may reflect multiple pandemic-related influences, including social disruption, changes in daily routines, family stress, and possibly infection-related factors. In contrast, mental health concerns among adolescent males appeared to stabilize during the later phases of the pandemic. These findings underscore the need for sex-specific approaches to mental health prevention and care. Additionally, our study shows that individuals with genetic risk for neurodevelopmental disorders remain especially vulnerable to varies mental problems, even outside of pandemic conditions.

There were limitations in our study. The lack of specific measurements on COVID-19 related social disruptions makes it difficult to fully understand the various ways in which the pandemic affected adolescents’ mental health. Generalized data often miss the complexity of disruptions such as school closures, changes in peer interactions, and the shift to virtual communication, which may have impacted youth differently. Without more targeted measures that account for these factors, it becomes challenging to assess the direct relationship between social disruption and mental health outcomes, hindering effective interventions. We could not separate infection-related effects, including possible long COVID, from broader pandemic-related social disruption because harmonized measures of infection history and persistent post-COVID symptoms were unavailable. Nevertheless, the longitudinal multi-informant design and age-adjusted repeated-measures models provided a strong framework for detecting broad pandemic-related changes in youth psychopathology, even though developmental stage and calendar time could not be fully disentangled.

Limited data for individuals of non-European-like ancestry introduces uncertainty in the findings for these groups. In addition, associations involving child PRS may partly reflect family-level pathways, including parental genetic liability and correlated family environment, and therefore should not be interpreted as isolating child-specific biological effects. PRSs derived mainly from adult GWAS may have limited developmental specificity for early-adolescent symptoms, and unmodeled factors such as medication use, parental psychopathology, and family-level pathways may have contributed to residual confounding. Sex-stratified findings were based on sex at birth and should not be interpreted as isolating pubertal or gender-related effects. Our PRS analyses were anchored to an 8-disorder Genomic SEM framework; while suitable for indexing broad transdiagnostic liability, newer 14-disorder models may capture additional dimensions of shared psychiatric risk.

During school closures and remote learning, caregivers may have had more direct opportunity to observe children’s behavior and learning difficulties than under typical school-based routines. Although the CBCL may under capture internal experiences and the BPM-Y may be influenced by self-report bias, using both parent- and youth-reported measures across multiple waves strengthened our ability to capture complementary and evolving patterns of youth psychopathology over time. The mixed-effects models used all available repeated observations from participants with at least one eligible assessment which preserves sample size, but it does not remove the possibility that missingness or attrition related to unmeasured factors influenced the observed associations.

Conclusion

Adolescent mental health worsened over the course of the COVID-19 pandemic, showing cumulative increases in symptom burden, and this study uniquely examined these associations using comprehensive measures, including psychopathology as assessed by the CBCL and BPM-Y, while adjusting for multiple genetic factors and for age at assessment as a time-varying covariate to mitigate confounding by developmental change. Our results highlight the importance of early intervention and sustained, individualized support for at-risk youth. More broadly, these insights call for a shift in how public health policies address mental health, especially during crises. Beyond medical or genetic vulnerabilities, social support, community connection, and equitable access to mental health resources must be prioritized. Future public health strategies should integrate these social dimensions to better protect the mental well-being of vulnerable populations, particularly in times of widespread disruption.
