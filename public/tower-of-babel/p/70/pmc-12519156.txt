Increasing postpartum family planning using an evidence-based approach: the success of a university teaching hospital in Rwanda
Authors: Alice Mukansanga, Mary Bernadette Hadley, Jean Baptiste Ukwizangira
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•The implementation of the hospital recommendations increased the uptake of postpartum family planning (PPFP) from 26% (April 2022) to 89% (March 2025).•The post-partum family planning PPFP uptake in hospitals in Rwanda can be increased through the implementation of the five key recommendations.Key words: health facility, postpartum family planning, Rwanda

AJOG Global Reports at a Glance

Why was this study conducted?

Postpartum family planning (PPFP) was only used by 42% of all women giving birth in a referral hospital in Rwanda. This study aimed to establish an evidence-based intervention to increase the PPFP rate that could be transferable to other African settings.

Key findings

The implementation of 4 recommendations, generated by the study, from district hospitals and mothers who delivered at the hospital increased the PPFP rate to 89% at no additional cost. The recommendations were as follows: (1) a higher importance attached to ensuring that all women were offered family planning after delivery in the hospital as a whole and (2) the service being provided by all midwives.

What does this add to what is known?

Our study provides a practical, affordable way forward to increase the PPFP uptake rates. The results are transferable to other health facilities in Africa.

Introduction

Reducing the unmet need for family planning is the most cost-effective intervention to reduce maternal deaths.1 Moreover, it is the cornerstone in harnessing the demographic dividend,2 as described in the Rwanda National Strategy for Transformation 1.3 In Rwanda, an investment case was made for family planning in 2019.4 The report predicted that a saving of $331 million in maternal and infant healthcare costs would be made by 2023 for $144 million spent on family planning. In terms of maternal and child (<5 years) mortalities, the reduction by 2050 was estimated to be from 210 to 40 per 100,000 live births and from 50 to 27 per 1000 live births, respectively. However, the 2019 Demographic and Health Surveys reported that the unmet need for family planning was 19% among women of reproductive age.5

To address this deficit, the Rwanda Ministry of Health introduced postpartum family planning (PPFP) as a strategy in 2017. Health facility delivery rates are high in the country, at more than 90%,6 thus providing the opportunity to offer family planning to women before discharge after delivery at one of the health facilities. The concept is based on the premise that (1) birth spacing is recommended for the health of the mother and child and (b) mothers can be provided with effective family planning before being discharged home by the nursing staff employed at the hospital, as 97% of all deliveries occur in the health facility.

The University Teaching Hospital of Butare (CHUB) is a teaching hospital located in the south of Rwanda. It provides delivery services to approximately 200 women each month and offers 16 family planning methods. Although the policy is to counsel all mothers giving birth in the hospital before being discharged home, the PPFP uptake in the hospital was only 45% of eligible women in 2022, compared with more than 60% of women in district hospitals situated in similar environments. In May 2022, the hospital embarked on a research project with the objective of improving maternal and neonatal outcomes through evidence-based interventions. This study on PPFP uptake was embedded in a broader research project. The approach used for the research was for existing hospital staff to conduct their studies to inform interventions with the aim of improving maternal and neonatal outcomes in their area of work. Technical assistance was provided to each staff member for study design, data analysis, and reporting. This study aimed to increase the uptake of PPFP at the hospital with interventions that can be transferred to other hospitals in Rwanda and the region.

Materials and methods

This was a mixed-methods cohort study with both quantitative and qualitative elements. Tariq and Woodman7 found that a combination of methods resulted in a more in-depth understanding of the “why” to be applied to the “what.” The study was composed of two distinct components: (1) interviews with family planning focal persons at district hospitals and (2) interviews with mothers before discharge. The PPFP rates were extracted from the Health Management Information System before the study was conducted and up to March 2025 to demonstrate the sustainability of the interventions introduced, which were based on the recommendations derived from the study.

All district hospitals within the hospital’s catchment area that reported PPFP uptake rates of more than 60% in 2021 were included in the first component. For the second component, mothers’ interviews, systematic sampling was used to reduce selection bias. Every fourth mother who had a normal delivery and every fourth mother who had a cesarean delivery in June 2022 were selected. There are two separate registers at CHUB, one for spontaneous vaginal and assisted deliveries performed in the maternity ward and one for cesarean deliveries performed in the operating theater. Numbering of clients starts at the first of each month, allowing for easy selection of every fourth woman giving birth.

The design of the interview guide for district hospitals included open-ended questions to elicit original responses with minimal interviewer bias. The use of open-ended questions for this purpose is well documented.8 The authors found that the use of open-ended questions minimized interviewer bias and allowed respondents to use their words and experiences without being influenced by the preconceived perceptions of the researchers. Basic information was collected, such as the type of hospital and when PPFP was introduced. These questions were followed by 5 open-ended questions related to the process followed at the hospital, how high PPFP rates were achieved, and recommendations for CHUB to increase PPFP uptake.

The design of the mother’s interview guides followed the same reasoning. There were 3 interview guides for mothers: all mothers, mothers who accepted family planning, and mothers who did not receive family planning. Single-word responses were included to allow for quantitative data analysis, and open-ended questions were used to obtain more in-depth information. Basic demographic data, reproductive health history, and information related to the recent delivery were collected from all mothers. In addition, the family planning history and experience of being offered family planning after delivery were requested. For mothers who accepted family planning, questions were posed on the process of obtaining the family planning methods in the hospital, including financial costs and the decision-making process. The mothers who did not receive family planning were questioned about the decision-making process. At the end of the interview, the mothers were asked for recommendations to ensure that more women accept family planning after delivery.

The study was conducted during a time when travel restrictions were in place due to the COVID-19 pandemic in Rwanda. Interviews with family planning focal persons at district hospitals were conducted by telephone. Interviews with mothers were conducted face-to-face in the hospital, either before discharge or during postnatal care visits. All interviews were conducted by the focal person for family planning in the hospital after obtaining written informed consent.

The data obtained from mothers’ interviews were entered into Microsoft Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA) and analyzed using frequency tables. The independent variables were “accepted” or “refused” family planning, with dependent variables being day of delivery, Ubedehe (socioeconomic) category, parity and gravida, and age group. Introduced in 2000, Ubedehe is a movement in Rwanda that aims to combat poverty through projects involving community participation. Each Rwandan citizen was allocated a category based on their socioeconomic status. In addition, social protection is available based on this category. The chi-square and Fisher exact tests were used. Analysis of the responses to open-ended questions was performed using inductive reasoning.9

The study obtained ethical approval from the National Ethics Committee in May 2022. Digitally recorded verbal consent was obtained from family planning focal persons at district hospitals. Written informed consent was obtained from all interviewed mothers. Names were not included in the questionnaires.

Results

The results were presented from interviews of district hospital focal persons by theme. This was followed by an analysis of the quantitative data from the mothers’ interviews, showing the frequency of variables for mothers who accepted family planning and mothers who did not receive family planning after delivery. The qualitative data from the mothers’ interviews were presented by theme to demonstrate the reasons why women did not receive family planning after delivery, which were addressed by the family planning program. Finally, the effect of the study was presented in terms of PPFP uptake at CHUB.

Results from district hospital interviews

The selected district hospitals, which had achieved more than 60% PPFP uptake, were as follows: Kaduha, Munini, Kigeme, Kabutare, Kibilizi, Nyanza, Gitwe, and Kinazi. Background information indicated that each hospital started providing PPFP at the same time (February 2018). The hospitals reported that all midwives provide PPFP, but the overall person responsible for PPFP is the focal person (nurse). Mothers are counseled on family planning during each antenatal visit, and a family planning method is selected by mothers and written into the antenatal card at the last visit before delivery. Family planning methods are free of charge. However, there are minimal charges for certain supplies.

The key themes that emerged related to reaching high PPFP uptake were counseling at prenatal clinics, teamwork, and PPFP engagement/emphasis within the maternity unit/hospital:

Results of the mothers’ interviews (quantitative)

The quantitative data from the mothers’ interviews are presented in 3 sections: Delivery and previous obstetrical history, demographic characteristics, and family planning history. The key findings are shown in the figures attached to each section.

Delivery and previous obstetrical history

Of the 41 mothers interviewed, 30 had accepted PPFP, and 10 did not receive any family planning method. This could be because they were not offered any method or because they were offered but refused. Of all mothers, 27 underwent cesarean delivery. Most women who underwent cesarean delivery accepted family planning (90%) compared with 62% of women who underwent vaginal delivery (P=.04) (Figure 1).

All women with more than five children accepted family planning, except 1 woman who had 9 children. The lowest percentage of acceptance (63%) was in the category with two previous children. There were nine women (23%) who delivered preterm infants, of whom 7 (77%) accepted family planning. The percentage of women who delivered at term and accepted family planning was 70%. The results were not statistically significant because of the small sample size.

Demographic characteristics

There was a difference between mothers accepting family planning and those not receiving family planning in different age groups. The 39- to 43-year age group showed the largest difference, with all mothers accepting family planning (Figure 2).

Family planning history

A higher proportion of mothers who had previous family planning (86%) accepted family planning than those who had never used family planning (61%). The results were not statistically significant because of the small sample size. Most women (90%) had considered family planning methods during the pregnancy. Most women who had discussed family planning during pregnancy accepted family planning, whereas just over half of those who had not discussed family planning during pregnancy accepted family planning (P=.00) (Figure 3). Moreover, those who had discussed with their partners (93%) had a 76% acceptance rate compared with 66% (n=2) mothers of those who did not discuss family planning with their partners.

Social economic status

In Rwanda, all adults aged >18 years had been assigned a socioeconomic category known as “Ubedehe.” Adults who had been classified in the lowest socioeconomic status category were in category 1, whereas those who had been classified in the highest socioeconomic status were in category 3. Women who were classified into Ubedehe category 1 (the lowest economic status) comprised 10% of women, of whom only 50% accepted PPFP. Women who were classified into Ubedehe categories 2 and 3 comprised 33% and 58% of women, of whom 85% and 74% accepted family planning, respectively. These results were not statistically significant (P=.04) (Figure 4).

Results of the mothers’ interviews (qualitative)

Improvements in the uptake of PPFP services necessitate an understanding of not only of the characteristics of women involved but also the reasons for not accepting family planning. In this section, the results of the open-ended questions are presented, showing the key themes.

Key themes

The identified key themes included both barriers and facilitators: counseling/information, myths/religion/cultural factors, experience, and weekend provision (Figure 5).

The role of counseling featured as both a key facilitator to women accepting family planning and the main recommendation of women to those who had accepted family planning. The women were “convinced” of the benefit of family planning immediately after delivery by the family planning focal person at the hospital. The health of the mother and the neonate were the most convincing arguments cited, along with the economic benefits of child spacing to the family as a whole. Recommendations of women who had accepted family planning compared with those who had not included information on both these points.

The good thing was that they explained to both me and my partner the reasons for taking family planning and all the different methods. Then we chose one together. (Mother who accepted family planning)

Discussing family planning during prenatal consultations was cited as a facilitator. This was particularly relevant when the partner was included in the discussion. However, although some women would not make a decision in the absence of their partner, others accepted family planning even when their partner showed reluctance.

After being given information I told my husband. He refused for me to take. I took the decision to take it even though he does not want because my health is important. (Mother who accepted family planning)

Personal experience was added to the information received during the prenatal and postpartum periods. The side effects of the different family planning methods were mentioned as a barrier to accepting contraceptive methods after delivery. However, many of these side effects are not backed up by biomedical evidence. Therefore, this theme overlaps with the myths/religion/culture theme.

They are afraid if they take family planning their breast milk will become like water. (Mother who accepted family planning)

Almost half of all Rwandans self-reported being Catholic in the 2012 census.10 It was not surprising that religious beliefs were cited as a barrier to accepting family planning after delivery. The need for a priest to advise a woman on whether she can use contraceptives was mentioned.

If I do take family planning God will punish me. God is the one who gives the children. The Bible says women should continue giving birth until the world is full. (Mother who refused family planning)

Cultural factors also play a role. In the event that the outcome of pregnancy resulted in the death of a neonate or premature delivery or if conception took longer than expected, the use of a contraceptive after delivery was not considered safe. Furthermore, family planning before the first menstruation after delivery or if a woman delivered a girl but wanted a boy was not considered appropriate. In addition, it was noted that contraception is relatively new. Therefore, mothers-in-law advised women against using contraception after delivery.

I was accompanied by my mother in law, she can’t accept my uptake of Family Planning methods because in our clan a woman is there for producing babies. (Mother who did not receive family planning)

In addition, knowledge and beliefs, such as that the woman would not be able to conceive again or that she is “too weak” after delivery to use family planning, were reported.

Service on the weekends

A more practical barrier to accepting family planning methods after delivery was linked to service provision. At the time of the study, there was 1 nurse (first author) responsible for counseling women for family planning and providing methods to them. As this nurse was not on duty on the weekends, if a mother was discharged from the maternity ward on a Saturday or Sunday, family planning was not offered by the nurses and midwives on duty at the time.

Postpartum family planning uptake following the study

There was a marked increase in PPFP uptake at CHUB in response to the study findings. This was primarily due to a heightened focus on PPFP and the appointment of a second family planning nurse, particularly to cover for the times when the PPFP focal person was not available. The increase was from 26% in April 2022 to 89% in March 2025 (Figure 6). The 2 low points, reaching only 21% in August 2021 and April 2022 (before recommendations were initiated), coincided with the absence of the family planning nurse due to training or annual leave.

Discussion

The study did not employ a recognized theoretical framework, which must be recognized as a limitation. The principle used for the overall research project was that using evidence to design and monitor context-specific initiatives would have more effect on improving maternal and neonatal outcomes than the introduction of standard “one-size-fits-all” interventions.

The results of this study must be interpreted within the limitations of the sample size (41 mothers; 6 district hospitals) and in the context in which it was conducted. However, the study findings provided direction to the hospital on how to increase PPFP and information to district health teams to address the cultural factors that limit the uptake of family planning after delivery. The recommendations from the high-performing district hospitals allowed changes to be made within resources, leading to an increase in women leaving the hospital with methods to support child spacing or the end of childbearing.

In a comparison of the findings of this study with external literature, a systematic review of the effect of birth spacing after cesarean delivery conducted by Ye et al11 found that an interval of longer than 18 months was related to decreased risk of maternal morbidity. Here, a higher proportion of women accepted family planning after a cesarean delivery. The implications are that the provision of PPFP in CHUB will further decrease maternal morbidity.

In addition, in a study conducted in Ethiopia evaluating the effect of family planning counseling during prenatal service, the authors found a significant effect on PPFP uptake.12 Here, acceptance of PPFP was higher in women who had been counseled during prenatal clinics (antenatal care) than in those who had not. This finding highlights the importance of family planning counseling during pregnancy at all health centers in Rwanda, which is already included in the Rwanda Antenatal Care Protocol.

In reference to Rwanda-specific literature, the use of modern family planning methods in Rwanda is fairly equal within the 5 wealth quintiles used in the Demographic Health Survey (National Institute of Statistics, Rwand and ICF 2022). Of note, 60% of individuals in the lowest wealth quintile used a modern family planning method compared with 58% of individuals in the highest wealth quintile. However, here, only half of the women in the lowest Ubedehe category accepted PPFP, although the number of respondents was small, and the results were not statistically significant. Introduced in 2000, Ubedehe is a movement in Rwanda that aims to combat poverty through projects involving community participation. Every Rwandan citizen is allocated a category based on their socioeconomic status, and social protection is available based on this category. The authors recommend further research to better understand the difference between the 2 studies.

Conclusion

This study aimed to increase PPFP uptake through a better understanding of the practices in high-performing district hospitals and the barriers and facilitators to women who have recently delivered in the hospital. The findings, supported by existing literature, highlight the importance of family planning counseling during pregnancy, particularly for individuals classified into Ubedehe category 1. Within the hospital setting, the key factors to improve PPFP uptake were increased awareness of the importance of PPFP among staff, teamwork, and involvement of all midwives in this service. The PPFP rates increased to a monthly average of >85%. The authors recommend that all hospitals with a PPFP uptake of <60% should incorporate this approach.
