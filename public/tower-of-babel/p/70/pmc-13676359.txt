Clinical Factors Associated with Implant Removal Following Surgical Site Infection after Spinal Instrumentation Surgery for Lumbar Degenerative Disease
Authors: Kazumasa Konishi, Kaito Jinnai, Yuhei Takamizawa, Kenichiro Yamagishi, Yosuke Kawano, Masaichi Hasegawa, Shunsuke Sato, Atsushi Hasegawa, Masaaki Tsuji, Takehiko Moroi, Takumi Takeuchi, Hideto Sano, Masahito Takahashi, Naobumi Hosogane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction Surgical site infection (SSI) is a serious complication of spinal instrumentation surgery that often necessitates implant removal due to biofilm formation. Implant removal adversely affects postoperative outcomes, making implant retention preferable. Although several risk factors for SSI have been reported, those specifically linked to implant removal in lumbar degenerative disease remain insufficiently examined. The purpose of this study was to examine factors associated with implant removal in patients who developed SSI after spinal instrumentation surgery for lumbar degenerative disease. Methods Among 4,743 patients who underwent spinal instrumentation surgery for lumbar degenerative disease between 2013 and 2021 at our institution and affiliated centers, 73 developed SSI. After excluding cases with missing data, 50 patients were retrospectively analyzed. They were categorized into an implant removal group (R group, n=15) and an implant retention group (N group, n=35). Patient characteristics, laboratory findings, surgical factors, and postoperative factors were compared. Univariate and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were performed to examine factors of implant removal. Results The implant removal rate was 30%. Univariate analysis showed associations with the number of previous surgeries at the same level (p=0.004), operative time (p=0.03), interval from surgery to SSI onset (p=0.03), and interval from SSI onset to the first surgical intervention (p=0.03). ROC analysis demonstrated cutoff values of 205 minutes for operative time (area under the curve [AUC]=0.692), 42 days from surgery to SSI onset (AUC=0.702), and 22 days from SSI onset to the first surgical intervention (AUC=0.697). Conclusions Implant removal after SSI in lumbar degenerative disease was associated with a history of surgery at the same level, prolonged operative time, and delayed SSI onset. Early surgical intervention after SSI onset was associated with implant retention. Keywords: lumbar vertebrae, surgical wound infection, orthopedic procedures / adverse effects, risk factors, spinal fusion

Introduction

The number of spinal instrumentation surgeries has markedly increased in recent years owing to advances in surgical techniques and perioperative management. These advances have expanded the feasibility of spinal instrumentation surgery for older and high-risk patients. However, this increase has been accompanied by growing concern about postoperative complications, particularly surgical site infection (SSI). SSI is a serious complication of spinal instrumentation surgery that not only adversely affects functional outcomes but also prolongs hospital stay, increases medical costs, and can be fatal in some cases1,2). The reported incidence of SSI in spinal instrumentation surgery ranges from 2.6% to 11.9%3,4), and several risk factors have been identified5-7). Because of biofilm formation on the implant, SSI can be refractory and may require implant removal for infection control. However, implant removal can significantly compromise postoperative outcomes; therefore, preserving the implant during infection management is preferable.

Understanding the clinical circumstances associated with implant removal during the treatment of SSI may help contextualize challenges encountered in infection management and postoperative care. To date, however, only a limited number of studies have examined factors associated with implant removal following SSI8-10). Most of these studies included heterogeneous patient populations and surgical approaches, potentially introducing multiple confounding factors. Therefore, investigations focusing on specific diseases and surgical approaches are warranted. Because of the relatively low incidence of SSI, even large-scale retrospective studies may not accumulate a sufficient number of cases to draw robust conclusions. Moreover, because SSI develops postoperatively, prospective interventional designs to clarify management-related factors are often impractical.

Although these issues cannot be entirely resolved, disease-specific retrospective observational studies may still provide valuable insights into the clinical course and management-related factors associated with implant removal after SSI. Therefore, in this study, we retrospectively analyzed cases of SSI following spinal instrumentation surgery for lumbar degenerative disease to examine clinical factors associated with removal of the spinal instrumentation used in the index surgery.

Materials and Methods

Ethics statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the participating centers. Informed consent was obtained through an opt-out process, and the requirement for written informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study.

Study design

This retrospective multicenter cohort study analyzed 4,743 patients with lumbar degenerative disease who underwent spinal instrumentation surgery at participating centers between 2013 and 2021. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were based on those of the Japanese Spinal Instrumentation Society Database. The inclusion criteria were spinal surgery involving implants for intervertebral fusion or spinal osteosynthesis.

The exclusion criteria were vertebroplasty alone, cervical laminoplasty alone, and fractures of the sacrum, coccyx, or pelvis. Lumbar degenerative diseases included lumbar spinal stenosis, lumbar disc herniation, lumbar isthmic spondylolisthesis, lumbar foraminal stenosis, lumbar disc disease, and lumbar kyphoscoliosis. Among these cases, 73 patients developed SSI. After excluding those with a follow-up period of <1 year, insufficient data, or perioperative death due to complications other than SSI, 50 patients (39 males and 11 females; mean age, 70.5 years) were included in the final analysis. Patients were categorized into two groups based on whether removal of implants inserted during the index surgery was performed during the study period: the implant removal group (Group R) and the implant retention group (Group N). Some patients in Group R underwent reinsertion of new implants after removal at the discretion of the operating surgeon. Both groups were followed up for at least one year, and follow-up continued until infection resolution was confirmed in the electronic medical records.

Definition of SSI

The definition of SSI in this study followed the guidelines established by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention11). Superficial SSI was defined as an infection confined to the skin and subcutaneous tissue occurring within 30 days after surgery, characterized by purulent drainage from the incision, detection of a pathogen, or clinical symptoms such as pain, tenderness, swelling, redness, or heat. Deep SSI was defined as an infection involving the fascia and muscle layers within one year after surgery, associated with purulent drainage from the deep incision, pathogen detection, or clinical symptoms such as fever, pain, or tenderness. In this study, patients diagnosed with SSI were identified through a review of electronic medical records, and diagnosis was based on laboratory findings and clinical symptoms consistent with the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention criteria, observed either during hospitalization or outpatient follow-up.

Data collection

Data were collected in a standardized manner by a single researcher. All variables were retrospectively extracted from electronic medical records. Patient characteristics included age, sex, body mass index, diabetes mellitus, and the American Society of Anesthesiologists Physical Status Classification System. Laboratory data were obtained from blood samples collected within three months before surgery and included platelet, neutrophil, lymphocyte, and monocyte counts, as well as serum albumin and C-reactive protein (CRP). From these data, the prognostic nutritional index, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio, lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio, lymphocyte-to-CRP ratio, and modified Glasgow Prognostic Score were calculated. CRP at the time of SSI onset was also evaluated. The three-month window reflected the standard preoperative assessment period used across the participating centers; when multiple results were available, the value obtained closest to surgery was used. Surgical factors included operative time, intraoperative blood loss, number of fused vertebrae, duration of drain placement, and number of previous surgeries at the same site. Postoperative factors included the presence of sepsis, the interval from surgery to SSI onset, and the interval from SSI onset to the first surgical intervention for infection control.

Statistical analysis

Normality was first assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Nominal variables were compared using Fisher's exact test or the chi-square test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test, and the results are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (Q1-Q3). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to determine reference cutoff values for continuous variables. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 28.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Because the implant removal group included only 15 cases, multivariate logistic regression analysis was not performed. Given the commonly recommended rule of at least 10 events per variable in logistic regression models, inclusion of multiple explanatory variables in the present dataset would have resulted in model overfitting and unstable estimates. Therefore, the present analysis was restricted to univariate comparisons to provide exploratory and descriptive insights.

Results

Among the 50 patients who developed SSI, implant removal was performed in 15 cases (30%). Lumbar spinal stenosis was the most common underlying disease (43 cases, 86.0%) (Table 1). Posterior lumbar interbody fusion was the predominant surgical procedure (42 cases, 84%) (Table 2). Bacterial cultures were obtained from all patients, with 22 (44.0%) testing positive. Methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus was the most frequent pathogen (five cases). Polymicrobial infections occurred in two cases, whereas the remaining culture-positive cases were monomicrobial (Table 3).

In Group R, the main reason for implant removal was difficulty in infection control (nine cases, 60%), followed by implant loosening (six cases, 40%). All patients underwent multiple irrigation and debridement procedures before implant removal. The type of removed implants and subsequent management after removal varied among patients (Table 4). Six patients underwent complete pedicle screw removal and external fixation with a body cast, and in two cases, screws were replaced. Bone fusion was assessed based on medical record documentation and routine radiographic evaluation in all cases, and no apparent dynamic instability was noted on radiographs. Univariate analysis showed no significant differences between Group R and Group N in age, sex, body mass index, diabetes mellitus, or American Society of Anesthesiologists Physical Status Classification System. Group R had significantly longer operative times (p=0.03) and a higher number of previous surgeries at the same level (p=0.004). No significant differences were observed in intraoperative blood loss, number of fused vertebrae, or drain placement duration. Discussion

Controlling SSI after spinal instrumentation surgery remains a significant clinical challenge. In cases of uncontrollable SSI, removal or replacement of implants inserted during the index surgery is sometimes unavoidable. Reported implant retention rates after SSI range from 20% to 60%9,12,13). However, implant removal or more extensive fixation can seriously compromise postoperative outcomes, resulting in poorer functional prognosis and reduced quality of life; therefore, such procedures should be avoided whenever possible. Several studies have reported risk factors for implant removal after SSI; however, many included heterogeneous diseases and surgical approaches, leading to inconsistent findings8-10). Accordingly, interpretation of these findings requires careful consideration of disease background and surgical context. The strength of the present study lies in its focus on patients with lumbar degenerative disease, allowing description of clinical circumstances within a relatively homogeneous disease context. To our knowledge, few studies have described clinical factors associated with implant removal after SSI specifically in lumbar degenerative disease. In this context, the present study provides a disease-specific observational description of clinical factors associated with implant removal following SSI. Although this was a retrospective study with inherent limitations, the findings offer descriptive insights into clinical circumstances encountered after SSI in lumbar degenerative disease.

The association between operative time and SSI has been well documented. Amorim-Barbosa et al.7) reported that the duration of surgery in degenerative spinal procedures was an independent risk factor for SSI, conferring a two- to fourfold increase in risk. In contrast, few studies have described the relationship between operative time and implant removal after SSI. Factors that prolong operative time include obesity, increased intraoperative blood loss, a greater number of fused levels, intraoperative complications, and revision surgery12,14,15). These factors likely interact in a complex manner. Moreover, the reasons for prolonged operative time vary depending on disease type and surgical procedure, making it difficult to determine within this study alone how extended operative duration contributes to implant removal. Therefore, operative time in the present study should be interpreted as a marker of overall surgical complexity rather than as an independent determinant; nevertheless, prolonged operative time may increase the risk of wound contamination and tissue damage, potentially complicating infection control and being associated with implant removal. Given the exploratory nature and limited sample size of this study, these observations should be interpreted cautiously.

Previous spinal surgery has also been reported as a significant risk factor for SSI. Schimmel et al.16) demonstrated that a history of spinal surgery increased the risk of SSI after spinal fusion by approximately fourfold. Bratschitsch et al.17) reported that poorly vascularized and dysfunctional scar tissue, which frequently develops after multiple spinal revisions, provides a vulnerable environment for chronic low-grade bacterial infection. Similarly, Tominaga et al.8) identified the number of previous surgeries as a risk factor for implant removal after SSI in spinal instrumentation surgery, suggesting that multiple surgeries may lead to inadequate soft tissue coverage and impaired vascular supply, thereby hindering wound healing. These reports are consistent with the present observations. Collectively, these findings indicate that an increased number of previous surgeries at the same site predisposes patients to impaired wound healing, likely because of vascular insufficiency and scar tissue formation. Moreover, poor vascularity may limit antibiotic penetration, complicating infection control and consequently increasing the likelihood of implant removal.

The interval from surgery to SSI onset was also identified as a significant factor influencing implant retention. Maruo and Berven18) reported that late-onset infection was associated with implant removal after spinal instrumentation surgery. Similarly, Kowalski et al.19) found that implants were successfully retained following surgical debridement in 93% of patients when infection occurred within 30 days of the index surgery, whereas implants were removed in 63% of patients with late-onset infections. These findings emphasize the critical influence of infection timing on treatment outcomes. The underlying mechanism explaining the need for implant removal is largely attributed to biofilm formation. Hickok et al.20) observed that the implant surface facilitates microbial adhesion and biofilm formation, while Gracia et al.21) demonstrated that mature bacterial biofilm markedly reduces bacterial susceptibility to antibiotics, allowing infection to persist despite appropriate antibiotic therapy. Consequently, late-onset infections are generally more refractory because of diminished antibiotic efficacy, often necessitating implant removal. However, the temporal definition of “late-onset” SSI has not been consistent across previous reports. For example, Maruo and Berven18) defined late-onset infection as occurring ≥90 days after surgery, whereas Kowalski et al.19) used a threshold of ≥30 days. Given this variability, a universally accepted temporal cutoff has not been established. In the present study, the interval from surgery to SSI onset was analyzed as a continuous variable, and no predefined categorical definition of “late-onset” was applied. Therefore, the term “late-onset” is used descriptively to indicate infections occurring later in the postoperative course within our cohort, rather than to imply a fixed temporal threshold.

Furthermore, the interval from SSI onset to the first surgical intervention for infection control was also a key determinant of implant retention. This finding can be attributed to the time-dependent nature of biofilm development. Oliveira et al.22) investigated biofilm formation by Staphylococcus aureus and Staphylococcus epidermidis isolated from mastitis using fluorescence in situ hybridization and reported that 80.8% of S. aureus and 75.9% of S. epidermidis isolates were biofilm-positive at 72 hours. In an in vivo mouse femur model with titanium implants, Nishitani et al.23) demonstrated that, by two weeks after infection, the implant surface was extensively covered with a thick extracellular matrix indicative of mature biofilm formation. These findings suggest that biofilm formation progresses rapidly and matures over time, making infection control increasingly difficult. Consistent with this, Chen et al.24) reported that in posterior spinal instrumentation surgery, delayed treatment of SSI by more than three months significantly increased the likelihood of implant removal. Taken together, these observations indicate that aggressive irrigation and debridement should ideally be performed before biofilm maturation, in combination with pathogen-specific antibiotic therapy, to maximize the likelihood of implant retention. Accordingly, earlier surgical intervention after SSI onset may be associated with a greater likelihood of implant retention; however, this interpretation should be viewed within the limitations of an observational study.

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective study, the accuracy and precision of data collection may have been influenced by biases inherent to available medical records. Second, because this was a multicenter study, the decision to remove implants was left to the discretion of each surgeon. The absence of standardized criteria for implant removal may have introduced variability and potential bias. Third, the sample size was relatively small. Owing to this limitation, multivariate analysis was not feasible, and the statistical power to detect significant differences in the univariate analysis was limited. In general, logistic regression models require an adequate number of outcome events per explanatory variable to ensure stable and reliable estimates. In the present study, only 15 cases required implant removal, and inclusion of multiple covariates under these conditions would have substantially increased the risk of model overfitting and unstable parameter estimation. To further assess the adequacy of the sample size, we conducted a post hoc power analysis based on the observed between-group differences, assuming a two-sided α error of 0.05 and a β error of 0.20 (80% power). This analysis indicated that approximately 28 cases per group would have been required to detect a significant difference in operative time, and 24–27 cases per group for the intervals from surgery to SSI onset and from SSI onset to the first surgical intervention. Although significant associations were identified in univariate comparisons, the possibility of limited statistical power and residual confounding must be acknowledged. Therefore, the absence of multivariate adjustment should not be interpreted as confirmation of independent associations, and the findings should be understood within the exploratory and descriptive framework of the present study. In addition, unlike many previous studies that included heterogeneous disease populations, the present study specifically focused on lumbar degenerative disease, thereby strengthening disease-specific interpretation but further limiting the number of eligible cases. Fourth, although surgical invasiveness and perioperative infection control measures are essential determinants of SSI risk, our dataset lacked consistent records of key variables such as incision technique, suture methods, perioperative antibiotic regimens, and wound care practices. Consequently, these variables could not be incorporated into the analysis. Accordingly, the present findings should not be interpreted as demonstrating independent or causal relationships. Given these limitations, the findings of this study should be regarded as exploratory and descriptive. Future multicenter studies with larger and more homogeneous cohorts are warranted to further examine these observations and support more robust interpretations across different clinical settings.

Conclusions

In this study, operative time, the number of previous surgeries at the same site, the interval from surgery to SSI onset, and the interval from SSI onset to the first surgical intervention for infection control were observed to differ between patients who underwent implant removal or replacement and those who retained implants inserted during the index surgery after SSI following spinal instrumentation surgery for lumbar degenerative disease. These observations highlight clinical characteristics that were more frequently noted in cases requiring implant removal, rather than establishing determinants of treatment decisions. In addition, a shorter interval between SSI onset and the first surgical intervention was more commonly observed in patients who retained implants, underscoring the potential importance of timely management after SSI onset. Given the exploratory and descriptive nature of this study, future multicenter studies with larger and more homogeneous cohorts are warranted to further examine these observations and support more robust interpretations across different clinical settings.
