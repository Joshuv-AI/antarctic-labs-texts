Utilizing ALD to fabricate ZnO ETL to improve the luminous efficiency of perovskite light-emitting diodes
Authors: Chia-Hua Chan, Kuan-Chi Yeh, Chien-Cheng Kuo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this work, we demonstrate an approach to fabricate perovskite light-emitting diodes (PeLEDs) by integrating atomic layer deposition (ALD) and single-source vapor deposition (SSVD), which can operate at low voltages without the hole transport layer, thereby achieving high luminescence. This study successfully eliminated the heterostructure in the CsPbBr3 emitting layer through thermal annealing, thereby improving the quality and crystallinity of the film. We also employ the ALD process to produce zinc oxide (ZnO) as the electron transport layer (ETL) to enhance the electron transport capability of the PeLEDs. By combining the optimized CsPbBr3 emitting layer with ZnO ETL, PeLEDs devices were successfully fabricated. These PeLEDs operate at just 2.67 V, producing high-purity green light (CIE 1931 coordinates: (0.1432, 0.8015)) at 17,661 cd m− 2, a significant 23% improvement in brightness compared to the device without ZnO ETL. It is worth noting that the green PeLEDs produced in this study only requires two commercially available 1.5 V batteries to activate and achieve maximum brightness, effectively overcoming the issue of excessively high voltage in PeLEDs. There is potential for its application in the field of PeLEDs production in the future.Keywords: CsPbBr3, Perovskite LEDs, ZnO ETL, ALDSubject terms: Lasers, LEDs and light sources; Optics and photonics

Introduction

Improving display performance requires advancements in backlighting and active emissive sources1–4. Perovskites are a promising alternative that has caught the interest of many researchers5. They have excellent optoelectronic properties, including high color purity6, adjustable bandgap4, better carrier mobility7, narrower full-width at half maximum (FWHM)8, and relatively lower costs in both material and manufacturing9. Due to these features, they are being adopted widely across various sectors, such as light-emitting diodes10, solar cells11, and photodetectors12. Inorganic perovskites are commonly used to prepare perovskite light-emitting diodes (PeLEDs) because of their high efficiency and stability in atmospheric environments. Although PeLEDs’ external quantum efficiency (EQE) was only 0.1% in a 2014 study13, their development has progressed rapidly in recent years. Many research results now show that PeLEDs’ EQE can exceed 20%8,14, which is comparable to the efficiency of organic light-emitting diodes (OLED). Additionally, the CIE color coordinate coverage area of PeLEDs can reach 137% of the NTSC standard15. This exceeds the color gamut of current liquid crystal display (LCD) and OLED on the market.

In the past few years, most PeLEDs emitting layers have been prepared using solution methods, which are simple and fast16. With advancements such as metal ion doping, perovskite material tuning, or the addition of polymers6,17–19, device efficiency has significantly improved, resulting in excellent performance of current PeLEDs. However, these methods need to remove residual ligands or solvents during the manufacturing process, which may cause defects that affect the film quality and, ultimately, the performance of the PeLEDs. To address this issue, scientists have turned to vacuum deposition methods, which not only ensure superior device performance but also enable precise control over film thickness and uniformity20.

Currently, the most common vacuum deposition technique involves dual-source co-evaporation to prepare perovskite emitting layers21–23. This method uses two evaporative sources to deposit different perovskite precursors onto the substrate. However, careful adjustment of the proportions and deposition rates of the two perovskite precursors is necessary during the evaporation process, making the preparation somewhat complex. Another approach for preparing PeLEDs emitting layers is single-source vapor deposition (SSVD). This method involves directly depositing pre-synthesized perovskite (CsPbX3) powder onto the substrate24–26, offering a simpler process compared to dual-source co-evaporation. However, research on SSVD is relatively scarce, and PeLEDs prepared using SSVD tend to exhibit slightly lower luminosity than those fabricated through dual-source co-evaporation24,25. Nevertheless, identifying suitable SSVD fabrication methods holds promise for enhancing device brightness and performance, potentially reducing equipment costs and simplifying processes.

PeLEDs performance can be enhanced by optimizing the quality of the emitting layer and adjusting the electron transport layer (ETL) and hole transport layer (HTL) to match their work functions with the emitting layer. This is crucial for improving PeLEDs efficiency and performance27–29. Currently, organic molecules and conductive polymers are the preferred ETL and HTL materials used in many studies. While they enhance PeLEDs performance significantly, they tend to be unstable when exposed to atmospheric moisture, oxygen, and heat, negatively impacting the stability and long-term operation of PeLEDs.

ZnO, a significant inorganic semiconductor material, possesses a wide bandgap, high electron mobility, and excellent optical transparency30–32. There are various methods for preparing ZnO, such as hydrothermal synthesis33, sputtering34, and atomic layer deposition (ALD)35, each with its unique advantages. As an n-type semiconductor material, ZnO is highly suitable for use as the ETL in PeLEDs. Choosing ZnO as the ETL can enhance electron injection and transmission into the emitting layer while avoiding unnecessary energy losses36,37. This, in turn, allows devices to operate at lower voltages, emit brighter light, and further enhance device performance, sparking further interest in the potential of ZnO in PeLEDs.

Our previous study utilized the SSVD method to deposit CsPbBr3 material, which simplified the process of creating CsPbBr3 PeLEDs38. Now, in this study, we take it a step further and explore the impact of different thicknesses of CsPbBr3 emitting layers on PeLEDs’ luminous efficiency. We also use ALD to deposit varying cycles of ZnO as the ETL for PeLEDs, aiming to improve electron transport efficiency. Our results demonstrate that an appropriate thickness of ZnO enhances perovskite crystallization and addresses electron injection imbalance issues. This ultimately allows PeLED devices to emit high-brightness green light at lower operating voltages, improving their luminous efficiency.

Experimental section

Materials

Acetone (ACE, 99.5%) and Isopropyl alcohol (IPA, 99.5%) was procured from LCY Chemical. The indium tin oxide conductive substrate (ITO, 25 mm X 25 mm X 7 mm, Resistor 7 ohms) was purchased from Ruilong Co., Ltd. Perovskite powder (CsPbBr3) was sourced from Utang Co., Ltd. Diethylzinc (DEZ) from PentaPro Co., Ltd. Carbon glue (C, PF-407 C) was acquired from Acheson. All materials and solvents were utilized without further purification.

Experimental methods

The ZnO thin films prepared by ALD exhibit good electron mobility, smooth surfaces, and low defect density, which effectively improve the interface contact and have the potential to reduce leakage current and non-radiative recombination losses in the device. Therefore, this study adopts the ALD process to fabricate the ZnO thin films. ZnO fabrication method: The cleaned glass or ITO substrate is placed inside the ALD chamber, where the internal pressure must reach a vacuum level lower than 9 millitorrs and a temperature of 200℃ before commencing the deposition process. Each cycle of the process consists of (1) a 0.1-second pulse of water, (2) a 5-second purge of N2, (3) a 0.05-second pulse of DEZ, and (4) another 5-second purge of N2. These four steps constitute one cycle and are repeated for 400, 600, and 800 cycles.

PeLEDs fabrication method: The ITO substrate is sequentially immersed in a cleaning solution, ACE, and IPA. After ultrasonic cleaning of the glass surface, it is dried with nitrogen gas, followed by UV-ozone irradiation for 10 min to complete the substrate cleaning process. The cleaned ITO substrate, with or without ZnO deposition, is then placed in the chamber of a thermal evaporation machine, CsPbBr3 powder is then loaded into a molybdenum boat within the chamber. The chamber is evacuated to reach a vacuum level of approximately 8 × 10− 6 torr. The CsPbBr3 powder is heated, causing it to sublime and deposit onto the substrate, thus forming a perovskite thin film. Subsequently, the samples are placed in a high-temperature furnace and heated in an ambient atmosphere at a rate of 10 °C /min up to 350℃ for 100 min of heat treatment. These annealing parameters were adopted based on our previous study, which identified this condition as optimal for achieving high-quality CsPbBr₃ perovskite films38,39. Following this process, the CsPbBr3 emitting layer thicknesses are approximately 300 nm, 500 nm, and 700 nm. The substrate is then placed into a patterned mask, and carbon glue is evenly applied and dried with a scraper. This completes the device assembly process for subsequent analysis and measurement. The device measurements are carried out in the atmosphere.

Characterization

The thermal evaporation coater (Taiwan Instrument Research Institute) was used to deposit perovskite films. The atomic layer deposition system (Taiwan Instrument Research Institute) was used to deposit the electron transport layer. Sample measurements were conducted using an Ultraviolet/visible Spectrophotometer (UV-vis, HITACHI U-2900) for wavelengths ranging from 300 to 800 nm, with a scanning speed of 100 nm/min. X-ray Diffractometer (XRD, Bruker D8 ADVANCE) analysis was performed by scanning the sample at a speed of 0.05°/s over the angle range of 10° to 50°. Sample morphology was observed using a Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM, HITACHI S-4300) at an acceleration voltage of 10.0 kV. The ultraviolet photoelectron spectrometer (UPS, Sigma Probe) sample was measured with helium excitation at 21.2 eV, and a negative bias voltage of -5 V was applied. Photoluminescence (PL) measurements of the thin film were performed using a light source at 402 nm for excitation, employing an integrating-sphere (Labsphere), spectrometer (Ocean Optics USB2000+), and power supply (Keithley2400). PeLEDs measurements were conducted in atmospheric conditions, utilizing a power supply (Keithley 2400) and a luminance meter (TOPCON, BM-7AC) to measure current density-voltage-luminance (J-V-L).

Results and discussion

Thermal annealing treatment for analysis of CsPbBr3 thin film quality with different thicknesses

In this research, the precursor CsPbBr3 powders were used to fabricate CsPbBr3 PeLEDs through the SSVD method. To explore the optoelectronic performance of PeLEDs, emitting layers of three different thicknesses (300 nm, 500 nm, and 700 nm) were created. Initially, freshly deposited films of the three thicknesses were subjected to UV-Vis measurements. As shown in Fig. 1a, the results indicated that the absorption peak at 520 nm increased with the thickness of the CsPbBr3 film. However, it was observed that regardless of the variation in film thickness, the absorption intensity at 320 nm and 360 nm was higher than that of CsPbBr3 absorption, possibly due to the presence of CsPbBr3 heterostructures. Earlier studies have shown that during the deposition process of CsPbBr3 powder, some PbBr2 might evaporate and deposit on the substrate, reacting with subsequently deposited CsPbBr3 to form CsPbBr3 heterostructures40,41. XRD analysis was also conducted to investigate the crystal structure and crystallinity of the three different thicknesses of CsPbBr3 films. The results revealed that the CsPbBr3 phase belonged to monoclinic (JCPDS No. 00-018-0364) and orthorhombic (JCPDS No. 98-009-7851) compounds (Fig. 1b). However, the films exhibited multiple characteristic peaks and wider FWHM, indicating disordered growth of crystal planes and poorer crystallinity. This could lead to the generation of defects in the film, which could negatively impact the optical or electrical properties.

Based on the analysis conducted above, it was found that the quality of the films was not satisfactory. To improve the quality of the films, we subjected them to a thermal annealing treatment at 350 °C for 100 min. After the annealing process, we performed UV-Vis analysis on the CsPbBr3 films (Fig. 1c). The results showed a significant increase in absorbance at 520 nm, while the absorption due to heterostructures at 320 nm and 360 nm decreased. This indicates that the annealing process transforms heterostructures into CsPbBr3 and improves the crystalline quality, thereby enhancing the optical performance of the CsPbBr3 films.

Next, we observed the crystal structure and crystallinity of the annealed films (Fig. 1d). We found noticeable changes, such as the crystallization phase transitioning to the cubic phase (JCPDS No. 00-054-0752) and the shift of the original 15.08°characteristic peak to 15.2° (100). The two characteristic peaks originally located at 30.4° and 30.7° merged into a single characteristic peak at 30.7° (200), and the predominant crystal planes gradually shifted towards (100) and (200). These changes indicate that sufficient heat allows the CsPbBr3 lattice structure to transform. Consistent results from XRD and UV-Vis measurements suggest that annealing plays a crucial role in improving film crystallinity and optical performance. The phenomenon may be attributed to disturbances in Pb and Br atoms near the melting point of PbBr2 (372 °C) during annealing, causing atomic rearrangements or detachment from the surface. Consequently, grain growth may occur via the Ostwald ripening effect, leading to changes in the film.

Using SEM, we observed changes in films of varying thicknesses. As shown in Fig. 1e–g, the films exhibit high density without any pinholes. The grain size increases from approximately 400 nm to about 1 μm with increasing film thickness. These SEM results confirm the enhancement of UV-Vis absorption peaks and the transformation of XRD crystal phases to the cubic phase. This emphasizes the importance of annealing treatment in ensuring stable film performance in application. We also conducted photoluminescence (PL) measurements on the films of varying thicknesses. After annealing treatment, the PL wavelength of films with different thicknesses remains at 533 nm, with a FWHM of less than 18 nm (Fig. S1). This suggests that the emission wavelength of the films does not vary due to differences in thickness or grain size.

The influence of CsPbBr3 emitting layer thickness on the current-voltage-luminance (J-V-L) performance of PeLEDs

We experimented to explore the effect of three different thicknesses of CsPbBr3 films on the luminous efficiency of PeLEDs and to determine the optimal thickness that can operate reliably in ambient conditions without encapsulation. As shown in Fig. 2, the experimental setup consisted of simple structured PeLEDs comprising ITO / CsPbBr3 / Carbon (C). We found that the devices with different thicknesses had similar starting voltages in the current density-voltage (J-V) curves, roughly around 1.8 V, which is similar to previous research literature42. However, the thicker emission layer of CsPbBr3 700 nm PeLEDs increases the resistance. Therefore, as the current density increases, the device’s voltage is significantly higher than that of other components. Furthermore, we examined the luminance-voltage (L-V) relationship of the devices. We determined that the CsPbBr3 500 nm device achieved a maximum luminance of 14,302 cd m−² at 2.89 V, demonstrating outstanding performance in both luminance and current efficiency. We compiled the optimal light emission data for PeLEDs with different thicknesses, as shown in Table 1, proving that the device with CsPbBr3 500 nm is the most efficient and reliable.

In addition, the PeLEDs with a thickness of 500 nm of CsPbBr3 emit light wavelength at 528 nm with a FWHM less than 18 nm, and its coordinates are (0.1449, 0.8031). These data suggest that the light emitted by the device falls within the green region (Fig. S2). As mentioned earlier, the thickness of CsPbBr3 affects the J-V-L characteristics of PeLEDs. However, the absence of the ETL in the device structure can cause energy losses, which involves the device’s performance. Therefore, it is necessary to add the ETL layer to the device structure to optimize electron transport capabilities and enhance device performance.

Investigation and analysis of ZnO thin films fabricated by ALD with different cycle

To enhance the efficiency and electron transport capability of PeLEDs, we utilized ALD to prepare ZnO as the ETL. ZnO was chosen because of its excellent conductivity, high transparency, and carrier mobility, making it less sensitive to water and oxygen while facilitating enhanced electron injection and transport. However, the efficiency of the device is also impacted by the thickness of the ETL. Therefore, we deposited ZnO thin films on glass with 400, 600, and 800 cycles to study their properties. We first employed UV-Vis spectroscopy to characterize their properties, and the results showed that the absorption intensity at 360 nm gradually increased with an increase in cycles (Fig. 3a). To gain deeper insights into the crystallinity of the films at different cycles, we performed XRD measurements. The results revealed that the crystal phase of ZnO was hexagonal wurtzite (JCPDS No. 00-036-1451), with characteristic peaks corresponding to 31.8° (100), 34.4° (002), 36.25° (101), and 56.65° (110) (Fig. 3b). However, as the cycle increased, the crystal structure also changed. The characteristic peak at 34.4° (002) gradually weakened and shifted towards 31.8° (100), indicating alterations in the ZnO crystal structure with increasing cycles. Considering the influence of ZnO thickness on electrical properties, we measured the mobility, carrier concentration, resistivity, and sheet resistance of the films using the hall effect, and compared the electrical characteristics of films with different cycles (Table S1). The measurement results indicate that the electron mobility of ZnO initially increases with the number of ALD cycles and then decreases. First, the mobility of bare ITO is 27.93 cm2 V− 1 s− 1, with a carrier concentration of 1.69 × 10²¹ cm⁻³. After depositing 400 ALD cycles of ZnO, the mobility is 27.61 cm2 V− 1 s− 1, and the carrier concentration is 1.30 × 10²¹ cm⁻³ both values are similar to those of bare ITO. This suggests that the ZnO film at 400 cycles may suffer from poor crystallinity and high defect density, thus failing to improve electron mobility. When the ZnO thickness increases to 600 cycles, the mobility significantly improves to 31.44 cm2 V− 1 s− 1, although the carrier concentration decreases to 9.27 × 10²⁰ cm⁻³. We attribute this enhancement to improved crystallinity and reduced defect density, which facilitate smoother electron transport to the emissive layer and contribute to better device emission efficiency. However, further increasing the ZnO thickness to 800 cycles results in a mobility drop to 23.51 cm2 V− 1 s− 1. This could be due to the excessive film thickness increasing the electron transport path, thus hindering carrier transport. Although the carrier concentration recovers to 1.29 × 10²¹ cm⁻³, it cannot compensate for the negative impact caused by the reduced electron mobility.

The surface morphology observations of ZnO are shown in Fig. 3c–e. After conducting a thorough analysis, it was determined that there were no noticeable holes or aggregated particles on the surface of the film during a wide-area observation. Upon further magnification for detailed observation, it was revealed that the films exhibited excellent flatness and density without any pinholes. Additionally, with an increase in cycles, the grain size gradually increased. The actual thickness of the ZnO films was observed from a cross-section, providing conclusive results: thicknesses were found to be 62 nm for 400 cycles, 123 nm for 600 cycles, and 158 nm for 800 cycles. The SEM results provided a clear confirmation of the enhancement observed in UV-Vis absorption and the change in XRD crystal structure. We also used UPS measurements to analyze energy levels, and the results are presented in Fig. S3. The valence band maximum (VBM) of the ZnO film is 7.01 eV, and the conduction band minimum (CBM) is 3.78 eV, indicating that the ZnO film fabricated by ALD can be used for ETL applications in PeLEDs. This favorable energy gap facilitates electron injection while blocking holes, thereby improving charge balance and recombination efficiency. In addition, ZnO can also improve the electron mobility of the device and reduce charge accumulation and non-radiative losses. The dense and smooth ALD ZnO film also promotes high-quality perovskite growth and suppresses leakage current, contributing to the overall device performance and stability.

In the structure of CsPbBr3 PeLEDs, ZnO thin films serve as crucial ETLs. We chose the most efficient CsPbBr3 emitting layer thickness of 500 nm and deposited it onto ZnO thin films with different cycles. However, the CsPbBr3 layer requires thermal annealing to enhance its quality, which may affect the density and interface structure between the two types of thin films. We observed the appearance of the films after thermal annealing using SEM. Upon a wide-scale observation of the ZnO and CsPbBr3 thin films annealed together, there were no signs of cracking or fissures (Fig. 4a1,b1,c1). Upon further magnification, the films exhibited high density between the grains, with no visible pinholes (Fig. 4a2,b2,c2). Upon examining the cross-section, we observed pinholes at the interface of the CsPbBr3 film on the ZnO 400 cycles sample (Fig. 4a3), possibly due to the poor crystallinity of the ZnO film. Conversely, in the cross-section of the ZnO 600 and 800 cycles samples, no pinholes were observed, and there was a tight fit between the two types of films (Fig. 4b3,c3). This indicates that the crystallinity of ZnO can affect the CsPbBr3 film, preliminarily demonstrating the feasibility of selecting ZnO as the ETL for PeLEDs and annealing it together with CsPbBr3.

Influence of ZnO film with varying cycles on PeLEDs performance

We fabricated the PeLEDs structure consisting of ITO anode (250 nm) / ZnO ETL (400 cycles, 600 cycles, 800 cycles) / CsPbBr3 emitting layer (500 nm) / Carbon (C) cathode (about 30 μm), as depicted in Fig. 5a. Despite the varying thickness of the ZnO ETL in different cycles, all devices showed consistent slopes in the J-V curves with starting voltages ranging from 1.7 to 1.8 V (Fig. 5b). This proximity to the theoretical threshold of CsPbBr3 LED, similar to previous research, indicates the occurrence of sub-threshold conduction caused by parasitic effects resulting from surface states and deep-level defect states in the semiconductor43–45. The device with ZnO 400 cycles exhibited a luminance of only 14,639 cd m− 2 and a current efficiency of 0.81 cd A− 1 at 2.76 V (Fig. 5c,d). When compared to the ETL-free device, the efficiency did not improve. This phenomenon can be mainly attributed to the poor crystallinity of the ZnO film deposited with 400 ALD cycles. Compared to the device with only ITO, the electron mobility of this ZnO layer did not significantly improve, resulting in a limited enhancement in electron injection efficiency into the emissive layer and, consequently, no obvious improvement in device luminous efficiency.

Next, we observed the device with ZnO 600 cycles, which realized the optimal performance in our PeLED system. At a voltage of 2.67 V, its luminance reached 17,661 cd m− 2 (Fig. 5c), at this point, the EQE was 0.43%. When compared to the ETL-free device, its luminance increased by about 23%. Notably, at maximum luminance, the voltage decreased from 2.89 V to 2.67 V, while the current efficiency also increased to 1.10 cd A− 1, resulting in a 15% improvement (Fig. 5d). This improvement can be attributed to the increase in electron mobility of the ZnO film, which reached 31.44 cm² V⁻¹ s⁻¹. The higher electron mobility facilitates smoother electron transport to the emissive layer, thereby promoting carrier recombination efficiency and improving the device’s brightness and luminous efficiency. Moreover, we fabricated 20 devices using the currently optimized parameters (ZnO 600 cycles + CsPbBr₃ 500 nm) for multi-pixel testing. As shown in Fig. S4, the devices exhibited a median peak luminance of approximately 15,458 cd m−² and a median maximum current efficiency of about 0.97 cd A− 1, demonstrating good reproducibility. This PeLEDs device exhibited an emission wavelength of 528 nm, with an FWHM of less than 18 nm, and its CIE 1931 coordinates were (0.1432, 0.8015) (Fig. 5e–g). Furthermore, based on these research findings (Table 2), ZnO 600 cycles can be confirmed as the optimal choice for the PeLEDs ETL. Compared to devices reported in other literature (Table S2), although our green PeLEDs lack the HTL, require lower voltage at its highest luminance, and it is closer to commercially available green LEDs, making it more aligned with practical application requirements. It can be powered on using just two commercially available 1.5 V batteries (Video S1).

However, the device with ZnO 800 cycles exhibited a decrease in luminance, with a maximum luminance of only 12,070 cd m− 2, and the operating voltage increased to 3.2 V. This decrease may be attributed to the increased thickness of the ETL, which lengthens the electron transport path and increases the probability of carrier scattering, trap formation, and carrier quenching, resulting in a significant decrease in mobility and ultimately causing a drop in the device’s luminous efficiency. Furthermore, the thicker ETL indirectly reduces the likelihood of electron-hole recombination, resulting in higher voltage and current density, leading to an increase in voltage and current density.

To evaluate the operational stability of the PeLEDs, we selected the device with the best performance, ZnO 600 cycles, and tested under atmospheric environmental conditions with a fixed current density of 200 mA cm−² and an initial luminance of 100 cd m−². Upon initiating the PeLEDs operation, the luminance initially increased slightly but then gradually decreased, ultimately reaching a T50 lifetime of 137 min (Fig. 5h). At the same time, to further illustrate the importance of a properly cycled ZnO ETL for PeLEDs, we compared the operational stability of devices without ZnO and those with ZnO ETLs (Table S3). These results suggest that the PeLEDs can operate for a certain period, but the luminance exhibits a trend of initial increase followed by a decline. According to previous research, this phenomenon is attributed to ion migration and accumulation in the emitting layer46, as well as the generation of Joule heating during operation, coupled with poor heat dissipation47. Although this study successfully enhanced the efficiency of the PeLEDs, the relatively high current density observed in the device’s J-V curve suggests that the device is prone to overheating issues. Therefore, there is still room for improvement in the device structure to achieve higher efficiency and stability in PeLEDs devices. Introducing quantum well structures into the perovskite emissive layer offers a promising strategy to enhance carrier confinement and radiative recombination, which could lead to improved electroluminescent performance in future PeLEDs.

Conclusion

In this study, we examined how different thicknesses of CsPbBr3 emitting layers and various cycles of ZnO ETLs affected the performance of PeLEDs. As the thickness of the CsPbBr3 emitting layer increased, so did its absorption intensity. However, an overly thick luminescent layer will increase the resistance and affect the luminous efficiency of PeLEDs. It was found that films without thermal annealing have heterostructures and poor crystallinity, which can negatively impact radiative recombination. Therefore, we used thermal annealing to improve the film quality. Experimental results showed that thermal annealing improved the absorption intensity of CsPbBr3, reduced absorption peaks associated with heterostructures, and transformed the crystal structure into the cubic phase, resulting in better film quality and crystallinity. We fabricated PeLEDs with different thicknesses of CsPbBr3 emitting layers into a simple structure. The device with a CsPbBr3 thickness of 500 nm performed the best, reaching a luminance of 14,302 cd m−² at a voltage of 2.89 V. It emitted at 528 nm and had CIE coordinates of (0.1449, 0.8031).

In order to improve the efficiency of PeLEDs, ZnO films were fabricated as ETL via ALD process. As the number of cycles increased, the ZnO films became more crystalline, leading to a decrease in resistivity and sheet resistance. Even after thermal annealing of the CsPbBr3 emitting layer and ZnO film, no visible pores formation occurred and the two layers remained closely adhered. Among the PeLEDs fabricated, the device with ZnO 600 cycles exhibited the best performance. It had a maximum luminance of 17,661 cd m− 2 at a voltage of only 2.67 V and showed a current efficiency of 1.10 cd A− 1. Its CIE coordinates were (0.1432, 0.8015). Compared to devices without the added ZnO ETL, its luminance increased by about 23%.

It is worth noting that our green PeLEDs, although without the HTL, achieves maximum luminance with just two commercially available 1.5 V batteries, making them well-suited for practical applications such as lighting and displays. These results demonstrate the potential of this technology for broader use. With further improvements in device design and encapsulation, PeLEDs may find applications beyond consumer electronics, such as in medical diagnostics and biosensing.
