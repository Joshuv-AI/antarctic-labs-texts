Evolutionary acquisition of an interaction between conserved proteins drives plant-specific cell cycle progression
Authors: Yan Li, Renshen Yuan, Xudong Shang, Qingmeng Xiao, Liqing Yang, Min Jiang, Qianfeng Zhang, Yilin Xu, Ruiqi Li, Yujia Zhang, Ying Wang, Ying Cao, Hui Chen, Ligeng Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The cell cycle is fundamental to eukaryotes and regulated in conserved and kingdom-specific manners. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying plant-specific cell-cycle control remain unclear. Here, we demonstrate that a plant-specific N-terminal extension of PAF1 facilitates the assembly of transcriptional machinery that regulates plant-specific cell-cycle progression. This exclusively-evolved extension mediates direct interaction between PAF1 and SKIP within plantae. The resulting PAF1c-SKIP complex binds to a plant-specific CDKB locus in a PAF1-dependent manner, activating its expression to drive cell-cycle progression from unicellular algae to angiosperms. The intrinsically disordered N-terminal extension of PAF1 undergoes phase separation. Both this extension and its phase separation capacity are critical for functional PAF1c-SKIP complex formation and cell-cycle progression. Our findings illustrate how evolutionary repurposing of ancient protein domains through evolutionary gain of an IDR-like N-terminal extension enables plants to develop a unique molecular mechanism for kingdom-specific cell cycle strategies.

Introduction

Plants and animals have evolved independently, originating from distinct ancestral unicellular organisms1–5. The regulation of the cell cycle is fundamental to eukaryotic life, as it is directly connected with the replication of genetic information and its transmission through generations6–10. Cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) drive the progression of the eukaryotic cell cycle. CDK-cyclin complexes phosphorylate substrates at the G1-S and G2-M phase transition points to initiate DNA replication and mitosis, respectively11–16. Therefore, tight regulation of CDK expression levels is of paramount importance. However, most molecular cell cycle research focuses on unikonts (including yeast and animals)—just one of five eukaryotic branches7,10. This narrow focus overlooks the vast majority of eukaryotic diversity that evolved over two billion years since the first eukaryotes emerged.

While sharing fundamental processes, the plant and animal kingdoms exhibit lineage-specific features of cell cycle progression. Plants share the core cell cycle machinery with other eukaryotes, but they belong to the archaeplastid supergroup and have evolved additional molecular and regulatory components for cell cycle progression10,11,13,17–19. Moreover, some conserved cell cycle components have undergone functional divergence in plants during evolution. In plants, CDKA- and CDKB-type CDKs together provide major CDK activities for cell-cycle progression7,10,12. CDKA;1, which is structurally conserved across eukaryotes, plays broad roles across the cell cycle, including regulation of S-phase entry7,10,20. In parallel, CDKBs are plant-lineage CDKs that contribute to S-phase and G2/M progression in a developmental- and tissue-dependent manner and show partial functional redundancy with CDKA-type activity in certain contexts20–29. In Arabidopsis, CDKBs comprise two subfamilies, CDKB1 (CDKB1;1/1;2) and CDKB2 (CDKB2;1/2;2). CDKB1 has been implicated in maintaining mitotic programs and restraining entry into the endocycle, supporting specific developmental divisions such as stomata development, and contributing to DNA damage–associated cell-cycle control30–33; whereas CDKB2 has been linked primarily to meristem integrity and the organization of proliferative tissues22,34. Since CDKB originated in the plant lineage, and given that tight control of its expression is critical for proper cell cycle regulation and cell proliferation, plants likely evolved a specific regulatory mechanism coinciding with CDKB emergence. However, neither the plant-specific molecular mechanisms underlying CDKB expression regulation nor the evolutionary origins of this regulatory machinery during eukaryotic evolution have been identified.

The PAF1 complex (PAF1c), a conserved eukaryotic regulator comprising five evolutionarily conserved components from yeast to animals and plants, mediates transcriptional control through its interaction with the C-terminal domain of RNA polymerase II (Pol II) during transcriptional initiation, elongation, and termination35–38. It is also required for post-transcriptional control of gene expression, including pre-mRNA 3’ end processing35–38. SKIP is a bifunctional regulator that serves as both a splicing factor and a transcriptional regulator in eukaryotes. SKIP is a core catalytic component of the spliceosome and is required for proper pre-mRNA splicing from yeast to human and plants39–42. SKIP also serves as a transcriptional co-regulator in animals by interacting with diverse transcriptional regulators, e.g. the vitamin D receptor, HDAC1, and the MLL1 histone methyltransferase complex, to modulate gene expression and regulate tumorigenesis and spermatogenesis in humans43–45. Our previous studies in Arabidopsis have established that plant SKIP interacts physically with ELF7 (the PAF1 ortholog) via its SNW domain, forming a PAF1c-SKIP regulatory module that activates FLC expression and delays flowering46–48.

Here, we demonstrate that the plant-specific interaction between SKIP and PAF1c originates from the evolutionary acquisition of an extended N-terminal domain in PAF1. This evolutionarily acquired region exhibits intrinsic disorder and drives liquid-liquid phase separation in the nucleus, serving dual conserved functions across plant lineages—from unicellular algae to angiosperms—by enabling both PAF1 condensate formation and PAF1c-SKIP complex assembly. Crucially, this plant-evolved PAF1c-SKIP complex directly engages chromatin at CDKB loci, activating expression of plant-specific cell cycle regulators across the plant phylogeny while coordinating two fundamental plant traits: specialized cell cycle progression and sustained cell proliferation. Collectively, the resultant PAF1c-SKIP complex, nucleated by phase separation, executes kingdom-specific cell cycle programming of CDKB expression to regulate plant-unique cell cycle progression. Our findings establish that evolutionary acquisition of lineage-specific, NTE-mediated protein associations can enable distinct molecular mechanisms.

Results

SKIP-PAF1 interaction is plant-specific across eukaryotes

Our previous study identified a direct physical interaction between SKIP and ELF7 (AtPAF1) in Arabidopsis46–48. Given the conservation of both SKIP and PAF1 across eukaryotes, we investigated whether this interaction is Arabidopsis-specific or a broader evolutionary pattern. We selected ten evolutionarily divergent species spanning major eukaryotic lineages—including budding yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae), nematode (Caenorhabditis elegans), fruit fly (Drosophila melanogaster), human (Homo sapiens), and six plant species ranging from unicellular red algae (Galdieria sulphuraria) and unicellular green algae (Chlamydomonas reinhardtii) to angiosperms—to systematically examine SKIP-PAF1 interactions. Using yeast two-hybrid assays, we observed no detectable interaction between SKIP and PAF1 homologs from yeast, nematode, fly, or human. Strikingly, all six plant species exhibited robust SKIP-PAF1 binding (Supplementary Fig. 1a), a pattern independently validated by bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) in Nicotiana benthamiana cells (Supplementary Fig. 1b, c). These results demonstrate a kingdom-specific SKIP-PAF1 interaction in represented plants, consistent with established eukaryotic phylogeny where fungi exhibit closer evolutionary relationships to animals than to plants10,29,49,50.

An extended N terminus enables plant-specific PAF1c-SKIP interaction

To elucidate the evolutionary basis for lineage-specific SKIP-PAF1 interaction, we conducted systematic pairwise binding assays using homologs from the aforementioned ten representative eukaryotic species. Intriguingly, plant-derived PAF1 produced strong Y2H reporter signals with almost all tested SKIP variants, while non-plant PAF1 remained at background levels across all SKIP homologs both in β-galactosidase (Fig. 1a) and in growth assays (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Immunoblotting of the corresponding Y2H fusion proteins in yeast confirmed the expression of the non-plant PAF1 constructs (Supplementary Fig. 2b). This result identifies PAF1 as the molecular determinant governing interaction specificity. Sequence alignment analysis of the ten PAF1 proteins revealed a plant-specific N-terminal extension (NTE) in plant PAF1 orthologs (Supplementary Fig. 3a). To establish evolutionary conservation, we selected three phylogenetically pivotal plant models spanning terrestrial adaptation: the unicellular alga Chlamydomonas reinhardtii (basal lineage), the bryophyte Physcomitrium patens (transition to land), and the angiosperm Arabidopsis thaliana. Pull-down and co-immunoprecipitation assays in all three systems confirmed endogenous SKIP-PAF1 complex formation, while N-terminal deletion PAF1 mutants completely abolished interaction (Fig. 1d–f). These complementary approaches demonstrate that the N-terminal domain extension—a synapomorphic trait acquired during plant evolution—serves as the molecular linchpin enabling assembly of the PAF1c-SKIP complex, a key innovation in plantae.

Plant PAF1 NTE mediates SKIP interaction and condensation

We next performed AlphaFold modeling of representative PAF1-SKIP pairs and found that, although the interaction could be predicted, the models did not resolve a single rigid, high-confidence binding interface, consistent with a fuzzy mode of association (Supplementary Fig. 3b−g). Consistent with this, structural predictions revealed that the evolutionarily extended N-terminal domains of plant PAF1 homologs exhibit high intrinsic disorder propensity, even though the NTE itself shows low primary-sequence conservation across plant lineages (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). Given the established role of intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) in driving liquid-liquid phase separation51, we investigated whether this structural feature enables PAF1 phase separation in planta. Initial observations in Arabidopsis demonstrated that ELF7/AtPAF1 forms nuclear condensations in root tip cells (Fig. 2a), exhibiting hallmark liquid-like properties through fluorescence recovery after photobleaching assays (Fig. 2b, c). Crucially, this phase separation capacity proved strictly dependent on the NTE (IDR of PAF1), as deletion mutants (ELF7ΔNTE/AtPAF1ΔNTE) failed to form condensates (Fig. 2d, j). Fluorescence observation and immunoblot analysis showed that deletion of the NTE reduced AtPAF1/ELF7 accumulation in stable Arabidopsis lines (Figs. 1f and 2d). To exclude the possibility that the loss of condensates reflects reduced protein abundance, we transiently expressed the corresponding constructs in Nicotiana benthamiana. Full-length ELF7/AtPAF1-GFP formed clear nuclear condensates, whereas ELF7ΔNTE/AtPAF1ΔNTE-GFP remained diffuse. Importantly, further increasing ELF7ΔNTE/AtPAF1ΔNTE-GFP expression using the Tobacco mosaic virus (TMV) Ω translational enhancer elevated its protein abundance52, but the ELF7ΔNTE/AtPAF1ΔNTE variant still showed a diffuse nuclear distribution and failed to form condensates (Fig. 2e, f). Evolutionary analysis of 155 plant PAF1 sequences revealed significant proline enrichment within the NTE (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Data 2), a residue class known to amplify IDR-driven phase separation51. To test the functional significance of these prolines, we systematically substituted all 53 NTE prolines with alanines in ELF7/AtPAF1. The engineered variant (ELF7N(P-A)/AtPAF1N(P-A)) lost both AtSKIP binding ability (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b) and condensate formation capacity (Fig. 2h, j), establishing proline-mediated conformational flexibility as essential for dual functionality. These findings mechanistically link IDR-driven phase separation to the evolutionary innovation of PAF1c-SKIP complex formation in plants.

Functional complementation experiments demonstrated that heterologous IDRs could fully substitute the native N-terminal domain of ELF7/AtPAF1. Hybrid constructs incorporating IDRs from Arabidopsis FCA or human FUS (FCAIDR-ELF7ΔNTE/AtPAF1ΔNTE or FUSIDR-ELF7ΔNTE/AtPAF1ΔNTE) not only restored AtSKIP binding in both yeast two-hybrid and FRET assays (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b), but also reconstituted nuclear condensate formation in Arabidopsis root tip cells (Fig. 2i, j). These chimeric proteins exhibited liquid-like phase separation dynamics indistinguishable from wild-type ELF7/AtPAF1 (Fig. 2k–m). This IDR substitution is consistent with the AtPAF1 NTE; AtPAF1 NTE, FCA IDR, and FUS IDR share key low-complexity IDR “molecular grammar” features (Supplementary Fig. 6). These results conclusively established that phase separation competence and interaction specificity depend on general IDR biophysical properties rather than sequence conservation.

Evolutionary conservation of this mechanism was observed in P. patens, where endogenous PpPAF1 formed nuclear condensates (Supplementary Fig. 7a–c). Deletion of PpPAF1’s N-terminal domain abolished phase separation (Supplementary Fig. 7d, e), while replacement with human FUS’s IDR (FUSIDR-PpPAF1ΔNTE) fully restored liquid-liquid phase separation capacity (Supplementary Fig. 7d–g), mirroring the findings in Arabidopsis.

Technical challenges posed by chloroplast autofluorescence in C. reinhardtii necessitated in vitro validation. Recombinant CrPAF1-GFP purified from bacterial systems formed dynamic, liquid-like condensates under the tested conditions, showing spherical droplet morphology (Supplementary Fig. 7h), rapid fluorescence recovery post-bleaching (Supplementary Fig. 7i, j), and spontaneous droplet fusion (Supplementary Fig. 7k). By contrast, N-terminal truncation (CrPAF1ΔNTE) failed to form droplets under the same conditions (Supplementary Fig. 7h), supporting an evolutionarily conserved requirement for PAF1 NTE-mediated condensate formation across plant lineages. These results collectively establish that plant PAF1 proteins have co-opted IDR-driven phase separation as a universal mechanism.

PAF1c and SKIP promote cell division across plant lineages

Functional analyses of PAF1c and SKIP in the three aforementioned evolutionarily divergent plant models revealed conserved defects in cell proliferation following disruption of PAF1c or SKIP. In Arabidopsis, loss-of-function mutants for ELF7/AtPAF1 or AtSKIP exhibited developmental defects characterized by reduced true leaf size and shortened roots (Fig. 3a). Quantitative cellular analysis demonstrated significant reductions in total cell numbers within first true leaves and root cortical tissues compared to wild-type plants (Fig. 3b–d). Further analysis revealed reduced meristem size and significantly decreased cell numbers in both shoot apical meristem (SAM) and root apical meristem (RAM, exemplified by cortex meristem) in elf7-3 or atskip-1 mutants compared to wild-type plants (Fig. 3e–g). These proliferative defects were fully rescued through genetic complementation with native ELF7/AtPAF1 or AtSKIP transgenes into elf7-3 or atskip-1, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 8a–d), confirming the specificity of observed phenotypes. Conservation of this regulatory mechanism was evidenced by similar developmental abnormalities in mutants of other core PAF1c components, e.g., VIP4/AtLeo1 and VIP5/AtRtf1, which resembled the leaf size reduction, root length reduction and meristem cell number deficits characteristic of elf7-3 and atskip-1 (Supplementary Fig. 8e–m). The essential role of PAF1c-SKIP interaction was further investigated in P. patens and C. reinhardtii to assess functional conservation across plant phylogeny. In Physcomitrium, targeted mutagenesis via homologous recombination and CRISPR/Cas9 generated two PpPAF1 knockout lines (Supplementary Fig. 9a–f). Extensive attempts to generate PpSKIP knockout lines using three sgRNA targets at different positions within the coding region failed to yield homozygous editing plants, consistent with a strong requirement for PpSKIP function, and we therefore shifted to a knockdown approach by targeting the 3′ UTR with two more sgRNAs, and recovered two insertion alleles associated with significant transcript reduction (Supplementary Fig. 9h–k). Both pppaf1-2 and ppskip-1 mutants exhibited profound developmental defects, including delayed gametophore growth (Fig. 3h), decreased protoplast proliferative activity (Fig. 3i, j) and reduced growth of protonema (Fig. 3k, m). Quantitative analysis demonstrated a significant reduction in total cell number per leafy gametophyte (Fig. 3l, n), consistent with a conserved proliferative regulation. Genetic complementation with native PpPAF1 or PpSKIP transgenes fully restored wild-type growth patterns (Supplementary Fig. 10a–h), confirming phenotypic specificity. Milder but directionally similar defects in ppleo1 and pprtf1 mutants further supported a role for PAF1c components in proliferative regulation, with phenotypes concordant in direction but differing in magnitude across subunits (Supplementary Fig. 11a–m). These findings reveal concordant proliferative impairments upon PAF1c/SKIP perturbation across terrestrial plant lineages.

Given the essential nature of CrPAF1 and CrSKIP in C. reinhardtii viability (as evidenced by lethality in knockout attempts), we established knockdown lines using CRISPR/Cas9 (Supplementary Fig. 9l, m, p, q). Two independent CrPAF1 knockdown lines and one CrSKIP knockdown line were generated, with quantitative RT-PCR confirming significant transcript reduction (Supplementary Fig. 9o, r). Notably, the level of CrSKIP showed an obvious decreased in crskip (Supplementary Fig. 9s). Phenotypic analysis revealed severe proliferation defects, with both crpaf1-1 and crskip-1 lines exhibiting significant reductions in cell division rates compared to wild-type controls (Fig. 3o, p and Supplementary Fig. 9t–w). This growth retardation phenotype was fully rescued through genetic complementation with native CrPAF1 or CrSKIP transgenes (Supplementary Fig. 10i–v), confirming the specificity of the observed defects. Collectively, the conserved proliferative impairment observed across mutants in the three examined plant species is consistent with conserved roles of PAF1c and SKIP in supporting proliferative growth across divergent plant lineages.

Mutation in PAF1 or SKIP impairs cell cycle progression in plants

Given that reduced cell numbers and abnormal cell division in organs or tissues may reflect altered cell cycle regulation, we examined cell cycle progression in the wild type, paf1 and skip plants using phase-informative assays. Flow cytometry-based DNA-content profiling of the first pair of true leaves from 7-day-old Arabidopsis seedlings revealed genotype-dependent shifts in DNA-content distributions in elf7-3 and atskip-1 relative to Col-0 (Fig. 4a). To obtain an approximate phase-resolved comparison, we estimated phase “durations” by combining the overall cell division time with the fraction of nuclei in each DNA-content population (Schematized in Fig. 4b). As these values are inferred from organ-level DNA-content fractions, they represent relative rather than absolute phase-length measurements. Using this approach, both elf7-3 and atskip-1 showed increases in the relative durations of G1, S, and G2/M compared with Col-0 (Fig. 4c). We next introduced the CYCB1;1-GFP reporter into Col-0, elf7-3, and atskip-1 backgrounds and performed time-resolved imaging in the same Arabidopsis root-tip zone under identical settings53,54. Hourly imaging over an 8-h window showed prolonged CYCB1;1-GFP signal persistence in both mutants compared with Col-0, with quantification confirming significantly extended per-cell signal duration (Fig. 4d, e). Consistent with the observations in Arabidopsis, flow cytometry analysis of P. patens pppaf1-2 and ppskip-1 mutants also revealed altered relative phase estimates compared with wild-type plants (Fig. 4b, f). This functional conservation supports a conserved contribution of PAF1 and SKIP to cell cycle progression across terrestrial plants.

In C. reinhardtii, we synchronized cultures and collected time-resolved DNA-content profiles across multiple time points, which directly tracked phase-distribution changes over the division cycle in crpaf1-1 and crskip-1 relative to WT (Fig. 4g). Consistent with these profiles, quantitative analysis indicated increased estimated durations of G1 and the S/M transition in the mutants compared with WT (Fig. 4b, h). Notably, those cell cycle defects in crpaf1-1 and crskip-1 mutants were fully rescued by genetic complementation with native CrPAF1 or CrSKIP transgenes, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 12a, b). Owing to the unique multiple fission division cycle of C. reinhardtii, the G2 phase is undetectably short, with preparatory processes occurring in an extended G1 phase; thus, S-M transition here is functionally equivalent to S phase and G2-M transition in other eukaryotes29,55. Collectively, these results demonstrate the PAF1c-SKIP complex as an evolutionarily conserved regulator of cell cycle progression across plant lineages.

Importantly, the functional efficacy of PAF1 variants (full-length, truncated, or chimeric) correlated with their nuclear condensation ability (Fig. 5a, e). Mutants lacking condensation capacity failed to regulate cell cycle progression in both plant models (Fig. 5a–i). These results establish that PAF1 phase separation, driven by its NTE, is a fundamental mechanism enabling cell cycle regulation in plants. PAF1c-SKIP directly regulates plant-specific CDKB expression

In parallel, metaplots and heatmaps showed that CrPAF1 and CrSKIP were enriched at the TSS, gene body, and TES regions, with higher enrichment at the TSS and TES than across gene bodies, consistent with their association with the promoter-proximal, gene body and 3′-end-associated regions in Chlamydomonas (Supplementary Fig. 13d–f). ChIP-seq further revealed widespread co-occupancy of CrPAF1 and CrSKIP at shared target loci, supporting their functional association in transcriptional regulation (Supplementary Fig. 13g). Biological replicates showed high reproducibility for both RNA-seq and ChIP-seq (Supplementary Figs. 14 and 15). In P. patens, two of seven CDKBs homologs exhibited reduced expression in pppaf1-1 and ppskip-1 mutants (Fig. 6e), with concomitant binding of PpPAF1 and PpSKIP to their chromatin loci (Fig. 6f), confirming the two PpCDKB genes as direct targets of PAF1c-SKIP. This locus-specific pattern may reflect additional, PAF1-independent functions of SKIP at a subset of genes.

In Arabidopsis, expression of CDKB2;1 and CDKB2;2 was significantly reduced in elf7-3 and atskip-1 mutants compared to wild-type plants (Fig. 6g). ChIP assays confirmed binding of ELF7/AtPAF1 and AtSKIP to CDKB2;1 and CDKB2;2 loci (Fig. 6h), establishing these genes as direct targets of the PAF1c-SKIP complex. Crucially, SKIP binding to CDKB2 loci requires ELF7/AtPAF1, as SKIP failed to bind in elf7-3 mutant plants (Fig. 6i). Furthermore, both the NTE and its IDR biophysical properties in ELF7/AtPAF1 are essential for mediating PAF1c-SKIP chromatin binding and CDKB2 transcriptional activation. This was evidenced by the rescue of CDKB2 expression defects and chromatin binding in elf7-3 plants by a chimeric PAF1 (FCAIDR-ELF7ΔNTE), but not by the truncated form (ELF7ΔNTE) (Fig. 6j, k). Notably, although SKIP is also a conserved alternative splicing factor in eukaryotes, we detected no obvious abnormalities in the alternative splicing patterns of CDKB genes in skip mutants from the three examined species (Supplementary Fig. 16a–c). These results indicate that the PAF1c-SKIP module regulates CDKB expression primarily at the transcriptional level, rather than through post-transcriptional splicing control.

Together, these results demonstrate that the PAF1c-SKIP complex binds plant-specific CDKB loci and regulates their expression to control cell cycle progression and division from unicellular algae to angiosperms.

CDKB2 contributes to PAF1-SKIP-dependent proliferative regulation

To test whether reduced CDKB2 abundance contributes to the proliferation/cell-division phenotypes in paf1/skip backgrounds, we generated CRISPR/Cas9-derived cdkb2;1 cdkb2;2 allelic combinations in Arabidopsis. We obtained a strong double-mutant combination (cdkb2;1-cr1 cdkb2;2-cr1) carrying early premature-stop alleles at both loci, but did not recover double-homozygous plants, suggesting that the double-homozygous genotype is not viable under our growth conditions, supported by silique seed-abortion scoring and the failure to recover double-homozygous progeny upon genotyping (Supplementary Fig. 17a–d). We also recovered a viable combination of a premature-termination allele of CDKB2;1 (cdkb2;1-cr1) with a hypomorphic allele of CDKB2;2 (cdkb2;2-cr2) carrying an in-frame 3-bp deletion that removes a conserved amino acid (Supplementary Fig. 17b). This cdkb2;1-cr1 cdkb2;2-cr2 double mutant displayed clear proliferation/cell-division phenotypes, including shortened roots, reduced RAM meristem cell number, and decreased leaf proliferative capacity, directionally similar to those observed in elf7-3/atskip-1 mutants (Supplementary Fig. 17e–m). These genetic results support that CDKB2-type function contributes to proliferative output in Arabidopsis.

We next tested whether restoring CDKB abundance could partially rescue these defects in Chlamydomonas and Arabidopsis. In synchronized Chlamydomonas cultures, transgenic expression of CrCDKB-HA in the crpaf1-1 background partially restored time-resolved DNA-content profiles toward WT, as observed in two independent complemented lines (Supplementary Fig. 18a). In Arabidopsis, co-expression of AtCDKB2;1 and AtCDKB2;2 in the elf7-3 background partially alleviated RAM defects in T1 transformants across two independent lines (Supplementary Fig. 18b, c), Together, these genetic and complementation analyses support that CDKB2 downregulation contributes in part to the proliferation/cell-cycle phenotypes, while the partial rescue indicates additional CDKB-independent functions of PAF1c-SKIP.

To extend the co-expression observations above and decouple condensate formation from native NTE sequence features, we implemented a CRY2PHR-based light-inducible condensation system57 and monitored CDKB2 transcript levels. Discussion

Cell cycle regulation is fundamental for control of organism life6–12. Although plants have evolved unique cell cycle mechanisms, the molecular basis of plant-specific cell cycle regulation remains poorly understood. Theoretically, kingdom-specific regulatory modules require two core elements: (i) lineage-restricted genes essential for specialized processes, and (ii) lineage-adapted molecular mechanisms regulating these genes. Functional repurposing of existing proteins through mechanisms like gain or loss of protein-protein interactions represents an evolutionarily economical strategy compared to de novo protein innovation29,58,59, with natural emergence of entirely distinct molecular mechanism being exceptionally rare58–60.

Here, we demonstrate that the extension of the N-terminal domain of the conserved eukaryotic protein PAF1 enables its physical interaction with SKIP, forming a plant-specific PAF1c-SKIP complex conserved from unicellular red algae to angiosperms but absent from other eukaryotes (Figs. 1, 8 and Supplementary Figs. 1, 2, 4). This complex binds the locus of CDKB—encoding a plant-specific and key cyclin-dependent kinase—activating its transcription in a PAF1-dependent manner across plant lineages (Fig. 6). Crucially, the PAF1c-SKIP-CDKB regulatory module originated in unicellular algae ~1.5 Ga and has been maintained throughout plant evolution. This module governs plant-specific cell cycle patterns by integrating conserved proteins into emergent functional contexts via evolutionary acquisition of PAF1’s NTE (Fig. 8). Thus, our work provides empirical evidence for the natural evolution of distinct molecular mechanisms through adaptive repurposing of conserved components and plant-lineage acquisition of an intrinsically disordered NTE, while elucidating both the molecular mechanism and deep evolutionary origin ( ~ 1.5 Ga) of plant-specific cell cycle control.Fig. 8Working model for PAF1c-SKIP-CDKB module regulating plant-specific cell cycle in plantae.Plant-specific N-terminal extension of PAF1 enables PAF1 condensation and formation of the PAF1c-SKIP complex from unicellular algae to angiosperms. It is noteworthy that the extended N-terminal domain of PAF1 exhibits intrinsically disordered features and undergoes liquid-liquid phase separation in plants (Figs. 2, 5, 7 and Supplementary Figs. 4–7). This condensation is essential for both the formation of the PAF1c-SKIP complex and its binding to CDKB chromatin, thereby regulating cell cycle progression (Figs. 2, 5, 7 and Supplementary Figs. 4, 7). Critically, the phase separation property of the IDR, rather than sequence conservation, determines PAF1’s ability to interact with SKIP and bind the CDKB locus (Figs. 2, 5 and Supplementary Figs. 4, 5, 6). These results demonstrate that natural selection favors IDRs as platforms for protein-protein interactions and selects protein condensation to enhance biochemical reaction efficiency during eukaryotic evolution.

Although our study demonstrates that the NTE of PAF1 enables the interaction between PAF1 and SKIP, and that the resulting PAF1c-SKIP complex regulates cell cycle progression by modulating CDKB expression in plants, we cannot exclude the possibility that PAF1c-SKIP interactions in non-plant species may occur independently of the PAF1 NTE. Such interactions might be driven by additional factors that are absent in yeast two-hybrid assays. Furthermore, given the partially overlapping yet distinct functions of PAF1c and SKIP in plants, both proteins likely perform roles beyond their mutual interaction. Specifically, the PAF1 NTE may recruit other proteins into the PAF1c complex in plants, while SKIP, which contains intrinsically disordered regions, has the potential to interact with other complexes and proteins in addition to PAF1c and the spliceosome. These possibilities warrant further investigation.

Methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Arabidopsis thaliana used in this study, including both wild-type and mutant lines, were of the Columbia-0 (Col-0) ecotype. The elf7-3 (SALK_019433), vip4-4 (SALK_039374), and vip5-2 (SALK_062223) mutants were obtained from the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center (ABRC). The skip-1 mutant was isolated from a T-DNA insertion library and characterized as previously described41. All mutation genotypes were confirmed by PCR and sequencing.

Seeds were surface-sterilized in 1.5% sodium hypochlorite, stratified at 4 °C for 3 days, and then plated on Murashige & Skoog (MS) medium supplemented with 1% sucrose and solidified with 0.3% agar. Seedlings were grown under long-day (LD) conditions (16 h light/8 h dark, 22 °C/18 °C, light/dark) with a light intensity of 70 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ in a Percival CU36L5 growth chamber (Percival Scientific, Perry, IA, USA) for 7 to 10 days. Adult plants were transferred to a greenhouse and maintained under LD conditions with a white light intensity of 150 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹.

Physcomitrium patens (Hedw.) Bruch & Schimp was used in this study. The Gransden wild-type strain was cultivated on BCD medium supplemented with 0.8% (w/v) agar and 0.5% (w/v) glucose under a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod at 25 °C, with a light intensity of 40 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹. Protonemal tissues were homogenized in fresh sterile water every 4–5 days and transferred onto BCDAT agar medium overlaid with cellophane for further cultivation.

Chlamydomonas reinhardtii strain CC-5325 (wall-less strain) was obtained from the Chlamydomonas Resource Center at the University of Minnesota (http://www.chlamycollection.org). CrPAF1 and CrSKIP intron1 insertion mutants were generated by gene editing using a CRISPR/Cas9 system in the CC-5325 background strain. The CC-5325 strain and all insertion mutant strains were maintained by mixotrophic growth on Tris-acetate-phosphate (TAP) medium under low light ( < 60 µmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹) at 23 °C.

Generation of PAF1 and SKIP mutants in P. patens

Protoplasts were isolated from 5-day-old protonemal tissue cultured under standard conditions. The tissue was incubated in a solution containing 1% Driselase (Sigma) dissolved in 8% (w/v) mannitol for 40 min at room temperature with gentle agitation. The resulting suspension was filtered through Miracloth to remove undigested tissue debris. Protoplasts were collected by centrifugation and washed twice with 15 mL of an 8% mannitol solution. Prior to transformation, the protoplast concentration was adjusted to 1.0 × 10⁶ cells/mL in MMM solution (0.1% MES, pH 5.6; 15 mM MgCl₂; 8% (w/v) mannitol).

For transformation, 20 µg of linearized DNA or plasmid was gently mixed with 300 µL of the protoplast suspension and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. Subsequently, 300 µL of PEG solution (0.1 M Ca(NO₃)₂, 8% (w/v) D-mannitol, 40% (w/v) PEG 6000, and 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0) was added, followed by an additional incubation for 30 min. The transformed protoplasts were washed with 1.5 mL of W5 solution (2 mM MES, pH 5.6, 5 mM KCl, 125 mM CaCl₂, 154 mM NaCl), then resuspended in 8 mL of PRMT medium (0.4% agar, 10 mM CaCl₂, 5 mM diammonium tartrate, 8% D-mannitol), and plated onto PRMB medium (0.8% agar, 10 mM CaCl₂, 5 mM diammonium tartrate, 6% D-mannitol) overlaid with cellophane. After 7 days of incubation, the cellophane was transferred to BCDAT selection medium supplemented with G418 antibiotic.

Generation of PAF1 and SKIP mutants in C. reinhardtii

Gene editing in C. reinhardtii was performed using the CRISPR/Cas9 ribonucleoprotein (RNP) system, as previously reported61. The recombinant SpCas9 protein was expressed using the pPEI-His-SUMO-SpCas9 vector and subsequently purified. Single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting CrPAF1 or CrSKIP were designed using the CRISPR RGEN Tools (http://www.rgenome.net/cas-designer/) and the CRISPR-P 2.0 platform (http://crispr.hzau.edu.cn/cgi-bin/CRISPR2/CRISPR).

To prepare a 96 bp double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) donor, complementary 96 nt sense and antisense oligonucleotides containing microhomologous arms and a 56 nt DNA sequence with three stop codons were synthesized. These oligonucleotides were annealed by heating in boiling water for 10 min, followed by gradual cooling to room temperature. The assembled RNP complex, donor DNA, and a hygromycin resistance gene expression cassette were co-transformed into wild-type CC-5325 cells by electroporation. Hygromycin-resistant colonies appeared after 5–7 days and were selected for further validation.

PAF1 phylogeny analysis and NTE definition

The amino acid sequence of A. thaliana ELF7/AtPAF1 was used as a query for a BLASTP search against the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) to identify orthologous proteins (one ortholog per species). Representative ELF7/AtPAF1 homologs were selected from Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Caenorhabditis elegans, Drosophila melanogaster, Homo sapiens, Galdieria sulphuraria, Chlamydomonas reinhardtii, Physcomitrium patens, Oryza sativa, and Arabidopsis thaliana for further analysis. The sequences were imported into MEGA11 and aligned using the MUSCLE algorithm with the following settings: Gap Open = −2.90; Gap Extend = 0.00; Hydrophobicity Multiplier = 1.20; Max Iterations = 16; Cluster Method (Iterations 1–2) = UPGMA; Cluster Method (Other Iterations) = UPGMA; Min Diag Length (Lambda) = 24; Max Memory = 2048 MB. The MUSCLE alignment was used to reconstruct a phylogenetic tree in MEGA11 using the Neighbor-Joining method. Node support was evaluated using 1000 bootstrap replicates. Amino-acid substitutions were modeled using the Poisson model, rates among sites were set to Uniform Rates, and the pattern among lineages was set to Same (Homogeneous). Gaps/missing data were treated by Partial Deletion with a site coverage cutoff of 95%.

For NTE-length quantification, we defined the NTE as the amino-acid segment N-terminal to the InterPro-annotated conserved PAF1 core domain and calculated its length for each full-length ortholog as the number of residues from the initiating methionine to the residue immediately preceding the InterPro core-domain start.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and immunoblotting analysis

A culture of 1 × 10⁷ cells of wild-type (WT) and transgenic C. reinhardtii (pCrPAF1::CrPAF1-HA/crpaf1-1 and pCrPAF1::CrPAF1ΔNTE-HA/crpaf1-1), 1.5 g of 3-day-old WT and transgenic P. patens (pPpPAF1::PpPAF1-HA/pppaf1-1 and pPpPAF1::PpPAF1ΔNTE-HA/pppaf1-1), or 1.5 g of 9-day-old WT and transgenic A. thaliana (pELF7::ELF7-GFP/elf7-3 and pELF7::ELF7ΔNTE-GFP/elf7-3) plants were harvested and ground into a fine powder using liquid nitrogen. The powdered samples were homogenized in 1.5 mL of immunoprecipitation (IP) buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl₂, 10% glycerol, 0.1% Nonidet P-40, 0.5 mM dithiothreitol, 1 mM PMSF, and protease inhibitor cocktail). After two rounds of centrifugation at 14,000 × g at 4 °C for 15 min, the resulting supernatant was incubated with 30 µL of Anti-HA Agarose or GFP-Trap Agarose at 4 °C for 1.5 h, followed by centrifugation at 3000 × g for 1 minute. The beads were then collected by centrifugation at 3000 × g for 1 minute and washed three times with 1 mL of IP buffer. The immunoprecipitate was separated by 8% SDS-PAGE, transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane, and immunoblotted using anti-HA (Clone 12CA5, Roche, Cat#11583816001, 1:2000 dilution), anti-GFP (Abcam, Cat#ab290, 1:2000 dilution) or anti-SKIP antibodies (custom polyclonal antibody, 1:1000 dilution).

Pull-down assays

For the pull-down assay, total protein extracts were prepared from transgenic complementary plants of C. reinhardtii (pCrPAF1::CrPAF1-HA/crpaf1-1 and pCrPAF1::CrPAF1ΔNTE-HA/crpaf1-1), P. patens (pPpSKIP::PpSKIP-HA/ppskip-1), and A. thaliana (pAtSKIP::AtSKIP-GFP/atskip-1) using IP buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 0.1% Nonidet P-40, 1 mM PMSF, and protease inhibitor cocktail). The following maltose-binding protein (MBP) fusion proteins were purified using Ni-NTA Agarose (Roche, Basel, Switzerland): MBP-6×His, MBP-CrSKIP-6×His, MBP-PpPAF1-6×His, MBP-PpPAF1ΔNTE-6×His, MBP-ELF7-6×His, and MBP-ELF7ΔNTE-6×His. The purified MBP-6×His-tagged proteins were then incubated with the corresponding total protein extracts at 4 °C for 1 hour with gentle rotation. The precipitates were collected by centrifugation at 2500 × g for 1 minute and washed three times with wash buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, 0.5% Nonidet P-40, 10% glycerol, 1 mM PMSF and protease inhibitor cocktail). Immunoblotting was performed using anti-HA (Clone 12CA5, Roche, Cat#11583816001, 1:2000 dilution) or anti-SKIP antibodies (custom polyclonal antibody, 1:1000 dilution).

Yeast two-hybrid assay and β-galactosidase assay

Full-length SKIP proteins from 10 representative model organisms were cloned into the pGBKT7 vector. Full-length PAF1 proteins from the same set of organisms, along with various ELF7/AtPAF1 variants (ELF7ΔNTE, ELF7ΔN70, ELF7ΔN100, ELF7ΔN130, ELF7ΔN160, ELF7ΔN170, ELF7ΔN180, ELF7N(P-A), FCAIDR-ELF7ΔNTE, FUSIDR-ELF7ΔNTE), were cloned into the pGADT7 vector. All constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing and co-transformed into the yeast strain AH109. Co-transformed yeast clones were initially grown on synthetic dropout (SD) medium lacking leucine (Leu) and tryptophan (Trp), followed by selection on SD medium lacking adenine (Ade), histidine (His), Leu, and Trp.

For β-galactosidase activity detection, yeast was grown in SD/–Leu/–Trp and shifted to YPDA to OD600 = 0.5–0.8. Cells were washed in Z buffer, and permeabilized by 3 freeze–thaw cycles. Reactions were initiated by adding ONPG (4 mg/mL in Z buffer, pH 7.0) and incubated at 30 °C until yellow; reactions were stopped with Na2CO3. After clarification (18,000 × g for 10 min), OD420 was measured and β-galactosidase units were calculated as β-Galactosidase units (Miller units) = 1000 × OD420 / (t × V × OD600), where t is reaction time and V = 0.1 × (concentration factor). Transformation procedures and yeast growth assays were performed according to the Yeast Protocols Handbook (Clontech, Mountain View, CA, USA).

Agrobacterium-mediated transient expression in Tobacco

The Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 harboring binary plasmids for the transient expression of PAF1 and SKIP proteins was cultured in YEB medium supplemented with 50 μg/mL kanamycin. Agrobacterium cells were collected by centrifugation, resuspended in infiltration buffer containing 10 mM MES, 10 mM MgCl₂ and 100 μM acetosyringone, and adjusted to OD600 = 1.0. To suppress RNA interference, the P19 plasmid was co-infiltrated. The Agrobacterium suspensions were infiltrated into leaves of Nicotiana benthamiana using a needleless syringe. Leaf tissues were observed 48 h post-infiltration using a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM 780, Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

Bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays in Tobacco

For the bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays, the full-length SKIP or PAF1 proteins from 10 representative model organisms were fused to the C-terminal (cYFP) and N-terminal (nYFP) fragments of yellow fluorescent protein (YFP), respectively. These constructs were then transformed into the Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101. The transformed Agrobacterium strains were co-infiltrated into Nicotiana benthamiana leaves along with Agrobacterium carrying the Histone 3.1-RFP construct. Fluorescence signals were observed 48 h post-infiltration using a Zeiss LSM 780 confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). The percentage of YFP-positive nuclei was calculated by dividing the number of nuclei displaying a YFP signal by the total number of nuclei displaying an RFP signal. Histone 3.1-RFP served as a transformation control.

Fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) analysis

An acceptor photobleaching assay was performed to assess the interaction between AtSKIP and various ELF7/AtPAF1 variants (ELF7ΔNTE, ELF7N(P-A), FCAIDR-ELF7ΔNTE, FUSIDR-ELF7ΔNTE). Imaging was performed on an LSM 780 confocal microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) using ZEN software. Before photobleaching, donor fluorescence (SKIP-GFP) was imaged, and a nuclear region of interest (ROI) was defined for subsequent bleaching and quantification. The acceptor (mCherry-tagged ELF7 variants) was then photobleached within ROI by repeated scanning with a 594-nm laser. A post-bleaching donor image was acquired in the same ROI. After image alignment, the fluorescence intensities within the ROI before and after bleaching were measured (with background subtraction). The FRET efficiency (EFRET) was calculated as EFRET = (IDpost - IDpre) / IDpost, where IDpre and IDpost represent the donor (GFP) intensities before and after acceptor photobleaching, respectively.

RNA extraction and RT-PCR

Plant materials from A. thaliana, P. patens, and C. reinhardtii were harvested, and total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). RNA samples were treated with RNase-free DNase I (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) to remove genomic DNA contamination. Three micrograms of RNA were used for cDNA synthesis with either oligo(dT) primers or random primers, using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using Takara SYBR Premix Ex Taq (Takara Bio, Otsu, Japan) on a QuantStudio™ 7 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). The primers used for RT-PCR and qRT-PCR are listed in Supplementary Data 4.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays

For the chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays, plant materials were fixed in PBS buffer containing 1% (v/v) formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. The crosslinking reaction was quenched by adding glycine for an additional 10 min. The fixed plant materials were ground into a fine powder using liquid nitrogen. Nuclei were isolated from the ground materials using Honda buffer (0.44 M Sucrose, 1.25% Ficoll, 2.5% Dextran T40, 20 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 10 mM MgCl₂, 0.5% Triton X-100, 5 mM DTT, 100 mM PMSF, 1× Protease inhibitor cocktail). The nuclei pellets were resuspended in 600 µL of Nuclei Lysis Buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 10 mM EDTA, 1% SDS, 1× Protease inhibitor cocktail) and sonicated using a Bioruptor (30 s ON/30 s OFF, 10 cycles) for three rounds.

For immunoprecipitation of the protein-DNA complexes, 10 µg of anti-HA or anti-GFP antibodies and Pierce™ ChIP-grade Protein A/G Magnetic Beads were added to the sonicated chromatin extract. The DNA–protein complexes were then eluted and subjected to reverse crosslinking. RNA and proteins were removed by treatment with RNase I and proteinase K, respectively. DNA was purified using acidic phenol:chloroform extraction, followed by ethanol precipitation, and used as a template for sequencing or qRT-PCR. The anti-HA antibody was used for ChIP-seq or ChIP-qPCR in C. reinhardtii and P. patens, whereas the anti-GFP antibody was used for ChIP-qPCR in A. thaliana (anti-HA, Roche, Cat#11583816001; anti-GFP, Abcam, Cat#ab290). The primers used for qRT-PCR are listed in Supplementary Data 4.

Recombinant protein expression and in vitro droplet assays

To express recombinant proteins in Escherichia coli, the full-length CrPAF1 coding sequence (CDS) and a truncated CrPAF1 CDS lacking its N-terminal extension were cloned into the pET28a vector, which includes an N-terminal His-SUMO tag. The resulting constructs were transformed into Rosetta competent cells. Protein expression was induced with 1 mM IPTG at 37 °C for 12 h, followed by incubation at 16 °C for 24 h. Cells were harvested and lysed by sonication in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 20 mM imidazole, 100 mM PMSF, and 1× protease inhibitor cocktail). After centrifugation, the supernatant was applied to a Ni-NTA affinity column and incubated at 4 °C for 2 h. The column was washed three times with wash buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 1 mM PMSF), and the bound proteins were eluted with elution buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 500 mM imidazole). The His-SUMO tag was removed by SUMO protease digestion in cleavage buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl) at room temperature for 1 h. The cleaved tag was removed using the Thermo Scientific Pierce system to obtain purified proteins.

For in vitro droplet assays, purified protein samples were diluted to a final concentration of 2 μM in 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.0) containing 150 mM NaCl, without addition of crowding agents. Reactions were assembled in a final volume of 20 μL, incubated at room temperature for 10 min, and then applied to glass slides with coverslips for imaging. Droplets were imaged using a Leica TCS SP8 microscope equipped with a 40 × oil immersion objective using 488-nm excitation and 498–530-nm emission detection. For time-lapse fusion analysis, images were acquired at 1 s per frame for 150 frames, and a fusion event was defined as two droplets contacting and merging into a single droplet; representative frames were selected for display in the figure panels.

Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) assays

Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) of PAF1-GFP condensates in A. thaliana, P. patens and C. reinhardtii was performed using a Leica TCS SP8 laser-scanning confocal microscope using a 40 × oil immersion objective. GFP was excited at 488 nm and emission was collected at 498–530 nm. Images were acquired with a frame size of 512 × 512, line averaging 1, and scan speed 600 Hz. Imaging settings (laser power and detector gain) were kept constant across samples and adjusted to avoid signal saturation. For each cell, a single nuclear condensate was selected. A circular ROI positioned within the condensate (ROI diameter ~1 μm) was photobleached using the 488-nm laser at 30% intensity for 1 iteration. Pre-bleach images were acquired, followed by post-bleach imaging at 1-s intervals for 50 s.

Fluorescence intensities were normalized to the mean pre-bleach intensity to generate recovery curves. Recovery kinetics (t½) and mobile fraction were quantified from normalized recovery curves. t½ was defined as the time required to reach I1/2 = I0 + 0.5 × (I∞ - I0) and was determined by linear interpolation between adjacent time points. Mobile fraction was calculated as (I∞ - I0)/(Ipre - I0), where Ipre is the mean pre-bleach intensity, I0 is the first post-bleach intensity, and I∞ is the plateau intensity (defined as the mean of the final 10 time points of each recovery trace).

Line-scan and condensate enrichment analyses

PAF1-GFP and SKIP-mCherry were co-expressed in Nicotiana benthamiana epidermal cells. Z-stack images were acquired by confocal microscopy at 0.5–1 μm intervals, and the resulting images were analyzed in Fiji. For line-scan analysis, fluorescence intensities in the GFP and mCherry channels were measured along a line drawn across individual condensates. For enrichment analysis, z-stacks were converted to maximum-intensity projections. Condensate regions of interest (ROIs) were defined in the GFP channel, and the corresponding mean fluorescence intensities in the mCherry channel were measured. Mean mCherry intensity was measured for each condensate ROI and for a non-punctate nucleoplasmic ROI within the same nucleus, and SKIP enrichment was calculated as the ratio of mean puncta fluorescence to mean nucleoplasmic fluorescence.

Cell synchronization in C. reinhardtii

To synchronize C. reinhardtii cells, cultures grown in TAP medium were plated onto TAP-N medium supplemented with 10% of the normal ammonium sulfate concentration. The cells were incubated at 18 °C under continuous light for 24 h, then resuspended and plated onto TAP medium and grown at 22 °C under continuous light.

Flow cytometry analysis

Leaves from A. thaliana wild-type, elf7-3 and atskip-1 plants (approximately 15 pairs of first leaves) or protonemata from 3-day-old subcultured wild-type, pppaf1-2, and ppskip-1 P. patens strains were finely chopped in ice-cold Nuclei Extraction Buffer (45 mM MgCl₂, 30 mM sodium citrate, 20 mM MOPS, 0.1% Triton X-100). After 10 min of incubation on ice to facilitate nuclei release, the homogenates were filtered through a 30-μm nylon mesh and stained with 50 µg/mL propidium iodide (Sigma-Aldrich) for DNA content analysis. For flow cytometry analysis in C. reinhardtii, synchronized cells from wild-type, crpaf1-1 and crskip-1 were fixed in ethanol:acetic acid (3:1, v/v) at 4 °C for 12 h, centrifuged, and resuspended in PBS containing RNase A. Cells were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h and subsequently stained with 500 nM Sytox Green nucleic acid stain. Flow cytometry was performed using a BD Accuri C6 instrument, and the data were analyzed with FlowJo software.

To determine the duration of each phase of the cell cycle, we first quantified the number of cell cycles in A. thaliana palisade mesophyll cells and the apical meristematic cells of P. patens over a 24-hour period. This allowed us to estimate the total duration of a complete cell cycle. Next, flow cytometry analysis was performed to assess the proportions of cells in the G1, S, and G2-M phases, using dedicated FlowJo software for DNA content-based cell cycle profiling. These proportions reflect the relative durations of each phase within the cell cycle. Finally, the absolute duration of each phase was calculated by multiplying the total cell cycle length by the corresponding relative proportion62.

RNA-seq and ChIP-seq library preparation and data analysis

Wild-type C. reinhardtii, crpaf1-1, and crskip-1 mutants for RNA-seq, as well as wild-type, PAF1-HA, and SKIP-HA strains for ChIP-seq, were cultured in TAP liquid medium under a 14 h light/10 h dark cycle at 22 °C for two generations and harvested at the late G1 phase.

For RNA-seq, total RNA was extracted and polyadenylated RNAs were enriched using oligo(dT) beads. Libraries were prepared using the VAHTS® Universal V8 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit (Illumina) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, followed by adapter ligation and PCR amplification. For ChIP-seq, immunoprecipitated DNA fragments were end-repaired, A-tailed, ligated to sequencing adapters, and amplified by PCR to generate sequencing libraries. All libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to produce 150-bp paired-end reads.

Raw reads from both RNA-seq and ChIP-seq were trimmed using Trimmomatic (v0.39)63 and aligned to the C. reinhardtii CC-4532 v6.0 genome using Bowtie2 (v2.5.4)64. PCR duplicates were removed with Picard (v2.25.5), and uniquely mapped reads were retained using SAMtools (v1.21)65.

For RNA-seq, genome-wide gene expression levels were quantified using FeatureCounts (v2.0.0)66 with parameters -t exon, -p, -C. Differentially expressed genes between mutants and WT were identified using DESeq267 with thresholds of p value < 0.05 and |log2 fold change | > 0.58. Correlations between RNA-seq biological replicates were assessed using Pearson correlation based on gene-level RPKM values.

For ChIP-seq, binding peaks were identified using HOMER (v5.1)68 with the parameters -style factor, -LP 0.05, and -fdr 0.001. Peaks detected in at least two biological replicates were retained for downstream analyses. MACS (v3.0.2)69 was applied to calculate the enrichment ratio of IP signals relative to background. The calculation and visualization of protein occupancy across transcripts were generated using computeMatrix and plotHeatmap from the deepTools package (v3.5.5)70, respectively. Read counts in 1-kb bins were first calculated using deepTools multiBamSummary, and Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated using plotCorrelation to assess the reproducibility of ChIP-seq biological replicates.

Statistics and reproducibility

Biological replicates and sample sizes are indicated in the figures, figure legends, or Source Data file. Unless otherwise stated, data are presented as mean ± s.d., with error bars indicating s.d. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10. Comparisons between two groups were performed using two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test or Welch’s t-test, as specified in the corresponding figure legends. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. No data were excluded from the analyses. The experiments were not randomized. The Investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment. Representative experiments were repeated at least three times with similar results, unless otherwise stated in the figure legends.
