Genetic polymorphisms associated with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease and cardiometabolic risk susceptibility in the Chinese Han population
Authors: Mingjia Dai, Yan Li, Jing Zhang, Yuhua Ruan, Ye Liu, Jungui Hao, Fang Ji, Xuebing Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGenetic insights into metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and its cardiometabolic subphenotypes in Asian populations remain limited. This study aimed to investigate the genetic polymorphisms associated with MASLD and its cardiometabolic subphenotypes susceptibility in a Chinese Han cohort.MethodsWe genotyped 24 candidate single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in 870 MASLD patients, previously associated with fatty liver disease or related traits in published literature, and 1165 controls without hepatic steatosis or any cardiometabolic risk factors. Subgroup analyses (obesity, glucose/lipid abnormalities, carotid plaque) were conducted using sex- and age-adjusted logistic regression and haplotype construction.ResultsThe HSD17B13rs72613567 "T/T" genotype was associated with a significantly reduced susceptibility to MASLD with carotid plaque compared to the "A/A" genotype (OR 0.33, 95% CI 0.16–0.66, P = 0.004). Additionally, the KLB rs12152703 "T/T" genotype demonstrated a protective effect against overall MASLD susceptibility (OR 0.33, 95% CI 0.13–0.81, P = 0.028), with consistent trends observed in subgroups stratified by obesity and glucose metabolism disorders. Meanwhile, GCKR rs1260326 "C/C" decreased MASLD susceptibility (OR 0.75, 95% CI 0.60–0.94, P = 0.012), while KLB rs12639940 elevated risk. Haplotype analyses further supported the roles of KLB and HSD17B13 in MASLD and its cardiometabolic subphenotypes (all P < 0.05).ConclusionsThis study demonstrated that rs72613567 in HSD17B13 might reduce the susceptibility to carotid plaque in MASLD. Moreover, KLB rs12152703 may reduce the susceptibility to MASLD and its cardiometabolic subtypes. These findings identify genes such as the HSD17B13 and KLB as key genetic determinants of MASLD and its cardiometabolic complications in Chinese Han individuals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40246-025-00834-6.Keywords: HSD17B13, KLB, Polymorphisms, Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, Cardiometabolic risk factors, Chinese Han population

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), previously termed non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), now affects nearly 38% of adults globally, emerging as the most prevalent chronic liver condition [1, 2]. In Asia, the epidemic has surged alarmingly, with recent studies reporting an ultrasound-based prevalence exceeding 30%. At the same time, the highest incidence is observed in mainland China (63·0 cases per 1000 person-years) [3]. Driven by unfavorable dietary patterns, sedentary lifestyles, and a high prevalence of metabolic comorbidities, Asia has become a hotspot for MASLD and its severe complications, including liver cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, and cardiovascular diseases [4]. Notably, MASLD is fundamentally a multisystem disorder intertwined with metabolic dysregulation, and cardiovascular events remain the leading cause of mortality among MASLD patients [5].

In addition to clinical factors [6], genetic factors also play a significant role in the susceptibility to fatty liver disease (FLD) [7]. Although genome-wide association studies (GWAS) in European populations have identified key variants such as PNPLA3 rs738409 [8], accumulating evidence highlights substantial ethnic heterogeneity. The distribution of risk/protective alleles varies markedly across global populations. For instance, the HSD17B13rs72613567 allele, recognized as a protective factor against hepatic fibrosis in Europeans [9], may exert its anti-steatohepatitis and anti-fibrotic effects in selected NAFLD patient subgroups (e.g., individuals with class ≥ 2 obesity or diabetes) [10]. Furthermore, Sun et al. demonstrated that the HSD17B13 rs72613567 variant occurs in 34% of Chinese NAFLD patients but exhibits divergent metabolic impacts [11]. Similarly, variants in KLB, a gene regulating fibroblast growth factor signaling, display population-specific allele frequencies in East Asians [12]. These disparities underscore the urgent need for race- and region-driven genetic investigations to unravel the unique pathophysiological mechanisms of MASLD in Asian populations.

Environmental, racial, and phenotypic differences may lead to genetic variations, and there are differences in diagnostic criteria and clinical features between NAFLD and MASLD [2]. In addition, the 2023 MASLD diagnostic criteria emphasize cardiovascular metabolic risk stratification [2]. However, to date, no studies have systematically explored the association between MASLD subphenotypes (e.g., obesity, glucose and lipid abnormalities, and carotid plaques) and genetic factors in this population. Therefore, we performed this study aimed at discovering the Asian MASLD population and the genetic background of cardiometabolic risk factors. After reading previous publications on NAFLD-related gene polymorphisms [13–16], we selected 24 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as candidate variants to explore their relationship with MASLD and its cardiometabolic subphenotypes susceptibility. The goal is to reveal the potential role of genetic polymorphisms in MASLD and its cardiometabolic subphenotypes while further assessing the genetic background of MASLD in Chinese Han populations. Furthermore, we performed stratified analyses to investigate whether these genetic associations differ across key cardiometabolic subphenotypes of MASLD, which may reflect distinct pathogenic pathways.

Materials and methods

Study population

A total of 2035 subjects aged 18–80 years were retrospectively enrolled from the Physical Examination Center of the Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University between October 2023 and March 2024. This case–control study involved the Chinese Han population, consisting of 870 MASLD patients (585 males and 285 females) diagnosed based on abdominal digestive system ultrasound, and 1165 controls (579 males and 586 females).

The diagnostic criteria for MASLD are based on the latest Delphi consensus [2]: confirmation of FLD (hepatic steatosis ≥ 5%) through imaging or liver biopsy; secondly, the presence of at least one of the following five metabolic-related risk factors: (1) body mass index (BMI) ≥ 25 kg/m2 (≥ 23 kg/m2 for Asians) or waist circumference (WC) > 94 cm for men/ > 80 cm for women or adjusted according to ethnicity; (2) fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 5.6 mmol/L or 2-h postprandial plasma glucose ≥ 7.8 mmol/L or glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) ≥ 5.7% or diagnosis of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) or undergoing treatment for T2DM; (3) blood pressure ≥ 130/85 mmHg or undergoing antihypertensive medication; (4) triglycerides (TG) ≥ 1.7 mmol/L or undergoing lipid-lowering medication; (5) high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) < 1.0 mmol/L for men, < 1.3 mmol/L for women, or undergoing lipid-lowering medication. Exclusion criteria were: (1) patients with hepatic steatosis < 5%; (2) those with concurrent hematologic malignancies or tumors; (3) individuals under the age of 18; (4) duplicate records or incomplete clinical data; (5) exclusion of other causes of hepatic steatosis such as long-term heavy alcohol consumption, long-term medication use, and viral hepatitis. The control group (n = 1165) consisted of individuals from the same source population who had no evidence of hepatic steatosis (FLD < 5% as determined by ultrasound) and none of the five cardiometabolic risk factors required for the MASLD diagnosis.

Definition of groups

To better elucidate the characteristics of cardiometabolic risks in MASLD patients, we further stratified the population into different subgroups. Firstly, based on the World Health Organization's definition of a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 [17], the population was divided into the MASLD with obesity group (580 cases) and the control group (non-MASLD and non-obesity, 913 cases). Secondly, within the MASLD population, those with diabetes and/or FPG > 5.6 mmol/L, and/or HbA1c > 5.7% were defined as the MASLD with abnormal glucose metabolism group (465 cases) and the control group (non-MASLD with normal blood glucose, 796 cases). Finally, based on carotid artery ultrasound reports, the population with carotid plaques was defined as the MASLD with carotid plaques group (302 cases) and the control group (non-MASLD without carotid plaques, 458 cases).

Laboratory measurements

Clinical data collected from patients included age, sex, height, weight, WC, and hip circumference. The BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. Laboratory indicators were measured under fasting conditions at the central laboratory of the affiliated hospital of Xuzhou Medical University. The measurements included aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), γ-glutamyl transferase (GGT), total bilirubin, albumin, creatinine, uric acid, TG, cholesterol, HDL-C, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), FPG, HbA1c, free T3, free T4, and thyroid stimulating hormone. Carotid artery color doppler ultrasound, thyroid color doppler ultrasound, and abdominal digestive system ultrasound were all performed by experienced radiologists.

DNA isolation and SNP genotyping

Genomic DNA was isolated from whole blood using the BioTeke DNA kit (BioTeke, Biotechnology, Wuxi, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. It was then quantified using an ultraviolet spectrophotometer. The 24 candidate SNPs were selected based on their established associations with NAFLD, non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), elevated liver enzymes, or related metabolic traits in previous GWAS and candidate-gene studies [13–16]. No novel or hypothesis-neutral markers were included. Thus, the 24 candidate SNPs were selected based on prior biological evidence and were not subject to a study-wide multiple testing correction, as is standard for hypothesis-driven candidate gene studies. The primers for polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification and extension were designed by the MassARRAY Assay Design 4.0 Software (Table S1). The steps of the PCR cycling program, shrimp alkaline phosphatase digestion, and extension were performed according to the manufacturer's protocol. Extension products were desalted and detected using matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization time-of-flight. Finally, the results were analyzed using TYPER 4.0 software (Agena Bioscience, San Diego, CA). The PCR primers for the 24 SNPs are shown in Supplementary Table S1. The loss frequency of genotyping was less than 0.1%. Deviations of SNPs from the Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) were tested in each population using Fisher's exact test. SNPs with a MAF < 0.05 and those for which primers could not be designed were excluded. Among them, rs738409 (PNPLA3), rs28929474 (SERPINA1), rs115038698 (ABCB4), and rs641738 (MBOAT7) were SNPs deviating from HWE (P < 0.05); the allele frequencies of all other SNPs reached HWE. The genotype and allele frequencies of the SNPs are listed in Table S2.

Statistical analysis

All continuous data are expressed as mean (± IQR), and categorical variables are expressed as percentages. We used the unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test (for parametric data) or the chi-square test (for categorical data) to compare the baseline characteristics of the samples between the two groups. We utilized PLINK 1.9 [18] and SNPStats software to control the quality of genotyping results and to perform association analyses. Quality control included the loss frequency of genotyping, minor allele frequency (MAF), and HWE testing. The allele frequency of each SNP in the control group was calculated, and Fisher's exact test was conducted to assess deviations from HWE. Variants with an HWE P-value greater than 0.05 were further analyzed. Five genetic models (models: recessive: AA vs (CA + CC); dominant: (CA + AA) vs CC; additive: AA vs AC vs CC; overdominant: (CC + AA) vs CA; co-dominant: AA vs CC; CA vs CC) (https://www.snpstats.net) were used to estimate the relationship between each SNP and MASLD as well as related cardiometabolic risks. For example, in the codominant model, all three genotype categories (e.g., AA, AT, TT) are compared directly, treating the heterozygous group separately. In the dominant model, the heterozygotes are grouped with the homozygous variant genotype (e.g., AT + TT vs. AA), while in the recessive model, they are grouped with the homozygous reference genotype (e.g., TT vs. AA + AT). This multi-model approach allows for a comprehensive assessment of the genetic effects. Logistic regression analysis was performed for association checks, and odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated using logistic regression models. Age and sex were adjusted as covariates to assess statistical significance accurately. Notably, all reported associations have undergone rigorous Bonferroni multiple testing correction to control for false positives. Finally, Haploview Software, version 4.2, and the Shesis software platform (http://analysis.bio-x.cn/myanalysis.php) [19] were used for linkage disequilibrium (LD) assessment as well as haplotype construction and analysis.

The public GeneMANIA database (http://genemania.org) [20] was used to analyze potential interactions between genes. For the functional assessment of SNPs, RegulomeDB (https://www.regulomedb.org/regulome-search/) [21] and Haploreg (https://pubs.broadinstitute.org/mammals/haploreg/haploreg.php) [22] version 4.2 were utilized to predict the possible roles of the selected SNPs in this study.

Results

Population characteristics

Table 1 presents the clinical and biochemical characteristics of 870 MASLD patients and 1165 control subjects. Compared to the control group, the MASLD population was older, more likely to be male, and had higher BMI, transaminase levels, creatinine, uric acid, blood lipids, blood glucose, and free T3 (P < 0.05). Additionally, MASLD patients had a higher proportion of carotid plaques (P = 0.001). There was no significant difference in HbA1c, free T4, thyroid stimulating hormone, and the proportion of thyroid nodules between the two groups. All samples were successfully genotyped for SNPs.

Association between genotypes and MASLD

Table 2 provides the genotype frequencies of SNPs associated with MASLD under five genetic models. For the KLB variant rs12152703, the frequency of the homozygous "T/T" genotype was significantly lower in MASLD patients compared to the control group (0.7 vs. 2.2%, respectively). In the codominant model, after adjusting for age and sex, the "T/T" genotype was associated with a reduced risk of MASLD compared to the "G/G" genotype (OR 0.33, 95% CI 0.13–0.81, P = 0.028). Similarly, the GCKR rs1260326 "C/C" genotype and the KLB rs7674434 "G/G" genotype were also associated with a reduced susceptibility to MASLD. Besides, the heterozygous "G/A" genotype of the KLB variant rs12639940 was more frequent in MASLD patients than in controls (51.3 vs. 46.0%, respectively). After adjusting for age and sex, the "G/A" genotype was associated with an increased susceptibility to MASLD (OR 1.26, 95% CI 1.05–1.51, P = 0.012). For the TM6SF2 SNP rs58542926, the "C/T" genotype was associated with an increased susceptibility of MASLD in all models except the log-additive model, after adjusting for age and sex (all P < 0.05). However, no evidence of association with MASLD susceptibility was found for the remaining variants in this cohort.

Association between genotypes and MASLD with obesity

The genotype frequencies of SNPs associated with MASLD with obesity are shown in Table 3. Similar to its role in MASLD, the homozygous "T/T" genotype of KLB rs12152703 was less frequent in MASLD patients with obesity compared to controls (0.5 vs. 2.3%, respectively). This genotype was associated with a reduced susceptibility of MASLD with obesity in both the codominant and recessive models after adjusting for age and sex (both P < 0.05). Additionally, the KLB rs7674434 "G/G" genotype was associated with a reduced susceptibility to MASLD with obesity. The heterozygous "C/T" genotype of the TM6SF2 variant rs58542926 was more frequent in MASLD patients with obesity than in controls (13.8 vs. 9.9%, respectively). After adjusting for age and sex, the "C/T" genotype was associated with an increased susceptibility to MASLD with obesity (OR 1.42, 95% CI 1.01–2.00, P = 0.047). For the SUGP1 SNP rs8107974, the heterozygous "T/A" genotype was more frequent in patients than in controls (18.8 vs. 14.4%, respectively). Compared to the "A/A" genotype, the "T/A" genotype was associated with an increased susceptibility of MASLD with obesity after adjusting for age and sex (OR 1.37, 95% CI 1.02–1.85, P = 0.038). No evidence of association with MASLD and obesity susceptibility was found for the remaining SNPs in this cohort.

Association between genotypes and MASLD with abnormal glucose metabolism

As shown in Table 4, several variants were associated with a reduced susceptibility of MASLD with abnormal glucose metabolism across all five genetic models. The KLB SNP rs12152703 "T/T" genotype, KLB rs7674434 "G/G" genotype, and PPARG rs17036160 "C/T" genotype were all associated with a reduced susceptibility of MASLD with abnormal glucose metabolism in multiple models after adjusting for age and sex (all P < 0.05). Apart from these, no significant association with MASLD with abnormal glucose metabolism susceptibility was found for the remaining variants in this cohort.

Association between genotypes and MASLD with carotid plaque

Table 5 showed the association between the HSD17B13 SNP rs72613567 and MASLD with carotid plaque. In the codominant model, compared to the "A/A" genotype, the "T/T" genotype of rs72613567 showed an association with a reduced susceptibility of MASLD with carotid plaque after adjusting for age and sex (OR 0.33, 95% CI 0.16–0.66, P = 0.004). No significant associations with MASLD and carotid plaque susceptibility were observed for the remaining variants in this cohort. To further clarify the relationship between SNPs and MASLD with carotid plaque, we analyzed the association between SNPs and carotid plaque in the MASLD population. The results (Fig. 1) showed that in the MASLD population, the T allele of KLB rs12137855 increased the risk of carotid plaque (OR 1.70, 95% CI 1.02–2.85, P = 0.040). Potential functional roles of the selected SNPs

Using the RegulomeDB and Haploreg databases, we assessed the potential functions of the 20 selected SNPs, with the results detailed in Supplementary Table S3. Based on the combined annotations from both online tools, all variants are predicted to have biological functions. In our cohort, SNPs associated with MASLD and cardiometabolic risks formed multiple haplotypes, some of which showed significant associations. The functional importance of these SNPs is further supported by low RegulomeDB scores and diverse functional predictions in HaploReg.

Discussion

The current study extends the investigation into the genetic background of MASLD and its cardiometabolic subphenotypes in Asian populations, providing new insights into the genetic variations contributing to the differences in the susceptibility of MASLD and its cardiometabolic subphenotypes in this population. Our genotyping data reveal that the polymorphisms of the HSD17B13, KLB, TM6SF2, GCKR, SUGP1, and PPARG genes (a total of 10 SNPs) are associated with susceptibility to MASLD and its cardiometabolic subphenotypes in the Chinese Han population. Additionally, haplotype analysis revealed that KLB haplotypes conferred significant protection against MASLD with abnormal glucose metabolism; HSD17B13 haplotypes showed differential associations with MASLD and its cardiometabolic phenotypes. Notably, these haplotype SNPs are all on the same chromosome 4. Furthermore, functional analysis revealed the potential impact of these SNPs, enhancing the importance and prospects of studying SNPs.

HSD17B13 encodes the HSD17B13 protein that belongs to the hydroxysteroid 17-β dehydrogenase family, primarily expressed in the liver, and plays roles in regulating sex hormones, fatty acid metabolism, and cholesterol biosynthesis [24]. Previous studies have demonstrated mainly that loss-of-function variants in HSD17B13 rs72613567 can protect against the progression of NAFLD from simple steatosis to NASH, hepatic fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma [9]. Currently, HSD17B13 has been identified as a retinol dehydrogenase catalyzing retinol-to-retinaldehyde conversion via lipid droplet targeting [25] and a rate-limiting enzyme in trans-retinoic acid biosynthesis during NAFLD progression [26]. Evidence suggests that vitamin A metabolites (retinaldehyde and retinoic acid) play crucial roles in hepatic mitochondrial fatty acid β-oxidation, immune regulation, inhibition of liver fibrosis, and insulin resistance [27], which may further elucidate the relationship between HSD17B13 and NAFLD. Nevertheless, the role of HSD17B13 in the pathogenesis of NAFLD/NASH remains unclear.

This study is the first to link the HSD17B13 SNP rs72613567 variant to a reduced risk of MASLD with carotid plaque in the Chinese Han population, with the "T/T" genotype showing lower risk than "A/A". Our research also found that in the MASLD population, the T allele at the rs13118664 locus, the T allele at the rs72613567 locus, and the A allele at the rs9992651 locus of the HSD17B13 gene all reduce the risk of carotid plaque. Notably, carotid plaque is an independent risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases [28]. It is also a manifestation of arterial wall lipid metabolism disorder, while atherogenic dyslipidemia is a risk factor for MASLD [29]. Additionally, a GWAS conducted by Rotroff et al. found that rare variants of HSD17B13 in T2DM patients have a specific effect on the metabolism of fenofibrate on plasma TG and HDL-C, suggesting that HSD17B13 may be related to blood lipids [30]. However, studies on HSD17B13 loss-of-function and siRNA-mediated silencing of HSD17B13 mRNA found that hepatic steatosis in subjects was unaffected, suggesting that the lack of preventive effects of HSD17B13 on NASH and related conditions is unlikely due to reduced hepatic fat storage metabolism [25]. Therefore, further research is needed to clarify the role of HSD17B13 in simple steatosis. Importantly, the HSD17B13 rs72613567 variant is highly prevalent in Chinese NAFLD patients (34%) [11], and our study confirms that the "T/T" genotype reduces carotid plaque risk in MASLD, suggesting a cardioprotective metabolic effect. Future functional studies are required to elucidate the underlying mechanisms of this protective association.

Moreover, HSD17B13 haplotypes (rs72613567, rs9992651, rs13118664) were significantly associated with risks of MASLD, MASLD with obesity, and MASLD with carotid plaque. These findings further expand the investigative spectrum of HSD17B13 genetic variants in the Asian population. Additionally, GeneMANIA analysis revealed HSD17B13 has genetic interactions with FGF20 and KL. FGF20 is a member of the FGF superfamily and primarily plays roles in the development of dopaminergic neurons, promoting hair growth, and facilitating tissue repair [31–33]. The KL gene, a homolog of KLB, has been associated with aging [34]. However, their roles in MASLD are poorly understood, necessitating further research on HSD17B13 and KLB interactions.

The KLB gene encodes a transmembrane protein that binds FGF21 and FGF19, hormones released by the liver, adipose tissue, and intestine, respectively, to regulate lipid, glucose, and bile acid metabolism [35]. Previous cohort studies have shown that the KLB rs17618244 variant is primarily associated with liver fibrosis and lobular inflammation in obese patients with FLD, but not with steatosis [36]. In contrast, our study found that KLB rs12152703 "T/T" and rs7674434 "G/G" genotypes reduced the risk of MASLD, MASLD with obesity, and MASLD with abnormal glucose metabolism, while KLB rs12639940 "G/A" increased MASLD risk. No significant role was observed for KLB rs17618244 in our population. Earlier GWAS studies identified the very classic gene PNPLA3 as associated with NAFLD [8], but in our study, PNPLA3 rs738409 deviated from HWE and was excluded due to a MAF (P < 0.05). This highlights the importance of genetic heterogeneity in population-based studies. Furthermore, discrepancies between these studies might also arise from regional differences, varying sample sizes, and the complexity of genetic and environmental interactions across different ethnic groups. Besides, inconsistent with our results, a study by Ji et al. suggested that KLB rs12152703 and rs7674434 variants are associated with an increased risk of NAFLD in obese populations [12]. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in diagnostic criteria between NAFLD and MASLD populations. Notably, our study also observed a significant association between KLB haplotypes (rs7674434, rs12639940, and rs12152703) and a reduced risk of MASLD with abnormal glucose metabolism, further strengthening the reliability of our findings.

The link between GCKR polymorphisms and NAFLD risk remains inconsistent. Gao et al. found no association between the GCKR rs1260326 polymorphism and NAFLD or coronary artery disease in northern Chinese Han patients [37]. Conversely, another study linked this polymorphism to NAFLD in elderly Chinese Han individuals [38], and Santoro et al. reported its association with NAFLD in children and adolescents [39]. Similarly, our study found the GCKR rs1260326 "C/C" genotype reduces MASLD risk. These differences may arise from variations in ethnicity, study design, and diagnostic criteria among FLD patients.

Furthermore, our study showed the TM6SF2 rs58542926 "C/T" genotype increases MASLD and MASLD with obesity risk, consistent with most previous studies [40, 41]. It is noteworthy that studies have shown rs58542926 to be in powerful LD with rs10401969 in Europeans (r2 = 0.95 and D' = 1), while TM6SF2 is the pathogenic gene within the Chr19p13.11 locus where these genes coexist [42–44]. In this study, TM6SF2 rs58542926, SUGP1 rs8107974, and rs10401969 formed three haplotypes in a strong LD block. We also identified, for the first time, that the SUGP1 rs8107974 "T/A" genotype raises MASLD with obesity risk in the Chinese Han population. Therefore, genetic variations within this region have a broad impact on human metabolism. Additionally, our study has discovered that the PPARG rs17036160 "C/T" genotype was linked to a lower risk of MASLD with abnormal glucose metabolism. This association was specific to MASLD with glucose metabolism disorders and persisted after adjustment for glycemic markers, suggesting a direct role in hepatic steatosis beyond diabetes-related pathways. PPARG regulates adipogenesis and lipid storage in hepatocytes independently of glucose homeostasis, as demonstrated in mechanistic studies [45, 46]. A Polish study noted women with the "TT" genotype had lower pre-pregnancy and birth BMIs [47], but research on this variant remains scarce.

Several limitations in this study should be noted. In our research, ultrasound was used to diagnose MASLD, as liver biopsy is difficult to obtain and invasive. Secondly, the severity of MASLD in patients was not graded. Moreover, while our cohort included adults aged 18–80 years, the mean age of MASLD patients (52.6 years) may not fully represent elderly-specific mechanisms. Future studies should include older cohorts to validate our findings in advanced MASLD stages. Lastly, a larger number of subjects should be included in further studies, and additional genetic research is still needed to evaluate further or confirm the role of gene polymorphisms in MASLD.

In summary, our study provides strong evidence that genetic polymorphisms in HSD17B13, KLB, TM6SF2, GCKR, SUGP1, and PPARG are associated with susceptibility to MASLD and its cardiometabolic subphenotypes in the Chinese Han population. These findings suggest the role of gene polymorphisms in the development of MASLD. They provide a reference for future research on the prediction of MASLD and its cardiometabolic subphenotypes based on genetic polymorphisms.
