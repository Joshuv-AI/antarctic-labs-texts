Investigating the Impact of Prolonged Exposure to Low Psychosocial Safety Climate on Serious Depressive Symptoms
Authors: Ali Afsharian, May Young Loh, Maureen Frances Dollard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrevious research has shown that the organization’s psychosocial safety climate (PSC) is crucial for protecting workers’ psychological well-being. In this study, we sought to understand how different exposure [prolonged (long-term) vs. short-term] to low levels of PSC affects new future depressive symptoms.MethodsThree waves of data from 1,160 full-time workers were used to analyze the effect of prolonged exposure to low PSC over two to three years on depressive symptoms and the emergence of new depressive symptoms, after excluding depressed cases at the baseline. Additionally, we tested the effects of short-term low PSC exposure.ResultsData were analyzed using SPSS and binomial logistic regression. Results showed that prolonged low PSC increased the risk of developing new depressive symptoms fourfold in the next three to four years (OR = 4.02, p < 0.001). Short-term low PSC exposure tripled the risk of newly developed depression (OR = 3.03, p < 0.001).ConclusionFuture research should consider prolonged effects of exposure to low PSC. To prevent future depressive symptoms, it is important to build and improve PSC in the organizations to prevent the accumulative deleterious effects on workers'’ psychological health.

Introduction

Depression is the most prevalent psychiatric condition in modern society and a leading cause of disability, significantly contributing to the global burden of diseases [1]. A recent meta-analysis estimated that depression is prevalent in 28% of the global general population [2]. Depression not only affects individuals but also has significant economic implications. In Australia alone, the cost of depression-related exits from the workforce were estimated to increase from AU$1,062 million in 2015 to AU$1,539 million by 2030 [3]. Working conditions and job-related stressors, such as work overload, time pressure, low job control, and low social support, are associated with the development of depressive symptoms [4] and clinically diagnosed depression [5]. Therefore, eliminating or managing these stressors are necessary to prevent depression [[6], [7], [8]].

To effectively prevent work stressors, it is important to consider their source. Primary intervention targeting upstream factors is considered the most efficient approach by work health and safety and public health practitioners. In the current study, we focused on an influential upstream workplace factor, psychosocial safety climate [9], and its preventive function on future depression. The organisation’s psychosocial safety climate (PSC) reflects the safety system for worker psychological health and is defined as employees’ perception of their workplace policies, practices, and procedures concerning employee psychological health and safety. PSC has been proposed as an upstream factor of other psychosocial risks, extending other work design models such as the job demands-resources (JD-R) model [10]. PSC theory emphasizes the importance of organizational policies and managerial actions aimed at protecting workers’ psychological health. A related aspect of organizational climate, safety climate, is highly focused on workplace physical safety, ergonomics, and environmental hazards. PSC specifically underscores the importance of the psychosocial safety system in addressing and preventing psychosocial risk factors at work, through shaping job design and social relations at work. There are four principles of PSC: management commitment, where commitment is reflected in actions taken by managers to prevent work-related stress and psychosocial risks; management priority for psychological health, which reflects management values and priority given to employee psychological health over productivity; organizational communication, which highlights the importance of open and effective communication within the organization about risky psychosocial factors; and organizational participation, which refers to whether the organization encourages personnel from all organization levels and stakeholders, such as managers, work health and safety representatives, worker unions, and employees, to participate in preventing and resolving psychosocial risks to psychological health. We specifically focus on PSC as empirical evidence has found that PSC is a better predictor of worker mental health compared to safety climate [11,12]. In moderation processes, PSC as an upstream resources is likely able to eliminate the detrimental impact of psychosocial job demands better than other psychosocial job resources, such as job control and rewards [13]. Exposure to low PSC is linked to increased risks of mental health issues and reduced work outcomes such as job engagement. Thus, PSC can serve as a modifiable target for organizations to manage and reduce psychosocial risks and implement changes in line with workplace mental health frameworks [9,[13], [14], [15]].

In support of the functions of PSC, previous cross-sectional and prospective evidence shows that, when PSC is low, the likelihood of psychosocial hazards [9,13], psychological distress [15], emotional exhaustion [16,17], and depression [16] increases. In their meta-analysis using 79 published and unpublished studies with 52 independent samples (over 200,000 workers), Zadow et al confirmed a negative relationship between PSC and depression at r = -0.20 [17]—hence, establishing high levels of PSC in working environments could serve as an effective preventive measure in mitigating the onset and progression of depressive symptoms [18].

A gap in the current literature, however, is the uncertainty about the potential impact of long-term exposure to a low PSC, which can be viewed as a stressor. Most research to date has only assessed the effect of PSC from one point in time. According to the work stress reaction model, stressors elicit a stress response which likely becomes more intense with prolonged exposure [19]. Investigations found that repeated exposure to stressors such as job strain increased the risk associated with depression in several studies [[20], [21], [22], [23]]. In a review and meta-analysis, Ford et al investigated the issue of cumulative exposure to stressors and found that synchronous stressor–strain effects increased more over time when stressors were prolonged (i.e., stable), suggesting the effects built up through continuous exposure [24]. However, in their study, the synchronous outcomes might be overstated due to prolonged exposure overlapping with future strain effects and because depressed employees may view their work negatively, creating a cycle of vulnerability [23]. We aim to address the limitation in this study with an exemplar of prolonged exposure to low PSC (i.e., stressor) and depression (i.e., strain). Fig. 1 shows that short-term low PSC exposure causes a stress reaction which is associated with new clinical depression, whereas prolonged exposure increases the risk of new clinical depression even further. Research on cumulative exposures to psychosocial risks at work suggest that prolonged exposure to poor work design significantly influences employee health outcomes. Notably, low-control and low psychosocial job demand (passive work) settings are associated with all-cause mortality [25]. Furthermore, cumulative exposure to work-related stressors is related to cardiovascular disease over and above the effects of negative affectivity [26]. In a meta-analysis of cumulative exposure to job strain and coronary heart disease (CHD), 26 prospective cohort studies were analyzed and suggested that additional observational research regarding the adverse effects of job strain is unlikely to provide new insights, thereby highlighting the need for enhanced study designs or alternative research methodologies [27]. This body of research underscores a relationship between psychosocial work exposures thereby emphasizing the imperative for preventive policies to improve the psychosocial work environment [28]. Focusing on exposure to prolonged PSC could provide insights into preventing exposure to psychosocial risks and in turn depression. Despite the established evidence that PSC (prospectively) is associated with (subsequent) poor mental health [18,29,30], all studies have assessed PSC at a single time point which could represent a fleeting or short-term exposure to PSC [31]. None have examined the effects of prolonged or long-term exposure to low PSC levels. For example, Zadow et al found low PSC led to new major depression symptoms within one year [16] but this study did not test for long-term exposure to low PSC. There is a genuine concern that the harmful effects of prolonged exposure to consistently low PSC levels might be underestimated due to this methodological limitation, considering that the negative impact of work stressors likely increases with exposure duration [19]. While short-term exposure may elicit immediate stress reactions, prolonged exposure has the potential to engender cumulative psychological pressure. A prolonged context of low PSC is arguably a more serious problem, and its impact is likely amplified over time.

Therefore, to address the gap, the present prospective study aims to examine the impact of prolonged exposure to low PSC at two consecutive time points [Time 1 to 2 (T1–T2)] on the development of depressive symptoms after three to four years (T3). Aligned with the stress reaction model, we hypothesized that prolonged exposure to low PSC results in increased levels of new depression, while short-term exposure has a weaker effect.

Moreover, in their study, Ford et al pointed out that their meta-analysis is limited by the fact that it does not account for the possibility that participants at the outset of research might already be experiencing prolonged exposure to the stressors being assessed and strain due to previous exposure and recommended that researchers assess past exposures to stressors [24]. We, therefore, also tested the effects of low PSC on newly developed depressive symptoms by excluding those who reported depression in the past. This helped isolate the effect of prior depressive symptoms and improved the study’s reliability by distinguishing new exposures from ongoing conditions.H1Employees who experience prolonged exposure to low PSC in their workplace (T1 to T2) are more likely to experience new clinical depressive symptoms over time (T3).H2The effects of prolonged low PSC exposure are higher than short-term low PSC exposure on new clinical depressive symptoms over time.

Materials and methods

Sample

This longitudinal study explored data collected by the Australian Workplace Barometer, an Australian population-based workplace panel study (in 2009/10[T1], 2011[T2], and 2014/15[T3]) [32], allowing sufficient time to observe meaningful change in PSC and depressive symptoms. Participants were randomly recruited from the White Pages, a local residential telephone directory. They were initially contacted through a letter of introduction and were subsequently interviewed via a computer-assisted telephone interviewing technique. The research followed the inclusion of employed adults (+18), as well as full-time and permanent workers. Participants with missing baseline data on PSC or depression were excluded. The selected participants aged 18 to 65 years, were mostly females, married and held diplomas or bachelor’s degrees and worked in various occupations and industries across six Australian states and territories. The study sample had a mean age of 46.44 years, with 45% male and 55% female. Most were married (64%) and education levels varied, with 36% holding a bachelor’s degree or higher. Income varied, with 20% earning up to $30,000 and 15% earning over $100,000. Prolonged low psychosocial states were noted in 21% of participants and 9% showed clinically depressive symptoms (See Table 1). Ethical approval was obtained from the authors’ university and all the participants declared their consent to participate in this study. The final sample comprises 1,160 respondents, of which 827 workers were working in the same organizations across the period of data collection (See Fig. 2). A power analysis was conducted using G∗Power Calculator for a binomial logistic regression with an effect size of 3.12 (odds ratio [OR] from Zadow et al [16]). The sample size needed to achieve a power of 0.95 at p = 0.05 was 295. The sample size included in this prospective study is therefore adequate. Measures

All measurements were measured concurrently at each assessment wave (T1, T2, and T3).

Psychosocial safety climate was assessed using the PSC-12 scale [33]. Example items are as follows: “senior management considers employee mental health to be as important as productivity” and “there is good communication here about mental safety issues which affect me”. Items are scored on a 5-point Likert response format from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The total score was then dichotomized with a cut-off point of 37 based on the PSC risk benchmarks established in the literature. Studies have linked low PSC to higher risks of psychological distress and depression, confirming that a PSC score ≤37 signals inadequate system support to prevent employee mental ill-health [14].

Prolonged low PSC (i.e., PSCT1–T2), was defined as PSC ≤37 at both T1 and T2 and coded as 1. All other combinations of PSC were coded as 0 (PSCT1Low, PSCT2High; PSCT1High, PSCT2Low, PSCT1High, and PSCT2High).

Short-term low PSC was defined as a low PSC score (PSC ≤37) at T2 (coded as 1). Those with PSC scores above 37 at T2 were coded as 0.

Depressive symptoms. The nine item patient health questionnaire (PHQ-9) [34] was used to assess the severity of depressive symptoms. The items assess the frequency of the symptoms experienced by the patient over the course of two weeks. Items are scored on a scale of 0 to 3 and summed, higher scores indicating greater severity with a score ≥10 as a threshold for depression. We therefore dichotomized the score into <10 = 0 and ≥10 = 1.

Newly developed depressive symptoms

To examine the new low PSC variable, we excluded all people with low PSC at T1 (≤37). For the newly developed depressive symptoms variable, we excluded people reporting PHQ over 10 at T1.

Statistical analysis

Data were prepared and analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences. To evaluate the potential bias resulting from participant attrition, we conducted a comparative analysis of individuals who completed all waves of data collection with those who disengaged after the first or second wave on their levels of PSC and depression using a t test. A Pearson correlation was conducted to gain insights into correlations between all the variables.

Main analysis

For hypothesis testing, we used binomial logistic regression. To test Hypothesis 1, in Model 1, we regressed depressive symptoms at T3 on prolonged low PSC at T1 and T2 (H1). For Hypothesis 2, we regressed depressive symptoms at T3 on short-term low PSC T2 (H2). We then ran a model for individuals who worked in the same organization over time (Model 2). Next, we specifically excluded those with high PHQ at T1 to explore the prediction of new depressive symptoms (Model 3). Finally, we adjusted this model and controlled for age, gender, and income (Model 3a). These potential confounders have been identified in previous research to be associated with depressive symptoms and age and gender with PSC. Because a few papers have been published from the datasets, hence to account for alpha inflation, we employed a Bonferroni correction [35]. We adjusted the p value by a factor of three due to the presence of three published studies [8,14,16] (See Supplementary Table 3), and the conclusions remained consistent across these analyses.

To check the robustness of the results, we also tested the hypotheses with different cut-off points. Substantial meta-analyses have discussed the issue of the cut-off point of 10 for PHQ-9. For example, Manea et al (2015) found large heterogeneity in the studies using PHQ >10 and suggested that while the cut-off at 10 performed well in primary care settings, the specificity and sensitivity is lower in the population with substantial heterogeneity [36]. In their meta-analysis, the most reported alternative cut-off points were 8, 9, 11, and 12. Moreover, different cut-off points have been proposed for specific populations and settings [37]. Based on research by Rose et al, it is advisable to reconsider using cut-off points derived from clinical populations as they may yield low numbers of depressed cases [38]. Their suggested approach involves considering a lower cut-off point, potentially as low as 5 for general populations. Hence, we ran additional analysis with the cut-off points of 5 and 9 to check the robustness of the results. We also used an alternative cut-off point of 26 for PSC [14], which refers to very low PSC. Noteworthily, the number of respondents exposed to this level of prolonged low PSC over T1 and T2 is small, with 5% or 58 individuals.

Additional analysis

Acknowledging that dichotomization will lose a certain level of statistical power and information of a construct [39], and there is potential impact of different time intervals [40], we conducted additional statistical testing with a continuous time structural equation modeling (CTSEM) [41]. CTSEM can be used to account for time interval effects and other unexplained heterogeneity. We conducted a fixed-effect CTSEM in the R statistical platform to assess the between-person relationship of the change in PSC and the future change in depression and vice versa (Appendix 3).

We also conducted an additional test on reversed effects of depression on future PSC. A similar procedure as the main analysis was adopted by regressing low PSC at T3 on prolonged serious depressive symptoms from T1 to T2 and short-term depressive symptoms at T2, respectively (Appendix 4). To explore the new experience of low PSC, we eliminated those exposed to low PSC levels (≤37) at T1 and then analyzed the relationship between prolonged depressive symptoms and future low PSC over time. Prolonged depression was defined as scores greater than or equal to 10 on the PHQ-9 from T1 to T2. Short-term depression was operationally defined as a PHQ score of 10 or higher at T2 (coded = 1), individuals with scores below 10 were coded as 0.

Results

Demographic information was shown in Table 1. The 1,160 participants had an average age of 46.44 years; 55% of the participants were female, 36% had a bachelor's degree or higher, 64% were married, and income distribution varied with most earning between $30,000 to $100,000 per year (see Table 1). The dropout analysis showed that there were slight differences between individuals who dropped out at T1 and the original sample but not for those who dropped out at T2. Individuals who dropped out at T1 reported both higher PSC and higher depressive symptoms, indicating no systematic trend one way or the other or a healthy worker effect for the sample (Appendix 1).

For the entire sample (n = 1160), PSC T1 is positively related to PSC T2 and T3, r = 0.59 (p < 0.01) and 0.45 (p < 0.01), respectively, and negatively related to depression (r range from -0.08 to -0.26, p < 0.01). This implies that individuals who have lower levels of PSC are more likely to experience depression over time (Appendix 2). Regarding short-term PSC effects, low PSCT2 also demonstrates a negative relationship with future depressive symptoms. In Model 1, the beta coefficient of short-term PSC at T2 on depression at T3 is 1.03 (SE = 0.25) with an OR of 2.81 (95% CI = 1.74; 4.56, p < 0.001). This relationship is stronger in the adjusted Model 2 where all participants were in the same organization across time, B = 1.36 [SE = 0.29, OR = 3.89 (95% CI = 2.18; 6.93)]. Models 3 and 3a show the ORs of 2.94 and 3.03, respectively, both significant at p < 0.001. Based on the final model (Model 3a), short-term low PSC exposure tripled the risk of newly developed depression compared to those with high PSC (>37) exposure at T2.

Additional analysis using CTSEM confirmed the reciprocal relationship between PSC and depression (Appendix 3). The results confirmed that a change in PSC is associated with subsequent change in depression with estimates = -0.0129 (95% CI = -0.0219; -0.0039) for the full sample; for a sub-sample, those who remained in the same organization, estimates were = -0.0170 (95% CI = -0.0268; -0.0070). The reversed effect was also supported with a change in depression negatively associated with a future change in PSC, estimates = -0.1649 (95% CI = -0.2365; -0.0927) for the full sample, and for those who remained in the same organization, estimates = -.1845 (95% CI = -0.2636; -0.1048).

Reversed causal analysis using binomial linear regression provides additional insights (Appendix 4). The results in the final model showed that the group of prolonged depression respondents was 6.54 times more likely to report low PSC in the future. However, this is no longer significant after applying a Bonferroni correction with a p value of 0.046 × 3 = 0.138. Comparing the prolonged versus short-term depressive effects, the odds for short-term depressive effects predicting future low PSC were lower than the prolonged depressive effects (OR = 4.76) but remains significant after correcting with the Bonferroni approach (p = 0.003 × 3 = 0.009) (See Appendix 4). These results suggested that, while the short-term depressive effects on PSC corroborate with the reciprocal results in CTSEM, prolonged cumulative depressive symptoms did not predict future low PSC.

Discussion

The current study underscores the importance of considering the long-term effects of exposure to low PSC on employees’ mental well-being. Repeated exposure to poor PSCT1–T2 (≤37) is strongly associated with an increased risk of new depression emerging across a four-to-five-year period. Individuals exposed to prolonged low PSC were four times more likely to experience new depressive symptoms than other combinations. Those exposed to short-term low PSC effects, were three times more likely to experience new serious depressive symptoms than those with high PSC. For short-term exposure, the effect is already bad, but if an organization remains reluctant to improve the situation such that exposure to low PSC is year on year, the likelihood of new serios depressive symptoms is even higher (worse). Additional analyses using different cut-off points supported the main results. In line with previous studies, our findings again support the proposition and evidence that PSC is a leading indicator of worker psychological health, particularly depression [8,14,16]. We further expanded the theoretical understanding on PSC and work stressors literature by providing empirical evidence for the argument on cumulative versus lagged effects of stressor on strain [24].

Tests on a reciprocal relationship between PSC and depression using CTSEM and reversed effects of long-term versus short-term depressive symptoms on future low PSC provide additional knowledge to stressor–strain literature. In line with existing evidence [7], we found strain–stressor effect (i.e., the reversed effect) is stronger than the stressor–strain effect in the CTSEM model. However, more interestingly, while individuals with depressive symptoms seem to imply a more negative PSC in a short-term period, this reversed effect was not evident when considering prolonged depressive symptoms. In sum, these results are in line with previous research that shows prolonged exposure to stressors can lead to poor mental health outcomes, more severe than for short-term exposure, which is captured in common longitudinal analysis [24], as well as ruling out the potential reversed effects of severe depression on stressors over the long term. Integrating these findings into theoretical models can enhance our comprehension of the origins and progression of work-related depression, providing a more nuanced perspective on the contribution of upstream stressors such as PSC to mental health outcomes.

Practically, investing in interventions aimed at improving the workplace climate could have a substantial positive impact on employees’ well-being and decrease the risk of depressive symptoms, as suggested by the study. Organizations should enhance PSC in the workplace through strategies like regular training, open communication, and supportive management practices. Periodic assessments of employee mental health can identify individuals at risk for depression, using surveys to create a secure environment for voicing concerns. Given the link between low PSC and depression risk, companies should implement targeted intervention programs and provide tailored mental health resources.

Ongoing support is crucial for preventing depressive symptoms, and leadership training should focus on recognizing low PSC indicators. Promoting work-life balance with flexible hours and remote work can boost employee satisfaction and PSC. Additionally, recognizing and rewarding PSC improvements can enhance engagement and foster a positive culture, with interventions tailored to diverse employee needs across demographics.

In public health and policy formulation, acknowledging both the effects of immediate versus long-term exposure can inform the development of programs and policies aimed at preventing and alleviating mental health issues. For instance, policies designed to mitigate prolonged stressors in the environment, such as persistent job insecurity, work-related stress, and inflexible work conditions for long-term caregiving responsibilities, by building a strong PSC, could significantly reduce the incidence and severity of depression in the population. Thus, a comprehensive understanding of the interplay between workplace PSC and mental health outcomes can guide organizations in developing effective strategies to foster a healthier and safer work environment, ultimately mitigating the risk of depression among employees.

Knowledge gaps, limitations, and future research directions

Despite the increasing attention given to PSC and its implications for workers’ psychological well-being, there remain notable gaps in knowledge that warrant further investigation. Specifically, the potential mechanisms by which prolonged exposure to low PSC contribute to the development of depressive symptoms is yet to be fully elucidated. Previous studies on PSC and workplace mental health have highlighted the link between PSC and more proximal psychosocial risks, such as supervisor support as well as its function as a resource ecology [13,17,18] and how these in turn affecting workers. Other papers have suggested that PSC protects worker mental health through fulfilling individual needs of competence, autonomy, and sense of belonging [see review, 20]. However, these are not captured in the current study. Future research could concentrate on identifying specific workplace and personal factors that moderate or mediate this relationship, with a view to informing targeted interventions aimed at enhancing PSC and reducing the risk of depression among employees.

Despite our efforts to implement demographic controls aimed at mitigating confounding effects and analyzed participants who stay in the same organization over time, we were unable to address all individual or contextual factors (e.g., personality traits, life events, broader social conditions). This limitation hinders our capacity to control for unobserved variables effectively. For a better understanding of the relationship between low PSC exposure and the risk of developing depressive symptoms, thus, implementing experimental or quasi-experimental methods to investigate non–work-related stressors could also be beneficial.

Intervention research using before-and-after measures and intervention versus control groups would also provide stronger evidence of causation. Although the longitudinal design of our study facilitates investigation of changes in PSC and depressive symptoms, there are some assumptions that should be recognized. We assumed a steady state in PSC and mood stability in depressive levels between timepoints but there may be variations. For example, an individual could experience a remission in depressive symptoms or changes in PSC between our assessed time points. Future research could use pulse data to explore these possible variations.

The credibility of research findings heavily depends on the quality and relevance of evidence. Nonetheless, using a single assessment tool to measure depression (PHQ-9) may not fully capture the spectrum of depressive symptoms or clinical severity, potentially limiting the accuracy of the categorization. The PHQ-9 is widely used to assess depressive symptoms over the past two weeks but has limitations due to fluctuating symptoms. High scores may indicate different episodes, risking misclassification. Future research should include measures for symptom duration and clinical interviews to differentiate between transient and recurring symptoms.

Conclusion

Evidence of a stronger association of prolonged versus short-term exposure to low PSC levels with new depressive symptoms extends empirical support for PSC theory. The results imply that, at high levels, PSC could play a significant role in preventing depressive symptoms and supports the notion of targeting upstream factors such as PSC as an efficient intervention strategy. The distinction between the effects of short-term and long-term exposure of PSC on depressive symptoms has significant theoretical and practical implications, requiring an extension of current models and approaches to the treatment and prevention of mental health issues. Therefore, worksite interventions should aim to target PSC as a useful starting point that could improve employee well-being and reduce the risk of depressive symptoms and a downward spiral.
