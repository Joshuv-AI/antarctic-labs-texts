Mycoflora, mycotoxin exposure and cancer risk assessment in Tuo-Zaafi, a fermented cereal meal consumed in northern Ghana
Authors: Nii Korley Kortei, Amos Adugbire Aduko, Lydia Quansah, Felix Kwashie Madilo, Clement Okraku Tettey, Celestine Boatemaah Osei, Crossby Osei Tutu, Nana Oye Pobi, Nelson Opoku, Seidu A Richard, George Tawia Odamtten
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTuo zaafi, a traditional dish prepared from cereals and often accompanied by dark green vegetable soup, is a revered dish well known for its medicinal and nutritional attributes. The cereals are usually prone to mycotoxin contamination. This study aimed to determine fungal diversity, mycotoxin contamination (Ochratoxin A, Aflatoxins, Fumonisins), and consumer risk associated with tuo-zaafi in the northern regions of Ghana. Fungi were identified using standard mycology protocols, and a high-performance liquid chromatography-fluorescence detector (HPLC-FLD) was used to analyze mycotoxin levels in the samples. Cancer risk assessments were done using deterministic models proposed by a Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Additives. The fungal counts were between the ranges of 3.19 and 4.27 log10 CFU/g. Some species of the genera Aspergillus, Fusarium, Trichoderma, Penicillium, Mucor, Rhizopus, Cladosporium, Alternaria, and Saccharomyces contaminated the food samples. Additionally, Aflatoxins (13.05–24.51 µg/kg) and Fumonisins (101.59–126.18 µg/kg) exceeded regulatory limits set by the Ghana Standards Authority (GSA) and the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) in a majority of samples. Risk assessment based on Margin of Exposure (MOE) calculations revealed values below 10,000, indicating a significant carcinogenic public health concern. These findings highlight the urgent need for regulatory interventions and public awareness campaigns to mitigate mycotoxin exposure.Keywords: Tuo-zaafi, Cereals, Ochratoxin A, Aflatoxins, Fumonisin, Fungi, Ghana

Introduction

The consumption of traditional foods holds significant cultural and nutritional value across many societies, exemplifying the rich culinary heritage deeply embedded in local traditions [1], [2]. In Africa, a wide variety of food is consumed, particularly in Ghana. A fermented cereal meal known as Tuo-zaafi (T.Z) is one of the most beloved dishes in northern Ghana.

Tuo-zaafi is a staple food, cherished for its wholesome ingredients and communal significance. Prepared from fermented maize and/or millet, this dish satisfies culinary preferences. It serves as a vital source of sustenance for communities across various regions, including the Northern, Ashanti, Bono East, and Upper East regions, regarding the quantity consumed as well as its frequency [3].

The most often consumed staple in Ghana and throughout Africa is maize (Adu et al., 2021). It serves as the primary ingredient for various traditional foods, including Ga and Fante Kenkey (fermented maize dampling), Banku, Tuo-zaafi, and Hausa Koko (porridge), all of which are prepared using fermented maize dough [4]. The main ingredients normally used in the preparation of Tuo-zaafi, are either maize or millet, depending on the consumer's choice [5].

Amidst its cultural importance, however, concerns have emerged regarding the safety and quality of Tuo-zaafi, particularly regarding fungal contamination and the presence of mycotoxins. Fungi, ubiquitous in agricultural environments [6], [7], can colonize cereals during growth, harvest, and storage phases, potentially producing mycotoxins [8], [9].

Mycotoxins are secondary metabolites produced by certain fungi under favourable conditions, which may cause various diseases in humans and animals [10], [11]. Some mycotoxins that pose a serious risk to human health include aflatoxins (AF), ochratoxins (OTA), fumonisins (FUM), patulins (PT), and trichothecenes [11]. Cereal crops may become contaminated with mycotoxins due to improper pre- and post-harvest practices [12], [13]. Notably, mycotoxins have severe health implications, including liver, kidney, and immune system damage [14], [15]. Additionally, they are associated with carcinogenicity, congenital abnormalities, infertility [16], and cellular mutations [17].

Particularly, aflatoxins and ochratoxins have been implicated as class 1 carcinogens to humans according to the literature [18], [19]. As for FB1, the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) classified it as a probable human carcinogen in group 2 B. In addition, it can be hazardous to a variety of animals, including mice, rats, and rabbits. Furthermore, the Joint Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) and World Health Organization (WHO) have established a temporary maximum tolerable daily intake for fumonisins of 2 μg/kg bw/day due to the lack of any observed adverse effects for nephrotoxicity and hepatotoxicity in male rats.

In Ghana, where traditional food practices intersect with modern challenges of food safety and public health, understanding the fungal diversity and mycotoxin contamination in this traditional meal, Tuo-zaafi becomes imperative. Regional differences in agricultural practices, climate, and storage conditions may influence the prevalence and types of fungi present in maize and millet. This may impact the safety and nutritional integrity of Tuo-zaafi across different geographic areas.

Existing reports only concern aflatoxin contamination of some ingredients, especially maize, often used in the preparation of Tuo zaafi in Ghana [20], [21], [22]. To the best of our knowledge, no paper reports the fungal mycotoxins and health risks associated with the consumption of Tuo zaafi in Ghana.

This paper aims to address these critical issues by assessing the fungal diversity and mycotoxin contamination in Tuo-zaafi across key regions in Ghana: the Northern, Ashanti, Bono East, and Upper East regions. By examining the fungal load, identifying prevalent fungal species, and quantifying mycotoxin levels in Tuo-zaafi samples, this study sought to provide evidence-based insights into the safety and quality of this traditional dish. Ultimately, this research aims to inform policymakers, health authorities, and local communities on strategies to mitigate risks associated with mycotoxin exposure in Tuo-zaafi, thereby safeguarding public health and preserving the cultural heritage of Ghanaian cuisine.

Methodology

Study design

An experimental study design was employed in this study to assess the fungal diversity and mycotoxins contamination of Tuo-zaafi from food vendors in the Northern Region (Tamale, Saboba, and Tolon), Ashanti Region (Kumasi, Adansi West, and Kwabre East), Bono East Region (Techiman, Sene East, and Sene West) and Upper East Region (Bolgatanga, Bongo, and Builsa South) (Fig. 1). The districts were simple randomly selected. These regions are in different agro-ecological zones and have varied geographical attributes (Table 1).

Sample collection and processing

A convenient sampling method was used to purchase samples from each district. Five (5) Tuo-zaafi samples of approximately 150 g were purchased from different vendors in the four (4) selected districts in each of the Northern Region, Ashanti Region, Bono East Region, and Upper East Region. In total, eighty (80) samples were purchased, preserved, and transported on ice to the laboratories of the University of Health and Allied Sciences and the Ghana Standard Authority for analysis. Upon reaching the laboratories, the samples were kept at a temperature of 8 °C until ready for analysis [23].

Mycological analysis

The plating of fungi was done as prescribed [24] while enumeration was carried out according to the procedure outlined for enumeration and identification by media and process modifications [25], [26]. Ten grams (10 g) of each test sample was added to 100 ml of sterile peptone water and agitated vigorously to form the stock solution for the analysis. The rinse stock was serially diluted up to 10−3. One (1) ml of each of the dilution factors was pour-plated on Oxytetracycline Glucose Yeast Extract Agar (OGYE 545 Oxoid, Basingstoke, UK) and Dichloran Rose Bengal Chloramphenicol Agar (DRBC, 727 Oxoid, Basingstoke, UK). The plates were then incubated at 22–25 °C for 3–5 days. Enumeration was carried out using a colony counter (STAR 8500 Funke Gerber, Germany). The colony-forming unit per gram was calculated using the formula according to Odamtten et al. [27].

Aflatoxins determination

Extraction of samples

The presence of four aflatoxins, namely AFB1, AFB2, AFG1, and AFG2, was identified using the official method EN14123 [30] of the European Committee for Standardization (CEN). About 25 g of the sample was extracted using Methanol in water (200 ml) (8 +2) and 5 g NaCl. Fat samples with a fat content of over 50% were extracted using 100 ml of hexane in the standard methanol extraction solvent. After, the mixture was homogenized for three minutes at a speed of 3000 rpm for two minutes and 3500 rpm for one minute. Using a preconditioned immunological affinity column that is specific for AFB1, AFB2, AFG1, and AFG2, 10 milliliters of the filtrate was added to 60 milliliters of phosphate buffer saline (PBS) for solid-phase extraction. After being placed onto the preconditioned column, the 70 ml filtrate-phosphate-buffered saline combination was allowed to elute by gravity at a flow rate of 1 ml min−1. Following this procedure, a 15 ml distilled water cleanup was started at a 5 ml min−1 flow rate. In a 5 ml volumetric flask, aflatoxins were eluted in two stages using 0.5 ml and 0.75 ml of HPLC-grade methanol, respectively, and allowed to elute by gravity. After adding deionized water to bring the eluate's volume to 5 ml, the eluate was vortexed and 2 ml was pipetted into HPLC vials for measurement.

Extraction and derivatization of aflatoxins (B1, B2, G1 and G2) from the samples

After weighing 20 g of homogenized sample, 80 ml of either 70% MeOH solution (84 v:16 v) was added. A magnetic stirrer was used for two hours to mix the samples. After rinsing the extraction jar with the extraction solution, a sucker was used to filter the material via a funnel. The material was subjected to a nitrogen stream until it dried. After adding 200 µl of trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), the mixture was stirred for a minute and then incubated at 250 °C for 30 min. After diluting the sample to 500 µl with a 30:70 acetonitrile-water solution and mixing it for a minute, a 0.2 µm membrane filter (GHP) was used to filter it. To prevent degradation, the samples were examined using fluorescence detection and high-pressure liquid chromatography.

HPLC parameters

High-Performance Liquid Chromatography HPLC (Agilent 1260 Series, OpenLab software, X-bridge column) (250 mm×4.6 mm, i.d., 5 μm), USA, with fluorescence detector and post-column derivatization using Kobra cells to produce bromine electrochemically was used to evaluate the aflatoxins. The injection volume was 10 μl, the flow rate was 1 ml/min, the column temperature was 35°C, the excitation and emission wavelengths were 360 and 440 nm, the mobile phase composition was 65:15:20 v/v/v water/acetonitrile/MeOH, and Kobra cells were used after column derivatization. Ochratoxin A analysis

Chemicals and standards

All solvents used in the mobile phase preparation were HPLC grade and obtained from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Sigma-Aldrich supplied the analytical grade methanol and hexane for extraction. All homogenized mixtures and eluates were filtered through 0.45 mm membrane filters and Whatman no. 4 filters, respectively (Whatman plc, Maidstone, UK). A Millipore Elix Essential purification system was used to obtain de-ionized water (Bedford, MA, USA). RBiopharm, Rhone limited, supplied OCHRA PREP immunoaffinity columns, which were used for SPE and cleanup. These columns had a minimum 90% recovery rate and a concentration capacity of 100 ng ml−1. Dissolving PBS tablets in distilled water produced phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (Sigma-Aldrich). Sigma-Aldrich supplied 99.0% sodium chloride. Six calibrations were performed on the pure Ochratoxin A standard, with concentrations of 5 g/kg, 10 g/kg, 15 g/kg, 20 g/kg, 25 g/kg, and 30 g/kg. Linearity for the regression curve was deemed acceptable at 0.99 or 99% (CEN official method EN14123 33).

Determination of Ochratoxin A

To find the amount of ochratoxin A in the samples, the CEN official technique EN14123 [31] was employed. About 250 ml of various Tuo-zaafi samples were tested, and the full batch of items was well-blended. Using a pipette, the Tuo-zaafi samples were split into four equal portions. Until a representative 50 ml sample was obtained, the remaining two portions were stored, and the process was repeated. About 5 g of sodium chloride and 200 ml of methanol were added to 25 ml of samples in distilled water at a 4:1 ratio. The Tuo-zaafi mixture was homogenized for three minutes (that is, at 3000 rpm for two minutes and 3500 rpm for one minute) after 100 ml of hexane was added.

The Tuo-zaafi mixture produced two organic layers: an upper layer composed of hexane and a lower layer composed of methanol. The lowest methanol layers of the Tuo-zaafi food combinations were filtered using Whatman number 4 filter paper. Ten milliliters (10 ml) of filtrate were used for solid-phase extraction and cleanup. Ten milliliters of filtrate were combined with Phosphate Buffer Saline (PBS), and the mixture was swirled. Ten milliliters (10 ml) of filtrate were combined with PBS, and the mixture was mixed. Ten milliliters (10 ml) of filtrate were used for preconditioning and activating antibodies in immunoaffinity columns specifically for Ochratoxin A. Ten milliliters (10 ml) of phosphate buffer saline were eluted through columns at a flow rate of 3 ml per minute. Precisely 15 ml of the 160–160 ml filtrate-PBS combination was added to the pre-conditioned immunoaffinity columns made specifically for Ochratoxin A.

Before eluting at a rate of 5 ml min−1, the columns were cleaned three times using 5 ml of PBS. Using a vacuum pump, the air was forced through the columns to eliminate every molecule of wash solvent. To elute ochratoxin A, 1 ml of the highest-grade methanol was added first, then, after a minute, another 1 ml of methanol was added to a 5 ml volumetric flask. Air was forced into the column to gather all of the eluates. The eluates were vortexed before being pipetted into HPLC vials for measurement after being diluted with 1 % aqueous acetic acid.

HPLC parameters

To quantify OTA, an Agilent high-performance liquid chromatography system (HPLC 1260 infinite series) with fluorescence detector and a quaternary pump was used. The Chem Station (Open Lab Edition) was used for data collection and quantification. The Agilent HPLC, which had a fluorescence detector, was tuned to excite at 333 nm and emit at 467 nm. The column compartment temperature was set to 30°C. The mobile phase consisted of a 40:30:30 blend of acetic acid (pH 2.4), methanol, acetonitrile, and 5 mM sodium acetate. With an injection volume of 10 µl and a flow rate of 1 ml/min, respectively, an isocratic administration technique was employed. The timer (CEN official method EN14123) was set for ten minutes [31].

Data analysis

Every procedure was carried out three times, and a single-factor analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied to the collected data. Using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software version 22, Duncan’s multiple range test (DMRT) was employed to distinguish mean differences, and significance was acknowledged at a 5% (P < 0.05) and a Confidence Interval of 95%. The analysis used standard forms for the mean counts, which were converted to logarithmic values and reported as means and standard deviations.

Discussion

Fungal count

Tuo zaafi samples from the Northern, Ashanti, Bono East, and Upper East regions were analyzed for fungal contamination. The results indicated no significant (p > 0.05) differences across the samples. The fungal contamination ranged from 3.19 to 4.27 Log 10 CFU/g. The counts of DRBC were generally greater and could mainly be attributed to the higher efficiency in species isolation than OGYE. This observation could be due to the variation in the nutrients in the media.

Fungal counts recorded in the present study were slightly greater than results reported by other researchers on similar foods made from boiled cereals. In Nigeria, Omemu et al. [50] encountered molds that ranged from 2.81 to 2.91 log 10 CFU/g in ogi (maize gruel). In Ouagadougou, Burkina Faso, pre-cooked cereal-based foods had a range of < 10 CFU/g to 2.20 ± 0.14 × 103 CFU/g, according to Sawadogo et al. [51].

In Turkey, Segun and Karapinar [52] reported a range of 4.5 × 101 - 9.5 × 104 CFU/g in cereal-based foods. The fungal counts reported by El-Hassan et al. [53] ranged from 2.2 102 −6.5 103 CFU/g in the cereal samples from Nigeria. Similarly, fungi in cereal-based breakfast reported by Piotrowska [54] in Poland ranged from 1.3 × 101 CFU/g - 9.0 × 102 CFU/g.

Nwokoro and Chukwu [55] have reported greater fungi counts of 2.0 × 104–6.8 × 108 CFU/g in maize flour samples for cereal-based food in Nigeria. In Kumasi, Ghana, Hackman [56] found that the early fungal population in a mixed grain variety ranged from 4.8 to 5.4 log 10 CFU/g. For maize samples intended for food, Ekwomadu et al. [57] found a range of 1.04–6.07 log10 CFU/g in South Africa.

The higher level of fungal contamination recorded in the Bono East Region may be attributed to fungi thriving in the specific temperature and humidity ranges of that region [58]. Different species have different optimal conditions, but moderate temperatures (20–30°C) and high humidity (above 70%) favour fungal growth. Tropical and subtropical regions often have these conditions year-round, making them hotspots for fungal proliferation (Pérez-Izquierdo et al., 2021). The Northern region has a monomodal seasonal rainfall distribution, with averages ranging from 900 to 1100 mm (Looby and Treseber 2014), which may contribute to the dampness of foodstuffs, allowing fungi to thrive.

Food contamination is not limited to contamination in the field and during storage; it can also result from unsafe handling practices and poor handling conditions by food vendors [5]. Food preparation involves multiple people working quickly to get food ready, and most of them may not wash their hands between handling money or using the restroom before handling the food. According to Kortei et al. [59], food sellers are typically held directly accountable for food contamination that occurs during the processing, preprocessing, and post-processing stages.

The Ghana Standards Authority's and the International Commission for Microbiological Specifications for Foods' (ICMSF) guidelines, which specify that ready-to-eat food with plate counts ranging from 0 to 103 (0–3 log10 CFU) is acceptable, between 104 and ≤ 105 (4–5 log10 CFU) is tolerable, and 106 (6 log10 CFU) and above is unacceptable, were used as a check against this study's findings.

Fungal species

The fungal species that were isolated from Tuo-zaafi from Northern Region, Ashanti Region, Bono East Region and Upper East Region were Aspergillus flavus, A. niger, A. fumigatus, Fusarium verticillioides, F. oxysporum, Trichoderma harzianum, Penicillium digitatum, P. chrysogenum, Mucor racemosus, Rhizopus stolonifera, Cladosporium cladosporoides, Alternaria alternata, Rhodotorula species, and Saccharomyces cerevisiae. Among the fungal groups identified, Saccharomyces cerevisiae was the most dominant, followed by Fusarium verticillioides, Rhizopus stolonifera, F. oxysporum, and Aspergillus flavus. According to Annan-Prah et al. [60], the predominant yeast found in nsiho (kenkey) fermentation was S. cerevisiae, with a prevalence of roughly 47.6%. Similarly, using molecular techniques, Hayford and Jesperson [61] as well as Hayford and Jakobsen [62] determined that S. cerevisiae and C. krusei were the predominant yeast species during steeping and dough fermentation. Furthermore, S. cerevisiae and C. krusei were again identified as the yeast species engaged in Kenkey fermentation by Obiri-Danso et al.[63]. The findings of these previous studies were in agreement with our studies.

The fungi species found in this investigation are consistent with results from related earlier studies. In a study carried out in Ghana (Cape Coast), Annan-Prah et al. [60] identified the primary fungi found in street meals, such as banku, which is fermented cooked maize dough. Aspergillus flavus, Aspergillus niger, Aspergillus candidus, Cladosporium herbarum, Necrospora crassa, Penicillium citrinum, Fusarium, Mucor, and Rhizopus species were the main fungi that were detected.

Additionally, Omemu et al. [50] observed that in ogi (a fermented cereal product), isolated molds included Aspergillus flavus, A. niger, Penicillium oxalicum, Mucor racemosus, and Rhizopus stolonifer. Isolated yeasts included Candida. krusei, C. tropicalis, C. kefyr, and Saccharomyces cerevisiae.

Adegbehingbe [64] reported Aspergillus niger, Rhizopus stolonifer, Mucor mucedo, Fusarium species, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Candida utilis and Penicillium citrinum as fungi isolated from musa (a sorghum-maize blend). Nwokoro and Chukwu [55] reported Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Aspergillus oryzae, Aspergillus niger, Penicillium citrinum, Rhizopus microsporus, Rhizopus oligosporus, Mucor circinelloides and Rhodotorula glutinis as the mycoflora of maize isolated from fermentation of maize for akamu/ogi production. In a related recent study, Kortei et al. [65] in their study of fungi profile of maize meant for consumption, reported Aspergillus niger, A. flavus, A. fumigatus, A. tamarii, A. ochraceous, and A. parasiticus, Curvularia lunata, Cladosporium herbarium, Fusarium moniliforme, Penicillium citrinum, Eurotium sp., Mucor racemosus, Rhizopus stolonifer, Paecilomyces variotii, Neurospora sitophilia, and Rhodotorula sp. In their studies, the genus Fusarium was the most dominant fungus.

Because the hot and humid tropical conditions encourage the growth of these fungi in the field and storage systems, fungus contamination of seeds, cereals, and feedstuffs is a persistent issue in developing nations [20], [66]. Fungal contamination has been linked to these agricultural raw components and their subsequent transfer to processed meals [20].

Adequate moisture is also crucial for fungi to grow and reproduce. Regions with high rainfall or humidity provide the necessary moisture for fungal spores to germinate and mycelium to grow [67]. Furthermore, altitude and terrain affect temperature and moisture levels, which in turn influence fungal distribution. High-altitude regions may have cooler temperatures and lower humidity, limiting the range of fungi that can thrive there compared to lowland areas with more favorable conditions. Lastly, Looby and Treseder [66] highlighted the impact of the climatic seasons on fungal growth cycles. Furthermore, warmer, humid seasons promote fungal growth, whereas colder or drier seasons may slow down or halt fungal activity. Seasonal changes also influence the types of fungi and mycotoxins that are prevalent.

Ochratoxin A

Ochratoxin A remains a worldwide concern primarily because of its toxicity and prevalence. Aflatoxin and ochratoxin contamination in food is a global issue during the pre- and post-harvest phases and is prevalent in grains [21]. The range of ochratoxins obtained in this study was 4.72–16.13 μg/kg (mean 12.33 μg/kg), similar to other earlier studies.

In Tunisia, Lasram et al. [68] reported higher quantities of the range 3.5–53.52 μg /kg ochratoxins in millet samples with a mean value of 24.54 ± 17.54 µg/kg as compared to Echodu et al. [69] who examined the levels of ochratoxins in a variety of products and discovered that sorghum had the mean concentration of (3.8 μg/kg), followed by sesame (1.4 μg/kg), millet (1.1 μg/kg), and maize (0.4 μg/kg). The total ochratoxins tested in millet ranged from 0 to 3.2 μg/kg, far less than the range recorded in millet samples from Tunisia, which was 3.5 – 53.52 µg/kg.

In a study by Iram et al. [70], levels of ochratoxin were measured in maize samples taken from storehouses in 15 districts spread across three agro-ecological zones in Punjab, Pakistan. No sample had ochratoxin A. According to Kortei et al. [20], ochratoxins (OTA) ranged in concentration from 0 to 97.51 µg/kg in Ghana.

Nalle et al. [71] found that corn grains and products from various sources had a mean value of 20.385 μg/kg, indicating moderate amounts in West Timor. The tentative tolerated weekly intake (PTWI) of OTA has been set by the Joint Committee FAO/WHO of Experts on Food Additives (JECFA) at 100 ng/kg of body weight, or 14 ng/kg body weight/day [72]. On the other hand, 120 ng/kg of OTA was reinstated by the European Food Safety Authority, which is equivalent to 17.1 ng/kg per Kortei et al. [21].

Notably, the different agroecological zones that comprise the biosphere imply distinct growth conditions for the multiplication of fungi due to the diverse environmental factors and resources required to support development [21]. Brzonkalik et al. [73] also noted that the species or strain, which is impacted by the growth substrate and environmental factors, is primarily responsible for the formation of mycotoxin. Several filamentous species in the genera Aspergillus and Penicillium produce ochratoxin A (OTA), a mycotoxin, as a byproduct of their secondary metabolism [74]. The two main environmental variables affecting the rate of fungal proliferation and mycotoxin generation are temperature and water activity (aw).

Penicillium species may produce OTA at a low temperature of 5 °C in tropical and semi-tropical climates, but Aspergillus species can produce OTA even at high temperatures and humidity [21]. According to this study, climatic and environmental variables may be implicated for the higher ochratoxin (OTA) concentration in Tuo-zaafi found in the northern region. Additionally, Nji et al. [75] highlighted how Aspergillus flavus prevalence in maize is directly impacted by high temperatures, higher CO2 concentrations, drought stress, and rainfall, all of which promote fungal growth, conidiation, and spore dispersal while hindering maize growth and development.

Ochratoxins can cause cancer and have a variety of other toxicological effects, including hepatotoxicity, immunotoxicity, genotoxicity, mutagenicity, and embryotoxicity [21], [76]. Battacone et al. [77] conducted a study to assess the toxicological effects of OTA intake on the performance of the main livestock (i.e., poultry, swine, cattle, goats, and sheep). They found that OTA had a toxicogenic effect on the liver and kidneys of these animals.

According to Wei et al. [78], food and feedstuffs frequently include OTA, which can expose humans and livestock to high levels of exposure. Due to its severe toxicological effects, which can have genotoxic, nephrotoxic, hepatotoxic, and carcinogenic effects on humans and animals, OTA has attracted a lot of attention from around the globe in recent years.

Aflatoxins

Because it is difficult to regulate the conditions that promote the establishment of the Aspergillus species, aflatoxin prevalence is becoming increasingly unavoidable [69]. The most important mycotoxins that infect different food and agricultural products are aflatoxins [79]. AFB1 is the most prevalent of the four major kinds of aflatoxins and, in animal models, can cause functional carcinogenesis if the toxicity limit is exceeded [80]. It is said that aflatoxins are common in both East and West Africa. According to earlier research, 90% of maize samples from East Africa had high levels of aflatoxins, while in some regions of West Africa, the exposure to aflatoxins can reach up to 99% [80].

Water activity (aw) is primarily associated with fungal infestation, progression, and aflatoxin development. This is explained by poor drying, which increases the growth of mycotoxigenic fungi like Aspergillus species. These fungi are predicted to grow for a longer period in storage, and stored grains and legumes become predisposed [6]. Table 3 presents the number of T.Z samples contaminated with AF (Total Aflatoxins).

In this study, higher amounts of Aflatoxins, which ranged from AFB1 (13.58–41.30 μg/kg), AFB2 (2.86 – 9.11 μg/kg), AFG1 (0.18 – 9.03 μg/kg), and AFG2 (0.46 – 0.62 μg/kg), were recorded. Mushtaq et al. [81] conducted a study in Pakistan to determine the occurrence of some selected processed food derived from cereals and reported values range, AFB1 (0.02 – 1.24 μg/kg), AFB2 (0.02 – 0.37 μg/kg), AFG1 (0.25 – 2.7 μg/kg), and AFG2 (0.21 – 1.3 μg/kg). The most contaminated samples with AFB1 were from the Northern Region, with a mean value of 41.30 ug/kg. Hajnal et al. [82] reported AFB1 contamination of maize samples in Serbia, which ranged from 1.3 to 88.8 µg/kg and was comparable with values obtained in this study. Also, Lasram et al. [68] recorded contamination of millet, which was in the range of 0.87–60.12 µg/kg for AFB1.

Lewis and colleagues (2005) investigated aflatoxin contamination of commercial maize products during an acute aflatoxicosis outbreak in Eastern and Central Kenya. They found that maize samples had aflatoxin amounts above 1000 ppb (μg/kg). The Ghana Standards Authority has established toxin quantity limits that are slightly higher and more flexible than those set forth by the European Union (EFSA). These limits are 5 µg/kg, 10 µg/kg, 2 µg/kg, and 4 µg/kg for total aflatoxins and AFB1, respectively. The study's findings were contrasted with the regulatory concentration limits set by the Ghana Standards Authority (GSA) and the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA).

Because aflatoxins can withstand chemical and thermal changes, the relatively high amounts of aflatoxins found in this study suggest that aflatoxins can linger in food long after the fungus has been rendered inactive, despite all rigorous processing procedures [20]. Aflatoxin can be present in any grain crop. Aflatoxigenic fungi that cause preharvest infections of commodities are likely caused by intensive farming techniques and a decline in genetic diversity in cereal crops [83]. In addition, according to Tola and Kebede [83], aflatoxin preharvest contamination of crops happens in tropical and temperate climates. Plants under growth stress are more vulnerable to fungal invasion and aflatoxin formation in their seeds. The most widely acknowledged stresses on plants include insects, drought, and watering schedules. Postharvest contamination is a global issue that arises from the presence of aflatoxigenic fungi in storage units. Manure from feedlots can harbor aflatoxigenic fungus [84]. Insects disperse the spores of aflatoxigenic fungi to plants, and the fungi themselves colonize regions damaged by insects.

Researchers have discovered that several variables interact to influence fungal colonization and/or mycotoxin generation after the development of precise and sensitive methods for qualitative and quantitative mycotoxin analysis [83]. There are three types of factors that influence the development and contamination of mycotoxins: physical, chemical, and biological. Insect infestation, temperature, and relative humidity are examples of physical elements that promote fungal colonization and mycotoxin formation. The relatively high variability observed in the mycotoxins determined in this work could be due to differences in environmental conditions affecting the growth and proliferation of toxigenic fungi. Using fertilizers and/or fungicides is one example of a chemical factor. Pfliegler et al. [85] reported that these biological factors arise from the interplay between the toxin-producing fungal species that colonize the substrate and the substrate itself.

Consumer risk assessment

According to Ji et al. [86], risk estimations are models that forecast the negative health effects of mycotoxin exposure and direct food regulators in establishing thresholds for these toxins in food. The ratio MOE was used to assess exposure and risk characterization for oral ingestion of food for various age groups in the Ghanaian population. A review of relevant literature showed that there aren't many studies on the safety of Tuo zaafi food.

Results obtained in this study revealed an adverse health effect being realized for all the investigated mycotoxins via the consumption of Tuo zaafi since all computed values for MOE were less than 10,000.

Additionally, regarding the Tolerable Daily Intakes (TDI) EFSA [40], and Verstraete [87] emphasized that no amount of these aflatoxins is safe and must be kept “As reasonably low as achievable” (ALARA) since they have a cumulative effect on humans and animals, especially the internal organs (liver and kidneys, etc.).

JECFA has prescribed 14 ng/kg (0.014 μg/kg) of ochratoxin A (Tolerable Weekly Intake: 100 ng/kg body weight). This study's results in all the regions studied were above the Tolerable Daily Intakes, which suggests some adverse health effects when the food is consumed.

Fumonisins has a TDI of 2 μg/kg body weight (JECFA). The EDIs calculated were not up to the prescribed dosage and so were not likely to produce any adverse effect.

Future research, practices and policies is focusing on several key areas

Detection and Monitoring- Developing rapid, accurate and cost-effective detection methods such as Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML) algorithms to detect mycotoxins in food products

Mitigating Strategies-Implementing Good Agricultural Practices, Integrated Pest Management (IPM) and post-harvest management techniques to reduce mycotoxin contamination.

Regulatory Frameworks- Strengthening regulatory standards and enforcement to limit mycotoxin levels in food and feed with international cooperation and harmonization

Biological Control- Exploring biological control methods, such as the use of microorganisms and enzymes to degrade mycotoxins

Climate Change Adaptation –Developing strategies to mitigate the impact of climate change on mycotoxin contamination, including climate-smart agronomy and early warning systems.

Awareness and Education-Raising public awareness and educating stakeholders about mycotoxin risks and prevention strategies.

Some promising technologies being explored include;

Nanotechnology-Developing nanodelivery systems for natural antimycotoxigenic agents, Genetic Engineering- using gene-editing techniques, such as CRISPR/Cas9, to develop disease-resistant crops. And Non-thermal Processing-applying non-thermal processing methods like cold plasma and pulsed electric fields to reduce mycotoxin contamination.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the Tuo zaafi samples collected from the Northern belt of Ghana (Northern Region, Ashanti Region, Bono East Region, and Upper East Region) contained fungal counts that were within the acceptable range. However, samples harbored toxigenic fungi belonging to Aspergillus, Fusarium, and Penicillium. Additionally, the ochratoxin levels were very substantial, with the majority being higher than the limits set by the Ghana Standards Authority (GSA) and the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA). Also, the study found a carcinogenic adverse health effect associated with the consumption of Tuo zaafi in the studied regions since all MOE values were less than 10,000. Continuous surveillance of mycotoxins in maize, millet, and sorghum crops, food (Tuo zaafi), and awareness creation through inspections of food to guarantee food safety, as well as local support and education to ensure that food grains are harvested, dried, and stored appropriately. Finally, the findings of this work satisfy SDG 3, which ensures healthy lives and promotes well-being for all ages, and SDG 17, which strengthens the mechanisms of implementation and revitalizes the Global Partnership for Sustainable Development.

Limitation of study

The data and results obtained from this research cannot be used as a good representation of the country because out of the sixteen (16) regions in Ghana, samples were sourced from only four (4) regions.
