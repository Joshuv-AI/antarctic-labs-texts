Letter to the Editor on AI-Based Segmentation of Cemento-Osseous Dysplasias
Authors: Jiajie Peng, Gang Cheng
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

I read with great interest the article titled “Segmentation of Cemento-Osseous Dysplasias Using an Artificial Intelligence Algorithm” by Çelik Özen et al., recently published in the International Dental Journal.1 The authors address an important application of artificial intelligence (AI) in oral radiology. Their work focuses on automated segmentation of cemento-osseous dysplasias (CODs) using CBCT images. This is a timely contribution to the field of AI-assisted diagnosis for fibro-osseous lesions. Below, I discuss the study's strengths and suggest potential future research directions.

The authors' choice of the nnU-Net v2 framework is a methodological strength. This framework is known for its adaptive configuration. The reported Dice coefficient of 0.839 and sensitivity of 0.889 are promising. This achievement underscores the potential of AI to assist in one of the more nuanced challenges in oral radiology.

Moreover, the authors' emphasis on the clinical relevance of automated segmentation deserves recognition. CODs are often asymptomatic and rely heavily on radiological follow-up, yet consistent monitoring can be subjective and variable across clinicians. By providing an objective, reproducible method for lesion boundary definition, this AI model could standardize follow-up assessments and facilitate more reliable longitudinal tracking.

The study also thoughtfully addresses an important gap in the literature. While several AI studies have focused on detecting periapical lesions, cysts, or tumours, fewer have targeted fibro-osseous dysplasias, particularly in 3D CBCT data. The authors' work thus expands the scope of AI applications in dentomaxillofacial imaging.

Looking ahead, several directions could build upon this solid foundation. The model faced difficulties separating lesions from adjacent tooth roots. A future study could integrate a prior tooth segmentation step. A cascaded AI model could explicitly exclude dental structures first. This approach may improve accuracy for periapical CODs. Similar methods have succeeded in other maxillofacial segmentation tasks.2

The study appropriately focused on CODs in the mixed radiographic stage. These lesions are most characteristic and clinically relevant. Yet, excluding purely radiolucent and sclerotic phases limits generalizability. COD follow-up often spans years and involves stage transitions. A longitudinal AI model capable of segmenting all phases would be highly useful. Incorporating sequential CBCT scans from the same patients could enable this. Such a tool could then monitor disease progression and stability.3

Clinically, CODs from radiographically similar lesions like ossifying fibroma and bone island pose challenges, especially for less experienced clinicians. Future AI research could focus on developing multi-class differential diagnosis models capable of assessing key imaging features, such as the presence of cortical expansions, density at the margins (high-density ‘white’ border vs low-density ‘black’ border), and characteristics (well-defined bony shell vs blurred fusion or translucent halo). By training on diverse datasets that include commonly confused lesions, such AI systems could provide reliable diagnostic support, particularly for junior clinicians, improving diagnostic consistency and accuracy.

The study has a single-centre design and a modest sample size. This is understandable for pioneering work. However, it affects the model's robustness and external validity. As Alahmari et al. emphasize, multicentre validation is essential.4 It ensures reliable performance across different CBCT devices and patient populations. Future work should compile larger, heterogeneous datasets. These should include diverse COD subtypes from multiple institutions. This would enhance model generalizability. It would also allow for subtype-specific performance analysis.

In conclusion, Çelik Özen et al. have presented a well-executed investigation. It demonstrates the feasibility of AI-driven COD segmentation. Their work lays a solid foundation for future research. This research should refine segmentation accuracy and expand clinical applicability. The ultimate goal is integrating such tools into routine workflows. These tools would support diagnostic consistency and long-term lesion monitoring.
