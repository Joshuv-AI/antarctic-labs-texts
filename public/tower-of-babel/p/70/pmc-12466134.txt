Malacoplakia of the testis in a renal transplant recipient: case report and review of the literature
Authors: Pui-long Wilson Hung, Stacia chun-chun Tsun, Karen Yuen, Hoi-lung Wong, Chiu-fung Tsang, Hok-leung Tsu, Ada Ng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Malacoplakia is an uncommon chronic inflammatory disease. The commonest form of malacoplakia after renal transplant is allograft parenchymal involvement while testicular malacoplakia remains one of the rarest forms amongst recipients of renal transplant.We report a case of a 58-year-old gentleman, with live donor renal transplantation 39 years ago, who presented to our unit with one-week history of acute left scrotal pain. Ultrasonography revealed a heterogeneous left testicular mass. Inguinal orchidectomy was done and the lesion was confirmed to be malacoplakia.Testicular malacoplakia is rare. Clinicians managing renal transplant recipients should be alert of this condition in their practice.

Introduction

Malacoplakia is an uncommon chronic inflammatory disease of uncertain etiology, but often associated with gram-negative bacterial infection. Whilst the condition was first described in 1902 by Michaelis and Gutmann, it was von Hansemann who coined the term in 1903 to define the pathology and its gross appearance.1,2 The urological tract has been said to be the most commonly affected, accounting for up to 58–70 % of cases3,4; however, the condition may involve any organ system. Microscopically, malacoplakia is characterized by the presence of large cells with abundant, eosinophilic cytoplasm (von Hansemann cells) and in these cells, typical cytoplasmic targetoid inclusion bodies named Michaelis-Gutmann bodies.

The exact underlying pathogenesis of malacoplakia is not certain but an immunocompromised state has been suggested to be associated with its development and this includes underlying malignancy, autoimmunity, immunosuppression, chronic alcoholism or general debility. It has been proposed that a deficit of the cyclic guanosine monophosphate in mononuclear cells leading to diminished release of beta-glucuronidase and subsequently reduced bactericidal action by macrophages plays a role in the pathogenesis of malacoplakia.5 The frequent association of malacoplakia with urinary tract infection by coliform bacteria, in particular Escherichia coli suggests also an infectious component in its etiology.

Malacoplakia has been increasingly described after all solid organ transplantation as well as hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. The commonest form of malacoplakia after renal transplant is allograft parenchymal involvement while testicular malacoplakia remains one of the rarest forms amongst recipients of renal transplant.

Case report

A 58-year-old gentleman, with known asphyxiating thoracic dysplasia (Jeune's syndrome) with end-stage renal failure treated with live donor renal transplantation 39 years ago presented to our unit with one-week history of acute left scrotal pain. He is all along on mycophenolate 250 mg twice daily and prednisolone 5 mg daily for immunosuppression. He suffered from graft hydroureteronephrosis about 6 months before requiring a percutaneous nephrostomy. Antegrade pyelogram revealed a likely stricture at the distal graft ureter.

Examination showed a markedly tender and swollen testis sized about 6cm. Mid-stream urine grew Escherichia coli that was susceptible to quinolones. Clinical diagnosis was acute left epididymo-orchitis and he was started on oral levofloxacin. Urgent ultrasonography revealed the entire left testis was replaced by a heterogeneous mass suspicious of an abscess or infected malignancy. In view of possible cancer, emergency inguinal orchiectomy was performed. Intraoperatively, the left testis was replaced by a large hard mass but there was no gross purulent infection.

On histological examination, the left testis as well as the entire scrotal content showed necrotizing inflammation with abscesses containing neutrophils. The testicular parenchyma was replaced by sheets of histiocytes but without well-developed granulomas. The cytoplasm of the histiocytes contained numerous Michaelis-Gutmann bodies (Fig. 1). Langhan's giant cells or caseous necrosis was absent. These findings are diagnostic of malacoplakia. Owing to the pathology, he was given a two-week course of levofloxacin 500mg/day.6,7 He remained well on follow-up with no evidence of recurrence. Discussion

Within the urologic tract, the urinary bladder is the commonest affected organ while testicular malacoplakia (TM) is a much rarer entity. TM was first described in 1958 and since then only 36 more cases have been reported in the English literature (Table).8 A review of these cases showed TM occurred in men predominately middle-aged or above with about a fifth of them having medical conditions that rendered them immunosuppressed. These include diabetes mellitus(5), uremia(1), liver cirrhosis(1) and chronic intake of immunosuppressant after renal transplant(3). The pathogenesis of malakoplakia was believed to be a granulomatous disease, driven by dysfunctional phagolysosomal activity of bacteria by macrophages. Such defective response is more commonly associated with chronic bacterial infections, immunocompromised patients.5,9 A number of bacterial organisms have been implicated, particularly E. Coli, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Proteus, and Staphylococcus aureus.10 Remarkably, our patient has also been on chronic immunosuppression after renal transplant for over 30 years, supporting the role of altered host immune system in the pathogenesis of this condition. Clinical presentations of most reported cases of TM include one or more of the following: 1) acute orchitis or epididymo-orchitis which did not respond to medical treatment, 2) testicular mass suspicious of cancer and 3) frank testicular or scrotal abscess. In all patients, orchiectomy was necessary and the diagnosis was only made after examination of the orchiectomy specimens. In some of the more recent reports, ultrasonography was mentioned but in all these cases, TM masqueraded as testicular tumor or severe epididymo-orchitis. This was echoed in our patient whose preoperative ultrasonographic diagnosis was a testicular abscess or an infected tumor. Similar to many reported cases, Escherichia coli was isolated from our patient's urine, again supporting the pivotal role of concomitant urinary tract infection in the pathogenesis. From the literature, the diagnosis of TM was invariably made from histology of orchiectomy specimen, illustrating the difficulty in preoperative diagnosis. In all but one reports, there was cure from the disease after orchiectomy. Sterling et al. reported one patient with TM progressing to renal allograft malacoplakia despite orchiectomy, eventually necessitating graft nephrectomy and cessation of all immunosuppression.11 Fortunately, this has not been observed in our patient whose malacoplakia process appeared to be limited to his testis.

Malacoplakia has been described after all solid organ as well as hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, but its incidence is highest amongst recipients of renal transplant, for uncertain reason. The commonest form of malacoplakia after renal transplant is allograft parenchymal involvement, presenting as recurrent urinary tract infection or graft dysfunction (74 %) or a pseudotumor in the allograft (33 %) with TM as one of the rarest forms. Treatment of malacoplakia after renal transplant depends on the clinical types but generally involves reduction of immunosuppression and antimicrobial treatment, with variable success. Although evidence on conventional antimicrobial therapy remains limited, antibiotics with intracellular activity are generally advised. Agents such as quinolones, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, gentamicin, and choline agonists that elevated cGMP levels have demonstrated effectiveness in resolving the infection in most cases, with favourable long-term outcomes.6,7 Surgery is reserved for refractory cases or isolated atypical site of malacoplakia, such as TM.6

In summary, we reported a case of TM in a renal transplant recipient. TM is a rare form of malacoplakia amongst transplant recipients. Preoperative diagnosis is difficult and most manifest as either orchitis or testicular tumor. Diagnosis is often made on histology of orchiectomy specimen only. Clinicians managing renal transplant recipients should be alert of this condition in their practice.
