Ipsilateral small saphenous vein panel graft for reconstruction of popliteal artery aneurysm associated with entrapment syndrome
Authors: Van-Nut Lam, Nha-Truc N. Lam, Thanh-Phong Le
Published: 2026

A 74-year-old male presented with a threatened right lower extremity (Rutherford class IIa) 15 days after an acute limb ischemia event. Duplex ultrasound identified a popliteal artery aneurysm (PAA) with heavy mural thrombus and noted bilateral great saphenous veins (GSVs) with diameters under 3 mm. Subsequent computed tomography angiography (A) revealed a type III popliteal artery entrapment where an accessory slip from the medial gastrocnemius head (long arrow) compressed the proximal neck (short arrow) of a 30-mm PAA (curved arrow), alongside severely obstructed tibiopedal arteries. (Supplementary Fig 1, online only).

Surgical exploration was performed via a posterior approach in the prone position under general anesthesia. The entrapment was relieved by dividing the accessory slip (B/Cover). The aneurysm sac was then isolated and its collateral blood supply ligated. Due to the inadequate GSV size, a 10-cm segment of the ipsilateral 3.5-mm small saphenous vein (SSV) was harvested from the sapheno-popliteal junction. This conduit was divided into two ∼3.5 cm segments, split longitudinally, devalvulated, and sutured side-by-side using a continuous 6-0 monofilament technique to create a panel graft (C).

Following PAA resection, embolo-thrombectomy, and local intraoperative thrombolysis, the popliteal artery was reconstructed using the SSV panel graft (D). End-to-end anastomoses (arrows) were established proximally and distally with 5-0 monofilament. Postoperatively, the patient received a dual regimen (aspirin 100 mg daily and rivaroxaban 2.5 mg twice daily) for 3 months, followed by aspirin monotherapy. Scheduled follow-up visits up to 15 months after surgery have shown the absence of chronic limb ischemia and patency of the SSV panel graft (Supplementary Fig 2, online only).

Although popliteal artery entrapment is uncommon, associated aneurysm formation occurs in 9.1% of young patients1; however, true aneurysms are exceptionally rare in the elderly. Open repair addresses both the aneurysm and the anatomical entrapment. Current literature favors the use of GSV when adequate2; however, guidelines note comparable mid-term outcomes between alternative autologous veins and polytetrafluoroethylene prosthetic grafts.3 In the present case of acute limb ischemia with poor runoff—a scenario where polytetrafluoroethylene performs poorly2,4—using an ipsilateral SSV panel graft potentially offers the primary advantage of an autologous conduit with a compatible diameter and length.5 The patient consented to the publication.
