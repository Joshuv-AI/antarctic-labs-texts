Labor support in low- and middle-income countries - transformative impact of birth companions and doulas on maternal and neonatal outcomes: a narrative review
Authors: Prosper Akankwasa, Jackson Kakooza, John Katongole, Esther Namutosi, Ahmed Aisha Abdullahi, Catherine R. Lewis, Emmanuel Okurut
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMaternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality remain disproportionately high in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), partly due to inadequate intrapartum support, psychosocial stress, and disrespectful maternity care. Continuous labor support from birth companions or doulas offers a low-cost, high-impact intervention with demonstrated benefits in high-income settings, yet LMIC-focused evidence remains fragmented. This review synthesizes current evidence on the effects of birth companionship and doula care on maternal, neonatal, psychosocial, and implementation outcomes in LMICs.MethodsA narrative review was conducted using structured searches of PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Lens.org, and WHO documents. Eligible studies were published between 2015 and 2025, conducted in LMICs or relevant to LMIC contexts, and reported empirical findings on continuous intrapartum support from informal companions, trained lay companions, or professional doulas. Data were synthesized thematically across maternal, neonatal, psychosocial, and implementation domains. A structured narrative appraisal was used due to heterogeneity in study design and outcome measures.ResultsA total of 27 studies were included in the narrative synthesis. These comprised 8 primary studies in LMICs, 6 high-income country (HIC) studies involving marginalized populations, and 13 systematic reviews. Continuous labor support was consistently associated with shorter labor, higher rates of spontaneous vaginal birth, reduced use of pharmacologic analgesia and oxytocin augmentation, and lower maternal anxiety and stress markers. Neonatal benefits included higher Apgar scores, earlier breastfeeding initiation, and enhanced mother–infant bonding. Psychosocial improvements were mediated through emotional reassurance, advocacy, improved communication, and culturally responsive support. Implementation barriers identified across LMIC studies included provider resistance, facility space limitations, inadequate training, and sociocultural constraints, while facilitators included community engagement, structured companion training, and phased integration into maternity systems. Evidence quality varied widely, with few LMIC-specific randomized trials and heterogeneous operational definitions of “continuous support.”ConclusionsEvidence demonstrates that birth companions and doulas improve maternal well-being, early neonatal outcomes, and respectful maternity care in LMIC settings. Effective scale-up requires context-adapted implementation strategies, provider orientation, policy alignment, and rigorous LMIC-based trials assessing clinical outcomes, cost-effectiveness, and equity impacts. Companion-inclusive intrapartum care represents a feasible, low-cost strategy for strengthening quality and experience of maternity care in resource-limited health systems.

Background

Despite global progress, many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) continue to record unacceptably high rates of maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality, with a substantial proportion attributable to preventable causes and suboptimal intrapartum care. Traditional forms of community-based labor support were historically central to childbirth in many settings but have been marginalized by medicalized, institutional care models that often do not address psychosocial needs or respect women’s autonomy [1, 2].

Systematic reviews and randomized controlled trials (RCTs) from high-income countries (HICs) have established that continuous labor support reduces cesarean section rates, shortens labor duration, and increases women’s satisfaction with birth experiences. A Cochrane review by Bohren et al. demonstrated that continuous support reduced cesarean births by 25% (RR 0.75, 95% CI 0.64–0.88) and increased spontaneous vaginal birth rates (RR 1.08, 95% CI 1.04–1.12) [3]. Despite this robust evidence, translation into LMIC policy and practice remains limited. A recent scoping review found that only 15–30% of facilities in sub-Saharan Africa permit companion of choice during labor [4], suggesting substantial implementation gaps. Furthermore, systematic reviews synthesizing LMIC-specific evidence are scarce, with most existing reviews aggregating predominantly HIC data, creating uncertainty for policymakers in resource-constrained settings.

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), women have generally been encouraged to give birth in health care facilities instead of the home to ensure access to health care professionals and timely interventions [5]. However, disrespectful care is common in underprivileged populations, which may pose additional barriers and lead to poor maternal outcomes [6]. Increasingly, the literature highlights continuous labor support whether from informal companions, trained lay companions, or professional doulas as a low-cost, high-impact strategy that can simultaneously improve clinical outcomes and the birthing experience [5]. Although systematic reviews and randomized trials largely conducted in high-income settings demonstrate benefits of continuous labor support, translation to LMIC policy and practice remains unbalanced [7]. The overall aim of this narrative review is to synthesize available evidence on birth companions and doula care in LMIC contexts. We specifically hope to examine the effects of birth companions and doulas on maternal clinical outcomes, to assess the impact of birth companions on neonatal outcomes, identify barriers and facilitators to implementing birth companionship programs in LMIC health systems, and provide evidence-based recommendations for policy integration and future research.

Methods

Study design

This narrative review employed a structured synthesis approach to examine the evidence on birth companions and doula care in LMICs. A narrative review was particularly suitable for this complex topic where heterogeneous study designs and contexts required interpretive synthesis rather than statistical pooling. Secondly, the objectives encompass diverse clinical outcomes, psychosocial mechanisms, implementation barriers, and policy considerations requiring interpretive synthesis rather than meta-analysis. Third, narrative reviews allow examination of both ‘what works’ (effectiveness) and ‘how it works’ (mechanisms), which is essential for translating evidence into context-specific implementation strategies. While lacking the quantitative precision of systematic reviews, this approach provides comprehensive understanding of a complex, context-dependent intervention.

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across four electronic databases including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Lens.org between November 15–22, 2024. Two reviewers (PA and JK) independently designed and executed the searches. For each database, we combined index terms and keywords using Boolean operators (AND, OR) to capture three concept domains: (1) population (LMIC contexts), (2) intervention (birth companions, doulas, labor support), and (3) outcomes (maternal health, neonatal outcomes). Search results were exported to EndNote 20 for duplicate removal. Complete search strings are provided in Table 1.

Inclusion criteria

Peer reviewed and grey literature written in the English language and published between January 2015, and August 2025, were included. This timeframe was selected to capture evidence published after the 2015 WHO intrapartum care guidelines [5], which explicitly recommended companion of choice during labor. RCTs, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, observational studies (cohort, case-control, cross-sectional), qualitative studies, mixed-methods studies, and implementation reports were included. Only studies conducted in LMICs as defined by World Bank classification, studies from HICs explicitly focused on migrant, refugee, or marginalized populations experiencing barriers similar to those documented in LMICs (e.g., language barriers, cultural disconnection from health systems, experiences of discrimination), were included. Studies that focused on any form of continuous labor support including informal companions, trained lay supporters, or professional doulas were included. Studies had to discuss maternal clinical outcomes, neonatal outcomes, psychosocial outcomes, implementation factors, or policy-related findings. Only studies focusing on the intrapartum period (during labor and birth) were included.

Exclusion criteria

The following articles were excluded: opinion pieces or commentaries without empirical data, studies conducted exclusively in high-income countries without LMIC relevance, studies focusing solely on antenatal or postnatal support without intrapartum components, and conference abstracts without full-text availability. Studies addressing only antenatal education or postnatal support were also excluded.

Data extraction and synthesis

Extracted information included population/context, interventions (informal companion, trained lay companion, or doula), outcome measures (maternal, neonatal, psychosocial), and implementation observations. Findings were synthesized thematically rather than pooled quantitatively, given heterogeneity in study designs, settings, and outcome definitions. The synthesis emphasized effect direction and consistency, contextual moderators, and gaps in implementation.

Maternal clinical outcomes

Evidence from systematic reviews consistently demonstrated that continuous labor support was associated with reduced active labor duration and higher rates of spontaneous vaginal birth. A meta-analysis of Brazilian studies found that continuous support reduced cesarean section rates [28]. A global systematic review and meta-analysis by Jayasundara et al., synthesizing 35 RCTs, reported that labor companions improved multiple outcomes, with the strongest effect on reducing the fear of childbirth [26]. However, high heterogeneity was noted across outcomes.

Regarding interventions, systematic reviews suggested associations between continuous support and reduced analgesic use, though causality cannot be definitively established from predominantly observational LMIC studies. The qualitative study by Mirzania et al. in Iran noted that companions provided massage and emotional support, which may contribute to non-pharmacological pain management [12].

There were no primary LMIC studies that directly compared trained versus informal companions in maternal clinical outcome analyses. Most studies examined informal companions [8–11] or feasibility of trained companions [12, 13]. Protocol studies propose testing trained companions but have not yet reported outcomes [14, 15]. Among systematic reviews, Jayasundara et al. found familiar companions had the strongest effect on reducing tocophobia (ES = 1.73), while Bohren et al. reported best cesarean reduction with companions chosen by the woman (RR 0.58 vs. 0.75 overall) [3]. Kiti et al. found in a Kenyan cross-sectional study (n = 865) that continuous support by informal companions (partner, mother-in-law, mother, sister, friend) was associated with significantly higher person-centered maternity care (PCMC) scores (25.7 vs. 21.0; p < 0.001; adjusted β = 4.04, 95% CI 2.87–5.20) [8]. This suggests that companionship improves the experiential quality of care, even in observational designs.

In regard to psychological outcomes, supported women demonstrated lower anxiety, lower physiological stress markers (e.g., cortisol), higher satisfaction, and greater empowerment [20, 21]. One high-quality RCT from Sweden demonstrated that continuous support reduced labor duration and promoted vaginal delivery in primiparous women, with associated reductions in cortisol levels [21]. This physiological mechanism may explain observed clinical benefits. Doulas and trained companions enhance perceived control during labor and help women articulate preferences and consent for important components of respectful maternity care [13].

Neonatal outcomes

The impact of continuous labor support on neonatal outcomes is impressive. Multiple studies report improvement in Apgar scores and the initiation of early breastfeeding. Babies born to women with continuous labor support were noted to have higher Apgar scores than those who did not [16, 28]. Jayasundara et al. identified labor companion support as having the strongest effect on reducing Apgar scores (ES = 1.52), suggesting an improved immediate neonatal condition [26]. Increased rates of early breastfeeding initiation was also noted when mothers received continuous support [16, 28]. Early breastfeeding is a key survival intervention and may be facilitated by supportive postpartum assistance. However, a retrospective study in Hong Kong comparing women with versus without continuous companion support during the COVID-19 pandemic found no significant differences in neonatal outcomes or breastfeeding rates, though this was a high-income setting with already-optimized care [16].

Evidence for reductions in broader neonatal morbidity is promising but less conclusive. Although some studies show a lower incidence of low Apgar scores and physiological stress markers, long-term neonatal outcomes are under-studied in LMIC contexts [14, 21]. There was no demonstrated decrease in neonatal deaths in women receiving labor support as compared to those who did not [21]. Neonatal benefits observed in the compiled studies are plausible mechanistically (reduced maternal stress, fewer interventions) and supported by short-term outcomes.

Psychosocial mechanisms and communication

The literature emphasizes several interlocking psychosocial mechanisms. Emotional support reduces maternal anxiety and stress. Advocacy ensures clearer communication with clinical staff, and physical comfort strategies (positioning, massage, breathing guidance) improve coping and labor progress [17, 22, 29]. Bilingual or community-based companions bridge linguistic and cultural gaps, improving shared decision-making and perceived respect [18, 30]. Continuous support maps directly onto the respectful maternity care (RMC) framework enhancing dignity, autonomy, and effective communication, which in turn relates to uptake of facility-based births and satisfaction metrics [8, 19]. Doulas have been shown to counter negative factors that may preclude women from seeking maternal care [19]. The presence of doulas or labor companions enabled effective communication between women and health care providers, overall increasing a woman’s satisfaction and decreasing mistreatment and adverse birth outcomes [8].

Acceptance of birth companions and doulas depends on cultural norms, family dynamics, and women’s expectations. Traditional support may exist but is not always compatible with facility routines or provider expectations [23]. Provider resistance based on role confusion, concerns about interference, or perceived professional threats emerge as a recurrent barrier. Facility space, privacy concerns, workforce shortages, and funding constraints complicate integration into widespread practice [12, 23].

Training programs for companions and providers, clear role delineation, incremental pilot implementation, and use of community resources reduce friction. Case studies and pilot trials in Ethiopia, Kenya, Nigeria, and Tehran illustrate strategies [12, 15]. Adequate training of birth companions is paramount, and women should be informed about the role of birth companions [12]. Women seen by health care providers trained in respectful maternity care or trained birth companions had higher birth satisfaction scores [31].

Implementation barriers and facilitators

LMIC implementation studies revealed multilevel barriers and facilitators. The qualitative study in Iran identified that birth companionship was considered appropriate for overcrowded settings, with companions assisting through massage and emotional support [12]. However, the study noted cultural norms and facility routines as potential barriers. A feasibility study in Pakistan training 98 staff in psychosocial support found that cultural adaptations were essential, with preference for female companions in that context [13].

The mixed-methods systematic review by Sangy et al., examining midwife-led care implementation in 21 LMICs, identified common barriers: sociocultural norms, physician resistance, poor referral systems, and facility constraints (space, privacy, workforce shortages) [23]. The review rated 23 studies as high quality, 7 as moderate, and 1 as low, providing high confidence in these findings.

Cross-sectional studies from Ethiopia and Rwanda documented low baseline utilization rates (58.2%, 19.5%, and 15.0%, respectively), suggesting substantial implementation gaps despite policy recommendations [9–11].

Facilitators identified included: structured training programs for companions and providers, clear role delineation, incremental pilot implementation, and leveraging community resources. The Berhanu et al. protocol for cluster RCTs in Ethiopia, Kenya, and Nigeria proposes evaluating these strategies through standardized operating procedures and training videos [15].

Policy and system integration

Baseline data from Berhanu et al. indicated birth companionship coverage of only 5–20% across study sites in Ethiopia, Kenya, and Nigeria [15], despite WHO 2018 intrapartum care recommendations supporting companion of choice [5]. A systematic analysis of historical data of health systems changes in 78 LMICs documented slow policy adoption of respectful maternity care principles between 2008 and 2018 [32].

Discussion

Principal findings and interpretation

This narrative synthesis of the supplied literature finds consistent, plausible benefits of birth companionship and doula care for maternal and neonatal short-term outcomes and for experiential dimensions of childbirth particularly in the domains of reduced labor duration, lower intervention rates, improved maternal psychological well-being, higher Apgar scores, and earlier breastfeeding initiation [16, 21, 28]. Mechanistically, emotional support, advocacy, improved communication, and non-pharmacologic physical strategies appear to mediate these effects [17]. These mechanisms map directly onto the core tenets of respectful, person-centered maternity care [4] and aligns with the WHO recommendations for a suitable companion to be present during labor and childbirth [5].

The professionalization of doulas requires training frameworks balanced with local adaptation, guidance on scope of practice, and sustainable financing models. Tiered certification and community-based doula models offer scalable options. Training midwives and doulas to international standards is emphasized [23]. A further tension arises in professional dynamics. Some clinician groups perceive doulas as encroaching on clinical authority. Successful integration requires delineation of roles, so doulas complement rather than replace clinical care [12, 23].

Key limitations in the compiled body of literature include heterogeneity in defining and operationalizing “support” (informal companion vs. trained doula vs. lay supporter), variability in outcome metrics, and predominance of high-income setting data for many RCTs [7]. LMIC-specific randomized evidence remains limited. Where present, studies often vary in training intensity and scope, complicating transferability. Cost-effectiveness and equity impacts are understudied. The degree to which doula programs reduce disparities rather than primarily benefiting those who already have access is not yet clear [26]. Policy traction is growing, but operationalizing doulas into routine health services demands investments in workforce development, regulation, and financing strategies. Evidence on long-term cost-effectiveness in LMICs is sparse and mixed. Economic evaluations remain a priority [24].

Implementation and policy implications

Current policy uptake remains inadequate. Despite WHO 2018 recommendations for companion of choice during labor [5], our review identified birth companionship coverage of only 5–20% in recent LMIC studies [9–11, 15]. This implementation gap highlights the need for concerted policy and programmatic action.

For policymakers and program managers in LMICs, the pragmatic message is that birth companions and doula models are relatively low-cost, acceptable, and plausibly effective. Strategies to advance scale include: (1) pilot and phased rollouts that adapt to local culture and infrastructure; (2) embedding training within existing community health initiatives; (3) clear role definition and provider sensitization; (4) inclusion of companion presence in facility checklists and quality improvement indicators; and (5) exploring sustainable financing, public funding, community health budgets, or targeted reimbursements [15].

Policy operationalization also requires addressing doula professionalization. Training frameworks balanced with local adaptation, scope of practice guidance, and sustainable financing models are paramount. Evidence on long-term cost-effectiveness in LMICs is sparse. A systematic review of economic evaluations found that midwife-led and doula-supported care models were cost-saving in high-income settings, but LMIC-specific economic evaluations are urgently needed [24].

Research agenda and priorities

Future research should prioritize context-specific randomized and hybrid implementation trials in LMICs using standardized outcomes such as labor duration, mode of birth, neonatal morbidity, breastfeeding initiation, and patient-reported experiences alongside process evaluations. Economic studies are needed to assess short- and long-term cost-effectiveness, and equity analyses should determine whether interventions reduce disparities among marginalized groups. Standardizing core outcome sets and definitions of “continuous support” would enhance comparability, while provider-focused implementation studies should examine training, acceptability, and health system adaptations for sustainable integration of doulas and birth companions. Specifically, controlled LMIC trials should compare structured birth-companion education interventions with usual care or untrained companionship and measure standardized maternal, neonatal, psychosocial, and implementation outcomes.

Broader implications for respectful maternity care

Integrating companion-based support aligns with rights-based frameworks and WHO recommendations and can act as a lever for broader system improvements [4]. As part of quality improvement initiatives, continuous support models can simultaneously improve clinical metrics and person-centered outcomes, synergies that are particularly valuable in resource-constrained settings where low-cost, high-return interventions are prioritized.

Strengths and limitations

This narrative review offers several methodological strengths that enhance its contribution to the literature on birth companionship in LMICs. First, the explicit focus on LMIC contexts addresses a critical gap, as most existing syntheses aggregate predominantly HIC data. Second, the review employed systematic search strategies across four major databases with transparent, reproducible inclusion criteria. Third, quality appraisal using validated, design-appropriate tools (RoB 2, NOS, JBI, COREQ, AMSTAR 2) provides context for interpreting findings and is explicitly reported for each study. Fourth, the multi-domain synthesis spanning clinical outcomes, psychosocial mechanisms, and implementation considerations offers actionable insights for diverse stakeholders (clinicians, policymakers, program managers, researchers). Finally, the integration of primary studies with systematic reviews balances depth with breadth, situating LMIC-specific findings within the global evidence base.

This narrative review has several limitations. First, the search was restricted to four databases and English-language publications, potentially excluding relevant studies from non-Anglophone LMIC regions published in regional databases or other languages. Second, as a narrative rather than systematic review with meta-analysis, the synthesis involved interpretive judgment rather than statistical pooling, precluding precise effect size estimates specific to LMICs. Third, while we used validated quality assessment tools, heterogeneity in study designs, companion types, and outcome definitions prevented formal GRADE evidence profiling. Fourth, the search was conducted in November 2024, and rapidly evolving literature may have produced additional relevant studies since then. Fifth, we did not formally assess publication bias, though the inclusion of grey literature and protocols may partially mitigate this concern. Finally, our final sample included only 8 LMIC primary studies, limiting the strength of LMIC-specific conclusions.

Conclusions

The compiled evidence supports the transformative potential of birth companions and doula care for improving maternal and neonatal short-term outcomes, birth experiences, and person-centered care in LMICs. Implementation success hinges on culturally sensitive program design, provider engagement, pragmatic training and certification frameworks, and clear policy support. Priority research includes LMIC-specific controlled trials comparing structured companion education interventions with usual care or untrained companionship, alongside implementation trials, economic studies, and equity analyses. Ministries of health and facility leaders should consider piloting and integrating companion models into respectful maternity care and quality improvement programs, guided by implementation science principles and community partnerships.
