Six‐pattern p53 interpretation in 1293 vulvar squamous cell carcinomas: inter‐pathologist variation and pattern distribution according to p16 status
Authors: Emma L Kaderly Rasmussen, Susanne K Kjær, Lise G Larsen, Else Mejlgaard, Maria B Franzmann, Alexander K Kjær, Nadia V Salinas, Doris Schledermann, Kirsten Frederiksen, Tatiana Hansen, Birgitte H Winberg, Marianne Waldstrøm, Louise Baandrup
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AimsVulvar squamous cell carcinoma (VSCC) is classified into human papillomavirus (HPV)‐associated and HPV‐independent types, primarily using p16 immunohistochemistry, with p53 staining playing a complementary role since a subset of HPV‐independent VSCC is driven by TP53 mutations. We aimed to assess the robustness of the six‐pattern p53 classification by evaluating interobserver agreement and mapping pattern distribution in relation to p16 status.MethodsWe performed p53 immunohistochemistry on 1293 VSCC cases, comprising 832 p16‐negative and 461 p16‐positive cases. Eight pathologists independently evaluated p53, with each case assessed by two pathologists. Expression was classified as wild‐type (scattered or mid‐epithelial) or mutated (basal overexpression, parabasal/diffuse overexpression, absent or cytoplasmic). Interobserver agreement was measured using kappa statistics.ResultsOverall concordance across the six p53 patterns was 66.7%, increasing to 86.9% when dichotomized as wild‐type versus mutated. In the p16‐negative cases, concordance was 68.8% across all six patterns and 82.6% when dichotomized. Corresponding rates in the p16‐positive cases were 62.9% and 94.6%. Kappa values for pairwise assessments ranged from 0.44 to 0.73 (six‐pattern) and from 0.60 to 0.88 (dichotomized). After resolving discordant cases, 79.9% of p16‐negative cases showed a mutated pattern, and 20.1% were wild type (scattered). Among the p16‐positive cases, 93.1% exhibited a wild‐type pattern.ConclusionsFindings support the clinical robustness of the six‐pattern p53 framework, as interobserver agreement was high and most discrepancies were unlikely to impact tumour classification. While p16 proved helpful in p53 interpretation, certain cases remained challenging due to p53 heterogeneity or ambiguous p16/p53 combinations indicating a need for additional molecular testing in such instances.

Introduction

Vulvar cancer mainly (>90%) consists of vulvar squamous cell carcinomas (VSCCs), which have previously been classified according to morphology. In the fifth edition of the WHO Classification of Female Genital Tumors, however, the classification was changed to one reflecting aetiology/molecular pathology separating VSCC into two distinct subtypes: human papillomavirus (HPV)‐associated and HPV‐independent types. 1 The HPV‐associated type is characterized by a diffuse, block‐positive immunohistochemical staining pattern of p16, which serves as a surrogate marker for integrated high‐risk HPV, 1 , 2 , 3 whereas the HPV‐independent type frequently exhibits aberrant p53 expression, indicative of mutations in the tumour suppressor gene TP53. 1 , 3 TP53 mutations carry significant prognostic implications, 3 supporting a further subclassification of HPV‐independent tumours based on p53 status. 4 , 5

Over the years, p53 staining in VSCC has been interpreted with great variation. Most studies have reported the percentage of positive tumour cell nuclei with a broad range of cut‐off levels without considering the distribution and localization of p53 positivity. 6 Recent studies, however, have revealed the crucial importance of the basal cell layer in p53 VSCC interpretation and have demonstrated that p53 expression in this tumour type can be grouped into six distinct patterns considering the proportion and intensity of basal layer staining, the extent of staining into the parabasal layer, and staining localization (nuclear vs. cytoplasmic). 7 , 8 The classification consists of two p53 wild‐type patterns and four p53 mutated patterns and has been shown to correlate with TP53 status in most previous studies. 7 , 8

The majority of studies reporting on the six‐pattern‐based p53 classification of VSCC have been based on cohorts of small size 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 or with preference given to HPV‐independent cases. 7 , 8 , 11 Moreover, there is limited knowledge regarding the practical application of the classification system, particularly regarding the difficulty of patterns assessment for individual pathologists and its reproducibility. 9 , 10 , 12

We aimed to explore the robustness of the six‐pattern‐based p53 classification by assessing inter‐pathologist variation among eight gynaecologic pathologists in the interpretation of whole‐slide p53‐stained tissue sections from 1293 unselected VSCC cases and to comprehensively map the distribution of these patterns in relation to p16 status.

Materials and Methods

Study Cohort

The study cohort has been previously described in detail. 13 In summary, we used the Danish Pathology Data Bank to identify all cases of primary vulvar cancer diagnosed between January 1990 and August 2017 across 13 pathology departments, covering all five regions of Denmark. The Pathology Data Bank contains comprehensive records of all pathology diagnoses performed in Danish pathology departments since 1997, with most departments contributing data dating back to 1970. 14 Formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) tumour tissue blocks were retrieved from hospital repositories, prioritizing surgical specimens when available; otherwise, diagnostic biopsies were used. Specialized gynaecologic pathologists (LGL, EM, MBF, NVS, DS, BHW, MW, LB) reviewed haematoxylin and eosin (HE)‐stained slides to confirm the VSCC diagnosis.

p16 and p53 Immunohistochemical Staining

Immunohistochemical staining was performed on whole‐slide tissue sections (3 μm thick) at a central laboratory (Department of Pathology, Lillebaelt Hospital, Vejle, Denmark) using the Benchmark Ultra automated staining system (Ventana Medical Systems, Roche, Tucson, AZ, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol. Each staining run included appropriate positive and negative controls. For p16 staining, the CINtec p16 Histology Kit (Roche, Tucson, AZ, USA), which contains a monoclonal primary mouse antibody, was used. For p53 staining, the ready‐to‐use monoclonal mouse antibody DO‐7 (Roche, Tuscon, AZ, USA) was applied. The reactions were visualised using the OptiView DAB Immunohistochemical Detection Kit (Roche, Tucson, AZ, USA).

Scoring of Immunohistochemistry and Classification of Cases

The group of eight gynaecologic pathologists that reviewed the VSCC diagnoses also evaluated p16 and p53 immunohistochemistry. Although p16 had been scored in a previous study conducted prior to p53 immunohistochemistry, 15 p53 was scored with access to both the corresponding p16 slide and the HE slide. Each p53 slide was independently assessed by two pathologists who were blinded to each other's scoring and to the clinicopathological data. In case of disagreement, a third pathologist (LGL or LB) evaluated the slide. If consensus was still not reached, the final p53 pattern was determined by consensus (LGL and LB). Two pathologists (LGL and LB) re‐evaluated p16 scoring in a small subset of cases where no prior consensus had been reached, as well as both p16 and p53 scorings in cases exhibiting unusual combinations of p16 and p53 that rendered tumour classification indeterminate (Tables S1 and S2).

Evaluation of p16 was based on the invasive tumour component, with overexpression defined as moderate to intense p16 staining in both the nuclei and cytoplasm of more than 70% of tumour cells (block positivity).

p53 staining patterns were categorized into the six major categories as suggested by Tessier‐Cloutier et al. 8 (Table S1). The wild‐type patterns included scattered, defined as heterogeneous nuclear staining of variable intensities in the basal and parabasal tumour cells, and mid‐epithelial, which shows heterogeneous strong staining in mid‐epithelial cells, with notable sparing of the basal cells. The mutant patterns included basal overexpression with uniformly strong and consecutive nuclear staining in at least 80% of the basal cells without significant parabasal staining, parabasal/diffuse overexpression with uniformly strong nuclear staining of at least 80% of the basal cells and strong parabasal extension, absent, showing complete absence of staining in the presence of a positive internal control, and cytoplasmic, defined by diffuse cytoplasmic staining with or without nuclear staining in the presence of a positive internal control. Prior to the scoring process, each pathologist was provided with a compendium outlining the six‐pattern‐based classification, including examples for each pattern. As part of the training phase, all eight pathologists independently scored 20 cases, which served as learning material. Each of the 20 test cases was subsequently discussed until a consensus was reached. In cases with heterogeneity, scoring was performed on the area with the strongest staining. To differentiate basal overexpression from parabasal/diffuse, we introduced an arbitrary rule that >10% of the tumour should show parabasal overexpression to be assigned parabasal/diffuse. 7

High‐Risk HPV Analysis

In selected cases, previously assessed high‐risk HPV DNA status was incorporated. The methods for the extraction, detection and typing of HPV DNA have been described. 13 In brief, HPV DNA was extracted from FFPE tissue samples using the Maxwell® 16 FFPE Plus LEV DNA Purification Kit and the corresponding Maxwell® 16 robot (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Detection and typing of HPV DNA were performed using the INNO‐LiPA HPV assay (Fujirebio, Belgium), which identifies 32 HPV types, of which the following were regarded as high‐risk types: 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 59 and 68.

Statistical Analysis

Interobserver agreement for p53 scoring was assessed both as observed agreement and using kappa statistics to evaluate the degree of agreement beyond chance for each pairwise combination of the eight pathologists. According to commonly accepted guidelines, kappa values were interpreted as follows: ≤0.20 indicated slight agreement, 0.21–0.40 fair agreement, 0.41–0.60 moderate agreement, 0.61–0.80 substantial agreement and >0.80 indicated almost perfect agreement. 16 All analyses were conducted for the full six‐pattern‐based classification and repeated after merging basal overexpression and parabasal/diffuse overexpression, as these patterns show considerable overlap and no distinct clinical significance. 8 , 11 Analyses were also repeated using a binary classification of p53 status differentiating between wild‐type and mutated. Analyses were stratified by p16 status (positive vs. negative) to explore differences in agreement across subgroups.

Results

Study Population

As previously described, 1544 potentially eligible cases were identified in the Pathology Data Bank. 15 After applying exclusion criteria and performing p16 immunohistochemical staining, 1302 cases remained in the cohort (Figure S1). For the present study, an additional nine cases were excluded due to the absence of invasive tissue in the tissue slides for p53 immunohistochemistry or due to unsuccessful p53 staining. The final study cohort comprised 1293 VSCC cases.

Agreement in p53 Assessment Using the Six‐Pattern Framework

Table 1 presents the correlation between pathologists in the assessment of p53. Concordance across all six p53 immunohistochemical patterns was 66.7% (862/1293). Most discordant pairs (315/431) involved cases where one pathologist had assigned the scattered pattern, whereas the other had classified it as a different pattern. The scattered pattern was also the most frequently reported pattern, and in 52.8% of the 667 cases where at least one pathologist had assigned the scattered pattern, the other pathologist agreed. In comparison, agreement was 30.0% or lower for those cases where at least one pathologist had assigned the mid‐epithelial (43/200), basal overexpression (46/189) or cytoplasmic (12/40) pattern. For parabasal/diffuse overexpression and absent patterns, the agreement rates were 62.0% (289/466) and 74.1% (120/162), respectively. The overall concordance was 73.8% (954/1,293) after merging basal overexpression and parabasal/diffuse overexpression, and 86.9% (1123/1293) when cases were categorized as either p53 wild‐type or mutated (Table 2).

Distribution of the Six p53 Patterns

The distribution of the final p53 patterns by p16 status is shown in Figure 2A,B. In p16‐negative cases, the majority (665/832, 79.9%) exhibited a mutated p53 pattern, distributed as follows: 365 cases with parabasal/diffuse overexpression, 124 cases with basal overexpression, 145 cases with absent expression and 26 cases with cytoplasmic expression. The wild‐type pattern was displayed in 20.1% (167/832) of the p16‐negative cases and all were scattered except for one case with a mid‐epithelial pattern (Figure 3A,B). This case showed high‐risk HPV DNA positive status (data not shown).

Discussion

This study represents the largest investigation to date into the robustness of the six‐pattern‐based p53 classification in VSCC. The overall concordance across all six p53 patterns was 66.7%, with kappa statistics indicating moderate to substantial agreement between pathologist pairs. When classification was simplified into wild‐type versus mutated, concordance increased to 86.9%, corresponding to predominantly substantial to almost perfect agreement. Following consensus review, 79.8% of p16‐negative tumours exhibited a mutated p53 pattern and 20.1% were wild‐type (scattered). Nearly, all (93.1%) p16‐positive cases showed a wild‐type p53 pattern. It is recognized that in certain p16/p53 combinations, the specific p53 pattern—rather than the dichotomized wild‐type/mutated distinction—may be decisive for tumour classification, and in some cases, additional molecular testing may be necessary. This applies particularly to tumours that remained challenging to classify or exhibited ambiguous p16/p53 profiles.

Classification Challenges

Assessment of p53 expression has recently been refined by the introduction of a standardized six‐pattern classification system, replacing the previously heterogeneous approaches. 5 , 7 , 8 It is crucial for pathologists to recognize that both wild‐type and mutated p53 expression can manifest through several distinct patterns, some of which are readily identifiable, while others present classification challenges due to overlapping features and tumour heterogeneity. In our study, we found that the strong scattered pattern was difficult to distinguish from mid‐epithelial expression and p53 overexpression, while the weak scattered pattern resembled absent staining. Likewise, distinguishing basal overexpression from the parabasal/diffuse overexpression pattern was difficult. These findings align with previous reports. 8 A consistent challenge in the evaluation of p53 has been tumour heterogeneity. This may have been particularly pronounced in our study, as whole‐slide sections were used for all cases, and approximately 50% were surgical specimens with extensive tumour areas. Yang et al. initially used tissue microarrays (TMAs) and found that 26 of 27 cases initially classified as absent showed adjacent mid‐epithelial staining when re‐evaluated on whole‐slide sections, 5 emphasizing the importance of examining sufficiently large tumour areas. However, larger tissue blocks may be more susceptible to suboptimal fixation, potentially resulting in weaker p53 staining in certain regions. Tessier‐Cloutier et al. further proposed that heterogeneity in p53 expression might reflect subclonal acquisition of TP53 mutations. 8

Given that the six‐pattern classification framework is relatively new, its robustness depends not only on technical and biological factors but also on experience and training. Kortekaas et al. reported that two experienced gynaecological pathologists agreed on the six p53 patterns in 48 out of 59 cases. 12 Similarly, Thompson et al. found substantial concordance, with a kappa value of 0.74, among seven consultant gynaecologic pathologists and two gynaecologic pathology fellows who classified 68 VSCC cases into HPV‐independent p53 abnormal, HPV‐independent p53 wild‐type, HPV‐associated or unknown categories based on combined evaluation of p16 and p53, with HPV DNA in situ hybridization (ISH) and TP53 sequencing performed in some cases. 10 Finally, Jeffus et al. highlighted the importance of experience and training, observing that nine pathologists with different levels of expertise in different practice settings and countries initially agreed on p53 classified into wild‐type or abnormal in only 25 out of 50 cases, which improved to 35 out of 50 cases after educational training. 9

Distribution of p53 Patterns

Following resolution of discordant cases, 79.8% of p16‐negative cases exhibited mutated p53 expression, whereas 20.1% showed a wild‐type (scattered) pattern. The distribution aligns with previous reports 5 , 11 including a large, unselected sample of 450 p16‐negative VSCC cases from a wide geographical origin. 11 Among the p16‐negative/p53 mutated cases in our cohort, the distribution of specific p53 patterns was also consistent with previous literature, with the majority showing a parabasal/diffuse overexpression pattern, followed by basal overexpression, whereas the cytoplasmic pattern was rare. 5 , 8 , 11 , 17

Among the p16‐positive cases, the distribution of p53 patterns showed that 93.1% were wild‐type, of which the mid‐epithelial pattern accounted for 34.7% (of p16‐positive cases). The mid‐epithelial pattern has been shown to be specific for HPV‐associated tumours 5 , 18 ; however, previous studies have included smaller sample sizes of p16‐positive VSCC cases. Yang et al. reported that 57.3% of 82 p16‐positive tumours showed the mid‐epithelial pattern, 5 whereas Thompson et al. found this pattern in 9 of 20 p16‐positive VSCC cases, 18 and all three p16‐positive cases studied by Tessier‐Cloutier et al. demonstrated the mid‐epithelial pattern. 8 Compared to previous studies, we observed a higher proportion of the scattered p53 pattern among p16‐positive cases. One possible explanation relates to differences in the interpretation of classification criteria. The mid‐epithelial pattern is defined by notable sparing of the basal cells, which can be subjective. Some of our p16‐positive cases classified as scattered may have been interpreted as mid‐epithelial by other groups. Conversely, certain cases we classified as scattered may have been close to the absent pattern (Figure 3E,F). Although the absent pattern is generally associated with mutated p53, it has also been described as a characteristic feature of HPV‐associated VSCC, where it is referred to as a ‘null‐like’ pattern. This staining is thought to result from HPV‐mediated degradation of p53 via the viral E6 oncoprotein and the ubiquitin‐mediated proteolytic pathway. 10 Differentiating weak scattered from absent (null‐like) p53 staining can be particularly challenging. In our study, we applied a strict definition of the absent pattern, requiring complete loss of staining in tumour cells in the presence of a positive internal control. This may explain the low frequency of absent cases among the p16‐positive group. A broader definition was used by Thompson et al., who classified cases as null‐like even when isolated tumour cells or small clusters showed positivity. Using this approach, they reported the absent (null‐like) pattern in 11 of 20 p16‐positive VSCC cases. 18 Similarly, Yang identified the null‐like pattern in 27 of 82 p16‐positive cases. 5

Ambiguous p16/p53 Profiles

Some cases exhibited rare combinations of p16 and p53. In p16‐positive tumours, overexpression (basal or parabasal/diffuse) or cytoplasmic staining of p53 may rarely occur, commonly referred to as double‐positive tumours. In our study, 25 out of 461 p16‐positive tumours (5.4%) displayed this combination (Figure 3C,D), with eight of these cases testing positive for high‐risk HPV DNA, all within the p53 parabasal/diffuse group. Similarly, Yang et al. reported four such cases, representing 4.9% of 82 p16‐positive tumours, which exhibited mutated p53 expression despite p16 positivity. 5 Rakislova et al. identified two cases with a mutated p53 pattern among 41 p16‐positive VSCC cases (4.9%) in a combined cohort from Spain and Mozambique. 19 Although they did not specify the p53 immunohistochemical pattern or assess TP53 mutational status, HPV DNA was detected in both cases, leading to their classification as HPV‐associated VSCC. 19 In contrast, Yang et al. performed HPV ISH, which was negative in all four double‐positive cases, and confirmed the presence of TP53 mutations through sequencing, concluding that these tumours are best classified as HPV‐independent, p53 mutated VSCC. 5 Likewise, Ordi et al. identified one of 10 p16‐positive tumours exhibiting mutated p53 expression, which also harboured a TP53 mutation. 17 Further research is needed to clarify this subset, particularly since the mechanism underlying p16 overexpression in the absence of HPV remains poorly understood.

Another ambiguous profile involved the p16‐negative case with mid‐epithelial p53 expression (Figure 3A,B). This case also tested positive for high‐risk HPV DNA. Yang et al. similarly reported one such case with this p16‐negative/mid‐epithelial p53 combination, which was HPV ISH positive and showed no TP53 mutation upon sequencing. 5 Based on these findings, the authors proposed that the presence of the mid‐epithelial pattern should classify a tumour as HPV‐associated, regardless of p16 status. The exact mechanisms underlying the mid‐epithelial pattern remain unclear. HPV infects the mitotically active cells of the basal layer, which are typically the primary site of viral integration, 20 and this may explain the degradation of p53 observed specifically in the basal layer and with possible compensatory upregulation of wild‐type p53 in the parabasal layers. 18 It is also suggested that the basal p53 sparing reflects the senescence of tumour cells infected with persistent hrHPV. 7

Classification of VSCC

From a clinical perspective, accurate classification of VSCC is essential, particularly when treatment strategies and follow‐up protocols differ between subgroups. It has been proposed that VSCC should be categorized into HPV‐associated, HPV‐independent p53 mutated, and HPV‐independent p53 wild‐type subtypes based primarily on p16 and p53 immunohistochemistry, and a specific classification system has recently been proposed by Yang et al. 5 Our findings support the clinical utility of this framework in a practical clinical setting. Most cases are readily classified based on these markers alone as interobserver agreement was high and not all discrepancies are clinically relevant. According to the classification suggested by Yang et al., p16‐positive tumours are considered HPV‐associated if p53 shows a mid‐epithelial or scattered pattern or an absent (null‐like) pattern in combination with HPV detection by ISH. In this context, distinguishing between these patterns has limited diagnostic relevance. In contrast, although rare, differentiating the scattered pattern from non‐absent p53 mutated patterns in p16‐positive tumours remains of significant diagnostic importance. In p16‐negative tumours, distinguishing the scattered pattern from all other p53 expression patterns is critical, as it directly determines tumour classification within Yang's framework, 5 whereas differentiating between the various p53 mutated patterns seems to have no clinical relevance. Although specific mutated p53 expression patterns may correlate with distinct TP53 mutations, 8 such distinctions are only clinically meaningful if they have prognostic implications. In challenging cases with ambiguous p53 patterns that could lead to different tumour classifications, p16 staining and morphology of possible precancerous lesions at the tumour margin may aid classification; however, molecular testing for HPV and TP53 status remains the most reliable approach.

Strength and Limitations

Our study has several strengths. To date, it represents the largest cohort of VSCC worldwide with assessment of p16 and p53. Analyses were performed at a central laboratory, where the p53 protocol has recently been approved by the immunohistochemical quality control NordicQC. 21 Whole‐slide tissue sections were used for immunohistochemistry, ensuring a comprehensive assessment of staining patterns, particularly in cases with tumour heterogeneity. Each slide was independently evaluated by two specialists in gynaecological pathology, with a third review conducted in cases of discordance, and a consensus decision when the third review was inconclusive. Finally, we were able to incorporate previously assessed high‐risk HPV DNA status in selected cases with ambiguous p16/p53 profiles. There are also potential limitations to consider. Although an initial pilot reading was performed to align interpretations and a classification compendium was distributed to all pathologists, some variability in scoring among the eight participating pathologists may still have occurred. To mitigate this, third reviews and consensus discussions were restricted to two pathologists. Additionally, tissue samples were up to 34 years old, and variation in fixation methods, as well as tissue age, may have influenced staining quality and antigen preservation. Ideally, p16 and p53 should have been assessed concurrently; however, p16 had already been evaluated as part of a previous study. 15 To address this potential limitation, cases with unexpected combinations of p16 and p53 expression were re‐evaluated, leading to an altered p16 status in seven cases. Lastly, TP53 sequencing was not available for this cohort. While some studies have reported strong concordance between p53 immunohistochemical patterns and TP53 mutations, 8 , 22 a recent study by Ordi et al. identified a discrepancy in 20% of cases, 17 underscoring the need for further research in this area.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the largest study to date evaluating the six‐pattern‐based p53 classification in an unselected cohort of VSCC cases demonstrates its robustness. Nevertheless, some cases remain challenging to classify, and uncertainty remains regarding the correct classification of a small subset of VSCC, highlighting the need for additional molecular testing with TP53 and high‐risk HPV status in such instances. Further research into the aetiology and underlying molecular alterations of VSCC is needed.
