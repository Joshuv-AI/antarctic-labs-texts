Arylpiperazine Derivative NAF19 Inhibits Prostate Cancer Activity and Its Molecular Mechanisms
Authors: Hua Jiang, Mingzhen Xu, Songsong Jiang, Weiqiang Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesAndrogen deprivation therapy (ADT) remains the primary treatment for advanced prostate cancer. However, most patients relapse within 18–24 months, progressing to castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) which is currently incurable. Our preliminary studies identified the arylpiperazine derivative NAF19 as a promising therapeutic agent against prostate cancer, though its precise mechanisms remained unclear. This study aims to systematically evaluate the antitumor effects of NAF19 in prostate cancer cells and elucidate its molecular mechanisms, with a focus on its multi‑target inhibition of the AR/AR‑Vs signaling pathway and key survival pathways.MethodsTo evaluate the effects of NAF19 on prostate cancer cell growth, we treated a panel of prostate cancer cell lines (LNCaP, C4-2, 22Rv1, DU145, and PC-3) representing both androgen-sensitive and castration-resistant phenotypes (including AR-expressing and AR-null subtypes) with varying concentrations of NAF19 for 72 h. Cell viability and sensitivity were subsequently assessed using the CCK-8 assay. In LNCaP and 22Rv1 cells, we further performed qRT-PCR to analyze the mRNA expression levels of AR/AR-Vs and their downstream target genes (PSA and UBE2C), flow cytometry to determine cell cycle distribution, and western blotting to examine the levels of cleaved PARP, antiapoptotic Bcl-2 family proteins, phosphorylated AKT (at Ser473 and Thr308), phosphorylated ERK, phosphorylated S6, as well as total AR and AR-V7. To assess the impact of NAF19 on tumor cell metastatic potential and proliferation, transwell migration and invasion assays, along with EdU incorporation assays, were conducted. Furthermore, a luciferase reporter assay was carried out to evaluate the transcriptional activity of the androgen receptor (AR).ResultsNAF19 exhibited growth-inhibitory effects across all five prostate cancer cell lines. It significantly suppressed AR/AR-Vs downstream gene expression, induced G1-phase cell cycle arrest in 22Rv1 cells, and reduced anti-apoptotic Mcl-1 protein levels while activating apoptosis. NAF19 dose-dependently induced PARP cleavage; NAF19 significantly reduced the phosphorylation levels of AKT (at T308 and S473 sites), ERK, and S6. Functional assays confirmed marked suppression of migration, invasion, and proliferation in NAF19-treated cells.ConclusionsThe novel arylpiperazine derivative NAF19 exerts multi-targeted antitumor effects by concurrently inhibiting AR/AR-Vs signaling pathways and activating apoptotic cascades, thereby potently suppressing the migratory, invasive, and proliferative capacities of prostate cancer cells.

Introduction

Prostate cancer has emerged as a significant health threat to elderly males, exhibiting high incidence rates in Western countries with a persistent upward trend [1]. Notably, Asian countries that previously reported lower incidence rates are now experiencing accelerated growth in prostate cancer cases [2]. Current clinical interventions include surgical resection, radiation therapy, and endocrine therapies targeting androgen blockade [3, 4]. Given the crucial role of androgens in prostate growth and carcinogenesis, endocrine therapy has become a cornerstone in prostate cancer management [5].

The androgen receptor, as a central mediator of androgen signaling, has become a major focus in biomedical research. Understanding its functional mechanisms and the development of AR antagonists is crucial for designing novel therapeutic strategies. Studies have shown that although androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) effectively reduces serum androgen levels by suppressing testicular testosterone production [6], the AR pathway can still be aberrantly activated during the castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) phase. This occurs even under low-androgen conditions, due to persistent adrenal androgen secretion [7] and self-sustaining mechanisms within the tumor microenvironment [8]—processes that directly drive CRPC progression. Of particular concern is the role of AR splice variants, especially AR-V7, which plays a pivotal role in this transition. Clinical data indicate that 20–40% of circulating tumor cells in patients with CRPC treated with abiraterone or enzalutamide exhibit constitutive activation of AR-V7. Patients expressing AR-V7 show significantly lower PSA response rates—indicating treatment resistance—as well as shorter median progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) [9]. Therefore, the development of bifunctional inhibitors capable of targeting both the full-length AR (AR-FL) and its splice variants holds promise as a strategic approach to overcoming drug resistance in CRPC [10, 11].

However, significant challenges remain. Although current therapies include both steroidal and nonsteroidal AR antagonists, their clinical benefits are limited—they have not substantially improved patient survival and often lead to complications such as antiandrogen withdrawal syndrome with long-term use [12, 13]. More critically, frequent mutations in the AR ligand-binding domain (LBD) can render traditional antagonists ineffective or even cause them to act as agonists, severely restricting their therapeutic utility [14].

In this context, identifying lead compounds with novel mechanisms of action is of critical importance. The arylpiperazine scaffold, owing to its unique conformational flexibility and favorable drug-like properties, has emerged as a promising pharmacophore in medicinal chemistry. Previous studies have shown that some arylpiperazine derivatives exhibit antiprostate cancer activity in vitro by targeting multiple tumor-related pathways, including the AR [15, 16]. However, most of these compounds possess limited potency, or their precise mechanisms of action—particularly their effects on AR splice variants and crosstalk with key resistance pathways such as PI3K/AKT—remain insufficiently elucidated. Therefore, developing novel, potent, and multi-target inhibitors based on the arylpiperazine scaffold to simultaneously inhibit AR/AR-Vs and block key survival signaling pathways represents an attractive strategy to overcome treatment resistance in CRPC.

Based on this strategy, we designed and synthesized a series of novel arylpiperazine derivatives. Among them, initial screening identified compound NAF19 (Fig. 1), which demonstrated significant antitumor activity in prostate cancer models [17]. However, its precise molecular mechanism in prostate cancer—particularly whether it possesses the intended dual inhibitory effect on AR/AR-Vs and multi-pathway intervention capabilities—remains not fully understood

Materials and Methods

Antibodies and Reagents

Total-AKT (Abcam, USA, Cat# ab200619); AR and AR-V7 specific antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, USA, Cat# 19672T and 68492 respectively); Anti-cleaved PARP1 antibody and Bcl-2 (Abcam, USA, Cat# ab215971, ab239794, ab54515, Cleaved Caspase-3 #2302, ab237432-100ul and Bcl-2 #16904 respectively); p-AKT (Thr308) and p-AKT (Ser473) (Affinity Biosciences, USA, Cat# Phospho-pan-AKT1/2/3(Thr308) and AF0016, respectively); Mcl-1(SAB, USA, Cat# Mcl-1 Polyclonal Antibody), and p-ERK (Santa Cruz, USA, Cat# sc-7383); Phospho-S6 Ribosomal(Abmart, China, Cat# Phospho-S6 Ribosomal Protein (Ser235+Ser236) Anti); Bax(Abcam, USA, Cat# ab77566); Recombinant Human Bcl-xL(Beyotime, China, Cat# P7248-500μg)

Cell Culture

The prostate cancer cell lines DU145, PC-3, and C4-2 were cultured in RPMI-1640 complete medium supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS). LNCaP and 22Rv1 cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 complete medium containing 10% (v/v) charcoal-stripped fetal bovine serum (FBS; androgen-depleted) [18]. All cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂.

Quantitative Reverse Transcription-Polymerase Chain Reaction (qRT-PCR)

After cell attachment, the negative control group was treated with PBS while the experimental groups were treated with either 4 μM or 8 μM NAF19, followed by culture in RPMI-1640 medium containing 2% FBS for 24 h. Subsequently, all cells were trypsinized and collected for further analysis. Total RNA was extracted from tissues, plasma, or cultured cells using TRIzol reagent. RNA concentration and purity were assessed by measuring the A260/A280 ratio with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer, and only samples with ratios between 1.8 and 2.1 were considered suitable for downstream applications. Subsequently, 5 µg of the obtained RNA was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using PrimeScript RT reagent. The primers were synthesized and sequenced by Shanghai Generay Biotech Co., Ltd., with the following sequences: AR-FL forward (5′-CCTATCCCAGTCCCACTTGTGTCA-3′) and reverse (5′-TACTTCTGTTTCCCTTCAGCGGC-3′), AR-v7 forward (5′- TCTGGGTGTCACTATGGAGC-3′) and reverse (5′-CAACCCGGAATTTTTCTCCCA-3′), PSA forward (5′-GTTGTCTTCCTCACCCTGTCCG-3′) and reverse (5′-AGCAAGATCACGCTTTTGTTCCTG-3′), UBE2C forward (5′-CTGGGCGATAAAGGGATTTCTGCC-3′) and reverse (5′-GCGAGAGCTTATCCTCAGGTC-3′), and 36B4 forward (5′‐CTCACTGAGATTCGGGATATG‐3′) and reverse (5′‐CTCCCACCTTGTCTCCAGTC‐3′). The qPCR reaction was performed in a 25 μL system containing 12.5 μL SYBR® Premix Ex Taq™ II (2×), 1.6 μL dNTP mix, 1 μL Taq DNA polymerase, 1 μL each of forward and reverse primers (10 μmol/L), and reaction buffer to volume, under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 92 °C for 20 s, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 96 °C for 2 s, extension at 85 °C for 20 s, and annealing at 80 °C for 6 s, with fluorescence acquisition at 75 °C and subsequent melt curve analysis. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method with triplicate experiments and mean values used for analysis.

Construction of AR Promoter–Luciferase Reporter Plasmids

Two fragments of the human androgen receptor (AR) gene 5′-flanking region were amplified by PCR from the BAC clone RP11-807F19 (GenePharma), which contains the AR gene: an 8-kb fragment (−6885 to +1115) and a 1.7-kb fragment (−600 to +1115). These fragments were individually cloned into the pCR™2.1-TOPO® vector (Invitrogen) and subsequently subcloned into the pGL4.19[luc2CP/Neo] rapid-response luciferase reporter vector (Promega). The accuracy of the resulting plasmids, designated pGL4-ARpro8 and pGL4-ARpro1.7, was confirmed by DNA sequencing.

Reporter Gene Assay

Cells were transiently transfected with either an androgen response element (ARE)–luciferase reporter plasmid (containing three tandemly linked ARE sequences upstream of the luciferase reporter gene) or the AR promoter–luciferase reporter constructs pGL4-ARpro8 and pGL4-ARpro1.7, following the protocol described in reference 21. To ensure consistent transfection efficiency, transfections were performed in 10-cm culture dishes; after transfection, cells were trypsinized and seeded in triplicate into 24-well plates, followed by overnight recovery before treatment. Luciferase activity was normalized to total protein concentration in each sample.

Luciferase Reporter Assays

After 48 h of cell culture, the medium was aspirated, and luciferase activity was measured using the Luciferase Assay System (Promega). Briefly, 1× Lysis Buffer was prepared with distilled water and added to the 24-well plate (100 µL per well). Following a 2-min incubation at room temperature to ensure complete cell lysis, the lysates were transferred to 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. A 20 µL aliquot of the supernatant was mixed with 100 µL of luciferase substrate in a fresh tube, and luminescence was quantified using a Promega GLOMAX 20/20 Luminometer [17].

Cell Viability Test

The Cell Counting Kit (CCK-8) is a WST-8 [chemical name: 2-(2-methoxy-4-nitrophenyl)-3-(4-nitrophenyl)-5-(2,4-disulfophenyl)-2H-tetrazolium, monosodium salt]-based assay kit primarily used for cytotoxicity and cell proliferation experiments. Compared with traditional MTT assays, the CCK-8 kit demonstrates superior characteristics including rapid detection, high sensitivity, and operational efficiency. Its working principle involves the reduction of WST-8 by mitochondrial dehydrogenases in the presence of an electron mediator, generating a highly water-soluble orange-yellow formazan product. The color intensity is directly proportional to the number of viable cells (indicating proliferative capacity) and inversely proportional to cytotoxic effects. After 1.5–2 h of incubation, the optical density (OD) is measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader, with the OD value serving as an indirect indicator of viable cell quantity. The experimental procedure consists of: (1) Harvesting logarithmically growing cells, washing with PBS, trypsinization, and seeding at 3 × 103 cells/well in 96-well plates for 12–24 h incubation; (2) Treating cells with NAF19 or enzalutamide at various concentrations (0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 μM) and time points (72 h) with appropriate controls and replicates; (3) Replacing media with 100 μL CCK-8 working solution (10% reagent in medium) and incubating for detection; (4) Measuring absorbance at 490 nm and calculating cell viability rates.

Western Blot Analysis

Total protein was extracted from cells of each treatment group according to the manufacturer’s protocol, with protein concentrations determined by Bradford assay. Proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and subsequently transferred to Polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes at 250 mA constant current for 60 min at 4 °C. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 90 min at room temperature (21.5 °C). Following blocking, membranes were cut to separate reference bands, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies at specified dilutions: AR (1:1000), AR-V7 (1:500), PSA (1:500), β-actin (1:3000), P21 (1:500), Cyclin D1 (1:500), CDK4 (1:1000), cleaved caspase-3 (1:500), cleaved PARP1 (1:1000), Bcl-2 (1:500), AKT (1:500), p-AKT (Thr308) (1:1000), p-AKT (Ser473) (1:500), Mcl-1 (1:500), and p-ERK (1:500). After washing, membranes were incubated with goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:10,000) for 2 h at room temperature (21.2 °C). Protein bands were visualized and quantified by densitometry, with relative protein expression levels calculated as the ratio of target protein band intensity to β-actin band intensity.

EdU Cell Proliferation Assay

For the EdU proliferation assay, transfected cells were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated with EdU-containing culture medium for 2 h at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Following incubation, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min and subsequently stained with Apollo staining solution for 30 min. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI at room temperature for 30 min before imaging. The experiment was performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

Transwell Migration Assay

At 48 h post-transfection, cells were suspended in serum-free medium and seeded into the upper chamber of Transwell inserts. The lower chamber contained RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. After appropriate incubation periods, cells that migrated through the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and stained with Giemsa solution. Nonmigrated cells on the upper surface were carefully removed, and migrated cells on the lower surface were counted under a microscope using ImageJ software for quantitative analysis.

Colony Formation Assay

Cells in optimal growth condition were plated at a density of 1×103 cells per well in culture plates and maintained in a 37 °C, 5% CO₂ incubator for approximately 14 days. During this period, cell growth and medium color changes were regularly monitored. Following the incubation period, colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.5% crystal violet solution. The number of visible colonies was counted microscopically, and the colony formation rate was calculated as (number of colonies/number of seeded cells) × 100%.

Cell Cycle Analysis

Transfected cells were collected by centrifugation at 500×g for 5 min and counted using a hemocytometer to adjust the cell concentration to approximately 2×10⁶ cells/mL. After fixation with 70% ethanol, cells were treated with RNase A to eliminate RNA contamination. Cells were then permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS and stained with propidium iodide (PI; final concentration 50 μg/mL, Sigma) for 15 min at room temperature in the dark for DNA labeling. Cell cycle distribution was analyzed by flow cytometry, with results expressed as the percentage of cells in each cell cycle phase.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 23.0), with graphs generated by GraphPad Prism (version 6.0). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Intergroup comparisons were conducted using Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), as appropriate. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Impact of NAF19 on Prostate Cancer Cell Proliferation

To evaluate the effects of NAF19 on prostate cancer cell growth, we treated five distinct cell lines—LNCaP, C4-2, 22Rv1, DU145, and PC-3—representing both androgen-sensitive and castration-resistant phenotypes (including AR-expressing and AR-null variants) with varying concentrations of NAF19 for 72 h. Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay to determine drug sensitivity.

Results demonstrated that NAF19 exhibited growth-inhibitory effects across all tested cell lines (Fig. 2A), with calculated IC50 values of 5.733 µM for 22Rv1 and 6.545 µM for LNCaP (Fig. 2B). To comprehensively model both androgen-sensitive and castration-resistant prostate cancer, we selected LNCaP (androgen-sensitive) and 22Rv1 (castration-resistant) for subsequent experiments.

NAF19 suppresses the growth of prostate cancer cell lines. A Following 72-h treatment with various NAF19 and enzalutamide concentrations, cell viability was assessed by CCK-8 assay and expressed as percentage relative to untreated controls. B The 72-h IC50 values of NAF19 in prostate cancer cell lines were determined. Data represent mean values from three independent experiments

NAF19 Inhibits Prostate Cancer Cell Proliferation and Metastasis In Vitro

The study investigated the antitumor effects of NAF19 on prostate cancer cells through comprehensive in vitro analyses. EdU incorporation assays revealed that 8 μM NAF19 treatment for 48 h significantly inhibited the proliferation of 22Rv1 cells compared with control groups (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3A). Colony formation assays further demonstrated a concentration-dependent reduction in clonogenic capacity, with NAF19-treated cells showing markedly fewer colonies than untreated controls (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3B). Transwell invasion assays using Matrigel-coated chambers showed that NAF19 treatment substantially decreased the number of penetrating LNCaP and 22Rv1 cells through the basement membrane (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3C), indicating significantly impaired invasive potential. Cell cycle analysis by flow cytometry revealed that 8 μM NAF19 treatment for 48 h induced G1-phase arrest in both cell lines, as evidenced by a significant increase in G1-phase cell population compared with untreated controls (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3D). These collective findings demonstrate that NAF19 effectively suppresses proliferation, invasion and cell cycle progression in prostate cancer cells through multiple mechanisms.

NAF19 suppresses prostate cancer cell growth and malignant progression in vitro. A, B Cell proliferation was assessed through Edu incorporation assays and colony formation experiments, demonstrating significant growth inhibition in prostate cancer (PCa) cells following NAF19 treatment. C Transwell migration and invasion assays revealed dose-dependent suppression of metastatic capabilities in LNCaP and 22Rv1 cell lines after NAF19 exposure. D Flow cytometric analysis showed NAF19-induced alterations in cell cycle distribution across PCa cell lines, suggesting cell cycle arrest as a potential mechanism of action

Effect of NAF19 on Prostate Cancer Cell Apoptosis

To further investigate the biological impact of NAF19 on prostate cancer cells, we treated cells with varying concentrations of NAF19 (0, 4, and 8 μM) for 48 h. Subsequent western blot analysis revealed dose-dependent cleavage of PARP in both tested cell lines, a hallmark of apoptotic activation. These findings demonstrate that NAF19 effectively induces apoptosis in prostate cancer cells (Fig. 4).

NAF19 induces apoptosis in prostate cancer cells. Treatment of 22Rv1 and LNCaP cells with varying NAF19 concentrations for 48 h resulted in cleavage fragment of PARP, as detected by western blot analysis

Effect of NAF19 on AKT and ERK Signaling in Prostate Cancer Cells

To investigate the mechanism of NAF19 action, we examined its impact on PI3K/AKT and ERK pathways. Treatment of 22Rv1 and LNCaP cells with 0, 4, or 8 μM NAF19 for 48 h reduced phosphorylation levels of AKT (T308 and S473) and ERK, as detected by western blot. Downregulation of p-S6, a downstream target of AKT/mTOR signaling, further confirmed NAF19-mediated inhibition of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR cascade (Fig. 5).

NAF19 inactivates AKT and ERK signaling. Cells were treated with varying concentrations of NAF19 for 48 h, followed by western blot analysis to determine protein expression levels

Impact of NAF19 on AR Signaling Pathway

Given the known negative feedback regulation between PI3K and AR signaling pathways, where PI3K inhibition may activate AR signaling, we investigated whether NAF19 modulates AR activity in prostate cancer. In LNCaP cells transfected with the ARR3-Luc reporter plasmid (containing three tandem androgen response elements to assess full-length AR [AR-FL] transcriptional activity) and 22Rv1 cells transfected with UBE2C-Luc (containing the promoter region of AR variant [AR-V]-regulated gene UBE2C to evaluate AR-V activity), NAF19 treatment significantly suppressed luciferase expression (Fig. 6A), indicating its dual inhibition of both AR-FL and AR-Vs.

NAF19 suppresses AR transcriptional activity and downstream gene expression. A Luciferase reporter assays were performed in LNCaP and 22Rv1 cells transfected with AR-responsive plasmids following treatment with varying NAF19 concentrations (12/24 h), demonstrating dose- and time-dependent inhibition of AR signaling. B qRT-PCR analysis revealed NAF19-mediated downregulation of AR target genes (PSA and UBE2C) under both androgen-deprived and androgen-supplemented conditions, with asterisks denoting statistical significance. (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001)

Further analysis of downstream targets revealed that NAF19 downregulated PSA mRNA (an AR-FL-regulated gene) under both androgen-depleted and androgen-supplemented conditions. Notably, in the presence of androgens, PSA reduction became evident only after prolonged exposure (>12 h), suggesting delayed pharmacological effects in this context. For AR-Vs (whose activity is androgen-independent), NAF19 consistently suppressed UBE2C mRNA expression in androgen-free conditions (Fig. 6B), confirming broad-spectrum AR pathway inhibition.

To further explore the mechanism by which NAF19 suppresses AR transcriptional activity, we examined the protein levels of AR. As shown in Fig. 7A, NAF19 treatment led to a reduction in both full-length AR (AR-FL) and AR splice variants (AR-Vs). To investigate how NAF19 downregulates AR expression, we performed real-time quantitative PCR to measure AR mRNA levels. The results revealed that NAF19 significantly decreased the mRNA levels of both AR-FL and AR-V7 (Fig. 7B). Furthermore, we transfected LNCaP and 22Rv1 cells with the AR promoter/enhancer luciferase reporter plasmid pGL-ARpr1.7 (–600 to +1500), which contains the AR gene promoter/enhancer region and the 5' untranslated region. Luciferase assay results demonstrated that NAF19 reduced reporter gene expression (Fig. 7C), indicating that NAF19 suppresses AR gene transcription. Taken together, these findings suggest that NAF19 decreases AR protein expression by inhibiting AR gene transcription, thereby reducing the activity of the AR signaling pathway.

B The effect of NAF19 on AR mRNA expression was assessed at 12 and 24 h post-treatment. C Cells transfected with an AR promoter-driven luciferase reporter gene construct were used to evaluate the effect of NAF19 (8 μM) on luciferase activity, reflecting AR transcriptional activity. Effects of NAF19 on Bcl-2 Family Proteins

To explore the effects of NAF19 on the Bcl-2 family of proteins, we investigated its potential mechanism in inducing apoptosis. Based on previous findings suggesting that NAF19 triggers a certain level of cell death and given reports linking PI3K pathway inhibition to apoptosis involving Bcl-2 family proteins, we examined the expression changes of these proteins at different time points after NAF19 treatment. Western blot analysis revealed that, 12 h following drug exposure, the antiapoptotic protein Mcl-1 was notably downregulated, whereas the levels of other Bcl-2 family members—including Bcl-2, Bcl-xL, and the proapoptotic protein Bax—remained unchanged (Fig. 8). These results suggest that the apoptotic effect induced by NAF19 may be closely associated with the reduction of Mcl-1 expression.

Discussion

The AR signaling pathway plays a pivotal role in the development and progression of prostate cancer. Although many currently available therapies approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) demonstrate promising anticancer effects at the initial stages, they often fail to halt disease progression over time, leading to the emergence of CRPC—a highly lethal stage of the disease [19]. This clinical challenge highlights the urgent need for the development of novel therapeutic agents or the identification of new molecular targets, which has become a central focus of ongoing research efforts.

In addition to the classical ligand-dependent AR signaling, nonligand-activated pathways also contribute significantly to CRPC progression, particularly the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, which is closely interconnected with AR signaling. The PI3K/AKT pathway regulates various cellular processes, including metabolism [20], tumor cell survival [21], proliferation [22], migration [23], and cytoskeletal remodeling [24]. Aberrant activation of this pathway has been widely observed across multiple cancer types [25]. Notably, abnormal activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway is detected in approximately 40% of primary prostate cancers and in 70–100% of metastatic cases [26]. This dysregulated activation is often driven by genetic mutations, gene amplifications, or altered mRNA expression levels of components within the PI3K pathway. These molecular alterations are strongly associated with patient prognosis. For instance, PTEN—a tumor suppressor gene that functions as a negative regulator of the PI3K/AKT pathway—has been shown to be downregulated in association with high Gleason scores, postprostatectomy recurrence, and early metastasis [27, 28]. Furthermore, loss of PTEN is commonly observed in about 30% of primary prostate cancers and 60% of CRPC cases, resulting in constitutive activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway [26, 29]. Accumulating evidence suggests that hyperactivation of this pathway correlates with resistance to ADT, disease progression, and poor clinical outcomes.

Based on these observations, we designed and conducted the present study to comprehensively evaluate the antitumor potential of compound NAF19 in prostate cancer using systematic experimental approaches. The compound NAF19 discussed in this study originated from our preliminary systematic screening of novel arylpiperazine derivatives as antagonists of drug-resistant AR. In that lead compound investigation, we synthesized and evaluated a series of derivatives, several of which (such as compounds 17, 19, 20, and 23) demonstrated excellent antiproliferative activity and AR antagonistic efficacy in vitro. Notably, compound 19 stood out with superior binding affinity (FP assay IC50 = 1.01 μM) and AR reporter gene inhibition rate (71.5%), and molecular docking analysis suggested its strong interaction with the AR ligand-binding pocket (LBP). However, that research primarily focused on preliminary compound screening, structure-activity relationship (SAR) studies, and binding mode predictions. The specific anticancer mechanisms of these promising lead compounds, including compound 19, at the cellular and molecular levels—particularly their effects on AR signaling pathway transcriptional regulation, on key survival pathways (such as PI3K/AKT and MAPK/ERK), as well as their ability to induce apoptosis and cell cycle arrest—have not yet been thoroughly elucidated. Therefore, selecting NAF19 for in-depth mechanistic investigation in this study is both methodologically consistent and purpose-driven, as its structure shares the core arylpiperazine scaffold with highly active members of the lead compound library. Our findings demonstrate that NAF19 markedly inhibits the proliferation of prostate cancer cells. Further mechanistic investigations revealed that NAF19 treatment leads to reduced phosphorylation of AKT, ERK, and S6 proteins, indicating its ability to simultaneously suppress both the AKT and ERK signaling pathways, ultimately triggering apoptosis.

Given the well-established link between AKT/ERK pathway inhibition and regulation of Bcl-2 family proteins [30, 31], we further examined the impact of NAF19 on key apoptosis-related proteins. Our results show that NAF19 selectively reduces the expression of Mcl-1, an antiapoptotic factor, suggesting that downregulation of Mcl-1 is a crucial mechanism underlying NAF19-induced apoptosis.

Moreover, considering the complex negative feedback regulatory relationship between the PI3K/AKT and AR signaling pathways [32], we further assessed the effect of NAF19 on AR signaling. Our data indicate that NAF19 suppresses AR gene expression at the transcriptional level, thereby reducing the levels of both full-length AR (AR-FL) and its splice variants (AR-V). This downregulation results in a significant decrease in the activation of downstream target genes.

Limitations and Future Research Directions

Despite the promising findings, several limitations should be acknowledged and addressed in future work. First, the current experimental design did not include conditions with exogenous androgen supplementation when evaluating the pharmacological effects of NAF19. Given that androgen levels critically regulate prostate cancer cell behavior and AR activity, this omission limits a more nuanced understanding of whether NAF19 effectively antagonizes AR function and induces apoptosis within a physiologically relevant hormonal milieu. Second, the lack of systematic testing across a range of androgen concentrations leaves open the possibility that varying hormone levels may influence drug efficacy. To address these gaps, future investigations should include apoptosis assays and AR signaling analyses under conditions supplemented with different androgen concentrations, including both physiological and castrate levels. Such experiments will be essential to validate the proposed mechanism of action for NAF19 and will form a key part of our subsequent research efforts.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates that NAF19 effectively inhibits prostate cancer cell proliferation and induces apoptosis through multiple signaling mechanisms. Mechanistically, the downregulation of both Mcl-1 and AR proteins appears to be central to its anticancer activity, with Mcl-1 playing a particularly important role in mediating apoptotic effects. While the complete molecular mechanisms of NAF19 in prostate cancer remain to be fully elucidated, these findings provide valuable insights into its mode of action and lay a solid theoretical and experimental foundation for its future development as a potential therapeutic agent for prostate cancer treatment.
