Interdisciplinary management of a viable term abdominal ectopic pregnancy
Authors: Sarena Hayer, Natasha Q. McGlaun, Katerina A. Furman, Michael R. Manuel, Gouri B. Sadananda, Daniel Manzoor, Swati Bhardwaj, Tanu Patel, Sara Dayanim, John A. Ozimek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAbdominal ectopic pregnancies are rare and associated with increased maternal morbidity and mortality. As the majority are diagnosed early in gestation, there are few reports of management of advanced abdominal ectopic pregnancies.CaseA 41‐year‐old gravida 2 para 1 presented with fatigue and a known left ovarian cyst. She was found to have a term, viable abdominal ectopic pregnancy and underwent exploratory laparotomy, abdominal delivery, left oophorectomy, and supracervical hysterectomy. The patient and her infant recovered well and were discharged home in stable condition.ConclusionIn this unusual case, multiple imaging modalities were needed to arrive at a diagnosis and plan for optimal surgical management. Interdisciplinary care planning at a well‐resourced referral center allowed for the successful management of this complex case.

INTRODUCTION

Abdominal ectopic pregnancies (AEPs) are highly uncommon, accounting for only 1% of ectopic pregnancies in the United States [1]. Although rare, AEP is associated with high maternal mortality and morbidity, with estimates of maternal mortality occurring in 3% of AEPs [2], almost eightfold higher than in tubal ectopic pregnancies [1]. AEPs involve pregnancy implantation in the peritoneal cavity with normal‐appearing tubes and ovaries, although abdominal reimplantation after a ruptured tubal ectopic pregnancy, ruptured rudimentary uterine horn, or uterine scar dehiscence has also been posited as a mechanism [3].

AEPs are now being diagnosed earlier in gestation, likely due to improved imaging access and modalities [4], and there are few cases of advanced AEPs (after 20 weeks of gestation) documented in the literature, with most arising from countries with less access to medical resources. In light of the rarity of advanced AEPs, as well as the challenges in diagnosis and management, we present a case report detailing interdisciplinary management of a term, viable AEP delivered at a quaternary referral center in the United States, complicated by the presence of a 30 cm adnexal mass. This case uniquely illustrates management of an AEP at a well‐resourced medical center, where multiple imaging modalities were used to clarify surgical planning and interdisciplinary collaboration was central to the formation of this patient's plan of care. The patient provided consent for publication of this case report.

CASE

A 41‐year‐old gravida 2 para 1 with chronic hypertension and recently confirmed positive urine human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) test presented to our center with fatigue and abdominal distention. Her history was also notable for a right oophorectomy for a dermoid cyst approximately two decades ago. She reported a left dermoid cyst at the time of that procedure that was not removed due to the risk of having to undergo bilateral oophorectomy at a young age. Over time, the patient reported that the left ovarian cyst had slowly enlarged; however, in the past several months, she noticed more rapid abdominal distention. Although the patient was not concerned for pregnancy due to a history of very irregular menses, a routine urine hCG was collected prior to a computed tomography (CT) scan ordered by an outpatient provider to evaluate her abdominal distention. When the test resulted positive, the study was not completed, and the patient presented to our center 4 days later. On exam, she had severe‐range blood pressures and her abdomen was extremely distended and firm. Laboratory evaluation was remarkable for protein to creatinine ratio of 0.33 mg/mg. On bedside ultrasound, no clear view of the uterus could be obtained due to obstruction from the ovarian mass. A transvaginal ultrasound was performed, which demonstrated a small uterus with a thin endometrial stripe. A repeat abdominal ultrasound was then performed by a maternal fetal medicine specialist who visualized a 30 cm left adnexal mass. The ultrasound was technically very difficult; however, posterior to the mass, a viable fetus was noted in the left abdomen with limited biometry corresponding to a gestational age of approximately 37 weeks (Figure 1).

Initial ultrasound findings (from left to right): (A) transvaginal sagittal view of the uterus with a thin endometrial stripe, (B) transabdominal transverse view of the fetal head, and (C) transabdominal view of the fetal femur.

The patient was initially treated with oral nifedipine immediate‐release 10 mg. In the absence of baseline laboratory evaluation, given proteinuria and multiple severe‐range blood pressures, the patient was diagnosed with chronic hypertension with superimposed preeclampsia with severe features. Betamethasone was administered as there was a degree of uncertainty surrounding the gestational age of the fetus, and delivery was recommended. Given maternal stability and unknown placentation of the pregnancy, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the abdomen and pelvis was ordered. Gynecologic oncology, trauma surgery, interventional radiology, anesthesiology, and neonatology teams were also consulted for interdisciplinary surgical planning.

MRI demonstrated a 30.7 cm, multicystic adnexal mass and confirmed an abdominal gestation with the fetal head in the splenic flexure and coccyx in the right hemipelvis (Figure 2). The placenta was located in the right hemiabdomen, inseparable from the ascending colon but without definite bowel invasion. A CT angiogram (CTA) was also obtained to clarify vascular supply of the placenta and showed that the placenta was contiguous with right lateral uterine wall with a vascular supply arising from the uterus and adnexa.

Sagittal view of the abdominal ectopic pregnancy in the left abdomen on T2‐weighted magnetic resonance imaging of the abdomen and pelvis.

On hospital day 2, an interdisciplinary conference was held to confirm the surgical plan. Due to the vascular supply arising primarily from the uterus and adnexa on imaging, with low suspicion for placental implantation on other intra‐abdominal structures, the decision was made to proceed with surgical management with anticipated removal of the placenta following delivery. Interventional radiology would be available in the event of need for postoperative vascular embolization. Trauma surgery would also be readily available intraoperatively and placement of a femoral arterial catheter was recommended if resuscitative endovascular balloon occlusion of the aorta (REBOA) was needed. The blood bank was notified of possible need for massive transfusion protocol activation and the patient was crossmatched for multiple blood products.

The patient was transported to the operating room where general endotracheal anesthesia was administered and radial and femoral arterial catheters were placed. A midline vertical incision was made. The left ovarian mass was visualized first and omental adhesions were dissected off the mass bluntly and with electrocautery. The left ovarian vessels were ligated and transected and the adnexal mass was removed. Meconium‐stained omentum was visible in the exposed abdomen and the fetus was identified in the left abdomen, encased in an amniotic sac with a large lateral defect and no appreciable surrounding amniotic fluid. The sac was removed and the neonate was delivered out of the abdomen (Figure 3, Video S1).

Intraoperative photo of abdominal delivery following displacement of left ovarian mass from the abdomen.

Following delivery, brisk bleeding was noted in the abdomen, which was packed for control of the hemorrhage. The placenta was examined and noted to be adherent to the right cornua with blood supply arising from substantially enlarged right pelvic sidewall vessels, which were identified as the primary source of blood loss. These vessels were clamped to control blood loss while a supracervical hysterectomy was completed. The vessels on the right pelvic sidewall were carefully dissected away from the surrounding structures, then ligated and transected, and the placenta was removed from the abdomen. Total blood loss was 4760 mL, primarily due to the initial brisk hemorrhage following delivery of the neonate from the large feeding vessels. Resuscitation was guided with intraoperative laboratory evaluation and the patient was ultimately transfused 13 units of packed red blood cells, 13 units of fresh frozen plasma, 3 units of platelets, and 2 units of cryoprecipitate.

The patient was transferred to the surgical intensive care unit (SICU) postoperatively. While she intermittently required vasopressor support intraoperatively, the patient was started on a nicardipine infusion postoperatively for hypertension and received IV diuretics. She was extubated on postoperative day 1 and transitioned off of the nicardipine infusion to oral labetalol. She was transferred back to Labor & Delivery for the remainder of her postoperative care the following day and was ultimately discharged on postoperative day 6.

Regarding the neonatal course, Apgar scores were 1, 5, and 6 at 1, 5, and 10 min of life, respectively, and the infant's birth weight was 3645 g. He was started on positive pressure variation (PPV) and then intubated at 3 min of life due to bradycardia thought to be from the effects of maternal general anesthesia. Chest x‐ray on admission to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) demonstrated normal appearance of lung volumes, lowering the suspicion for pulmonary hypoplasia. He was extubated on day of life 1 and weaned to room air on day of life 3. He did have some initial joint stiffness and movement limitations attributed to fetal positioning, which improved with physical therapy. He was tolerating oral feeds without difficulty by day of life 12 and discharged on day of life 14.

The final surgical pathology of this case was notable for a peritoneum‐like membrane with a smooth external surface that was almost entirely adherent to the maternal surface on gross examination (Figure 4). Only a small portion of the basal plate was exposed, which appeared intact. The ovarian cyst was composed of a mature cystic teratoma with struma ovarii in association with a mucinous cystadenoma.

CONCLUSION

Accurate diagnosis of AEP is challenging given its rarity and lack of specific symptoms [5]. This case did uniquely demonstrate that an AEP may also present as a hypertensive disorder of pregnancy. Advanced AEPs in particular are difficult to diagnose given the limitations of ultrasound alone and likely need for MRI to clarify the pregnancy location [4]. In early AEPs, successful medical management—often with systemic or local methotrexate—has been described, especially with pregnancies implanted in highly vascular areas such as the liver or spleen [5]. Arterial embolization can also be considered, as well as surgical removal, with consideration of a laparoscopic approach depending on the patient's clinical characteristics and the provider's experience [5].

Advanced AEPs, however, are managed surgically with the recommendation for interdisciplinary planning and surgical optimization given the high risk of maternal morbidity and mortality. In this case, given that the vascular supply appeared to primarily arise from the uterus and right adnexa, the tentative surgical plan was for placental removal at the time of abdominal delivery. There are multiple case reports of the placenta being left in situ in advanced AEPs when embedded in vascular abdominal structures, with placental removal occurring in 50%–70% of cases [6]. Additional complications have been noted however when the placenta is left in situ, including abscess formation, ileus, intraabdominal hemorrhage, and prolonged hospital stay; the prevalence of complications appears worsened with methotrexate administration [7], although there are limited data to guide optimal management when the placenta cannot be safely surgically removed.

Fetal demise is common, and in more advanced abdominal gestations, if the pregnancy remains viable, the developing fetus is at risk of abnormalities in development secondary to vascular disruption and/or extrinsic compression. At some point during gestation in this case, the amniotic sac had ruptured, perhaps from increasing compression of the adnexal mass; however, there was no evidence of pulmonary hypoplasia or joint abnormalities on neonatal evaluation.

Little is known about placentation outside of uterine endometrium. A review of implantation in tubal and cesarean scar ectopic pregnancies found higher prevalence of pregnancy failure, likely secondary to less histiotrophic support and limited access to decidual gland secretions [8]. In this case, placental development was sufficient to support normal fetal growth to term. Despite extensive sampling, microscopic evidence of an implantation site was not identified on the surface of the uterus or corresponding adnexa. Rather, histologic sections of the maternal surface of the placenta revealed fibrin/fibrinoid and extravillous trophoblasts involving an adherent layer of fibromuscular/fibrovascular tissue (corresponding to the peritoneum‐like membrane seen grossly), likely representing the site of implantation (Figures 4 and 5). Because the pathology‐related literature pertaining to a situation such as this is lacking, the significance of the remaining pathologic findings in this case is not certain.

In conclusion, while uncommon, advanced AEPs may require multiple imaging modalities to clearly arrive at a diagnosis. Advanced AEPs are managed surgically, and imaging studies beyond ultrasound may be helpful to plan for optimal surgical management. Interdisciplinary planning and collaboration were essential to the outcome in this case however notably, the patient still had an almost 5 L blood loss requiring transfusion of multiple units of blood products, and both the patient and infant required ICU‐level of care postoperatively. While delivery at a well‐resourced referral center allowed for successful management of this complex case, we acknowledge that these resources may not be available in all instances where advanced AEPs are managed. In a resource‐limited setting, there may have been consideration of leaving the placenta in situ to prevent life‐threatening hemorrhage, although there are little data to guide intra‐ and postoperative management of these cases due to their rarity.
