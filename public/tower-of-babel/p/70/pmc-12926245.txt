Hemodialysis elevates postoperative seizure risk without worsening overall outcomes after surgical clipping of unruptured intracranial aneurysms
Authors: Mayuko Miyata, Takuma Maeda, Nobuaki Naito, Hiroki Sato, Shiho Sakai, Akio Teranishi, Yushiro Take, Tomomichi Kayahara, Kaima Suzuki, Hiroki Kurita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatients with end-stage renal disease on long-term hemodialysis (HD) are at increased surgical risks due to chronic uremia, vascular fragility, and associated comorbidities. Limited data exist regarding outcomes of surgical clipping of unruptured intracranial aneurysms in patients on HD. We retrospectively reviewed the data of 425 patients who underwent surgical clipping of unruptured intracranial aneurysms. Among the 425 patients, 15 were on maintenance HD. We performed 1:2 propensity-score matching, adjusting for patient demographics, comorbidities, and aneurysmal characteristics, to yield 30 matched non-HD controls. Clinical data, including postoperative complications and modified Rankin scale (mRS) scores at three months postoperatively, were compared between the two groups. No significant differences were observed between the HD and non-HD groups regarding the incidence rates of postoperative stroke, hemorrhage, or infection. A good functional outcome (mRS score ≤ 3) was achieved in 93.3% and 96.7% of patients in the HD and non-HD groups, respectively (p = 1.000). However, postoperative seizures were significantly more frequent in the HD group than in the non-HD group (53.3% vs. 3.3%, p < 0.001). In the HD group, patients who developed postoperative seizures were significantly less likely to have received prophylactic anti-seizure medication than those who did not develop postoperative seizures (25.0% vs. 85.7%, p < 0.05). Surgical clipping of unruptured intracranial aneurysms in hemodialysis patients appears safe and effective, with outcomes comparable to non-hemodialysis patients despite a higher risk of postoperative seizures. Introduction

Patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD) on long-term hemodialysis (HD) represent a high-risk population for neurosurgery owing to systemic comorbidities and unique neurophysiological challenges. Among the causes of ESRD, autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) is particularly relevant to neurosurgery, as it is associated with an intracranial aneurysm prevalence rate of > 10%—three to four times higher than that in the general population [3, 4, 16, 26]. Patients on HD also have inherent bleeding diatheses, uremic platelet dysfunction, and rapid fluid/electrolyte shifts that may affect surgical outcomes [6, 8]. Prior reports have highlighted that maintenance HD is a risk factor for postoperative seizures in patients who have undergone neurosurgical procedures [14, 28]. This heightened seizure risk is considered to result from a combination of dialysis-related neurophysiological factors—rapid osmotic shifts between blood and the brain during dialysis (dialysis disequilibrium), uremic toxin buildup, and electrolyte imbalances that can decrease the seizure threshold [20, 25]. These concerns underscore the need for tailored perioperative strategies when treating unruptured intracranial aneurysms in patients on HD.

Compared with the general population, individuals receiving HD have a markedly short life expectancy [13]. The candidacy profile of prophylactic microsurgical clipping of unruptured intracranial aneurysms may be narrower than that of patients who are not undergoing HD, given the comorbidity burden and limited life expectancy [13]. However, as outcomes of dialysis continue to improve [13], an increased proportion of patients may live long enough to accrue aneurysmal rupture risk. Thus, the rationale for preventive clipping could be strengthened on an individualized basis, consistent with American Heart Association/American Stroke Association guidance to weigh patient age, comorbidities, and aneurysmal characteristics [19].

Microsurgical clipping remains the definitive treatment for unruptured intracranial aneurysms; however, its risks in patients undergoing HD are not well-defined. A clinical study suggests that patients with ADPKD undergoing surgical treatment for unruptured intracranial aneurysms, involving coiling or clipping, have an increased risk of poor outcomes [17]. Another study reported that poor outcomes occurred only in patients with ADPKD who had ruptured aneurysms [24]. However, studies that specifically examine aneurysmal treatment in patients undergoing HD are scarce. We conducted a matched analysis comparing patients on HD with non-HD controls to evaluate the impact of HD on outcomes after surgical clipping of unruptured intracranial aneurysms.

Materials and methods

This study was approved by the ethics committee of Saitama Medical University International Medical Center (2023-067). The requirement for informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective nature of the study. Patients could opt out based on the information provided on a publicly available website. This study is based on the current version of the Declaration of Helsinki. We retrospectively reviewed a prospectively maintained institutional registry of 478 consecutive cases of surgical clipping performed for unruptured aneurysms between April 2015 and March 2019 (Fig. 1). After excluding patients who underwent bypass-only procedures or had incomplete data, we included 425 patients who underwent surgical clipping of unruptured aneurysms. HD status was confirmed based on documentation of at least three months of thrice-weekly maintenance therapy. The cohort included 15 patients on HD and 410 non-HD patients. After comprehensive counseling on the average life expectancy of patients on HD and anticipated procedure-related complications, surgical clipping was performed only in patients who expressed a strong preference for surgery and provided written informed consent.

A propensity score was computed using logistic regression with seven baseline variables, including patient demographics (age and sex), comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, and seizure), and aneurysmal locations, to ensure balanced cohorts. Prophylactic anti-seizure medication (ASM) use was intentionally not included in the propensity score model because it represents a deliberate perioperative management decision rather than a baseline patient characteristic. Moreover, prophylactic ASM was administered in only 8 patients (1.9%) among the entire non-HD control cohort (N = 415), and inclusion of this variable would have substantially compromised matchability and covariate balance. Propensity score matching was performed using optimal matching with a 1:2 ratio, minimizing the overall distance between matched subjects based on the propensity score, without applying a fixed caliper width, in order to retain all patients in the HD group. After matching, the covariate balance was assessed using standardized mean differences, with < 0.10 considered acceptable (Table S1).

During the study period, prophylactic ASM administration was generally considered in patients perceived to be at higher risk for postoperative seizures, including those undergoing HD; however, the final decision regarding initiation, choice of agent, and duration was left to the discretion of the treating physician. When prophylactic ASM was administered, it was typically initiated at least 1 day before surgery. In patients without perioperative seizures, ASM was discontinued on postoperative day 7, whereas in patients who developed postoperative seizures, ASM was continued for a longer period as clinically indicated.

Intraoperative angiography was not performed in this series; accordingly, patients on HD did not require prophylactic HD sessions for intraoperative contrast administration. Postoperative MRI was performed on postoperative day 5 or earlier if neurological symptoms developed, whereas CT angiography was routinely performed on postoperative day 7. These imaging protocols were applied uniformly, regardless of HD status.

Outcomes were compared between the matched cohorts. The primary outcome was the clinical outcomes at three months after surgery. Secondary outcomes included operative time, postoperative seizures (within 30 days of surgery), surgical complications (infarction, hemorrhage, contusion, and infection), and length of stay. A favorable outcome was defined as a modified Rankin scale (mRS) score of 0–2 at three months after surgery, and a good functional outcome was defined as an mRS score of 0–3 at three months after surgery. All mRS scores were determined by board-certified neurosurgeons. Statistical comparisons were performed using Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables. Multivariate logistic regression was used to identify independent predictors of postoperative seizures. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. SPSS version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA) was used for all statistical analyses.

The median age was 60 [IQR 56–70] and 64 [IQR 58–69] years in the HD and non-HD groups, respectively (p = 0.772). Female sex predominated in both cohorts (60.0% vs. 63.3%; p = 1.000). Comorbid conditions were similarly distributed: hypertension was present in 86.7% of patients on HD vs. 83.3% of non-HD patients (p = 1.000), diabetes mellitus in 13.3% vs. 13.3% (p = 1.000), and hyperlipidemia in 26.7% vs. 23.3% (p = 1.000). In the HD group, the most common underlying cause of ESRD was polycystic kidney disease, accounting for 60.0% of patients, consistent with the known association between ADPKD and intracranial aneurysm formation [16], followed by nephrosclerosis in 13.3%. Chronic glomerulonephritis, IgA nephropathy, diabetic nephropathy, and chronic pyelonephritis were observed in 6.7% of patients each. Aneurysmal characteristics were also comparable between the groups. The distribution of aneurysm locations did not differ significantly; the middle cerebral artery was the most common site in both groups (p = 1.000), followed by the anterior communicating artery (p = 1.000) and internal carotid artery (p = 1.000). No significant difference in aneurysmal size was observed between the groups. The median aneurysm size was 7.0 [IQR 4.0–7.0] mm in the HD group and 5.0 [IQR 4.0–8.0] mm in the non-HD group (p = 0.971). These results indicated that propensity matching achieved well-balanced cohorts for meaningful outcome comparisons (Table S1).

Prophylactic anti-seizure medication (ASM) use differed markedly between groups: eight of 15 patients on HD (53.3%) received prophylactic ASM compared with one of 30 (3.3%) non-HD patients. In the HD group, prophylactic ASM included levetiracetam in 50% of patients, phenobarbital in 16.7%, carbamazepine in 8.3%, perampanel in 8.3%, and lacosamide in 16.7%. In the non-HD group, levetiracetam was used in one patient (100%).

No significant differences in most of the surgical complications were observed between the groups. No major complications, defined as events resulting in permanent neurological deficits or death, were observed in both groups. The rates of minor complications, defined as transient or mild events without lasting neurological sequelae, were the following. Cerebral contusions on postoperative imaging were observed in 6.7% in both groups (p = 1.000). Similarly, the incidence of intracranial hemorrhage was 6.7% of patients on HD and 10.0% of non-HD patients (p = 1.000), and asymptomatic cerebral infarction occurred in 13.3% and 10.0% of patients in the HD and non-HD groups, respectively (p = 1.000). Surgical site infections were not observed in either group. Three months postoperatively, most patients in both cohorts had a favorable neurological status. Favorable outcome (mRS score, 0–2) was achieved in 80.0% of patients on HD and 96.7% of non-HD patients (p = 0.101). Similarly, a good functional outcome (mRS score, 0–3) was achieved in 93.3% of patients on HD and 96.7% of non-HD patients (p = 1.000). Notably, preoperative mRS scores of one patient in the HD group (mRS score, 3) and one patient in the non-HD group (mRS score, 4) did not change after surgery. Thus, patients on HD did not experience excess surgical morbidity in terms of stroke, bleeding, infection, or functional dependency compared with non-HD patients. However, postoperative seizures have emerged as a significant adverse outcome of HD. Postoperative seizures occurred in eight of 15 patients (53.3%) in the HD group and one of 30 patients (3.3%) in the matched non-HD group (absolute risk difference, 50.0%; odds ratio, 33.14; 95% confidence interval, 3.64–302.1; p < 0.001). These findings highlight that HD is strongly predictive of postoperative seizure risk after aneurysm clipping, even when other factors are controlled.

Discussion

In this propensity score-matched analysis, we found that patients on long-term HD who underwent unruptured aneurysmal clipping generally had surgical outcomes comparable to those of non-HD patients, with the notable exception of postoperative seizures. More than half of the HD cohort experienced seizures, with an incidence rate significantly higher than that of the matched controls, indicating that HD dependence was a strong independent risk factor for seizures after surgical clipping of unruptured aneurysms. However, prophylactic ASM administration may help prevent postoperative seizures in patients undergoing HD. We observed that when postoperative seizures were carefully managed, surgical clipping of unruptured intracranial aneurysms in patients undergoing HD yielded outcomes comparable to those in non-HD patients.

Although an association between HD and postoperative seizures after clipping of unruptured intracranial aneurysms has been previously reported, the present study extends prior work by applying a propensity score–matched design and by demonstrating that overall functional outcomes remain preserved despite a markedly increased seizure risk. Fushihara et al. (2019) reported that maintenance HD was associated with early postoperative seizures following surgical clipping of unruptured aneurysms [5], although the number of HD patients in their cohort was small (n = 4). The present study confirmed this association in a matched design and quantified the risk. Similarly, a recent study on chronic subdural hematomas reported that pre-existing chronic kidney disease conferred an adjusted odds ratio of approximately 27 for postoperative seizures [27], highlighting that renal failure, even before HD, profoundly lowered the seizure threshold in patients who had undergone neurosurgical procedures.

Several pathophysiological mechanisms may result in increased vulnerability to seizures in patients on HD. Rapid changes in osmolarity during HD can create dialysis disequilibrium syndrome, wherein fluid moves into the brain faster than solutes, causing cerebral edema. Seizures are the most common manifestation of dialysis disequilibrium syndrome [25]. In the present study, none of the patients had intra-dialytic seizures. However, the long-term dialysis milieu may generate stressors, including electrolyte fluctuations, transient hypotension, and uremic toxins, all of which contribute to neuronal hyperexcitability [11, 20]. Seizures are often caused by uremic encephalopathy [11], and a history of seizures in a patient on dialysis is linked to poor overall survival [22]. In the perioperative context, additional factors may exacerbate risk—for example, surgical manipulation of the brain can provoke cortical irritation, and blood–brain barrier disruption or microinfarcts may serve as seizure foci [10]. The present data revealed no significant differences in the incidence rates of contusion or stroke between the two groups, yet the synergy of surgical stress with HD-related metabolic disturbances likely tipped the balance in favor of seizures in the HD group.

A previous study had advocated selective anticonvulsant use in high‑risk patients such as those on HD [5]. When prophylaxis is considered, levetiracetam is commonly preferred in neurocritical care for its tolerability and low interaction burden. In patients with ESRD, approximately 50% of the drug is removed during a 4‑hour HD, and the labeling recommends a 250–500-mg post‑dialysis supplementation [1, 2, 9, 12, 23]. Lacosamide is likewise effectively dialyzable, with guidance to consider up to a 50% supplemental dose after a 4‑hour session, whereas perampanel is not recommended in cases of severe renal impairment or patients undergoing HD. In the present study, prophylactic ASM administration was associated with fewer postoperative seizures in patients undergoing HD, suggesting its potential for preventing postoperative seizures. Prophylactic ASM may be considered in all patients on HD undergoing intracranial surgery, as suggested by previous studies [5, 14], but with careful attention to dosing and timing relative to dialysis sessions.

The present study did not identify any other adverse outcomes with significantly increased incidence in the HD cohort after matching. Notably, the incidence rates of surgical complications, including bleeding, infarction, and infection, were comparable to those in the non-HD group. This is an encouraging finding, which suggests that with optimized medical management, surgical clipping can be performed safely, even in patients on HD. Most patients in the HD group achieved a good functional outcome, indicating that most of the patients recovered well after surgery regardless of the presence or absence of postoperative seizures. These data contrast with the intuitive expectation that patients with ESRD would have poor outcomes in general; instead, in the present study, the impact of ESRD appeared to be specific to neurological complications, such as seizures, rather than generalized surgical outcomes. The perioperative care at our center might have mitigated some risks—for instance, meticulous blood pressure control and dialysis scheduling might have avoided fluid overload or substantial electrolyte swings perioperatively. Such measures might explain why patients in the HD group did not have an increased incidence of hemorrhage or infection, unlike other reports of surgical contexts involving ESRD [7, 18].

Postoperative seizures may be linked to poor health-related quality of life, even if they do not affect the mRS score [15]. In addition, in cases where postoperative seizures recur and persist for more than 30 min, neuronal damage leading to long-term sequelae may occur [21]. Therefore, careful perioperative management is essential after surgical clipping of unruptured intracranial aneurysms in patients undergoing HD.

Limitations

The present study was limited by the small sample size of patients on HD (n = 15), reflecting the relative rarity of this scenario. While the limited sample size restricts statistical power and precludes definitive conclusions regarding equivalence, the observed effect sizes suggest that our findings are clinically meaningful and hypothesis-generating, warranting validation in larger multicenter studies. Importantly, reports specifically focusing on microsurgical clipping outcomes in patients undergoing HD remain scarce, and the present cohort represents one of the largest single-center series addressing this issue.

The propensity score-matched design strengthened our comparisons, but unmeasured confounders (e.g., subtle differences in general health or medication use) could still influence outcomes.

We focused on early postoperative events; however, long-term outcomes were beyond the scope of this study. Additionally, the definition of postoperative seizures primarily encompassed clinical seizures within 30 days; subclinical epileptiform activity would have been missed without routine electroencephalographic monitoring.

Despite these limitations, the present data clearly identified a clinically relevant hazard of seizures and provided impetus for preventative strategies in this high-risk group.

Conclusion

Patients on HD who undergo surgical clipping of unruptured intracranial aneurysms may have an increased risk of postoperative seizures even when their baseline profile is similar to that of non-HD patients. HD was a predictor of postoperative seizures in the present matched analysis, implicating the unique metabolic and fluid shifts in patients with ESRD as key contributors to neurosurgical morbidity. By anticipating postoperative seizures and adjusting treatment plans accordingly, including the use of prophylactic ASM, patients on HD may derive the full benefit of surgical clipping with minimal harm.
