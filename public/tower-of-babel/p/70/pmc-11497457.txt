A systematic literature review of brownfield sustainability: Dimensions, indicators, and stakeholders
Authors: Dan He, Rosilawati Zainol, Nor Shahida Azali
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn recent years, the necessity of brownfield redevelopment has garnered increasing attention in academic literature. This paper employs a systematic review methodology to analyse current perspectives on brownfield sustainability, with a focus on dimensions, indicators, and stakeholders. Using the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) methodology, this study systematically reviews 40 articles from the WoS and Scopus databases. The findings highlight a shift towards prioritising ecological resilience and cultural preservation, alongside traditional economic, environmental, and social dimensions. The complexity of sustainability indicators is discussed, emphasising their crucial role in assessing redevelopment success. The review also identifies key stakeholders, including governments, developers, and community groups, who prioritise these dimensions due to their impact on sustainable urban development and community well-being. Future research should further explore cultural, ecological, and governance aspects, and refine sustainability indicators to more accurately capture the multidimensional performance of redevelopment projects. This analysis offers valuable insights for both academia and practice, advocating for policies that emphasise inclusive stakeholder engagement and comprehensive sustainability assessments to optimise brownfield reuse.Keywords: Brownfield sustainability, Dimensions, Stakeholders, Indicators, PRISMA, Systematic review

Introduction

Since its emergence in the mid-1980s, sustainable development has gained widespread acceptance as a model for integrating environmental, economic, and social considerations in advancing human progress [1]. The 1992 UN Conference on Environment and Development reinforced this concept through the introduction of Agenda 21, a comprehensive framework addressing sustainability issues [2]. Sustainability is commonly understood through the triple-bottom line framework [3], which highlights the balance between economic, environmental, and social dimensions. In 2015, the United Nations [4] expanded on this by presenting 17 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), aimed at addressing global challenges with targets set for 2030. Among these, SDG 11 focuses on “making cities and human settlements inclusive, safe, resilient, and sustainable.” This goal advocates for the redevelopment of neglected urban areas, such as city centres and green spaces, to foster sustainable urban growth [5]. In this context, revitalising dormant sites like brownfields presents a strategic opportunity for initiating new urban development projects.

The term “brownfield” was first introduced by the United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) in 1992 [[6], [7], [8]]. The EPA defines brownfields as “abandoned, idled, or under-used industrial and commercial facilities where expansion or redevelopment is complicated by real or perceived environmental contamination” [9,10]. In recent years, brownfields have attracted growing attention from both academic researchers and industry experts [[11], [12], [13]]，with extensive discussions highlighting their environmental, social, and economic benefits [[14], [15], [16], [17], [18]], as well as the barriers and challenges associated with their redevelopment [19,20].

The concept of brownfield sustainability has evolved significantly over the decades. Initially, brownfield projects focused primarily on remediating contaminated sites for industrial reuse [21]. Over time, the definition expanded to encompass broader sustainability goals, integrating not only environmental remediation but also economic regeneration and social inclusivity. Key milestones include the establishment of the U.S. EPA's Brownfields Program in the 1990s, which prioritised environmental cleanup, and the subsequent incorporation of sustainable development principles that redefined brownfields as opportunities for urban revitalisation, rather than liabilities to be addressed. This shift was driven by critical legislative developments across various countries, shaping the operational definitions and approaches to brownfield redevelopment. For example, the U.S. Small Business Liability Relief and Brownfields Revitalisation Act not only addressed liability concerns but also encouraged the integration of sustainable practices in redevelopment projects [21]. Similarly, European Union directives enforced stringent environmental standards, necessitating sustainable approaches to site remediation [22]. These legislative frameworks underscore the complexities and challenges of defining and achieving sustainability in brownfield redevelopment, emphasising the need for a holistic approach that incorporates social equity and long-term ecological health alongside economic and environmental considerations.

This expanded definition brings various implications and limitations. While it broadens the scope of brownfield projects to include social and economic dimensions, it also introduces complexities in balancing these aspects with environmental objectives. The financial viability of such projects is often constrained by high remediation costs and uncertainties surrounding the extent of contamination [23]. Although there is potential for community engagement and social benefits, resistance may arise due to historical disenfranchisement or skepticism regarding the effectiveness of cleanup efforts. Environmentally, the emphasis often remains on contamination removal, potentially overlooking long-term ecological health and the integration of green infrastructure.

In recent years, research on the economic, environmental, and social aspects of brownfield sustainability has expanded, broadening its scope. Scholars such as Dixon et al. (2008) and De Sousa (2021) have provided critical frameworks for understanding these complex interactions and emphasised sustainable strategies for transforming these problematic areas into viable, livable places [24,25].

Further elaboration on brownfield regeneration principles and practices is provided by Ferber et al. (2006), Hollander et al. (2010), and Tiesdell (2008). These authors discuss cleanup, design, and adaptive reuse strategies that are essential to sustainable redevelopment. Their work advocates for integrated approaches that combine environmental remediation with economic and social redevelopment, underscoring the need for policies that balance economic vitality with environmental and social integrity [15,21,26]. For example, Hollander et al. (2010) present actionable principles for reusing contaminated and abandoned sites, promoting solutions that enhance livability and community resilience [21].

The economic dimension of brownfield sustainability involves assessing economic viability, sustainable growth, and returns on investment. Numerous studies have explored the economic impacts of brownfield development, including its contributions to employment, investment [[27], [28], [29], [30]], cultural and creative industries [31], and the revitalisation of dense urban areas [32]. Within the environmental dimension, research has focused on understanding the environmental impacts of brownfield development and implementing protective measures. This includes extensive work on soil remediation [33], environmental justice [34,35], and ecological assessments [36], all critical to the restoration and preservation of brownfield sites. In the social dimension, brownfield sustainability requires considerations of community participation [37,38], equity [35,39], and social welfare [32]. Several studies emphasise the importance of involving and empowering local residents in the decision-making processes of brownfield development [40,41].

Sustainable brownfield development requires the active involvement and coordinated efforts of diverse stakeholders [42,43]. These stakeholders include governmental bodies, developers, community organisations, environmental agencies, and other relevant parties, all of whom play crucial roles in advancing brownfield projects. Scholars such as Dair and Williams [44] and Rizzo et al. [45] highlight the importance of stakeholder collaboration and communication in achieving sustainable development goals. Effective cooperation among stakeholders can enhance project outcomes and support urban policies that improve the quality of life for local residents. This collaboration also contributes to improvements in the economic and environmental conditions of the surrounding area [40].

To evaluate the progress and effectiveness of sustainable brownfield development, researchers have proposed various indicators and assessment methods. These indicators span environmental, social, and economic [40,46] dimensions, enabling a holistic evaluation of the sustainability of brownfield projects. For example, Wedding and Crawford-Brown [2] identified 40 indicators to measure the success of brownfield redevelopment, categorised into four groups: environmental-health, financial, habitability, and socio-economic. The combined use of these indicators and assessment methods provides a comprehensive framework for assessing the performance and benefits of brownfield projects within a sustainable development context.

The aim of this study is to conduct a systematic literature review to explore the dimensions, indicators, and stakeholders that influence the sustainable development of brownfields, providing a comprehensive understanding of the subject. To achieve this aim, three objectives were formulated:

Methodology

This research employed the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) 2020 framework to conduct a systematic literature review on the sustainable development of brownfields. The systematic literature review methodology involves evaluating articles based on predefined research questions, selecting and assessing relevant literature in a structured and objective manner [47]. It aims to minimise bias and subjectivity [48,49], and is characterised by its structure, comprehensiveness, transparency and repeatability [48]. A systematic review allows for a thorough understanding of key findings from the reviewed articles and supports the development of a robust research model, thereby enabling more precise future research directions [50]. Although PRISMA guidelines are traditionally used in biomedical research, their rigorous nature is also applicable to environmental studies, facilitating the comprehensive integration of diverse research findings. This approach enables an in-depth exploration of complex interactions in brownfield redevelopment and broader urban planning contexts. However, this study did not register a protocol in accordance with PRISMA guidelines, as these are primarily designed for biomedical research and are not applicable to this study.

Planning the review

This stage involves defining research objectives that address the research question, selecting keywords, and establishing criteria for inclusion and exclusion. This study focused on key definitions related to the research questions and theoretical background of brownfields, including "brownfield (re)development", "brownfield transformation”, “brownfield regeneration”, “sustainable development,” and "sustainability". Synonymous terms were also defined. The study utilised digital databases Web of Science (WoS) and Scopus, which have been employed in previous research. The inclusion and exclusion criteria used in the review are detailed in Table 1. Articles published from 1992 onwards were considered to reflect the formal recognition and subsequent developments in brownfield redevelopment. The year 1992 is significant as it marks the first formal recognition of the term 'brownfield' by the U.S. Congress during a field hearing session on June 28, 1992 [51], marking the beginning of a new phase in urban development policy focused on assessing and remediating previously underutilised or abandoned contaminated sites. The article selection and search criteria were established through a collaborative consensus process. Screening and evaluation of the papers were conducted by three independent reviewers using a standardised checklist to ensure an unbiased approach.

To refine the selection process according to the specified criteria, several steps were undertaken. First, the document types were limited to articles, focusing on scholarly research contributions. Reporting the review

According to Siddaway et al. [48], best practices for systematic reviews includes summarising the literature search and screening process using a flow chart, such as the PRISMA flowchart. Fig. 1 presents the PRISMA flowchart as compiled by the authors. The review concentrated on examining the elements of brownfield sustainability, the range of stakeholders involved, and the indicators used to measure the effectiveness of sustainability practices.

The PRISMA flowchart of article searching and screening used in this study.

Following the initial retrieval of records from WoS and Scopus, a total of 1423 articles were identified. Duplicates and records that did not meet basic inclusion criteria - such as non-English language, publication before 1992, or non-article formats (e.g., conference papers or book chapters) - were removed, reducing the pool to 628 records. These records were then screened for relevance based on their titles and keywords related to brownfield sustainability, narrowing the selection to 276 articles. The abstracts of these 276 articles were assessed for their alignment with the study's research objectives. This focused evaluation aimed to identify articles offering significant insights into brownfield sustainability dimensions, resulting in 112 articles advancing to full-text review.

To narrow the selection from 112 to 40 articles, stringent criteria were applied to ensure each study substantively addressed sustainable development within brownfields. Emphasis was placed on articles that integrated sustainability as a core element of their analysis, rather than merely mentioning related terms. This involved a thorough evaluation of how each study contributed to understanding sustainable practices in brownfield redevelopment, focusing on innovative approaches or significant empirical findings. Only those articles demonstrating a comprehensive and insightful examination of sustainability themes, with clear implications for brownfield practices, were included in the final selection. The 40 selected articles were used in our analysis based on their specific contributions, with different subsets cited in various specialised discussions throughout the paper. This approach ensured that each thematic discussion was supported by the most relevant and impactful articles, maintaining the rigour and coherence of the overall research.

No automation tools were used in the data collection process. This meticulous approach, illustrated in Fig. 1, ensured a systematic and rigorous review, adhering to the PRISMA guidelines to minimise bias and enhance the reliability of the research findings.

Results

Following the rigorous article search and selection process detailed in Section 2, a total of 40 articles (see Appendix A) were deemed suitable for inclusion in this study. This section provides a comprehensive analysis of the selected articles, organised into four main categories: descriptive analysis, dimensions of brownfield sustainability, stakeholders and corresponding indicators.

Although the initial search included articles published since 1992, the final set of 40 articles selected for in-depth review dates from 2006 onwards. This period marks a significant integration of sustainability practices into brownfield redevelopment strategies, driven by evolving environmental policies and a growing global focus on sustainable urban planning. Consequently, selecting articles from 2006 onwards ensures that the review captures the most relevant and contemporary perspectives, reflecting the latest advancements and understanding in the field.

Dimensions of brownfield sustainability

The sustainability dimensions used in this study are rooted in the foundational concepts of the Triple Bottom Line, encompassing the universally recognised economic, environmental, and social aspects of sustainability. In addition to these core dimensions, the study's approach involved a rigorous literature review tailored specifically to brownfield redevelopment. This review identified and integrated additional dimensions frequently highlighted in recent studies as critical to brownfield sustainability. This methodology ensures that the analysis is both theoretically grounded and empirically relevant to contemporary challenges and practices in the field.

The inclusion of sustainability dimension was a key criterion in selecting the articles, guided by secondary criteria. All 40 reviewed articles addressed one or more sustainability-related dimensions, encompassing research areas such as brownfield sustainability assessment, frameworks, methods, indicators, models, and case studies (see Table 4).

Key sustainability dimensions identified in brownfield redevelopment literature.

To ensure a rigorous and structured analysis, the literature has been systematically classified into distinct categories based on the primary focus and methodological orientation of each study. This classification provides a nuanced understanding of the multifaceted approach to brownfield sustainability research:

Assessment: Studies dedicated to evaluating and measuring the effectiveness of sustainability initiatives in brownfield redevelopment projects.

Framework: Works proposing or critiquing theoretical frameworks that guide the conceptualisation and implementation of sustainability in the brownfields.

Approach: Research detailing specific methodological approaches used to address unique challenges in brownfield sustainability.

Indicators: Articles focused on the development and application of quantitative and qualitative indicators to assess sustainability impacts.

Model: Studies employing predictive or simulation models to forecast outcomes or analyse data related to brownfield redevelopment.

Case Study: In-depth analyses of individual brownfield projects, providing empirical insights into the practical application of sustainability principles.

Others: Contributions extending beyond conventional classifications, including policy analyses, technological innovations, and the establishment of sustainability criteria.

This categorisation not only underscores the breadth of the research landscape but also highlights the specific contributions of each article towards advancing the discourse on sustainable brownfield redevelopment. It ensures a comprehensive synthesis of the existing literature, enabling a critical evaluation of both current practices and potential future directions.

In total, 87.5 % of the articles (n = 35) focused on the three key dimensions of brownfield sustainability: Economic, Environmental, and Social. The remaining papers examined one or two of these dimensions. The economic dimension generally addresses the financial aspects and viability of redevelopment projects; the environmental dimension concerns the management of ecological impacts such as pollution, and the social dimension focuses on community engagement, health, and well-being.

Additionally, 27.5 % of the articles (n = 11) addressed dimensions beyond the core economic, environmental, and social aspects of brownfield sustainability, highlighting their importance in comprehensive redevelopment strategies:

Cultural Dimensions [7,40,[53], [54], [55], [56]]: Focus on preserving heritage and community identity, crucial for maintaining cultural legacy and ensuring projects are culturally sensitive, distinct from broader “Social” dimensions.

Communitarian Dimensions [53]: Emphasise enhancing local community cohesion and fostering a sense of belonging, underscoring the importance of community-centred approaches in sustainability.

Governance Dimensions [54]: Involve policy frameworks, regulatory compliance, and stakeholder engagement, essential for effective project management and accountability.

Strategic Dimensions [53,57]: Concern long-term planning and alignment with broader urban development strategies, ensuring sustainability goals are integrated into brownfield redevelopment.

Ecological Dimensions [35,58,59]: Focus specifically on local biodiversity and ecosystem health, distinct from broader “Environmental” management issues.

Physical (Urbanistic) Dimensions [60]: Relate to physical planning and urban design, critical for effectively reintegrating brownfields into the urban fabric.

Spatial Dimensions [58]: Address spatial arrangement and land-use planning, important for optimising space usage and enhancing functional connectivity.

These distinctions are crucial for understanding the targeted impacts and interventions in brownfield redevelopment. As highlighted, besides the three primary dimensions, the cultural dimension emerged most prominently, cited six times. Equally noteworthy is the ecological dimension, mentioned three times in recent literature. These dimensions reflect the different concerns and research perspectives regarding brownfield sustainability.

The literature review demonstrates that brownfield sustainable development adopts a comprehensive, multi-dimensional approach, addressing economic, social, environmental, governance, and ecological aspects across various research contexts. Studies emphasise the importance of incorporating these dimensions into assessment and decision-making processes. Notably, Schädler et al. [57] and Morio et al. [70] utilised the same set of 23 indicators within an integrated assessment model and a multi-criteria genetic algorithm framework. Laprise et al. [54] expanded the SIPRIUS sustainability indicator system, originally developed for brownfield regeneration issues [56], by adding a new governance dimension. This expansion increased the number of indicators from 42 to 55, further enhancing the understanding of brownfield sustainability.

The reviewed articles utilise a range of 10–63 indicators, reflecting the diverse factors considered in measuring brownfield sustainability. The identified sustainability dimensions include not only the three core pillars of sustainability but also extend to ecological [57,59,70], governance [54,73] and cultural [40,54,56] aspects. This comprehensive approach ensures the consideration of economic feasibility, social welfare, environmental protection, and ecological conservation in pursuit of sustainability. The articles also differ in their classification of indicators; while three primarily categorise indicators based sustainability dimensions, eight adopt alternative perspectives to specify particular indicators.

The citation analysis shows that the 2007 article by Wedding and Crawford-Brown is the most cited, with 159 citations. This study focuses on measuring the success of brownfield redevelopment at the site level and includes 40 indicators covering economic, social, and environmental aspects. Its widespread citation can be attributed to the comprehensive system of indicators it provides, serving as a valuable reference for researchers and practitioners. The second most cited article, authored by Schädler et al., in 2011, has 115 citations and presents an integrated assessment model for developing economically and environmentally viable brownfield revitalisation options. These highly influential studies underscore the significance of their proposed indicators for sustainable brownfield development, demonstrating their widespread use and impact in the field.

Stakeholders involved in brownfield sustainability

In recent years, scholars from various countries have increasingly focused on the stakeholders involved in brownfield sustainability. The diverse and evolving perspectives of stakeholders in brownfield projects significantly influence redevelopment efforts [66]. This review includes 16 articles examining the importance of stakeholders in sustainable brownfield. Stakeholder roles were explicitly defined based on descriptions and functions consistently identified in the reviewed literature. For example, developers are highlighted as key drivers of sustainable practices, while governments are noted for their regulatory and policy-making roles. These roles were synthesised from common themes and contributions across the literature, ensuring that the definitions are both representative and empirically grounded. The review identified and ranked key stakeholder groups in descending order of frequency: governments, developers, consultants, environmental groups, investors, community, end-users, contractors, insurers, regulators, media, academic, NGOs, and site owners (see Table 6).

Environmental groups, including environmental companies [67] and associations [76], advocate for environmentally responsible practices. Conversely, investors have the potential to drive sustainable outcomes through financial investments that align with economic, social, and environmental goals. Community members and end-users are identified as key stakeholders in 43.8 % of the articles (n = 7), emphasising their role in ensuring that projects meet local needs and aspirations. Although contractors, insurers, regulators, media, academic, NGOs, and site owners are mentioned less frequently, they still contribute to brownfield sustainability through service provision, regulatory enforcement, awareness-raising, research, and site ownership.

Discussion

The systematic review in Section 3 identifies the key dimensions, indicators, and stakeholders influencing the sustainability of brownfield sites. This section provides an in-depth discussion and analyse of the findings, offering insights and valuable contributions to the practice of brownfield sustainability.

Unveiling the dimensions of brownfield sustainability

The systematic review on sustainable brownfield development highlights a broad range of dimensions explored by researchers, including economic, environmental, and social aspects, along with cultural, community, governance, strategic, ecological, physical (urbanisation), and spatial dimensions. These dimensions reflect the varied focal points and research perspectives within the field. Building on Lin et al. (2019), who noted the expansion of brownfield research beyond environmental aspects to include economic and social factors, this review emphasises a multi-dimensional approach to brownfield sustainability [82]. Zheng and Masrabaye (2023) further expand this perspective by demonstrating the growing global focus on integrating these dimensions into sustainable redevelopment practices [83]. Their bibliometric analysis supports a holistic understanding of brownfield impacts, aligning with trends observed in this review and underscoring the complexity and interconnectedness of sustainable brownfield development across various studies worldwide.

The prominence of economic, environmental, and social dimensions is evident, as they are addressed in 87.5 % of the reviewed articles (n = 35), highlighting their fundamental roles in achieving sustainable brownfield development. These dimensions are widely recognised as essential drivers of successful projects, embodying the economic viability, environmental responsibility, and social inclusivity needed for sustainable outcomes [84,85]. Notably, the cultural dimension is increasingly acknowledged, appearing in six instances within the literature [7,40,[53], [54], [55], [56]], reflecting a growing recognition of the importance of preserving cultural heritage in brownfield redevelopment efforts [86]. Williams and Dair [69] also emphasise the cultural significance of local industrial heritage and events, incorporating cultural content under social sustainable development goals rather than as a distinct category. Given the increasing importance of culture and its influence on shaping communities, as well as its impact on social, environmental, and economic aspects of sustainable development [87], future research should consider effectively integrating the cultural dimension into frameworks and assessments of sustainable brownfield development.

Additionally, the ecological dimension has been highlighted three times in the review, indicating a growing recognition of the role of ecosystems in achieving sustainable brownfield development. Acknowledging and protecting ecological values within brownfields can enhance biodiversity conservation, support ecosystem services, and strengthen overall environmental resilience [35,58,59]. Therefore, adopting an ecosystem-based approach that integrates ecological aspects alongside economic, environmental, and social dimensions is crucial for promoting sustainable brownfield revitalisation.

Shedding light on indicators for brownfield sustainability

The findings in section 3.3 indicate that brownfield sustainability indicators are in a dynamic stage of development. Through the literature review, the authors have highlighted existing indicators related to the sustainability of brownfield sites. As shown in Appendix B, the authors have compiled and presented a comprehensive set of indicators aligned with the three core dimensions of sustainable development, specifically tailored for sustainable brownfield redevelopment. By organising and summarising these indicators, the authors aim to facilitate their practical use in achieving sustainable development goals within brownfield areas. It is important to note that during the classification process, some researchers, such as Cappai et al. [40], Laprise et al. [54], Laprise et al. [56], Chan et al. [75], have combined culture and society into a single category for a more comprehensive analysis. Accordingly, this paper adopts a similar categorisation approach as a reference point.

In researching brownfield sustainability indicators, the authors found that current research primarily focuses on the three main dimensions of sustainable development: economic, environmental, and social. Although there is a growing recognition of cultural, ecological, and governance dimensions, these areas remain relatively under-explored. Additionally, the classification of certain indicators can be contentious. For example, Cappai et al. [40] classify indicators related to proximity to services such as schools, mosques, airports, and health centres as classified under the economic dimension, while most researchers [2,54,56,57,70] categorise these indicators within the social dimension. This discrepancy highlights the subjective nature and complexity of indicator classification, emphasising the need for further research and discussion to address these issues. Future research should aim to expand the sustainability assessment framework to incorporate cultural, ecological, and governance factors and to develop a more comprehensive and precise indicator classification system. This approach will enhance the assessment of brownfield sustainability and provide more targeted policy and management recommendations to support the achievement of sustainable development goals.

The findings of this review illuminate the multidimensional nature of brownfield redevelopment, which integrates economic, environmental, and social considerations with emerging cultural and ecological concerns. This integrative perspective highlights the need for adaptive policy frameworks that can address dynamic stakeholder interactions and complex sustainability challenges. Such frameworks are essential for unlocking the transformative potential of brownfields as valuable assets for urban revitalisation, promoting a shift toward sustainability practices that are both inclusive and ecologically sensitive.

Exploring the role of stakeholders in brownfield sustainability

The sustainable redevelopment of brownfield sites has gained growing attention from scholars and practitioners worldwide. The active involvement of various stakeholders is crucial in enhancing the sustainability aspects of these sites [88,89], as their roles and contributions significantly influence the redevelopment process.

A meticulous literature review systematically documented and analysed the diverse influences and contributions of stakeholders such as governments, developers, consultants, and environmental groups, as outlined in Table 7. Research indicates that not all stakeholders are involved in every brownfield development, nor do they equally influence the sustainability of these projects [69]. Governments and developers, as shown in Fig. 4, are frequently mentioned across all sustainability dimensions, highlighting their central role in brownfield redevelopment processes. Their widespread involvement reflects a multifaceted approach to sustainability, integrating economic growth with environmental stewardship and social responsibility. However, the extensive involvement also raises concerns about potential conflicts of interest, particularly when economic priorities risk overshadowing environmental and social considerations.

Conversely, specialised groups such as environmental organisations and NGOs exhibit a concentrated focus on environmental issues, as indicated by their frequent mentions in this dimension. This suggests that these stakeholders advocate for specific sustainability outcomes, often prioritising environmental concerns. However, this targeted approach may sometimes come at the expense of a more integrated perspective on sustainability.

The critical role of governments is further complicated by the challenges in managing project timelines, which often leads them to collaborate with consultants, academic institutions, or associations. Public sector entities frequently lack detailed control over these timelines, making partnerships with external experts crucial for effectively advancing sustainability goals in brownfield redevelopment projects [40]. This reliance on external expertise underscores the need for governments to integrate diverse perspectives and specialised knowledge to balance and achieve comprehensive sustainability objectives.

This analysis underscores the need for a more strategic engagement of stakeholders to ensure comprehensive integration of diverse perspectives into sustainability planning. Such an approach would better address community needs and align economic, environmental, and social objectives in a more cohesive manner.

Conclusions

This systematic review offers comprehensive insights into the dimensions, indicators, and stakeholders involved in brownfield sustainability. It identifies key dimensions, including economic, environmental, social, cultural, and ecological aspects, underscoring the need to integrate economic viability, environmental responsibility, social inclusion, cultural preservation, and ecological resilience in brownfield redevelopment efforts. The review also emphasises the critical role of various stakeholders – such as developers, governments, consultants, environmental groups, community members, and academic institutions – in advancing sustainable practices and supporting the sustainability of brownfield sites. Additionally, the complexity of sustainability indicators is addressed, with a detailed list of relevant indicators provided to guide future research and practice.

The implications of these findings are significant. Future development policies should emphasise comprehensive sustainability assessments and promote inclusive stakeholder engagement to maximise the effectiveness of brownfield redevelopment. Additionally, the evolving nature of sustainability practices necessitates ongoing refinement of indicators to ensure they accurately reflect the long-term success of redevelopment projects, extending beyond immediate economic benefits to include environmental, social, and cultural outcomes.

Future research should further address the relatively under-explored cultural, ecological, and governance dimensions of brownfield sustainability to create more comprehensive redevelopment strategies. Investigating decision-making processes and power dynamics among stakeholders will will also shed light on their influence on policy implementation and project outcomes, which is vital for evaluating long-term sustainability. Furthermore, as sustainable development practices evolve, refining and validating indicators to capture the multifaceted performance of brownfield projects will be crucial for ensuring accurate and effective assessments.

Future studies should investigate how governmental policies and professional standards impact brownfield redevelopment practices. Understanding these regulatory frameworks’ influence on shaping strategic directions and aligning sustainability practices with broader environmental and social goals will provide insights into their effectiveness. Research could focus on how these frameworks affect the adaptability and success of sustainability strategies in urban development, offering valuable guidance for refining policies and practices.

The limitations of this study are notable. The time frame and language constraints during article selection could have influenced the breadth of literature reviewed. Additionally, the focus on peer-reviewed journal articles indexed in Web of Science (WoS) and Scopus, while ensuring rigour, excludes review papers and conference proceedings. This approach might have omitted potentially innovative ideas from less formal sources. Thus, while the findings highlight significant trends, they should be considered within a broader context of urban development research.
