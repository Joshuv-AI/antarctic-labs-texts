Two-week prevalence and correlates of suicidal ideation among first-visit general-hospital psychiatric outpatients: a cross-sectional study using the PHQ-9 and EPQ
Authors: Ziming Liu, Yiran Zhang, Qingwei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSuicide-related concerns are a clinical priority in general-hospital psychiatry, yet evidence on suicidal ideation (SI) at first visit and its links with sociodemographic factors, personality traits, and depressive symptom patterns remains limited.MethodsWe analyzed electronic intake assessments from first-visit psychiatric outpatients at Shanghai Tongji Hospital (May 2023 to December 2024). SI was defined as PHQ-9 item 9 > 0. Personality traits were assessed using the Eysenck Personality Questionnaire (EPQ). Multivariable logistic regression estimated adjusted correlates of SI. We examined the latent structure of depressive symptoms using factor-analytic approaches, and then applied structural equation modeling (SEM) to compare one-factor versus two-factor depressive symptom structures and to evaluate their associations with SI while accounting for demographic and personality characteristics.ResultsOf 4,389 patients, 2,430 (55.4%) reported SI in the prior 2 weeks. In logistic regression, SI was associated with younger age, female sex, lower education, higher neuroticism and psychoticism, and higher depressed mood, guilty, appetite disturbance, and psychomotor change scores; higher extraversion and fatigue were inversely associated. In SEM, overall depression was positively associated with SI (R² = 0.50). The two-factor model fit well (CFI = 0.977, TLI = 0.987, RMSEA = 0.055, SRMR = 0.045) and explained more variance in SI (R² = 0.93). The cognitive–affective factor was positively associated with SI (b = 3.357, p < 0.001), whereas the somatic–functional factor showed an inverse conditional association (b = − 2.893, p = 0.001).ConclusionsSI was common at first psychiatric presentation and demonstrated heterogeneity across demographic characteristics, personality traits, and depressive symptom patterns. Differentiating cognitive–affective from somatic–functional symptoms provided additional perspective beyond overall depression severity. Background

Suicide is a major global public health concern [1, 2]. Approximately 740,000 people die by suicide each year worldwide, and suicide ranks as the third leading cause of death among individuals aged 15–29 years [2, 3]. Within the ideation-to-action framework of suicide theories [4–7], suicidal ideation (SI), which is commonly defined as thoughts or intent to die by suicide without having enacted suicidal behavior, is regarded as the initial stage of the continuum from suicidal attempt to suicide death and is closely linked to subsequent adverse outcomes. Retrospective cohort studies have shown that individuals with SI have an approximately tenfold higher risk of future suicide death and an approximately threefold higher risk of all-cause mortality [8]. In addition, endorsement of SI on the PHQ-9 suicide item (PHQ-9 item 9, PHQ9s) has been associated with significantly increased risks of suicide attempt and suicide death over a two-year follow-up period [9]. Accordingly, SI represents a key target for the early identification and intervention of suicide risk [9].

Individuals with mental health conditions, particularly those seeking care in general-hospital psychiatric outpatient settings, constitute one of the highest-risk groups for suicide. A history of mental disorders is widely recognized as a major risk factor for suicide [10, 11]. The suicide mortality rate among psychiatric patients has been reported to be more than five times that of the general population [12], and over half of individuals who die by suicide are known to have a mental disorder [13]. Therefore, in general-hospital psychiatric outpatient practice, improving SI detection, clarifying its correlates, and further identifying key symptom domains as treatment target, have important clinical implications.

In routine clinical practice, however, the identification and assessment of SI remain challenging. On the one hand, patients may not voluntarily disclose SI due to shame, fear of hospitalization, and related concerns [14], which can lead clinicians to underestimate suicide risk based on clinical judgment alone [15]. On the other hand, existing SI assessment instruments may be overly broad and introduce substantial confounding [16], potentially limiting rigorous testing of contemporary suicide theories. Moreover, systematic evidence remains scarce regarding the prevalence, correlates, and pathways of SI among patients presenting for their first psychiatric outpatient visit in a general hospital, although this group is a key population for early detection and preventive intervention.

The increasing adoption of electronic medical record (EMR) systems and electronic self-report assessments provides a timely opportunity to leverage routinely collected data for SI risk detection, correlates identification, and mechanistic research. Compared with face-to-face disclosure, patients may be more willing to report SI in electronic questionnaire-based assessments [17], yielding potentially more reliable risk information. In parallel, structured clinical and psychological data can enable clinicians to identify high-risk individuals more efficiently and at lower cost, and can also inform the selection of candidate features for the development of risk prediction models.

Against this background, the present study aimed to characterize SI at first psychiatric contact in a general-hospital outpatient setting using routinely collected intake data, with particular emphasis on understanding how depressive symptom patterns relate to SI. Specifically, we (1) estimated the prevalence of SI indicated by PHQ9s and described associated sociodemographic, depressive symptom, and personality characteristics; (2) examined multivariable associations between SI and clinically accessible intake indicators; and (3) investigated whether considering the dimensional structure of depressive symptoms provides additional insight into how patient characteristics relate to SI at intake.

Methods

Study design, participants, and procedure

This single-center cross-sectional study included patients who attended their first outpatient visit at the Department of Psychosomatic Medicine and Psychiatry, Shanghai Tongji Hospital, from May 2023 to December 2024. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age > 16 years; (2) first visit to the clinic during the study period; and (3) willingness to complete information collection and questionnaire assessments. To enable efficient data collection, we used an electronic self-report platform to obtain sociodemographic information, a self-report measure of depressive symptoms, and a personality assessment. Questionnaire completion time and related indicators were also recorded to facilitate the identification of valid responses.

Sociodemographic variables

Sociodemographic variables included age, gender, and education. Education was categorized into four levels: junior high school or below, high school/technical secondary school, undergraduate, and postgraduate or above.

Depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation

Depressive symptoms over the past two weeks were assessed using the Chinese version of the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [18]. The PHQ-9 is widely used for rapid outpatient screening and assesses nine depressive symptom domains (e.g., anhedonia, depressed mood/hopelessness, concentration difficulties, sleep and appetite problems, and suicidal ideation). Each item is rated on a 4-point scale from 0 to 3: “not at all” (0), “several days” (1), “more than half the days” (2), and “nearly every day” (3), yielding a total score of 0–27.

SI was defined using item 9 of the PHQ-9 (PHQ9s): “Thoughts that you would be better off dead or of hurting yourself in some way.” A score of 0 indicates no SI in the past two weeks, whereas a score of 3 indicates SI nearly every day. Prior evidence indicates that endorsement of PHQ9s is associated with increased risk of subsequent suicide attempts and suicide-related outcomes, supporting its utility as an initial screening indicator [19, 20]. In routine outpatient practice, once SI is disclosed on self-report, they warrant clinical attention at the initial visit because of patient-safety considerations and potential future risk [21], and should not be dismissed solely because they are reported infrequently. Accordingly, SI was defined as any endorsement of PHQ9s (PHQ9s > 0) for the primary analyses.

Personality traits

Personality traits were assessed using the Chinese adult version of the Eysenck Personality Questionnaire (EPQ). The EPQ measures three major personality dimensions: Neuroticism (N), reflecting emotional stability and higher scores indicate greater proneness to negative affect such as anxiety and depressed mood; Extraversion (E), reflecting sociability and activity level (higher scores indicate greater sociability and outward engagement); and Psychoticism (P), reflecting impulsivity and aggressiveness and aspects of social adaptation (higher scores indicate stronger tendencies in these traits). The Chinese version has been revised and validated in Chinese populations, with acceptable reliability and validity [22].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were conducted for all variables. For continuous variables, normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed; normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, whereas non-normally distributed variables are summarized as median and interquartile range. Categorical variables are reported as counts and percentages. Between-group comparisons used independent-samples t tests or nonparametric rank-sum tests for continuous variables, as appropriate, and chi-square tests for categorical variables.

To visualize the age-specific pattern of SI prevalence, we fitted a binomial generalized additive model (GAM) with a logit link, modeling SI status (PHQ9s > 0) as a smooth function of age, and plotted the fitted curve.

To identify independent correlates of SI, we fitted multivariable logistic regression models with SI defined as PHQ9s > 0. Age, gender, educational attainment, Age, gender, educational attainment (modeled as a categorical variable with junior high school or below as the reference category), PHQ-9 items 1–8, and EPQ traits were entered simultaneously as predictors. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Potential multicollinearity was evaluated using generalized variance inflation factors (GVIFs); for predictors with more than one degree of freedom, GVIFs were transformed as GVIF1/(2df) to facilitate comparability across terms.

To examine how participant characteristics and depressive symptoms might relate to SI, we first explored the latent structure of the PHQ-9 using exploratory factor analysis (EFA), including all nine items (PHQ1–PHQ9). Based on the item correlation matrix, we fitted one- to four-factor solutions using minimum residual factor extraction, applying an oblique rotation (oblimin) for solutions with more than one factor. The number of factors was guided primarily by parallel analysis and model indices, including the Bayesian information criterion (BIC), the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and the Tucker–Lewis index (TLI), while also considering statistical fit, parsimony, and theoretical interpretability.

We then conducted structural equation modeling (SEM) to evaluate pathways linking participant characteristics, depressive symptom constructs, and SI. Because PHQ-9 items were treated as ordered categorical indicators, models were estimated using a diagonally weighted least squares approach (DWLS/WLSMV) with robust standard errors. We report unstandardized coefficients (b) on the latent response scale with 95%CIs and standardized coefficients (Std β) for key paths and indirect effects. Model fit was evaluated using the chi-square statistic (χ² and df), the comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis index (TLI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR).

Sensitivity analyses were performed to examine the robustness of the multivariable logistic regression and SEM results. Specifically, both analyses were repeated using a stricter definition of SI (PHQ9s ≥ 2). All tests were two-sided, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed in R (version 4.5.1).

Results

Participant characteristics and prevalence of suicidal ideation

A total of 5,679 questionnaires were collected. After age screening and subsequent exclusions due to missing data or quality control, 4,389 participants were included in the analysis (Fig. 1).

Study flow diagram. Flowchart showing the identification of eligible first-visit psychiatric outpatients, inclusion and exclusion procedures, and the final analytic sample used for multivariable logistic regression and structural equation modeling analyses

Among 4,389 participants, the median age was 29 years (IQR 24–39). The sample comprised 1,192 men (27.2%) and 3,197 women (72.8%). Overall, 3,338 participants (76.1%) had an undergraduate degree or higher. SI, defined as PHQ9s greater than 0, was reported by 2,430 participants in the two weeks prior to the clinic visit, corresponding to a prevalence of 55.4% (95% CI 53.9–56.8) (Table 1). Regarding frequency, 1,249 participants (28.5% of the total sample) endorsed SI on several days, 686 (15.6%) on more than half the days, and 495 (11.3%) nearly every day (Supplementary Table S1).

Compared with first-visit patients without SI, those reporting SI were younger, with a median age of 26 versus 33 years (W = 3,216,740; p < 0.001), and the proportion of SI was relatively higher in young adulthood, lowest in midlife, and slightly increased in older age, yielding a mild U-shaped pattern (Fig. 2). Across depressive symptom domains, PHQ-9 item scores were higher in the SI group, and EPQ traits differed, with higher neuroticism and psychoticism and lower extraversion (all p < 0.001; Table 1).

Multivariable logistic regression: correlates of suicidal ideation

Multivariable logistic regression results are shown in Table 2. Younger age (aOR 0.97 per year, 95% CI 0.97–0.98) and female gender (aOR 1.34, 95% CI 1.13–1.59) were associated with higher odds of SI. Among PHQ-9 symptom items, the strongest associations were observed for depressed mood (PHQ2; aOR 1.48, 95% CI 1.32–1.65) and worthlessness or excessive guilt (PHQ6; aOR 1.72, 95% CI 1.58–1.87). Appetite disturbance (PHQ5; aOR 1.12, 95% CI 1.03–1.21) and psychomotor changes (PHQ8; aOR 1.18, 95% CI 1.09–1.28) showed smaller positive associations, whereas fatigue or low energy was inversely associated (PHQ4; aOR 0.86, 95% CI 0.77–0.96). Neuroticism (aOR 1.05, 95% CI 1.04–1.06) and psychoticism (aOR 1.02, 95% CI 1.01–1.03) were positively associated with SI, while extraversion was inversely associated (aOR 0.98, 95% CI 0.98–0.99).

In sensitivity analyses using a stricter SI definition (PHQ9s ≥ 2), the overall pattern of associations was broadly consistent, although the inverse association for fatigue (PHQ4) was attenuated and no longer significant (Supplementary Table S2). Multicollinearity was acceptable (maximum adjusted GVIF = 1.39; Supplementary Table S3).

Exploratory factor analysis

An exploratory factor analysis was conducted on all nine PHQ-9 items. Sampling adequacy was high, with an overall KMO value of 0.92 and item-level MSAs ranging from 0.90 to 0.96. Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (χ² (36) = 24,210.22; p < 0.001). Parallel analysis supported retaining two factors (Supplementary Figure S1). Model fit improved as additional factors were extracted (Supplementary Table S4); however, we selected a two-factor solution based on parsimony and interpretability, consistent with the planned use of PHQ-9 symptom dimensions in subsequent SEM analyses. Using minimum residual extraction with oblique rotation, the two factors were strongly correlated (r = 0.80). Standardized loadings ranged from 0.34 to 0.95, with loadings below 0.30 suppressed (Supplementary Table S5). The two factors accounted for 62% of the common variance. Factor 1 primarily reflected somatic–functional symptoms, whereas Factor 2 captured cognitive–affective symptoms, including SI.

Confirmatory factor analysis

Based on the EFA findings, we conducted CFA on PHQ-9 items 1–8, comparing a one-factor depression model with a two-factor model comprising cognitive–affective and somatic–functional dimensions. In the two-factor model, PHQ2, PHQ6, and PHQ8 loaded on the cognitive–affective factor, whereas PHQ1, PHQ3, PHQ4, PHQ5, and PHQ7 loaded on the somatic–functional factor. Both models showed good overall fit, with highly similar fit indices (Supplementary Table S6).

Model 1: Single-factor depression mediation SEM

The single-factor mediation SEM, in which a latent depression factor indicated by PHQ1-8 items served as the mediator, showed acceptable fit and explained 50% of the variance in SI (CFI = 0.972, TLI = 0.985, RMSEA = 0.057, SRMR = 0.053). Standardized factor loadings ranged from 0.73 to 0.85 (Supplementary Table S7). Depression showed the strongest direct association with SI (b = 0.439, 95% CI 0.401–0.477; Table 3). Several participant characteristics also showed direct associations with SI, and indirect effects via depression were evident, most notably for neuroticism (b = 0.026, 95% CI 0.023–0.028; Table 4). The indirect effect of education via depression was not significant (b = 0.008, 95% CI − 0.008–0.025). Model 2: Two-factor mediation SEM with correlated cognitive–affective and somatic–functional depression factors

Model 2 decomposed PHQ1–8 items into two correlated latent dimensions: a cognitive–affective factor (PHQ2, PHQ6, PHQ8) and a somatic–functional factor (PHQ1, PHQ3–5, PHQ7). The same set of exogenous predictors was retained as in Model 1. Model fit was good (CFI = 0.977, TLI = 0.987, RMSEA = 0.055, SRMR = 0.045), and the model explained 93% of the variance in SI (R² = 0.93, reflecting variance explained on the latent response scale). Standardized factor loadings ranged from 0.63 to 0.86 (Supplementary Table S8).

In the two-factor mediation SEM, the cognitive–affective factor was positively associated with SI (b = 3.357, 95% CI 1.661–5.054), whereas the somatic–functional factor was inversely associated (b = − 2.893, 95% CI − 4.591 to − 1.195). Given the high correlation between the two symptom factors (r = 0.965), these coefficients represent the unique associations of each factor with SI after accounting for the other factor. After accounting for both symptom dimensions and covariates, female gender (b = 0.468, 95% CI 0.179–0.757) and lower extraversion (b = − 0.014, 95% CI − 0.023 to − 0.005) remained directly associated with SI (Table 5). Indirect effects supported a dual-channel pattern; neuroticism increased SI via the cognitive–affective factor (b = 0.224, 95% CI 0.108–0.340) but decreased it via the somatic–functional factor (b = − 0.151, 95% CI − 0.241 to − 0.062) (Table 6). Taken together, the two-factor model highlighted differential symptom pathways linking stable traits and demographics to SI (Tables 5 and 6; Fig. 4).

Sensitivity analyses of SEM

When SI was redefined using a stricter threshold (PHQ9s ≥ 2), the overall pattern of SEM findings was largely consistent (Supplementary Tables S9–S12). In the single-factor model, the depression factor remained strongly associated with SI (b = 0.545, 95% CI 0.509–0.582; Supplementary Table S9), and indirect effects via the depression factor persisted for neuroticism, extraversion, psychoticism, age, and gender (all p < 0.001; Supplementary Table S10), whereas the indirect effect of education remained non-significant. In the two-factor model, the cognitive–affective and somatic–functional factors continued to show opposing conditional associations with SI (b = 2.897 for cognitive–affective factor, p < 0.001 and b = − 2.333 for somatic–functional factors, p = 0.002; Supplementary Table S11). Specific indirect effects via the two factors remained bidirectional across key predictors, resulting in a positive total indirect effect for neuroticism and a negative total indirect effect for age, while total indirect effects for extraversion, gender, and education were not significant (Supplementary Table S12). Overall, conclusions regarding differential symptom pathways were robust to the stricter SI definition.

Discussion

Prevalence of SI among first-visit psychiatric outpatients

In this single-center cross-sectional study of first-visit patients in a general-hospital psychiatry outpatient clinic, 55.4% reported SI during the two weeks prior to presentation. Most reports were in the lower-frequency range, with fewer patients endorsing frequent or near-daily ideation. The observed overall prevalence was higher than that reported in the general population (16.4%) [23] and fell within the broad range reported among patients with depressive disorders (25%–75%) [24]. Differences across studies may reflect variation in patient populations, clinical settings [25–30], and assessment approaches [31]. In this study, SI was assessed through self-report at intake. Prior work suggests that patients may disclose SI more readily in self-administered formats than during face-to-face interviews [14, 17], which may partly explain the relatively high detection rate. Given that more than half of first-visit patients endorsed recent SI, current study indicates that SI is common at psychiatric outpatient setting and warrants careful clinical attention during the initial evaluation.

Independent correlates of suicidal ideation

In the fully adjusted logistic regression model, younger age and female gender were associated with higher odds of SI. Prior studies report heterogeneous age patterns across populations and clinical settings, including lower rates in midlife, potential non-linear trends, and context-dependent findings in inpatient samples [23, 32, 33]. In first-visit psychiatric outpatients, differential disclosure or recognition across age groups may contribute to the observed association, and SI in older adults may be under-detected because of alexithymia, generational differences in reporting, or attribution of suicidal thoughts to other symptoms [34, 35]. Higher educational attainment showed an inverse association with SI, this was most evident for undergraduate versus junior high school or below, with a similar direction for postgraduate education, consistent with reports that education may confer protection through greater mental health literacy and access to coping resources [36, 37]. At the individual symptom level, depressed mood and worthlessness or excessive guilt showed the strongest independent associations with SI. This is consistent with prior meta-analytic and symptom-network findings [38, 39], and aligns with cognitive models relevant theory emphasizing psychological pain and hopelessness as central mechanisms [4, 40]. Other depressive features showed weaker or less consistent associations after mutual adjustment, indicating heterogeneity in how individual symptoms relate to SI. Psychomotor changes, for example, have been linked in network analyses to cognitive symptoms and SI [38, 41], although the interpretation of this association warrants caution [42]. Furthermore, sleep disturbance has been reported associated with suicidal outcomes in several researches [43, 44], but its independent contribution may vary depending on symptom context and model specification [45–47] and experimental sleep-deprivation studies similarly suggest a complex relationship between sleep and mood-related outcomes [48]. The inverse association observed for fatigue was not robust and is consistent with prior network studies reporting weak or absent direct links between fatigue and SI [38, 41, 49].

Personality traits, as relatively stable dispositions, remained independently associated with SI after accounting for demographic characteristics and individual depressive symptoms, consistent with previous studies [50–56]. Within Eysenck’s framework, neuroticism reflects heightened emotional reactivity [57], Psychoticism-related traits such as hostility and impulsivity have likewise been linked to increased suicide risk under stress [58, 59]. In contrast, extraversion has been associated with lower levels of SI, potentially through greater social engagement and positive-affect resources [60–63], although cross-cultural variation has been reported [64].

Taken together, the multivariable findings indicate heterogeneity across symptoms and stable characteristics in their associations with SI. A dimensional framework may therefore provide a more clinically informative way to understand how these factors relate to SI at first contact.

Depressive symptom dimensions and mediated pathways to suicidal ideation

Depression is increasingly conceptualized as a heterogeneous construct, with accumulating evidence supporting the identification of symptom dimensions or subtypes rather than reliance on global severity alone [65]. Consistent with this view and prior PHQ-based research [66, 67], depressive symptoms were examined as cognitive–affective and somatic–functional dimensions in current study. These dimensions have been linked to partially distinct biological and stress-related processes [68–72], although their implications for SI remain less clearly delineated.

Across models, relatively stable characteristics, including personality traits and demographic variables, were associated with SI either directly or indirectly through depressive symptoms. In the single-factor model, overall depressive severity was positively associated with SI, consistent with prior findings [52, 73]. However, the two-factor model provided a more differentiated account of these pathways.

Specifically, the association with SI was primarily carried by the cognitive–affective dimension. This indicates that the suicide-related signal observed at the item level reflects a broader configuration of affective and self-evaluative distress rather than isolated symptoms, consistent with conceptual models emphasizing psychological pain and negative self-appraisal in suicidal thinking [74–76]. By contrast, the somatic–functional dimension showed an inverse association with SI after accounting for cognitive–affective symptoms. Given the strong correlation between dimensions, this finding represents a conditional association rather than an independent protective effect. Prior literature on somatic symptoms and suicide risk is mixed, with studies reporting positive [77], indirect [78], and weak or non-significant associations [79, 80], suggesting that somatic burden may not convey a consistent suicide-related meaning across depressive presentations. Furthermore, the present finding suggests that, at comparable levels of cognitive–affective distress, additional somatic burden may not proportionally increase SI and may reflect heterogeneity in depressive presentations. One possible interpretation is that somatic-dominant presentations may involve less sustained self-focused or metacognitive processing of suicidal thoughts [81, 82]. However, given the strong correlation between symptom dimensions, alternative explanations such as statistical suppression cannot be excluded.

The two-factor framework also clarified pathways from distal characteristics to SI. Neuroticism influenced both symptom dimensions, with the pathway through the cognitive–affective dimension accounting for most of the suicide-related association, consistent with neuroticism as a trait linked to heightened negative emotional reactivity [83]. For extraversion and psychoticism, indirect effects through the two symptom dimensions were smaller and partly offsetting. Some direct associations persisted beyond symptom mediation, suggesting that their relationships with SI were not fully accounted for by depressive symptom expression alone. Gender and age effects were likewise partially mediated through symptom dimensions, in line with evidence that demographic factors are associated with differences in depressive symptom expression [84–87].

In summary, the two-factor framework supports a symptom-profile interpretation of SI within depression. Once overlapping variance was considered, SI was more closely aligned with cognitive–affective distress than with somatic or functional complaints, indicating differential and conditional associations across symptom dimensions. Clinically, this pattern suggests that assessment at first visit may benefit from attention to symptom configuration rather than global severity alone, and that understanding how demographic and personality characteristics map onto specific depressive profiles may help refine risk stratification in psychiatric outpatient settings.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and does not establish temporal ordering among personality traits, depressive symptom dimensions, and SI. Longitudinal studies are needed to test whether the proposed dual-pathway framework predicts subsequent suicidal behaviors. Second, the sample comprised first-visit patients from a single general-hospital psychiatry outpatient clinic, which may limit generalizability. Replication across sites and clinical settings is warranted. Third, SI was assessed via self-report using PHQ9s. Although suitable for intake screening, this approach is not equivalent to a structured suicide risk assessment and may involve misclassification. Concerns regarding the specificity of PHQ9s relative to comprehensive instruments have been noted [88–91]. Future studies should incorporate structured suicide assessments and longitudinal follow-up to clarify case status and to test the stability of the key pathways identified here. Fourth, the sample was diagnostically heterogeneous and no structured DSM-5 interviews were conducted. Detailed diagnostic information was unavailable, precluding subgroup analyses across specific disorders. The absence of diagnostic stratification limits interpretability and should be addressed in future research. Fifth, the cognitive–affective and somatic–functional factors were highly correlated, indicating substantial shared variance. Coefficient magnitudes may be influenced by collinearity. Future research should examine whether the observed associations are replicated under alternative model specifications. Finally, other clinically relevant factors such as social support, trauma history, comorbid anxiety, substance use, medication exposure [92], and inflammatory markers [93], were not modeled and may provide additional explanatory value.

Conclusions

This study indicates that SI is commonly detected at first psychiatric presentation in a general-hospital outpatient setting. The findings demonstrate heterogeneity in SI across demographic characteristics, personality traits, and depressive symptom patterns. Differentiating cognitive–affective and somatic–functional symptom dimensions provided additional perspective beyond overall depression severity in understanding how patient characteristics relate to SI. These findings suggest that, at first psychiatric clinical contact, SI was more closely aligned with cognitive–affective distress than with somatic–functional complaints once overlapping variance was considered. Accordingly, evaluation of SI in outpatient settings may benefit from attention to symptom configuration in the context of demographic and personality characteristics.
