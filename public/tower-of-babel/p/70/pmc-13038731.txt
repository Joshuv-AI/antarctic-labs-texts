Nomogram based on serum interleukin-33 levels and clinical characteristics for predicting overall survival in hematologic malignancy patients receiving haplo-HDPSCT
Authors: Gulisitan Aisikaer, Kaile Zhang, Ming Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Haploidentical hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (haplo-HSCT) is a crucial modality for managing hematologic malignancies, such as refractory relapsed leukemia and lymphoma. Disease conditions before and after transplantation substantially influence HSCT outcomes. This work focused on establishing a nomogram for predicting outcomes in patients who underwent haploidentical high-dose peripheral blood stem cell transplantation (haplo-HDPSCT). A total of 165 patients who developed hematologic malignancies and who received haplo-HDPSCT at the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University between January 2017 and August 2023 were recruited. A landmark analysis was performed at 1 month post-transplantation. The independent predictors associated with overall survival (OS) were analyzed through Cox regression, and these prognostic factors were subsequently used for nomogram construction. A bootstrap resampling approach was utilized for internal validation. Multivariate Cox regression analysis identified high interleukin-33 (IL-33) levels and pre-transplant measurable residual disease (MRD) positivity as independent predictors of OS. A prognostic nomogram was established on the basis of these risk factors. The nomogram demonstrated superior goodness-of-fit compared with the MRD-only model, as evidenced by lower Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian information criterion (BIC) values (324.796 vs. 331.014; 327.907 vs. 332.570, respectively). The bootstrap-corrected concordance index (C-index) of the nomogram was (0.682, 95% CI: 0.592–0.772), which outperformed that of pre-transplant MRD positivity alone (0.598, 95% CI: 0.522–0.674) and high IL-33 alone (0.624; 95% CI: 0.540–0.708). This superior discrimination was further confirmed by time-dependent C-index analysis. With respect to calibration, the nomogram-predicted survival probabilities were in good agreement with the observed survival rates. Furthermore, decision curve analysis (DCA) indicated that the nomogram had superior net clinical benefit. Stratified by the nomogram-derived cutoff value, patients in the high-risk group experienced significantly poorer OS than those in the low-risk group (P < 0.001). IL-33 can independently predict prognosis in haplo-HDPSCT patients. High IL-33 levels are related to unfavorable prognostic outcomes in haplo-HSCT patients. The present work offers a precise nomogram for OS prediction in haplo-HDPSCT patients, with possible clinical usefulness.

Introduction

Haploidentical hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (haplo-HSCT) has been gaining increasing attention in recent years for the treatment of hematologic malignancies and has become a commonly used strategy when completely matched donors are unavailable [1]. The therapeutic effect is mediated through the graft-versus-leukemia (GVL) effect, which primarily arises when donor T cells recognize the histocompatibility antigens of malignant cells through their receptors [2]. Moreover, when donor T cells recognize these identical antigens in the normal tissues of the host, they can trigger GVHD, which is a potentially fatal condition. About 25% of the patients with malignant tumors following HSCT experience post-transplant recurrence, and another 20% die due to non-recurrent causes, primarily GVHD [3]. In order to increase the overall survival of patients after haplo-HSCT, maintaining the GVL effect while preventing acute GVHD is essential. Although refined transplant techniques—such as haploidentical high-dose peripheral blood stem cell transplantation (haplo-HDPSCT)—have significantly reduced transplant-related mortality, reducing disease recurrence rates remains a challenge, and effective treatment options for post-transplant relapse are scarce [4–6]. Hence, identifying a potent prognostic approach that integrates the above candidate prognostic factors is important for better predicting the outcomes of patients undergoing haplo-HDPSCT.

The use of non-in vitro-generated T-celled peripheral blood hematopoietic stem cells (PBSCs) as grafts results in a good GVL effect at an early stage, which is likely associated with the NK cells within the graft and T cells with anti-tumor effects, which decreases the risk of recurrence [7] and ultimately improves patient prognosis. Numerous transcription factors and cytokines may contribute to the enhanced GVL effects, including IL-10, IL-12, IL-15, IL-18, TNF-a, IFN-γ, and RORγT [8–10]. Among these, Interleukin-33 (IL-33), a member of the IL-1 superfamily, has recently emerged as a key regulator of tissue immunity, yet its specific prognostic value in haplo-HDPSCT remains to be fully elucidated.

Traditional prognostic models often rely on static pre-transplant variables and may overlook dynamic changes in the post-transplant immune environment. A nomogram is considered a reliable and easy-to-use approach with exquisite statistical prediction that allows for predicting individual risk through the introduction of variables according to the disease and patient features [11]. Nomograms are extensively utilized to estimate specific survival, recurrence, and overall survival (OS) rates in cancer patients, helping clinicians design and implement personalized treatments [12–14].Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the prognostic value of serum IL-33 levels in patients undergoing haplo-HDPSCT. Furthermore, we sought to establish and validate a predictive nomogram incorporating IL-33 and other clinical factors, such as MRD status, to stratify patient risk better.

Materials and methods

Study objects

This work enrolled hematologic disease patients who underwent haplo-HDPSCT between January 2017 and August 2023 (n = 175) at the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University. Figure 1 shows the patient screening flowchart. The following patients were included: (1) those with a diagnosis of hematologic malignancy; (2) those with haplo-HDPSCT as the transplantation protocol; (3) those with a peripheral blood hematopoietic stem cell (PBSC) infusion dose (MNC) of 12–20 × 108/kg or CD34+ ≥ 6 × 106/kg; (4) those with sufficient basic clinical and follow-up information. Two classes of patients were excluded from this study: (1) patients with severe organ dysfunction or those with active infection (defined as fever > 38.5 °C, positive blood culture, or distinct clinical symptoms requiring antibiotic treatment) at the time of sampling and (2) those who had previously received allogeneic or autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT). OS was defined as the duration between disease diagnosis and final follow-up or all-cause death. The final follow-up was completed on August 31, 2025. The Hospital Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University approved this study. Definitions

Baseline data collection was completed within 10 days prior to haplo-HDPSCT to assess the pre-transplantation status.The basic clinical information collected included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), existing disease status, donor age, donor-recipient relationships, donor-recipient sex-matched status, ABO blood type-matched status, conditioning regimen, HCT-CI score, complete remission (CR) pre-transplantation disease status, European Society for Blood and Marrow Transplantation (EBMT) score, white blood cell (WBC) count upon diagnosis, MRD status pre-transplant, mononuclear cell (MNC) count (×108/kg), and CD34+ concentration (×106/kg). Measurable residual disease (MRD) was assessed before transplantation using multicolor flow cytometry (MFC) with a sensitivity of 10− 4. For the purpose of this study, an MRD level of ≥ 0.1% was defined as positive, while levels < 0.1% were considered MRD-negative, in accordance with the European Leukemia Network (ELN) recommendations [15]. The HCT-CI was adopted to evaluate the underlying diseases in HSCT recipients [16]. Relapse was defined as the recurrence of peripheral blood leukemic blasts, ≥ 5% blasts within the bone marrow aspirate, new appearance or reappearance of extramedullary leukemia, and biopsy not performed for other reasons. CR referred to < 5% blasts within the BM without extramedullary disorder. Neutrophil engraftment was defined as the first of 3 consecutive days with a neutrophil count > 0.5 × 109/L. Platelet engraftment was defined as the first of 3 consecutive days when the platelet count was > 20 × 109/L with no transfusion. aGVHD and cGVHD grading was performed according to previously reported methods [17, 18]. At 1, 2, 3, and 6 months post-transplantation, the effect and recurrence of transplantation were monitored using flow cytometry immunophenotyping (leukemia minimal residual monitoring), genetic testing, bone marrow aspirate smear, and other relevant testing methods.

Cytokine level measurement

At 1 month post-transplantation, fasting peripheral blood samples (5 mL) were collected, and the serum was isolated via centrifugation within 24 h. To investigate the prognostic impact of immune cytokines associated with GVHD and GVL effects, the serum levels of a cytokine panel (including IL-33) were quantified via a cytometric bead array (CBA) on a flow cytometer. The detailed experimental protocols are provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Donor screening, transplant regimens, and aGVHD prevention and management

Donors were selected using the non-HLA system [19]: (1) Seven patients received the cytarabine (Ara-c) + BUCY regimen; (2) 107 patients received the Ara-c + BUCY + rabbit anti-human thymus immunoglobulin (2ATG) regimen; (3) 33 patients received the fludarabine (FLU) + Ara-c + Bu + ATG regimen; (4) 18 patients received the cyclophosphamide (CTX) + Ara-c + Bu + ATG regimen. The specific pretreatment and mobilization methods used in previous studies [20–22] were employed in this study. The enhanced GVHD prevention plan adopted at our center for preventing and treating GVHD was as follows: 165 patients were subjected to the enhanced aGVHD prevention plan. The details have been explained in a previous report by our research group [20, 23, 24].

Model construction and assessment

The optimal cutoff values for continuous variables (specifically cytokines) were determined via time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and the Youden index at 5 years. To mitigate immortal time bias, a landmark analysis was performed with the landmark time point set at 1 month post-transplantation (coinciding with the timing of serum IL-33 measurement). Consequently, post-transplant events occurring after this landmark time (such as aGVHD) were excluded from the predictor list. On the basis of the independent prognostic factors identified by multivariate Cox regression analysis, a prognostic nomogram was constructed to predict 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS. The discrimination ability and goodness-of-fit of the nomogram were evaluated via the concordance index (C-index) and calibration curves, respectively. Furthermore, decision curve analysis (DCA) was conducted to estimate the clinical utility of the model. Internal validation was performed via a bootstrapping approach with 1,000 resamples.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were compared using the Mann‒Whitney U test, and categorical variables were analyzed via the chi‒square test or Fisher’s exact test. Survival curves were estimated via the Kaplan‒Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to identify prognostic factors for OS. Variables with P < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate model. All the statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 19.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and R software (version 4.0.2). Specifically, the R rms package was used for the nomogram, calibration curves, and DCA; the pec package was used for time-dependent C-index curves; and the survminer and survival packages were used for survival curves. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Screening of prognostic cytokines and optimal cutoff for IL-33

To identify potential biomarkers, we initially analyzed a panel of GVHD/GVL-related cytokines. Our screening revealed that serum levels of IL-12, IL-15, IL-18, TNF-a, IFN-γ, and IL-10 showed no significant correlation with clinical outcomes (all P > 0.05; data detailed in Supplementary Material 2). In contrast, IL-33 exhibited a significant association with survival. Therefore, we focused our subsequent analyses on IL-33. With all-cause patient death as the outcome variable, a 5-year time-dependent ROC curve was created, and the maximum Youden index for OS was utilized to determine the optimal cutoff. The optimal cutoff of IL-33 was finally regarded as 132.50. Patients were then separated into low IL-33 and high IL-33 groups based on the appropriate cutoff for IL-33. Kaplan–Meier survival curves demonstrated that patients in the high IL-33 group had a higher mortality rate (P = 0.004, Fig. 2). There were no statistically significant differences in patient age (P = 0.802), BMI(P = 0.299), sex(P = 0.484), Underlying disease(P = 0.767), Cytogenetic risk(P = 0.171), EBMTstatus(P = 0.278), HCTCI status(P = 0.085), ABO blood match(P = 0.899), Conditioning regimen (P = 0.667), Disease status pre-transplant(P = 0.812), Pre-transplant MRD status(P = 0.945), aGVHD status(P = 0.826), cGVHD status(P = 0.904) and Recurrence status(P = 0.172) between the high and low IL-33 groups (P > 0.05). Nomogram establishment by incorporating clinical and serological factors

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to identify prognostic markers. Univariate analysis revealed that elevated IL-33 levels (P = 0.006) and pre-transplant MRD positivity (P = 0.002) were significantly associated with inferior OS. Subsequently, multivariate Cox regression analysis confirmed that both high IL-33 status (P = 0.004) and pre-transplant MRD positivity (P = 0.001) served as independent prognostic factors for OS following haplo-HDPSCT (Table 3).

Discussion

Haploidentical hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (haplo-HSCT) has revolutionized the treatment landscape for high-risk hematologic malignancies, offering a curative option when matched donors are unavailable. However, the delicate balance between the graft-versus-leukemia (GVL) effect and life-threatening complications, particularly recurrence and GVHD, remains the primary challenge influencing overall survival (OS) [25, 26]. While traditional scoring systems such as the EBMT score rely heavily on pre-transplant static variables, they often fail to capture the dynamic biological changes occurring immediately post-transplant. In this study, we constructed a novel dynamic nomogram based on landmark analysis at 1 month post-transplantation, identifying high serum IL-33 levels and positive MRD status as two independent predictors of inferior OS. The new nomogram model constructed in this study incorporating the above two prognostic factors for OS prediction was subsequently constructed for those receiving haplo-HDPSCT, and internal validation was also conducted. The constructed nomogram had better prediction accuracy and discrimination performance. Moreover, patients who underwent haplo-HDPSCT were classified into high- or low-risk groups, and OS was significantly different between the two groups.

In the unique immune environment of haplo-HDPSCT, cytokines act as critical messengers regulating the delicate balance between GVL effects and GVHD. Our research group has focused intensively on screening a broad panel of GVHD/GVL-related cytokines—including IL-12, IL-15, IL-18, IL-10,TNF-a and IFN-γto identify robust prognostic biomarkers. Interestingly, among these candidates, IL-33 emerged as the sole cytokine with independent predictive value for poor survival in our multivariate analysis. With respect to the timing of measurement, we defined 1 month post-transplantation as the critical landmark for our model. Our team’s parallel animal experiments revealed that while cytokine fluctuations initiate as early as days + 7 and + 14 post-transplant, these early spikes often represent the acute phase of the conditioning-induced cytokine storm. In contrast, the 1-month time point (approximately days + 28 to + 30) typically coincides with neutrophil engraftment and early T-cell reconstitution. As suggested by previous studies, cytokine profiles assessed around Day 28 offer a more reliable window for predicting the onset of significant complications such as aGVHD and long-term outcomes [27]. Therefore, elevated IL-33 levels detected at this specific juncture likely reflect a sustained, pathogenic pro-inflammatory state rather than a transient fluctuation, effectively predicting the trajectory of the allo-immune response.

In haplo-HSCT, cytokines play crucial regulatory roles, ranging from activating immune cells to regulating GVL effects, throughout the transplantation process. IL-33 in the IL-1 superfamily serves as a multifunctional protein that regulates immunity, and its expression is detected mainly within fibroblasts [28], endothelial cells [29], and epithelial cells [30]. IL-33 can bind to the corresponding receptor suppression of tumorigenicity 2 (ST2), which includes three isoforms, namely, the membrane-bound, variant, and soluble forms [31, 32]. The IL-33/ST2L pathway is related to polarizing and mobilizing immune cells [33]. Mechanistically, the association between elevated IL-33 and inferior OS in our cohort contrasts with the classical view that IL-33 is solely a driver of GVHD. Interestingly, our data revealed no significant correlation between serum IL-33 levels and aGVHD incidence, suggesting that an alternative pathway influences survival. IL-33 is a pleiotropic cytokine with a dual nature: while it can drive inflammation, it is also a potent inducer of immune tolerance and tissue repair. Recent studies have indicated that IL-33 signaling preferentially expands ST2-expressing regulatory T cells (Tregs) and promotes type 2 (Th2) immune responses [34, 35]. On the basis of these findings, we hypothesize that a sustained increase in IL-33 might tip the delicate immune balance toward excessive suppression. Specifically, high IL-33 levels may dampen the graft-versus-leukemia (GVL) effect by expanding suppressive Tregs or myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), thereby creating an immunosuppressive microenvironment that facilitates the escape of residual leukemic cells. This mechanism is particularly relevant to our team’s unique transplantation model (haplo-HDPSCT). Although this transplantation model is characterized by potent anti-tumor activity and a generally low recurrence rate, the specific subgroup of patients with elevated IL-33 appears to lose this advantage owing to the aforementioned immune tolerance. Consequently, the poor survival observed in patients with high IL-33 levels is likely driven by an increased risk of disease recurrence due to a compromised GVL effect rather than by cytokine storm-induced organ damage. Recognizing this highlights a clear therapeutic objective: for patients identified with high IL-33, future strategies must focus on modulating this pathway to enhance the GVL effect, thereby eradicating the survival blind spot in our haplo-HDPSCT model.

In the present retrospective analysis, the relevant clinical information from 165 patients with hematologic malignancies was reviewed before, during, and after haplo-HSCT, and the potential risk factors for OS following transplantation were further explored to achieve an effective basis for improving transplantation efficiency, reducing recurrence, and increasing patient survival. The results also support that pre-transplantation MRD status has a predictive value for post-transplant OS, with a predictive value of 0.598. Importantly, however, MRD status alone may be insufficient for predicting overall survival in patients receiving haplo-HDPSCT. In summary, understanding the interplay between cytokines such as IL-33 and the MRD status is critical to optimize outcomes in haplo-HSCT and improve patient prognosis. Further exploration of these factors may increase transplantation efficiency, reduce recurrence rates, and ultimately improve patient survival rates.

Furthermore, the integration of MRD status with IL-33 significantly enhances the predictive precision. MRD monitoring facilitates the independent prediction of long-term prognostic outcomes in AML patients. During HCT, MRD pre-HCT is related to a dismal prognosis. Among the different methods, multiparameter flow cytometry-based MRD analysis has been extensively utilized for patients with no molecular targets. Lucero et al. [36] analyzed 336 patients in CR1 before transplantation, noting that MRD status significantly impacted outcomes. However, our study revealed that while MRD-positive patients had poor outcomes, the addition of IL-33 status allowed for better stratification. For example, MRD-negative patients with high IL-33 levels might still face a dismal prognosis due to immune-related complications or suppressed GVL effects—a subgroup often overlooked by traditional leukemia-focused monitoring. The nomogram demonstrated a superior C-index (0.682) compared with MRD alone (0.598), underscoring the necessity of combining tumor burden markers (MRD) with immune environment markers (IL-33) for holistic prognosis prediction.

Taken together, the results of the Cox regression analysis and the nomogram prediction revealed three predictors: IL-33 status and MRD status. According to the results, the constructed nomogram exhibited superior goodness-of-fit in predicting OS for haplo-HDPSCT patients. Moreover, on the basis of the time-dependent C-index results, this nomogram showed favorable accuracy in predicting the clinical outcomes of haplo-HDPSCT patients. Simultaneously, the calibration and decision curve analysis plots for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS revealed that the established nomogram exhibited superior prediction accuracy and total net benefits. Additionally, the nomogram successfully discriminated haplo-HDPSCT patients into high- and low-risk groups, with high-risk patients exhibiting poorer prognoses (median OS: 54 months vs. 19 months, P < 0.001). This stratification provides a practical framework for personalized post-transplant management. Overall, high-risk patients may require more frequent monitoring.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as a single-center retrospective study, selection bias cannot be entirely ruled out; although internal bootstrapping validation confirmed the model’s stability, external validation in larger, multi-center cohorts is strictly needed before routine clinical application. Second, while we focused on OS, future studies should investigate the specific impact of IL-33 on cause-specific mortality (relapse vs. non-relapse mortality) in larger cohorts. Finally, we must acknowledge a temporal limitation regarding our sampling schedule. As the IL-33 measurements and subsequent nomogram construction relied on data collected beyond the first month post-transplantation, the current model is unable to predict clinical events occurring during the initial 30-day window. Future studies incorporating earlier sampling time points are warranted to address this ‘blind spot’ in the early post-transplant phase.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we successfully established a landmark-based dynamic nomogram that incorporates IL-33 levels and MRD status. This tool outperforms conventional single-factor predictors and provides a reliable, easy-to-use method for early risk stratification in haplo-HDPSCT patients. High IL-33 levels at 1 month post-transplant serve as a critical biomarker for poor prognosis, highlighting the potential of the IL-33/ST2 axis as a therapeutic target in the haploidentical setting.
