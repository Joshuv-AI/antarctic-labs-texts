Enterococcus hirae QT4713-derived dopamine ameliorates intestinal inflammation and MPTP-induced Parkinson’s disease in mice
Authors: Tingting Zhao, Bin Li, Yiming Liu, Jiarui Zhang, Xueni Fan, Mingyang Ao, Yuanlin Niu, Diantong Li, Jin He, Dong Sun, Hui Wu, Tuoxian Tang, Zhenjiang Liu, Xiaodan Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gut microbiota dysbiosis contributes to Parkinson’s disease (PD) pathology by altering dopamine metabolism in the gut–brain axis. Although probiotics and other functional strains have been proposed as microbiome-based interventions, few naturally occurring gut microbes show therapeutic potential for PD. Here, we isolated Enterococcus hirae QT4713 (QT4713) from the hypoxic, low-pressure Qinghai–Tibet Plateau (4713 m altitude). Whole-genome sequencing revealed that QT4713 harbors a tyrosine decarboxylase gene (TyrDc), enabling conversion of L-tyrosine to dopamine in vitro. In mice, QT4713 enhanced antioxidant enzyme activity, reduced inflammatory mediators, reshaped gut microbial composition, and promoted short-chain fatty acid production. Metabolomic analyses indicated activation of L-tyrosine metabolism, with increased L-DOPA and dopamine levels in the colon and feces, accompanied by improved motor performance. In an MPTP-induced PD mouse model, QT4713 alleviated motor and gastrointestinal dysfunction, reduced oxidative and inflammatory damage, and attenuated dopaminergic neuron loss. QT4713 also increased dopamine and tyrosine levels in the striatum. Extending beyond an acute toxin model, QT4713 partially rescued PD-like phenotypes in TMEM175 knockout mice, preserving tyrosine hydroxylase–positive neurons in the substantia nigra. Together, these findings suggest that QT4713 can mitigate gastrointestinal disturbances and other PD-related deficits, consistent with combined effects on catecholamine-related metabolism and gut microbiota remodeling.

Introduction

Trillions of microbes reside in the intestine, forming the gut microbiota, which plays pivotal roles in gastrointestinal homeostasis, basic physiological processes, and central nervous system (CNS) functions. These microbes communicate with the brain through neural, immune, endocrine, and microbiota-derived metabolites1. Germ-free mice exhibit abnormal brain development, function, and behavior2. In both animal models and humans, modulation of the microbiota—through the administration of specific bacterial strains, fecal microbiota transplantation, or antibiotic exposure—has been shown to alter behavior and exert long-term effects on the brain, spinal cord, and enteric nervous system3,4. Additionally, infections disrupt gut-brain communication and contribute to neurophysiological symptoms5. An imbalance or structural change in the gut microbiota has been documented in various mental disorders and neurodegenerative diseases. Moreover, the microbiome, along with its metabolites and components, can influence an individual’s susceptibility to neurological conditions6,7. Given its importance in disease, there is growing interest in understanding the composition and function of the gut microbiome. However, the multidimensionality of host-microbiome interactions and the individual variability of gut flora pose significant challenges in interpreting disease-related shifts in microbiome composition and function8.

In recent years, specific bacterial strains that influence brain activity and cognitive behavior through neurotransmitters and neurotrophic factors have garnered increasing attention9,10. Evidence suggests that certain microbes, including Prevotella, Bacteroides, Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Clostridium, Enterococcus, and Ruminococcus, can directly or indirectly modulate receptors, transporters, and specific targets of dopaminergic pathways11,12. These neurotransmitters activate ganglia in the myenteric plexus and submucosal plexus of the ENS, and they are important mediators in the interface between the gut and the brain through the gut-brain axis13. Although functional strains or probiotics are closely associated with the plasticity of the central nervous system, further research is needed to elucidate underlying mechanisms.

Due to population aging and various environmental factors, Parkinson’s disease (PD) has become one of the fastest-growing neurodegenerative disorders worldwide14. Its etiology stems from the combined effects of genetics, environment, aging, inflammation, and oxidative stress15,16. α-Synuclein (α-syn) accumulation and dopaminergic neuron loss lead to hallmark motor symptoms, including rigidity, resting tremor, and bradykinesia17. The “ascending anatomy theory,” which proposes that PD pathology propagates from the intestine to the brain, has gained considerable attention, positing that intestinal α-syn may be transported to the brain via the vagus nerve in a prion-like fashion. Notably, many PD patients experience gastrointestinal dysfunction or constipation before motor symptoms18. The intestinal microbiota has drawn increasing interest in PD pathogenesis, and beneficial bacteria or functional strains emerge as a promising PD prevention and treatment strategy19,20. However, most current research has focused on probiotics and their capacity to alleviate PD-related motor and gastrointestinal dysfunction, as well as reshape intestinal microecology, leaving the characteristics of functional strains and their mechanisms less explored21. High-altitude environments such as the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau impose chronic hypobaric hypoxia, low temperatures and intense ultraviolet irradiation, which profoundly remodel gut microbial communities and metabolites and are tightly linked to oxidative stress, barrier dysfunction and neurocognitive symptoms in humans and animal models22,23. These extreme conditions are thought to select for microbial taxa with enhanced stress resistance, antioxidant capacity and immunomodulatory properties that facilitate host adaptation to plateau hypoxia, making plateau-derived strains an underexplored reservoir of candidate microbiota-based therapeutics22.

To address this gap, we focused on plateau-adapted enterococcal strains isolated from grassland soils and fecal samples collected on the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau and screened for candidates with robust stress resistance and neuroprotective signatures. From this collection, we identified Enterococcus hirae QT4713 a high-altitude–adapted strain previously shown to exhibit strong antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities in vitro and in vivo. Previous studies demonstrated that QT4713 effectively ameliorates DSS-induced colitis and exerts potential neuroprotective effects in mice24. Building on these findings, we performed whole-genome sequencing and targeted metabolite profiling of QT4713, followed by animal experiments to elucidate its functional characteristics in vivo, aiming to identify the strain’s key functional genes and active metabolites. Finally, an MPTP-induced PD mouse model was used to further investigate the neuroprotective potential of QT4713 in PD pathology.

Results

Isolation and identification of QT4713, whole genome sequencing, and metabolite identification

We collected samples from a grassland inhabited by wild yaks at an elevation of 4,713 meters on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau and immediately transported them to the laboratory. A strain of Enterococcus hirae was identified and designated as Enterococcus hirae QT4713 (QT4713) (Fig. 1a). The QT4713 bacteria were cultured and harvested for whole-genome sequencing. Evolutionary analysis of QT4713 based on housekeeping genes is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1, and the sequencing data (e.g., number of coding genes and total gene length) are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. The number of QT4713 coding genes is 2740, the total length of genes is 2,539,140 kb, and the total number of gene island groups is 8. Additionally, functional annotation was conducted using databases such as the Non-Redundant Protein Sequence Database (NR), the Database of Clusters of Orthologous Genes (COG), and Gene Ontology (GO), and a whole-genome map was drawn (Fig. 1b). Among the 2,740 coding genes, we identified nine genes associated with redox homeostasis, including arsenate reductase (sulfur reductase), thioredoxin disulfide reductase, and thioredoxin (Supplementary Table 2). In terms of acid resistance, QT4713 possesses Na+/H+ antiporter genes, and proton pump F0F1-ATPase genes, as well as choloylglycine hydrolase genes linked to bile salt tolerance (Supplementary Table 3). Notably, the genome sequencing data revealed a typical tyrosine decarboxylase gene (TyrDc) at the 280-282 kb locus (NCBI gene ID: 305471808). It is well known that TyrDC can metabolize L-tyrosine into dopamine (Fig. 1c). L-tyrosine can be metabolized to L-dopa, and then decarboxylated to dopamine under the action of TyrDC, or directly metabolized to dopamine under the action of TyrDC (Fig. 1d). The L-tyrosine metabolic pathways are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. A BLAST search for TyrDC homologs in the NCBI reference genome database indicated that most hits were in closely related Enterococcus species, with some found in Lactobacillus and Staphylococcus strains (Fig. 1e). Furthermore, to verify the L-tyrosine metabolic pathway mediated by TyrDc of QT4713 (L-tyrosine → dopamine), the bacteria were cultured in either M17 medium or M17 medium supplemented with 2 mM L-tyrosine for 18 hours. The fermentation products were analyzed, and the results showed that QT4713 could utilize L-tyrosine in M17 medium, with the exogenous 2 mM L-tyrosine completely consumed. Dopamine was undetectable in the M17 medium alone, but it was found in high concentrations in the QT4713 groups both with and without L-tyrosine supplementation. Moreover, dopamine production in the presence of 2 mM L-tyrosine was double that in the M17 medium alone (Fig. 1f). These findings confirm that QT4713 can metabolize L-tyrosine to produce the active neurotransmitter dopamine via TyrDC.

QT4713 regulates the tyrosine metabolic pathway and promotes the production of L-dopa and dopamine

To evaluate the in vivo functional properties of QT4713, thirty mice were randomly divided into two groups: a Control group and a QT4713 treatment group. The experimental period lasted for 28 days (Fig. 2a). From day 0 to day 21, both groups exhibited steady weight gain without showing significant differences, but a slightly higher rate of increase observed in the QT4713-gavaged mice (Fig. 2b). To determine whether QT4713 could also metabolize L-tyrosine in vivo following oral administration, we performed non-targeted metabolomics analyses on mouse fecal samples. The PCA results showed a clear separation between the metabolomes of the two groups, indicating that QT4713 intervention markedly changed intestinal metabolites (Fig. 2c). In addition, results from the sample clustering tree and OPLS-DA further confirmed this finding; the OPLS-DA evaluation model was stable and reliable (Q2 = 0.925, P < 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 3a–c). L-dopa and dopamine were significantly increased in the fecal samples of mice in the QT4713 group relative to the Control group, mirroring the in vitro QT4713 metabolism results (Fig. 2e). We next quantified L-tyrosine and dopamine in the mouse colon tissue and found that mice administered with QT4713 exhibited a significant increase in colonic dopamine (Fig. 2f). Subsequent pathway enrichment analysis of the KEGG data revealed multiple amino acid metabolic pathways, including the Tryptophan, Tyrosine, and Phenylalanine pathways. Notably, 31 differential metabolites were annotated in the Tyrosine metabolic pathway, demonstrating that QT4713 can strongly influence the L-tyrosine metabolism in mice (Fig. 2g). In conclusion, QT4713 increases L-dopa and dopamine production in the mouse intestine by modulating the Tyrosine metabolic pathway.

QT4713 enhances motor function and reduces oxidative stress and inflammatory responses of mice

Dopamine plays an important role in controlling movement, enhancing memory, and improving cognition25. Therefore, we conducted a series of behavioral tests to evaluate the effects of QT4713 administration on mouse locomotor activity, learning, and memory. Motor function was assessed through the rotarod test, pole test, wire hang test, and open field test. The results showed that only in the open field test did mice administered QT4713 exhibit a significantly greater total travel distance compared with the control, suggesting that QT4713 enhanced motor function (Fig. 3a). No significant differences were observed in the other motor function tests (Supplementary Fig. 4a–c). Memory and learning abilities were evaluated using the Y maze test, which revealed no significant differences between the QT4713 and control groups (Supplementary Fig. 4d). QT4713 reshapes the intestinal microbiota structure and influences L-dopa, dopamine and SCFAs production in mice

Mouse fecal samples were collected for 16S rRNA analysis to evaluate the effects of QT4713 on the structure of the mouse intestinal microbial community. A Venn diagram revealed that mice in the QT4713 group shared 355 OTUs with the control group, while 18 OTUs were unique to the QT4713 group and none were unique to the Control group (Fig. 4a). Next, we compared the α-diversity of the intestinal microbiota between the two groups using the ACE index, Chao1 index, Observed OTU index, Shannon index, and Simpson index. Compared with the Control group, the ACE index, Chao1 index, and Observed OTU index were significantly higher in the QT4713 group, suggesting that QT4713 increased the overall abundance and richness of the mouse gut microbiota (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 5a). However, no significant differences were observed in the Shannon or Simpson indices between the two groups (Supplementary Fig. 5b, c). The PCoA scores based on Bray–Curtis distances showed a significant difference between the two groups, indicating that QT4713 markedly altered the composition and structure of the mouse gut microbiota (Fig. 4c). At the phylum level, compared with the Control group, the relative abundance of Bacteroidota decreased in the QT4713 group, whereas the relative abundance of Firmicutes and Verrucomicrobiota increased (Fig. 4d). Further LEfSe analysis identified significant differences in relative abundance at the family, genus, and species levels (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 5d). Specifically, the relative abundance of Butyricicoccaceae, Lachnospiraceae (including Roseburia and Lachnospiraceae_bacterium_A4), Prevotella, Faecalibaculum, Lactobacillus, and Prevotella_sp_MGM1 significantly increased in the QT4713 group, whereas Muribaculaceae, Bifidobacterium, and Parasutterella significantly decreased (Fig. 4f). Correlation analyses between the differentially abundant microbial groups and dopamine and L-dopa contents showed that L-dopa and dopamine were positively or negatively associated with certain microbial species. For instance, L-dopa and dopamine were positively correlated with Faecalibacterium, Prevotella, and Lachnospiraceae_bacterium_A4, but negatively correlated with Muribaculaceae and others (Fig. 4g). It can be seen that QT4713-driven production of L-dopa and dopamine in the mouse intestine is closely related to the intestinal microbiota. The content of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) in mouse feces was quantified to evaluate the intestinal microbiota’s fermentative capacity. Compared with the Control group, mice treated with QT4713 showed significantly higher fecal levels of propionate, butyrate, isobutyrate, and isovalerate (Supplementary Fig. 5e). Correlation analysis between the differentially abundant microbial groups and SCFAs levels indicated that SCFAs production was positively correlated with Butyricicoccaceae, Faecalibaculum, Prevotella, Lachnospiraceae_bacterium_A4, and Prevotella_sp_MGM1 (Supplementary Fig. 5f). Moreover, there were complex interrelationships among these microbes. For instance, Prevotella and Faecalibacterium were strongly positively correlated, whereas Roseburia, Lactobacillus, and Lachnospiraceae_bacterium_A4 were negatively correlated with Muribaculaceae (Supplementary Fig. 5f). In summary, these results suggest that crosstalk among gut microbiota members is tightly linked to SCFAs production.

QT4713 alleviates intestinal dysfunction and impairments in the MPTP-induced PD mice model

To investigate if QT4713 has a protective effect on PD mice, an MPTP-induced PD mouse model was used and the mice were administered with QT4713 for 4 weeks (Fig. 5a). Starting on day 7 (the initial day of model induction), the body weight of mice in both the MPTP group and the MPTP + QT4713 group decreased substantially, followed by a gradual recovery. Notably, PD mice given QT4713 showed significantly greater weight gain than those in the MPTP group (Fig. 5b). We evaluated QT4713’s effect on the intestinal motility of PD mice. Constipation was measured via fecal water content. The MPTP group displayed a significantly lower fecal water content than the Control group. Notably, QT4713 intervention restored fecal water content to near-normal levels, suggesting that QT4713 can ameliorate constipation in PD mice (Fig. 5c). The total and cumulative fecal pellets within 20 min were counted to evaluate intestinal motility. MPTP-induced PD mice produced significantly fewer pellets, indicating reduced intestinal motility frequency, while QT4713 intervention partially improved this decreased motility (Fig. 5, e). To determine whether MPTP-induced PD causes intestinal symptoms, we examined immune infiltration and structural damage in the colon via H&E staining. Compared with the Control mice, the MPTP-treated mice exhibited notable immune infiltration, lumen enlargement, thinning of the muscularis propria, and epithelial damage. However, QT4713 supplementation markedly reduced immune infiltration in PD mice and improved luminal enlargement (Fig. 5f). Consistent with this, AB-PAS staining revealed that goblet cell counts in PD mice were nearly halved compared with the Control, while QT4713 intervention restored these cells, thus enhancing intestinal mucus secretion (Fig. 5f). Immunofluorescence staining showed that MPTP significantly decreased the expression of tight junction proteins in the colons of PD mice, including ZO-1 and Claudin-1, whereas QT4713 supplementation significantly upregulated these proteins (Fig. 5g). Additionally, SOD and Gpx levels, which were decreased in PD mice, returned to near-control levels with QT4713 treatment (Fig. 5h). Meanwhile, IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α levels were significantly elevated in the colons of MPTP-induced PD mice, whereas QT4713 slightly reduced these inflammatory mediators (Fig. 5i). In summary, these findings suggest that MPTP-induced PD not only damage the intestinal structure and the intestinal barrier but also increases intestinal inflammation and oxidative stress. QT4713 can significantly alleviate these intestinal impairments.

QT4713 inhibits the decrease of TH+ neurons and reduces inflammation and oxidative stress

On day 21 of QT4713 gavage intervention, motor symptoms and emotional states were assessed using the pole test, wire hang test, and tail suspension test. The results indicated that MPTP-induced PD mice exhibited marked movement disorders and depressive-like behavior. Their T-turn and T-total times for climbing down the pole significantly increased (Fig. 6a), the time spent on the inverted grid significantly decreased (Fig. 6b), and the immobility time in the hanging state significantly increased (Fig. 6c). By contrast, after QT4713 treatment, PD mice showed significantly shorter pole descent times, along with increased durations on the wire grid and reduced immobility time (Fig. 6a–c). A defining pathological feature of PD is the loss of dopaminergic neurons. Tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) is one of the important markers of dopaminergic neurons. Therefore, we performed immunohistochemical staining for TH in the substantia nigra. Compared with the Control group, MPTP-treated mice showed a significant reduction in TH-positive (TH⁺) neurons in the substantia nigra. It has been shown that the onset and progression of PD were accompanied by inflammation and oxidative stress. Therefore, we quantified key inflammatory mediators and oxidative stress markers in both brain tissue and serum. MPTP-induced PD mice showed reduced SOD and increased MDA levels in the brain, and QT4713 treatment shifted these oxidative stress markers toward control levels (Fig. 6f). Meanwhile, ELISA results showed that, relative to the Control group, IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α were significantly elevated, while IL-10 was reduced in the brains of MPTP-treated mice. Following QT4713 intervention, selected inflammatory markers, including IL-6 and TNF-α, were significantly reduced compared with MPTP-treated mice, whereas the inflammatory profile was not fully normalized (Fig. 6g). Moreover, in MPTP-induced PD mice, serum levels of SOD, T-AOC, and GpX were significantly lower, whereas MDA levels were higher. QT4713 treatment partially attenuated these MPTP-associated serum changes, with several antioxidant indices shifting toward control (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Taken together, these findings indicate that QT4713 partially attenuates MPTP-associated dopaminergic neuronal loss, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses in PD mice.

To further elucidate the regulatory effects of QT4713 on key microbial taxa implicated in PD progression, we compared the relative abundances of microbial taxa across different levels. The MPTP and MPTP + QT4713 groups showed significant differences (Supplementary Fig. 6b). QT4713 intervention significantly reduced the abundances of Erysipelotrichaceae, Parasutterella, and Helicobacter_typhicoccaceae, while increasing the abundance of beneficial bacteria such as Lachnospiraceae, Butyricicoccaceae, Paraprevotella, Roseburia, Lachnoclostridium, Lachnospiraceae_bacterium_10_1, and Christensenella_minuta (Fig. 7d).

QT4713 altered the fecal metabolite profiles in MPTP-induced PD mice

In the bidirectional communication between the intestine and the brain, secondary metabolites produced by the gut microbiota may play a crucial role in brain function. Therefore, we investigated whether QT4713 affects intestinal secondary metabolites and brain function in PD mice by analyzing fecal metabolites. PCA results revealed a clear separation between the Control group and the MPTP group, indicating significant changes in intestinal metabolites in the MPTP-induced PD mouse model (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Furthermore, a sample hierarchical clustering tree showed that the MPTP group and the MPTP + QT4713 group formed two distinct clusters, suggesting marked differences in intestinal metabolites between them (Fig. 7e).

Among these metabolites, compared with the Control group, leukotriene A4 and glucose were significantly upregulated in the MPTP group, whereas ursodeoxycholic acid, tauroursodeoxycholic acid, se-methylselenocysteine, and hydroxyisocaproic acid were significantly downregulated (Supplementary Fig. 7b, c). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of these differential fecal metabolites revealed that several inflammation-related pathways, including ‘Th1 and Th2 cell differentiation’, ‘NF-κB signaling’ and ‘T-cell receptor signaling’, as well as amino acid and energy metabolism pathways such as ‘cysteine and methionine metabolism’, ‘galactose metabolism’ and the ‘pentose phosphate pathway’, were significantly over-represented (Supplementary Fig. 7d). Metabolites contributing to these immune-related annotations included bile acid derivatives (e.g., ursodeoxycholic acid and tauroursodeoxycholic acid) and the eicosanoid leukotriene A4, which are linked in KEGG to cytokine and T cell–differentiation modules. We therefore interpret these patterns as indicative of altered immune-related metabolic activity in the gut, rather than as direct evidence of concomitant activation of Th1 and Th2 cell populations.

Specifically, compared with PD mice, QT4713 treatment significantly downregulated 16,16-dimethylprostaglandin E2, leukotriene B4 ethanolamide, prostaglandin D1, and prostaglandin B1, while notably upregulating the level of L-homocysteic acid. These changes suggest improved antioxidant capacity and reduced inflammation in PD mice. Additionally, bile acid-related metabolites shifted significantly, such as an increase in taurochenodeoxycholic acid and a decrease in lithocholic acid (Supplementary Fig. 7e).

Notably, compared with the MPTP group, fecal samples from the MPTP + QT4713 group contained markedly higher levels of L-phenylalanine, L-tyrosine, and L-dopa derivatives, including L-homophenylalanine, 3-chloro-L-tyrosine, and DL-DOPA (Fig. 7f). Furthermore, KEGG enrichment analysis highlighted the top 15 metabolic pathways that the MPTP + QT4713 group differed most significantly from the MPTP group, including bile secretion, primary bile acid synthesis, propionate metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation, pentose phosphate pathway, glutathione metabolism, arginine biosynthesis, and vitamin digestion and absorption, among others (Supplementary Fig. 7f). These findings suggest that QT4713 may partially rectify abnormalities in metabolic pathways involving energy, amino acids, bile acids, vitamins, and other metabolites in PD mice (Fig. 7g, h).

QT4713 elevates striatal dopamine and mitigates PD-like phenotypes across toxin-induced and genetic models

To directly assess whether QT4713 treatment elevates central dopaminergic tone, we quantified dopamine and tyrosine levels in the striatum after oral gavage. QT4713-treated mice exhibited increased striatal dopamine content, accompanied by higher striatal tyrosine levels, compared with MPTP-treated controls (Supplementary Fig. S8A). To further substantiate the therapeutic relevance beyond an acute neurotoxin model, we evaluated QT4713 in TMEM175 knockout mice, a genetically defined model that exhibits age-dependent PD-like neurodegeneration and motor deficits (Supplementary Fig. S8B). Starting at 10 months of age, mice received daily QT4713 gavage for 2 months. QT4713 treatment attenuated dopaminergic neuronal loss in the SNpc, as evidenced by increased TH⁺ neuron counts compared with untreated TMEM175 knockout mice (Supplementary Fig. S8C–D). Consistently, behavioral assays showed improved locomotor performance, including increased total distance traveled in the open-field test and reduced descent time in the pole test (Supplementary Fig. S8E–F). Future work quantifying striatal dopamine in TMEM175 knockout mice will be important to further link the histological and behavioral rescue to central dopaminergic restoration.

Discussion

In this study, we identify Enterococcus hirae QT4713 as a naturally derived bacterial candidate that mitigates gastrointestinal dysfunction, inflammatory and oxidative changes, and dopaminergic neuronal loss in PD mouse models. These effects were accompanied by TyrDC-associated catecholamine-related metabolic changes, gut microbiota remodeling, increased striatal dopamine and tyrosine levels, and partial protection in TMEM175 knockout mice, consistent with a multifactorial rather than dopamine-only mode of action. These findings fit within a growing body of work linking gut microbial dysbiosis, enteric dysfunction and neuroinflammation to PD progression, but extend this literature by focusing on the strain-level metabolic and host effects of a naturally derived E. hirae candidate. Recent advances in microbial therapeutics have shown that genetically engineered probiotics can be programmed to modulate host physiology through diverse mechanisms, including the local production of therapeutic metabolites, delivery of bioactive molecules, and regulation of immune or metabolic pathways in experimental disease settings26,27. However, the clinical application of such engineered strains is limited by regulatory hurdles, biosafety concerns, and challenges in long-term stability28. Our study introduces QT4713, a naturally occurring Enterococcus hirae strain isolated from the Tibetan Plateau. Unlike genetically engineered delivery systems, QT4713 does not require exogenous genetic modification and provides an unmodified bacterial intervention that improves motor and gastrointestinal dysfunction, reduces inflammatory and oxidative phenotypes and preserves dopaminergic neurons in PD mouse models. Importantly, these intervention data support a beneficial effect of QT4713 administration, but do not establish intestinal dopamine or L-DOPA as direct causal mediators of neuroprotection. Therefore, QT4713 represents an unmodified bacterial candidate with probiotic-relevant properties, but its long-term persistence and clinical safety remain to be established.

L-tyrosine serves as a key precursor for catecholaminergic neurons (dopamine, norepinephrine, and epinephrine) and is mainly derived from the essential amino acid phenylalanine via phenylalanine hydroxylase29,30. Genetic and biochemical studies indicate that TyrDC decarboxylates both L-dopa and L-tyrosine into dopamine, though it favors L-tyrosine31. Consistent with these observations, QT4713 encodes an active TyrDC and converts L-tyrosine to dopamine in vitro. Oral administration of QT4713 was also associated with increased dopamine and L-DOPA-related metabolites in the feces and colon. We therefore interpret these catecholamine-related changes as metabolic readouts of QT4713 activity in the gut, rather than as direct predictors of disease protection. Importantly, the role of bacterial TyrDC in PD should be considered in the context of previous work on L-DOPA pharmacokinetics. In patients receiving L-DOPA therapy, gut bacterial TyrDC activity has generally been viewed as detrimental, because intestinal decarboxylation of therapeutic L-DOPA to dopamine can reduce systemic L-DOPA availability and contribute to variable treatment responses31,32. This differs from the experimental context of the present study, in which QT4713 was administered to mouse models without exogenous L-DOPA treatment. Thus, our data should not be interpreted as evidence that bacterial TyrDC activity is universally beneficial in PD. Rather, TyrDC represents one metabolic feature of QT4713 that, under defined experimental conditions, is associated with changes in catecholamine-related metabolites, microbiota remodeling, reduced inflammatory and oxidative phenotypes, and preservation of dopaminergic neurons. Future studies using L-DOPA pharmacokinetic assays and TyrDC-deficient QT4713 mutants will be required to determine whether this activity is beneficial, neutral or detrimental in the setting of therapeutic L-DOPA administration.

Although peripheral dopamine does not cross the blood-brain barrier, it may play an essential role within the gastrointestinal tract. Locally, dopamine modulates enteric nervous system signaling, regulates motility and intestinal barrier integrity, and influences mucosal immune responses33,34. Probiotic supplementation, either alone or combined with specific functional strains-has been shown to exert antioxidant effects and mitigate oxidative damage both in vivo and in vitro35,36. In our study, increased fecal dopamine levels following QT4713 administration coincided with improved intestinal motility, reduced inflammation, and restored goblet cell and tight junction function in MPTP-induced PD mice, supporting a gut-localized neuroprotective role. These findings align with the growing appreciation that gut-derived neurotransmitters are key modulators of gut-brain communication and can influence systemic physiological processes, including immune tone and autonomic function37,38. Consistent with a potential gut–brain link, QT4713 administration also increased striatal dopamine levels. Nevertheless, these observations remain correlative and do not formally establish causality; future studies using isotope tracing, pharmacological blockade of L-DOPA transport across the blood–brain barrier (e.g., LAT1), and/or a TyrDC-deficient QT4713 mutant will be required to directly test the proposed pathway. In parallel, our fecal metabolomics and KEGG enrichment analyses indicated modulation of immune-related pathways, including “Th1 and Th2 cell differentiation”, T-cell receptor signaling and NF-κB signaling, driven by changes in bile acid– and eicosanoid-related metabolites39,40. Because these annotations arise from metabolite-based pathway enrichment rather than direct T-cell measurements, we interpret them as evidence of altered immune-metabolic tone in the gut, rather than concomitant activation of Th1 and Th2 subsets41. Future studies combining QT4713 treatment with mucosal and systemic immunophenotyping will be required to map these metabolic shifts onto specific T helper cell responses.

Beyond direct neurotransmitter generation, administration of QT4713 considerably remodeled the intestinal microbial ecosystem. The gut microbiota has co-evolved with its host and is integral to host health, playing pivotal roles in immune, metabolic, and neurological functions42. Interestingly, we identified enriched relative abundances of Lachnospiraceae, Prevotella, Paraprevotella, and Faecalibacterium, all of which have been widely associated with preserving intestinal and neurological homeostasis43,44. Such microbial changes are of special significance in neurological diseases such as Parkinson’s disease, in which dysbiosis appears as a consistent feature across human and animal studies45,46. Moreover, systemic exposure to bacterial endotoxins and increased gut permeability, due to diminished Prevotella, can trigger uncontrolled α-synuclein expression and misfolding in the colon47,48. Conversely, interventions or treatments in AD and PD that reshape the gut microbiota have been shown to restore these bacterial taxa, suggesting that Prevotella and Lachnospiraceae may mediate gut-brain interactions49. Studies have shown that probiotic supplementation or fecal microbiota transplantation can remodel the composition of the gut microbiome, thereby regulating the production of neurotransmitters such as serotonin and dopamine, and influencing brain activity, cognition, and mood50,51. A recent study also demonstrated that transplanting Enterococcus faecalis or Enterococcus faecium into a Parkinson’s mouse model significantly elevated L-dopa and dopamine in the brain and colon, thereby alleviating Parkinson’s-like symptoms52. This suggests that locally produced dopamine could act as a signaling molecule not only within the host but also among microbial populations, influencing community structure and metabolic output. Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), primarily acetate, propionate, and butyrate, are among the most intensively studied metabolites derived from the gut microbiota53,54. They influence various host processes, including gastrointestinal motility, blood pressure regulation, circadian rhythms, and neuroimmune function55,56. Importantly, QT4713 administration increased levels of several SCFAs, metabolites closely linked to gut homeostasis and neuroinflammation, further reinforcing the systemic relevance of this gut-local neurochemical modulation. Overall, we propose that the increased dopamine and L-dopa in mice reflect both the enzymatic activity of QT4713 and its synergistic effects on the host microbiota.

Recognizing its ability to produce dopamine and regulate the gut microbiota, we investigated the therapeutic potential of Enterococcus hirae QT4713 in an MPTP-induced PD mouse model. Treatment with QT4713 significantly alleviated motor impairments, constipation, and dopaminergic neuron loss caused by MPTP, highlighting both neuroprotective and gut-stabilizing effects. These findings are in line with previous studies demonstrating that Clostridium butyricum mitigates MPTP-induced motor deficits via the gut microbiota-GLP-1 pathway57,58. A key pathological feature of PD is the interplay between oxidative stress and inflammation, both of which contribute to dopaminergic neuron degeneration59. In our study, QT4713 significantly reduced oxidative damage and suppressed pro-inflammatory cytokine expression across the colon, serum, and brain. These improvements were accompanied by enhanced gut barrier integrity, as indicated by restored goblet cell numbers and increased expression of tight junction proteins. This suggests that QT4713 helps prevent systemic inflammation by reinforcing the mucosal barrier and limiting microbial translocation. Consistent with previous observations linking gut dysbiosis to PD, MPTP exposure disrupted the composition of the gut microbial community60,61. QT4713 counteracted these shifts by enriching beneficial taxa such as Lachnospiraceae and Paraprevotella, while reducing potentially harmful genera including Parasutterella and Helicobacter typhicoccaceae. These bacteria have been associated with mucosal inflammation and barrier dysfunction, and their modulation may play a role in restoring both intestinal and systemic homeostasis62. While QT4713 showed promising neuroprotective and microbiome-modulating effects in mouse models of Parkinson’s disease, its efficacy and safety in humans remain to be established. Because a systematic safety evaluation was not performed in the present study, QT4713 should be considered a probiotic candidate rather than an established probiotic strain. Moreover, the observed effects were obtained during repeated oral administration, and we did not directly determine long-term intestinal persistence, stable engraftment or survival after discontinuation of dosing. Future studies should assess antimicrobial susceptibility, hemolytic activity, potential virulence determinants, horizontal gene transfer risk, intestinal persistence or engraftment, and long-term toxicity during repeated administration. Thus, the current findings provide a preclinical basis for further evaluating QT4713 as a naturally derived microbiota-based candidate for PD-related phenotypes.

In conclusion, we identified Enterococcus hirae QT4713 as a naturally occurring, functional gut strain capable of converting L-tyrosine into dopamine through tyrosine decarboxylase (TyrDC) activity. Both in vitro and in vivo studies showed that QT4713 promotes intestinal dopamine synthesis, modulates L-tyrosine metabolic pathways, and reshapes gut microbial composition. In an MPTP-induced PD mouse model, QT4713 alleviated motor impairments, gastrointestinal dysfunction, and neuroinflammation, while also reducing oxidative stress and enhancing gut barrier integrity. Previous studies have established that gut microbiota can modulate PD-related motor deficits, neuroinflammation and intestinal pathology, while bacterial tyrosine decarboxylases, particularly those from enterococci, participate in L-DOPA/dopamine metabolism in the gut32,45. In this context, our findings extend previous work by identifying a naturally occurring E. hirae strain that alters intestinal catecholamine-related metabolites and is associated with improved behavioral, intestinal and dopaminergic neuronal phenotypes in PD mouse models. These phenotypic improvements occurred together with increased intestinal L-DOPA/dopamine-related metabolites, elevated striatal dopamine and tyrosine levels, and remodeling of gut microbial and metabolic profiles. Thus, rather than establishing a direct causal role for intestinal dopamine in neuroprotection, our data support a working model in which QT4713 is associated with coordinated changes in catecholamine-related metabolism, microbiota-derived metabolic outputs and dopaminergic neuronal preservation. Thus, the broader implication of this study is not that intestinal dopamine alone predicts protection from PD-like pathology, but that strain-level microbial functions may shape disease-relevant host physiology through multiple interacting metabolic and immune pathways. Future studies defining QT4713 persistence, safety, TyrDC dependency, L-DOPA pharmacokinetics and efficacy in additional disease contexts will be essential before translational application can be considered.

Methods

Isolation and cultivation of plateau active strains

Grassland samples were collected from areas inhabited by wild blood yaks on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau at an elevation of 4713 m. Each sample was immediately mixed with M17 medium (Coolaber, China) containing 20% glycerol, then frozen and transported to the laboratory as quickly as possible. Upon thawing, an aliquot of each sample was inoculated into M17 medium and incubated under facultative anaerobic conditions at 37 °C for 12–18 h to enrich bacterial populations. The enriched cultures were subsequently serially diluted and spread onto M17 agar plates, followed by a 48-h incubation at 37 °C. Colonies displaying white coloration, well-defined edges, and moderate size were selected and subcultured for three consecutive rounds to ensure purity, then preserved in 20% glycerol stocks. To identify the selected isolates, a fully automatic microbial mass spectrometer (MBT Sirius, Bruker, Germany) was employed63.

Bacterial whole genome sequencing

Strain QT4713 was cultured three consecutive times in M17 medium at 37 °C for 12–18 h. Cultures were then centrifuged at 8000 × g for 10 min to pellet the cells, and the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was immediately frozen at −80 °C. Genomic DNA was extracted using the CTAB method. The DNA concentration was measured with a TBS 380 fluorometer, and purity was assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (OD₂₆₀/₂₈₀ = 1.8–2.0). The DNA with no degradation and a total amount of at least 15 μg was used for library construction, and sequencing was performed using the high-throughput Illumina NovaSeq 6000 third-generation sequencing platform. (Meiji Bio, China).

Paired-end sequencing (PE150) was performed, ensuring a sequencing depth of at least 100×, with a minimum total data output of 1 G per sample and genome coverage exceeding 95%. The final sequencing data were stored in FASTQ format for subsequent analysis. GeneMarkS, Glimmer, and Prodigal were employed to predict coding sequences within the genome, and the resulting protein sequences were annotated by comparison with the Cluster of Orthologous Groups (COG) database. BLAST software was utilized to align the predicted genes with the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database, and Blast2GO was used for functional annotation based on the Gene Ontology (GO) database. Additionally, tRNAscan was applied to identify tRNA genes, and rRNAmmer 1.2 was used to predict rRNA sequences. The genome circular map was generated using CGView software.

Identification of metabolites in fermentation supernatants of strains

Sample extraction and LC-MS analysis were performed as previously described with minor modifications64. Briefly, fermentation supernatants were thawed, vortexed, and mixed with 20% acetonitrile-methanol (Merck, Germany). After low-temperature centrifugation, the supernatants were chilled at -20 °C and re-centrifuged to remove precipitates. A final aliquot was filtered and used for mass spectrometric analysis. Chromatographic separation was conducted using a Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm) with a mobile phase consisting of 0.05% formic acid in water (A) and acetonitrile (B). The gradient program was optimized to increase from 5% to 95% B over 9.5 minutes and return to baseline within 12 minutes. The injection volume was 2 μL, and the flow rate was 0.35 mL/min. Electrospray ionization (ESI) was performed in both positive and negative ion modes at 550 °C, and quantitative data were acquired using a standard curve generated from reference compounds (Sigma-Aldrich, purity >99%). UPLC-MS analysis was performed using a Waters ACQUITY HSS T3 C18 column with formic acid-modified water and acetonitrile as mobile phases. A gradient elution was applied with a flow rate of 0.35 mL/min at 40 °C. MS detection was conducted using ESI in positive and negative ion modes under standard operating voltages and source conditions.

Animal study

Mouse use was approved by the Experimental Animal Ethical Committee of School of Public Health, Lanzhou University (NO. IRB23051901). All experiments with mice were performed under protocols approved by the Lanzhou University Animal Care and Use Committee. Six-week-old SPF male or female C57BL/6 J mice (weighing 22 ± 2 g) were purchased from Lanzhou University (Lanzhou, China). TMEM175-knockout mouse lines were generated by our lab using CRISPR–Cas9 methods, as previously described65. Littermates were used as controls. The sample sizes were not predetermined. All the mouse uses in Supplementary Fig. 8 were blinded. The mouse numbers in the behavioral studies in Supplementary Fig. 8 were randomized before studies. The housing environment was maintained at a constant temperature of 22 ± 1 °C and 55–65% humidity, with a 12 h light/dark cycle. The mice had free access to food and water, and experiments began after a one-week acclimation.

The schematic diagram of the experimental design is shown in Fig. 2A. A total of 30 mice were randomly divided into two groups. The Control group (n = 15) was subjected to daily gavage with 200 μL of PBS, while the QT4713 group (n = 15) was subjected to daily gavage with 200 μL of Enterococcus hirae QT4713 suspension (1 × 10⁹ CFU/mL). Mice were allowed to eat and drink freely during the experiment, and their body weight was recorded daily. Gavage was performed at 2:30 pm every day for 4 weeks. Behavioral tests were conducted on days 21–28, and the mice were sacrificed on day 29. Before sacrifice, feces were collected and quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen. After sacrifice, colon contents, serum (obtained by letting the blood stand overnight, then centrifuging at 4 °C and 3000 × g for 10 min, and collecting the supernatant), colon tissue, and brain tissue were collected for subsequent analysis. Subsequently, we gavaged 26 C57BL/6 J mice (half male and half female) with QT4713 or PBS to measure dopamine and L-dopa levels in colon and brain tissues and further evaluate behavioral performance. The Control group consisted of 10 mice (half male and half female), and the QT4713 group had 16 mice (half male and half female). The rest of the methods were the same as above. The same experimental secheuld were also used in TMEM175 knock-out test in Supplementary Fig. 8.

Forty-five mice were randomly divided into three groups to assess the protective effect of QT4713 in PD mice in Fig. 5. The Control group (n = 15) was subjected to daily gavage with PBS on days 0–28, and intraperitoneally injected with PBS on days 7–14. The MPTP group (n = 15) was subjected to daily gavage with PBS on days 0–28, and intraperitoneally injected with 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP) at 20 mg/kg/day on days 7–14 to establish a PD mouse model. The MPTP + QT4713 group (n = 15) was subjected to daily gavage with QT4713 (1 × 10⁹ CFU/mL) on days 0–28, and intraperitoneally injected with MPTP on days 7–14. Behavioral tests and gastrointestinal function tests were performed on days 21–28, and the mice were sacrificed on day 29.

Non-targeted determination of metabolites in feces (LC-MS/MS)

Non-targeted fecal metabolomics was performed using LC-MS/MS as previously described with slight modifications66. Frozen fecal samples (20 mg) were thawed on ice and extracted with 400 μL of methanol: water (7:3, v/v) containing internal standards. After vortexing, sonication, and cold precipitation, the samples were centrifuged twice at 12,000 rpm (4 °C) to remove debris. A 200 μL aliquot of the clear supernatant was collected for LC-MS/MS analysis.

Metabolite signals were processed using standard multivariate workflows. Principal component analysis (PCA), orthogonal projections to latent structures discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), and univariate statistics (fold change, VIP, and p-value) were used to identify differentially abundant metabolites. VIP values > 1 and p < 0.05 were considered significant. KEGG pathway annotation and enrichment were subsequently conducted to interpret metabolic changes. Pathway enrichment p-values were calculated using KEGG over-representation analysis, and pathways with p < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Quantitative detection (LC-MS/MS) of dopamine and L-tyrosine in the colon of mice

A series of working solutions (10,000, 1000, 100, 10, 1, 0.2, and 0.1 ng/mL) was prepared by diluting L-tyrosine and dopamine stock solutions (1 mg/mL) with water. An aliquot (5 μL) of each solution was injected into the LC-MS/MS system for analysis, and a standard curve was prepared. Mouse colon tissue was homogenized in saline at a weight/volume ratio of 1:4, and 3,4-dihydroxybenzylamine (used as an internal standard) was added to 100 μL of the colon tissue homogenate. After protein precipitation with 300 μL of acetonitrile, the sample was mixed for 30 s and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was injected into the LC-MS/MS system for analysis.

16S rRNA amplicon sequencing of intestinal microorganisms in feces

The genomic DNA of each sample was extracted using the CTAB method, and its purity and concentration were assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis. An aliquot of the DNA sample was then diluted to 1 ng/μL with sterile water. The diluted DNA served as a template in PCR reactions targeting the 16S V4 region, using high-efficiency and high-fidelity enzymes. The resulting PCR products were examined on a 2% agarose gel; qualified products were purified with magnetic beads, quantified by enzyme labeling, and mixed in equal proportions according to DNA concentration. The target band was recovered using a gel recovery kit. After confirming the library’s quality, sequencing was carried out on a NovaSeq6000 system. Fastp was used to filter the raw reads, resulting in high-quality reads that were clustered into OTUs (operational taxonomic units) at 97% similarity. Finally, α-diversity, PCoA analysis, and LDA Effect Size (LEfSe) analysis were performed.

Quantitative detection of SCFAs in mouse feces

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) in mouse fecal samples were measured using gas chromatography (GC). The feces were thawed at room temperature, weighed, and mixed with distilled water. The mixture was shaken at 60 Hz for 1 min at 4 °C until it was homogenized. After standing for one hour at 4 °C, the homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. Next, 200 μL of the supernatant was collected, and 20 μL of 25% metaphosphoric acid solution containing the internal standard 2-ethylbutyric acid (2-EB) was added. After mixing, the sample was placed in an ice bath for over 30 min and centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 15 min. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm filter, and the filtrate was analyzed using an Agilent GC instrument (USA). The GC conditions were as follows: nitrogen (N2) was used as the carrier gas with a split ratio of 40:1. The injection volume was 0.6 μL, and the injection port temperature was set at 250 °C. Additionally, the auxiliary box temperature was maintained at 250 °C.

Inflammatory factors and oxidative stress assay

The inflammatory factors IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-α in the colon, serum, and brain were measured using commercial ELISA kits (mlbio, China). Superoxide Dismutase (SOD), Malondialdehyde (MDA), Total Antioxidant Capacity (T-AOC), and Glutathione Peroxidase (Gpx) kits were purchased from Solarbio (Solarbio, China). All procedures were carried out following the manufacturer’s instructions.

RotaRod test

The mice were placed on a rotating rod. Once each mouse had a firm grip, the rod was rotated at 30 rpm for 180 s, and the time the mouse remained on the rod was recorded. If a mouse rotated continuously with the rod instead of walking on it, it was considered to have fallen.

Pole test

A wooden pole (50 cm long, 1 cm in diameter, and rough in texture) was placed in the cage. Each mouse was positioned at the top of the pole with its head facing upward. The time to turn its head downward (T-turn) and the total time to reach the cage floor (T-total) were recorded within 60 s. If a mouse fell or jumped off within 60 s, the test was repeated. If a mouse failed to turn its head downward or repeatedly fell from the pole, both T-turn and T-total were recorded as 60 s.

Open field test

Before the experiment, the entire open field box was cleaned with 75% alcohol. The mouse was taken from its cage and placed in the center of the open field (50 cm × 50 cm × 50 cm). The video acquisition software and camera system were then started to record the mouse’s activities in the open field for 10 min.

Wire Hang test

The wire hang test evaluates the grip strength, balance, and endurance of mice. A wire cage lid was secured with tape around the edges to prevent the mouse from walking off. The mouse was placed on top of the lid, then the lid was slowly flipped over and gently shaken. The time before the mouse fell was recorded.

Y maze

The Y-maze (50 cm × 18 cm × 35 cm) consists of three arms of equal length, each separated by a 120-degree angle. The mouse was placed at the end of any arm and allowed to explore freely for 10 min. An “alternation” was defined as entering all three arms once in sequence. The number of maximum possible alternations (total arm entries -2) within 3, 5, and 8 min was also noted. Spontaneous alternation (%) = (Number of actual alternations/Number of maximum alternations) × 100%.

Novel object recognition

Adaptation period: the mouse was allowed to move freely for 10 minutes in an open field. Familiarization period: Two identical objects were placed in the apparatus, 10 cm from two adjacent walls. The mouse was placed in the apparatus with its back to the objects, equidistant from each object, and the time spent exploring each object was recorded. Test period: One of the identical objects was replaced with a novel object in the same apparatus. The mouse was again placed with its back to both objects, equidistant from each, and allowed to explore for 10 min. The camera and software recorded the time spent exploring each object. The recognition index (RI) was calculated as: RI (%) = [Time exploring the novel object/(Time exploring the novel object + Time exploring the old object)] × 100%.

Tail suspension test

A piece of tape was attached to the mouse’s tail ~1–2 cm from the tip. The mouse was suspended head-down for 8 min, and the total immobility time from 2 to 8 min was recorded.

Fecal pellet output and fecal water content

After a 2-hour fasting, each mouse was placed in a clean, transparent plastic cage. Fresh fecal pellets were collected to determine wet weight, and then dried at 85 °C to measure dry weight. Fecal water content (%) was calculated as: (feces wet weight - feces dry weight)/feces wet weight × 100%. Intestinal motility was assessed by collecting feces over 20 minutes. Each mouse was placed in a clean, transparent plastic cage, and the number of fecal pellets was recorded every 5 minutes for a total of 20 minutes.

Histopathological analysis

Five mice were randomly selected from each group. After anesthesia, they were transcardially perfused with ice-cold PBS, followed by 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer. The colon and brain tissues were dissected, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 hours, and then transferred to PBS. Both colon and brain samples were embedded in paraffin and sliced into 5-μm-thick sections67. For histological quantification, five mice per group were analyzed unless otherwise indicated. For each mouse, five anatomically matched sections were selected, and three non-overlapping fields per section were quantified. Values from multiple sections or fields from the same mouse were averaged to generate one biological replicate, and statistical analyses were performed using mouse-level averages rather than section-level values.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Paraffin sections of mouse colon tissue were dewaxed, treated with a high-definition constant staining pretreatment solution for 1 min, stained with hematoxylin (Servicebio, China) for 3–5 min, washed with tap water, differentiated in a differentiation solution, and washed again. The sections were dehydrated in 95% alcohol for 1 min, stained in eosin solution (Servicebio, China) for 15 s, and decolorized in absolute ethanol. They were then sealed with neutral resin, naturally dried, and observed under a microscope for the tissue structure of the colon and inflammatory infiltration.

Alcian blue periodic acid Schiff (AB-PAS) staining

Paraffin-embedded colon sections were dewaxed and rehydrated using standard protocols. PAS staining was performed following the manufacturer’s instructions (Servicebio, China) and previous reports with minor modifications68. In brief, sections were oxidized with periodic acid, stained with Schiff reagent in the dark, and counterstained briefly. Slides were then differentiated using acid-alcohol, neutralized with ammonia water, dehydrated, and mounted. Goblet cell density was assessed microscopically.

Immunofluorescence staining

Briefly, paraffin sections were subjected to antigen retrieval in sodium citrate solution, blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin for 30 min, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-ZO-1 (1:100), anti-Claudin-1 (1:100), and anti-Occludin (1:100) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). After washing three times in PBS (pH 7.4) for 5 min each on a destaining shaker, the corresponding secondary antibodies-FITC-labeled goat anti-mouse (1:400, Servicebio), goat anti-rabbit IgG-CY3 (1:300, Servicebio), and goat anti-mouse IgG-CY3 (1:300, Servicebio)-were added and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 50 min. DAPI (Servicebio, China) was used to counterstain cell nuclei. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (NIKON ECLIPSE C1, Japan).

Immunohistochemical detection of tyrosine hydroxylase in substantia nigra

Mouse brains were perfused and fixed as described above. The midbrain substantia nigra was harvested and embedded in paraffin. Endogenous enzymes were inactivated by incubating sections with 3% H₂O₂ at room temperature for 10 min, followed by three washes in distilled water. Brain slices were blocked with serum blocking solution at room temperature for 20 min and incubated with the mouse anti-TH monoclonal antibody (1:1000) (Roche, Switzerland) overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, anti-mouse IgG (1:200) (Merck, Germany) was added and incubated at 37 °C for 20 min, followed by SABC incubation at 37 °C for 20 min. The DAB solution was used for 8 min of color development. The sections were then washed, dehydrated, and sealed. The images of the substantia nigra region were collected, and the number of TH-positive (TH⁺) neurons in both sides of the SN was quantified. TH⁺ neurons were counted within the bilateral substantia nigra from matched sections, and section-level counts were averaged per mouse before statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

R (4.3.3) was used to visualize the results of gut microorganisms and their metabolites. SPSS (26.0) and GraphPad Prism (9.5.1) were used to analyze and visualize all other data. Sample sizes were selected based on prior studies using similar experimental models and are indicated in the corresponding figure legends. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. For animal experiments, mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups where applicable. In experiments involving genotype comparisons or histological quantification, data collection and analysis were performed with the experimenter blinded to genotype or treatment group where possible. No data were excluded from the analyses. Data distribution was assessed before choosing statistical tests. For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test was used for normally distributed data, whereas the Mann–Whitney test was used for non-normally distributed data. For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparison test was used for normally distributed data, whereas the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple-comparison test was used for non-normally distributed data. The significance values reported for multiple-group comparisons correspond to post hoc adjusted P values. Data are presented as mean ± s.e.m. or mean ± s.d., as indicated in the figure legends. Each dot represents one biological replicate or one animal unless otherwise stated. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
