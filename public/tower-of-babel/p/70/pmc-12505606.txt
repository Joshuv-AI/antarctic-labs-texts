The surgical effect of inferior mesenteric artery ligation level in rectal cancer and sigmoid colon cancer: a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Jing-Hong You, Yan-Bin Deng, Yu-Bing Li, Yong Zhang, Shi-Yun Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn radical surgery for sigmoid and rectal cancer, two main approaches are used to ligate the inferior mesenteric artery (IMA): high ligation at the root of the IMA and low ligation at the distal origin of the left colonic artery (LCA). There has been debate as to which technique is more optimal.MethodsPubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and CNKI databases were searched for relevant information from their inception to March 10, 2025. Methodological quality of reviews was assessed by the AMSTAR-2 tool.ResultsSixteen relevant studies involving a total of 1778 patients were included in the meta-analysis. Compared with high ligation, low-position ligation of the IMA was associated with a significantly lower incidence of anastomotic leakage (RR: 0.44, 95% CI: 0.26–0.72, P < 0.05) and faster postoperative recovery of gastrointestinal function (SMD: −0.21, 95% CI: −0.37 −0.05, P < 0.05). No significant differences were observed in the length of hospitalization, tumor recurrence, lymph node harvest, urinary retention, urinary incontinence, operative bleeding, overall survival at 5 years, disease‑free survival at 5 years and sexual dysfunction were not statistically significant (P > 0.05).Conclusion In colorectal cancer surgery, low ligation of the IMA can reduce the incidence of anastomotic leakage and improve postoperative defecation function without increasing intraoperative blood loss, the risk of tumor recurrence, or affecting sexual function. Therefore, low ligation may a better technique than high ligation in this context.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-14959-3.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the most common gastrointestinal tumor, ranking third in incidence globally, accounting for 10.0% of all cancers, and second in mortality, according to global cancer statistics in 2020 [1]. Male and female case fatality rates are the fourth and third highest among all cancers, respectively [2]. In radical surgery for sigmoid and rectal cancer, two primary techniques are used.

to ligate the inferior mesenteric artery (IMA): high ligation at the root of the IMA and low ligation at the distal origin of the left colonic artery (LCA) [3]. There has been debate as to which technique is more optimal.

Girard et al. reported that, compared with low ligation, high ligation can result in more lymph node dissection, which may improve tumor staging accuracy and guide the corresponding adjuvant treatment. Moreover, high ligation of the IMA is easier to achieve during surgery, and therefore more strongly recommended [4]. However, high ligation can increase the incidence of postoperative urogenital and defecation disorders in patients, prompting some scholars to recently recommended low ligation as the preferred approach [5, 6]. Notably, clinical studies have reported no significant differences in surgical or oncological outcomes between patients with low and high ligation [7, 8].

To date, controversy has persisted. Some systematic reviews and meta-analyses have found no differences in the total number of lymph nodes dissected, the incidence of anastomotic leakage, or survival outcomes between the two ligation methods [9]. In contrast, other meta-analyses have shown that low ligation IMA is associated with a lower incidence of anastomotic leakage [10]. At present, it is unclear whether low IMA ligation improves postoperative urogenital and bowel function for sigmoid and rectal cancer.

Although studies on these controversies are not uncommon, due to the need for invasive surgical procedures and ethical limitations, few randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have been included in previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses. Most available studies were retrospective, often with low levels of evidence and large time spans, providing limited reference value for clinical practice. To address this question and provide guidance for surgeons in selecting IMA-level ligation methods, this meta-analysis includes numerous RCTs that systematically evaluated the effects of IMA ligation on postoperative colorectal cancer patients, as well as surgery-related and oncologic outcomes.

Methods

Study protocol

This study was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [11] and was registered in INPLASY (DOI: 10.37766/inplasy2025.3.0066).

PICOS question

We evaluated the effects of low ligation (LL) versus high ligation (HL) of the IMA in patients undergoing surgery for colorectal cancer. Our goal was to assess clinical RCT outcomes after high or low ligation around the root of the IMA in patients with colorectal cancer.

Search strategy

Two researchers (You JH and Li YB) independently searched PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science and the China National Knowledge Internet (CNKI) databases from inception to March 10, 2025. Searches were conducted using medical subject headings (MeSH), Emtree terms, and text, with no language limitations.

The following keywords were used in the search strategy: “rectal cancer”, “rectal carcinoma”, “sigmoid colon cancer”, “sigmoid tumor”, “sigmoid mass”, “rectal tumor”, “rectal mass”, “left colic artery”, “left colon artery”, “inferior mesenteric artery”, “ligation”, “high”, “low” and “randomized controlled trial” (Supplementary Table S1). Reference lists from the identified studies were also searched for potentially eligible articles. The identified publications were imported into EndNote X9.1 (Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia, PA, USA). After removing duplicate records and irrelevant literature, eligible studies with detailed classifications were identified.

Eligibility criteria

Two authors (You JH and Zhang Y) independently performed the primary review to identify trials that met the inclusion criteria (Supplementary Table S3). Any discrepancies were resolved by discussion and consensus (Fig. 1).Fig. 1PRISMA 2009 flow diagram

PRISMA 2009 flow diagram

The following inclusion criteria were used: (1) the study design was an RCT; (2) studies that compared high tie with low tie of the IMA for sigmoid or rectal cancer surgery; and (3) the findings included at least one of the following: anastomotic leakage, time to first flatus, hospitalization duration, tumor recurrence, lymph node harvest, urinary retention, urinary incontinence, intraoperative bleeding, conversion from laparoscopic to open surgery, overall survival at 5 years, disease‑free survival at 5 years, or sexual dysfunction. The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) data could not be extracted from the published results; (2) the studies were retrospective cohort studies; (3) the studies contained republished data; and (4) the publications were editorials, comments, letters, or review articles.

Data extraction and methodological quality assessment

Two reviewers (You JH and Deng YB) independently extracted data from the included publications. The following information was recorded: author, nation, year of publication, sample size, study design, clinical diagnosis, tumor staging, methodological quality assessment with AMSTAR-2 tool, type of surgical access and proportion of male participants. The extracted outcomes were divided into: (1) primary outcomes: anastomotic leakage (AL), time to first flatus, overall survival at 5 years and disease‑free survival at 5 years; and (2) secondary outcomes: sexual dysfunction, time of hospitalization, lymph node harvest, urinary retention, urinary incontinence, operative bleeding and tumor recurrence.

Summary of effect sizes

Dichotomous variables were expressed as risk ratios (RRs), whereas continuous variables were expressed as standard mean differences (SMDs). Meta-analyses were performed using the Mantel–Haenszel method with a random-effects model. Study weights were accounted for by considering the sizes of the treatment and control groups, as well as the total sample size. Heterogeneity and inconsistency were assessed using Cochran’s Q test and I2 statistic [12], and heterogeneity was defined as P < 0.1 and I² >50%, respectively. Subgroup and meta-regression analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. While the I² statistic indicates the percentage of total variability due to between-study differences, its interpretation should consider the number of studies and effect sizes, as small studies or extreme effects may inflate I² values [13].

Risk of bias

The quality of all trials was independently evaluated by two authors (You JH and Deng YB) according to the Cochrane quality criteria (Supplementary Fig. S1). An overall risk-of-bias assessment was also performed by each reviewer (Supplementary Table S5). Any disagreements between the authors were resolved through discussion with a third author (You JH). A weighted kappa value was calculated to assess the agreement between reviewers for the overall risk of bias assessment (Supplementary Table S4). Publication bias was evaluated via Begg’s plots, Egger’s tests, and funnel plots. The AMSTAR-2 tool was used to assess quality of reviews: full reporting can be found in Supplementary Table S6.

GRADE quality assessment

The overall quality of evidence was evaluated by two authors (You JH and Deng YB) according to the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) criteria by assessing the following evidence [14, 15]: (1) study limitations; (2) inconsistency; (3) imprecision; (4) indirectness; and (5) publication bias. Any disagreements between the two authors were first resolved through discussion and then, if necessary, by consulting with a third author (Zhang Y) or the senior author (Zhang Y). The results and the overall quality of evidence are presented in Supplementary Table S2.

Statistical analysis

STATA 16.0 (Stata Corp LP, College Station, TX, USA) was used to perform the statistical analyses. For the remaining studies, a random effects model was used to pool the effect sizes to calculate the statistical heterogeneity. Heterogeneity was assessed using I2 and χ2 statistics. Galbraith plots and sensitivity analysis were generated to evaluate the consistency and robustness of the results. If there was significant heterogeneity, subgroup analysis and meta-regression were performed to identify potential sources of heterogeneity [16].

Results

Study selection

After excluding duplicate records, a total of 2534 studies were identified during the initial search. Of these, 1991 articles were excluded based on title/abstract screening to ascertain the presence or absence of the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The full texts of the remaining articles were reviewed, and 16 RCTs [3, 7, 8, 17–29] encompassing a total of 1778 patients were included in the quantitative synthesis (Fig. 1). The primary characteristics of the included RCTs were anastomotic leakage (AL), time to first flatus, length of hospitalization, tumor recurrence, lymph node harvest, urinary retention, urinary incontinence, intraoperative bleeding, conversion from laparoscopic to open surgery, bowl obstruction, overall survival at 5 years, disease‑free survival at 5 years and sexual dysfunction.

Primary outcomes

Anastomotic leakage (AL)

Twelve RCTs [3, 8, 17–23, 25, 27, 28] (610 LT, 601 HT) reported anastomotic leakage as the primary outcome in the LL and HL groups. Pooled analysis revealed that LL of the IMA significantly decreased the risk of anastomotic leak, with a pooled RR of 0.44 (95% CI: 0.26 to 0.72, p < 0.01). No heterogeneity was observed. Secondary outcomes

Sexual dysfunction

Two studies [7, 25] reported on sexual dysfunction (n = 158 patients). Statistical analysis revealed no significant difference between the two groups (SMD 0.90, 95% CI: 0.57 − 1.22, P = 0.65; Supplementary Fig S22). There was no heterogeneity among the studies (I2 = 0%). Due to the small sample size of the included studies, we did not conduct further tests, such as sensitivity analysis.

Time of hospitalization

Four RCTs [17, 18, 20, 28] (n = 409) were included to evaluate the duration of hospitalization. Statistical analysis revealed no significant difference between the two groups (SMD 0.02, 95% CI: −0.18– − 0.21, P = 0.86, I2 = 0%; Supplementary Fig S23). The results of the sensitivity and bias analyses are presented in the supplementary materials (Supplementary Figs. 24–28).

Lymph node harvest

Six RCTs [3, 7, 19, 22, 26, 27] (n = 569) were included in the evaluation of lymph node harvest. Statistical analysis revealed no significant difference between the two groups (SMD 0.07, 95% CI −0.60– − 0.74, P = 0.83, I2 = 93.35%; Supplementary Fig S29). Due to high heterogeneity, subgroup analysis by country was performed (Supplementary Fig S30), but no significant differences were found. Meta-regression using bubble plots revealed no significant heterogeneity attributed to country (p = 0.06, Supplementary Fig S31), sample size (p = 0.17, Supplementary Fig S32), or year of publication (p = 0.04, Supplementary Fig S33). Sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Fig S34) and Galbraith plots (Supplementary Fig S35) indicate that none of the individual studies significantly affected the pooled SMD, and the results were statistically robust. No significant publication bias was found according to Begg’s plots (P = 0.642, Supplementary Fig S36), Egger’s test (P = 0.331, Supplementary Fig S37) and funnel plots (Supplementary Fig S38).

Operative bleeding

Seven RCTs [3, 17–20, 22, 23] (n = 632) were included to evaluate intraoperative bleeding. Statistical analysis revealed no significant difference between the two groups (SMD − 0.04, 95% CI: −0.44–0.36, P = 0.83, I2 = 84.50%; Supplementary Fig S39). All studies were from China. Meta-regression with a bubble plot revealed no significant heterogeneity in the year of publication (p = 0.13, Supplementary Fig S40) or sample size (p = 0.69, Supplementary Fig S41). Sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Fig S42) and Galbraith plots (Supplementary Fig S43) indicated that none of the individual studies significantly affected the pooled SMD, suggesting that the results were statistically robust. No significant publication bias was found according to Begg’s plots (P = 0.764, Supplementary Fig S44), Egger’s test (P = 0.897, Supplementary Fig S45), or funnel plots (Supplementary Fig S46).

Urinary retention

Eight RCTs [8, 17, 18, 20–22, 24, 25] (n = 1003) were included to evaluate urinary retention. Statistical analysis revealed no significant difference between the two groups (RR 0.58, 95% CI: 0.29–1.14, P = 0.11, I2 = 0%, Supplementary Fig S47). The results of the sensitivity and bias analyses are presented in the supplementary materials (Supplementary Figs. 48–52).

Urinary incontinence

Only two RCTs [17, 25] (n = 147) were included in the evaluation of urinary incontinence. Statistical analysis revealed no significant difference between the two groups (RR 0.92, 95% CI 0.20–4.25, P = 0.92, I2 = 0%; Supplementary Fig S53).

Tumor recurrence

Four RCTs [7, 21, 23, 24] (n = 568) were included to evaluate tumor recurrence. Statistical analysis revealed no significant difference between the two groups (RR = 0.75, 95% CI: 0.37–1.53, P = 0.43, I2 = 0%; Supplementary Fig S54). The results of the sensitivity and bias analyses are presented in the supplementary materials (Supplementary Figs. 55–59).

Discussion

Total mesorectal excision is the primary approach for treating sigmoid colon and rectal cancer [30]. The IMA is a key vessel in surgical anatomical dissection; it emerges from the anterior surface of the aorta at the L2–L3 level, approximately 3–4 cm above the aortic bifurcation. The LCA is the first branch of the IMA and is located 1.5 cm from its origin. The LCA is divided into ascending and descending branches. The ascending branch forms an arterial arch with the terminal vessels of the left branch of the middle colic artery. The descending branch constitutes the marginal artery of the descending colon, and the two branches form the arterial arch of the descending colon [31].

Anastomotic fistula is a serious complication after rectal cancer surgery, and blood flow to the anastomosis is a critical factor affecting healing [32, 33]. Whether high-level ligation of the IMA reduces blood supply to the anastomosis, thereby increasing the incidence of anastomotic fistula, has long been debated. The LCA was not retained in the high ligation area 1 cm below the IMA ligation site. The LCA is preserved in low ligation. The blood supply to the high ligation anastomosis and the proximal descending colon depends entirely on the marginal artery of the superior mesenteric artery. If the marginal artery blood supply is insufficient, the proximal colon of the anastomotic orifice may become ischemic, leading to an anastomotic fistula [24, 34]. Preserving the LCA can increase blood flow in the marginal arch of the descending colon. According to statistics, 5% of individuals lack the LCA and the arch of the middle artery of the colon [35]. High ligation further reduces blood flow to the descending colon. However, some studies have suggested that the length of the colon preserved by high ligation is increased by approximately 9 cm compared with that preserved by low ligation, which reduces the degree of anastomotic tension and the incidence of anastomotic fistula formation [4].

In Japan, Komen N et al. [36] used a laser Doppler blood flow meter, and Sung et al. [37] used near-infrared fluorescence imaging technology to measure anastomotic blood supply after rectal cancer surgery. Both studies reported that IMA low ligation could provide a more adequate blood supply to the anastomosis. A retrospective study by Hinoi et al. [38] involving 28 hospitals in Japan and an RCT by Guo Y et al. [26] also demonstrated that preserving the LCA during laparoscopic surgery can ensure a lower incidence of anastomotic leakage. In contrast, Hall et al. compared the changes in intestinal tissue oxygenation before and after high IMA ligation with the index of intestinal tissue oxygenation, and the results revealed that intestinal tissue oxygenation was not significantly affected after vascular ligation [39]. Similarly, Rouffet et al.‘s [40] randomized controlled multicenter trial study and many other studies [24, 41, 42] found no significant difference in the incidence of anastomotic fistula between the HL group and the LL group. The 2008 edition of the laparoscopic radical colorectal surgery guidelines [43] vaguely noted that attention should sometimes be paid to preserving the LCA to avoid anastomotic fistula, while the American College of Colorectal Surgeons (AS-CRS) guidelines [44] and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines [43] do not include high ligation as a recommended procedure. The results of a meta-analysis of 16 articles included in this study revealed that retention of the LCA was indeed effective in reducing the incidence of anastomotic fistula. Greater vessel preservation leads to improved blood flow, a lower incidence of anastomotic fistula, and an earlier time to the first anal exhaust postoperatively. Additionally, LL preserves the autonomic nerves near the IMA root, which may reduce sympathetic disruption and accelerate bowel motility recovery. This rationale is supported by prior anatomical and clinical studies [45, 46].

Nerve damage is a significant cause of urinary dysfunction, especially in patients with low rectal cancer. Studies have shown that despite efforts to protect autonomic nerves during surgery, nearly one-third of patients with rectal cancer develop urinary system dysfunction postoperatively [47]. A better understanding of pelvic neuroanatomy may be key to improving patient prognosis. The results of this meta-analysis revealed no statistically significant differences in urinary retention, urinary incontinence, or postoperative sexual function between patients with high versus low ligation.

From an oncological perspective, some surgeons believe that higher ligation during radical resection of colorectal cancer may result in a greater total number of lymph nodes dissected to improve the prognosis. However, it has also been suggested that metastasis of IMA root lymph nodes is rare [48]. Perhaps when there is IMA root lymph node metastasis, there is a lower possibility of radical tumor resection and a poor prognosis for the patient. Lymph nodes serve not only as pathways for cancer cell metastasis but also as sentry stations that protect the human body. However, dissection of IMA root lymph nodes is necessary for accurate pathological staging of rectal cancer and is highly important for postoperative follow-up treatment and prognosis estimation [49]. In this meta-analysis, there was no statistically significant difference in the total number of lymph nodes dissected between the two ligation methods. Additionally, there were no statistically significant difference in the 5-year survival rate, 5-year disease-free survival rate, intraoperative blood loss, hospital stay time, or tumor recurrence rate.

The evolution of minimally invasive techniques, including robotic surgery, has introduced new dimensions to colorectal resection. Robotic platforms may enhance precision in vascular dissection and autonomic nerve preservation, potentially influencing outcomes related to the level of IMA ligation. For example, Kossenas et al. [50] demonstrated comparable short-term outcomes between robotic and laparoscopic right colectomy in elderly patients, underscoring the role of technological advancements in optimizing oncological safety and functional recovery. While our analysis did not directly compare surgical platforms, future studies could explore whether robotic assistance mitigates the technical challenges associated with high ligation (e.g., proximal vascular control) or amplifies the benefits of low ligation (e.g., nerve preservation).

Our meta-analysis contributes to the current evidence base in three key aspects compared to existing systematic reviews (Supplementary Table S7): First, whereas prior meta-analyses (e.g., Zhong et al., 2022 [51]) frequently incorporated observational studies alongside RCTs, our study exclusively synthesized data from 16 RCTs. This design eliminates confounding inherent in non-randomized designs (e.g., selection bias in retrospective cohorts) and adheres to PRISMA-RCT standards for high-grade evidence. Second, we implemented the AMSTAR-2 tool as a standardized quality benchmark for both included studies and our methodological rigor revealing critical distinctions in quality assessment. Third, our findings resolve contradictions in the literature through enhanced analytical precision: We confirmed a significant protective effect of low LL against anastomotic leakage (RR = 0.44, P < 0.01) with minimal heterogeneity (I² = 0%), whereas Cirocchi et al. (2023) [42] reported non-significant trends (RR = 1.43, P > 0.05) despite similar RCT inclusion.

The quality of the studies included in this review was relatively high. Nevertheless, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the meta-analysis outcomes. First, significant heterogeneity was identified in pooled analyses of specific variables (lymph node harvest and operative bleeding), potentially compromising the reliability of these estimates. Second, comparisons between the two ligation techniques were confounded by variability in tumor staging, surgical protocols, and unmeasured surgeon- or team-level factors (such as technical proficiency) across studies. Third, as this study involved invasive surgical procedures and ethical restrictions, random allocation and blinding were not implemented in some RCTs. Fourth, despite comprehensive literature searches, the potential omission of unpublished data raises concerns regarding publication bias. Finally, our study did not standardize surgical techniques based on tumor height; thus, the generalizability of low ligation benefits to ultralow rectal cancers requires validation in trials with protocol-defined resection criteria (Table 1). Conclusion

In summary, our findings indicate that LL with preservation of the LCA may enhance anastomotic and descending colon perfusion, thereby reducing the incidence of anastomotic leakage, accelerating postoperative recovery (such as a shorter time to first flatus), and decreasing overall postoperative complications. These results suggest that LCA preservation is a safe and effective technique for patients undergoing rectal cancer surgery. However, the scarcity of prospective multicenter trials in the current analysis limits the generalizability of these conclusions. The redundancy of the sigmoid mesocolon, mobilization of the splenic flexure, and patency of the IMA (determined preoperatively) are other technical factors that impact outcomes, and prospective RCTs should standardize the reporting of surgical technical parameters to enable stratified analyses and refine clinical guidelines. Therefore, future rigorously designed RCTs are necessary to validate these findings and strengthen the evidence base.
