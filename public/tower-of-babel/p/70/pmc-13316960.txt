Lessons From Astaxanthin Therapy to Combat Oral Premalignant and Malignant Lesions: A Narrative Review
Authors: Reza Arefnezhad, Zhina Mohamadi, Shervin Shahianjahromi, Amirali Fallahian, Maryam Ashrafi, Armin Nikdehqan, Mitra Saeedi Valashani, Reza Nasiri, Fatemeh Zeinali, Hossein Fatemian, Niloofar Sefati, Fatemeh Rezaei‐Tazangi, Marziye Ranjbar Tavakoli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsOral cancer is one of the most common head and neck malignancies and remains associated with high morbidity, mortality, and treatment‐related toxicity. Despite advances in surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, there is still a need for safer adjunctive therapies with better therapeutic potential.ResultsAstaxanthin, a natural lipophilic carotenoid, has gained attention because of its antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, and anticancer activities. The available evidence suggests that astaxanthin may suppress oral premalignant and malignant progression by inhibiting inflammation, oxidative stress, proliferation, invasion, angiogenesis, and survival signaling pathways, while promoting apoptosis and reactivating tumor suppressor mechanisms. Preclinical studies also indicate that astaxanthin‐based formulations may improve delivery efficiency and enhance antitumor effects in oral cancer models.ConclusionOverall, astaxanthin appears to be a promising natural candidate for oral cancer prevention and treatment. However, most findings are based on preclinical studies, and further well‐designed clinical investigations are required to confirm its efficacy and safety.Keywords: astaxanthin, oral cancer, oral malignant lesions, oral premalignant lesions

Introduction

Cancer, referred to as the unmanaged proliferation of tissue cells, is still one of the main threatening diseases of humans around the world. In this regard, oral cancer, which involves regions like the lip, retromolar area, buccal mucosa, hard palate, gum, floor of mouth, and tongue, has attracted the attention of scientists and clinicians in the current era [1, 2]. According to the World Health Organization (2020), oral cancer accounted for over 370,000 new cases and more than 170,000 deaths worldwide. Recent Global Burden of Disease analyses reveal a concerning upward trend, with incident cases rising from 174,077 in 1990 to 421,577 in 2021, driven largely by aging populations and persistent exposure to risk factors. Projections indicate that the age‐standardized incidence rate will increase from 4.88 to 5.09 per 100,000 by 2036. Globally, mouth and oral cancer ranks as the 16th most common cancer, with a higher incidence in males (12th most common) than in females (18th) [3, 4, 5].

Although risk increases significantly in males over 50, cases also occur in younger individuals. The predominant form is oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), which frequently develops from oral potentially malignant disorders (OPMDs). Major risk factors include tobacco smoking and smokeless forms, which contain over 30 carcinogens linked to precancerous lesions and OSCC; betel quid chewing, especially with tobacco, synergistically heightens risk (adjusted odds ratio up to 17); human papillomavirus (HPV) infection, particularly HPV‐16 (adjusted odds ratio 11.2); alcohol abuse; and UV radiation exposure [6, 7].

The pathogenesis of oral cancer is a multifactorial process driven by endogenous and exogenous factors. These factors lead to various molecular alterations, including changes in protein and gene expression, epigenetic modifications, and somatic mutations. These molecular phenomena exert their pathological role in the disease by promoting cell proliferation, inducing angiogenesis, metastasis, and invasion, and repressing apoptosis [8, 9]. Histopathological assessments and surgical biopsy are two valid and standard diagnostic tools for oral cancer [10]. This cancer, at its early stages, is chiefly asymptomatic and is clinically observed as a red or white patch, lump, or ulcer [11]. In clinical settings, based on the disease stage and overall health status of patients, various therapeutic approaches are offered for oral cancer, for example, surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, etc [12]. However, there are some challenges for clinicians to use these current therapeutic modalities. It has been reported that they can have adverse effects on the human body, including anemia, myelosuppression, kidney damage, hearing problems, and vomiting. Moreover, surgical and chemotherapy‐related strategies may confer poor prognosis and toxic influences [13]. Ergo, there has been a growing interest in exploring alternative therapies for oral cancer with considerable effectiveness and minimal side effects [14].

In the last decade, natural compound‐based therapeutic strategies have received much interest in developed and developing countries thanks to their broad pharmacological and biological capacities [15, 16, 17]. One of the popular natural compounds with tremendous pharmacological and remedial potential is astaxanthin (3,3′‐dihydroxy‐β,β‐carotene‐4,4′‐dione; AXT), a natural xanthophyll and lipophilic carotenoid detected in fungi, microalgae, seafood, higher plants, quail, and flamingos [18, 19]. It has been declared that astaxanthin has different pharmacological effects, like anticancer [20], anti‐inflammatory [21], and antioxidant [22, 23] impacts. Recent research has indicated that astaxanthin could be a useful agent against different kinds of tumors, including oral cancer, as it can suppress their growth by inhibiting cell division, activating apoptosis, and regulating immune functions [24, 25, 26].

Hence, in this review, we aimed to assess the current knowledge about the functionality of astaxanthin therapy in oral cancer, with a mechanistic insight by relying on experimental data obtained from research publications.

Astaxanthin: General Features, Origin, and Pharmacological Benefits

Astaxanthin is known as a red, fat‐soluble pigment observed in different marine animals and microorganisms [27]. The application of astaxanthin in fish and animal feed as a food colorant has been confirmed by the United States Food and Drug Administration (USFDA). Also, the European Commission has offered it as a food dye [27]. It was first extracted from lobsters in 1938 and has since been utilized as a natural pigment for cultured salmon and crustaceans [28]. Generally, crayfish, shrimp, krill, trout, salmon, algae, and yeast are assumed to be natural sources of astaxanthin (Figure 1) [29, 30]. After capsanthin, astaxanthin is the second most significant carotenoid sold worldwide [31]. It has been declared that astaxanthin have anti‐tumor [20], anti‐inflammatory [21], and anti‐oxidative [22] influences, and can be effective in the treatment of metabolic disorders (particularly obesity, diabetes, and non‐alcoholic fatty liver disease [NAFLD]) [32, 33], reproductive impairments [34], respiratory problems [35], neurological disorders [36], mental conditions [37], and cardiovascular diseases (Figure 1) [38]. Collectively, these features make astaxanthin a valuable substance in the medical arena.

Despite its promising anticancer and chemopreventive properties, astaxanthin is highly lipophilic, chemically unstable, and exhibits low oral bioavailability, which can limit its systemic exposure and therapeutic efficacy in vivo [39, 40]. To overcome these challenges, various nano‐based and lipidic delivery systems (including polymeric nanoparticles, nanostructured lipid carriers, solid lipid nanoparticles, and liposomal or other lipid‐based formulations) have been developed to improve astaxanthin's aqueous dispersion, stability, and intestinal absorption. Such nanocarrier platforms can enhance tumor targeting, sustain release, and potentiate anticancer activity while potentially reducing off‐target toxicity, making them attractive strategies for delivering astaxanthin in oral cancer settings [41, 42, 43].

Astaxanthin Source Clarification

Overall, reviewed studies primarily utilized synthetic astaxanthin or did not specify the source, which is a common practice in preclinical research due to concerns about purity and consistency. Natural astaxanthin (esterified, higher oxygen radical absorbance capacity, better membrane integration) has shown greater efficacy or safety in general studies but lacks head‐to‐head oral cancer information.

Chemical Structure and Pharmacological Features

Astaxanthin's chemical structure consists of two terminal β rings and a linear polyene chain [44]. The eleven conjugated double bonds within this structure are responsible for its antioxidant properties as well as its pink and red coloration. Two polar functional groups are also present in astaxanthin's terminal rings: keto (= O) groups at carbon C4 and hydroxyl (OH) groups at asymmetric carbons C3 and C3′ (Figure 1). In acetone and methanol (477 nm), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (492 nm), dimethylformamide (486 nm), and chloroform (86 nm), astaxanthin shows maximal absorption rate [45]. Astaxanthin's hydrophobic polyene carbon chain incorporates into the lipid bilayer of cell membranes through a polar–non‐polar structure, and its polar terminal rings are located near the membrane surface [46]. Astaxanthin, due to its unique location, exhibits exceptionally high antioxidant activity in lipid systems, surpassing other carotenoids such as lutein and β‐carotene (Table 1) in protecting lipids and membrane phospholipids from peroxidation [45].

According to its chemical structure, astaxanthin possesses different isomers. Indeed, conjugated double bonds present in the polyene chain have a key role in the formation of geometric isomers of astaxanthin, chiefly the all‐trans, 15‐cis, 13‐cis, and 9‐cis isomers [59]. The isomeric variability observed in dietary astaxanthin is the result of selective absorption and metabolic conversion that occurs prior to deposition in different animals. Moreover, astaxanthin isomerization can be influenced by environmental factors and food processing conditions, including metal ions, solvents, temperature, and pH [59]. The stability of astaxanthin varies depending on its molecular form, since free astaxanthin is easily oxidized and aggregated, natural forms that have been esterified are more stable. The terminal ring structures of these esterified forms can be conjugated with fatty acids in one (monoester) or both (diester) forms [60, 61]. The bioavailability of astaxanthin is a critical factor for its suitable physiological activity in vivo. Taking its structure into account, astaxanthin has poor hydrophilicity and stability, giving rise to low bioavailability. Astaxanthin, as a carotenoid, is released from the food matrix and is then distributed and dissolved by various digestive enzymes. Thereafter, astaxanthin is emulsified and combined with lipids, such as phospholipids and cholesterol [28]. Polymeric nanoparticles, such as ethyl cellulose‐based astaxanthin nanoparticles, enhance tumor targeting and bioavailability, outperforming free astaxanthin in hamster buccal pouch models by inhibiting PI3K/NF‐κB pathways. Nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs) and solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs) provide sustained release, protecting against peroxidation and improving intestinal uptake via endocytosis. Electrospun nanofiber mucoadhesive patches with polycaprolactone/gelatin deliver astaxanthin directly to oral lesions, reducing COX‐2/Ki‐67 without systemic side effects [62].

Liposomes, formed from phospholipids like soybean lecithin, encapsulate astaxanthin to boost bioaccessibility from 6.5% (free form) to nearly 40% during digestion by stabilizing micelles. Nanosized liposomes achieve high encapsulation efficiency (up to 89% at low doses) and narrow size distribution, enhancing solubility and cellular penetration. These systems protect against enzymatic degradation, support controlled release, and improve therapeutic delivery across mucosal barriers [63].

Pharmacokinetics of Astaxanthin

Astaxanthin, highly lipophilic, undergoes ~90% first‐pass gastrointestinal extraction with ~10% bioavailability in rats (flip‐flop kinetics; time to maximum concentration (T_max) ~6–12 h in humans); fat co‐ingestion enhances micelle formation/intestinal absorption via lymphatic transport to liver [64].

Astaxanthin and Oral Cancer

Precancerous Lesions

Research efforts have been made to investigate the potential of astaxanthin in oral premalignant lesions (Figure 2 and Table 2, and Table 3). In line with this notion, Zhang et al. have scrutinized the functionality of astaxanthin‐loaded mucoadhesive patches using electrospun nanofibers for oral premalignant lesions (OPL) treatment. They showed that this mucoadhesive astaxanthin nanofiber patch can promote drug delivery of astaxanthin to oral lesions without affecting saliva. Also, this astaxanthin‐based formulation was able to abrogate the expression of cyclooxygenase‐2 (COX‐2) and Ki67, markers related to inflammation and cell proliferation, respectively (Figure 2). Other results of this research group pointed out that in comparison with clinical tretinoin cream formulation, the patches did not cause side effects or complications, for example, oral ulcers and hair loss [66]. In another attempt, the epigenetic modifications by which astaxanthin serves its anticancer impacts on oral potentially malignant disorders (OPMDs) were studied with a special focus on its ability to regulate DNA methylation of tumor suppressor genes (TSGs) in oral dysplastic keratinocytes (DOK cells). Whole‐genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) displayed SOX1 and HOXA3 as TSGs with considerably changed CpG methylation patterns in their promoter regions in DOK cells. Following astaxanthin therapy (8 μM), a remarkable reduction in promoter CpG methylation of the TSGs was observed, which consequently promoted their expression. Astaxanthin also exerted diverse effects on DOK cells, comprising attenuation of cell viability and migration, resulting in cell cycle arrest at the G 0/G1 phase and apoptosis stimulation [67]. Collectively, it is inferred that astaxanthin reactivates TSGs and represses cancer cell growth by orchestrating methylation and instigating apoptosis.

Cancerous Lesions

Kavitha et al. have scrutinized the protective effect of astaxanthin in the hamster buccal pouch (HBP) carcinogenesis model with a molecular insight. They found that astaxanthin suppresses the NF‐κB and Wnt pathway signaling by attenuating the regulatory enzymes GSK‐3β and IKKβ (Figure 3). Gene expression and molecular docking evaluations have divulged that astaxanthin probably represses these pathways by deactivating the upstream kinases Erk and Akt. This carotenoid also triggered mitochondrial apoptosis by upregulating apoptotic agents (Bax/Bad) and downregulating anti‐apoptotic factors (Bcl‐2/p‐Bad/survivin), leading to cytochrome c/Smac release and PARP cleavage, showing cell death induced by astaxanthin. It is worth mentioning that the highest dose of astaxanthin was 15 mg/kg body weight, under which no tumor was seen in the buccal pouch of hamsters or only mild hyperplasia was observed. This study confirmed that astaxanthin is a promising candidate for preventing and treating oral cancer [68]. Furthermore, the effect of dietary astaxanthin on JAK‐2/STAT‐3 signaling in the HBP carcinogenicity model with 7,12‐dimethylbenz[a]anthracene (DMBA) has been investigated in another experimental research. It is stated that astaxanthin inhibits JAK/STAT signaling and STAT‐3 phosphorylation and its translocation to the nucleus. The suppressive role of astaxanthin in oral tumor progression has also been reported by inhibiting cell proliferation (by decreasing the expression of proteins regulating the cell cycle (PCNA and cyclin D1)), invasion (by downregulating degrading factors of extracellular matrix (MMP‐2 and MMP‐9) and upregulating negative regulators of MMPs (RECK and TIMP‐2)), and angiogenesis (Figure 4) (by attenuating vital mediators of this process, i.e., VEGF and VEGFR2). In vitro assessment of this work on ECV 304 cells further emphasized the capability of astaxanthin (50 μM) to suppress tube formation and hypoxia‐caused cell migration [69].

A study conducted in 2019 found that astaxanthin limits the kinase function of the PI3K/Akt signaling, which in turn blocks NF‐κΒ and STAT‐3 pathways. These pathways play a role in developing angiogenesis, invasion, and the tumor cell cycle. In vitro findings of this study revealed that astaxanthin, besides decreasing cell viability and inducing caspase‐dependent apoptosis, weakens oncogenic noncoding RNAs regulating PI3K signaling, including HOTAIR and miR‐21, in SCC131 and SCC4 cells. Interestingly, this project indicated that astaxanthin nanoparticles formulated with ethyl cellulose not only potentiate bioavailability and tumor targeting capacity of this lipophilic terpene, but also exert better chemotherapeutic efficacy compared with the native astaxanthin in a DMBA‐induced hamster buccal pouch model [70].

Notably, preclinical studies indicate that astaxanthin modulates key transcription factors involved in oral cancer progression, including NF‐κB, STAT‐3, and possibly Nrf2, suggesting that its anti‐tumor effects are at least partly mediated through transcription factor–directed regulation of inflammation, oxidative stress responses, and cell survival pathways.

Evidence from in vitro DOK, SCC4, and SCC131 cell models and in vivo DMBA‑induced hamster buccal pouch carcinogenesis has confirmed that astaxanthin‑based formulations suppress oral tumor initiation and progression by decreasing COX‑2/Ki67 expression, reactivating tumor suppressor genes, and blocking key oncogenic signaling cascades.

Integrated Mechanisms of Astaxanthin in Oral Cancer

Astaxanthin targets interconnected pathways of oxidative stress modulation, apoptosis induction, and inflammation suppression in oral premalignant and malignant lesions.

Oxidative Stress Pathway

Astaxanthin reduces oxidative stress through direct scavenging of ROS/RNS. It also activates the Nrf2/HO‐1 signaling pathway, which upregulates antioxidant enzymes such as SOD, catalase, and GPX4. This activity helps prevent mitochondrial dysfunction and lipid peroxidation. In oral cancer models, it inhibits ROS‐induced mPTP opening, maintains MMP, and blocks cytochrome c release via MAPK activation, countering pathways like PI3K/Akt‐NF‐κB that amplify oxidative damage.

Apoptosis Modulation Pathway

Astaxanthin induces mitochondrial apoptosis by downregulating PI3K/Akt (reducing p‐Akt), which inhibits NF‐κB/STAT‐3 nuclear translocation, lowers anti‐apoptotic Bcl‐2/survivin, and elevates pro‐apoptotic Bax/Bad, leading to cytochrome c release, caspase‐3/9 activation, and PARP cleavage. In hamster buccal pouch models of oral cancer, it synergizes with these shifts to block proliferation/invasion while promoting G0/G1 arrest [71].

Anti‐Inflammatory Mechanisms

NF‐κB Pathway Suppression

Astaxanthin inhibits NF‐κB signaling by downregulating IKKβ phosphorylation and activity, preventing p65 nuclear translocation in DMBA‐induced hamster buccal pouch models and OSCC cell lines like SCC‐131 and SCC‐4. This blocks NF‐κB‐driven transcription of COX‐2, a central mediator linking chronic inflammation to oral lesion progression, as evidenced by reduced COX‐2 expression in astaxanthin‐loaded nanofiber patches applied to oral premalignant lesions. Upstream, astaxanthin restrains PI3K/Akt activation, a kinase hub that amplifies NF‐κB in oral carcinogenesis [72].

STAT3 Inhibition

Astaxanthin attenuates JAK2/STAT3 signaling by limiting STAT3 phosphorylation at Tyr705 and Ser727, impairing its dimerization, nuclear import, and binding to promoters of cyclin D1, VEGF, and MMPs in hamster buccal pouch carcinogenesis. In OSCC models, this reduces STAT3‐dependent production of protumorigenic cytokines like IL‐6, reshaping the inflammatory tumor microenvironment toward anti‐tumor immunity. Nanoformulated astaxanthin enhances these effects, outperforming free astaxanthin in suppressing STAT3‐nuclear translocation [48].

COX‐2 and Cytokine Modulation

Mucoadhesive astaxanthin patches directly reduce COX‐2 and Ki‐67 in oral premalignant lesions, outperforming tretinoin without causing ulcers or irritation. By scavenging ROS and inhibiting upstream NF‐κB/STAT3, astaxanthin curbs PGE2‐mediated inflammation, angiogenesis, and epithelial‐mesenchymal transition in OSCC progression. These actions collectively mitigate chronic inflammation, driving OPMD‐to‐OSCC transformation [73].

Combinatorial Treatments for Oral Cancer with Astaxanthin's Synergistic Effects

Recently, the synergistic influences of this natural compound with ionizing radiation (IR) in the treatment of OSCC have been inspected by Du and co‐workers. The in vitro outcome of this research team implicated that astaxanthin further augmented cell death, iron levels, and MDA content conferred by IR, but repressed the GSH adjusted by IR in these malignant cells. Astaxanthin could also further repress SLC7A11 and GPX4 and enhance ACSL4 in oral cancer cells. SLC7A11 and GPX4 have a protective role against ferroptosis and thus their suppression makes cancer cells more susceptible to ferroptosis. On the other hand, ACSL4 potentiates lipid peroxidation, an essential part of the ferroptosis process. In vivo findings showed that astaxanthin along with IR have a higher inhibitive function on tumor growth compared with each of them separately. On the whole, this study suggested ferroptosis induction as a possible therapeutic mechanism of this combination therapy against OSCC [25]. On the other hand, the importance of combinatorial therapy for this oral malignancy with astaxanthin and other effective therapeutic approaches has been accentuated by this study. Beyond its single‐agent activity, astaxanthin may interact with standard treatments, and available preclinical data suggest that it can potentiate the effects of radiotherapy and certain chemotherapeutic agents by enhancing oxidative or ferroptotic stress, while also potentially mitigating treatment‐related toxicities in normal tissues; however, systematic evaluation of synergistic versus antagonistic interactions with drugs such as cisplatin in oral cancer models remains limited. To enhance astaxanthin's therapeutic efficacy in clinical settings, it is suggested that future research should focus on harmonizing combination medicines, developing improved delivery technologies, and improving dose regimens [24].

Study Limitations

This review is limited by the absence of human clinical trials and the predominance of preclinical evidence from cell‐line and animal studies. In addition, the available studies are relatively few, use different experimental models and doses, and often rely on short‐term endpoints, which limits direct translation to clinical practice.

Conclusion

Preclinical evidence demonstrates astaxanthin's multi‐target efficacy against diverse cancers, especially oral cancer (Table 4). In premalignant models, it reduces COX‐2/Ki67 and reactivates tumor suppressors via epigenetic modulation. For malignant lesions, it inhibits key oncogenic pathways (NF‐κB, PI3K/Akt, JAK‐STAT) to curb proliferation, invasion, and angiogenesis, and can synergize with radiotherapy. These mechanisms underscore astaxanthin's potential as a safe, multi‐target adjunct therapy, leveraging its superior antioxidant capacity and nanoformulation compatibility for improved bioavailability. Clinical translation could involve mucoadhesive patches or nanoparticle deliveries in OPMD/OSCC trials, addressing current gaps like human dosing safety and Nrf2‐mediated effects. Further preclinical optimization and phase I/II studies are essential to validate these promises and integrate astaxanthin into personalized oral oncology regimens. In future work, integrating omics‐based approaches (such as transcriptomics and proteomics) may help clarify how astaxanthin modulates tumor suppressor pathways and global signaling networks in oral premalignant and malignant lesions, thereby strengthening the mechanistic basis for its tumor‐suppressive effects. Overall, these results emphasize the functionality of astaxanthin in the forms of pure, nanoformulation, or combined with other therapies against oral premalignant and malignant lesions; however, more in‐depth preclinical investigations are warranted to confirm these outcomes.
