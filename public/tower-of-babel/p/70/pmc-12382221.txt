A percutaneous core needle biopsy of deep suprahyoid head and neck lesions with CT-guided: study of diagnostic performance and factors associated with diagnostic failure
Authors: Yingyu Pan, Zhe Ren, Hongbo Zhao, Weiqing Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposePathological diagnosis is important for the treatment of deep suprahyoid head and neck lesions, and tissue sampling needs to balance minimal invasiveness and accuracy. The purpose of this study was to evaluate diagnostic accuracy and factors associated with diagnostic failure of core needle biopsy (CNB) with CT-guided in deep suprahyoid head and neck lesions.MethodsThe records of 204 patients who underwent CT-guided CNB were retrospectively reviewed. CT-guided CNB was conducted for pathological diagnosis with the use of 18-G coaxial biopsy needles. Diagnostic accuracy for the diagnosis of lesions were calculated by comparing the biopsy results with the operative specimen or based on treatment response and clinical follow-up more than 6 months. Factors associated with biopsy failure was identified by chi-square test and logistics regression of procedure characteristics and lesion features.ResultAll 204 specimens were deemed adequate for histological diagnosis, with no immediate or delayed severe complications encountered. The diagnostic performance showed a sensitivity of 89.2% (141/158), specificity of 97.8% (45/46), and overall accuracy of 91.2% (186/204). Respectively, lesions with poorly defined margins or pre-procedural diagnostic imaging were the potential factor for diagnostic failure.ConclusionCNB with CT-guidance is an effective procedure for tissue diagnosis of patient with primary deep suprahyoid head and neck lesions and skull base lesions. Notably, lesions with poorly defined margins and suboptimal pre-procedural imaging emerged as potential factors contributing to diagnostic failure. Specifically, for lesions with indistinct boundaries-wherein the extent of the lesion is difficult to delineate—pre-procedural assessment using magnetic resonance imaging is recommended to enhance the clarity of lesion margins, thereby potentially improving diagnostic accuracy.

Introduction

Deep suprahyoid head and neck region contains a variety of anatomic structures such as maxilla, mandible, masticatory muscle, carotid arteries, jugular veins, and cranial nerves [8]. Due to complex anatomy, vague clinical symptoms and diverse pathologies, it may be a challenge to preoperatively diagnose lesions in this region.

Imaging-guided core needle biopsy(CNB)is proven a safe and effective technique which widely used in the pathological diagnosis of head and neck lesions [3, 5, 11, 14, 15]. It is more accurate than fine needle biopsy [11], and less invasive than surgical biopsy. For lack of an adequate acoustic window, ultrasound-guided CNB are only applied to the diagnosis of superficial head and neck lesions [8]. As an alternative approach, CNB with CT guidance can avoid the degradation of image caused by air-containing spaces and bony structures, which makes it suitable for the diagnosis of deep head and neck lesions. However, despite the high accuracy of CT-guided CNB, Cunningham et al. still reported that about 27% cases were not correctly diagnosed [4]. And there few studies refer to factors which may influence the accuracy of diagnosis.

In this study, the diagnostic performance of CT-guided CNB in the diagnosis of deep suprahyoid head and neck lesions was retrospectively analyzed, and factors associated with diagnostic failure were determined.

Patients and methods

From February 2016 to December 2019, patients who underwent CT-guided core biopsy of deep suprahyoid head or neck lesions were reviewed in this retrospective study. Inclusion criteria were that patients with primary and untreated lesions which could not be confirmed by clinic and imaging, or that pathological diagnosis of whose lesions was needed to develop a therapy strategy. All patients gave their signed informed consent before undergoing this invasive procedure. The radiological, histopathological, and clinical records reviewed were approved by the Hospital Ethics Committee.

Preprocedural imaging examination including contrast-enhanced (CE) CT or MRI, blood routine, prothrombin time, partial thromboplastin time were checked within three days before procedure. After above relevant information assessed by two well-trained, interventional radiologists, lesion characters included size, location, margin and adjacent tissue was recorded to determine the shortest and safest route of the biopsy. Subzygomatic (Fig. 1), retromandibular (Fig. 2), and paramaxillary (Fig. 3) access techniques were used in this study [8]. In addition, submandible approach was applied in the lesion at root of tongue or mouth floor (Fig. 4). This study was approved by the hospital’s Medical Ethics Committee, with a waiver of informed consent for participants [Ethics approval reference number: No.: SH9H-2021-TK534-1].

All procedures were guided by the SOMATOM Definition Flash CT scanner (Siemens Medical Systems, Erlangen, Germany) in the room fitted with an in-room monitor and hand controls. The matrix and slice thickness of CT imaging was 512 × 512 and 1.25 mm, respectively. Patient was placed in a supine position, with head properly turned to the contralateral side of lesion when necessary, to facilitate the needle approach. The entry point was defined by placing a skin mark. After conventional disinfection, local skin was covered with sterile drapes exposing only the entry site. Local anesthetic (1% w/v lidocaine hydrochloride injection, Braun, Ireland) was injected subcutaneously. A CT scan with a range limited to incorporate local anesthesia needle and target lesion was performed to measure the distance and biopsy needle angle.

All procedures were performed by using a coaxial technique, which allowed getting multiple tissues from one puncture meanwhile preventing tumor seeding along the biopsy tract. A procedural CT imaging would be performed after the 17-guage coaxial outer needle punctured to confirm the position. To create appropriate views, three-dimensional image-processing software was applied in manipulating the axial images (Fig. 4). After confirming the tip of outer needled passing through the margin of lesion, the inner 18-gauge needle with automatic aspiration (Fullopty, Sterylab, Italy) was put inside and triggered for biopsies. The process was repeated twice to take additional specimens. All above procedures were performed by the same radiologist. Each group of specimens was numbered and processed with hematoxylin & eosin stains and immunohistochemical stains as required. The pathological results of each numbered specimen were recorded. And diagnosis of CNB was given by considering all tissue specimens from same patient.

The results of diagnosis were divided into 3 categories: malignant tumors, borderline tumors and benign lesions. Diagnostic accuracy was calculated by comparing the CNB results with the gold standards such as surgical pathology, treatment response, or clinical follow-up more than 6 months. Biopsy results were categorized as biopsy-success group, when core biopsy was consistent with final diagnosis, and the rest cases were categorized as biopsy-failure group.

Sensitivity was calculated by dividing the true positives by the total number of malignancies neoplasms identified by the gold standard test. Specificity was calculated dividing the true negatives by the total of borderline or benign lesions. The accuracy measure was calculated by adding the true positives and true negatives and then divided by all cases. A chi-square analysis of variance between two groups was performed with the following factors: lesion characteristics (size, heterogeneity, margin or location) and pre-procedural imaging, to determine potential influencing factors of diagnostic failure. Chi-square test and logistic regression models was to determine the association among multiple variances. All statistical analyses were performed with statistical software (SPSS, Version 21.0; IBM Corp, Armonk, NY). A difference with a P value less than 0.05 was considered to be significant.

Result

204 individuals were included in this study (69 female, 135 male, age range, 17 to 77 y, mean age:46 y ± 15.1). The lesions ranged in size from 1.6 to 7.4 cm, with a median size of 3.5 cm. The epicenter of the mass was used to classify its location. Anatomical location was listed in Table 1. The skull base was the most frequent anatomical location of lesion (n = 62), which was inaccessible to biopsy by using ultrasound guidance. Other characteristics of lesions included heterogeneity and margin can be observed in Table 2. Procedure related complications include minimal bleeding (n = 38) and pain (n = 23).

All the specimens of 204 cases were adequate for histological diagnosis. 158 cases malignant neoplasms were diagnosed as squamous cell carcinoma(n = 67), adenoid cystic carcinomas (n = 40), mucoepidermoid carcinoma (n = 12), chondrosarcoma (n = 10), malignant pleomorphic adenoma (n = 9), osteosarcoma (n = 6) synoviosarcoma (n = 6), non-Hodgkin lymphoma (n = 5) and rhabdomyosarcoma (n = 3). The borderline tumors include 2 inflammatory myofibroblastic tumors and 1 solitary fibrous tumor. The remaining 43 cases diagnosed as pleomorphic adenoma (n = 11), schwannoma (n = 8), vascular malformations (n = 8), meningioma (n = 6), inflammation (n = 5), synovial chondromatosis (n = 3), adenolymphoma (n = 2). The final diagnosis of 2 adenoid cystic carcinomas, 2 squamous cell carcinoma,1 non-Hodgkin lymphoma, 2 vascular malformations and 5 inflammations were confirmed by treatment response and follow up more than 6 months. The rest cases were confirmed by surgical biopsy.

9 squamous cell carcinoma, 6 adenoid cystic carcinoma, 1 non-Hodgkin lymphoma, 1 malignant pleomorphic adenoma and 1 inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor were misdiagnosed as normal tissue, fibrosis or chronic inflammation, due to none specimens of them presented positive results. Sensitivity, specificity and accuracy were 89.2% (141/158), 97.8% (45/46) and 91.2% (186/204), respectively. Statistical values are shown in Table 2. Margin of lesions and pre-procedural imaging were the factors which significantly affected the diagnostic accuracy.

Discussion

The diagnosis and treatment of head and neck lesions are complicated for differing in pathogenesis, tumor biology, anatomical sublocation, and prognosis. Biopsies are necessary for patients with primary head and neck lesions detected on CT/MR or other radiological modalities to develop the treatment strategies. CT-guided CNB is a well-established procedure in the diagnosis of head and neck lesions with a reported accuracy yield of 73–98% in studies of diverse designs [2–4, 7, 11, 14, 15]. However, there are still some cases that were not diagnosed correctly due to inadequate or erroneous tissue sampling. Wu et al. indicated that false negative results can be avoided by targeting the most aggressive part of the lesion when encountering a large and infiltrative lesion, and contrast enhancement techniques or pre-procedural MRI can also be used for better delineation of the target lesion [14, 15]. These views were not proven for limited cases included in their studies. In this study, after a series of large sample variables analyzed, the statistical result suggested that lesions with poorly defined margin or pre-procedural assessment only by CE-CT had lower detected rate. It was indicated that margin of lesions or pre-procedural imaging were factor of diagnostic failure.

To ensure the accuracy and safety of diagnosis, it is necessary to determine the target lesions by diagnostic imaging before procedure. Both CE-CT and CE-MR are commonly chosen as the pre-procedural imaging for this purpose [14, 15]. In general, the imaging characteristics of head and neck lesions include abnormal thickening/mass of soft tissue, loss of fat space, destruction of bone or abnormal enhancement. Margins of head and neck malignancies are sometimes difficult to define due to peritumoral edema and inflammation. Moreover, it is difficult to determine the margins when head and neck malignancies exhibit perineural invasion. Perineural invasion in head and neck malignancies is a well-known disease entity in the range of 2.5–5% [6]. It may occur from any tumor arising from the masticator space or adjacent structures, such as the buccal mucosa, retromolar trigone, maxillary sinus, or nasopharynx. Denervated muscles of mastication may present abnormal enhancement on diagnostic imaging and should be differentiated from actual muscle involvement [1]. In these cases, CT is usually limited in determining the extent of disease, while MRI is the first choice for evaluation given its superior soft tissue contrast resolution [1]. However, to determine margin by MRI are complex because of multiple sequences, which should be evaluated by the experienced radiologist (Fig. 1). Besides, the performance of anatomic structure is difference between MRI and procedural CT (Fig. 1). Therefore, our strategy was to identify the locational relationship between lesions and bone structure. The bone structures usually include mandibular condyle, coronoid process of mandible styloid process or pterygoid processe of sphenoid bone. Moreover, these bony structures also contribute to confirm whether the needle gets to the target tissue during procedure (Fig. 1).

In addition to the above factors, Cunningham et al. suggested that either increasing the number of biopsy needle passes or using coaxial technique was helping to improve the accuracy [4]. Meanwhile, several systematic reviews [10, 13] have indicated the risk of needle track seeding following biopsy, which suggested that it is unadvisable to blindly increase the number of biopsy needle passes. With this taken into account, coaxial technique was performed in each case in this study. Coaxial technique made it possible to get adequate tissues form the same needle pathway, which avoid diagnostic failure due to inadequate sampling. In previous studies [9, 12] of percutaneous imaging-guided needle biopsy for the diagnosis of lung nodules, size of lesion smaller than 1 cm was a significant factor affecting diagnostic accuracy. The sizes of included cases in this study were all larger than 1 cm. Besides, CNB of head and neck lesions are not affected by respiratory movements, which make it easier to get the target tissue. Inner nature of lesions may perform heterogeneous because of hemorrhage, necrosis or calcification, which can be distinguished in contrast enhanced images. These are the reasons that size or inner nature did not significantly affect the detected rate in this study.

Several needle approach methods have been proposed for the CT-guided needle biopsy of head and neck lesions [8]. Subzygomatic, retromandibular, paramaxillary and submandible approaches were applied in this study. The subzygomatic approach with or without caudal-to-cranial needle angulation is suitable for biopsy not only in masticatory space, but also in temporal fossa and to the skull base, including the pterygopalatine fossa region [8]. Compare other two approaches, the puncture point and angle were easier to determined based on the superficial location of the zygomatic arch and the mandibular condyle during procedure (Fig. 1). Retromandibular approach is usually used for lesions located in the parotid space, parapharyngeal space or lateral skull base. To avoid injury to the posterior mandibular vein and facial nerve, the puncture point should be chosen carefully (Fig. 2). Meantime, the styloid process serves as a useful bony landmark which allow to avoid injury to the internal carotid artery. However, room of retromandibular approach is limited for needle angulation, because of the presence of surrounding structures. In this study, paramaxillary approach was used as a supplement for the lesions that cannot be punctured by previous two approaches (Fig. 3). Moreover, this approach is preferred for lesions located in the parapharyngeal space and cervicovaginal space. At last, a submandible approach (Fig. 4) was used in this study. The approach was suitable for the tongue root and mouth floor lesions which laryngoscopy was negative. As the lesions usually surround by mandible, sagittal reconstruction of CT imaging was necessary to confirm the puncture angel (Fig. 4).

There were two limitations in this study. Firstly, population bias may exist in this study because of its retrospective nature. Secondly, because CNB with CT-guided needs high expertise, all procedures were performed by the same experienced radiologist in a single, high-volume medical center. The influence of experience and equipment on diagnostic failure cannot be discussed.

In conclusion, core needle biopsy with CT-guidance is an effective procedure for tissue diagnosis in patients with primary deep suprahyoid head and neck lesions and skull base lesions, with a diagnostic accuracy of up to 91.2%. Lesions with poorly defined margin, inadequate pre-procedural imaging may be factors contribute to diagnostic failure.
