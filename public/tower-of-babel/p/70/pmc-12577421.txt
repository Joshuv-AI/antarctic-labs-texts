Incident cases and healthcare costs of non-communicable diseases attributable to industrialized and total sugar sweetened beverage intake in Mexico
Authors: Francisco Canto-Osorio, Ana Basto-Abreu, Dalia Stern, Rossana Torres-Álvarez, Francisco Reyes-Sánchez, M Arantxa Colchero, Tonatiuh Barrientos-Gutiérrez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo estimate incident cases and healthcare costs of non-communicable chronic diseases (NCD) attributable to sugar-sweetened beverages (SSB) intake in Mexican adults.MethodsWe estimated the population attributable fractions (PAF) of NCDs– including obesity, hypertension, type-2 diabetes, cardiovascular diseases and diet-related cancers– and healthcare costs attributable to the consumption of industrialized and all SSBs. Association measures for SSB-NCD were obtained from published literature. Data on SSB intake were obtained from the 2018 Mexican National Health and Nutrition Survey (ENSANUT), incident cases of NCDs from health registries, and annual NCDs healthcare costs from Mexican studies.ResultsIndustrialized SSB intake contributed to 11.3% of obesity, 10.4% of hypertension, 17.4% of type-2 diabetes, 3.9% of cardiovascular disease, and 12.8% of diet-related cancers (PAFs are disease-specific and not additive due to multimorbidity), resulting in an annual excess healthcare cost of 202 million USD.ConclusionsDespite efforts to reduce SSB consumption, their consumption continues to affect population health and increase healthcare costs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24887-3.Keywords: Population attributable fraction, Sugar-sweetened beverages, Healthcare costs, Incident cases, Non-communicable diseases

Introduction

Sugar-sweetened beverage (SSB) intake is associated with an increased risk of obesity, hypertension, type-2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and cancer [1]. In 2010, 1.2% of the total deaths in adults worldwide were attributable to SSB intake, representing 184 000 deaths [2]. In Mexico, 6.9% of all-cause mortality and 21.4% of cardiometabolic deaths were attributable to SSB intake in 2012 [3].

While prior studies have estimated deaths attributable to SSB intake, few have focused on other outcomes, such as incident cases [4–7]. Recent studies showed that 8.7% of diabetes cases in the United States and 3.6% in the United Kingdom were attributable to the SSB intake [4]. Estimating the attributable fraction of incident cases enables projections of healthcare costs associated to SSB. Cost–benefit analyses of SSB taxes have shown substantial potential savings from reduced consumption [8, 9]. Yet, for most countries, the overall costs of SSB imposed on health systems remain unquantified.

We aimed to estimate the number of incident cases and costs of obesity, hypertension, type-2 diabetes, cardiovascular diseases and diet-related cancers (i.e., liver, prostate, ovary, gallbladder, kidney, colorectal, esophagus, postmenopausal breast, pancreas, endometrium and stomach) attributable to all and to industrialized SSB intake.

Methods

Data sources

Sugar-sweetened beverage intake

We used dietary intake and demographic data from the 2018 National Health and Nutrition Survey (ENSANUT by its acronym in Spanish), a nationally representative, cross-sectional, multi-stage survey representative at the national, regional, rural and levels. The design and methods of the ENSANUT 2018 had been described elsewhere [10]. The survey research protocol was approved by the ethics, biosafety, and research committees of the Mexican National Institute of Public Health.

Dietary intake was collected in a subsample of ENSANUT 2018 by trained interviewers using a validated food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) [11]. For each item, participants were asked to specify, on average, how often did they consume a common unit or portion size of a given food or beverage over the previous week. Data collected included: number of days, times per day, portion size, and number of portions consumed. For the analysis of all SSBs, we included homemade fruit drinks, homemade flavored drinks with sugar, regular soft drinks, industrialized fruit drinks, and industrialized flavored drinks with sugar; for the analysis of industrialized SSBs, the last three items were included. FFQ data for individuals aged 20 years and older was considered for the study (n = 16 978). Participants with non-valid dietary information as defined for ENSANUT by Ramírez, et al. [12] were excluded (n = 1 242). Our final analytic sample included 15 736 individuals, representing 79 223 787 Mexican adults in 2018. Flow chart is available in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Measures of association

We conducted a literature review to identify longitudinal studies and meta-analyses that studied the association between industrialized SSB intake and NCD risk. We included five NCDs with evidence of an increased risk associated with industrialized SSB intake: obesity [13], hypertension [13], type-2 diabetes [4, 14], cardiovascular diseases (ischemic heart disease and stroke) [15], and diet-related cancers (liver, prostate, ovary, gallbladder, kidney, colorectal, esophagus, postmenopausal breast, pancreas, endometrium, and stomach)[16] (Table S1: Magnitudes, characteristics, and data source of relative risk by NCD, in Appendix 1). We looked for studies that estimated the total effect of SSB intake on NCD risk, excluding studies that adjusted for BMI, since BMI is a mediator between SSB and NCD. For type-2 diabetes, we used the hazard ratio (HR) from a large longitudinal study in Mexico [14]. For the other diseases, no longitudinal studies were found in Mexico, so we relied on meta-analyses. For cancer, the most recent meta-analysis included studies that adjusted for BMI. Thus, we used a longitudinal study from Australia evaluating the association between SSB and diet-related cancers [16]. The measures of association for each disease standardized to 355 ml (1 serving) are available in Table S1: Magnitudes, characteristics, and data source of relative risk by NCD, in Appendix 1.

Incident cases

Incident cases were obtained from publicly available records for the 20–99-year-old population in Mexico (Table S3: Data source and incident cases by NCD, in Appendix 1). Incident cases of obesity, hypertension, type-2 diabetes, and cardiovascular disease were extracted from the 2018 Ministry of Health Morbidity Yearbook, which gathers data from the National Information System for Epidemiological Surveillance (SUIVE by its acronym in Spanish) [17]. The morbidity yearbook is an open-source database from the Ministry of Health that collates notified new cases in public and private hospitals at the national level. We could not find incident diet-related cancers within Mexican sources; therefore, we obtained them from the 2020 Global Cancer Observatory [18].

Direct healthcare costs

We used yearly direct healthcare costs per case which include ambulatory and hospital care at public health Mexican institutions for four NCDs: hypertension [19], type-2 diabetes [19], cardiovascular disease [20, 21], and diet-related cancers [20, 22] (Table S4: Data source and direct healthcare costs by NCD, in Appendix 1). Obesity costs were not considered in the analyses because they are already included in obesity-related co-morbidities costs, which could cause double counting. For cancers (ovarian, breast, endometrium, prostate, colorectal, esophagus, and pancreas), we estimated the corresponding proportion of diet-related cancer costs considering the number of cases for each cancer type. Costs from liver, gallbladder, kidney, and stomach cancers were not available for Mexico, so we could not include them in this estimation. To compare our costs with other studies, we converted Mexican pesos (MXN) from the year of study into Mexican pesos in 2018 using the national Consumer Price Index of the National Institute of Statistics and Geography (INEGI from its Spanish acronym). 2018 Mexican pesos were then converted into USD dollars using the average exchange rate for 2018 (Table S4: Data source and direct healthcare costs by NCD, in Appendix 1).

Statistical analysis

Figure 1 provides an overview of our modeling strategy. We estimated PAF for each disease, using the SSB intake distribution and the association measures from published literature. To estimate the number of excess cases by NCD, we multiplied the PAFs by the corresponding incidence cases reported in the Ministry of Health or international registries. Finally, we obtained excess costs by multiplying the excess cases by the annual direct healthcare costs. More details of the estimation process are described in the next subsections.

Modeling strategy to estimate excess NCD cases and excess healthcare costs associated with industrialized SSB intake in Mexico. First, we estimated the distribution of SSB consumption in Mexico. We obtained hazard ratios from the literature to estimate the PAF. We then multiplied the PAF by the yearly incident cases to estimate excess cases. Excess cases were multiplied by yearly NCD diseases’ costs to estimate excess direct healthcare costs. PAF: Potential attributable fraction, NCD: Non-communicable chronic diseases, RR: Relative risk, SSB: Sugar-sweetened beverages, ENSANUT: National Health and Nutrition Survey. Details about the PAF equation are available in the methods section and Appendix 1

Estimation of population attributable fraction

In our study, the PAF quantifies the contribution of SSB intake to the burden of disease for five diseases by comparing the observed disease burden under current SSB consumption levels to a counterfactual scenario with no SSB intake. Each NCD was analyzed independently, without accounting for individuals who might experience NCDs multimorbidity, meaning the PAFs should not be summed across diseases to avoid double-counting preventable cases [23]. For each disease, individuals in the sample were assigned an incidence risk based on their SSB consumption; PAF was calculated using a non-parametric approach to estimate the proportion of cases attributable to SSBs (Sect. 1.3 in Appendix 1) [24]. Analyses were conducted in R software with the “pifpaf” package, developed by our research group and used the code set.seed(9371) for reproducible results [25]. Details about the estimation process are available in Appendix 1. We used population-weighted factors for all the statistical analyses and considered the survey’s complex sampling design.

Estimation of healthcare costs

For each selected disease, we estimated direct healthcare costs attributable to SSB intake. For this purpose, we multiplied the yearly direct healthcare costs per case by the number of incident cases attributable to SSB intake estimated for 2018.

Sensitivity analyses

We conducted three sensitivity analyses to evaluate the robustness of our findings. First, we used a meta-analytic relative risk (RR) for type-2 diabetes (RR = 1.42; 95%CI: 1.19, 1.69) (Table S1: Magnitudes, characteristics, and data source of relative risk by NCD, in Appendix 1), instead of the Mexican longitudinal estimate (HR = 1.27; 95%CI: 1.16, 1.38) because the Mexican study was conducted among women, and only included soft drinks. The RRs standardized to the same volume of SSBs in ml is available in Table S1: Magnitudes, characteristics, and data source of relative risk by NCD, in Appendix 1. Second, not all NCDs are notified to the national health registry, leading to underreporting; to estimate the potential impact of this underestimation we used estimated cases as reported by the Global Burden of Disease Study (GBD). To estimate incident cases in Mexico, the GBD study uses hospital discharge records, epidemiological surveillance records, and epidemiological studies, in addition to national health and nutrition surveys, representing an adequate alternative data source [21]. We extracted incident cases for type-2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and diet-related cancers from GBD (Table S3: Data source and incident cases by NCD, in Appendix 1). Incident cases of hypertension and obesity were not available in GBD and could not be subjected to sensitivity analysis. Third, we estimated PAFs by age group considering the incident cases by the five age groups reported in the Ministry of Health Morbidity Yearbook (20–24, 25–44, 45–59, 60–64 and 65 years), assuming constant relative risks within each age group (Table S5: Population attributable fraction, NCD cases associated to all SSBs by age group in Mexican adults and S6: Population attributable fraction, NCD cases associated to industrialized SSBs by age group in Mexican adults, in Appendix 1).

Results

Table 1 shows SSB consumption in Mexican adults in 2018; the mean intake of all SSBs was 361.6 ml/day, with 234.0 ml/day corresponding to industrialized SSBs. Table 2 shows the incident morbidity and costs attributable to the consumption of all and industrialized SSBs in Mexico. Consumption of all SSBs resulted in 107 471 excess cases of type-2 diabetes (PAF: 25.4%; 95%CI: 18.3, 34.0), followed by 99,748 cases of obesity (PAF: 16.9%; 95%CI: 10.8, 25.4), 80,362 cases of hypertension (PAF: 15.5%; 95%CI: 10.1, 23.0), and 21 088 cases of diet-related cancers (PAF: 19.0%; 95%CI: 11.3, 30.1). Consumption of industrialized SSB resulted in 73 622 excess cases of type-2 diabetes (PAF: 17.4%; 95%CI: 12.3, 24.0), followed by 66 696 cases of obesity (PAF: 11.3%; 95%CI: 7.1, 17.5), 53 921 cases of hypertension (PAF: 10.4%; 95%CI: 6.7, 15.7) and 14 206 cases of diet-related cancers (PAF: 12.8%; 95%CI: 7.5, 21.1). The overall excess direct healthcare cost for incident cases in 2018 was estimated to be 302.7 million USD annually for all SSBs and 201.8 million USD annually for industrialized SSBs.

Sensitivity analyses

Figure 2 shows the results of the sensitivity analyses. Using data from the GBD, type-2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease cases attributable to all SSBs intake were higher than in the main analysis (type-2 diabetes cases: 157 424 vs 107 471; cardiovascular disease cases: 19 881 vs 6 648). For industrialized SSBs intake, a similar increase was observed (type-2 diabetes cases: 107 842 vs 73 622; cardiovascular disease cases: 12 115 vs 4 051). In contrast, diet-related cancer cases attributable to all SSBs intake using data from the 2020 Global Cancer Observatory were slightly less than in the main analysis (19 976 vs. 21 088, respectively). For industrialized SSBs intake, a similar pattern was observed (13 563 vs. 14 206, respectively). For each included disease, the total number of incident cases attributable to all SSBs in the main analysis was similar to the one estimated using the PAFs by age group (sensitivity), as shown in Figures S1 (all SSBs) and S2 (Industrialized SSBs) (Appendix 1); the largest difference in incidence were found for diabetes: main analysis ~ 233 K cases attributable to all SSBs vs sensitivity with age groups ~ 210 K.

Discussion

We aimed to estimate the fraction of incident NCDs and health-related costs attributable to all- and industrialized-SSB in Mexico in 2018. The intake of all SSB contributed to 25.4% of type-2 diabetes cases, 19% of diet-related cancers, 16.9% of obesity cases, 15.5% of hypertension cases, and 6.4% of cardiovascular disease cases. The intake of industrialized SSB contributed to 17.4% of type-2 diabetes cases, 12.8% of diet-related cancers, 11.3% of obesity cases, 10.4% of hypertension cases, and 3.9% of cardiovascular disease cases. The estimated direct annual healthcare cost was 302.7 million USD for all SSB and 201.8 million USD for industrialized SSB.

This study focused on incident disease burden, as an update and follow-up to a previous study that estimated SSB attributable mortality in Mexico using data from 2012 [3]. We estimated similar attributable fractions for diabetes and higher for diet-related cancers than the previous paper. While mean SSB consumption for all SSBs (361.6 ml) is similar to the previous study (355 ml), some methodological differences might explain some observed differences. First, the previous study estimated population attributable mortality (PAM) using 2015 mortality data stratified by sex, age, and region, following the methodology by Singh, et al. [2], meaning that the estimation of the PAM required to be weighted by the number of deaths in each group. Since PAFs in older groups are usually lower and mortality is higher, the age-weighting in PAM calculations may explain its lower overall estimates compared to our incident-based PAFs. Second, in this study, we used updated risk ratios for the association between SSB consumption and each disease; updated risk ratios were not adjusted for BMI, whereas the previous study relied for some diseases on BMI-adjusted RRs. For cardiovascular disease we estimated a lower PAF than the previous PAM estimates, potentially due to our use of more conservative RRs and the fact that while the SSB-obesity-CVD pathway is well-established, direct SSB-CVD links remain under investigation.

Attributable fractions due to industrialized SSB intake were previously reported in other countries. The estimated attributable fraction of industrialized SSBs in our study for diabetes (17.4%), obesity (11.3%) and cardiovascular disease (3.9%) were lower than in Argentina (23.2%, 18% and 5%, respectively), but similar to Brazil (16.4%, 11.3% and 3.4%, respectively) [26]. Cross-country comparisons are limited by methodological differences including dietary assessment tools, serving sizes (Argentina and Brazil 240 ml vs Mexico 355 ml), consumption, disease definitions, relative risk functions and BMI-adjusted vs unadjusted RRs. For instance, the diabetes RR was 1.37 in Brazil/Argentina (BMI-adjusted RRs) versus 1.27 in our study from a Mexican longitudinal study and 1.42 in the sensitivity analysis (BMI-unadjusted RRs). Mexican adults' mean SSB consumption (234 ml/d) was comparable to Argentina (192 ml/d females, 264 ml/d males) but higher than Brazil (120–144 ml/d), which along with different RRs explains the variations in PAF. For hypertension, Korea reported a lower PAF (3.7%) compared to our study (10.4%). However, SSB consumption in Korea was lower and used categories instead of continuous intake, an approach that fails to capture the exponential increase in risk seeing at high levels of consumption [6]. For cancer, some Latin American countries have reported lower estimates [26]; however, the types of cancer included varied, making direct comparison challenging (e.g., our study includes breast and prostate cancer, the most common types in Mexico) [27].

Mexico has implemented specific interventions to reduce the consumption of industrialized SSBs and lower the burden of NCDs. Two years after the introduction of the SSB tax, purchases of industrialized SSBs decreased by 7.6% [28]. However, our findings indicate that by 2018 SSB consumption levels still posed a substantial health risk to the Mexican population. It is important to highlight that Mexico’s tax is relatively low (representing only 5.3% to 6.5% of final prices for a 355 ml and 1,000 ml SSB, respectively). In contrast, other Latin American and Caribbean countries, such as Belize, Chile, and Ecuador, had imposed higher SSB taxes, ranging from 12.7% to 22.4% [29]. To further reduce SSB intake, Mexico must strengthen existing strategies, including revising the tax structure [30], while implementing complementary policies. In 2020, warning labels were introduced for ultra-processed foods and beverages [31], and in 2023, a set of measures was approved to regulate school food environments by banning the sale and advertising of unhealthy products [32]. Effective policies should also promote environmental changes, such as increasing access to public drinking water, and subsidizing healthier food and beverage options [33]. Finally, efforts to reduce SSB consumption should be supported by educational campaigns to promote healthier choices among individuals [34].

This study has some important limitations. First, we used a validated FFQ in Mexican population to estimate SSBs intake [11]. However, FFQ tend to underreport SSB intake, especially among women and people with overweight and obesity [35]. Therefore, we could be underestimating SSB consumption and the corresponding PAF. Second, the 2018 Health Ministry Morbidity Yearbook data underreport incident cases, mainly because these correspond to diagnosed cases, and in Mexico a high percentage of people with obesity, diabetes and hypertension do not know their disease status. For this reason, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using data from the GBD, as this study integrates a greater number of sources to estimate incident cases, and we estimated a higher number of cases attributable to SSBs. Third, for diet-related cancers, our analysis used risk estimates from an Australian cohort, as no meta-analyses without BMI adjustment were available at the time of this study. However, estimates from Australia and an existing meta-analysis rendered similar effect sizes (Australian RR 1.10; 95% CI 1.03, 1.18 vs meta-analytical RR 1.10; 95%CI (1.03, 1.17) [36]. Given potential differences in dietary patterns, cancer diagnosis, and other factors across populations, future studies—particularly from Latin American cohorts—are needed to refine these estimates. Also, while SSB intake data came from 2018, cancer incidence reflected 2020 estimates due to data availability, though changes in cancer incidence in such a short time are unlikely. Fourth, the estimated PAFs represent disease-specific estimates and should be interpreted as the proportion of attributable cases of a single disease, without accounting for multimorbidity. Fifth, our cost analysis was limited to direct healthcare expenditures and did not account for indirect societal costs such as productivity losses, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), or quality-adjusted life years (QALYs). While this approach provides a conservative estimate of the economic burden, it likely underestimates the true societal impact of SSB-related diseases. Finally, the PAF estimations assume a causal relationship between SSB intake and NCD risk, with no confounding or effect measure modification.

Conclusions

Our results suggest that SSB intake in Mexico is associated with a significant burden of NCDs– particularly type-2 diabetes, cancer, and obesity– and elevated healthcare costs. Because the intake of SSB continues to affect population health, policymakers should strengthen measures such as revising and increasing SSB tax to reduce consumption, mitigate the NCD burden, and lower associated costs.
