Evaluation of an Automated Software Method Versus a Delphi Process to Translate Emergency General Surgery ICD Diagnosis Codes: Lost in Translation
Authors: Van Christian Sanderfer, Sullivan A. Ayuso, Mary M. Jordan, Kyle W. Cunningham, Susan Evans, Pooja Palmer, Marc Kowalkowski, Huaping Wang, A. Britton Christmas, Michael Houston, Brent D. Matthews, Addison K May, Caroline E. Reinke, Samuel W. Ross
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective:This study provides an up-to-date diagnosis framework for the study of emergency general surgery (EGS) patients. A final list of International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes was the main outcome for the study. Codes were compared with the number codes generated by MapIT alone.Background:Since transition to ICD-10, a Delphi process to define EGS diagnoses, as originally described for the ICD, Ninth Revision (ICD-9) codeset, has not been performed. Automated mapping software (MapIT) has been utilized, with a few studies verifying the translation.Methods:Using previously defined ICD-9 EGS codes, MapIT was used to identify ICD-10 EGS codes. Review of adjacent codes in a Delphi process resulted in a finalized list of ICD-10 codes. Delphi and MapIT codes were quantified in the Nationwide Inpatient Sample to compare rates to the ICD-9 era.Results:MapIT identified 935 ICD-10 codes from 485 ICD-9 codes. Manual review identified an additional 1907 adjacent codes. In total, after the modified Delphi process, 1579 (55.6%) of manually and MapIT-identified codes were included in the final codeset. After initial mapping, 880 (55.7%) of the final codes did not automatically map through the software. MapIT codes resulted in a significantly decreased number of patient encounters in the Nationwide Inpatient Sample compared with Delphi codes in the ICD-10 era.Conclusions:The Delphi-created ICD-10 EGS codeset provides a more robust, accurate translation of the ICD-9 codes than MapIT software. This codeset can be used to inform EGS research to study and improve EGS patients’ care.

INTRODUCTION

Nearly 1 in 10 patients admitted to a hospital in the United States is admitted with an emergency general surgery (EGS) diagnosis.1 Annually, this equates to over 4 million hospitalizations at a cost of approximately $30 billion.2 With an increasing and aging population, the burden of EGS patients to hospitals and hospital systems is expected to dramatically increase over the coming decades.1,3 On average, EGS patients are older, more comorbid, and present with greater physiologic derangement than their non-EGS counterparts.4 It is estimated that up to half of EGS patients will go on to develop postoperative complications, and 15% of EGS patients will be readmitted within 30 days following discharge.5,6 When comparing mortality for alike procedures, EGS patients are more than 5 times likely to suffer a mortality.4 However, despite the prevalence and morbidity of EGS patients, fewer resources have traditionally been allocated to EGS in comparison to elective surgery or the sister arms of acute care surgery (ACS), critical care, and trauma. Additionally, there is a significant geographic and workforce deficit to cover the volume and acuity of EGS within the United States.7,8

In 2012, the American Association for the Surgery of Trauma (AAST) established a set of International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD-9) codes that identified primary EGS diagnoses through a collaborative Delphi process.9 These ICD codes, which are used to describe and justify billing, were obtained through billing data at academic medical centers and agreed upon following surgeon review.10 These ICD codes have helped to provide a uniform definition for EGS and have guided burgeoning research in the field since that time. In last quarter of 2015, there was a transition from the use of ICD-9 codes to ICD-10 codes in the United States. Unfortunately, there is not a direct 1:1 correlation between the 2 coding systems.10 In the new ICD-10 system, there was a drastic increase from 14,025 diagnosis codes to 69,823 codes.11 Determining the translation of codes from one system to another has been challenging, and while there have been some attempts to codify an ICD-10 codeset, to this point, such codes have not been precisely defined and validated for EGS.12,13

To help create crosstalk between ICD-9 and ICD-10, the National Center for Health Statistics and the Center for Medicaid and Medicare Services developed General Equivalence Mappings (GEMs) in conjunction with 3M (Saint Paul, MN).14 GEMs are text files that promote forward and backward conversion of ICD codes. The Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality’s MapIt tool is one of the most commonly used GEMs for code conversion.14 To this point, there is evidence to suggest that GEMs, such as MapIt, are inadequate alone and may require a more detailed process beyond automated mapping (eg, manual review of codes) to accurately identify the correct codes. In this study, we aim to compare the results of ICD-10-generated EGS codes from MapIt to those formulated by manual review and a subsequent Delphi process conducted by acute care surgeons.15 The ultimate goal of this study will be the creation, compilation, and validation of a comprehensive and standard list of ICD-10 EGS codes that can be used to direct future research and impact patient care.

METHODS

EGS ICD-9 Codeset and MapIt

The previous study by the AAST Committee on Severity Assessment and Patient Outcomes defined which patients constitute the EGS population and the associated ICD-9 codes using a Delphi process.9 This codeset has been previously published (Supplemental Content 1, https://links.lww.com/AOSO/A530) and accounts for 485 unique code groups. A total of 485 individual codes were entered into the MapIt software. To begin the translation to ICD-10 codes and ensure as many clinically relevant codes were identified, multiple rounds of forward and reverse mapping were performed. The ICD-9 codeset was mapped forward to ICD-10 to find adjacent and relevant codes and then mapped backwards to ICD-9 to ensure the corollary was utilized. After 3 iterations of forward and reverse automated mapping were performed, manual review of a tabular list of codes by 2 of the authors (C.E.R. and S.W.R.) identified additional related codes utilizing ICD-10 code tables by diagnosis group (eg, A, B, J, etc.).

Modified Delphi Process

A committee consisting of acute care and general surgeons who practice EGS within Atrium Health then reviewed the identified ICD-10 codes using a modified Delphi methodology. The surgeons came from a diverse training and practice setting to include community, academic, urban, and rural populations, compromising as wide and diverse patient experience as possible. Three rounds of review and voting occurred for the thousands of codes, and initial vote was binary: “yes” to include and “no” to exclude. After each round, the votes were reviewed in committee, and unanimity for exclusion was required for the first round. During each round, participants were blinded to the voting of other participants as they voted; however, the results were available to participants following completion of each round. The number and type of codes included and excluded for each round were tracked.

In July and August of 2019, the first 2 rounds were conducted electronically via the distribution of a spreadsheet containing all codes and their descriptions. For the first round, unanimity was set as the standard for inclusion or exclusion. Those without unanimity were carried forward to the next round. In round 2, a threshold of 85% consensus for inclusion or exclusion was set, and those that did not meet the threshold were carried forward. For the final round, a committee met in August 2019, including a core group of the authors, to discuss the remaining indeterminant codes. Agreement was met among all surgeons through rational discussion and comparison to the original ICD-9 code set as the established standard. The complete EGS ICD-10 Delphi codeset was then categorized according to the original AAST diagnosis groups: cardiothoracic, colorectal, general abdominal, hepatobiliary, hernias, intestinal obstruction, resuscitation, soft tissue, upper gastrointestinal tract, vascular, and other (Supplemental Content 2, https://links.lww.com/AOSO/A531). Since that time, the EGS ICD-10 Delphi codeset has been used on internal research through an EGS Datamart, until enough ICD-10 national data could accrue to evaluate the codeset.16–19

National Inpatient Sample and Statistical Analysis

To evaluate trends over time, our group utilized the ICD-9 EGS codes in the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project National Inpatient Sample (NIS) previously, which has been extensively validated and used for multiple EGS population-based studies in the past.1,2 The NIS was queried from 2012 to 2020 for AAST-defined EGS ICD-9 Codes in the primary diagnosis field and ICD-10 translation of these codes through 2 methods: MapIT and the Delphi code set described above. Data from 2015 were not utilized in the analysis, given the transition in diagnosis codes that year. For categorical variables, Pearson-chi square test was applied. For continuous variable, Wilcoxon two-sample test was used. EGS prevalence using both unweighted and weighted hospital discharge after the ICD-10 transition was the primary outcome of interest (unweighted presented in tables and figures). Additionally, volume was stratified by AAST EGS category and reported over time to compare volumes before and after the transition to ICD-10. In our analyses, the large sample size overpowered the analyses even with correction for multiple comparisons, with a statistical significance set at a lower threshold (0.01), and very small differences achieved statistical significance but not clinical relevance. Data was analyzed using Statistical Analysis Software v9.4. (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC).

RESULTS

A flowchart of the Delphi process is presented in Figure 1. MapIt software automatically mapped 935 ICD-10 codes from the 485 AAST ICD-9 codes after 3 forward-reverse iterations. Manual review of the adjacent tabular list of codes identified another 1907 codes for a total of 2842 codes. Therefore, automated software identified only 33% of the codes included for review.

Each round of the Modified Delphi Process with the number of codes reviewed and either included, excluded, or were indeterminate and moved onto the next round of the Delphi process.

In Delphi round one, the threshold was met for 1196 (42.1%) codes. Of these codes, 403 were included and 793 were excluded. The majority of excluded codes were medical diagnoses associated with the EGS diagnoses. The remaining 1646 codes were included in round 2. The threshold was met for 1192 codes (41.9%), leaving only 454 (16.0%) to be resolved. Of the 1192 codes adjudicated, 1031 were included and 161 were excluded. In the final round, on review of the 454 indeterminant codes, a further 146 codes were included while 308 were excluded. In total, 1580 (55.6%) of the codes that were reviewed were included, and 1262 (44.4%) were excluded. After the Delphi process, 3 additional codes were removed by the senior authors after querying the NIS, which showed that those codes were never utilized within the NIS. Also, 880 (55.7%) of the included codes did not map automatically through the MapIt software. The complete Delphi ICD-10 codeset categorized by diagnosis group is presented in the supplemental content and includes 1577 ICD-10 codes(Supplemental Content, https://links.lww.com/AOSO/A531).

Utilizing the NIS dataset, there were 2,753,878 EGS patient discharges in the ICD-9 era. In a weighted sample, this translates into a nationwide 13,769,389 EGS patient discharges. In the ICD-10 era, there were 3,691,441patients utilizing the Delphi codeset and 3,033,212 using the MapIT codeset. This translates into a weighted nationwide sample of 18,457,199 and 15,166,055, respectively. Unweighted EGS prevalence per year is displayed in Figure 2, and by quarter in Figure 3, demonstrating a statistically significant decrease in patient number from ICD-9 to MapIT per quarter (median per quarter of 183,837 vs 146,922, P < 0.0001) but similar from ICD-9 to Delphi 183,837 vs 180,208, P = 0.06). The variance in volume per quarter throughout the timepoints and codesets is due to sampling volume changes within the NIS methodology. The number of patient discharges was significantly higher in the Delphi group compared with MapIT (P < 0.0001).

DISCUSSION

EGS patients represent a complex and vulnerable patient population and are at high risk for postoperative complications, readmission, and reoperation.16,20–24 EGS incidence rates exceed those for diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and cancer, and are expected to increase with time.1,25 With little opportunity for preoperative optimization or surgical patient selection, patients are at increased risk compared with their nonelective counterparts.4 While there is inherent variability in general surgery practice, the effort by the AAST to define a standard set of EGS diagnoses9 has led to progress in the increased research in the field and established priorities for the disciplines future,26 declaration as its subspecialty of ACS,27 led to advancement in quality improvement,28 and now culminated in verification of EGS centers through the American College of Surgeons AAST/ACS Quality Verification program. However, the transition from ICD-9 to ICD-10 presented a unique challenge for surgeons trying to study the same EGS population, whether at the national, regional, or local level. In our study, the results of ICD-10 EGS codes generated by a Delphi process resulted in a more valid codeset to the original AAST ICD-9 codes, than automated mapping alone. When the MapIt software was used independently, over half of the final ICD-10 codes were excluded. The results of this work demonstrate the inadequacy of automated mapping alone and provide a defined set of EGS codes for EGS research in the ICD-10 era. The inadequacy of the automated methods is also reflected in the stark difference noted in EGS prevalence between the 2 translation methods.

The AAST Committee on Severity Assessment and Patient Outcomes set out to develop a unified definition for EGS coding in the fall of 2011.9 Using its own previously agreed upon criteria to identify 621 ICD-9 codes within the EGS scope of practice, a two-round Delphi process was conducted. At the end of 2 rounds, a final manual review was performed to ensure the final codes were adequately reflective of EGS practice. The AAST study provided a blueprint for the Delphi process that was conducted for our study. However, unlike the original AAST study, which did not require a translation step, the authors undertook an extensive manual review at the beginning of the process to identify codes adjacent to those that were mapped. This resulted in increased fidelity to the ICD-9 codeset, and more importantly, to clinical practice and EGS populations. For example, in the MapIt software translation key clinical diagnoses were left out on manual review, like various types of perforated or bleeding diverticulitis. This manual review revealed the multitude of adjacent, more specific diagnoses that were not able to code with details in the ICD-9 era. For example, take K57.41 (diverticulitis of both small and large intestine with perforation and abscess with bleeding) and its multiple (32) permutations versus ICD-9 (8) codes.

There are few published studies that have attempted to translate ICD-9 EGS codes to ICD-10 EGS codes. One was conducted by Ingraham et al.12 Ingraham et al12 used GEMs to translate the ICD-9 codes defined by the AAST and then iteratively refined their list by way of physician review. During their process, committee members were allowed to suggest additional codes that fit one of the previously defined EGS codes from a comprehensive list of ICD-10 codes. They then used these codes to estimate the EGS burden of disease by utilizing data from the NIS. Their list was finalized at 985 codes, compared with 1580 in our study. The authors systematically reviewed all adjacent codes for each code that was mapped by code group, rather than suggesting additional codes from a tabular list, which is likely why our list of final ICD-10 codes was more extensive. While their study did evaluate the ICD-10 codeset in the NIS, they did not compare it to the ICD-9 era to confirm fidelity to the original codeset and ensure EGS patients were not missed utilizing the new methodology.

In a second study performed by Dalton et al13 the authors automatically mapped 1583 codes using GEM, and 1206 unique ICD-10 codes were identified. In this process, 2 physicians reviewed the matched codes independently to identify any incomplete or inappropriate matches. Additional codes were not added. Their results concluded that automated mapping software showed a reasonable crosswalk towards translation, but noted key deficiencies to create an accurate EGS patient cohort. Importantly, this study also compared the ICD-9 to 10 eras and found a reasonably good observed to expected ratio in volume by AAST diagnosis groups overall, but significant variability by the groups themselves (ie, high in one group and lower in others). Dalton et al13 identified 5 key barriers to the crosswalk (which our study mitigated with the Delphi codeset utilizing the manual review process), including capturing more specific codes, inclusion of modifiers, eliminating mapping to different conditions, and identifying naming changes in diagnoses.

Relying on ICD-10 codes generated by the MapIt software would exclude a significant number of diagnoses and patients, which is evident when evaluating the NIS projected patient encounters. Per quarter, there was a discrepancy of approximately 156,000 patient encounters between the MapIt codes and the Delphi codes. If, for instance, a study was evaluating trends using NIS data over a 5-year period, this would result in a difference of 3.1 million patient encounters. A discrepancy of this magnitude could alter the significance and direction of an analysis and its resulting interpretation. EGS has only recently been recognized as a distinct surgical specialty, and it is undergoing a rapid transition as it seeks to establish credibility and a universally recognized identity. Becher et al27 attempted to describe the tenets that will help to establish EGS as a specialty. Of the tenets described, Becher et al27 emphasized the importance of quality data and evidence-based practice as well as research support by leadership in the field. If evidence-based data is incomplete or inaccurate, then it could be extremely detrimental to the future of EGS as a specialty. It is essential to have reliable and trustworthy data to use it and be able to enact meaningful changes in practice. Given that national estimates of EGS disease burden are still based on the original ICD-9 codes proposed by Shafi et al9, accurately mapping the ICD-10 codes may lead to a new estimate of disease burden currently present, and more importantly, lead to new understanding and changes in treatment of EGS disease.

Although automated mapping proved imperfect, and the manual conversion of codes was both arduous and laborious, the transition to ICD-10 does have an apparent benefit. Principally, given the increased number of codes, there is enhanced granularity of disease diagnosis with the use of ICD-10. For example, when considering the diagnosis of diverticulitis, ICD-10 now allows for the specification of diverticulitis with or without perforation, bleeding, location, etc.10 With much of EGS research performed using large national databases, direct chart review of patients is precluded, which limits the conclusions that can be drawn from research. Moreover, this study, as well as the previous study conducted research by Shafi et al9 and the AAST, focused only on ICD-10 Clinical Modification (CM) codes. ICD-10 Procedure Coding System (PCS) codes were not included. There is even greater opportunity to interpret data when codes that were defined in this study are paired with procedure codes, which will help to determine the operative management of a disease process, and is the next focus in this line of research.

Given the high rate of postoperative complications and mortality, EGS is ripe for quality improvement.2,4 This updated ICD-10 codeset may provide specific direction in identifying areas of focus in quality improvement for EGS. There is also the ability for subspecialists to utilize these codes to evaluate their specific practice, including elective procedures. Surprisingly, an article by Ingraham et al29, attempted to quantify adherence to EGS best practices and found that of the majority of the 20 best practices, there was less than 50% adherence, suggesting substantial room for performance improvement.29 However, in 2021, the American College of Surgeons initiated the creation of EGS Standards, which led to the creation of the EGS Verification Program to improve and promote consistent, timely, and standardized care for all EGS patients.30,31 Additionally, in recent years, ACS fellowships have developed, which have placed more of an emphasis on EGS research and performance improvement. There are now 32 AAST-accredited ACS programs, which is up from 7 just a decade ago.27,32 While structures and processes that influence EGS outcomes, such as hospital volume, patient care models, and interhospital transfer, have become increasingly studied, there are still many critical questions that remain unanswered.33–35 Currently, nonoperative datasets are based on a select list of diagnoses identified by the EGS standards; however, the expanded diagnoses granularity that the EGS Delphi codeset provides may ultimately help answer these critical questions.36 These questions are critically important for understanding of disease burden, allocation of resources, and training of the next generation of ACS, especially with a general surgery workforce that is declining.8,37

This study is not without limitations. Perhaps the biggest limitation of the study is the inherent nature of subjectivity in the Delphi process. The Delphi process was carried out by a group of surgeons in a single healthcare system in a metropolitan region. The scope of practice of these surgeons, therefore, may not be entirely reflective of surgeons who practice in another area of the country. Having representatives from different healthcare systems and from different geographic regions may have resulted in an alteration of the final list of codes that were agreed upon. However, the group did have a diverse practice pattern and surgical education history, including General, ACS, and minimally invasive fellowship training. Future research should focus on applying the list of ICD-10 codes that were identified to study specific EGS disease processes and outcomes to identify opportunities for improvement and facilitate improved patient outcomes.

The most recent trends in EGS outcomes, resource utilization, and regionalization have largely been left unstudied, with uncertainty in disease identification with the transition to the ICD-10 system. There is ample opportunity to leverage this research to evaluate unanswered questions and areas of controversy, both regionally and nationally, and help contribute to research, quality, and innovation in this burgeoning surgical subspecialty,8,38,39 and this new EGS diagnosis codeset, now freely and electronically available, is a valuable tool in that endeavor.
