National program for cardiovascular disease and hypertension in primary healthcare: WHO-PEN program initial scale-up in Iran (IraPEN)
Authors: Davood Khalili, Leila Molaeipour, Koorosh Etemad, Alireza Mahdavi Hezaveh, Elham Yousefi, Farzad Hadaegh, Tooba Kazemi, Farzad Masoudkabir, Kazem Rahimi, Ramin Heshmat, Afshin Ostovar, Fereidoun Azizi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionWe evaluated the IraPEN program, a localized adaptation of the WHO PEN initiative in Iran, with respect to cardiovascular disease and hypertension screening and management.MethodsA cross-sectional ecological study utilizing the Input-Process-Output-Outcome framework was conducted during 2020–2021. Aggregated data from non-communicable disease (NCD) facilities were collected and analyzed by urban/rural primary healthcare settings.ResultsThe study evaluated 610 NCD facilities, serving 1.8 million adults aged ≥ 30, of whom 515,488 underwent 10-year WHO/ISH CVD risk scoring and hypertension assessment, indicating coverage of 30%. Input/process showed all NCD facilities had functional blood pressure devices, 87% had POCT for measurement of glucose and cholesterol and 14% experienced medication stock-outs in the past three months. All staff received CVD risk scoring training, with 60% re-trained during the last two years. Output/outcome indicators revealed 3% of individuals were at moderate risk (CVD risk 10–19%), 0.6% at high risk (20–29%), 0.9% at very high risk (≥ 30%), and 2.7% had a prior CVD history. Prevalence of hypertension was 17%, compared with a national survey, indicating an observed-to-expected ratio of 55%. 80% of patients with hypertension received blood pressure-lowering medications, and 74% of them achieved blood pressure control; 38% of CVD patients received antiplatelet/anticoagulant therapy, and 66% of eligible patients took statins.ConclusionIraPEN demonstrated low coverage for CVD risk scoring and hypertension detection but acceptable treatment and control rates. Management of patients with CVD seems suboptimal. The WHO/ISH CVD risk scoring may lack in detecting high and very high-risk individuals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05992-6.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are the leading cause of premature mortality globally, accounting for about 32% of deaths, primarily in low and middle income countries [1]. Hypertension, the silent killer, affects approximately 1.3 billion people and is a major risk factor for CVDs [2]. In Iran, CVDs represent nearly 80% of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) and around 50% of all deaths [3], with a hypertension prevalence of 25.6% in adults aged 25–70 years [4].

In 2012, the World Health Assembly set a target for member states to achieve a 25% reduction in premature mortality from NCDs by 2025, emphasizing CVDs [5]. To facilitate this objective, the World Health Organization (WHO) introduced the Package of Essential Noncommunicable Disease Interventions (PEN), specifically designed for low-resource settings. This package provides cost-effective strategies for the prevention and management of NCDs, enhancing healthcare delivery in underserved areas [6].

To reduce the burden of NCDs, Iran launched the National Program for NCDs, including Prevention and Control of Diabetes within the healthcare system since 1999 [7]. In 2014, as an adopted WHO’s PEN protocols, the IraPEN program was integrated into the health care settings. A pilot phase of the IraPEN was started in four cities in different provinces during 2015–2016, which was modestly effective in reducing blood pressure, plasma glucose and total cholesterol [8, 9]. After that, the first scaled-up phase expanded coverage to 50,000 individuals under each of the 63 medical universities, which cover the Iranian primary healthcare (PHC) system throughout the country. A key component of IraPEN is the 10-year cardiovascular risk assessment for adults aged 30 years and older, which supports the management of CVDs and hypertension while ensuring repeated risk assessment, systematic follow-up care, and appropriate referrals within PHC settings [10–12]. The WHO/ISH risk prediction chart for the Eastern Mediterranean region B was used to classify individuals into four risk categories, low (< 10%), moderate (10–19%), high (20–29%), and very high (≥ 30%), based on their age, sex, smoking status, systolic blood pressure, diabetes, and total blood cholesterol [13].

Evaluating primary healthcare services is vital for optimizing performance, identifying gaps, and addressing inefficiencies before nationwide scale-up. Following our previous report on diabetes [14], this study assesses the first scaled-up phase of IraPEN in screening and managing CVDs and hypertension. Our findings offer insights into NCDs management in PHC, highlighting strengths and shortcomings to help policymakers prioritize improvements.

Materials and methods

Study design and program evaluation framework

This is a cross-sectional ecological study to evaluate a national program using routine health system data. This study provides a broad evaluation of the IraPEN program implemented in primary healthcare centers selected for the initial nationwide scale-up phase. Each of the 63 medical universities covered a comprehensive health center with approximately 50,000 individuals. IraPEN has been introduced in these centers to deliver all components of PEN, addressing CVD, diabetes, cancer, and respiratory diseases. A summary of the health system structure and primary health care facilities for CVD in Iran is described in the Additional file 1.

We are focusing on the implementation and results of the program in screening and managing CVD and hypertension from September 2020 to September 2021. Utilizing Brown’s Input-Process-Output-Outcome (IPO) framework, we analyzed key elements: inputs (resources), processes (activities), outputs (immediate results), and outcomes (specific results). This structured approach enhances clarity, aids evaluation, and informs effective program planning [15]. In this study, indicators were defined for each IPO component, as shown in Additional file 2.

Risk assessment, follow-up, and specialist referrals are tailored to individual risk as follows: Individuals with < 10% CVD risk undergo annual care and follow-up, while those with 10–19%, 20–29%, and ≥ 30% risk are cared for and followed every 9, 6, and 3 months by community-health worker, respectively. Hypertension is defined as having a systolic blood pressure of 140 mm Hg or higher, a diastolic blood pressure of 90 mm Hg or higher, or a prior medical diagnosis of high blood pressure [8]. Hypertension at any assessment prompts physician referral.

The IraPEN initiative utilizes a collaborative and multi-level referral system for CVD and hypertension management. Referrals, both urgent (for people with a risk of ≥ 30%) and non-urgent (for people with a risk of 20–29%), guide patients to appropriate diagnostic, counseling, or treatment services, encompassing primary care professionals (physicians, nutritionists, psychologists) and, when indicated, secondary and tertiary prevention interventions. Additionally, IraPEN integrates counseling services to promote healthy lifestyles, emphasizing physical activity, balanced nutrition, and smoking cessation. A summary of the protocol of service delivery can be found in the Additional file 1.

Data collection procedure and analysis

To compute these indicators, aggregated data were collected from all facilities involved in the scale-up of the IraPEN program at both facility and university levels. An Excel spreadsheet was designed to outline the necessary data, allowing for manual entry from the integrated electronic health records in Iranian health centers (SIB, NAB, and SINA software) [17, 18]. A guide was created to assist with the accurate extraction of data from the electronic health records. Data collection was conducted by NCD facility heads and verified by NCD health officers at medical universities. The data were reviewed, and feedback was provided to district health officers regarding any missing values or inconsistencies. If needed, further evaluation took place at the Ministry of Health (MoH) level. The data were double-checked, validated, and analyzed using STATA 14.1. Indicators were presented as both numbers and proportions by urban, rural, and urban-rural NCD settings, as well as by medical universities that support and manage NCD facilities.

Estimating the prevalence of hypertension using STEPS

To estimate the coverage of PHC in detecting hypertensive patients, we calculated the ratio of the observed to the expected prevalence of hypertension. The expected prevalence of hypertension was estimated based on data from the STEPs 2016 survey [19]. Of note, Iran’s national STEPS survey, based on the WHO Stepwise approach, estimates NCD risk factor indicators through household surveys. Conducted over eight rounds (2005–2021), it collected comprehensive data on NCD risk factor prevalence. This study utilizes data from the seventh round (2016) due to the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on the eighth round’s (2021) data collection. The ratio of observed to expected prevalence was reported for each population covered by medical universities.

Results

The evaluation of the IraPEN scale-up involved 1,785,226 individuals aged 30 years and older across 56 out of 63 medical universities (Additional file 3). These universities operated 184 comprehensive health centers, comprising 95 in urban areas, 60 in rural areas, and 29 in urban-rural areas. Each comprehensive health center includes multiple NCD facilities, referred to as Health Posts in urban areas and Health Houses in rural areas. Overall, the Universities supervised 610 NCD facilities, which included 203 urban, 257 rural, and 150 urban-rural facilities.

By September 2021, approximately 31% of the targeted population had been called and referred for CVD screening, achieving a screening response rate of 93.2% among people called for screening (Table 1).

Input indicators results

All physicians and Community-health worker were trained in CVD risk scoring, with 68.2% of physicians and 56% of Community-health worker re-trained in the past two years. Among urban and rural-urban NCD facilities, 66.4% had at least one trained nutritionist, and 82.8% at least one trained psychologist. None of the rural NCD facilities had these specialists. All nutritionists and psychologists were trained in CVD risk scoring, with 67.1% and 60.4%re-trained recently. All NCD facilities had functional blood pressure measuring devices. POCT was available in 87.2% of all NCD facilities and only eight universities reported less than 100% (52–91%) POCT coverage; however, the lack of POCT had been compensated for by on-site laboratories test. Besides, among the 56 universities, 30 lacked on-site drugstores due to nearby private pharmacies. Of those with drugstores, 14.1% of NCD facilities experienced at least one stock-out in the past three months (Table 2).

Process indicators results

As previously reported [14], smoking cessation and healthy lifestyle counseling services were available in all rural and urban NCD facilities. All facilities had a formal follow-up process for CVD and hypertensive patients. Approximately 89.3% of universities established formal referral processes to higher management levels (Additional file 4).

Output indicators results

Overall, 28.9% of individuals aged ≥ 30 years under the coverage of NCD facilities were screened using WHO/ISH risk scoring, with higher rates in rural (42.6%) compared to urban settings (27.0%). Screening coverage exceeds 50% at only 5 universities (Babol, Nishabur, Ilam, Shiraz, Zahedan). Conversely, 30 universities had coverage below 30% (Fig. 1).

Physician visits in the past three months occurred in 46.8% of hypertensive patients, with higher rates in rural (61.7%) than urban (40.6%). Additionally, 90.2% received lifestyle education. Outcome indicators results

The IraPEN program revealed a hypertension prevalence of 17.4% among screened individuals over 30 years of age. Notably, only 5.0% of these cases represented new diagnoses identified through IraPEN, with the majority of individuals already aware of their hypertension status prior to enrollment.

Based on the 2016 STEPS survey, the overall expected HTN prevalence in the Iranian adult population (30 years old and over) was 31.7%. These results indicate that only 55% of the estimated hypertensive population were enrolled in NCD facilities (Table 5).

Discussion

As one of the earliest studies, this investigation assessed the national CVD and hypertention management program within Iran’s Package of Essential Non-communicable Diseases, employing an input-process-output-outcome framework. Despite seemingly satisfactory input and process indicators, these did not yield commensurate output and outcome indicators. Specifically, the key finding revealed that IraPEN identified approximately 50% of the expected hypertensive patients. Among this group, 80% were undergoing treatment for hypertension, and nearly 74% demonstrated controlled blood pressure. Of those screened, 2.7% were known CVD patients, but only 40% received antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy and 66% statin therapy. WHO/ISH risk scoring showed that around 5% of the general population had a CVD risk of 10% or above.

The WHO PEN initiative seeks to improve NCD care in low-resource environments through standardized approaches, but implementation barriers persist. These include lack of systematic training, essential resources [20, 21], and effective policy execution influenced by healthcare access, medication availability, financial constraints, and service provider values [20–25]. Zimbia [26], Moldova [27], Nigeria [28, 29], Palestine [30], Samoa [31] Egypt [32], Nepal [33] and African countries [34] have faced inadequate healthcare provider training, essential equipment disparities, funding shortages, high patient costs, and limited access to specialized care. Similar health systems, such as those in Turkey, also struggle with weak oversight, disrupted referral pathways, and uneven healthcare workforce distribution [22]. In Iran, IraPEN implementation faces similar hurdles, including insufficient retraining (only 60% of staff retrained over two years), and critical shortages of experts for lifestyle education (35% of urban centers lack nutritionists; 18% lack psychologists and all rural NCD facilities lacked both). It is noteworthy, although 70% of the population resides in urban areas, disparities in access to supportive services persist. Counseling services targeting a healthy lifestyle and facilitating smoking cessation are essential for the effective management of CVD and hypertension. Despite the provision of such services through IraPEN, 11% of hypertensive patients still smoke (10% urban, 15% rural), near the national average of 14% [35]. The lower smoking rates in urban may reflect access to psychological services, consistent with findings from IraPEN’s diabetes management [14].

Our findings further identify gaps in pharmaceutical service availability as a critical limitation within the continuum of care. Among facilities with reported on-site pharmacy status, more commonly in area without nearby private pharmacy, 14.1% reported some stock-outs. Even intermittent shortages of essential medicines can disrupt treatment continuity and compromise disease control. Reduced availability of key medications points to inequitable distribution of health resources. For example, despite achieving screening coverage above the national average (30%), Zabol University of Medical Sciences reported blood pressure control rates of approximately 50%, substantially below the national average (73.7%). This mismatch between screening uptake and disease control suggests deficiencies in post-diagnostic care, particularly in sustained access to medications. Consistent with these findings, Reesi et al. reported that over 30% of primary health care centers in Sistan and Baluchestan province have limited or no access to pharmacies [36]. Collectively, these findings underscore the need to address structural inequities in medication access to improve the effectiveness of NCD control programs, particularly in rural and underserved settings.

The full implementation of formal follow-up and referral systems in nearly 90% of IraPEN universities has not translated into improved output and outcome indicators. Substantial non-adherence to scheduled follow-up visits was observed in hypertensive patients and only 53% of these patients had been visited by physicians in the past three months. Urban areas exhibited 20% greater non-adherence than rural areas, suggesting differential effectiveness of follow-up care, potentially related to Behvarz (community-health worker) performance [37]. The IraPEN pilot (2016–2017) also indicated low visit adherence (< 20%) [11]. Adherence was particularly low among High-risk CVD individuals and men compared to those with lower CVD risk scores and women [11]. Absence of follow-up reminder is a similar barrier noted in Nepal [33]. Additionally, limitations in the IraPEN electronic health information system (SIB) compromise referral process effectiveness; while 70% of CVD patients attend secondary prevention clinics, poor feedback to primary care hinders systematic linkage. Strengthening referral systems [9] and inter-organizational relationships is vital for program establishment and sustainability [38].

The STEPS 2016 study estimated hypertension prevalence at 32% among Iranian adults > 30; however, only 17% of individuals enrolled in IraPEN NCD facilities were identified with hypertension. Moreover, STEPS 2021 indicated 40% of hypertensive patients remain unaware of their condition, while IraPEN diagnosed only 5% as new cases. These discrepancies highlight an underlying structural and implementation challenges, including limited access to PHC services, lack of general population awareness regarding NCD facilities, community-health workers’ dissatisfaction, workforce and system capacity constraints, and potential inefficiencies in screening strategies. Together, these findings suggest that improving screening coverage and early detection requires strengthening program implementation and addressing health system barriers. Furthermore, preliminary IraPEN scale-up results indicated promising hypertension management outcomes, with 80% of hypertensive patients receiving treatment and nearly 74% achieving blood pressure control. These figures exceed the national averages reported in the STEPS 2021 study, which noted treatment rates of 52% and control rates of 42% among Iranian adults with hypertension. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as they may be overestimated due to selection bias, a more health-seeking PHC population, and information bias, incomplete reporting across universities. Additionally, routine program data may preferentially capture patients retained in care, potentially inflating control rates. Therefore, these results likely reflect outcomes among engaged PHC users rather than the general hypertensive population.

Around 5% of individuals screened in IraPEN were classified as having moderate to very high 10-year CVD risk (≥ 10%), which is substantially lower than national estimates. A national STEPs study applying the new WHO laboratory risk score identified approximately 31.5% of Iranian adults with moderate to very high risk in 2016 [39], suggesting that the sensitivity of CVD case finding within the IraPEN program appears to be suboptimal. Several factors may explain this discrepancy: First, the use of WHO/ISH risk charts for the Eastern Mediterranean Region B, which have known limitations in sensitivity, may underestimate true risk [40, 41]. Second, suboptimal screening coverage and potential under-detection of high-risk individuals within PHC settings may contribute to lower observed prevalence [11, 13]. Third, inaccuracies in the measurement or recording of key risk factors (e.g., blood pressure, diabetes status) could lead to misclassification. Finally, selection bias, where individuals attending PHC may differ systematically from the general population, may further contribute to underestimation. These findings suggest that both methodological and implementation-related factors may limit the effectiveness of current risk stratification in IraPEN, highlighting the need for adopting updated WHO risk models and developing locally calibrated risk prediction tools and increasing the coverage of screening among the general population [42–44].

CVD prevention strategies encompass lifestyle modifications and targeted pharmacotherapy with statins and antiplatelet/anticoagulant agents, guided by accurate risk stratification [45–48]. However, IraPEN revealed inadequate CVD management, with only 38% of eligible patients receiving antiplatelet/anticoagulant therapy and 66% receiving statins, rates significantly below recommended guidelines, particularly considering the updated 7.5–10% absolute risk threshold [49, 50] and patient preference [51] for statin initiation. Furthermore, geographic disparities exist, with rural areas exhibiting lower prescription rates (26% versus 41% in urban areas), highlighting potential inequities in treatment access, staff awareness, or variations in guideline implementation.

Several limitations merit consideration. First, a comprehensive evaluation of all universities involved in the IraPEN initial program scale-up was not feasible, although a substantial proportion (56 out of 63) were included in the analysis. Second, some output and outcome indicators were not reported by all participating universities. Third, data collection relied on self-reporting from healthcare managers at the facility level, which introduces the potential for response bias and indicator over-reporting; however, detailed training and supervision on data extraction from the registry system was provided to mitigate this effect. Forth, it is important to note that this is a descriptive study and we did not perform any formal association or regression analyses to test relationships between program inputs, outputs, and population‑level outcomes. As such, our findings should be interpreted primarily as a national monitoring profile rather than as evidence of causal impact. Because data are aggregated at the NCD facility level, any implicit interpretation of group‑level patterns to individual‑level associations would be subject to the ecological bias/fallacy. Future individual‑level studies will be needed to assess the causal effects of specific program components on CVD outcomes. Lastly, the study’s timeframe coincided with the COVID-19 pandemic, disrupted routine NCD services, including screening activities, follow-up visits, and healthcare utilization, potentially leading to underestimation of service coverage and performance indicators [52].

Despite data limitations, this study provides a near-comprehensive evaluation of the IraPEN program’s initial scale-up, leveraging routinely collected health system data alongside rigorous quality control measures and standardized data extraction protocols from Iran’s electronic health systems. Widespread university participation yielded valuable insights into the program’s successes and challenges in managing CVD and hypertension within the national PHC system.

Conclusions

This pioneering evaluation of Iran’s IraPEN program reveals disparities in screening coverage and care delivery, especially for the detection of high-risk people and early detection of hypertension. While most hypertensive patients receive treatment and achieve control, CVD management remains inadequate, and under half of eligible patients receive antiplatelet/anticoagulant therapy and statins. The WHO/ISH CVD risk scoring may lack in detecting high and very high-risk individuals and the use of updated WHO CVD risk models is recommended.
