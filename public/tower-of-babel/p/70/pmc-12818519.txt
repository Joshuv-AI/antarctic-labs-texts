Population Annoyance by Below-Limit Industrial Noise from the Mining Industry: A Questionnaire Survey Methodology
Authors: Markéta Stanovská, Hana Šlachtová, Ľubica Argalášová, Dana Potužníková, Hana Tomášková
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective:The operation of open-pit mines relies on powerful equipment that produces noise emissions reaching up to 120 dB. Populations living in the vicinity of mines often complain about noise even when the noise levels are below the permissible limits. This study aims to assess noise perception and related environmental risks among residents living near the Turow open-pit lignite mine, located at the tri-border of the Czech Republic, Poland, and Germany.Methods:A modified ISO/TS 15666:2003 questionnaire was used to collect data from residents in exposed areas (n = 59 of 230 adult residents) and in a control area (n = 54 of 360 adult residents) during 2022 to 2023. All adult residents were contacted. The χ2 test and a binary logistic regression model at the 5% significance level were used for the analysis.Results:Industrial noise, described as “humming” and “rumbling,” was identified as the primary concern in exposed areas (P = 0.030), with residents reporting high levels of annoyance and a perceived worsening of the noise situation over the past 5 years. Another significant problem was dust pollution (P = 0.024). The overall perceived quality of life in the exposed area was more than two times worse than that in the control area (odds ratio = 2.25, 95% confidence interval: 1.01–4.99).Conclusion:Despite the small number of exposed residents and the low response rate in this study, its findings suggest that even legally acceptable noise levels can significantly affect the perceived quality of life of residents.

INTRODUCTION

People are exposed to various environmental risks. According to the World Health Organization, nonoccupational noise is classified into five categories: road traffic, railway, aircraft, wind turbine, industrial, and leisure noises.[1] Environmental noise is a leading factor affecting the quality of life of residents, especially in large cities.[23] This major public health problem has already been associated with adverse cardiovascular and metabolic events, impaired cognitive development in children, compromised mental health, and the development of irritability or sleep disorders.[45] The exposure and actual burden of environmental noise on the population have been systematically assessed in the member states of the European Union.[6] Studies from around the world have focused on the effects of various noise sources on public health.[178] A large number of studies have focused on the impact of road, rail, and air traffic noises on public health. Epidemiological studies have found that long-term exposure to the above noises amplifies the risk of cardiovascular disease,[9101112] reduces sleep quality, elevates levels of nervousness and irritability,[13141516] and increases the risk of cognitive impairment.[17] Many studies have focused on the effects of a single noise source and its negative effect on public health[17] or within a specific environment (e.g., schools and workplaces).[18] Scientific studies most commonly use questionnaire surveys to obtain relevant data from exposed residents. The collection of such data helps in understanding how noise affects individuals across various environments and under different circumstances. Using questionnaires allows the assessment of perceived noise exposure and its effect on health, including both psychological and physiological stress. In addition to basic demographic information (age, gender, and education), survey questions typically focus on the subjective perception of the most annoying noise source, the intensity of noise from the perceived source, and the degree of disturbance caused by this type of noise. Furthermore, these questions often address health effects, such as whether residents experience sleep problems (“Do you have difficulty falling asleep due to noise?”) and feel irritated, nervous, or stressed (“How often does noise make you feel nervous or irritable?”), or focus on the influence of noise on sleep quality and overall quality of life.[1319]

The publicly available Weinstein’s Noise Sensitivity Scale (WNSS) is an example of a questionnaire focusing on environmental noise. It is a tool used to measure individual sensitivity to noise. The scale assesses how much people perceive and react to different levels of noise in their environment. WNSS is employed in studies aiming to identify individuals who are highly susceptible to noise-induced stress and to evaluate the potential influence of such stress on their health.[20] Other questionnaires assessing noise exposure from traffic and industrial sources include the Environmental Noise Questionnaire, which covers questions about noise perception, its sources, and its influence on daily life and health, and the European Quality of Life Survey, which includes modules evaluating the effect of noise on health and well-being.[21] In addition to traditional questionnaire surveys, modern approaches now utilize data from social media and crowdsourcing systems to monitor noise pollution and its consequences for health.[22]

As part of a study conducted in the Czech Republic, a project of the Technology Agency of the Czech Republic (TA CR), we developed a questionnaire to gather information on the perceived severity of various environmental factors, including noise pollution.[23] Our study concentrates on the area surrounding the Turow mine, which is located in the southwestern part of Poland near its border with the Czech Republic. The Turow mine is a large open-pit mine covering approximately 30 km2, with a depth exceeding 200 m and conveyor belts extending over a distance of approximately 70 km. It has been the subject of media attention for many years because it is a source of environmental and health concerns for the local population.[24]

The operation of open-pit mines is relies on powerful equipment that produces noise emissions of up to 120 dB; however, monitored noise levels in the exposed Czech areas remain below the permissible limits.[25] The area surrounding the Turow mine in Poland is unique because of the combined effect of multiple negative environmental factors, including noise from mining activities, groundwater depletion, and increased dust levels.

The aim of the presented questionnaire, which is based on a socioacoustic study within the TA CR project, is to assess how residents living near the Turow open-pit lignite mine, located at the tri-border of the Czech Republic, Poland, and Germany, perceive noise annoyance relative to other environmental and general problems. We hypothesize that, in contrast to that in the control area, noise from industrial sources would be the main problem in the exposed area.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design

This study was designed as a questionnaire survey among residents of three Czech villages near the Turow mine in Poland. It involved a sample of people from noise-affected areas (two exposed villages, namely, Uhelna and Oldrichov na Hranicich) and a reference group from a non-noise-affected area (one control village, namely, Vaclavice). Firstly, a pilot study was conducted to verify the validity and comprehensibility of the questionnaire and to identify the most appropriate method for its distribution. The eligibility criteria for inclusion in the study required participants to be at least 15 years old and to have permanent residence within the selected study area. In cases in which the questionnaire was completed by individuals under 18 years of age, data collection was conducted under parental supervision. In accordance with the applicable legislation of the Czech Republic, information related to the health status of minors may be provided in the presence of a parent or legal guardian. All residents who met the inclusion criteria were contacted. Subsequently, 230 and 360 questionnaires were distributed in noise-affected and non-noise-affected areas, respectively. The questionnaire could be completed either in printed or electronic form, and each participant received a financial reward for completing it. An informed consent form was prepared for all participants and was either included as part of the printed version of the questionnaire or embedded within the online format. Participation in the study was voluntary, and completion of the questionnaire was considered as consent to participate, in accordance with standard ethical practices used in population-based surveys. The project methodology was approved by Ethics Committee Faculty of Medicine, University of Ostrava (number 07/2022). Individuals who refused to complete the questionnaire (nonrespondents) were asked to provide basic demographic information and the reason for their refusal.

The main study was conducted in two phases: September to October 2022 (n = 80) and September to October of 2023 (n = 54). A total of 22 people completed the questionnaire for nonrespondents. Twenty-one individuals participated in both phases of the study, and the reliability of the questionnaires was assessed based on their responses [Figure 1].

Data Collection

The questionnaire is a modification of the internationally validated questionnaire ISO/TS 15666:2003: Acoustics–Assessment of Noise Annoyance Using Social and Socioacoustic Surveys. Moreover, it is based on standardized questionnaires (health literacy survey european network questionnaire (HELEN), european health interview survey (EHIS), consolidated european survey on accident risks (CESAR), short form-12 health survey (SF-12), and pittsburgh sleep quality index (PSQI-9)) as well as the self-esteem scale (SES) questionnaire from the project Healthy Ageing in an Industrial Environment CZ.02.1.01/0.0/0.0/16_019/0000798. Some questions were slightly modified (Supplement–Questionnaire). The questionnaire consisted of five sections. Section A (general questions) included a set of standardized categorical variables. Family status was assessed using the following response options: single, married, cohabitating, divorced, and widowed. Educational attainment was categorized as basic (including incomplete), vocational without diploma, high school diploma (general or vocational), higher vocational (postsecondary), and university or higher education. Social status and current economic activity were assessed with the following categories: employed; private entrepreneur/self-employed/freelance; at home (including parental leave); permanently caring for a close person; retired (due to age or disability); unemployed; and other (with a free-text field). The perceived economic situation of a household was evaluated by employing the following scale: excellent, very good, satisfactory, poor, and very poor (note: no respondent selected the “very poor” option). Some selected categories were merged for the evaluation of the questionnaire survey. Section B (risk perception: 20 general problems and 14 environmental problems) is the core part of the questionnaire. Question B1 examined how serious the respondent considers general problems to be (evaluating 20 different issues on a 5-point Likert scale), whereas question B2 asked the respondent to assess specific environmental problems that cause nuisance in their place of residence. This section included 14 problems, which covered not only noise but also other sources of concern. In cases of high levels of noise annoyance (3–5 on a 5-point Likert scale), respondents were asked to specify the type of noise. Scores of 4 and 5 were rated as a very serious problem/annoyance. Section C of the questionnaire focused on the respondent’s health status. The presence of chronic diseases was assessed using yes/no questions, with respondents indicating which specific condition(s) they had. Subjective health status and physical condition were assessed separately using a five-point Likert scale. Participants were asked whether they suffered from any chronic disease. They were then asked a question specifying whether they were affected by one or more of six predefined chronic conditions. An open-ended option was also provided, allowing respondents to indicate any other chronic disease not listed among the given options. Section D focused on lifestyle factors, including sleep duration and quality and smoking. Section E centered on life satisfaction/quality of life (quality of social contacts). Section F concentrated on the duration of residence in the monitored area.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, including mean, median, standard deviation, frequency, and percentage. Statistical tests, including the χ2 test, Fisher’s exact test, Mann–Whitney test, and t-test, were used to compare the areas. A binary logistic regression model was employed to evaluate the factors affecting quality of life (where scores of 1 and 2 on the Likert scale were classified as “good quality of life” and scores of 3 to 5 as “poor quality of life”) and the most serious problems. Logistic regression models were applied to calculate crude and adjusted odds ratios (ORs), with adjustments made for sociodemographic factors and chronic diseases. All ORs are presented with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The power of the test with respect to the sample size was evaluated based on the significant annoyance caused by industrial noise.

Reliability was assessed using the kappa index (strength of agreement: 0–0.20 poor, 0.21–0.40 fair, 0.41–0.60 moderate, 0.61–0.80 substantial, and >0.80 almost perfect) and the paired Wilcoxon test. Statistical tests were evaluated at the 5% significance level using Stata, version 17. The graphical and tabular outputs included in this manuscript were processed and formatted using Microsoft Word and Microsoft Excel software tools.

RESULTS

Baseline Characteristics

The sample consisted of 113 participants. The noise-affected area included the villages of Uhelna (n = 16) and Oldrichov na Hranicich (n = 43), whereas the non-noise-affected area included the village of Vaclavice (n = 54). The response rate was 15.0% in the non-noise-affected area Vaclavice, 25.3% in the noise-affected area Oldrichov na Hranicich, and 35.7% in the noise-affected area Uhelna. The overall response rate across the studied region was 19.2%. On average, participants had lived in the area for 21 years. The study population included 54.0% women and 46.0% men. The mean age was 51.1 years, with a range of 17 to 91 years. The demographic data of respondents from the noise-affected and non-noise-affected areas did not differ significantly (P > 0.05) in terms of age distribution, marital status, educational level, and economic activity, including work outside the residence [Table 1].

Perception of General and Environmental Problems

The ranking of the perceived concern for general problems is presented in Figure 2. Residents from both areas most frequently perceived rising energy prices, health concerns, and drug addiction/AIDS issues as the most pressing among general problems. A statistically significant difference between areas was found in the perception of groundwater levels (χ2 = 3.905, P = 0.048), with more residents in the non-noise-affected area (72.2%) perceiving groundwater depletion as a more serious problem than those in the noise-affected areas (54.2%). Similar patterns were found in the case of wind turbines (33.3% vs. 13.6%, z = 6.223, P = 0.013). Although disturbance and sleep disruption due to noise were identified as more serious issues in the noise-affected area than in the non-noise-affected area (45.8% vs. 37.0%), no statistically significant difference was found between the areas (χ2 = 0.884, P = 0.347). The perception of other general problems by respondents did not differ between the noise-affected and non-noise-affected areas (χ2-test, P > 0.05).

Perception of the general problems (ranked in accordance with the noise-affected area. A very serious problem is scored 4 and 5 on a 5-point Likert scale, * χ2-test, P < 0.05).

Among the environmental problems, noise from industrial activities and groundwater depletion were perceived as the most serious by residents [Figure 3]. On the five-point Likert scale, noise from industrial activities was rated as 3.63 (median 4) by residents in the noise-affected area and 3.02 (median = 3) (t = 2.302, P = 0.023, power = 89.4%) by those in the non-noise-affected area. Noise from industrial/commercial activities was perceived as a very significant problem in the noise-affected area (59%) relative to that in the non-noise-affected area (39%) (χ2 = 4.709, P = 0.030). Respondents in the noise-affected area who reported being annoyed by industrial noise (Likert scale 3–5, n = 47) indicated that the most common type of noise was humming (85.1%) and that the noise varied over time (63.8%). A total of 62.7% of the respondents in the noise-affected area reported that the noise situation had worsened over the past 5 years, whereas 37.0% of those in the non-noise-affected area reported the same (χ2 = 8.379, P = 0.015). Construction noise (22.0% vs. 3.7%, χ2 = 8.229, P = 0.004) and dust pollution (52.5% vs. 31.5%, χ2 = 5.118, P = 0.024) were other significant problems in the noise-affected area. Residents in the non-noise-affected area were most concerned about the lack of drinking water (74.1%), with a statistically significant difference observed between the non-noise-affected and noise-affected populations (23.7%) (χ2 = 28.642, P < 0.001). The perception of other environmental problems did not differ between areas (χ2-test, P > 0.05).

Quality of Life and Serious Environmental Problems in the Noise-Affected Area

A statistically significant difference between the noise-affected and non-noise-affected areas was found in terms of quality of life and annoyance caused by industrial noise and dust at the place of residence. Table 2 shows that the probability for a worse quality of life in the noise-affected area was approximately 2.25 times higher than that in the non-noise-affected area. After adjustment for baseline demographic factors (sex, age, education, family status, and economic activity) and health status (presence of chronic diseases), this probability remained 2.07 times higher in the noise-affected area than in the non-noise-affected area. However, this relationship was no longer statistically significant. The probability of being annoyed by industrial noise and dust at the place of residence persisted after adjustment for demographic factors and health status and was significantly higher (by more than twice) in the noise-affected area than in the non-noise-affected area.

Study of Nonrespondents

A total of 22 individuals completed the short questionnaire for nonrespondents. The reasons for refusal to participate in the main survey varied and reflected both personal attitudes and contextual factors. The most frequently reported reason was a general lack of interest in the topic (n = 5). Several respondents stated that they did not perceive any problems in the locality and considered their quality of life to be satisfactory (n = 5). A few individuals mentioned a lack of time to complete the questionnaire (n = 2) or expressed doubts about the usefulness of the research and its potential outcomes (n = 3). Some responses reflected distrust in institutions (n = 1), concerns about anonymity (n = 1), or frustration regarding the unresolved Turow mine issue (n = 1). Other reasons included short-term residence in the locality (n = 2), temporary absence due to vacation (n = 1), or participation in similar surveys in the past (n = 1). One respondent also pointed out that the lack of financial compensation as a demotivating factor.

Reliability Study

In the reliability study, 8 men and 13 women (n = 21) were included, with an average age of 53.6 years (min. 19, max. 79 years). Approximately 67.0% of the participants were from the noise-affected area. The agreement for general questions (A) (sex, education, marital status, and economic status) was substantial to almost perfect (kappa index ranging from 0.7 to 1.0). A real change (agreement of 76.2%) was observed in marital status, where transitions from cohabitating to single status (n =1) and from cohabitating to married status (n = 2) were observed. A real change (agreement 95.2%) was also observed in educational level, where one respondent moved from secondary education without a diploma to secondary education with a diploma (n = 1).

The comparison revealed changes in risk perception, particularly for most perceived risks. Between 2022 and 2023, statistically significant reductions in perception were observed in three areas: health perception (z = 2.081, P = 0.039), economic situation in the country (z = 2.079, P = 0.049), and rising energy prices (z = 2.115, P = 0.046) [Figure 4].

Comparison of the perception of the general problems based on the first and second data collection phases.

Based on the findings from 2022, a question regarding the perception of light pollution was added in 2023. The severity of this issue was rated, on average, 2.4 on a 5-point scale.

Among the 14 environmental factors, the perceived concern of air pollution significantly decreased in 2023 (z = 2.562, P = 0.017). No significant differences were observed for the remaining factors, including the perceived risk of industrial noise. Industrial noise remains one of the most serious environmental concerns perceived by people, even though no statistically significant difference was found for this problem between 2023 and 2024 (z = 1.560, P = 0.158).

DISCUSSION

Our questionnaire survey identified general issues and environmental concerns specifically related to the operation of the Turow mine. Studies addressing this topic typically focus on a single source of noise within the context of the studied location.[17] Our questionnaire survey provides insights into the prioritized assessment of perceived risk severity in the specific cross-border region near the Turow mine. It also facilitates the identification of differences between the noise-affected and non-noise-affected areas, as well as changes over time.

Regarding general risks, participants from both areas identified rising energy prices as the most statistically significant issue. Among the general problems, noise disturbance ranked tenth in terms of perceived severity. However, in the ranking of environmental issues, industrial noise emerged as the most serious problem in both areas. However, no universally accepted definition of high levels of noise annoyance or a standardized method for their measurement exists. Noise annoyance is mainly assessed by using a five-point Likert scale,[26] which was also applied in our survey. Our survey results revealed heightened concerns about the state of groundwater in the non-noise-affected area.

Wind turbines, which are located close to the non-noise-affected area, were considered the least significant issue by respondents from both areas. However, the perceived severity of this problem slightly increased in the second phase of the survey. Studies examining the influence of wind turbines on human health offer mixed conclusions.[27] Dust pollution was also identified as a significant concern in both areas, given that it is a well-documented risk to human health.[28] The second phase of the survey included an assessment of the effect of light pollution originating from safety features and mining machinery at the Turow mine. Until 2016, residents were also disturbed by light from greenhouses used for tomato cultivation, which used waste hot water from a lignite power plant. Although the greenhouse owner installed shading curtains, these measures were not always fully effective.[29] Long-term exposure to artificial light at night has been shown to have negative health effects.[30] While industrial noise was the primary focus of our study, our findings also highlight the importance of considering the combined perception of multiple environmental factors, such as dust pollution and groundwater depletion, that are frequently reported in the affected regions. When residents perceive several environmental threats simultaneously, their overall sense of burden may intensify, leading to strengthened effects on physical and mental health. These factors do not act in isolation; rather, their cumulative perception may amplify the subjective experience of environmental discomfort, ultimately affecting quality of life more significantly than any single factor alone.[31]

Our questionnaire survey included a reliability study, which aimed to verify the reliability and validity of the constructed questionnaire. In 2020, Ning Jiang conducted a study focusing on verifying the reliability of a questionnaire.[32] The results of the reliability study in our research indicate a high level of response reliability, positioning our constructed questionnaire as a valid and effective tool for assessing the risk perception in the studied area. Overall, the high agreement rates for factual questions suggest that our questionnaire is a reliable instrument for assessing perceptions of environmental risks. The ranking, significance, and level of risk perception may have been influenced, particularly in the second phase of the survey, by the COVID-19 pandemic.[33] The pandemic of COVID-19 has led to increased concerns about health in general and the health of family members. In addition to rising energy prices, people are worried about potential future economic problems. These findings are supported by the COVID-19 Health Literacy Survey[34] and the analysis by the International Energy Agency, which examined how the pandemic affected investments in clean energy, energy security, and energy prices.[35] With respect to the reliability of our questionnaire, contextual factors that may have influenced the respondents’ responses—aside from the potential effect of the COVID-19 pandemic on the participants’ emotional well-being and perceived risks—must be considered. These factors include the season during which the data collection occurred and prevailing weather conditions. Weather-related factors are known to affect emotional states and mood. This effect could, in turn, influence not only the decision to participate in the survey but also the way questions, particularly those concerning environmental risk perception, are interpreted and answered.[36] In the case of our study, both phases occurred simultaneously. However, excluding the subjective perception of the situation is impossible.

The primary limitations of our questionnaire survey are its very small sample size (the total population of the noise-affected and non-noise-affected areas is 590 individuals) and low response rate (only 19.2%). We could not increase the number of respondents, given that the affected municipalities have few residents. A relatively small sample size can reduce the statistical power needed to detect subtle associations and, as a result, limit the extent to which the findings can be applied to the wider population. In our study, the test power was calculated based on the main factor of interest, namely, industrial noise annoyance, and the test power was 89%; the minimum required test power is 80%. We adjusted the statistical models for key demographic and health-related factors. However, the small number of participants constrained the possibility of conducting nuanced subgroup analyses or testing interactions with increased reliability. We employed adjusted logistic regression techniques that account for important background variables to mitigate the above limitation. Nevertheless, the absence of advanced corrective strategies, such as data weighting based on population characteristics, means that the overall representativeness of our sample remains limited. Despite these methodological constraints, the patterns observed in our data align with the findings of previous research on environmental noise and perceived health risks, lending support to the validity of our conclusions within the context of the studied population.[19]

The chosen method of questionnaire distribution involved postal delivery, which was replaced by direct personal contact because of the low response rate. Similarly, low response rates were reported in a 2019 study by Smith and Witte.[37] By contrast, the 2021 study by Miller et al. found a significantly higher proportion of annoyed individuals when printed questionnaires were used than when other methods (e.g., telephone interviews) were employed.[38] In general, residents were unwilling to participate in the survey despite financial incentives, which were even more than doubled in the second phase of data collection. Additionally, participants from the first phase received compensation adjustments for any differences. Financial incentives for study participation may introduce bias because they are likely to attract individuals from low socioeconomic backgrounds.[39] Residents were at least encouraged to provide information about why they declined to participate in the study. Affected residents reported feeling persistently affected by the issues related to the Turow mine, whether due to media coverage,[40] activities of professional organizations and research institutes,[25] government authorities,[4142] or even political actions.[43] They expressed frustration over these issues, given that no environmental improvements have been observed despite several years of discussion. In the second phase of the survey, residents pointed out the lack of financial compensation. Conversely, some residents chose not to participate because they did not perceive their place of residence as problematic. Another limitation may be the extended time between survey phases. Such a lengthy interval is common for studies assessing the long-term effect of noise sources. A longitudinal study from Montreal, the Canada, investigated noise annoyance related to the construction of major highway infrastructure over 2 years. The surveys were conducted at different stages of construction, allowing the researchers to track changes in noise perception over time.[44] A long interval between survey phases was also implemented in a longitudinal cohort study from the Netherlands, where data were collected in two 10-year intervals.[45] Although a detailed socioeconomic or health-related profile of nonrespondents was unavailable, our qualitative follow-up revealed various reasons for refusal to participate. These reasons ranged from a general lack of interest or time constraints to distrust in institutions or beliefs that the issue is no longer relevant. Some respondents cited good quality of life or short-term residence in the area as reasons for nonparticipation. This situation may also indicate a low level of concern about environmental risks. These findings suggest a potential selection bias because those who declined to participate might systematically differ in their risk perception or level of engagement from those who completed the survey.

Notably, despite the noise measurements conducted in the vicinity of the Turow mine on Czech territory indicating that noise levels do not exceed the legal limits set by Czech regulations,[40] industrial noise is still perceived as a significant issue by the exposed residents. When assessing the perceived severity of environmental problems, industrial noise even ranked higher than other noise sources in the studied area (e.g., wind turbines or construction noise). In light of our study’s findings, several practical recommendations can be made to help mitigate the influence of industrial noise on public health. Local and regional authorities should establish systematic and frequent environmental noise monitoring in industrial zones, particularly in communities where long-term annoyance and sleep disturbance have been reported, with special emphasis on nighttime exposure. In parallel, policies should consider introducing compensation measures or mitigation support (e.g., housing insulation programs) for residents living in persistently affected areas. From a research perspective, future work should investigate the potential synergistic effects of noise in combination with other environmental stressors, such as dust or air pollutants, that are commonly present in industrial environments.

CONCLUSION

Our questionnaire study demonstrates that noise is perceived as a statistically significant issue by residents in the noise-affected area surrounding an open-pit mine. By contrast, residents in the non-noise-affected area, which lacks a water supply, perceived groundwater depletion as a prominent problem.

Despite the small number of exposed residents and low response rate, the findings of our work suggest that even legally acceptable noise levels can significantly affect the perceived quality of life of residents. A broad sociopolitical context should be considered to improve the interpretation of our results. The perception of the noise concern and annoyance of the population might be affected by other general and environmental problems. Understanding of this issue and contextualization will be enabled by a newly developed questionnaire.
