Opioid-Free Pain Management Strategies for Patients on Oral Anticoagulation Undergoing Soft Tissue Hand Surgery
Authors: Ellis M Berns, Thompson Zhuang, Hannah H Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNon-narcotic multimodal analgesia has become standard for patients undergoing soft tissue hand surgery. These regimens often include nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs such as ketorolac and ibuprofen, which can elevate bleeding risks in patients on anticoagulation therapy. This review aims to (1) elucidate the physiology of coagulation in relation to nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and anticoagulants, (2) examine the current surgical literature on risks and benefits of limited-duration nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug use in anticoagulated patients, (3) identify viable pain management alternatives, and (4) highlight areas warranting further research in nonopioid analgesia for anticoagulated patients.Keywords: Anticoagulation, bleeding, NSAID, opioid

Postoperative pain control is critical following soft tissue hand surgery; however, opioid use is increasingly discouraged because of its associated risks of dependence and adverse effects.1 Nonopioid multimodal analgesia regimens, which synergistically combine medications with distinct mechanisms, can provide comparable or superior pain relief to opioids and are widely endorsed.1-4 Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) constitute a cornerstone of multimodal regimens, but their use in patients on anticoagulants is complicated by an elevated bleeding risk with coprescription.5 The number of patients on anticoagulants is increasing, with direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) being the most frequently prescribed class, accounting for 70% of oral anticoagulants.6 This review examines NSAID—anticoagulant interactions, explores alternative nonopioid analgesic options for postoperative pain management after soft tissue hand surgery, and proposes future directions for research.

ROLE OF NSAIDS IN POSTOPERATIVE PAIN MANAGEMENT

Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs provide robust analgesic and anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting cyclooxygenase 1 and 2 (COX-1 and COX-2, respectively) enzymes, reducing prostaglandin production and thus inflammation-driven pain.7 Meta-analyses consistently validate NSAIDs’ effectiveness in postoperative pain control across surgical fields.7,8 In soft tissue hand surgery, NSAID-based multimodal regimens, including ibuprofen, ketorolac, meloxicam, celecoxib, and naproxen, achieve pain relief comparable, or even superior to opioids.1,3,4 These findings support the use of NSAIDs as a key element in postoperative analgesic protocols.

HOW NSAIDS INTERACT WITH THE COAGULATION CASCADE

Effect of NSAIDs on platelet aggregation

By inhibiting COX-1, NSAIDs reduce thromboxane A2 (TXA2) production, a critical promoter of platelet aggregation and vasoconstriction. This reduction impairs clot formation and can thus increase bleeding risk.9 Additionally, decreased prostaglandin synthesis compromises the gastrointestinal (GI) tract mucosal lining, increasing vulnerability to peptic ulcer disease and GI bleeding.10 Cyclooxygenase 2 inhibitors (eg, celecoxib) selectively inhibit COX-2 while sparing COX-1, theoretically reducing bleeding risk and GI side effects, as they do not considerably inhibit platelet aggregation.11 A meta-analysis of 35 randomized controlled trials found that short-course perioperative COX-2 selective inhibitors without combined anticoagulant therapy did not notably increase postoperative bleeding or blood loss, indicating their safety in postsurgical pain regimens.11 However, COX-2 inhibitors can increase thrombotic cardiovascular events because of a paradoxical increase in prothrombotic enzymes, necessitating cautious use.12 The effects of NSAIDs on hemostasis, GI protection, and pain pathways is visualized in Figure 1.

The synergistic effects of NSAIDs with anticoagulation

Anticoagulants are indicated for medical conditions such as atrial fibrillation, venous thromboembolism, and coronary artery disease prophylaxis and carry an inherent increased risk of bleeding. Anticoagulants such as DOACs (eg, apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran), vitamin K inhibitors such as warfarin, and antiplatelet agents such as clopidogrel reduce clot formation by interfering with various coagulation factors or platelet activation pathways.13-15 Anticoagulants are often continued perioperatively during soft tissue hand surgery to mitigate the thrombotic risk of pausing therapy, without the risk of increasing surgical complications.16 The effects of DOACs on the coagulation cascade are represented in Figure 2.

When anticoagulants are co-prescribed with NSAIDs, the synergistic effect on bleeding can be substantial, with some studies suggesting an increased risk of clinically significant bleeding of up to 12-fold.5,10,17 A further complicating factor in this interaction is the widespread use and self-administration of low-dose aspirin—a nonselective NSAID—for colorectal cancer prevention and secondary cardiovascular prophylaxis. When aspirin— which a surgeon may be unaware a patient is self-administering—is combined with other NSAIDs or anticoagulants, the risk of bleeding is further amplified.17

SAFETY OF CONCOMITANT, LIMITED-DURATION NSAID AND ANTICOAGULANT USE

Given NSAIDs’ established efficacy in postoperative pain management, understanding the magnitude of bleeding risk when they are used for controlled, short-term treatment and at the lowest effective dose alongside anticoagulants becomes paramount. The historical view that NSAIDs uniformly increase bleeding complications in surgery has been called into question when these medications are prescribed in the lowest effective dose for only a few days.7 There is a paucity of evidence on whether their synergistic risk of bleeding with anticoagulants is clinically significant in the postoperative period.

A study by Schjerning Olsen et al5 reported the following crude bleeding event rates in patients on any anticoagulant with or without NSAID use: 7.3 per 100 person-years for 0—3 days of NSAID use, 6.0 per 100 person-years for 4—7 days, and 2.2 per 100 person-years for those with no NSAID use. These data imply a number needed to harm (NNH) of 2,400 for 0—3 days and 1,370 for 4—7 days of NSAID use. Though this calculation is imperfect because of linear scaling and the distinction between crude and rates adjusted for confounders, it suggests that brief NSAID use with anticoagulants might not confer a significantly increased bleeding risk at the population level.

However, post hoc analyses of randomized trials for patients on DOACs with NSAID coprescription show more concerning NNH values. For example, in one study with patients on dabigatran, the reported incidence of major bleeding events increased from 3.1% in patients not using NSAIDs to 5.4% in those who did, yielding an NNH of 43.18 In a study on apixaban use, the rate of major bleeding rose from 2.1% to 3.4% with incident NSAID use, producing an NNH of 78.19 In patients taking rivaroxaban, NSAID use increased the major bleeding rate from 1.4% to 4.7%, yielding an NNH of 30.20 These studies, however, calculated risk on an annual per-patient basis, and this may not accurately reflect risk from short-term exposure (eg, only a few days of NSAID use). Annual per-patient risk is an average based on longer stretches of exposure to NSAIDs and may not scale linearly for brief NSAID exposure, making it challenging to estimate the real risk for a short postoperative course of NSAID use. Furthermore, selective COX-2 inhibitors were not included in analysis for the studies on dabigatran or rivaroxaban and made up only 0.2% of the NSAIDs used in the study on apixaban.

In contrast, other studies have reported no significant increase in bleeding risk with combined DOAC and NSAID use. For instance, Friedman et al21 found that patients taking dabigatran with concurrent NSAID or aspirin therapy had similar bleeding rates compared to those on dabigatran alone. Likewise, Worsham et al22 observed no increased bleeding risk in patients using NSAIDs alongside rivaroxaban or apixaban; notably, they developed a “DOAC Dashboard” to support pharmacists in implementing risk-reduction measures, such as selecting a COX-2 inhibitor or adding a proton pump inhibitor. Considering that persistent opioid use after surgery can be as high as 6%, short-duration NSAID therapy might still be preferable to opioids.23 This comparison may suggest that a short NSAID course, such as a COX-2 selective NSAID plus a proton pump inhibitor for GI mucosal protection, could still be preferable to an opioid in anticoagulated patients. Regardless, risk mitigation remains essential. First, patients should receive the lowest effective dose of NSAIDs for the shortest duration needed. Second, COX-2-selective NSAIDs are generally preferred because of their decreased bleeding risk compared to nonselective NSAIDs, and third, cotherapy with a proton pump inhibitor is advised for GI protection.

ALTERNATIVES TO NSAIDS FOR PATIENTS ON ANTICOAGULATION

Acetaminophen

Acetaminophen provides analgesic effects through complex mechanisms distinct from opioids or NSAIDs. Given its efficacy in reducing postoperative pain and its relatively benign side-effect profile, acetaminophen is often prescribed as a scheduled medication to serve as the foundation of an opioid-free multimodal regimen. Proposed protocols suggest that for patients unable to take NSAIDs, acetaminophen alone, dosed at 975 mg every 6 hours, could be used following soft tissue hand surgery to provide adequate pain control.4

Although higher daily doses (>2 g) may interfere with warfarin metabolism and necessitate closer International Normalized Ratio (INR) monitoring, there is no evidence that acetaminophen elevates bleeding risk in patients taking DOACs.24,25 Consequently, acetaminophen is a pivotal component of nonopioid multimodal pain management for patients on anticoagulation given its efficacy and relative safety, with mild caution advised for those on warfarin.

Gabapentinoids

Gabapentinoids (gabapentin and pregabalin) modulate voltage-gated calcium channels, reducing excitatory neurotransmitter release and dampening neuropathic pain signals. As nonopioid analgesics, they have been popularized in multimodal pain regimens and have shown to be effective in decreasing postoperative opioid use.26,27 Still, some studies question their overall efficacy in reducing post-surgical pain scores.28

Given that gabapentinoids are primarily excreted unchanged by the kidneys, they do not significantly interact with anticoagulant metabolism, nor do they affect the coagulation cascade. However, they must be dose-adjusted in patients with renal impairment. Their side effects include central nervous system alteration including dizziness, somnolence, and headache. Elderly patients are particularly susceptible to these adverse effects.28 In patients on anticoagulation, the benefit of pain control must be weighed against the risk of central nervous system effects and possible resulting falls and trauma.

Regional anesthesia

Regional anesthesia, including peripheral nerve blocks and local anesthetic infiltrations, can significantly reduce postoperative opioid requirements and pain scores during the first 24—72 hours after surgery.29 Liposomal bupivacaine, a long-acting anesthetic formulation, has shown promise in hand surgery by decreasing postoperative opioid consumption and effectively managing pain.30

However, the use of nerve blocks in anticoagulated patients requires caution because of the potential for hematoma formation, which may compromise neurovascular structures or significantly decrease hematocrit. Current regional anesthesia subspecialty guidelines acknowledge the paucity of high-quality data on the frequency or severity of hemorrhagic complications associated with peripheral nerve blocks in anticoagulated patients. Clinical judgment should therefore weigh the benefits of regional blocks against the local vascularity, compressibility, and potential ramifications of a bleeding complication.31 In some cases, local infiltration of long-acting anesthetics at the surgical site, rather than a proximal nerve block, may be a safer option.

Nonpharmacologic interventions

Although standard postoperative instructions are routinely given to patients, those on anticoagulation benefit from specific guidance on nonpharmacologic strategies. Strict elevation and the intermittent application of ice through a soft dressing can reduce swelling and resultant pain. Additionally, research indicates that preoperative patient education about postoperative pain expectations can significantly lower overall opioid consumption after minor procedures like carpal tunnel surgery.32 Such education can address the frequency, dosage, and possible side effects of medications. Providing psychological support and setting realistic expectations regarding postoperative pain can improve patient satisfaction and reduce reliance on pharmacologic treatments.

OPPORTUNITIES FOR FURTHER RESEARCH

Some surgeons consider tramadol a “weaker” narcotic, assuming it to be safer than conventional opioids such as oxycodone, hydrocodone, or morphine, and prescribing data indicate its continued use.33,34 Tramadol is classified by the U.S. Drug Enforcement Administration as a Schedule IV controlled substance, indicating a relatively lower potential for abuse compared to Schedule II opioids. Nevertheless, it retains opioid-like properties and carries risks of dependence, adverse effects, and misuse, and requires judicious prescribing and monitoring to minimize harm similar to other opioid medications.35 Additional research is needed to confirm whether tramadol is indeed safer or more effective for anticoagulated patients compared to short-duration NSAIDs or other options.

Furthermore, although the scope of this review is limited to soft tissue procedures, the safety and effectiveness of opioid-free multimodal regimens in patients on oral anticoagulation should be studied for bony procedures as well. Patients undergoing bony procedures often have more postoperative pain and the addition of these recommendations could reduce opioid prescribing tendencies after these procedures.

Other outstanding questions include whether a brief, lowest effective dose of COX-2 selective NSAID’s paired with a proton pump inhibitor and scheduled acetaminophen provides sufficient pain control without substantially increasing bleeding risk. What are the frequency and severity of hemorrhagic complications of nerve blocks as well as the efficacy of long-acting local anesthetic infiltration, and which is superior in anticoagulated patients? Does the risk of bleeding with NSAID coprescription change with DOACs dosing at prophylactic or therapeutic levels? Are hand surgeons aware of the widespread use of low-dose aspirin, and counseling patients on risks of combination with anticoagulation and NSAIDs? Finally, future studies should explore how frequently surgeons counsel anticoagulated patients on the risks of polypharmacy, potential bleeding complications, and the pros and cons of using opioids versus nonopioid pain management. In anticoagulated patients undergoing soft tissue hand surgery, postoperative pain management requires a nuanced balance between minimizing bleeding risk, risk of opioid prescriptions, and ensuring effective analgesia. Although NSAIDs are potent analgesics, they can amplify bleeding risks when combined with anticoagulants, underscoring the need for brief treatment durations, choosing COX-2 selective NSAIDs, and GI protection measures. Acetaminophen, gabapentinoids, and regional anesthetic techniques also serve as valuable components of a multimodal opioid-free strategy. Nonpharmacologic interventions such as intensive and directed patient education further enhance pain control while minimizing pharmacologic risks. Ultimately, more research is required to compare different multimodal regimens, including COX-2 selective NSAIDs and emerging local anesthetic formulations, to refine best practices and optimize pain management for the growing anticoagulated patient population.
