Adsorption performances of boron and oxygen co-doping of carbon materials as sensors for ciprofloxacin: a DFT investigation
Authors: Onyebuenyi I. Brown, Yussuf Ayinla Fasasi, Ernest Godswill, Moses M. Edim, Musa Runde, Francisca Bassey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Due to the rising pollution levels across various sectors, discovering innovative methods to mitigate different types of pollution has become essential for enhancing crop growth and extending the lifespan of humans and animals. Using certain adsorbent, such as biochar (BCH), is beneficial in numerous studies, which led to its selection in this work. In this study, density functional theory (DFT) calculations at the ωB97XD/6-311+G(d) level of theory were used to model the studied systems, which have been chemically modified with boron and oxygen (B, BC2O, BCO2, and B–O) on the biochar complex to enhance ciprofloxacin (CPF) adsorption capacity and selectivity. A noticeable difference was observed in the properties of B/O-functionalized biochar compared to BCH. When examining the adsorption characteristics, the energies ranged from −30.9 to −34.8 kcal mol−1, with CPF-BC2O@BCH showing the highest potential for detection due to its lowest adsorption energy. This highlights the positive impact of functionalizing BCH for CPF adsorption which is a major pollutant when introduced into water bodies. Although CPF-BCH still demonstrated strong adsorption capability, various computational studies, including NCI, QTAIM, electronic properties, and sensor mechanisms, were conducted to validate the findings and provide insights into how BCH's adsorption behaviour changes upon modification.

Introduction

One notable emerging increase of antimicrobial-resistant bacteria has been recently linked to the extensive utilization of ciprofloxacin (CIP) in the treatment of various diseases. Several factors have contributed to the detection of rising concentrations of ciprofloxacin in the ecosystem, including the partial metabolism of ciprofloxacin in the human body and its slow degradation upon contact with the environment.1,2 According to the World Health Organization (WHO) model list of essential medicines, ciprofloxacin has been ranked as one of the frequently used antibiotics.3,4 It is considered a fluoroquinolone antibiotic which poses a risk factor to animal and human health when detected in high concentrations in the ecosystem. The diverse effect of pharmaceuticals, hospitals and treatment waste on aquatic life has sparked the need for removal and improved treatment methods of these contaminants. In recent research, the selective adsorption method is currently explored, alongside other low-cost methods such as degradation, advanced oxidation, and photodegradation etc.5–7 In water, CIP is commonly detected in levels of about 31 mg L−1, which is considered toxic for cyanobacteria and macrophytes.8

Various materials are conceptualized and engineered for the efficient adsorption of ciprofloxacin from the environment and similarly related wastes. These selected materials are usually porous with high surface area, such as biochar, graphene, carbon nanotubes, etc.9,10 Chemical processes have been utilized in various research targeting CIP adsorption, but some limitations have been observed, including its expensive application, and more environmental problems are speculated to arise.11,12 Biochar, a multifunctional adsorbent material consisting of aromatic hydrocarbons, is highly promising in the adsorption of various wastes from diverse sources.13

Due to its diverse surface functionalities, biochar can be easily modified and enhanced for increased efficiency of the material's performance.14 Also, its large surface areas and high porosity are unique properties that position biochar as a great adsorbent material. Its surface can be modified employing a range of methods such as doping, chemical and physical modifications. Some studies have documented an increase in the adsorption performance of biochar upon activation or modification.15–17 In a study by Lee et al., the activated biochar from the chemical manufacturing industry sludge possessed a great adsorption capacity of CIP due to the π–π interactions and hydrogen bond formation.18 Also, Penafiel et al. posited in their article investigating the adsorption of CIP that sugarcane bagasse was a low-cost, efficient adsorbent with high capacity for the adsorption of CIP.19 Furthermore, Al Masud and Samaraweera explored the N/S functionalization of polymer-seaweed biochar composite for the degradation and adsorption of the antibiotic, sulfamethoxazole (SMX) and concluded that the materials are sustainable, reusable, stable and portrayed an efficiency of 97.24% in the adsorption of SMX.20 In another study by Hu et al., the biochar surface was interacted with perrhenate and other oxygen-containing groups. They concluded that the oxygen-containing groups possessed better chemisorption and greater affinity towards ReO4−. A better adsorption stability towards ReO4− was showcased by pyrone.21

In this study, the density functional theory was utilized in investigating the adsorption of ciprofloxacin on B/O functionalized biochar surfaces. Biochar was selected for its unique properties, such as porosity and high modification of its surface. Herein, the biochar pure surface was conceptualized employing computational tools and further modified with B, BC2O, BCO2, and B–O for better performance in the adsorption of ciprofloxacin molecule. Various analysis was incorporated, such as natural bond orbital analysis, electronic property of the understudied surfaces and complexes, quantum theory atoms-in-molecules, adsorption energy, etc., for an enhanced and efficient investigation of ciprofloxacin adsorption using the respective complexes. Overall, this study introduces an effective method and materials in the adsorption of ciprofloxacin from various sources, thereby contributing to the reduction in the rate of antimicrobial-resistant bacteria.

Computational approach

In this study, all quantum chemical calculations were performed using the Gaussian 16 software package,22 and molecular structures were visualized through GaussView 6.0.16.23 The density functional theory (DFT) framework was employed, applying the long-range corrected hybrid functional ωB97X-D, which can describe electrons at both long and short distances, with the 6-311+G(d) basis set. This level of theory, which includes empirical dispersion corrections, has demonstrated high reliability in studying interaction mechanisms, particularly in adsorption processes involving complex surfaces and organic molecules. The biochar surfaces used as adsorbents were modeled based on established structures from literature sources and further functionalized with boron and oxygen to evaluate their selective interaction with ciprofloxacin. Geometry optimization of all studied structures was carried out without any symmetry restrictions. The adsorption behavior between ciprofloxacin and the pristine or functionalized biochar surfaces was quantitatively assessed using the adsorption energy, calculated as:1Ead = Ecomplex − Esurface + ECIPwhere Ead represents the adsorption energy, Ecomplex is the total energy of the adsorbate–adsorbent complex, Esurface is the energy of the isolated biochar surface, and Egas is the energy of the free ciprofloxacin molecule. A negative adsorption energy indicates spontaneous interaction and effective adsorption. To examine the electronic characteristics of the adsorbent and the adsorbed systems, density of states (DOS) analysis was carried out using the Gaussian09W package and Multiwfn 3.7.6.24 The frontier molecular orbitals (HOMO and LUMO) were visualized using Chemcraft v1.8 (ref. 25) to understand electronic transitions, reactivity, and charge mobility upon adsorption. Natural bond orbital (NBO) analysis was further used to evaluate electron delocalization, charge transfer interactions, and stabilization energies due to donor–acceptor interactions. Quantum chemical descriptors, including energy gap, electronegativity, and chemical potential, were derived from the HOMO and LUMO energies to evaluate the chemical reactivity and stability of the systems under investigation. Topological analyses were performed to explore interatomic interactions and bonding characteristics. The Quantum Theory of Atoms in Molecules (QTAIM) analysis was conducted using the Multiwfn 3.7.6 program, allowing the identification of bond critical points and the classification of interaction types. Additionally, non-covalent interaction (NCI) analysis was conducted using the reduced density gradient (RDG) approach, and the resulting RDG plots were visualized with the VMD software to examine weak interactions such as hydrogen bonding, van der Waals forces, and π–π stacking. To understand the potential of the functionalized biochar as a sensing material for ciprofloxacin, the sensor mechanism was theoretically examined. This involved observing the changes in electronic properties, particularly the HOMO–LUMO energy gap, before and after adsorption. Significant modulation in the energy gap or redistribution of electron density was interpreted as an indication of the system's sensitivity to the adsorbed molecule. This theoretical sensor behavior forms the basis for understanding how biochar-based materials can be developed for environmental sensing applications, especially for detecting pharmaceutical contaminants like ciprofloxacin in water systems.26 This comprehensive computational strategy provides a detailed understanding of the adsorption characteristics, electronic structure, and sensing behavior of B/O-functionalized biochar surfaces, paving the way for their practical applications in environmental remediation and sensor technologies.27–29

Results and discussion

Geometry optimization

Geometric optimization was performed to determine the most stable structural configurations of the interacting systems and to evaluate the changes in bond lengths resulting from molecular interactions with doped surfaces. Optimizations were conducted for both isolated and complex systems to ensure energetic stability and to understand the geometric sensitivity of each surface upon ciprofloxacin adsorption. Additionally, the optimized geometries of the functionalized pristine biochar are shown in Fig. 1, while the values are shown in Table S1 of the SI. The B35–O36, B35–C3, C14–C15, and C11–H20 of the B-O@BCH structure maintained a more planar geometry initially, with B35–O36 and B35–C3 bond lengths near 1.41 Å and 1.43 Å, respectively. However, following optimization, these bonds increased modestly to around 1.50 Å and 1.46 Å respectively, indicating structural relaxation upon interaction while maintaining overall geometric stability. Likewise, the C14–C15 and C11–H20 bonds maintained the same bond lengths of 1.37 Å and 1.10 Å, respectively, before and after optimization. When boron was introduced into the BCH surface, as in CPF-B@BCH, no pronounced structural change in bond lengths was observed. The C14–C15 and C23–C24 bond lengths increased slightly from their typical values (1.360 Å and 1.416 Å) to approximately 1.361 Å and 1.417 Å respectively after CPF adsorption. This suggests a minimal structural perturbation and limited charge redistribution between the boron site and the functional groups on CPF. For the BC2O@BCH system, the incorporation of oxygen alongside carbon introduced subtle distortions in the local geometry around the dopant sites. Typically, B35–C5, B35–O36, C14–C15, and C11–H20 bond lengths before interaction were estimated to be 1.540 Å, 1.369 Å, 1.369 Å, and 1.086 Å, respectively. Upon optimization, B35–O36 increased to 1.590 Å, while B35–C5, C14–C15, and C11–H20 decreased slightly to 1.281 Å, 1.350 Å, and 1.086 Å, respectively. These variations reflect local electronic redistribution induced by oxygen doping and structural relaxation of the surface. For the BCO2@BCH system, the B34–O35, B34–C2, C21–C22, and C13–H22 bond lengths before interaction were approximately 1.43 Å, 1.41 Å, 1.43 Å, and 1.10 Å, respectively. After optimization, B34–O35 and B34–C2 increased slightly to 1.44 Å and 1.45 Å, while C21–C22 and C13–H22 decreased to 1.09 Å and 1.08 Å, respectively. In the case of CPF-BCO2@BCH, which also features multiple dopants, there was a slight geometric response upon CPF binding. The presence of additional oxygen atoms facilitated stronger local interaction with CPF, leading to a marginal increase in selected bond lengths such as C11–C12 from 1.407 Å to approximately 1.408 Å. Lastly, the CPF-B-O@BCH system displayed intermediate behavior. The C14–C15 bond, initially stable near 1.366 Å, experienced a minimal increase to around 1.368 Å following CPF attachment. Although slight distortions were observed in the local structure, the overall framework retained its stability.

Across all systems, it was observed that the incorporation of heteroatoms (B and O) and their arrangement influenced the local geometric response of the biochar surface upon CPF adsorption. Oxygen-containing doped systems generally exhibited relatively larger variations in selected bond lengths compared to less oxidized systems, indicating greater local structural and electronic perturbation upon adsorption. In contrast, systems with fewer structural changes maintained a more rigid framework after interaction, reflecting structural stability under adsorption conditions. Electronic properties

Natural bond orbital (NBO) analysis

NBO analysis, also known as the second-order perturbation energy analysis, was employed in the study to serve as a benchmark in understanding how electrons are distributed within a system.30 This analysis aids in understanding the movement of electrons from the donor to the acceptor orbital across various states, which sheds light on the distinct chemical interactions present within molecular systems.31 In Table 2, the NBO parameters are represented. Amongst the interactions in this study, CPF-BCO2@BCH possessed the highest perturbation energy, measuring at 8558.91 kcal mol−1, with its surface counterparts, BCO2@BCH at 71 405.34 kcal mol−1. This showcases the differences in energy values under varied conditions upon interaction. Comparing the complexes, BCH and CPF-BCH, each exhibited energies of 87 530.93 kcal mol−1 and 36 618.31 kcal mol−1, respectively, from a donor to an acceptor point of (σ C2–C3 and σ* C33–C35) and (π* C7–C25 and σ* C29–H32) orbital interactions. This showcases a trend in the energy demand during electron transfer and the orbital changes that occur within the molecular systems, which in a sense characterise the system. The σ–σ* and π*–σ* showcase unique energy movements, indicating strong chemical covalent bonds as compared to the π–π* bond, which would suggest the presence of a weaker covalent bond. Also, Table 2 elucidates the presence of Rydberg bonds across a few systems. This adds to the system's complexity. Amongst the systems, B-O@BCH holds the highest perturbation energy of 94 935.71 kcal mol−1 and B@BCH the lowest of 10 027.65 kcal mol; both of which are surfaces that highlight the changes that occurred upon adsorption with CPF as compared to the surfaces.

Quantum descriptors

To understand the stability and reactivity of the studied molecule, ciprofloxacin (CPF), it is essential to evaluate the electronic properties embedded in the structures and their respective complexes.32,33 These properties include the energy gap, ionization energy, electron affinity, chemical hardness, softness, and related variables. The electronic states of the systems are described by the HOMO (Highest Occupied Molecular Orbital) and LUMO (Lowest Unoccupied Molecular Orbital), which represent electron excitation from the ground state to the excited state.34,35 In the existing literature, HOMO–LUMO energy gap values are widely used to assess relative stability and reactivity trends, and to provide qualitative insight into adsorption-related electronic behavior. However, it should be noted that these values are derived from finite cluster molecular orbital calculations and do not represent true band gaps in periodic systems. Instead, they provide comparative information on electronic excitation energies and reactivity differences among the studied systems. The higher the Eg values, the less reactive the compound and the more stable it is, and vice versa. In Table 3, the electronic descriptors are presented. The Eg values range from 4.70 eV to 7.320 eV, with the B-O@BCH surface exhibiting the lowest energy gap and the pristine BCH system showing the highest value. It is observed that the energy gap values decrease progressively upon modification of BCH with respective dopants. Among the CPF–biochar complexes, CPF-B-O@BCH exhibited the lowest energy gap value, indicating higher reactivity and greater electronic response toward ciprofloxacin adsorption, while CPF-BCH exhibited the highest energy gap, suggesting relatively lower reactivity and higher stability. The other complexes show Eg values of 7.135 eV, 7.088 eV, 5.119 eV, 4.929 eV, and 4.740 eV for CPF-BCH, CPF-B@BCH, CPF-BC2O@BCH, CPF-BCO2@BCH, and CPF-B-O@BCH, respectively. In terms of electronic conductivity, it generally decreases with increasing energy gap, as shown in Table 3. Previous theoretical studies have shown that changes in HOMO–LUMO energy gap upon analyte adsorption can provide insight into sensing behavior. In general, a reduction in Eg after adsorption is associated with increased electronic conductivity, enhanced charge transfer, and improved sensitivity of the sensing surface toward the analyte. Therefore, the observed variations in Eg values upon CPF adsorption on the functionalized biochar systems suggest changes in electronic interaction strength and sensing responsiveness rather than absolute conductivity values. Visual studies

QTAIM

To understand the nature of bonding and the interaction mechanisms between ciprofloxacin (CPF) and the doped BCH-based surfaces, the Quantum Theory of Atoms in Molecules (QTAIM), as formulated by Bader, was employed.36 This method enables a detailed topological analysis of the electron density and to gain an understanding into the type and strength of intermolecular interactions through the bond critical points (BCPs), located between interacting atomic pairs.37 The derived parameters are summarized in Table 5. The interaction type was interpreted using the Laplacian of electron density (∇2ρ(r)) and the total energy density (H(r)). According to established QTAIM criteria, covalent interactions are typically characterized by ∇2ρ(r) < 0 and H(r) < 0, partial covalent interactions by ∇2ρ(r) > 0 and H(r) < 0, and noncovalent interactions by ∇2ρ(r) > 0 and H(r) > 0.38 The topological analysis revealed that most interactions between CPF and the doped BCH surfaces were noncovalent, as evidenced by the positive values of both ∇2ρ(r) and H(r). However, exceptions were observed in the CPF-BC2O@BCH system, where noncovalent interaction was identified at N73–C31, H52-036 bonds at specific critical point of 164 and 184, due to ∇2ρ(r) > 0 and H(r) > 0. The electron density at the BCP (ρ(r)) also offers insight into the interaction strength. Typically, ρ(r) values above 0.1 a.u. denote strong covalent bonding, while values below 0.1 a.u. suggest weaker, noncovalent interactions.39 From the result of Table 5, ρ(r) ranged from 0.206 to 0.797 a.u. for most interactions, confirming the dominance of non-covalent forces. Notably, the CPF-BCO2@BCH system showed slightly elevated ρ(r) value of 0.850, consistent with its enhanced interaction stability.

Additionally, the Electron Localization Function (ELF) was evaluated to explore the spatial distribution of electrons. High ELF values (0.5–1.0) indicate electron localization, while low ELF values (0–0.49) indicate delocalization.40 The ELF analysis revealed substantial electron localization in systems like the CPF-BCH, CPF-B@BCH, CPF-B-O@BCH and CPF-BC2O@BCH at the CP of 183 and 149, 176 and 146, 127 then 164, 184 and 85 respectively. To further characterize the interactions, the ellipticity (ε) at the BCPs was calculated. Ellipticity reflects the anisotropy of the electron density and thus the stability of the bond; low ε values.

Indicates stable bonding environments, while high values suggest bond instability or π-interaction character.40,41 The lowest ellipticity (ε = 0.018) was found in CPF-BC2O@BCH, indicating the highest bond stability among the systems, whereas the highest ellipticity (ε = 5.711) was recorded in CPF-B-O@BCH, suggesting a more flexible or less stabilized interaction network. Hence, the QTAIM results corroborate the noncovalent nature of interactions between ciprofloxacin and the functionalized BCH, with CPF-BC2O@BCH showing relatively stronger and more stable interactions due to it non-covalent character, higher electron density, and favorable ELF and ellipticity profile.

NCI

To further expand on the nature and strength of interactions between ciprofloxacin (CPF) and the biochar-based adsorbents, Non-Covalent Interaction (NCI) analysis was performed and visualized as NCI isosurfaces. The NCI plots provide a qualitative representation of weak interactions such as van der Waals forces, hydrogen bonding, and steric repulsion, using a color scale: blue for strong attractive interactions (e.g., hydrogen bonds), green for weak dispersion interactions, and red for strong repulsion (steric clashes).42,43

From the result of the NCI as shown in Fig. 3, the CPF-B@BCH system revealed a predominance of green isosurfaces, indicating that the adsorption of CPF onto unmodified biochar was dominated by weak van der Waals interactions. The absence of significant blue or red regions confirms that the interaction is largely physisorptive with minimal hydrogen bonding. This suggests a non-specific adsorption pathway relying on π–π stacking and dispersion forces. In contrast, the system CPF-BC2O@BCH displayed a more complex interaction. Notably, blue isosurfaces appeared in regions between the CPF molecule and the oxygen-containing functional groups, signifying the presence of moderate hydrogen bonding or electrostatic interactions. The CPF-B-O@BCH systems main interactions are mostly van der walls and steric because of the green, red regions and a few H-bounds, this suggest that its moderate or low in times of it stability. The strongest interaction profile was observed in the carboxylated biochar system (CPF-BCO2@BCH), which featured extensive blue regions along the interface between CPF and the carboxyl-functionalized surface. These intense blue isosurfaces are indicative of strong hydrogen bonding, arising from interactions between the –COOH groups on the adsorbent and the polar functional moieties of CPF. The green dispersion areas remained visible, but to a lesser extent, while the absence of significant red regions suggests a favorable alignment of the adsorbate on the functionalized surface, with negligible steric hindrance.

Collectively, these observations confirm the QTAIM analysis that surface functionalization significantly enhances CPF adsorption by introducing polar sites that facilitate stronger non-covalent interactions. While pristine biochar exhibits primarily weak physisorption, the incorporation of oxygen-containing groups (epoxide or carboxyl) inhances more specific and stronger interactions, transitioning the adsorption mechanism toward hydrogen bonding-dominated chemisorption, particularly in the carboxylated system.

Sensor mechanism

Understanding the sensor mechanism is essential for predicting the real-time responsiveness and reliability of a material in environmental or pharmaceutical sensing applications.44 The sensor performance of boron-modified biochar surfaces interacting with ciprofloxacin (CPF) was explored through several electronic parameters that collectively define the nature of adsorption and signal generation. These include: Fermi energy level (EFL): predicts surface reactivity and electron availability, fraction of electron transfer (DT): quantifies the degree of charge exchange between surface and adsorbate, charge transfer (Qt): gives analysis of how much electronic density is transferred between the adsorbate and the sensor surface, back donation (ΔE): evaluates the extent to which the sensor donates electrons back to the molecule after initial electron acceptance. The systems investigated include CPF-B@BCH, CPF-BC2O@BCH, CPF-BCO2@BCH, and CPF-B-O@BCH. Although the pristine CPF-BCH adsorption system serves as an important reference for evaluating the effect of heteroatom doping, the charge-transfer descriptors reported in Table 5 were primarily focused on the doped systems to examine the influence of B/O functionalization on the electronic interaction behavior toward ciprofloxacin adsorption.

Charge transfer (Qt)

Charge transfer analysis evaluates how much electronic density is transferred between the adsorbate and the adsorbent. A positive Qt value means the analyte donated electrons to the sensor, while a negative Qt indicates that the analyte accepted electrons from the sensor.45,46 A common method to estimate charge transfer is through Mulliken population analysis, Bader charge, or Hirshfeld charge analysis.47 The generic representation that was used in this study is:9Qt = Qadsorbed − Qisolatedwhere Qadsorbed is the net charge on the sensor after adsorption. Qsensor is the net charge before adsorption. This difference gives an estimate of how many electrons have been transferred. From Table 5, the CPF-B@BCH and CPF-BC2O@BCH systems show positive Qt, implying the analyte acts as an electron donor. This would increase the carrier concentration in this type of system, enhancing conductivity, which is a good sign for detection. Whereas, the CPF-BCO2@BCH and CPF-B-O@BCH Systems show electron withdrawal, especially in CPF-B-O@BCH. Electron withdrawal can reduce conductivity in certain materials.

Fraction of electron transfer (ΔN)

The electron transfer (ΔN) quantifies how much charge is redistributed between the adsorbed molecule and the surface material during adsorption.48 This metric is essential for characterizing the interfacial electronic interaction between the sensing surface and the target compound. It provides valuable information about the charge exchange mechanism and helps reveal how adsorption affects the material's electrochemical properties.49 Variations in ΔN directly impact the sensor's electrical behavior, often leading to measurable shifts in parameters such as conductivity or capacitance both of which are commonly exploited in analyte detection systems.50 In this study, the ΔN values were determined using eqn (10), offering a quantitative assessment of electron transfer behavior across the different adsorbate-surface complexes.10(ΔN) = Xisolated − Xsystem/2(µ)

A more negative (ΔN) indicates stronger electron transfer from the surface to the drug, which is useful for signal amplification. Back donation ΔE

Back donation (ΔE) describes the extent to which the sensor donates electrons back to the molecule after initial electron acceptance. It typically occurs through orbital overlap, where filled orbitals (e.g., metal d-orbitals or π-backbonding orbitals in doped graphene) transfer charge to antibonding orbitals (π* orbitals) of the analyte.51 Back donation is often represented in energy units (eV) and computed using orbital interaction energies. The stronger the back donation values, the more interactive the system and vice versa. The sensor not only accepts charge (via charge transfer) but also feeds back electrons to the adsorbate, stabilizing the interaction.52

Back donation (ΔE) is a key indicator of chemical reactivity and sensor-analyte coupling strength. The value across all the systems ranges from 0.593–0.894 eV, which suggests significant orbital overlap and bonding interaction (ideal for stable and responsive sensing). The CPF-B@BCH system, with the highest ΔE (0.894 eV), shows the strongest interaction, suggesting that CPF-B@BCH will provide the most effective sensing performance in terms of both adsorption strength and signal response.53

Conclusions

The adsorption and sensing performance of ciprofloxacin (CPF) on pristine and B/O-functionalized biochar (BCH) surfaces were systematically investigated using density functional theory (DFT) calculations. The study combined structural optimization, electronic structure analysis, charge transfer behavior, and topological analyses to provide a comprehensive understanding of CPF–surface interactions. Geometric optimization results showed that all systems maintained structural integrity after adsorption, with only slight variations in bond lengths. Oxygen-containing doped systems exhibited larger local distortions, indicating stronger surface polarization and enhanced interaction with CPF, whereas less modified systems preserved greater structural rigidity. Electronic property analysis, including HOMO–LUMO energy gaps, showed that CPF adsorption generally reduced the energy gap of the biochar systems, suggesting increased electronic reactivity and improved sensing potential. It is important to note that the HOMO–LUMO gaps were obtained from finite cluster calculations and therefore provide qualitative trends rather than true band gaps. QTAIM and NCI analyses confirmed that the interaction between CPF and the doped surfaces is predominantly non-covalent, characterized mainly by hydrogen bonding, electrostatic attraction, and van der Waals interactions. The presence of significant electron density at bond critical points, together with negative energy density contributions in selected interactions, further indicates regions of enhanced interaction strength in oxygen-rich systems. Charge transfer and sensor mechanism analysis revealed measurable electron redistribution between CPF and the functionalized surfaces. Variations in charge transfer (Qt), fraction of electron transfer (ΔN), and back-donation values demonstrate that surface doping significantly influences the material's electronic response upon CPF adsorption. Among all systems, oxygen-functionalized biochar exhibited stronger electronic coupling with CPF, suggesting improved sensitivity and signal responsiveness. Overall, the results indicate that B/O-functionalized biochar surfaces provide tunable electronic and structural properties that enhance CPF adsorption and sensing performance. While all systems exhibit stable adsorption behavior, oxygen-rich configurations show comparatively stronger interaction strength, whereas less distorted structures offer improved structural stability and potential reusability. These combined properties highlight B/O-modified biochar as a promising candidate for environmental sensing and pharmaceutical pollutant detection.
