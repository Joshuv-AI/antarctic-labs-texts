Real‐world trends in pre‐dialysis blood pressure levels of patients undergoing dialysis in Japan using a web‐based national database
Authors: Hirofumi Sakuma, Motoki Matsuki, Naoyuki Hasebe, Naoki Nakagawa
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe prevalence of hypertension is high among patients undergoing dialysis. We extracted data of patients undergoing dialysis between 2012 and 2020 with recorded pre‐dialysis systolic blood pressure (SBP) using a web‐based national database in Japan. Following the 2019 Japanese Society of Hypertension guidelines, we classified SBP and assessed its trends over time based on sex, age, diabetes status, and the anti‐hypertensive medication use. Using the 2020 database, we examined 336,759 Japanese patients undergoing dialysis (114,249 female; 222,510 male). The mean age was 69.4 ± 12.5 years, and the mean SBP was 152.3 ± 24.7 mm Hg. The prevalence rate of pre‐dialysis hypertension was 70.2%, with 32.5%, 24.5%, and 13.2% of patients having grade I, grade II, and grade III hypertension, respectively. From 2014 to 2020, prevalence rate of pre‐dialysis hypertension and absolute values of pre‐dialysis SBP were higher in dialysis patients with diabetes than in those without diabetes across all age groups and sexes. Younger patients with diabetes or those on anti‐hypertensive medication exhibited an SBP of approximately 160 mm Hg. Cerebrovascular death in patients with diabetes was associated with a higher rate of pre‐dialysis hypertension than that in those without diabetes, and there was a significant difference in the prevalence of grade III hypertension between the two groups. In conclusion, the mean pre‐dialysis SBP among patients undergoing dialysis remained high, and younger patients with diabetes or those receiving anti‐hypertensive medications had poor blood pressure control. Optimal blood pressure management may be necessary to reduce the risk of cardiovascular mortality.Keywords: dialysis, pre‐dialysis hypertension, registry

INTRODUCTION

Pre‐dialysis hypertension is common in patients undergoing dialysis. 1 , 2 In Japan, the prevalence of pre‐dialysis hypertension was 77.5% among 65,393 patients undergoing dialysis, as determined using data from the Japanese Society for Dialysis Therapy (JSDT) registry. 3 Elevated blood pressure is associated with reduced survival and an increased incidence of cardiovascular disease. 1 , 4 Despite our knowledge of the impact of pre‐dialysis hypertension on the prognosis of patients undergoing dialysis, inadequate blood pressure control remains a persistent issue. In fact, 58% of pre‐dialysis hypertensive patients undergoing dialysis received treatment that failed to control their condition, whereas only 30% were successfully controlled. 5

Although several systematic review and meta‐analyses have suggested that anti‐hypertensive medication may result in improved survival rates and a lower incidence of cardiovascular events among patients on chronic dialysis, 6 , 7 whether appropriate anti‐hypertensive medication provides adequate blood pressure control remains controversial. To better manage pre‐dialysis hypertension in patients undergoing dialysis, it is necessary to obtain information on current trends in pre‐dialysis blood pressure measurements.

Diabetic nephropathy is the most common primary disease among patients undergoing dialysis, and its incidence is increasing every year in Japan. 8 Despite improvements in dialysis techniques, cardiovascular complications and mortality rates remain higher in patients with diabetes undergoing dialysis than in those without it. 9 , 10 Hypertension is commonly recognized as a contributing factor to the development of cardiovascular complications in patients with diabetes undergoing dialysis. However, data on pre‐dialysis hypertension in this patient population in Japan are limited.

This study had three main objectives: (1) to clarify the clinical characteristics, including pre‐dialysis systolic blood pressure (SBP), of Japanese patients undergoing dialysis; (2) to compare SBP levels among patients undergoing dialysis according to age, sex, diabetes status, and use of anti‐hypertensive drugs; and (3) to determine the prevalence of cardiovascular death based on pre‐dialysis SBP in patients undergoing dialysis with or without diabetes using Web‐based Analysis of Dialysis Data Archives (WADDA) database. 8

METHODS

Study design and data source

This observational study used retrospective data obtained from the JSDT Renal Data Registry (JRDR). 8 JSDT administered a questionnaire survey each year to all dialysis facilities in Japan, with a response rate exceeding 98%. The questionnaire covered the epidemiological background, treatment status, and dialysis treatment outcomes. In 2006, a WADDA system was established as an automated data transfer system from the JRDR, providing access to patient information for members of the JSDT. 8 We extracted data from patients undergoing dialysis between 2012 and 2020 using the WADDA system. This retrospective study was conducted according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and used existing national data without individual patient data; therefore, institutional review board approval and informed consent were not required as previously reported. 11

Patients

Our study included patients undergoing dialysis who were registered with the WADDA system in 2020. Of these, we excluded patients under 20 years of age. We extracted patient information, including age, history of dialysis, dialysis modality, comorbidities, body weight, body mass index (BMI), pre‐dialysis systolic and diastolic blood pressure, pulse rate, and laboratory data such as hemoglobin, total cholesterol, high density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, non‐HDL cholesterol, and albumin levels. The pre‐dialysis blood pressure was measured with the patients in the seated or supine position, depending on the setup at each facility. 12 Using the data from 2020, we summarized the clinical characteristics of patients undergoing dialysis. Additionally, we extracted pre‐dialysis SBP data for patients undergoing dialysis between 2012 and 2020, stratified by age and sex. We were also able to extract pre‐dialysis SBP data of patients undergoing dialysis, with or without diabetes and anti‐hypertensive medications, from 2014 to 2020. For a patient who was 50 years old in 2012, the blood pressure data were monitored every year from 50−54 years or 55−59 years of age for as long as the patient lived. We examined trends in pre‐dialysis SBP in patients undergoing dialysis over the years, stratified by age, sex, presence of diabetes, and anti‐hypertensive medication use. Hypertension was classified according to the guidelines of the Japanese Society of Hypertension guidelines 2019 (JSH 2019). 13

Prevalence of hypertension by cause of death

The cause of death among patients undergoing dialysis was determined using the JRDR coding system and classified according to JRDR categories. 8 We extracted data from patients undergoing dialysis who died of cerebrovascular disease or heart failure and had available pre‐dialysis SBP data using the WADDA system. Additionally, we categorized these patients according to pre‐dialysis SBP and calculated the percentage of patients with pre‐dialysis hypertension.

Statistical analysis

The data of clinical characteristics are presented as means with standard deviations (SD) or counts with percentages, as appropriate. These data were compared between study subgroups using the chi‐square test for categorical variables and unpaired t test for continuous variables. Actual SBP data and the prevalence of hypertension are presented as annual means ± SD. The mean differences in the prevalence of hypertension by cause of death were evaluated based on diabetes status using multiple t tests with the Holm–Sidak method. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism for Mac version 6 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA). Figures were created using GraphPad Prism for Mac version 6 accordingly.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

Using the 2020 database, we examined 336,759 Japanese patients undergoing dialysis, of whom 168,227 had diabetes, 129,936 did not have diabetes, and the data were missing for the remaining patients. Table 1 shows the clinical characteristics of all patients undergoing dialysis. The percentage of pre‐dialysis hypertension was 70.2%, with 32.5%, 24.5%, and 13.2% of the patients having grade I, grade II, and grade III hypertension, respectively. Trends in prevalence of pre‐dialysis hypertension among patients undergoing dialysis

From 2012 to 2020, the percentage of pre‐dialysis hypertensive patients undergoing dialysis was 60%−80% in each age group (Figure S1A‐F). These percentages remained high, and there were no changes in the percentage of hypertension in each age group over time. The percentage of patients with pre‐dialysis hypertension according to sex and age is shown in Figure 1(A–D). The percentage of grade II and III hypertension was predominantly male in the younger population (the twenties to fifties) but predominantly female in the older age population (over sixties). This sex disparity was more pronounced in grade II and III groups than in grade I group.

The pre‐dialysis SBP in patients with diabetes undergoing dialysis was clearly higher than that in patients without diabetes in both sexes and all age groups (Figure 2A–C). This difference was more pronounced among younger age groups. In addition, the rate of grade II and III hypertension in patients undergoing dialysis with diabetes was clearly higher than that in those without diabetes in both sexes and all age groups (Figure 3A–F).

Pre‐dialysis SBP values and prevalence of pre‐dialysis hypertension by sex, age, and anti‐hypertensive medication use

The pre‐dialysis SBP in patients undergoing dialysis with anti‐hypertensive medication use was clearly higher than that in patients without anti‐hypertensive medication use (Figure 4A, B). For each sex and age group, the pre‐dialysis SBP in patients undergoing dialysis with anti‐hypertensive medication use was approximately 150 mm Hg (Figure 4A). In the younger population, pre‐dialysis SBP in male patients undergoing dialysis was higher than that in female patients with or without anti‐hypertensive medication use (Figure 4C–E). The rate of anti‐hypertensive medication use in younger male patients was also higher than that in female patients (Table S1).

Prevalence of pre‐dialysis hypertension according to cause of death and presence of diabetes

We evaluated the prevalence of pre‐dialysis hypertension according to the cause of cerebrovascular and heart failure‐related deaths. Patients with cerebrovascular death had a higher prevalence of pre‐dialysis hypertension than those who died due to heart failure death. The prevalence of pre‐dialysis hypertension in patients with cerebrovascular and heart failure‐related deaths tended to be consistently higher among those with diabetes than in those without diabetes. Among patients undergoing dialysis with diabetes, the proportion of patients with grade III hypertension was significantly higher when compared to that in those without diabetes in the context of cerebrovascular death. Furthermore, in the context of death due to heart failure, the prevalence rates of pre‐dialysis hypertension and grade III hypertension were significantly higher among patients with diabetes than that among those without diabetes (Figure S2A, B).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we found that the management of pre‐dialysis hypertension in patients undergoing dialysis was still poor with the mean pre‐dialysis SBP being 156.6 mm Hg based on a web‐based national database. Specifically, patients undergoing dialysis who were younger, had diabetes, and were on anti‐hypertensive medication had a higher pre‐dialysis SBP.

In previous observational trials, pre‐dialysis SBP showed a U‐ or J‐shaped relationship with cardiovascular mortality. 1 , 14 , 15 In particular, patients with pre‐dialysis SBP > 160 mm Hg have been found to have a significantly increased risk of cardiovascular mortality. In our study, we found that the mean pre‐dialysis SBP in the younger population (in their aged 30 and 40 s) exceeded 160 mm Hg. Therefore, we considered that even stricter control of hypertension is critical in these populations. At low pre‐dialysis SBP levels, past analyses have found a higher mortality rate among patients with pre‐dialysis SBP < 130 mm Hg, 14 indicating that low pre‐dialysis SBP is also a cardiovascular risk factor. The 2017 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guidelines recommend a blood pressure threshold and target of 130/80 mm Hg, 16 while the 2018 European Society of Hypertension/European Society of Cardiology guidelines recommend an SBP target of <130 mm Hg for those under 65 years of age and 130−140 mm Hg for the other age groups. 17 In the JSDT, the target pre‐dialysis SBP at the beginning of the week is <140 mm Hg. 12 These guidelines, along with the findings of our analysis, indicate that blood pressure control is inadequate in younger populations. However, we must also be cautious about hypotension resulting from excessive anti‐hypertensive therapy in older patients. As patients get older, their blood pressure levels and the rate of anti‐hypertensive medication use decrease, suggesting that hypotension is common in older patients undergoing dialysis. 18 Therefore, appropriate management is essential to achieve an optimal range of pre‐dialysis SBP. 19

A comparison of diabetes status revealed that the pre‐dialysis SBP in patients with diabetes undergoing dialysis was markedly higher than that in patients without diabetes in our analysis. According to the 2021 UK guidelines, 20 the interdialytic blood pressure target for patients with diabetes is <140/90 mm Hg. However, there is a dearth of evidence from randomized controlled trials guiding blood pressure management in patients undergoing dialysis for diabetes. Moreover, accurate blood pressure measurement in patients with diabetes undergoing dialysis is difficult for medical staff because of altered fluid volume and autonomic neuropathy. Therefore, we considered that the correct assessment of pre‐dialysis SBP in patients with diabetes is problematic; however, our analysis found that many age groups of patients undergoing dialysis with diabetes had a pre‐dialysis SBP > 160 mm Hg and a high prevalence of grade II or higher hypertension, indicating inadequate blood pressure management in such populations. Conversely, the pre‐dialysis SBP of non‐diabetic patients undergoing dialysis was <150 mm Hg, particularly in younger age groups.

Regarding the presence of anti‐hypertensive medication use, the pre‐dialysis SBP of patients taking these medications was >150 mm Hg, mainly in the younger age groups. In contrast, the pre‐dialysis SBP of patients not taking anti‐hypertensive medications was <150 mm Hg in almost all groups. This indicates that patients undergoing dialysis and taking anti‐hypertensive medications do not achieve appropriate blood pressure control despite treatment. Although information on fluid volume and the type and number of anti‐hypertensive drugs was not available, the significant difference in pre‐dialysis SBP between patients undergoing dialysis with and without anti‐hypertensive medication use can be considered to be a novel finding. Anti‐hypertensive treatment in patients undergoing dialysis needs to be reevaluated to reduce pre‐dialysis SBP and cardiovascular risk.

Our analysis had certain limitations. First, this was a retrospective cohort study, which prevented us from establishing a clear association between pre‐dialysis SBP and clinical outcomes such as cardiovascular risk. Second, the pre‐dialysis SBP measurement method was determined by each facility. In the JSDT guideline for the evaluation and the treatment of cardiovascular complications in hemodialysis patients, pre‐dialysis blood pressure should be measured under fixed conditions although it can be measured with the patient in either the seated or supine position depending on the setup at each facility. 12 Although the most common way to measure pre‐dialysis blood pressure in the JSDT database is with the patient lying on the bed in the supine position in preparation for the needle puncture, 3 there may have been differences in the measurement equipment and environment. Third, we were unable to obtain information on the assessment of fluid volume or the number and type of anti‐hypertensive medications used during the blood pressure measurements from the database used. This information is critical and has a significant impact on patients’ blood pressure values. Finally, in the analysis of the cause of death, we faced challenges in extracting an adequate number of patients due to the limited number of cases where blood pressure was measured. Although we extracted only approximately 10% of the total for each cause of death, we believe that we were able to discern an overarching trend.

In conclusion, we utilized a web‐based national database to provide a detailed summary of the trends in pre‐dialysis blood pressure values of patients undergoing dialysis over the last decade, stratified by age, sex, diabetes status, and use of anti‐hypertensive medications. Specifically, our findings suggested inadequate pre‐dialysis blood pressure control in younger patients, those with diabetes, and those taking anti‐hypertensive medications, highlighting the need to review the pre‐dialysis blood pressure control strategies in these populations.
