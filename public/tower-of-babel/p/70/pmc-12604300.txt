Effects of an exoskeleton robot on motor function in patients with spinal cord injuries: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Shuai Guo, Yican Yang, Mutong Wang, Dezheng Wang, Yang Zhang, Qing Wang, Yunqing Deng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the impact of exoskeleton robotic training on motor function in spinal cord injury (SCI) patients.MethodsWe systematically reviewed randomized and non-randomized controlled trials from five databases (PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Scopus, Web of Science) up to September 2025.ResultsA total of 13 trials with 247 participants were included. Results showed that exoskeleton robotic training significantly improved several motor function measures: the 6-min walk test (6-MWT) (0.75 [95% CI = 0.42, 1.08]), 10-m walk test (0.91 [95% CI = 0.49, 1.33]), Walking Index for Spinal Cord Injury (WISCI-II) (0.76 [95% CI = 0.36, 1.15]), timed up and go (TUG) test (− 1.05 [95% CI = − 1.50, − 0.60]), and lower extremity motor score (LEMS) (0.65 [95% CI = 0.40, 0.90]). Subgroup analysis indicated that improvements in walking speed were consistent regardless of injury duration (> 6 vs. < 6 months). In contrast, when stratified by cumulative training time, a non-linear dose–response relationship was observed, with the greatest improvement occurring at 1000–2000 min of training, while both lower and higher doses yielded attenuated effects. Although there was high heterogeneity in some measures (6-MWT, 10-MWT, WISCI-II, TUG), excluding individual studies did not significantly alter the findings, indicating stable results.ConclusionExoskeleton robotics significantly improves motor function in SCI patients, with walking function benefiting both within and beyond 6 months after injury, although the effects of ultra-early (< 3 months) versus early (3–6 months) intervention remain unclear, with maximal improvements observed at medium intensity (cumulative training duration of 1000–2000 min).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13643-025-02974-1.

Introduction

The global annual incidence of spinal cord injuries (SCIs) is 13.0–220.0 cases per million people [1]. These injuries reduce quality of life and burden families and society [2]. Improving walking ability is crucial for SCI patients’ motor function. Research shows walking-related tasks are better than non-walking ones [3, 4], and specific training parameters can boost neuromuscular and cardiorespiratory plasticity, affecting motor performance post-injury [5–7]. For instance, much pedaling (walking) practice aids motor recovery in SCI animal models, but the effectiveness of walking and exercise training varies in subacute or chronic SCI patients [8–10]. Current gait training for them includes traditional on-ground walking, body weight-supported treadmill training, and robot-assisted gait training.

Rehabilitation exoskeleton robots, a type of medical robot, are crucial in rehabilitation. They assist with limb motions for weight-bearing walking, aided walking, rehabilitation, and reducing work intensity [11–13]. Their ability to perform long-term, repetitive tasks consistently ensures the effectiveness of rehabilitation training, helping patients improve outcomes through a standard regimen [14, 15]. For lower-limb exoskeleton robots helping spinal cord injury (SCI) patients regain motor function, the situation is complex. Their effectiveness depends on factors like damage nature, location, injury length, and rehabilitation timing [16]. Their impact on SCI patients is unclear, as most evaluations have focused on stroke, amputation, and elderly patients [17]. Implementing exoskeleton treatment for SCI patients is difficult. SCI patients’ varied lower-limb and back muscle functions require individualized strategies [18]. Also, comparative clinical assessments are challenging because exoskeleton controls must be customized, making a unified standard hard to establish [19].

With the rapid development of robotic devices, researchers focus more on robotic gait rehabilitation. Stampacchia et al. [20] found that for patients with complete SCI lesions, exoskeletons improved dexterity but not independent walking, and symptoms like spasticity, pain, and cardiovascular endurance got better. Different exoskeletons can be used for SCI patients’ ambulatory rehabilitation, and the choice depends on lesion severity and possible complications. Aach et al.’s trial [21] showed that powered exoskeletons as a training intervention for ambulatory SCI adults led to gains in gait speed and endurance, possibly due to neuroplasticity and motor learning. The HAL (Hybrid Assistive Limb) exoskeleton with high-treadmill repetition and active-stepping feedback may strengthen brain connections in partial SCI [22]. But in Benson et al.’s trial [23], gait speed and endurance did not improve for two partial-SCI individuals. These conflicting results call for more research on exoskeleton robotics to fully explore the potential of powered exoskeletons as training aids.

Several reviews have been published on the application of exoskeleton robots in spinal cord injury rehabilitation. For instance, a review [24] mainly presents a narrative summary of relevant studies, with most of the content being descriptive and lacking systematic quantitative analysis. Due to the limitations of the research, when elaborating on the effects of robotic rehabilitation therapy, the article mostly discusses emerging trends and fails to clearly present well-verified benefits, resulting in a relatively low level of certainty in its conclusions. In Louie et al.’s research [25], there is marked heterogeneity in study characteristics, including device types, control methods, training durations, and outcome measurements. This heterogeneity makes it difficult to compare research results and undermines the generalizability of findings. A meta-analysis [26] found that exoskeleton robots can drive slower limb movements. After 4-week training, SCI patients had significant locomotion and LEMS (lower extremity motor score) improvements. Subgroup analyses showed improved walking speed and endurance in patients with 6-month disease duration. But the article lacks sensitivity analyses, and some metrics have a small number of included literature and patients.

To systematically investigate how exoskeleton robots improve motor function in patients with spinal cord injury (SCI), this meta-analysis specifically aims to provide new evidence on the functionality of exoskeleton robot rehabilitation in this patient population. Through these methodological strengths, the study offers more comprehensive and reliable evidence for the clinical application of exoskeleton robots in SCI patients’ rehabilitation, addressing both multidimensional outcome assessments and potential influencing factors through rigorous analytical approaches.

Methods

Before starting, this review was registered in the PROSPERO database (registration number ID: CRD42023487474). The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) checklist was used to record the study selection process [27] (Additional file 2 of the Supplementary Materials).

Search strategy

Five electronic databases (PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Scopus, and Web of Science) were searched for preferred reporting items according to the guidelines for systematic evaluation and meta-analysis, in September 2025. We searched for all articles related to the improvement of motor function in patients with spinal cord injury treated with exoskeleton robotics. The search strategy combined MeSH terms such as [“spinal cord injury (MeSH)”], [“Exoskeleton Device (MeSH)”], and [“Locomotion (MeSH)”] with relevant non-MeSH terms (e.g., “Robotic gait training,” “Exoskeleton-assisted rehabilitation”) and specific device names (e.g., “HAL Exoskeleton,” “Ekso Bionics”) to improve the sensitivity of the search. The literature search flowchart is shown in Fig. 1. The inclusion criteria were:

(1) Patients were clinically diagnosed with SCI; (2) patients with SCI were treated with exoskeleton robots; (3) either single-group studies or controlled studies. The control intervention could be routine rehabilitation training without exoskeleton robot; (4) aimed to evaluate the effects of exoskeleton robot on paraplegia lower limbs extremity functional recovery; (5) the exoskeleton robot training was administered across more than one session; (6) at least one assessment related to clinical effects was conducted before and after the intervention; (7) at least one outcome of interest, such as the 6-min walking test (6-MWT), the 10-m walking test (10-MWT), the Walking Index for Spinal Cord Injury (WISCI)-II score, the timed up and go (TUG) test, and the lower extremity motor score (LEMS); (8) published in English.

The exclusion criteria were:

(1) reviews; (2) studies having incomplete or unretrievable data; (3) animal experiments; and (4) studies including redundant patient data.

Two researchers independently selected the studies based on the titles and abstracts, and a third reviewer’s consensus was used to settle disagreements. An evidence table including the following information was created using data retrieved from the included studies and a data extraction template: authors, year, sample size, age, sex, length of disease, device, intervention program, and outcome.

Statistical analyses

Reviewer Manager version 5.4 was used to calculate the data. The 6-min walk test (6-MWT), 10-m walk test (10-MWT), Walking Index for Spinal Cord Injury (WISCI)-II score, timed up and go (TUG) test, and lower extremity motor score (LEMS) were the primary observables. Following therapy, the patients’ ability to walk was expressed as standardized mean differences. To determine the overall effect, the mean difference of each trial’s findings was computed using the 95% confidence interval (CI). The I statistic was utilized to evaluate statistical heterogeneity among the studies; I values less than 50% suggested homogeneity. A fixed-effects model was employed to analyze the data. The data were analyzed as continuous variables with I2 values of > 50%, and the standardized mean differences with 95% CI were computed using a random effects model. Possible sources of heterogeneity were investigated using subgroup analyses (e.g., based on injury duration and intervention intensity). P-values less than 0.05 denoted overall effects and study heterogeneity that were statistically significant. Utilizing Stata 17 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA), sensitivity analyses were carried out. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plot analysis when ≥ 10 studies were included in a meta-analysis.

Quality and bias risk assessment

Two reviewers used the Cochrane Collaboration Risk of Bias 2.0 tool to assess the quality of randomized controlled trials, evaluating domains such as randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of the reported result [28]. For non-randomized controlled trials, the ROBINS-I tool was applied to assess risk of bias across domains including confounding, participant selection, intervention classification, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting [29]. These two tools were selected to ensure appropriate and comprehensive bias assessment for both randomized and non-randomized studies.

In the two included studies [30, 31], a small number of participants withdrew during follow-up, with the proportion of withdrawals being minimal, resulting in a discrepancy between baseline and follow-up sample sizes. Due to incomplete outcome data, baseline values of all enrolled participants were used as a proxy for effect size calculation in the forest plot, although outcome measurements were available only for completers. This approach may introduce minimal bias, and in the risk of bias assessment, the domain “Bias due to missing outcome data” was rated as moderate risk or some concerns for these two studies.

Differences of opinion were discussed until consensus was reached. The RevMan 5.4 software (Cochrane Collaboration, London, UK) was used for the evaluations.

Results

Search results

At first, 682 articles were found; however, 645 of them were eliminated since their titles and abstracts showed no relevance. After reviewing the remaining 46 papers, 33 were excluded for various reasons, including unavailable study results, irrelevant intervention methods, and unrelated methodological content [32–64]. Ultimately, 13 papers totaling 247 SCI patients that were published between 2015 and 2023 were included in the meta-analysis [30, 31, 65–75]. 6-MWT and 10-MWT

Ten studies comprising 204 patients with SCI had 6-MWT data. The I2 value was 51% (P = 0.03); thus, the data were analyzed using a random-effects model. The distance traveled during the 6-MTW significantly improved with the exoskeleton robot compared with the baseline value (0.75 [95% CI = 0.42, 1.08]; P < 0.0001; Fig. 4). 10-MWT data were available for 228 SCI patients across eleven investigations. Since the I2 value was 72% (P < 0.0001), a random-effects model was used to evaluate the data. Using the exoskeleton robot, the walking speed during the 10-MWT increased considerably compared with the baseline value (0.91 [95% CI = 0.49, 1.33]; P < 0.0001; Fig. 5).Fig. 4Change from baseline in the 6-MWTFig. 5Change from baseline in the 10-MWT

Nine studies comprising 211 patients with SCI had WISCI-II data and nine studies comprising 159 patients had TUG test data. Among all outcome measures, the heterogeneity of 10-MWT was the highest (I2 = 72%, P < 0.0001). Since one study did not provide information on injury duration, it was excluded from the subgroup analysis [73]. Therefore, a subgroup analysis for 10-MWT was performed, stratified by injury duration (< 6 months and > 6 months) (Fig. 9). For < 6 months, the I2 value was 0% (P = 0.88); thus, a random-effects model was used, yielding a pooled SMD of 0.89 (95% CI = 0.53, 1.26; P < 0.00001). For > 6 months, the I2 value was 83% (P < 0.00001); thus, a random-effects model was applied, yielding a pooled SMD of 0.98 (95% CI = 0.37, 1.58; P = 0.002). These findings suggest that exoskeleton robot therapy significantly improves walking function in SCI patients both within and beyond 6 months after injury, although the comparative effects of ultra-early (< 3 months) versus early (3–6 months) intervention remain unclear.Fig. 9Subgroup analysis of 10-MWT by injury duration

Subgroup analysis of 10-MWT by injury duration

To further explore the source of heterogeneity, total training time was used as a proxy for intervention intensity and categorized into three subgroups: low intensity (< 1000 min), moderate intensity (1000–2000 min), and high intensity (> 2000 min) (Fig. 10). For the low-intensity subgroup, the I2 value was 0% (P = 0.47); thus, a random-effects model was used, yielding a pooled SMD of 0.56 (95% CI = − 0.20, 1.32; P = 0.15). For the moderate-intensity subgroup, the I2 value was 83% (P < 0.00001); thus, a random-effects model was applied, yielding a pooled SMD of 1.13 (95% CI = 0.38, 1.88; P = 0.003). For the high-intensity subgroup, the I2 value was 41% (P = 0.19); thus, a random-effects model was applied, yielding a pooled SMD of 0.73 (95% CI = 0.36, 1.09; P = 0.0001). These results indicate a non-linear dose–response relationship, with the greatest therapeutic effect observed at 1000–2000 min, whereas both lower and higher cumulative training doses produced attenuated improvements.Fig. 10Subgroup analysis of 10-MWT by intervention intensity

Sensitivity analysis

The 6-MWT, 10-MWT, WISCI-II, and TUG test results were highly heterogeneous (6-MWT I2 = 51%, P = 0.03; 10-MWT I2 = 72%, P < 0.0001; WISCI-II I2 = 69%, P = 0.001; TUG I2 = 65%, P = 0.004). Therefore, we conducted sensitivity analyses for these four indicators using the one-by-one exclusion method to identify the source of heterogeneity and determine the stability of the results (Fig. 11). We found that individual studies may affect the statistical significance of the indicators, but overall, eliminating individual studies did not significantly alter the results. Thus, the results were relatively stable.Fig. 11A 6MWT sensitivity analysis results; B 10MWT sensitivity analysis results; C WISCI II sensitivity analysis results; D TUG sensitivity analysis results

Publication bias

Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots for outcomes with ten or more included studies. The funnel plots are presented in Fig. 12. For the 6-MWT (Fig. 12A), the distribution of studies was generally symmetrical around the pooled effect size, although several studies deviated from the central axis, particularly in regions with larger standard errors, suggesting potential small-study effects. For the 10-MWT (Fig. 12B), the funnel plot also exhibited an approximately symmetrical shape, but a few outliers were observed on the right side, indicating possible publication bias or heterogeneity among smaller studies. Overall, the funnel plots indicate a relatively balanced distribution of effect sizes for both outcomes, although a slight risk of publication bias cannot be ruled out.Fig. 12Funnel plot for publication bias: A 6-MWT, B 10MWT

Discussion

Exoskeleton robotic rehabilitation was developed about 30 years ago, and as technology has advanced, so has its application in clinical rehabilitation. Despite the existence of multiple reviews on exoskeleton robots, there is insufficient data to support their efficacy and usefulness in improving motor function in individuals with spinal cord injuries. Consequently, the clinical benefits of exoskeleton robots on these patients’ improvements in motor function are summarized in this meta-analysis.

Thirteen trials with 247 SCI patients were considered in this analysis. With the exception of LEMS, all examined indicators showed significant heterogeneity. Consequently, in order to determine the source and evaluate the stability of the results, we performed sensitivity analyses utilizing the one-by-one exclusion method. We discovered that eliminating specific studies had no effect on the overall statistical significance of the 6-MWT, 10-MWT, TUG, and WISCI-II data, suggesting that the results are stable. The various kinds of exoskeleton robots employed in the various trials lead to the variability. For example, body weight-supported treadmill training with a voluntarily driven exoskeleton was employed in six experiments with the HAL® exoskeleton robot [67, 69–72, 74], three with the EKSO™ exoskeleton robot [65, 66, 73], and one each with the HANK lower limb exoskeleton robot [68], the REARS exoskeleton robot [75], the MoreGait exoskeleton [31], and the Lokomat [30].

Most of the studies included in this paper used the 6-MWT, 10-MWT, TUG test, WISCI-II, and LEMS to evaluate motor function. We found that the scores for all tests significantly improved with exoskeleton robot training compared with the baseline values in patients with SCI, suggesting that exoskeleton robot training improves lower limb motor function in these patients, similar to the results of Giulia Stampacchia et al. [20]. Importantly, different exoskeletons can promote recovery of walking function in patients with various types of SCI. For instance, static and dynamic exoskeleton gait training improves walking skills in patients with incomplete SCI who still have residual walking ability. The Indego exoskeleton helps patients with SCI who are unable to walk upright, improving speed and independence when walking indoors and outdoors. Walking function appears to improve in SCI patients receiving exoskeleton training both within and beyond 6 months after injury. However, the current subgroup analysis only compared patients with < 6 months versus > 6 months since injury and did not specifically evaluate the effects of ultra-early (< 3 months) versus early (3–6 months) intervention. Studies on ultra-early intervention are relatively limited, as patients in this phase often present with severe concomitant injuries (e.g., fractures, traumatic brain injury), unstable vital signs, spinal cord edema, or unhealed postoperative wounds. During this period, clinical management focuses on stabilization and medical care, making it difficult to initiate exoskeleton-assisted rehabilitation immediately. Therefore, although earlier initiation of exoskeleton training may be beneficial, this conclusion should be interpreted with caution and requires further confirmation in future studies with more refined temporal stratification. Future research should also investigate ultra-early (< 3 months) exoskeleton-assisted rehabilitation in SCI patients, with patient safety as the foremost priority. Analysis of total training time revealed a non-linear dose–response relationship. Low-intensity training (< 1000 min) produced limited effects, moderate-intensity training (1000–2000 min) yielded the greatest improvements, and high-intensity training (> 2000 min) was significant but less effective than the moderate-intensity group. This pattern may be related to patient adherence and fatigue resulting from excessive training, suggesting that future rehabilitation program designs should optimize total training time to achieve maximal recovery, rather than simply increasing training duration. A meta-analysis [26] reported that the exoskeleton robot can drive slower limb movements in patients through levers, and after 4 weeks of effective training, patients with SCI showed significant improvements in locomotion and LEMS. Subgroup analyses also showed significant improvements in walking speed and walking endurance after training with the exoskeleton robot in patients with a disease duration of 6 months. However, this article suffers from the lack of sensitivity analyses to analyze the stability of the data as well as the small number of literature and patients included in some of the metrics.

Some studies included in this meta-analysis used other parameters to measure different recovery indicators, such as endurance, balance, functional recovery, daily living ability, and functional independence. These parameters included the 2-min walk test, the Berg Balance Scale, and the Barthel Index and Functional Independence Measure. The Spinal Cord Independence Measure III was also used, which assesses patient endurance, balance, functional recovery, the ability to perform activities of daily living, and functional independence. In addition, Sale et al. included the visual analog pain score, the Borg Rating of Perceived Exertion, and the visual analog fatigue score to assess the patient’s degree of pain and subjective perceived exertion, which help evaluate recovery and develop individualized treatment plans. However, these indicators were not included in the meta-analysis because of the small number of trials and participants.

In this meta-analysis, some studies [31, 70, 75] employed additional parameters to evaluate various recovery outcomes, including endurance, muscle tone, balance, functional recovery, activities of daily living, and functional independence. These parameters included the 2-min walk test, the Modified Ashworth Scale (MAS), the Berg Balance Scale, and the Barthel Index and Functional Independence Measure. In addition, the Spinal Cord Independence Measure III was used to assess patient endurance, balance, functional recovery, activities of daily living, and functional independence. Sale et al. [73] further included the visual analog pain score, the Borg Rating of Perceived Exertion, and the visual analog fatigue score to evaluate patients’ pain intensity and perceived exertion, aiding in recovery assessment and the development of individualized rehabilitation plans. Moreover, Ma et al. [30] incorporated the Physiological Cost Index (PCI), self-selected walking speed (SWS), and maximum walking speed (MWS). However, due to the limited number of trials and participants, these additional outcomes were not included in the meta-analysis.

Notably, two of the included studies [30, 31] conducted long-term follow-up assessments, extending up to 1 month or 20 weeks after completion of exoskeleton-assisted training. These data provide preliminary evidence that functional improvements may persist beyond the immediate post-intervention period, although the durability of effects was heterogeneous across studies. In addition, one study [30] included pediatric patients with spinal cord injury, offering initial insights into the feasibility and potential benefits of exoskeleton use in children and enhancing the generalizability of exoskeleton-based rehabilitation. However, current evidence regarding long-term follow-up as well as pediatric and elderly subgroups remains limited, highlighting the need for further high-quality studies to confirm sustained efficacy and to evaluate safety and applicability across different age groups.

Recent evidence indicates that exoskeleton robotic therapy can not only greatly enhance motor function in individuals with SCI but also allow them to walk faster and for longer, and engage in more intense exercise. Numerous difficulties associated with SCI patients are also improved with exoskeleton robotic therapy [16, 26]. For example, it lessens secondary health issues caused by deep vein thrombosis, spasticity, discomfort, defecation abnormalities, urinary tract infections, and decubitus ulcers. It also enhances cardiorespiratory fitness through increasing aerobic activity, which lessens cardiovascular issues. One of the more innovative and promising training techniques for those with spinal cord injuries seeking to enhance their quality of life is the use of exoskeleton robots. The way the healthcare system approaches SCI rehabilitation may alter as a result of this innovative approach to rehabilitation training. Nevertheless, as an emerging rehabilitation modality, additional clinical trials are required to fully understand the therapeutic possibilities of this type of rehabilitation.

Strengths

This meta-analysis has several unique features. We systematically retrieved relevant studies from five major databases (PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Scopus, Web of Science) up to September 2025, covering a wider range of research sources than previous reviews. Our study included both randomized and non-randomized controlled trials, with a total of 247 participants, providing a larger and more diverse sample for analysis. We evaluated the effects of exoskeleton robots on multiple motor function indicators, including 6-MWT, 10-MWT, WISCI-II, TUG, and LEMS. Moreover, we conducted subgroup analysis based on injury duration and total intervention time, and performed sensitivity analysis to explore the stability of the results and the influence of different factors.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the overall methodological quality of the included studies was moderate, with potential biases related to inadequate randomization, missing outcome data, confounding factors, participant selection, and deviations from intended interventions. Second, substantial heterogeneity existed across intervention protocols—including differences in group allocation, training duration, and exoskeleton type—which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Third, long-term follow-up data are lacking (e.g., maintenance of effects 3–6 months post-intervention). Fourth, studies including pediatric patients with spinal cord injury are scarce, and no elderly patients were included, limiting the representativeness of the study population. Fifth, fewer than ten studies reported outcomes such as WISCI-II, TUG, and LEMS, precluding assessment of potential publication bias for these measures. Finally, the overall sample size was relatively small, reducing statistical power and limiting the robustness of subgroup analyses. Future research should prioritize large-scale, multicenter trials with standardized intervention protocols, extended follow-up periods, and more diverse patient populations to strengthen the reliability and clinical applicability of the evidence.

Conclusion

Exoskeleton robot training can significantly enhance motor functions in patients with SCI, including walking speed, walking distance, and lower limb function. Overall, exoskeleton robot therapy markedly improves walking function in SCI patients both within and beyond 6 months after injury, although the comparative effects of ultra-early (< 3 months) versus early (3–6 months) intervention remain unclear. Notably, the greatest improvements were observed at medium training intensity, corresponding to a cumulative duration of 1000–2000 min.
