Epicardial Adipose Tissue (Pericoronary Adipose Tissue) in Cardiovascular Diseases
Authors: Eun Jeong Cho
Published: 2025

Epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) is defined as the adipose tissue located between the myocardium and the visceral pericardium. Pericoronary adipose tissue (PCAT) is defined as a localized portion of EAT that surrounds the coronary arteries.

EAT is located between the myocardium and the visceral pericardium, predominantly over the surface of the right ventricle and the anterior wall of the left ventricle. It also surrounds the atrioventricular and great coronary grooves, reaching its greatest thickness along the anterior and lateral walls of the right atrium. Owing to its elasticity and compressibility, EAT serves a mechanical role in protecting the coronary arteries from excessive bending caused by arterial pulsation and myocardial contraction.1)

The amount and thickness of EAT are known to be influenced by genetic, epigenetic, and environmental factors. Aging, excessive caloric intake, sedentary lifestyle, exposure to pollutants, and alterations in the microbiota are also recognized as environmental factors that may contribute to EAT accumulation.2) EAT thickness is closely associated with abdominal adiposity and is generally increased in individuals with obesity. Consequently, EAT thickness has been reported to correlate with relative clinical features such as metabolic syndrome, hypertension, elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels, and insulin resistance.3)

EAT has been reported to act as a direct mediator of paracrine signaling by secreting anti-inflammatory cytokines such as adiponectin. Accordingly, as EAT expands, its secretory profile is known to shift toward a pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic state, characterized by increased secretion of cytokines including interleukin-6, tumor necrosis factor-alpha, and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1.4)

Higher levels of EAT have been reported to be associated with impaired cardiac function, increased filling pressures, reduced pericardial compliance, increased atrial and ventricular load, and decreased exercise tolerance.5,6,7,8,9) In previous studies, increased EAT showed a positive association with both incident heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) and mortality, even after accounting for established cardiovascular risk factors.10) Furthermore, in patients with established HFpEF, EAT was associated with an increased risk of heart failure hospitalization and mortality.11,12) And, 2 meta-analyses published in 2017 confirmed the association of EAT with outcomes, independent of the definition of coronary artery disease (CAD), including obstructive or significant coronary stenosis, coronary artery calcification, or myocardial ischemia.13,14) Furthermore, PCAT has been shown to be particularly involved in the development of atherosclerosis (Figure 1).15)

Quantification of EAT can be relatively easily assessed using various imaging modalities, including echocardiography, computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging.

Among the previously mentioned EAT measurement methods, fat attenuation index (FAI), measured in CT Hounsfield units, represents the relative lipid content and adipocyte size within the EAT volume and was used to assess inflammation, fibrosis, and overall fat density. This low-density EAT is thought to be associated with adipocyte hypertrophy, proliferation, fibrosis, increased inflammation, and increased white adipose tissue content.16) Previous studies have shown that lower EAT density, as measured by FAI on CT scans, is associated with increased mortality and hospitalization for heart failure.17)

In this issue of the International Journal of Heart Failure, Agarwal et al.,18) this study investigated the relationship between PCAT characteristics - specifically volume, FAI, and thickness - and both CAD severity and left ventricular (LV) function in 100 patients who underwent coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) and echocardiography. The results showed that right coronary artery (RCA) FAI had the strongest association with CAD severity and LV diastolic dysfunction, while RCA volume was linked to LV mass index and diastolic parameters. Hypertension was related to increased RCA FAI and volume, whereas diabetes had no significant effect.

Many interventional studies have now demonstrated that EAT is a modifiable factor. Several studies have shown that a reduction in EAT can be achieved through weight loss induced by dietary interventions, bariatric surgery, or pharmacological treatments, including statins, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists, and sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors.19,20,21,22,23,24)

In summary, this study demonstrates that PCAT FAI, particularly around the RCA, may serve as a sensitive imaging biomarker for detecting subclinical inflammation, early CAD progression, and LV diastolic dysfunction.

The anatomical location of EAT determines its unique significance in cardiovascular biology. Therefore, based on the findings of this study, incorporating PCAT analysis into routine CCTA assessments may help improve risk stratification for CAD, facilitate the early detection of functional myocardial impairment, and support the development of personalized treatment strategies for patients with CAD.

However, this study has several limitations, including a small sample size (n=100), retrospective design, and single-center setting. Thus, large-scale, multicenter, prospective studies are required to validate these results and further clarify the clinical implications.

In addition, while current studies are gradually elucidating whether dysfunctional EAT plays a causal role in cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) such as atherosclerosis, heart failure, and atrial fibrillation, many aspects remain unclear. Therefore, future research should not only focus on improving imaging assessment of EAT, but also on determining whether reductions in EAT volume or thickness and changes in its phenotype actually confer cardioprotective effects.

As clinical imaging technologies for the identification and characterization of EAT continue to advance, future clinical trials are expected to focus on evaluating the associations between EAT and CVDs, as well as investigating the effects of EAT-modifying therapies in patients with CVD.
