High‐quality genome of elite peanut cultivar ZH05 reveals subgenome asymmetry, pan‐genome diversity, and breeding insights
Authors: Taihua Yang, Nian Liu, Li Huang, Jianbin Guo, Xiaomeng Xue, Mingjun Wang, Xiaohan Zhang, Chengtao Quan, Enyou Feng, Jiazhuang Tan, Zhenzhen Zhang, Yu You, Youlin Xia, Dongxin Huai, Yuning Chen, Liying Yan, Kede Liu, Huifang Jiang, Yong Lei, Boshou Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPeanut (Arachis hypogaea) is an allotetraploid legume crop of agricultural and economic importance worldwide. Despite advances in peanut genomics, the integration of genetic diversity with multi‐omics landscapes remains limited, and a comprehensive understanding of subgenome asymmetry is still lacking. Here, we present a high‐quality genome assembly of an elite peanut cultivar Zhonghua 5 (ZH05; 2,622.85 Mb with 73,718 predicted genes) and construct a species‐specific pan‐genome based on 1,286 accessions, uncovering 849.82 Mb of non‐reference sequences and 18,484 novel genes. Over 23 million genetic variants and extensive gene PAVs substantially expand the landscape of genetic diversity in peanut. Integrative multi‐omics (transcriptome, DNA methylation, chromatin accessibility, and three‐dimensional genome architecture), combined with population genomics analyses, reveal pronounced and multi‐layered subgenome asymmetry. Specifically, SubA shows higher chromatin accessibility and overall gene expression, whereas SubB displays more transposable elements, elevated DNA methylation, more genes under selection during domestication, and stronger three‐dimensional chromatin interactions with more stable topologically associating domains. Furthermore, genetic dissection using a recombinant inbred line population identified several major stable QTLs for seed size, whose pyramiding contributes to ZH05's superior yield performance. Collectively, this study establishes an integrated genomic framework for peanut, illuminates asymmetric subgenome evolution across sequence, epigenetic, and chromatin architecture, and provides valuable resources for accelerating molecular breeding and crop improvement.

INTRODUCTION

Peanut (Arachis hypogaea, 2n = 4x = 40, AABB) is an important allotetraploid legume crop and a major source of plant‐based oil and protein, contributing substantially to global nutrition and agricultural economies, especially in developing countries, with China and India leading production (Akram et al., 2018; Toomer, 2018). The origin of A. hypogaea has been traced to one or two hybridization events involving its two diploid progenitors, A. duranensis (2n = 20, AA) and A. ipaensis (2n = 20, BB), estimated to have occurred either less than 10,000 years ago or approximately 0.42–0.47 million years ago (Seijo et al., 2007; Bertioli et al., 2019; Zhuang et al., 2019; Zheng et al., 2024). One such hybridization event likely gave rise to the wild allotetraploid A. monticola (2n = 4x = 40, AABB), the only existing wild allotetraploid species in the genus Arachis and the presumed undomesticated ancestor of modern cultivated A. hypogaea, around 3,500 years ago (Moretzsohn et al., 2013; Yin et al., 2020; Pan et al., 2023). This relatively recent and complex evolutionary history makes peanut an attractive, yet challenging system for dissecting subgenome evolution and its implications for agronomic traits.

Whole‐genome duplication (WGD), resulting from allo‐ or autopolyploidization, has been a pervasive force shaping plant evolution, speciation, and crop domestication (Soltis et al., 2014; Van de Peer et al., 2021; Heslop‐Harrison et al., 2023). By duplicating entire chromosome sets, WGD generates extensive genetic redundancy, which can buffer deleterious mutations, enhance adaptive potential, and drive the functional divergence of duplicated genes (De Smet and Van de Peer, 2012; Tasdighian et al., 2017; Tong et al., 2025). In allopolyploid crops such as wheat (Triticum aestivum), rapeseed (Brassica napus), cotton (Gossypium hirsutum), and peanut, subgenome asymmetry is a pervasive feature, manifested in biased gene retention and loss, expression divergence, epigenetic regulation, and structural variation (Bird et al., 2018; Edger et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2019b). Previous studies indicated that such asymmetry is often established early during interspecific hybridization and is associated with differences in transposable element (TE) content and DNA methylation (Hollister and Gaut, 2009; Freeling et al., 2012; Vicient and Casacuberta, 2017; Bird et al., 2018). Furthermore, accumulating evidence indicates that higher‐order epigenomic features, including three‐dimensional (3D) chromatin architecture, chromatin accessibility, and histone modifications, also play critical roles in shaping and maintaining subgenome asymmetry (Wang et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2021a; Hu et al., 2024a; Huang et al., 2024). In A. hypogaea, gene expression, structural variation, and population genomics analyses provide evidence for subgenome asymmetry (Chen et al., 2019; Yin et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2022; Pan et al., 2023; Lu et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025). However, subgenome asymmetry in peanut remains incompletely characterized, particularly across epigenetic modifications, chromatin accessibility, and 3D genome architecture.

With a relatively large genome size (~2.8 Gb) and high TE content (~77%), A. hypogaea presents considerable challenges for accurate genome assembly (Pan et al., 2023). Early efforts focused on its two diploid progenitors, A. duranensis and A. ipaensis, providing essential reference frameworks (Bertioli et al., 2016), followed by the wild allotetraploid A. monticola (Yin et al., 2020). Advances in long‐reads sequencing (ONT and PacBio) and scaffolding technologies (Hi‐C and BioNano) have enabled multiple A. hypogaea genome assemblies, including the first telomere‐to‐telomere assembly of cultivar YZ9102 (Bertioli et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2019; Zhuang et al., 2019; Newman et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2025c; Zhao et al., 2025). In parallel, pan‐genomes, which capture the full genetic diversity of one species, have proven powerful for uncovering presence–absence variations (PAVs), structural variants, and novel genes underlying phenotypic diversity, thereby accelerating crop improvement (Della Coletta et al., 2021; Schreiber et al., 2024). In Fabaceae, large‐scale pan‐genome studies have markedly advanced our understanding of genetic diversity, revealing extensive dispensable genes and structural variation (Hu et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025a). Recently, a peanut pan‐genome integrating eight high‐quality genomes was constructed, comprising 50,097 pan‐gene families, and the impact of structural variation on seed size was systematically elucidated (Zhao et al., 2025). However, the limited sampling used for pan‐genome construction captures only part of the spectrum of intraspecific genetic variation, thereby constraining robust assessment of PAV frequency and their contributions to complex traits.

In this study, we selected Zhonghua 5 (ZH05), an elite high‐yield peanut cultivar widely grown in China since the 1990s and a core breeding parent of 35 improved varieties, as the reference for developing integrative genomic resources (Table S1; Liao et al., 2010). Specifically, we aimed to (i) generate a high‐quality chromosome‐scale genome assembly; (ii) systematically characterize subgenome asymmetry through multi‐omics and population genomics analyses; (iii) construct a species‐wide pan‐genome by integrating 1,286 accessions to comprehensively profile PAVs and genetic diversity; and (iv) dissect the genetic basis of high yield in ZH05 using a RIL population derived from this cultivar. Together, these genomic resources expand our understanding of peanut genome evolution and subgenome asymmetry, establish a comprehensive genomic framework, and offer a valuable foundation for molecular breeding in peanut.

RESULTS

High‐quality genome assembly and annotation of ZH05

A total of 89.81 Gb of PacBio HiFi long reads were generated and used for preliminary assemblies (Figures S1, S2; Table S2). The assembly produced by Hifiasm was further polished using both HiFi long reads and Illumina short reads (231.02 Gb), yielding 482 contigs (Tables S2, S3). Hi‐C data (364.54 Gb) were then utilized to anchor 95 contigs onto 20 chromosomes, corresponding to the A subgenome (SubA: Chr01 to Chr10) and the B subgenome (SubB: Chr11 to Chr20) (Figure 1A, B; Table S2). Subsequently, 13 gaps were closed by integrating alternative assemblies, and 13.97 Mb of homoeologous replacement (HR) sequences between the two subgenomes were incorporated (Figures 1D, S3; Tables S3–S6). The final ZH05 genome assembly spans 2,622.85 Mb, with contig N50 of 57.94 Mb, and 98.81% of sequences (2,591.61 Mb) anchored to 20 chromosomes (Figures 1A, S4; Table S6).

The quality of the ZH05 genome assembly was comprehensively evaluated using multiple complementary approaches. Over 99.79% of resequencing reads and 94.87% of RNA‐seq reads were successfully aligned to the genome. BUSCO analysis recovered 99.26% of Embryophyta and 97.56% of Fabales conserved genes, and the assembly achieved an LAI score of 22.58, meeting gold‐standard reference criteria (Figure S5; Tables S7, S8). Together, these assessments indicated high continuity, completeness, and accuracy of the ZH05 genome, representing a substantial improvement over most previously published Arachis genomes (Table S9).

The ZH05 genome contains 2,073.37 Mb (79.05%) of repetitive sequences, including 61.35% retroelements and 8.64% DNA transposons (Figure 1A, C; Table S10). Gene annotation resulted in 73,718 annotated protein‐coding genes, of which 99.59% were anchored to 20 chromosomes and 75.83% were supported by RNA‐seq evidence (Figure S6; Table S11). BUSCO evaluation recovered 99.69% of Embryophyta and 98.10% of Fabales genes, supporting the high completeness of gene annotation (Table S7). Moreover, 93.34% of genes were functionally annotated against public protein databases (Figure S7). Collectively, these results showed a high‐quality genome assembly and annotation of ZH05, providing a valuable genomic resource for advancing research in evolutionary biology, functional genomics, and molecular breeding in A. hypogaea.

Comparative genomics and phylogenetic relationships

To investigate evolutionary relationships among Arachis genomes, a phylogenomic analysis was performed, with SubA and SubB treated as independent evolutionary units (Table S12). Across all genomes and subgenomes, 41,639 gene families were identified, including 2,661 single‐copy orthogroups (Figure S8; Table S13). Phylogenetic trees reconstruction based on these single‐copy orthologues clearly resolved the SubA and SubB into two distinct clades (Figure 2A). Within the SubA clade, ZH05 clustered with Chinese cultivars, including Fuhuasheng, Shitouqi, and YZ9102. In the SubB clade, ZH05 grouped with Fuhuasheng and Shitouqi, while YZ9102 grouped with non‐Chinese accessions, possibly reflecting the divergent hybrid origin of YZ9102 involving wild Arachis species. Notably, A. monticola occupies an intermediate position between Chinese and non‐Chinese lineages in both subgenomes, which may reflect distinct allopolyploidization events and domestication trajectories contributing to the divergence of the two A. hypogaea subspecies (Yin et al., 2020; Zheng et al., 2024). Synteny analysis further showed that chromosomal collinearity is highly conserved from the two diploid progenitors through A. monticola to modern A. hypogaea (Figure 2B).

Genome‐wide structural variations

Pairwise whole‐genome alignments showed the highest sequence‐level collinearity between ZH05 and YZ9102, followed by BaileyII and Tifrunner (Figure 2C; Table S14). Only a few large‐size inversions and translocations were detected between ZH05 and YZ9102, supporting the high structural accuracy of the ZH05 assembly (Figure 2C). Structural variation (SV) analysis identified 45.65 Mb of SVs relative to YZ9102, 67.52 Mb relative to BaileyII, 92.87 Mb relative to Tifrunner, and 298.74 Mb relative to Shitouqi, with the latter likely reflecting its potential misassemblies or scaffolding errors (Figure S4; Tables S9, S14). SVs were more abundant in SubB, and several large inversions were validated by Hi‐C contact pattern disruptions (Figure 2C). In total, excluding Shitouqi, 2,482 genes overlapped with SVs, including 864 located in SubA and 1,618 located in SubB (Figures S9, S10). Functional enrichment analysis indicated that SV‐associated genes were enriched in pathways related to secondary metabolism, energy conversion, and developmental regulation (Figure S11), reflecting the contribution of SV to genome diversity among cultivated peanut accessions.

Subgenome asymmetry in transposable elements, gene expression, and DNA methylation

To characterize subgenome asymmetry in peanut, we first examined TEs' composition and found a higher TE abundance in SubB than in SubA (Table S10). K‐mer abundance analysis clearly separated the 20 chromosomes into SubA and SubB, reflecting distinct repetitive sequence landscapes between the two subgenomes (Figure 3A). The analysis of LTR insertion times revealed more recent transposon activity in A. hypogaea compared with its diploid progenitors and A. monticola (Figure 3B). Notably, SubB showed two peaks of Ty3/Gypsy‐type LTR insertions, representing two waves of LTR expansion that differ in number or magnitude from those observed in its diploid progenitors and in A. monticola (Figure 3B, C). Furthermore, gene‐flanking regions in SubB harbored a higher density of TEs than those in SubA (Figure 3D), suggesting a stronger influence of TE activity on the genomic landscape of SubB.

At the transcriptomic level, analysis across eight tissues showed that overall gene expression levels were higher in SubA than in SubB (Figure S12). In contrast, among 23,846 homoeologous gene pairs identified between the two subgenomes, expression profiling revealed a predominant bias toward SubB (Figures 3E, S13; Table S15). Then, weighted gene co‐expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed and 25 modules were identified, with slightly more hub genes located in SubB than in SubA (Figure S14; Table S16). Homoeologous genes in SubB showed stronger intra‐subgenomic co‐expression connectivity, whereas genes in SubA showed more inter‐subgenomic connections with SubB genes (Figure 3F), indicating distinct regulatory network architectures between the two subgenomes.

DNA methylation patterns were further assessed in gene bodies and their 2‐kb upstream promoter regions. All three methylation contexts (CG, CHG, and CHH) were consistently higher in the SubB, with the most pronounced differences observed in promoter regions (Figures 3G, S15). To assess the potential influence of TEs, homoeologous gene pairs were classified based on the presence or absence of promoter‐associated TE insertions. Genes with promoter TEs showed elevated methylation levels in both promoters and gene bodies, with a relatively greater increase observed in SubA (Figure 3H). Despite these epigenetic differences, no clear association was observed between promoter TE insertions or methylation variation and gene expression levels. However, homoeologous gene pairs with asymmetric TE insertions displayed a higher proportion of subgenome‐biased expression, although without a consistent directional preference (Figure 3I). Together, these results reveal coordinated, yet complex asymmetry between SubA and SubB in TE dynamics, DNA methylation, and transcriptional regulation, highlighting divergent regulatory landscapes between the two subgenomes and suggesting that TE‐driven epigenetic remodeling may underlie subgenome‐specific regulatory divergence in peanut.

Subgenome asymmetry in chromatin accessibility and 3D genome architecture

To investigate regulatory asymmetry between peanut subgenomes, we performed ATAC‐seq to profile genome‐wide chromatin accessibility. In total, 506.20 million reads were generated, enabling the identification of 24,867 accessible chromatin regions (ACRs), including 12,002 in SubA and 12,865 in SubB (Figure 4A; Table S17). Despite the slightly higher number of ACRs in SubB, SubA showed a higher overall density of ACRs (Table S17). Chromatin accessibility around ACRs and transcription start sites (TSSs) of the homoeologous gene pair was stronger in SubA than in SubB (Figure 4B). Classification of homoeologous pairs based on the presence or absence of promoter ACRs showed that promoter accessibility influenced expression bias between homoeologs, with a stronger effect observed in SubB (Figure 4C). Promoter‐associated ACRs in SubA also showed lower DNA methylation and slightly stronger accessibility signals than those in SubB (Figure 4D). Motif enrichment analysis identified distinct transcription factor binding motifs in ACRs between the two subgenomes, and functional enrichment of ACR‐associated genes suggested subgenome‐specific biological roles (Figures S16, S17).

To examine higher‐order chromatin architecture, Hi‐C data were generated, yielding 2.65 billion valid interaction read pairs. Genome‐wide interaction maps revealed stronger intra‐subgenomic interactions within SubB compared with SubA, and intra‐subgenomic interactions were stronger than inter‐subgenomic interactions (Figure 4E, F). Notably, the smallest chromosome, Chr08, displayed relatively strong interactions with multiple chromosomes (Figure 4F). At 100‐kb resolution, A/B compartment analysis revealed that the proportion of A compartments was higher in SubB than that in SubA (Figures 1A, 4G; Table S18). Among homoeologous gene pairs, transitions from B compartments in SubA to A compartments in SubB were more frequent than the reverse pattern, suggesting asymmetric chromatin compartment architecture between the two subgenomes (Figure 4G). At 25‐kb resolution, topologically associating domains (TADs) were identified, with SubB contained more numerous and larger TADs than SubA (Figure 4H; Table S19). Consistent with this, insulation score analysis showed that TAD boundaries were stronger and more sharply defined in SubB, suggesting a more stable and mature 3D chromatin architecture (Figure 4I; Table S19). Motif composition of TAD boundaries and functional enrichment of boundary‐associated genes also differed between the two subgenomes (Figures S18, S19). At 5‐kb and 10‐kb resolutions, 15,444 chromatin loops were identified, including 7,200 in SubA and 8,422 in SubB (Table S20). These loops were classified into genic–genic (G–G), genic–intergenic (G–I), and intergenic–intergenic (I–I) categories, with the SubB containing more G–G loops, which may indicate a denser gene‐centered chromatin interaction network in the SubB (Figure 4J; Table S20). In both subgenomes, genes located in A compartment, TAD boundaries, and loop anchors showed higher expression levels, lower DNA methylation, and greater chromatin accessibility than those in B compartments, TAD interiors, and non‐loop regions (Figure 4K–M). Moreover, under comparable chromatin states, genes in SubA generally showed higher expression, lower DNA methylation, and stronger accessibility than their counterparts in SubB (Figure 4K–M).

Together, these results reveal pronounced asymmetry between SubA and SubB in chromatin accessibility and 3D genome architecture, suggesting a more structurally organized chromatin architecture in SubB and more locally active regulatory landscapes in SubA.

Evolutionary divergence and subgenome‐biased selection

Selection pressure was evaluated using Ka/Ks ratios across two evolutionary stages. During the polyploidization phase (from the diploid progenitors to A. monticola), SubA showed stronger purifying selection (lower Ka/Ks) than SubB. However, a larger proportion number of homoeologous genes in SubB lacked sufficient nucleotide substitutions in the aligned regions to allow Ka/Ks calculation, likely reflecting differential evolutionary constraints (Figure 5A; Table S21). During the domestication phase (from A. monticola to A. hypogaea), no significant Ka/Ks difference was observed between the two subgenomes among genes with valid estimates. Nevertheless, SubB retained a higher proportion of genes with calculable Ka/Ks values (SubA: 36.87%, SubB: 40.79%), implying more frequent sequence divergence in SubB (Figure 5A; Table S21). In addition, divergence homoeologous gene pairs between the two subgenomes was strongly associated with gene expression and DNA methylation, with more conserved genes (lower Ka/Ks) showing higher expression and lower methylation levels, indicating coordinated evolutionary and regulatory constraints (Figure 5B, C).

To further investigate genome‐wide variation and population‐level selection in A. hypogaea, whole‐genome resequencing data of 779 accessions were obtained from two high‐quality studies, representing the most comprehensive peanut population resource to date (Table S22; Lu et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2024). In total, 20.94 million SNPs and 2.24 million Indels were identified specific to A. hypogaea, among which 1.27 million SNPs and 0.18 million Indels were retained as high‐quality variants (MAF > 0.01). SubB harbored more variants and showed a higher variant density than SubA (SubA: 474,142 SNPs, 0.41 SNP/kb, and 57,265 Indels, 0.05 Indel/kb; SubB: 768,522 SNPs, 0.53 SNP/kb, and 113,961 Indels, 0.08 Indel/kb), suggesting higher genetic diversity in SubB. Phylogenetic and population structure analyses revealed that wild Arachis and foreign A. hypogaea accessions formed two clusters distinct from Chinese accessions. Also, Chinese accessions were further broadly resolved into two subgroups corresponding to their subspecies background. Two subsets of accessions showing irregular subspecies clustering suggested extensive inter‐subspecific hybridization during modern Chinese peanut breeding, including the cross‐subspecies origin of ZH05 (Figure 5D). Phylogenetic incongruence between the two subgenomes highlighted their asymmetric evolutionary trajectories (Figure 5D). Principal component analysis (PCA) further supported partial separation between Chinese and non‐Chinese accessions and revealed substantial overlap between northern and southern Chinese groups, suggesting frequent gene flow among Chinese production regions and contributions from internationally introduced germplasm during peanut breeding (Figure 5E, F). Linkage disequilibrium (LD) decay revealed a more rapid decay in SubB than in SubA, indicating historically higher recombination rates in SubB (Figure 5G).

Genomic signatures of selection were detected via selective sweep scans across three contrasts: (i) subsp. fastigiata versus subsp. hypogaea, (ii) Chinese versus non‐Chinese accessions, and (iii) northern versus southern Chinese accessions (Figure S20). A total of 4,634, 4,158, and 2,281 genes were identified under selection in each contrast, respectively (Figure 5H; Table S23). Except for the north versus south comparison, SubB harbored more selected genes than SubA, highlighting subgenome‐biased selection. Moreover, the limited overlap of selected genes between the two subgenomes further underscores their asymmetric evolutionary responses (Figure 5H). Functional enrichment analysis indicated that selected genes in each contrast were enriched in distinct biological pathways, broadly consistent with their evolutionary trajectories and biological characteristics (Figures S21–S23). Collectively, these results demonstrate asymmetric evolutionary dynamics and subgenome‐biased selection in cultivated peanut, with SubB showing higher genetic diversity, faster recombination, and stronger selection signals, reflecting its pivotal role in both natural adaptation and breeding improvement.

Pan‐genome construction and gene presence–absence variation

To comprehensively capture genomic diversity in A. hypogaea, a pan‐genome was constructed using de novo contigs assembled from whole‐genome resequencing data of 1,286 accessions (Table S22). Individual assemblies had an average length of 1,992.79 Mb, with a mean contig N50 of 3.89 kb (Figure S24). Comparative mapping to the ZH05 reference identified an average of 3.72 Mb of unaligned sequences per accession (Figure S24; Table S22), which were extracted as the non‐reference sequence (NRS) component of the pan‐genome based on the map‐to‐pan strategy. After redundancy removal and contaminant filtering, a total of 849.82 Mb of NRS (comprising 1,168,429 contigs) were retained. Further annotation revealed that 85.93% of the NRS consisted of repetitive sequences and 18,484 protein‐coding genes were predicted. Incorporating both the ZH05 reference and NRS, the final A. hypogaea pan‐genome spans 3,441.42 Mb and encodes a total of 91,898 protein‐coding genes.

Gene PAV analysis of 743 A. hypogaea accessions identified 82,750 pan‐genes (90.05% of total), including 44,721 core genes (54.04%), 27,747 softcore genes (33.53%), 5,407 dispensable genes (5.63%), and 4,875 private genes (5.89%) (Figure 6A–C; Table S18). Subgenome‐specific patterns were observed: SubA harbored a higher proportion of core genes, whereas SubB contained more variable genes, indicating greater gene content plasticity in SubB (Figure 6D). NRS‐derived genes showed greater alignment frequency and sequence similarity to SubA genes (Figure 6E). Gene categories showed a clear evolutionary and functional gradient: from core to private genes, UniProt‐annotation rates and average gene length progressively decreased (Figure 6F, G). Core genes displayed lower nucleotide diversity and higher expression levels compared with softcore and dispensable genes in the two subgenomes (Figure 6H, I), consistent with stronger purifying selection inferred from Ka/Ks analysis (Figure 6J). Subgenome differences were also observed in nucleotide diversity, gene expression, and Ka/Ks ratios, especially among dispensable gene (Figure 6H–J). DNA methylation profiling revealed contrasting subgenomic patterns: Dispensable genes showed slightly higher methylation levels in SubA but marginally lower methylation in SubB (Figure 6K). Functional enrichment analyses revealed that core and softcore genes were enriched in fundamental biological processes, whereas dispensable and private genes were associated with stress responses and specialized metabolism (Figure 6L, M). Overall, the comprehensive A. hypogaea pan‐genome reveals extensive genomic diversity while highlighting persistent subgenome asymmetry in gene presence–absence variation and evolutionary features.

Genetic dissection of seed traits in the elite cultivar ZH05

To elucidate the genetic basis underlying the high yield of ZH05, a RIL population was developed from a cross between ZH05 and an exotic line ICGV86699 (Reddy et al., 1996). Using whole‐genome resequencing data (average coverage ~6.56×), a high‐density genetic map was constructed, containing 3,157 bin markers spanning 2,070.83 cM (Figure 7C; Tables S24, S25). Phenotypic traits were evaluated across eight environments, enabling the identification of QTLs associated with seed size (HSW, hundred‐seed weight; SW, seed width; and SL, seed length), oil content, and fatty acid composition (Figures 7C, S25–36; Tables S26, S27).

Four major and stable QTLs for seed size were consistently detected: qSS.Chr07 (0–2.13 Mb), explaining an average of 14.42% of the variation for HSW and 15.13% for SW; qSS.Chr08 (56.0–58.06 Mb), explaining 15.01% for HSW and 15.40% for SW; qSS.Chr16.2 (136.95–140.73 Mb), explaining 13.92% for HSW and 22.97% for SL; and qSS.Chr16.3 (143.03–147.58 Mb), explaining 15.42% for HSW and 14.69% for SW (Figures 7A–C, S25–S27; Table S27). Among these, the causal genes underlying qSS.Chr07 and qSS.Chr16.3 have previously been fine‐mapped. Consistent with earlier reports, the same mutations were detected in the RIL population, and the corresponding genotypes showed significant phenotypic differences (Figure 7D; Zhao et al., 2023; Lu et al., 2025). In addition, several genes harboring nonsynonymous mutations were identified within the qSS.Chr08 and qSS.Chr16.2 intervals, and haplotype analysis in the natural population highlighted several promising candidate genes (Figure 7E), whose functional roles remain to be experimentally validated. For oil content, no stable QTLs were consistently detected across environments, likely reflecting a strong environmental influence on this trait. However, two loci with relatively large effects were detected on Chr08 (44.45–45.12 Mb and 47.03–48.10 Mb), explaining 15.83% and 19.19% of the phenotypic variation, respectively, and located close to previously reported major oil‐content QTL regions (Figures 7C, S28; Table S27; Liu et al., 2020; Sun et al., 2022; Mo et al., 2025). It should be noted that oil‐related traits are strongly influenced by environmental conditions (e.g., temperature and other climatic factors), which may obscure stable genetic effects across different environments. QTLs associated with fatty acid composition were also identified, including several pleiotropic loci that could be broadly classified into two groups: (i) loci primarily affecting unsaturated fatty acids (e.g., qFA.Chr09.2 and qFA.Chr11) and (ii) loci associated with long‐chain fatty acids (e.g., qFA.Chr13, qFA.Chr14.2, qFA.Chr16.2, and qFA.Chr18.2) (Figures 7C, S29–S36; Table S27). Notably, the well‐known gene FAD2A resides within qFA.Chr09.2; however, the classical 448‐bp G‐to‐A mutation allele associated with elevated oleic acid content was not carried by ZH05.

Collectively, the pyramiding of multiple high‐effect seed size QTLs likely contributed to the superior yield performance of ZH05.

DISCUSSION

Peanut (A. hypogaea), the only domesticated species in the genus Arachis, originated from a relatively recent allopolyploidization event involving two diploid progenitors and a wild tetraploid lineage (Bertioli et al., 2016, 2019; Yin et al., 2020; Pan et al., 2023). Its agronomic significance and evolutionary complexity highlight the need for comprehensive genomic resources that capture structural, functional, and adaptive diversity. Here, we generated a high‐quality genome assembly for the elite cultivar ZH05, integrating multi‐omics profiling, population genomics, and a species‐wide pan‐genome, to explore the evolutionary dynamics of peanut, and revealed the genetic basis of the high performance of ZH05.

The first peanut reference genome provided a foundation for studies of gene evolution, structural variation, and domestication, and subsequent population resequencing efforts greatly expanded understanding of genetic diversity and trait‐associated loci (Bertioli et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2022; Lu et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025). Building on these advances, we generated a highly contiguous chromosome‐scale genome assembly for ZH05, representing one of the most complete peanut genomes currently available and providing a robust reference for genetic and functional analyses (Figure 1A; Table S9). To further capture genomic diversity, resequencing data from 1,286 accessions were integrated to construct a large‐scale peanut pan‐genome containing 18,484 novel genes and 849.82 Mb of non‐reference sequences (Table S22). Compared with the previously reported pan‐genome based on eight high‐quality genomes, our data set specifically focuses on cultivated peanut and integrates substantially broader sequencing resources (Zhao et al., 2025). The previous study benefits from higher assembly quality and reliability, enabling precise characterization of large‐scale structural variations at the genome level (Zhao et al., 2025). In contrast, although the non‐reference sequences in our study are relatively fragmented due to the nature of de novo assembly from resequencing data, they nonetheless capture substantial genetic information and significantly expand the genomic repertoire of cultivated peanut (Figure S24). Population‐scale analyses revealed extensive genomic variation across the peanut genome. More than 23 million of SNPs and InDels together form a comprehensive landscape of genetic polymorphism, and 77.26% of annotated genes harbor coding‐sequence variation, indicating widespread functional diversification. At the pan‐genome level, over 91,000 genes were classified into core and dispensable components, and numerous non‐reference sequences containing novel genes highlight substantial gene‐content variation among peanut accessions. Such extensive genomic variation provides a rich genetic basis for adaptive evolution in peanut, while these integrated genomic resources greatly expand the spectrum of genetic diversity and facilitate the dissection of genotype–phenotype relationships for future breeding.

Subgenome asymmetry represents a fundamental feature of polyploid genomes and is often associated with differences in gene expression, TE accumulation, epigenetic regulation, and evolutionary rates between subgenomes, as reported in cotton, wheat, and Brassica (Cheng et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2020; Li et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2022). Consistent with previous studies in peanut, SubB contains a higher proportion of TEs and shows elevated DNA methylation levels relative to SubA, and more homoeologous gene pairs show expression bias toward SubB, although the proportion of biased pairs is lower than that reported in many other polyploid crops (Figure 3E, G; Li et al., 2014; Wu et al., 2018; Ma et al., 2024). Interestingly, when all genes are considered, genes located in SubA show higher overall expression levels than those in SubB, consistent with differences in DNA methylation (Figure S12; Yu et al., 2025). This discrepancy between genome‐wide expression patterns and homoeolog expression bias suggests that the two subgenomes contribute differently to transcriptional regulation and are shaped by distinct epigenetic environments. Transposable element activity appears to play a key role in shaping this asymmetry. One wave of Ty3/Gypsy‐type LTR retrotransposon amplification was identified specifically in SubB, indicating that TE expansion may have substantially reshaped its genomic and regulatory landscape (Figure 3B, C). The higher TE load and associated DNA methylation in SubB may therefore represent a potential source of regulatory variation that could have contributed to adaptation to environmental pressures and human‐mediated selection during peanut domestication, like in wheat (Yuan et al., 2020; Xie et al., 2023; Jia et al., 2025). Evolutionary analyses revealed stage‐dependent selection dynamics during peanut evolution: SubA experienced stronger purifying selection during the early diploid‐to‐tetraploid transition, whereas SubB accumulated more mutations during domestication (Figure 5A). Consistently, population genomic analyses show that SubB harbors greater genetic variation, contains more regions under selection, and shows faster linkage disequilibrium decay while maintaining relatively high nucleotide diversity (Figure 5H).

Beyond sequence variation, higher‐order chromatin organization has emerged as an important regulator of gene expression and genome evolution in polyploid species (Wang et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2021a; Huang et al., 2024). However, most previous epigenomic studies in peanut have largely focused on comparisons between cultivated peanut and its diploid progenitors or on specific biological processes (Zhang et al., 2021b; Li et al., 2023, 2026; Wang et al., 2025b; Yu et al., 2025). These studies have provided important insights into chromatin architecture and its evolutionary origins. In contrast, our study integrates Hi‐C with additional regulatory layers, including chromatin accessibility (ATAC‐seq), DNA methylation, and transcriptomic data, enabling a more comprehensive dissection of subgenome asymmetry across multiple regulatory dimensions. This integrative framework allows us to link higher‐order chromatin organization with transcriptional regulation and epigenetic states, providing new insights into how structural and regulatory features jointly contribute to subgenome divergence in peanut. ATAC‐seq analyses revealed that SubA shows a more active chromatin accessibility landscape, indicating stronger transcriptional regulatory potential and a more permissive epigenetic environment (Figure 4B). This pattern is consistent with the generally higher expression levels of genes in SubA, whereas SubB appears to be more strongly influenced by TEs and accessible chromatin regions, which may contribute to the observed patterns of homoeolog expression bias (Figure 3I, 4C). In contrast, Hi‐C analyses revealed that SubB shows stronger global chromatin interaction frequencies, a higher proportion of A compartments, and more chromatin loops, together with relatively stable large‐scale chromatin organization characterized by conserved TAD boundaries (Figure 4E–I). These findings suggest that the two subgenomes may use distinct regulatory strategies: SubA appears to facilitate more dynamic local gene regulation through active chromatin accessibility, whereas SubB maintains a more stable global chromatin architecture. Moreover, the smallest chromosome, Chr08, shows unusually frequent inter‐chromosomal interactions, suggesting a potentially distinct evolutionary origin or regulatory role within the peanut genome (Figure 4F; Du et al., 2026). Collectively, these features suggest that the higher TE load in SubB, together with recent Ty3/Gypsy‐type LTR insertions, contributes to a more repressive epigenetic environment, larger and more compact TAD structures, and stronger intra‐subgenome interactions. In contrast, the TE‐poor SubA maintains a more open and transcriptionally permissive chromatin state.

The genetic dissection of the superior agronomic performance of ZH05 further illustrates the practical value of the genomic resources generated in this study, as several stable quantitative trait loci controlling seed size‐related traits were identified using a high‐density genetic linkage map combined with multi‐environment phenotypic data (Figures 7C, S25–S27; Table S27). The presence of multiple environmentally stable QTLs suggests that the high yield of ZH05 likely results from the accumulation of favorable alleles affecting seed weight and morphology, consistent with its widespread use as a parental line in breeding programs. In contrast, ZH05 lacks the favorable high‐oleic alleles in FAD2A and FAD2B, explaining its moderate oleic acid content and reflecting historical breeding priorities that emphasized yield and agronomic stability rather than oil quality (Jung et al., 2000; López et al., 2000; Chu et al., 2009). Although several candidate genes within QTL intervals harbor nonsynonymous mutations or distinct haplotypes, their functional roles remain to be experimentally validated in future studies.

Overall, this study establishes an integrated genomic framework for peanut by generating a high‐quality chromosome‐scale reference genome for ZH05 and a large‐scale pan‐genome capturing extensive diversity across cultivated populations. Integrative analyses of comparative genomics, population genetics, transcriptomics, and epigenomics reveal pervasive subgenome asymmetry spanning sequence evolution, epigenetic regulation, and three‐dimensional genome organization. These resources and insights deepen our understanding of polyploid genome evolution and provide a solid foundation for molecular breeding and crop improvement in peanut.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant materials and DNA/RNA extraction

The A. hypogaea cv. Zhonghua 5 (ZH05) was planted in the experimental field of the Oil Crops Research Institute of the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (OCRI‐CAAS), Wuhan, Hubei, China, during the 2023 and 2024 growing season. The young leaf tissue, stem, root, peg, shoot, radicle, and seed samples were collected from the same ZH05 plant and immediately flash‐frozen in liquid nitrogen. The RIL mapping population consisting of 170 lines was developed from a cross between ZH05 and ICGV86699. The female parent ZH05 was an improved cultivar with high yield and the male parent ICGV86699 was an advanced breeding line from the International Crop Research Institute for the Semi‐Arid Tropic (ICRISAT), India. The field planting trials were conducted in eight different environments (Table S26). The young leaf tissues from each of the 170 RILs along with the parental lines were also collected.

High‐quality genomic DNA was extracted from leaves using a modified CTAB method (Allen et al., 2006). Total RNA was isolated using the TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen Life Technologies, Carlsbad, California, USA). RNA purity and integrity were monitored using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies, Wilmington, DE) and a Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, California, USA). RNA contamination was assessed using a 1.5% agarose gel.

Whole‐genome, RNA, and Hi‐C sequencing

DNA extracted from young leaves of ZH05 and 170 RILs was used to construct a library for sequencing on the Illumina paired‐end high‐throughput sequencing platform NovaSeq. 6000 and DNBSEQ‐T7 with a read length of 150 bp. The library was generated following the standard library building process. For the construction of PacBio libraries, a young leaf DNA sample of ZH05 was sheared using a Covaris ultrasonicator. Magnetic beads were utilized for the enrichment and purification of large DNA fragments. The library was generated following the standard library building process. The libraries constructed by the SMRTbell prep kit 3.0 and the Revio Polymerase kit were sequenced using the PacBio Revio system.

RNA‐seq libraries were prepared using the TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA). To select cDNA fragments that are approximately 200 bp in length, the library fragments were subjected to purification using the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Beverly, CA) and quantified using the Agilent High‐Sensitivity DNA assay on a Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent). The sequencing library was sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq NovaSeq. 6000 platform.

Leaf tissues were collected and utilized for Hi‐C, as described in previous studies (Rao et al., 2014). First, the young leaves were immersed in MS buffer containing a 1% formaldehyde solution. Next, the DNA from the leaves was extracted and digested using the DpnII restriction enzyme. An Illumina paired‐end sequencing library was constructed with an insert fragment size of 350 bp and sequenced using the Illumina HiSeq X‐Ten platform. All the extraction, database construction, and sequencing work were carried out by Novogene Co., Ltd (Beijing) and BGI Genomics Co., Ltd (Shenzhen).

Genome assembly and quality assessments

Pacbio hifi long reads were used for preliminary assemblies with four assemblers, including Hifiasm (v0.19.9‐r616) (Cheng et al., 2022), HiCanu (v2.2) (Nurk et al., 2020), LJA (v0.2) (Bankevich et al., 2022), and Verkko (v1.4) (Rautiainen et al., 2023), with default parameters. Among these, the assembly produced by Hifiasm software showed the highest contiguity and accuracy, and was selected as the primary framework for subsequent refinement. The assembly was polished using NextPolish2 (v0.2.0) (Hu et al., 2024b) software with long reads and Pilon (v1.23) (Walker et al., 2014) software with short reads. A pseudo‐chromosome was constructed using Hi‐C data through the Juicer (v1.6) (Durand et al., 2016) and the 3D‐DNA (Dudchenko et al., 2017) pipeline. The default parameters were used for grouping, ordering, and orienting the contigs. The results were polished using the Juicebox Assembly Tools (v1.11.08). Gap‐closing was performed by referencing alternative assemblies using minimap2 (v2.1.1‐r341) (Li, 2018) with parameters “‐x asm5”, followed by manual sequence filling based on visual inspection in IGVtools (v2.162) (Robinson et al., 2017). For HR filling, paired‐end short reads from ZH05 were mapped to the assembly to identify the HR regions using BWA (v0.7.17) (Li and Durbin, 2009), and then HR sequences were compared to the diploid progenitors (A. duranensis and A. ipaensis) to determine their position and orientation in the other subgenome.

To examine the assembly integrity, the long reads were aligned to the assembly using minimap2 and the short reads were aligned to the assembly using BWA. RNA‐seq data were also aligned back to the genome to verify the quality of the genome assembly using Hisat2 (v2.1.0) (Kim et al., 2019). The quality of the assembled genome was assessed using Benchmarking Universal Single‐Copy Orthologs (BUSCO; v5.4.3) (Manni et al., 2021) with embryophyta_odb10 (creation date: 2020‐09‐10) and fabales_odb10 (creation date: 2020‐08‐05). The LTR_FINDER (v1.07) (Xu and Wang, 2007) and LTRharvest (v1.5.10) (Ellinghaus et al., 2008) software packages were utilized to identify and annotate LTRs in the assembly. These results were integrated and used to calculate the LAI with LTR_retriever (v2.9.0) (Ou et al., 2018).

Genome annotation

Prior to gene prediction, annotation of the genome repeat sequences was performed using the EDTA (v2.0.1) (Ou et al., 2019) pipeline. The first step involved conducting de novo identification of repetitive elements, where individual TE libraries were constructed with parameters “‐species others ‐‐sensitive 1 ‐‐anno 1 ‐‐evaluate 1”. These libraries served as crucial inputs for the subsequent step of structural annotation of the TEs. RepeatMasker (v4.1.1) (Price et al., 2005) with the parameters “‐e rmblast ‐s ‐gff ‐nolow ‐no_is ‐norna” was applied to mask the whole‐genome sequences using the TE library constructed by EDTA. DeepTE (v3.7.1) (Yan et al., 2020) software was utilized to accurately identify the unknown type LTRs that were identified in the EDTA pipeline.

Protein‐coding genes were predicted using three methods: ab initio gene prediction, homoeologous prediction, and transcriptome‐assisted gene prediction. The ab initio prediction was performed using Augustus (v3.5.0) (Stanke et al., 2004) software, with models trained on a set of high‐quality proteins derived from RNA‐seq data. For homoeology‐based prediction, reference protein sequences of Arachis species, including Amon2, Tifrunner, BaileyII, Shitouqi, and Fuhuasheng, were aligned to the genome using Exonerate (v2.4.0) (Table S12; Slater and Birney, 2005). Transcriptome‐assisted prediction was conducted using the PASA (v2.4.1; Haas et al., 2008) pipeline to align transcripts to the assembled genome, enabling ORF and gene prediction. A consensus gene set was generated by merging all predictions from the three methods using Evidence Modeler (v1.1.1) (Haas et al., 2008) software.

Gene functions were inferred by aligning sequences to the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) NonRedundant (NR) and UniProt (Swiss‐Prot and TrEMBL) protein databases using Diamond (v2.1.8.162) (Buchfink et al., 2015) with an E‐value of 1e−10. Predicted protein sequences were then uploaded to KAAS (Moriya et al., 2007) to obtain the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) annotation and to EggNOG (Huerta‐Cepas et al., 2019) to obtain additional information. The protein domains were annotated using HMMER (v3.3.1) (Mistry et al., 2013) software based on Pfam databases with an E‐value of 1e−5. Gene ontology (GO) annotations for each gene were obtained from InterProScan (v5.75‐106.0) (Zdobnov and Apweiler, 2001) software.

Phylogenetic and synteny analyses

OrthoFinder (v2.5.5) (Emms and Kelly, 2019) along with Diamond software was used to identify common gene families (Table S13). Protein sequences of 2,661 single‐copy orthologous families were aligned using MUSCLE (v3.8.31) (Edgar, 2004). The phylogenetic relationship among these species was estimated using RAxML (v8.2.12) (Stamatakis, 2014). To investigate the degree of collinearity, the jcvi package (v1.414) (Tang et al., 2024) was used to identify syntenic blocks and gene pairs between the genomes using protein sequence. The collinearity dot‐plot and collinearity line‐plot were also constructed using the jcvi package.

Structural variant detection

The ZH05 genome assembly was aligned to the genomes of Amon2, Shitouqi, YZ9102, Tifrunner, and BaileyII, using minimap2 with parameters “‐a asm5 ‐‐eqx”. The collinearity, structural rearrangements, and local variations were identified using SyRI (v1.6.3) (Goel et al., 2019) software. The visualization of the results was carried out using plotsr (v1.1.1) software.

Transcriptome, ATAC, and 3D‐genomic analyses

For RNA‐seq analysis, FASTP (v0.23.4) (Chen, 2023) software was used to trim the paired‐end reads, removing adaptors, and short (< 100 bp) and low‐quality reads. Then, clean reads were aligned to the ZH05 genome reference using Hisat2 with the default settings. StringTie (v1.3.0) (Kovaka et al., 2019) was used to calculate the read counts and gene expression levels in TPM (transcripts per million).

For ATAC‐seq analysis, the clean reads were mapped to the ZH05 genome reference with bowtie2 (v2.5.4) (Langmead and Salzberg, 2012). MACS2 (v2.2.9.1) (Zhang et al., 2008) peak calling software was used to identify ATAC‐seq peaks. The genomic distribution of ACRs and associated genes was confirmed using ChIPseeker (v1.40.0) (Yu et al., 2015). ACR motifs were identified by using findMotifsGenome.pl in HOMER (v4.9.1) (Heinz et al., 2010) software.

For Hi‐C analysis, the clean reads were mapped to the ZH05 genome reference with Juicer software. Only the uniquely mapped paired‐end reads were used for subsequent analysis. The contact matrices were generated at different resolutions (5, 10, 25, 50, and 100 kb) using Juicer. The eigenvector program in Juicer was used to delineate A/B compartments at a 100 kb resolution. TAD‐like structures were identified using the hicFindTADs program embedded in HiCExplorer (v3.7.2) (Wolff et al., 2020) at 25 kb resolutions. Loops were identified using the hicDetectLoops program embedded in HiCExplorer at 5 and 10 kb resolutions, and merged using the hicMergeLoops program.

Population genetic diversity, structure, and selective sweep analyses

In this study, previously released whole‐genome resequencing data of 779 accessions from two high‐quality studies were reanalyzed using an updated high‐quality reference genome ZH05 (Table S22; Lu et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2024). Raw sequencing data were preprocessed using FASTP, and then clean data were aligned to the ZH05 reference genome using BWA. SNP and INDEL calling were performed using the GATK (v4.5.0.0) (McKenna et al., 2010) with the HaplotypeCaller method. Detected SNPs and Indels were then filtered using the VariantFiltration program of GATK. Only high‐quality SNPs were retained for subsequent analyses using vcftools (v0.1.14) (Danecek et al., 2011) with parameters “‐maf 0.01 ‐minQ 30 ‐max‐missing 0.1”.

The SNP data set was divided into two subgenomes of A. hypogaea for subsequent population analyses. A neighbor‐joining phylogeny was constructed based on the P distance matrix calculated by VCF2Dis (v1.47) (Xu et al., 2025) software. The population structure was analyzed using the ADMIXTURE (v1.3.0) (Alexander and Lange, 2011) program. The principal component analysis was performed with GCTA software (v1.94.1) (Yang et al., 2011). The LD decay was calculated for all pairs of variations using PopLDdecay (v3.43) (Zhang et al., 2019a).

Three metrics were used to identify the selective region of selection sweeps, including genetic Fst, the π ratio, and XP‐CLR. The calculation of the π ratio and Fst was accomplished using VCFtools with parameters “‐‐window‐pi 100,000 ‐‐window‐pi‐step 20,000”, and “‐‐fst‐window‐size 100,000 ‐‐fst‐window‐step 20,000”. XP‐CLR (v1.1.2) (Chen et al., 2010) was also applied to scan for selective regions with the parameter “‐‐ld 0.95 ‐‐size 100,000 ‐‐step 20,000”. The windows with the top 5% of values were identified as candidate sweep regions and windows filtered with at least two metrics were considered as sweep regions. For population‐based analyses, Chinese accessions were defined as germplasm resources, breeding lines, and cultivars developed or maintained in China; non‐Chinese accessions included germplasm resources, breeding lines, and cultivars collected outside China; and wild Arachis species referred to all Arachis taxa other than the cultivated tetraploid peanut (A. hypogaea).

Pan‐genome construction and PAV analyses

The pan‐genome was constructed using de novo assemblies generated from resequencing data of 1,286 accessions (Table S22). Each accession was de novo assembled from resequencing data using MEGAHIT (v1.2.9) (Li et al., 2015) software with the parameter “‐‐k‐list 65,85,105,125,145,165 ‐‐min‐contig‐len 500”. The assembled contigs (> 500 bp) from each individual assembly were aligned to the ZH05 reference using minimap2 and MUMmer (v4.0.0rc1) (Marçais et al., 2018). The unaligned contigs and contigs with an identity less than the threshold were extracted as non‐reference sequences. These sequences were then searched against the NT database using BLASTN (v2.15.0+) and those with best hits from outside the green plants, or with known plant mitochondrial or chloroplast genomes, were removed as possible contaminations. The clean non‐reference sequences obtained were then clustered using CD‐HIT (v4.8.1) (Li and Godzik, 2006) to remove redundancy. The sequence threshold was set to 90% in all of the above filtering steps. Finally, the non‐redundant and non‐reference sequences were merged with the ZH05 reference genome as the A. hypogaea pan‐genome. The same annotation pipelines were used to annotate the gene structure and function of the pan‐genome.

A total of 743 A. hypogaea accessions were aligned to the pan‐genome using BWA and then PanDepth (v2.25) (Table S22; Yu et al., 2024) to calculate CDS coverage. Genes with 2× coverage on at least 20% of the entire CDS region were considered present; otherwise, they were considered absent. By estimating the frequency of PAVs among all 743 accessions, genes were divided into two categories: core (present in all accessions) and variable genes. Four subcategories, core, softcore, dispensable, and private were defined as genes present in 100%, 97%–100%, 1%–97%, and < 1% of all accessions, respectively.

Linkage map construction and QTL mapping

A total of 170 RILs derived from the cross between ZH05 and ICGV86699 were sequenced. Using the ZH05 genome as the reference, raw sequencing reads were subjected to quality control using FASTP, reads mapping using BWA, and SNP calling using GATK following the procedures described above. SNPs were retained if they met the following criteria: (1) homozygous polymorphic sites between the two parents; (2) missing rate less than 50% across the RIL population; and (3) presence of only biallelic variants in the RIL population. The resulting high‐quality SNPs were used to construct a binmap with SNPbinner (Gonda et al., 2019). Genetic distances between bin markers were calculated, and a linkage map was generated using QTL IciMapping (v4.2.53) (Meng et al., 2015) software. QTL mapping for phenotypic traits was performed in IciMapping using the BIP (QTL mapping in bi‐parental populations) module with default settings. The scanning window size was set to 1 cM, and the logarithm of odds (LOD) threshold was determined by 1,000 permutations at P < 0.05. A LOD score of 2.5 was applied to detect putative QTLs related to seed‐associated traits.
