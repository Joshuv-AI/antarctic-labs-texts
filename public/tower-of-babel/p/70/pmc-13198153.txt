Outcome of Patients Presenting With High-Risk Pulmonary Embolism Treated With Mechanical Thrombectomy: A Subgroup Analysis From the STRIKE-PE Study
Authors: Andrew SP Sharp, Suhail Y Dohad, Ido Weinberg, Marcin Kurzyna, Hugh C Davis, Jakub Stepniewski, Paul E Perkowski, Andrew Holden, Frances M West, W Brent Keeling, Sahil A Parikh, John M Moriarty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHigh-risk pulmonary embolism (PE), defined as PE causing hemodynamic instability, remains associated with high acute mortality and currently warrants acute treatment with systemic thrombolysis. Mechanical thrombectomy can be considered where thrombolysis is inappropriate, as an alternative, or in addition to, but more prospective data are needed. This analysis evaluates the safety, effectiveness, functional, and quality of life (QoL) outcomes in high-risk PE patients who were treated using mechanical thrombectomy with computer-assisted vacuum thrombectomy (CAVT) devices.MethodsAmong the first 595 patients enrolled in the prospective, international STRIKE-PE study, 48 were identified as having high-risk PE. Outcomes included change in right-to-left ventricular ratio from baseline to 48-hour follow-up, 48-hour composite major adverse events (MAE), additional safety end points, and 90-day changes in functional and QoL assessments.ResultsMean right-to-left ventricular ratio decreased from 1.50 ± 0.36 to 1.02 ± 0.18 (P < .001). One study-defined MAE (major bleeding) occurred. One all-cause mortality and 1 symptomatic PE recurrence occurred within 30 days. Borg scale at rest improved from baseline to discharge (P < .001) and discharge to 90 days (P = .045). Borg scale after 6-minute walk test, 6-minute walk test distance, EQ-5D-5L index value, EuroQoL Visual Analog Scale, and Pulmonary Embolism QoL score improved (all P < .001). New York Heart Association class returned to pre-PE baseline. Six patients received systemic thrombolysis (1 periprocedure and 5 postprocedure); majority of patients received none.ConclusionsCAVT for high-risk PE was feasible and associated with a low incidence of MAE and a low 30-day mortality rate. CAVT treatment facilitated improvements in hemodynamic status and RV strain. Dyspnea, distance walked, functional status, and QoL significantly improved.Keywords: computer-assisted vacuum thrombectomy, functional outcomes, high-risk pulmonary embolism, mechanical thrombectomy, quality of life

Introduction

Acute pulmonary embolism (PE) is a life-threatening event impacting 370,000 Americans and results in 36,000 deaths each year.1 PE may be associated with a precipitous increase in pulmonary vascular resistance and right ventricular (RV) afterload, potentially causing RV failure and hemodynamic instability.2 When marked by persistent hypotension, cardiac arrest, and/or obstructive shock, patients are classified as having high-risk PE, also referred to as massive PE, which carries a mortality risk of 15% to 50%.3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 Therapeutic anticoagulation is the mainstay of acute PE treatment, with systemic thrombolysis principally reserved for high-risk PE patients; however, systemic thrombolysis is not used in over 50% of candidates because of concerns over bleeding.2,10,11 Even where systemic thrombolysis is used, major bleeding rates in excess of 10% can occur, with the most feared complication of all—intracranial hemorrhage—occurring in up to 3%.10,11 Room remains for consideration of new treatments in patients with high-risk PE.

Multiple guidelines recommend the consideration of endovascular therapy for managing patients with hemodynamic instability and clinical deterioration who have failed and/or are contraindicated for the use of thrombolysis; early, mostly retrospective data support this.5,6,12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17

Mechanical thrombectomy using computer-assisted vacuum thrombectomy (CAVT) is one form of endovascular therapy. CAVT utilizes a microprocessor to regulate thrombus removal while minimizing blood loss via a single percutaneous procedure. The STRIKE-PE study, an ongoing prospective, global, multicenter, single-arm study, is evaluating the safety and effectiveness of CAVT devices (Penumbra, Inc) for the treatment of PE. Given the ongoing unmet need in the treatment of hemodynamically unstable patients, this subanalysis of STRIKE-PE describes feasibility, safety, efficacy, functional, and quality of life (QoL) outcomes after treatment with CAVT for patients with high-risk PE.

Materials and methods

Data source

The ongoing STRIKE-PE study is prospectively enrolling approximately 1500 patients with PE across 80 sites internationally.18 All site investigators, including the site investigators included in this subanalysis (Supplemental Table S1), are experienced in treating PE and performing thrombectomy procedures. They were trained on the clinical protocol and device according to the instructions for use. A prespecified analysis at approximately 600 patients was planned to allow early evaluation of outcomes in predefined subgroups, including high-risk PE; the analysis ended up including the first 595 patients enrolled, of which 48 high-risk PE patients treated with CAVT were identified and included in this interim analysis. These patients were enrolled between March 2022 and August 2024 across 22 sites, and their outcomes were assessed through 90-day postprocedure. The research presented in this subanalysis was conducted ethically in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Study protocols and informed consent process were approved by the institutional review board or ethics committee for each participating site (NCT04798261).19

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion and exclusion criteria for STRIKE-PE have been previously published.18 Briefly, the inclusion criteria were adult patients aged ≥18 years with PE symptoms of ≤14 days, a clinical indication for treatment according to investigator judgment, and a right-to-left ventricular (RV/LV) ratio of ≥0.9 on computed tomography pulmonary angiography or echocardiography. The exclusion criteria were patients with contraindication to systemic or therapeutic doses of anticoagulants, stage III/IV cancer, or cancer requiring active chemotherapy during the study, and current treatment with extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO).

For this subanalysis, only patients with high-risk PE were included (Figure 1). Sites were instructed to report the index PE event diagnosis that reflected the highest severity of PE prior to venous puncture. Patients were classified by the treating physicians as either submassive or massive PE. High-risk patients were verified by site documentation and confirmed by global principal investigators using 1 or more of the following criteria consistent with high-risk PE informed by the European Society of Cardiology guidelines:

•Systolic blood pressure (SBP) <90 mm Hg or SBP drop ≥40 mm Hg (determined from SBP values obtained between admission to the procedure), lasting longer than 15 minutes and not caused by new-onset arrhythmia, hypovolemia, or sepsis

Device

Patients in the current analysis received treatment with 1 of 2 CAVT devices—the Lightning 12 device or the Lightning Flash device which are 12F and 16F catheters, respectively (Penumbra, Inc). Mechanical thrombectomy with CAVT constitutes a microprocessor with clot detection algorithms to automatically regulate aspiration, helping quickly remove thrombus while minimizing blood loss from a single percutaneous procedure, typically performed under local anesthesia. The 12F and 16F catheters are made of an atraumatic laser-cut stainless steel hypotube to provide support and deliverability.

Study outcomes

Primary and secondary effectiveness and safety end points for STRIKE-PE were predefined and used for this subanalysis of patients with high-risk PE.18 Briefly, the primary effectiveness end point was a change in RV/LV ratio at 48 hours postprocedure compared to baseline (at time of presentation of index PE), calculated using matched computed tomography pulmonary angiography echocardiography pairs (Supplemental Table S2).

The primary safety end point was a composite of major adverse events (MAE) within 48 hours, including device-related death, device-related clinical deterioration, device-related pulmonary vascular injury, device-related cardiac injury, and major bleeding. Clinical deterioration was defined as described previously.18 Major bleeding was defined as meeting Bleeding Academic Research Consortium (BARC) types 3a, 3b, 3c, or 5, which corresponds to a 3 to 5 g/dL hemoglobin drop, >5 g/dL hemoglobin drop, intracerebral hemorrhage, or fatal bleeding, respectively.20 BARC type 3a was not considered major bleeding if it was related to an expected decrease in hemoglobin level because of fluid administration and if transfusion was less than 2 units.

Secondary safety end points were device-related serious adverse events, 30-day all-cause mortality, and 30-day symptomatic PE recurrence. All safety end points were adjudicated by an independent medical reviewer.

Additional secondary end points included 90-day functional and QoL assessments.

Functional assessments out to 90-day follow-up

Three mechanisms were used to assess functional recovery. Firstly, the Borg scale of perceived dyspnea (0 = nothing at all; 10 = maximal dyspnea) was assessed at rest and after the 6-minute walk test (6MWT) at baseline and/or discharge and 90 days.21 Secondly, the New York Heart Association (NYHA) classification estimated pre-PE event and then recorded at discharge and 90 days. Third, 6MWT was measured under standardized, recommended conditions at discharge and at 90 days.

QoL assessments out to 90-day follow-up

Widely-used and standardized self-assessment tools, including the EuroQoL 5-dimension 5-level (EQ-5D-5L) index values for US, the EuroQoL Visual Analog Scale (EQ VAS), and the PE-specific Pulmonary Embolism Quality of Life (PEmb-QoL) questionnaires were used to assess QoL.22 The EQ-5D-5L evaluates mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain, discomfort, anxiety, and depression related to health conditions and treatments, with each domain ranging from 1 (best) to 5 (worst). The EQ VAS, which is the other component of EQ-5D-5L, evaluates health status, ranging from 0 (worst imaginable health) to 100 (best imaginable health). The PEmb-QoL evaluates the frequency and intensity of PE-related complaints across activities of daily living, work, social, and emotional dimensions; score ranges from 0 (best) to 100 (worst). These patient-reported outcomes were measured at presentation (baseline) and 90 days postprocedure.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed for all enrolled patients. Missing data were not imputed. Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables; continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD, median (Q1-Q3), and range. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. Unadjusted 2-sided 95% CIs were calculated for all end points. A 2-tailed paired t test was performed on paired mean differences, and a 2-tailed Wilcoxon signed-rank test was performed on median differences. P values for symmetry of all categories of the NYHA class distribution (I or none vs II vs III vs IV) were calculated between baseline and discharge, baseline and 90 days, and discharge and 90 days using the Bowker test of symmetry. P values of change from baseline to 90 days of each EQ-5D-5L dimension (score of 1 vs 2-5) were calculated between baseline and 90 days using the McNemar test. P values for the change of none or class 1 category for NYHA between timepoints were also calculated using the McNemar test. All statistical tests are 2-tailed with a significance level of 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute).

Results

Patient baseline characteristics

Forty-eight patients (64.7 ± 15.2 years; 50% were male) met 1 or more predefined criteria of high-risk PE (Table 1). Among 19 patients in the United States cohort for whom race and ethnicity data were collected, 9 (47.4%) were White and 6 (31.6%) were Black. At baseline, 17 patients (35.4%) reported syncope, and 41 (85.4%) had sPESI score ≥1 (Table 1, Supplemental Tables S3 and S4). Bilateral thrombi were present in all but 2 patients (95.8%; Supplemental Table S5). Median time from symptom onset to admission was 47.0 (10.5-121.0) hours. Forty-two patients had an SBP <90 mm Hg or an SBP drop ≥40 mm Hg, lasting longer than 15 minutes and not caused by new-onset arrhythmia, hypovolemia, or sepsis; 20 needed vasopressor support, and 5 had cardiac arrest requiring CPR (Table 1).

Three patients were treated with CAVT after intravenous systemic lytic. One patient received intrapulmonary arterial lytic treatment during the CAVT procedure; 5 patients received adjunctive intrapulmonary arterial lytic treatment after the CAVT procedure (Table 1).

QoL assessments at discharge and 90 days

Mean EQ-5D-5L index score for the United States cohort increased from 0.301 ± 0.465 at presentation (baseline) to 0.803 ± 0.187 at 90 days after treatment, a change of 0.502 ± 0.398 (P < .001; Table 4). At the 90-day follow-up, fewer patients reported “slight problem or higher” than at baseline on all dimensions of the EQ-5D-5L (Table 4). Mean EQ VAS score increased from 35.4 ± 24.0 at baseline to 77.0 ± 17.7 at 90 days, a change of 41.6 ± 28.9. The PEmb-QoL scores for each dimension also improved from baseline to 90 days (Table 4). Mean overall PEmb-QoL score decreased from 42.8 ± 23.3 at baseline to 17.5 ± 18.0 at 90 days, a change of 25.2 ± 23.8 (P < .001).

Secondary safety end points and additional safety details

Secondary end points included 1 case (2.1%) of symptomatic PE recurrence and 1 all-cause mortality (2.1%) occurring within 30 days; no device-related serious adverse events were observed during the follow-up period (Table 3).

The single mortality event occurred at the end of their thrombectomy procedure, where, despite successful aspiration of the clot, the RV function and respiratory insufficiency further deteriorated. An independent medical reviewer adjudicated this event as related to the index PE event and procedure, but not related to the device (Table 3 and Supplemental Table S9).

One additional mortality event occurred on day 53 because of stomach cancer unrelated to the device (Supplemental Table S9). No additional mortality events occurred for 90 days.

Discussion

This interim analysis from the prospective, global, multicenter, STRIKE-PE study is the first to report periprocedural and 90-day outcomes in high-risk PE patients treated with CAVT. RV offloading was demonstrated at 48 hours via the RV/LV diameter ratio, with an MAE rate of 2.1% at 48 hours. All but one patient survived to 30 days. Further significant clinical, dyspnea, and QoL improvements were demonstrated out to 90 days, with functional status returning to levels similar to those described before the PE event (Central Illustration).

High-risk PE encompasses a broad and clinically diverse spectrum of clinical scenarios from systemic hypotension to cardiac arrest. Mortality rates range from 15% to 50%, even with contemporary data.15,23 Most patients in this analysis exhibited systemic hypotension and/or vasopressor requirement, rather than the more adverse prognostic indicators of cardiac arrest, CPR, or the need for ECMO support. The patients selected in this study met the diagnostic criteria for high-risk and massive PE, but most did not meet the recently described concept of “Catastrophic PE,” whereby patients are in refractory and spiraling shock precardiac or postcardiac arrest.15 These distinctions are clinically meaningful, as the requirement for CPR and/or ECMO significantly increases the risk of adverse outcomes, supported by data reporting in-hospital mortality rates of 42.1% for Catastrophic PE and 17.2% for noncatastrophic PE.15 The current data do not purport to indicate how to treat all patients with high-risk PE, particularly those in extremis. Rather, the data demonstrate that when rapid escalation of care is possible and where cases are carefully selected by experienced centers, and the patient can be stabilized for a procedure, outcomes following reperfusion with CAVT can be delivered safely and with excellent results. Individual patient data are included in Supplemental Table S3 to convey the severity of patients’ initial clinical instability and the extent to which many had been stabilized from near-imminent deterioration prior to intervention, underscoring the importance of timely recognition, coordinated escalation, and multidisciplinary decision-making in this population. Although the body of evidence is growing, further research is needed to delineate the phenotypes of catastrophic and noncatastrophic PE within the high-risk population and understand the risk-benefit ratio for different treatment options.

Pulmonary embolism–related mortality is most often due to acute RV failure, especially in patients with high-risk PE.6 RV dilation/strain is an early manifestation in acute PE and acts as a catalyst for subsequent adverse physiological changes that can lead to mortality. The RV/LV diameter ratio quantifies this in a standardized fashion and is a powerful prognostic tool: an elevated ratio (≥0.9) is an independent predictor of mortality, and progressively higher cut-off values are associated with incrementally poorer prognosis.24 Therefore, the primary treatment aim in PE is to achieve a rapid reduction in PA obstruction, facilitating pulmonary reperfusion and stabilizing RV function. Patients in this study presented with marked RV dilation, which has been observed in prior studies.17,25 Following CAVT, the RV/LV ratio was notably improved, a finding consistent with outcomes observed when CAVT is utilized in PE without hemodynamic instability.18

In patients with high-risk PE, interest in catheter treatment options has arisen partly because of the limitations of systemic thrombolytics as a first-line treatment.26 Firstly, systemic thrombolysis is associated with intracranial hemorrhage and a substantially increased risk of major bleeding, which is itself a common cause of further hemodynamic decompensation in this patient population.6,10 Use of CAVT has not been associated with intracranial events, and in this study, bleeding complications were minimal, despite the use of systemic thrombolysis in 1 patient during the CAVT procedure and 5 via adjunctive infusion postprocedure. Device-related blood loss was low, at 300 mL, and only 1 study-defined MAE occurred within 48 hours, which was a major bleeding in a patient who received CAVT as bailout therapy following failed systemic lysis. Length of hospital stay and postprocedure ICU stay were short, with QoL data indicating early and significant recovery.

High-risk PE carries a high 30-day mortality risk without effective treatment. We observed a mortality event rate of 2.1%, which is in line with results reported from a different thrombectomy device.14,17 This single event was adjudicated independently by an independent medical reviewer as PE-related and could have been procedure-related, despite successful thrombus aspiration. There may be several reasons for the very low mortality event rate in this study. First, in this analysis, we demonstrate “on-table” improvements in RV hemodynamics that suggest recovery starts immediately, offloading the RV quickly, and avoiding “the spiral of RV shock” reported to occur in PE.6,27 Postprocedural imaging at 48 hours further supports this, with RV/LV ratios falling to near normal. Median time from admission to venous puncture was 7.0 (3.0-17.5) hours. This may reflect the possibilities that patients either presented with hemodynamic instability or developed it while admitted for management of PE, and therapy was suggested or escalated as clinically indicated. The reported systemic blood pressure findings at the time of enrollment may also partly explain why these results are so favorable. These patients had undergone resuscitation with fluids, vasopressors, and inotropes by the time of enrollment, with blood pressure stabilizing in much of the cohort before being taken to the lab. This may be an instructive, albeit intuitive, finding—that the ability to stabilize a patient in order to undergo a procedure is likely to be an important prognostic indicator. Furthermore, we cannot be sure whether any mortality could have occurred outside of the cath lab prior to being stabilized enough to undergo informed consent for CAVT or enrollment in the study. An additional factor that could plausibly contribute to both the safety and efficacy of the procedure is the device time. In the current analysis, high-risk PE patients treated with CAVT had a median device time of 42.0 minutes, which is consistent with prior published data using contemporary iterations of this device.18 Lying flat on the table is sometimes difficult for sick patients with PE to tolerate, and procedural efficiency may be an important contributor to procedural success.

Early survival is not the only potential goal of PE treatment. Longer-term functional outcomes and QoL after PE remain a concern, with up to half of all such patients reporting long-term PE symptoms.28,29 In the current analysis, high-risk PE patients treated with CAVT experienced significant improvements in dyspnea at rest/on exertion, walking distance, and QoL. These patients also returned to their preindex PE functional status by NYHA class. Preindex functional status may serve as a predictor of post-PE recovery potential. The degree of functional improvement observed in this cohort, with patients returning to their preindex PE status, suggests that patients with good functional status prior to the PE event may be more likely to experience favorable recovery.

The single-arm, observational design of this study contains some limitations, including a lack of randomization and the ability to obtain deferred consent to postthrombectomy, which occurred in a small number of cases because of the emergent nature of presentation. STRIKE-PE is not limited to patients with high-risk PE and excluded patients on ECMO. Additionally, a few patients had undergone CPR, and many within the cohort did appear to be amenable to stabilization preprocedure. Given these limitations, the participants in this study may reflect a noncatastrophic subset of high-risk PE patients. Two patients died before the 90-day follow-up and were therefore not included in the 90-day assessments of dyspnea, functional status, and QoL. This exclusion could slightly overestimate these outcomes; however, given the small number of cases, the overall impact on the findings is likely minimal. Although this dataset used both American Heart Association and adapted European Society of Cardiology criteria, risk stratification remains a dynamic process subject to the interpretation of existing guidelines and shifts when societies modernize guidelines. Although this is a relatively small cohort within a prespecified interim analysis, the high-risk PE cohort represents a small but critically vulnerable subgroup with a predicted mortality rate of 15% to 50%15,23; early data offer scientific value that may influence patient care, given the scarcity of prospective data on these high-risk patients.

Conclusion

This subanalysis of the STRIKE-PE study demonstrates the utility of CAVT for the treatment of high-risk PE. Our findings show excellent safety and efficacy parameters in a selected population of high-risk PE patients with evidence of cardiovascular instability, but who had minimal administration of CPR and who did not require preprocedural ECMO support. We demonstrate a low 30-day mortality rate of 2.1% in patients who met the criteria for high-risk PE, a condition traditionally associated with mortality rates exceeding 15%. Invasive measurements and 48-hour RV imaging suggest early physiological improvements. These events were followed by excellent functional recovery and improved QoL. Together, these findings suggest that CAVT could be a promising option for high-risk PE patients.
