Effect of olanzapine on changes in serum complement factors levels in patients with first-episode schizophrenia
Authors: Yin Cao, Xuan Zhou, Chunyu Yan, Jun Liang, Biao Dai, Qingrong Xia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAn increasing number of studies have demonstrated that patients with schizophrenia (SCZ) exhibit complement system disorders, and alterations in serum complement factor levels may serve as biomarkers for guiding the diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment of SCZ. Olanzapine is an atypical antipsychotic medication known for its immune-regulatory and anti-inflammatory properties. In this study, we evaluated alterations in serum complement factor levels in patients with first-episode SCZ, explored their diagnostic biomarker potential, and examined the impact of olanzapine treatment on these complement profiles.MethodsA cohort of 35 individuals with first-episode SCZ and 35 healthy controls were included in this study. The severity of psychiatric symptoms in individuals with SCZ was assessed using the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale-18 Item Version (BPRS) and the Scales for the Assessment of Negative Symptoms (SANS) and Positive Symptoms (SAPS). A panel of 5 serum complement-related assays, namely measurements of complement components (C1, C2, C3, C4) and assessment of total complement activity via the 50% hemolytic complement (CH50) test, was performed using commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits. The levels of serum complement factors in healthy controls and individuals with SCZ were compared at baseline. All patients received olanzapine at a dosage range of 10–20 mg/day for a duration of 4 weeks. The BPRS, SANS, SAPS scores, and serum complement factor levels were compared before and after olanzapine treatment.ResultsSerum C2, C3, C4 levels, and CH50 activity were significantly higher in patients with first-episode SCZ compared to healthy controls (P < 0.05). A combined panel of C1, C2, C3, C4, and CH50 demonstrated high accuracy in differentiating patients with SCZ from healthy controls, with a sensitivity of 91.4%, specificity of 77.1%, and AUC = 0.926. Moreover, there was a positive correlation between SANS scores and serum C3 levels (r = 0.357, P = 0.036), while BPRS scores were negatively correlated with serum C4 levels (r = -0.397, P = 0.018) at baseline. Furthermore, significant reductions were observed in SAPS scores (t = 4.795, P < 0.001), SANS scores (t = 3.809, P = 0.001), and BPRS scores (t = 8.538, P < 0.001) after treatment. Additionally, after 4 weeks of treatment, serum levels of C2 (t = 11.812, P < 0.001), C3 (t = 7.649, P < 0.001), and C4 (t = 2.902, P = 0.006) decreased significantly, whereas the CH50 activity increased significantly (t = -9.202, P < 0.001) compared to pre-treatment levels.ConclusionThese findings demonstrate the potential of olanzapine to reduce serum levels of complement factors C2, C3, and C4 while increasing serum CH50 activity in patients with first-episode SCZ. However, the exact mechanism underlying this regulatory effect remains unclear and warrants further investigation.Keywords: First episode schizophrenia, Olanzapine, Serum, Complement factor, Patients

Introduction

Schizophrenia (SCZ) is a chronic psychiatric disorder marked by cognitive, perceptual, and motivational deficits, exerting adverse effects on both individuals and society. Global statistics indicate that the lifetime prevalence of SCZ is approximately 5.5 per 1,000 people [1, 2]. According to the World Health Organization, approximately 24 million individuals are diagnosed with SCZ worldwide [3]. The direct and indirect annual costs associated with SCZ are estimated to be in the billions of dollars, while life expectancy is reduced by 10–20 years [4]. This condition not only severely impacts patients’ daily lives but also imposes a substantial mental and economic burden on both patients and their families [5]. At present, the pathogenesis of SCZ has not been fully elucidated. It is a multifaceted psychiatric disorder with a complex etiology involving genetic, environmental, and immunological factors. In recent years, the immune system has become a focal point in SCZ research [6, 7].

In recent years, multiple studies have shown that the dysfunction of the central nervous system immune-inflammatory pathways may be related to the occurrence of SCZ [8, 9]. Levels of pro-inflammatory markers, such as cytokines, have been found to be elevated in the blood and cerebrospinal fluid of patients with SCZ [10]. Numerous epidemiological and clinical studies have provided evidence indicating that various infectious agents may serve as risk factors for SCZ and other mental disorders [11]. For instance, a large-scale epidemiological study conducted in Denmark demonstrated that autoimmune diseases and the frequency of hospitalization infections are risk factors for SCZ [12]. Several potential mechanisms indicate that immune system dysfunction may contribute to the pathophysiology of SCZ. These mechanisms involve complex cascade reactions, including microglial activation, upregulation of cytokine signaling, and apoptosis [13, 14].

The complement system is a crucial component of the innate immune system, consisting of more than 50 secretory and membrane-bound proteins. It aids in the clearance of apoptotic cells and invading pathogens, thereby modulating inflammatory immune responses and playing a vital role in maintaining brain homeostasis. Dysregulation, impairment, or inadvertent activation of complement components contributes to the pathogenesis of certain autoimmune neurological disorders and may also play a role in neurodegenerative diseases [15]. It is worth noting that complement components C1, C2, C3, and C4 have been identified as crucial for promoting the solubility and clearance of immune complexes [16]. Additionally, the determination of serum 50% hemolytic complement (CH50) activity is the most widely used method for evaluating the functional activity of the classical complement pathway [17]. Research on the complement system in relation to SCZ primarily focuses on changes in hemolytic activity of classical pathway components [18]. Our recent study further confirms that circulating complement factors (namely C1, C2, C3, and C4), and total complement activity (CH50), may hold potential for identifying biomarkers in the diagnosis of first-episode SCZ [19]. The baseline serum levels of C2 and C3, as well as their variations before and after treatment, may serve as potential biomarkers for predicting clinical efficacy in patients with first-episode SCZ who are undergoing treatment with aripiprazole [20]. These findings highlight the critical role of complement dysregulation in SCZ pathogenesis and its potential as a therapeutic monitoring biomarker.

Olanzapine, a second-generation atypical antipsychotic (SGA) of the thienobenzodiazepine class, is commonly prescribed for SCZ management. It exhibits efficacy in alleviating positive, negative, and mood-related symptoms of SCZ, and its more favorable safety and tolerability profile compared to first-generation antipsychotics has contributed to widespread adoption in long-term therapeutic regimens [21]. Pharmacologically, olanzapine acts as an antagonist at serotonin (5-HT2A), dopamine (D1, D2, D3, D4), and histamine (H1) receptors [22]. Notably, olanzapine is associated with notable common side effects, primarily weight gain and metabolic syndrome [23, 24]. Specifically, SCZ patients treated with olanzapine may experience an average 3.3 kg weight increase within the first 8 weeks [25]; among SGAs, olanzapine (second only to clozapine) is linked to more severe metabolic issues, including significant elevations in triglycerides and total cholesterol [26, 27]. Preliminary evidence also supports a dose-response relationship between olanzapine plasma concentrations and metabolic outcomes [28]. Against this backdrop of olanzapine’s well-established clinical efficacy, mechanism of action, and side effect profile, the present study focused on investigating its effects on serum complement factor levels in SCZ patients. This investigation aimed to expand understanding of olanzapine’s immunomodulatory impacts beyond its known therapeutic effects and metabolic/weight-related adverse events.

Given the emerging evidence implicating immune dysregulation, particularly complement system activation, in the pathophysiology of SCZ, we investigated the changes in serum complement factor levels (C1, C2, C3, C4) and total complement activity (CH50) in patients with first-episode SCZ before and after treatment. Based on previous findings suggesting elevated inflammatory markers in SCZ, we hypothesized that (1) untreated first-episode SCZ patients would exhibit higher baseline levels of complement factors compared to healthy controls; (2) the elevated complement factor levels would positively correlate with the severity of psychotic symptoms at baseline; and (3) olanzapine treatment would normalize these levels, leading to a significant decrease in complement components.

Materials and methods

Participants information

This retrospective study involved 35 patients registered between September 2020 and March 2022. All patients were inpatients from the Hefei Fourth People’s Hospital. These patients were carefully selected by two experienced researchers through structured clinical interviews, based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), and psychiatric interviews were conducted. The two experienced researchers involved in these assessments are board-certified psychiatrists with over 10 years of clinical experience each in diagnosing and managing psychiatric disorders. Inter-rater reliability was established prior to the study when both interviewers independently assessed 20 subjects (10 had clinical SCZ) and agreed on the diagnosis of 20 (κ = 1.00). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Patients aged 18–65 years; (2) First-episode patients who had never received antipsychotic medication; (3) No history of major organ diseases, such as diabetes, hyperlipidemia, or liver disease; (4) No history of other mental illnesses, alcohol or drug dependence, or use of immunomodulators. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Presence of intellectual disability; (2) Pregnant or lactating women; (3) Patients with severe cardiovascular, metabolic, or endocrine diseases. We also recruited 35 heatlhy controls simultaneously, who collectively matched the patient group in age, gender and ethnicity. The inclusion criteria for healthy controls were as follows: (1) Age range: 18–65 years; (2) No history of antipsychotic medication use; (3) No history of major organ diseases, such as diabetes, hyperlipidemia, or liver disease; (4) No history of mental illnesses, alcohol dependence, or drug dependence; (5) No use of immunomodulators; (6) No intellectual disability; (7) Not pregnant or lactating; (8) No severe cardiovascular, metabolic, or endocrine diseases. This is a retrospective study using de-identified data from an institutional biobank. The original samples were collected for clinical purposes between 2020 and 2024 and approved by the Ethics Committee of Hefei Fourth People’s Hospital (No. HFSY-IRB-PJ-XQR-2020001, 2020), with informed consent obtained at the time of collection. The retrospective analysis was approved by the the Ethics Committee of Hefei Fourth People’s Hospital, with the registration number HSY-IRB-PJ-CY (HFSY202109).

Clinical assessment

The severity of psychiatric symptoms was measured by the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS) and the Assessment of Negative/Positive Symptoms (SANS/SAPS) [29, 30]. All assessments were conducted by two board-certified psychiatrists, each with > 10 years of clinical experience in psychiatric diagnosis and management. Prior to the study, both raters completed standardized training and demonstrated excellent inter-rater reliability (ICC > 0.85). The BPRS is the most widely used scale for measuring psychiatric symptoms. It consists of 18 items that researchers can use to assess symptoms such as depression, anxiety, mannerisms and posturing, hallucinatory behavior, and hostility. Items are rated on a 7 points scale, where one is not present and seven is extremely severe [31]. The SANS scale is a tool assessed by the interviewer and used to measure the severity of negative symptoms in SCZ. It consists of 24 items grouped into five domains: affective flattening, alogia, avolition/apathy, anhedonia/asociality, and attention deficits. Each item has a score of 0 to 5, with a total score of 0–120 [32]. This SAPS scale is administered by the interviewer and serves to measure the severity of positive symptoms in schizophrenia. It comprises four subscales and 34 items that assess hallucinations, delusions, bizarre behavior, and positive formal thought disorder. Each item is scored on a scale of 0 to 5, with total scores ranging from 0 to 170 [32].

Blood sample collection and serum complement factor testing

Before treatment in the first-episode of SCZ, and after a four-week regimen of olanzapine maintenance therapy, venous blood was collected from patients between 7 a.m. and 8 a.m., followed by immediate centrifugation at 2000 g for 3 min. The resulting serum was extracted from the supernatant and stored at −80 °C until subsequent analysis. Additionally, the timing of control sample collection was matched as closely as possible to that of the SCZ group. Serum complement factors, namely C1 (catalog number: JL53181), C2 (catalog number: JL29878), C3 (catalog number: JL10735), C4 (catalog number: JL10177), and CH50 activity (catalog number: JL10736), were measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Jianglai Bio, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For the assessment of overall complement system activity, we employed an ELISA-based CH50 test rather than the classical 50% complement haemolytic function test. The rationale for this selection includes three key considerations: (1) Sample efficiency. The ELISA-based assay requires only 50–100 µl of serum per sample. In contrast, the haemolytic CH50 test typically requires 200–500 µl of serum, which was less feasible for our cohort. (2) Operational stability. The haemolytic CH50 test is highly sensitive to pre-analytical variables (e.g., serum storage time, temperature fluctuations, and presence of hemolysis), which can introduce significant variability. The ELISA-based assay, however, has stricter quality control standards and greater resistance to pre-analytical interference, ensuring consistent results across batches of samples. (3) Convenient for quantitative analysis: ELISA can quantitatively measure the content of target substances by detecting the color-depth of the reaction product, and can accurately obtain the specific value of CH50. The traditional hemolysis method determines the complement activity by observing the hemolysis degree, and the quantitative accuracy is relatively low.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS (version 17.0; Chicago, Illinois, USA). Statistical significance was set at a P value < 0.05. The normality of data distribution was assessed using the one-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Quantitative variables following a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Gender differences were analyzed using chi-square tests. Independent samples t-tests were conducted to compare age, BMI, and serum complement factors at baseline between healthy controls and SCZ patients. Paired samples t-tests were used to evaluate differences in SANS, SAPS, BPRS scores, and serum complement factors before and after treatment. Bonferroni correction was used for multiple comparisons. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to assess the diagnostic performance of serum complement factors in distinguishing SCZ patients from healthy controls. Pearson’s correlation test was used for correlation analyses. Post hoc power analysis for a two-sample t-test with two groups was conducted using G*Power (version 3.1.9.2, University of Kiel, Kiel, Germany), given the small sample size.

Diagnostic value of different serum complement factors in discriminating patients with SCZ from healthy volunteers

The diagnostic efficacy of various serum complement factors in distinguishing patients with SCZ from healthy volunteers was assessed through the use of ROC curve analysis (Fig. 1). Discussion

The present study aimed to investigate the effects of olanzapine treatment on serum complement factors by measuring the serum concentrations of circulating C1, C2, C3, C4, and total complement activity (CH50) in first-episode SCZ patients before and after 4 weeks of treatment. The present study yielded four primary findings. First, the pre-treatment serum levels of C2, C3, C4, and CH50 activity were significantly higher in patients with SCZ compared to healthy controls. Second, ROC curve analysis revealed that the AUC value of a combined panel of C1, C2, C3, C4, and CH50 was 0.926, with a sensitivity of 91.4% and a specificity of 77.1%, for distinguishing SCZ patients from healthy controls. Third, serum C3 levels were positively correlated with SANS scores, while serum C4 levels were negatively correlated with BPRS scores at baseline. Fourth, following olanzapine treatment, there was a notable decrease in the serum levels of C2, C3, and C4, along with a significant increase in the activity of CH50.

The complement system, as a key effector of innate immunity and an adjuvant for adaptive immunity, has been extensively investigated in the context of SCZ. Current research primarily focuses on the dysregulation of complement factor levels in SCZ patients, though inconsistent findings have been reported for C1, C2, C3, and C4. The majority of studies consistently demonstrate elevated activities of C1, C3, and C4 in individuals with SCZ [20, 33, 34]. In contrast, some studies have shown significantly lower peripheral blood C3 concentrations in SCZ patients compared to healthy controls [35], while others have reported no significant differences in C3 and C4 levels between first-episode SCZ patients and healthy controls after adjusting for body mass index (BMI) [36]. To further clarify complement status in SCZ, one study for the first time analyzed C4 levels in both cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and plasma [37]. This study enrolled 32 patients with SCZ spectrum disorders and 32 healthy controls for CSF sampling, as well as 33 patients with SCZ spectrum disorders and 31 healthy controls for peripheral blood sampling. Results revealed a significant correlation between CSF C4 levels and age; however, no significant intergroup differences were observed in plasma C4 levels, nor was there a significant correlation between CSF and plasma C4 concentrations. Regarding plasma C4 dynamics, recent studies have shown that its levels increase during the acute phase of SCZ and decrease following antipsychotic treatment [38]. Inconsistencies have also been reported for CH50 (total complement hemolytic activity). Multiple studies have documented elevated CH50 levels in SCZ patients relative to healthy controls [33], whereas others have found no significant intergroup differences in CH50 activity [39]. Further research exploring the effects of antipsychotics on CH50 activity has yielded mixed results: most studies indicate that antipsychotics do not significantly affect CH50 hemolytic activity, while a few suggest a potential upward trend in CH50 hemolytic activity among SCZ patients receiving antipsychotic treatment [40]. Our recent study has indicated that after 4 weeks of aripiprazole treatment, serum levels of C2, C3, and C4 were significantly reduced relative to baseline, whereas CH50 activity was significantly increased [20]. In the present study, our results demonstrated elevated serum levels of C2, C3, C4, and CH50 activity in SCZ patients, and further proposed that the combination of these complement components may serve as potential diagnostic biomarkers for SCZ. The baseline complement data reported herein, while consistent with the diagnostic trends observed in our prior work on first-episode SCZ [19], are derived from an independent, later-recruited cohort with no participant overlap. The inconsistencies across aforementioned studies may be attributed to several factors, including differences in disease stages (acute vs. chronic), variations in detection methods, and the use of diverse antipsychotic agents. Although substantial evidence supports an association between the complement system and SCZ, most existing studies are correlational and lack direct causal evidence. Future research should prioritize investigating the causal relationship between specific complement components and SCZ, which could be achieved through gene-specific knockout models or the administration of targeted complement inhibitors. Additionally, greater attention should be directed toward the role of synaptic phagocytosis or pruning in the pathogenesis of SCZ.

To investigate the potential value of these abnormal complement factors as diagnostic biomarkers for SCZ, we evaluated their performance using ROC analysis. According to traditional criteria, an AUC greater than 0.7 is generally considered suitable for clinical application [41]. The 2 complement factors (namely C3, and C4) and CH50 activity, comprehensively satisfy the aforementioned conditions. Considering that combining multiple serum proteins into a group can improve the sensitivity or specificity of a single biomarker [42]. This study assessed the diagnostic value of combined detection of C1, C2, C3, C4, and CH50 for SCZ. The results demonstrated that the AUC of this panel increased to 0.926, suggesting its significant potential as a biomarker for identifying individuals with SCZ.

The BPRS, SANS and SAPS scales are widely used to assess the severity of psychiatric symptoms [43, 44]. Therefore, the severity of psychiatric symptoms in individuals with SCZ was assessed using these scales in the present study. Some studies have investigated the relationship between the severity of psychiatric symptoms in individuals with SCZ and the complement system. Studies have shown that the C4/C3 ratio is significantly positively correlated with the positive BPRS score in patients with SCZ [45]. Another research result indicates that the total scores of PANSS were negatively correlated with C3 levels [35]. Furthermore, our previous study found that serum C2, C3 levels, and CH50 activity were positively correlated with SANS, SAPS, and BPRS scores in individuals with SCZ, respectively [19]. Further research on the effects of aripiprazole treatment on the complement system in individuals with SCZ revealed that baseline concentrations of serum C2 and C3 before and after treatment, as well as their changes, were negatively correlated with the reduction rate of PANSS/BPRS scores [20]. In this study, the serum C3 level in individuals with SCZ was positively correlated with the SANS score, while the serum C4 level was negatively correlated with the BPRS score at baseline. These research results suggest that the severity of psychiatric symptoms may be associated with the regulation of the complement system, but the specific reasons remain unclear. Given the relatively small sample size and single-center design of this study, further multi-center studies are needed to confirm these findings. Additionally, the specific mechanisms underlying these relationships require further investigation.

In this study, a four-week duration was selected to assess the impact of olanzapine treatment on serum complement factor levels. This timeframe was chosen based on evidence indicating that the therapeutic responses to most antipsychotic medications, including those for first-episode psychosis, typically manifest within four weeks [46, 47]. Additionally, early responses observed during this period are predictive of long-term outcomes [48]. After olanzapine administration for 4 weeks, the SAPS scores, SANS scores and BPRS scores decreased significantly respectively. These clinical symptom improvements, coupled with the predefined 4-week timeframe, provide a robust foundation for subsequent analyses of olanzapine’s effects on serum complement factor levels in this cohort.

Emerging evidence increasingly supports immune system dysregulation as a critical etiological factor in SCZ [49]. Specifically, aberrant immune function and inflammation contribute significantly to both the pathogenesis of SCZ and the efficacy of therapeutic interventions [50], with early antipsychotic administration shown to mitigate neuroinflammation progression [51]. Olanzapine, an atypical antipsychotic widely used in clinical practice for SCZ, effectively alleviates both positive and negative psychiatric symptoms [52]. Recent studies have further identified its multifaceted immunomodulatory properties: olanzapine exerts anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, inhibits the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines [53], and activates the IKKβ/NF-κB inflammatory signaling pathway [48]. Notably, olanzapine is thought to achieve its therapeutic effects in SCZ by inducing inflammatory and immune responses in the prefrontal cortex (PFC) and activating endoplasmic reticulum stress signals [54]. Concurrently, accumulating research indicates complement system dysregulation in individuals with SCZ [55]. As a key component of the immune system, the complement system plays a pivotal role in inflammation activation and has recently been implicated in brain neural development processes [56]. While cognitive deficits in SCZ are associated with alterations in inflammatory factors [57, 58], those in Alzheimer’s disease are linked to complement system changes [59], highlighting the potential relevance of complement pathways to neuropsychiatric disorder-related cognition. In the present study, we observed that 4 weeks of olanzapine treatment significantly reduced serum levels of complement components C2, C3, and C4 while markedly increasing CH50 activity. These findings align with our prior research, which reported similar complement profile changes following aripiprazole treatment [20]. Additionally, correlation analyses revealed a positive association between serum C3 levels and SANS scores, as well as a negative association between serum C4 levels and BPRS scores. Taken together, while our findings demonstrate an association between olanzapine treatment and complement protein level changes, the study design does not permit causal inference regarding whether symptom improvement is mediated by complement system modulation. Given the observational nature of the present findings and the complexity of immune-neuropsychiatric interactions, additional studies with larger cohorts and more comprehensive mechanistic assessments will be necessary to further explore and validate this potential relationship.

The observed increase in CH50, a marker of classical complement pathway functional activity, alongside decreased levels of C2, C3, and C4 in olanzapine-treated schizophrenia patients suggests a complex drug-mediated modulation of complement system regulation. This dissociation may arise from several mechanisms: (1) compensatory activation of the alternative or lectin pathways, maintaining overall hemolytic capacity despite reduced classical pathway components; (2) altered regulatory dynamics via olanzapine’s immunomodulatory effects on complement inhibitors, potentially reducing consumption of functional proteins [60]; or (3) dose-dependent metabolic and inflammatory changes influencing complement activation patterns. Similar CH50-component discrepancies have been documented in autoimmune conditions, where divergent functional and protein measurements reflect increased turnover or regulatory imbalances [61]. Further investigations are needed to determine whether this profile represents reduced inflammatory burden or a unique pharmacological effect on complement homeostasis.

The observed associations between olanzapine and complement factor levels may arise from several non-causal mechanisms, necessitating careful interpretation. First, confounding by indication could play a role, as patients prescribed olanzapine may differ systematically from non-users in disease severity, metabolic profiles, or inflammatory states, which themselves influence complement activation. For instance, SCZ and metabolic comorbidities are linked to altered complement activity, potentially creating spurious associations. Second, residual confounding by unmeasured variables (e.g., diet, concurrent infections, or genetic predispositions) might contribute. Complement factors are sensitive to systemic inflammation, and olanzapine users may exhibit higher baseline inflammation due to lifestyle factors or antipsychotic side effects (e.g., weight gain). Third, shared genetic or environmental factors linking psychiatric disorders and immune dysregulation could contribute to the observed associations.

Notably, ELISA-based CH50 assays have emerged as the dominant method for evaluating complement activity in recent research [62–64]. This methodological shift is primarily attributed to their well-documented advantages over the traditional haemolytic CH50 assay, including higher sensitivity, superior specificity, and enhanced reproducibility [65]. These merits are further supported by established literature: a comparative analysis revealed that the ELISA-based format exhibits substantially higher sensitivity (96.3%) and specificity (99.4%) than classical haemolytic assays [66]. Consistent with these findings, an ELISA-based CH50 assay was therefore selected to assess complement activity in the present study.

The current study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the relatively small sample size of this single-center investigation may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. Second, the current study is limited by its retrospective study design, which introduces the potential for confounding bias due to unmeasured confounding variables. Third, the lack of assessment of serum olanzapine concentrations precludes the examination of potential correlations between olanzapine levels and complement factor levels. Fourth, financial constraints prevented the analysis of all complement factors, which may affect the comprehensiveness of the results.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrated that serum levels of C2, C3, C4, and CH50 were significantly elevated in patients with SCZ compared to healthy controls. Combined detection of C1, C2, C3, C4, and CH50 may serve as potential diagnostic biomarkers for distinguishing SCZ patients from healthy individuals. Additionally, our results indicate that a 4-week treatment with olanzapine led to decreased levels of C2, C3, and C4, but increased CH50 activity in SCZ patients. These findings provide further evidence supporting the hypothesis that complement factors may play a role in the pathogenesis of SCZ and suggest that the modulation of complement factors by antipsychotics may contribute to their therapeutic effects in SCZ patients. Given the growing interest in complement-targeted therapies, future clinical studies should focus on evaluating their efficacy and potential applications in SCZ treatment.
