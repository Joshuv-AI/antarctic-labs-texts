Mycobacterium farcinogenes-induced lymphadenitis in a pediatric patient: a case report

Background

Non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) encompass all mycobacteria excluding the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex and Mycobacterium leprae. NTMs are prevalent in natural environments, including water, soil, and dust [1–3]. NTMs can invade various human organs, including the lungs, lymph nodes, bones, joints, skin, and soft tissues, and may lead to systemic disseminated diseases. To date, over 200 NTM species have been identified. Among these, 140 are known to be pathogenic to humans or animals, while the remaining 60 exhibit potential pathogenicity. Mycobacterium farcinogenes is an uncommon NTM belonging to the rapidly growing mycobacteria (RGM) group, typically linked to bovine farcy and only rarely causing human infections [4]. Mycobacterium farcinogenes was initially described in 1973 as a causal agent of bovine farcy in eastern and central Africa [5]. Cases of human infection were seldom reported. This case report details the first isolation of Mycobacterium farcinogenes from the pus of a child diagnosed with lymphadenitis.

Case description

The patient, a 6-year-3-month-old female, was admitted to the hospital with a complaint of a “left submandibular mass for nearly 3 months.” In November 2022, she developed a left submandibular mass without any apparent cause. Initially, the mass was painful but was not accompanied by redness, swelling, ulceration, difficulty swallowing, fever, or chills. The patient was previously hospitalized at a local facility, where she received anti-infective therapy and underwent puncture treatment. However, the mass did not decrease significantly in size, and the pain persisted. Seeking further treatment, she presented to our hospital. A physical examination at the outpatient clinic led to her admission with a diagnosis of “lymphadenitis.” Throughout the illness, the patient remained in stable general condition, with no notable weight loss. She denied any history of hepatitis, tuberculosis, or other infectious diseases, and her medical history was unremarkable. Regarding her family history, her grandmother had previously been diagnosed with “lymph node tuberculosis,” which had been successfully treated.

On examination, a raised lesion was observed on the left submandibular skin, approximately 5 × 5 cm in size, with poorly defined boundaries, limited mobility, slight redness, mild swelling, and no ulceration (Fig. 1A). Ultrasound imaging of the cervical lymph nodes revealed a hypoechoic area in the left submandibular region, indicating inflammation and minor pus accumulation. Enlarged lymph nodes were detected in the left submandibular and cervical areas, with the largest measuring approximately 3.0 × 0.8 cm. Following admission, the patient underwent comprehensive evaluations, which showed no absolute contraindications to surgery. On January 12, 2023, under general anesthesia, the patient underwent a “left submandibular lymph node biopsy with abscess incision and drainage.” During the procedure, multiple enlarged lymph nodes were observed in the left submandibular area. Some superficial lymph nodes exhibited necrosis and infection. Approximately 5 ml of pus was drained, and a sample of superficial lymph tissue was collected for analysis. Postoperatively, the patient received wound care, supportive treatments, and symptom management. The microbiological results confirmed an infection caused by Mycobacterium farcinogenes. Drug susceptibility testing indicated sensitivity to rifampicin and clarithromycin, while azithromycin showed resistance. The patient was treated with a combination of clarithromycin and rifampicin for 10 days. Clarithromycin dry suspension (5.5 ml per dose) was administered twice daily at 12-hour intervals, and rifampicin capsules (0.285 g per dose) were given once daily. After 10 days of treatment, the patient’s condition improved significantly, and her symptoms resolved (Fig. 1B). Following discharge, the patient continued outpatient treatment with clarithromycin dry suspension (5.5 ml per dose) and rifampicin capsules (0.285 g per dose) for a total duration of 6 months. Long-term follow-up at 9 months post-treatment confirmed sustained clinical remission without evidence of recurrence.

Bacterial culture and identification

Bacterial culture

During the abscess incision and drainage procedure, approximately 5 ml of pus was collected and inoculated onto Löwenstein-Jensen medium, incubated at 35 °C with 5% CO₂. No colony growth was observed on the Columbia blood agar plate after 2 days of incubation. By the 3rd day, small grayish-white colonies appeared in the first zone of the blood agar plate. On the 5th day, the colonies had grown larger, exhibiting an irregular yellowish-white appearance on the blood plate (Fig. 2). Colonies from the Columbia blood agar plate were subjected to acid-fast staining (Fig. 3), which showed unevenly acid-fast positive bacilli with noticeable branching. These colonies were subsequently subcultured onto both Columbia blood agar plates and Löwenstein-Jensen medium. On Columbia blood agar plates, small grayish-white colonies were visible after 1 day of incubation (Fig. 4a) and had increased in size by the second day (Fig. 4b). On Löwenstein-Jensen medium, small pale yellow colonies were observed after 1 day (Fig. 4c) and showed further enlargement by the second day (Fig. 4d).

MALDI-TOF MS identification

A pure colony from the blood agar plate was picked using an inoculation loop and smeared onto the target plate. A microliter of 70% formic acid was applied to the sample and left to dry at room temperature. Subsequently, 1 µL of matrix solution was added, and the plate was dried again at room temperature before being processed in the Matrix assisted laser desorption ionization-time of flight mass spectrometry(MALDI-TOF MS)system (Bruker Biotyper; Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) for analysis. Results were analyzed using the MALDI Biotyper compass identification software. Following the manufacturer’s recommendations, a score of 2.0 or above indicated correct identification at the species level, scores between 1.700 and 1.999 indicated identification at the genus level, and a score below 1.7 was considered unreliable.Results in our experiment, which identified the bacterium as Mycobacterium farcinogenes with a score of 2.1 (a MALDI score >2.0 is regarded as identification to the species level) [6, 7].

Discussion and conclusion

In recent years, the incidence and prevalence of nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) diseases have risen significantly. This trend is attributed to an increasing population of immunocompromised individuals, the growing complexity of surgical procedures, and improved diagnostic methods for detecting NTM. Mycobacterium farcinogenes is primarily known as the causative agent of farcy, a disease initially believed to result from Nocardia species [8, 9]. It was first identified by Chamoiseau et al., after which reports of infections caused by this pathogen have remained rare [10]. Only a limited number of studies have described the isolation of Mycobacterium farcinogenes from human clinical specimens. We report a rare case of lymphadenitis caused by Mycobacterium farcinogenes in a previously healthy 6-year-old female. The patient had no history of trauma, surgery, or animal contact prior to symptom onset. At two months before presentation, she received ​root canal treatment for dental caries at a local clinic. While invasive dental procedures have been implicated in non-tuberculous mycobacterial infections via contaminated water systems [11, 12], the lack of environmental sampling data from the clinic precludes definitive attribution in this case.

In this case, the pathogen was identified using MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry. Based on 16 S rRNA sequence information, it is difficult to differentiate M. farcinogenes from M. senegalense [13].However, owing to constraints in our resources, we were unable to validate the strain identification through 16–23 S Ribosomal DNA Internal Transcribed Spacer Sequencing. Notably, the MALDI-TOF MS method was reportedly valuable for detecting and identifying Mycobacterium species [14]. In this particular instance, MALDI-TOF MS technology indicated that the causative agent was M. farcinogenes, with a score of 2.1. This score denotes a high level of confidence in the identification of Mycobacteria species [6, 7].

Previously, Wong et al. reported a case of Mycobacterium farcinogenes infection in a prosthetic joint six months after total hip arthroplasty [15]. Isozaki et al. described a case of osteomyelitis in a 66-year-old male caused by Mycobacterium farcinogenes, which developed five months following bone fusion surgery for a compound fracture of the left lower thigh [16]. In both cases, definitive diagnoses were established approximately one year after the onset of symptoms. Following discharge, both patients recovered and regained independent mobility. Uslan et al. reported a series of skin and soft tissue infections caused by rapid-growing mycobacteria, noting that the median time from symptom onset to pathogen-based diagnosis was 86 days [17]. This highlights the importance of considering infections caused by rapid-growing mycobacteria in patients presenting with non-healing postoperative wounds. The most recent report was by Ye F et al. [18], which reviewed seven cases. In this review, all of the cases were adults, and there were no cases of children. Six of the seven cases were immunocompetent. The sites of infection varied. All cases were triggered by surgery or trauma, and the infection foci appeared several months later. One report did not describe the diagnostic method, two used MALDI-TOF MS only, two used MALDI-TOF MS and genetic testing, and two used genetic testing only.

This case is particularly significant as it marks the first successful isolation of Mycobacterium farcinogenes from the purulent fluid of a child with lymphadenitis. While non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) infections have been documented in adults, they are exceedingly rare in immunocompetent individuals, especially in children. This highlights the importance of addressing the risk of NTM infections in pediatric populations. NTMs can be transmitted through specific pathways, such as water contamination during dental procedures. This finding emphasizes the critical need for stringent infection control measures and proper water source management to prevent NTM infections, offering new perspectives for clinical practices and public health strategies [11, 12].

Moreover, the successful diagnosis and treatment of this case have provided invaluable insights for clinicians. Using surgical biopsy and tissue culture, MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry precisely identified Mycobacterium farcinogenes, guiding the subsequent treatment approach. Following extensive surgical debridement to reduce the pathogen load, we employed a combination antibiotic regimen of clarithromycin and rifampin based on drug sensitivity testing. Owing to the mycobacterium farcinogenes is rarity, there is a lack of consensus on the standard treatment. There is scarce information regarding the choice of antibiotics for M. farcinogenes infections. In this instance, our current treatment was selected based on antimicrobial susceptibility. Wong et al. employed ciprofloxacin and doxycycline, whereas Isozaki et al. utilized levofloxacin alone [15, 16]. Surgical debridement along with the removal of infected hardware, followed by combined antibiotic therapy, was reportedly efficacious in treating nontuberculous mycobacterial soft tissue infections and prevented the development of resistance [19].In our case, antibiotics were used for six months and clinical outcome was favorable. This combination achieved significant therapeutic effects within a short period, demonstrating its effectiveness and practicality. This case offers valuable experience in the diagnosis and management of Mycobacterium farcinogenes infections in children.
