Effect of patent complete revascularization on the akinetic myocardial segments
Authors: Min-Seok Kim, Min-Jeong Kim, Hyeon Ju Jeong, Seong Wook Hwang, Ki-Bong Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOBJECTIVESThe aims of the study were (i) to examine the changes in echocardiographic parameters and (ii) to compare the fate of myocardial segments with akinesia and without akinesia on preoperative echocardiography after coronary artery bypass grafting.METHODSOne hundred one patients who underwent complete revascularization, who were assessed by preoperative, before discharge, postoperative 3- and 12-month echocardiographic examinations, and who showed all patent grafts at postoperative 1-year angiograms were included. Echocardiographic left ventricular ejection fraction was assessed, and a 16-segment model was adopted for regional analysis of the left ventricle. A total of 1616 segments were analysed based on a 6-point scale: 1 = normal (N = 1083), 2 = mild hypokinesia (N = 2), 3 = moderate hypokinesia (N = 74), 4 = severe hypokinesia (N = 150), 5 = akinesia without thinning (N = 259) and 6 = akinesia with thinning (N = 48).RESULTSThe serial left ventricular ejection fraction measured preoperatively, before discharge, at postoperative 3- and 12-months were 0.48 ± 0.14, 0.49 ± 0.12, 0.49 ± 0.10 and 0.54 ± 0.10, respectively. The left ventricular ejection fraction significantly increased over time during the postoperative 12 months (P < 0.001). Wall motion scores tended to decrease over time in both segment groups with akinesia and without akinesia (P < 0.001), and improvement of the wall motion was significantly higher in the segment group with akinesia than in the segment group without akinesia (P < 0.001).CONCLUSIONSThe left ventricular ejection fraction and regional wall motion improved over time during the postoperative 12 months, regardless of the presence of an akinetic segment. Complete revascularization including akinetic myocardium should be considered when performing coronary artery bypass grafting.

INTRODUCTION

Revascularization of dysfunctional myocardium such as stunned or hibernating myocardium improves myocardial function [1]. However, revascularization of non-viable infarcted myocardium would not be justified when considering the potential benefits versus risks of revascularization therapy. Echocardiography is a widely used method for preoperative evaluation of cardiac function in patients undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG). Hypokinetic or akinetic myocardium on preoperative echocardiography may represent stunned, hibernating or non-viable infarcted muscle, and revascularization will not improve myocardial function in non-viable myocardium [2]. A myocardial segment is defined as akinetic if there is no systolic myocardial thickening, even if a slight inward endocardial motion is present during systole on echocardiography [3]. In contrast to hypokinetic myocardium, which is reversible in cardiac function in most instances, akinetic myocardium is not completely reversible after revascularization [4]. The presence of akinetic myocardium on preoperative echocardiography may provide surgeons with additional factors to consider in making decisions before revascularization.

The aims of this study were (i) to examine the changes in echocardiographic parameters after CABG and (ii) to compare the fate of myocardial segments with akinesia and without akinesia on preoperative echocardiography in CABG patients having all patent grafts at 1 year postoperatively.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The institutional review board reviewed the study protocol and approved the present study as a minimal risk retrospective study (approval number MJH 2024–01-013, approval date: 6 February 2024) that did not require individual patient consent based on the institutional guidelines for waiving consent.

Patients and follow-up

Of the patients who underwent isolated CABG and complete revascularization, the patients who completed 1-year follow-up were included in the present study. All the study patients underwent echocardiographic examinations preoperatively, before discharge, 3- and 12-months postoperatively; and also underwent 1-year follow-up coronary and graft angiograms as a standard postoperative strategy for CABG patients, regardless of the presence or absence of anginal symptoms. Patients who refused angiographic evaluation or had renal function impairment were excluded from the angiographic follow-up. Patients receiving renal replacement therapy were included in the angiographic follow-up. Informed written consent, including the possible benefits and risks of postoperative angiography, was obtained from all patients.

Echocardiographic follow-up

The echocardiographic parameter of left ventricular (LV) systolic function [ejection fraction (EF); LVEF] was assessed by modified Simpson’s method. A 16-segment model was adopted for the analysis of regional wall motion of LV: basal anterior, basal anteroseptal, basal inferoseptal, basal inferior, basal inferolateral, basal anterolateral, mid anterior, mid anteroseptal, mid inferoseptal, mid inferior, mid inferolateral, mid anterolateral, apical anterior, apical septal, apical inferior and apical lateral segments. Regional wall motion of the segments was reported as normal, mild hypokinesia, moderate hypokinesia, severe hypokinesia, akinesia without thinning and akinesia with thinning, and graded on a 6-point score (1 = normal, 2 = mild hypokinesia, 3 = moderate hypokinesia, 4 = severe hypokinesia, 5 = akinesia without thinning and 6 = akinesia with thinning).

All echocardiograms were reviewed by 2 independent cardiologists (K.M-J, J.HJ) who reached a consensus on the diagnoses in a blinded fashion.

Operative procedures

Complete revascularization, defined as at least 1 graft to each of the significantly diseased major coronary artery territories (left coronary artery territories with ≥70% stenosis diameter and right coronary artery territories with ≥90% stenosis diameter shown on invasive coronary angiography), was performed in all patients regardless of the presence of akinetic segments on a preoperative echocardiogram. The internal thoracic artery (ITA) was harvested using a semi-skeletonization technique, in which the whole length of ITA along with accompanying veins and surrounding tissue were mobilized from the endothoracic fascia [5]. The saphenous vein (SV) harvested with the no-touch technique was commonly used as the second conduit of choice. Harvested no-touch SV was mostly used as a composite graft based on the ITA or occasionally used as a free graft anastomosed to the ascending aorta. In patients who had an SV of poor quality, a right gastroepiploic artery (RGEA) or contralateral ITA was harvested and used as a composite graft based on the left ITA.

Transit-time flow measurement (Medi-Stim AS, Oslo, Norway) to verify the anastomosis status was performed after each anastomosis and just before pericardial closure [6]. All patients received preoperative aspirin therapy (100 mg daily) until the day of surgery and resumed postoperatively as soon as possible (usually postoperative day 1). Clopidogrel (75 mg daily) was added simultaneously to the aspirin for 1 year postoperatively. If the patient had a high blood level of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (>70 mg/dl), statin therapy was initiated and maintained postoperatively.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analysis was performed with R software, version 4.2.2. Continuous data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed variables or as medians (interquartile ranges) for non-normally distributed variables, according to the Shapiro–Wilk test; and categoric data were expressed as counts (percentages).

Comparison of changes in regional wall motion of LV between groups was performed using a linear mixed model with time and group as the fixed effect and segment of the patient as the random effect. Further analyses were performed to demonstrate whether differences in changes of regional wall motion existed between akinetic segments with and without thinning, and according to the coronary artery territories. Changes in LVEF according to time were analysed.

RESULTS

Patients and follow-up

Of the 127 patients who underwent isolated CABG between April 2021 and December 2022, 101 patients who underwent complete revascularization, who were assessed by preoperative, before discharge [median 7 (6, 11) days postoperatively], postoperative 3- [median 3.3 (3.1, 4.1) months] and 12-month [median 12.1 (12.0, 12.3) months] echocardiographic examinations, and who showed all patent grafts at postoperative 1-year (12.05 ± 0.41 months) angiograms were included in the present study (Table 1). Eighteen patients who did not undergo postoperative 1-year coronary angiograms (5 patients died before postoperative 1-year, and 13 patients refused angiographic follow-up studies) were excluded. Postoperative angiograms performed in 109 patients showed a 1-year patency rate of 96.5% (306 of 317 anastomoses), and 8 patients who showed ≥1 occluded graft also were excluded from this study. A total of 1616 segments from 101 patients who had all patent grafts at 1-year angiography were divided into 6 segment groups according to the wall motion abnormalities: normal (N = 1083), mild hypokinesia (N = 2), moderate hypokinesia (N = 74), severe hypokinesia (N = 150), akinesia without thinning (N = 259), and akinesia with thinning (N = 48) (Fig. 1).

Operative data

The CABG procedures were performed using in situ left ITA-based SV composite grafting (N = 84), in situ left ITA and free SV aortocoronary bypass grafting (N = 7), in situ right ITA-based SV composite grafting (N = 5), in situ left ITA-based RGEA composite grafting (N = 3) and in situ left ITA-based right ITA composite grafting (N = 2). Off-pump CABG was performed in 94 patients (93.1%). A total of 298 anastomoses were performed in 101 patients with a median of 3 [2,4] anastomoses per patient. The number of distal anastomoses to left anterior descending (LAD), left circumflex (LCX), and right coronary artery (RCA) territories were 131, 91 and 76, respectively.

Postoperative echocardiographic results

Serial LVEF measured preoperatively, before discharge, 3 months and 12 months postoperatively were 0.48 ± 0.14, 0.49 ± 0.12, 0.49 ± 0.10 and 0.54 ± 0.10, respectively. The LVEF increased significantly over time during the postoperative 12-month period (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2A). When the patients were divided into 2 groups [patients with preoperative LVEF ≤0.35 (N = 23) vs patients with preoperative LVEF >0.35 (N = 78)], improvement of LVEF during the postoperative 12 months was significantly higher in the patient group with LVEF ≤0.35 than in the patient group with LVEF >0.35 (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2B).

(A) Changes in left ventricular ejection fraction during the postoperative 12 months. (B) Changes in left ventricular ejection fraction of patients with ejection fraction ≤0.35 vs > 0.35 during the postoperative 12 months. LV: left ventricular; EF: ejection fraction.

When all the 1616 segments were divided into 2 groups [segments without akinesia (N = 1309) vs segments with akinesia (N = 307)], the serial wall motion scores measured preoperatively, before discharge, and at 3 months and 12 months postoperatively were 1.5 ± 1.0, 1.4 ± 1.1, 1.5 ± 1.2 and 1.2 ± 0.8, respectively, in the segment group without akinesia, and 5.2 ± 0.4, 4.5 ± 1.3, 3.2 ± 1.9 and 3.1 ± 1.9, respectively, in the segment group with akinesia (Fig. 3). Wall motion scores tended to decrease over time during the postoperative 12 months in both groups (P < 0.001), and improvement of wall motion during the postoperative 12 months was significantly higher in the segment group with akinesia than in the segment group without akinesia (P < 0.001). When akinetic segments were further divided into 2 groups [akinetic segments without thinning (N = 259) vs with thinning (N = 48)], the serial wall motion scores measured before discharge, and at 3 months and 12 months postoperatively were 4.3 ± 1.3, 3.1 ± 1.8 and 2.9 ± 1.9, respectively, in the akinetic segment group without thinning, and 5.6 ± 0.5, 4.0 ± 2.0 and 4.1 ± 1.9, respectively, in the akinetic segment group with thinning. Wall motion scores of both groups tended to decrease over time during the postoperative 12 months; however, improvement of the wall motion during the postoperative 12 months was not significantly different between the 2 groups (P = 0.711) (Fig. 4). When the changes in wall motion were examined according to coronary artery territories, the serial wall motion scores measured preoperatively, before discharge, and at 3 months and 12 months postoperatively were: 2.2 ± 1.7, 2.0 ± 1.7, 2.0 ± 1.7 and 1.6 ± 1.4, respectively, in the LAD territory; 1.9 ± 1.5, 1.9 ± 1.5, 1.8 ± 1.4 and 1.3 ± 1.0, respectively, in the LCX territory; and 2.4 ± 1.8, 2.2 ± 1.8, 2.2 ± 1.8 and 1.8 ± 1.5, respectively, in the RCA territory (Fig. 5). Wall motion scores in all 3 coronary artery territories tended to decrease over time during the postoperative 12 months (P < 0.001), and there were no significant differences among the 3 coronary artery territories (P = 0.629).

DISCUSSION

The present study demonstrated two main findings. First, LVEF significantly improved over time during the postoperative 12 months after CABG, and the improvement was higher in the patient group with LVEF ≤0.35 than in the patient group with LVEF >0.35. Second, wall motion tended to improve over time during the postoperative 12 months after CABG, and the improvement of the wall motion over time was significantly higher in the segment group with akinesia than in the segment group without akinesia.

In patients with ischaemic LV dysfunction, complete revascularization has demonstrated improvement of LV function [7–9]. The clinical and prognostic benefits of complete surgical revascularization are based on the premise that the grafts maintain their patency after CABG. Although the SV has been and is still a widely used conduit for CABG, the SV has shown decreasing patency rates with time resulting in worse clinical outcomes compared with patency and outcomes of arterial grafts [10, 11]. In a previous study examining serial comparison of graft patency rates early postoperatively, 1 year and 5 years after CABG, SV grafts showed a significantly decreased patency rate during the first postoperative year. The ITA patency rates also decreased significantly during the follow-up period, although the ITA demonstrated a > 90% patency rate at 5 years postoperatively [12]. Because complete revascularization does not guarantee the graft patency and benefits of CABG, we examined the change of myocardial function and wall motion by echocardiography in CABG patients with proven graft patency on follow-up coronary angiograms. In the present study, the LVEF significantly increased over time during the postoperative 12 months, and the improvement was significantly higher in the patient group with LVEF ≤0.35 than in the patient group with LVEF >0.35.

For the echocardiographic assessment of regional LV function, the LV is divided into segments. Segmentation schemes reflect coronary perfusion territories, resulting in 16, 17 or 18 segments with comparable myocardial mass, and allowing standardized communication within echocardiography [13]. In the present study, a 16-segment model was adopted for regional analysis of the LV. In echocardiography, regional myocardial function is assessed based on the wall thickening and endocardial motion of the segment. A semi-quantitative wall motion score can be assigned to each segment to calculate the LV wall motion score index, and the present study adopted a 6-grade scoring (1 = normal, 2 = mild hypokinetic, 3 = moderate hypokinetic, 4 = severe hypokinetic, 5 = akinetic without thinning and 6 = akinetic with thinning) instead of the 4-grade scoring (1 = normal, 2 = hypokinetic, 3 = akinetic and 4 = dyskinetic) recommended by an update from the American Society of Echocardiography and the European Association of Cardiovascular Imaging [13]. Although subdivision into 6-grade scoring may cause suboptimal reproducibility and inter-vendor measurement variability, wall motion assessment frequently needs to be subdivided to reflect the differences in the same grade.

A myocardial segment is defined as akinetic if there is no systolic myocardial thickening, even if a slight inward endocardial motion is present during systole on echocardiography [3]. A study by Weiss et al. [14] examined the correlation of echocardiography with postmortem histopathology. While 100% of the pathologically transmural infarcted segments were akinetic or dyskinetic, 8% of the akinetic or dyskinetic regions were histologically normal and 29% of the akinetic or dyskinetic regions were subendocardially infarcted zones. Akinetic myocardial segments may represent stunned, hibernating or non-viable infarcted myocardium, and are not completely reversible after revascularization because revascularization will lead to improved function in the stunned or hibernating myocardium but not in the non-viable myocardium [7]. In addition, the presence of regional akinesia is known to be a predictor of mortality in patients with coronary artery disease [15]. In a previous study by Ismael et al. [16] which included 121 hypokinetic and 13 akinetic or dyskinetic segments from 50 patients, there was an improvement in wall motion scores of hypokinetic segments but there were no changes in the akinetic or dyskinetic myocardial segments during the postoperative 3 months after CABG. On the contrary, another study by Soraas et al. [17], which included 69 hypokinetic and 32 akinetic segments from 42 patients, showed improvement of segmental wall motion in 35 hypokinetic and 4 akinetic segments at a median of 7 weeks after CABG. The present study included 307 akinetic segments of a total 1616 segments from 101 patients and showed that improvement in wall motion during the postoperative 12 months was significantly higher in the segment group with akinesia than in the segment group without akinesia. Wall motion tended to improve in both akinetic segments without and with thinning during the postoperative 12 months and there were no differences in wall motion score decrease between the akinetic segments without and with thinning. The present study suggests that akinetic segments still have viable myocardium in addition to non-viable infarcted myocardium, and the wall motion of akinetic segments could be improved by complete patent revascularization.

Some previous studies, which had no mention of graft patency, showed that there were differences in wall motion improvement according to coronary artery territories after CABG [18, 19]. The present study included CABG patients who underwent complete revascularization and had all patent grafts, and showed that segmental wall motion of all three coronary artery territories improved after CABG and that no significant differences among the three coronary artery territories were observed.

In conclusion, the LVEF and regional wall motion improved during the postoperative 12 months after patent revascularization, regardless of the presence of akinetic segments. Complete revascularization including akinetic myocardium should be considered when performing CABG because the stunned or hibernating myocardium might also be included in the akinetic segments.

Study limitations

This study has limitations that must be recognized. First, it was a retrospective study performed at a single institution. Second, the echocardiography is not a perfectly reproducible study and is operator-dependent, which can lead to bias in assessment of ischaemic cardiomyopathy. Third, a 6-grade scoring was used to assess wall motion instead of a 4-grade scoring recommended by an update from the American Society of Echocardiography and the European Association of Cardiovascular Imaging. Subdivision into 6-grade scoring may cause suboptimal reproducibility and inter-vendor measurement variability. Fourth, the medications that might have influence on the results, such as sacubitril or dapagliflozin, were not included in the analyses.
