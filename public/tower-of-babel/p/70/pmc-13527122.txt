A pilot study of the effect of norepinephrine dose on left ventricular-arterial coupling in patients with septic shock
Authors: Xueqing Wang, Yunyin Xu, Xuwei Lin, Yingxin Lin, Bin Huang, Qing Feng, Ying Shan, Lei Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Left ventricular-arterial (V-A) coupling is a key determinant of cardiovascular performance, which integrates intrinsic myocardial contractility with ventricular afterload. Septic shock frequently disrupts this coupling, and norepinephrine (NE) may worsen it in some scenarios. In this study, we aimed to quantify the dose–response relationship between NE and V-A coupling and to test for interactions between NE and elapsed treatment time or cumulative infusion volume (IV) in patients with septic shock. In this single-center prospective cohort study, NE dose, hemodynamic indicators, IV, and pulse indicator continuous cardiac output (PiCCO) related parameters were collected every 3 h for 3–24 h and every 6 h for 24–48 h. The V-A coupling index was computed as the ratio of arterial elastance (Ea) to ventricular end-systolic elastance (Ees). Linear mixed effects models were used to estimate the effects of NE dose on Ea/Ees: Model 1 included NE dose, time, and interaction between NE and time (NE×Time); Model 2 added sex, age, body mass index, central venous pressure (CVP), IV, NE×Time, and NE×IV. The NE dose correlated positively with Ea/Ees. NE×Time was non-significant, indicating that the relationship between NE and Ea/Ees did not change over time. Each 1000 mL increase in IV decreased the Ea/Ees by 0.360; NE × IV showed no significance. High-dose NE exacerbated V-A decoupling without a time-dependent change. Cumulative IV modestly mitigated uncoupling, whereas the interaction between NE and IV was non-significant.

Introduction

Sepsis, especially septic shock, is a serious threat to human health. Although a Sepsis-3 guideline, with detailed and specific practice recommendations, has been proposed, mortality rates have not tended to decrease1. The hemodynamic profile of septic shock is characterized by vasodilation, leading to severe hypoperfusion with secondary myocardial impairment2. To maintain blood pressure and improve organ perfusion, guidelines advocate early fluid resuscitation and vasopressors. Norepinephrine (NE) is recommended as a first-line vasopressor to maintain mean arterial pressure (MAP)1,3. However, the cardiovascular action of NE is complex, and the relationship between NE and MAP is not purely positive and linear4.

The concept of left ventricular-arterial (V-A) coupling was first proposed by Suga5 and then elucidated by Sunagawa et al.6, who recognized it as a determinant of cardiovascular performance and cardiac energetics2,3,7. V-A coupling integrates ventricular afterload and cardiac pump, defined as the ratio of arterial elastance (Ea) to ventricular end-systolic elastance (Ees)2,7. When Ea equals Ees, the cardiovascular system functions most efficiently with the least energy expenditure2,8,9. Ea is a comprehensive index that includes all left ventricular afterload factors, and tends to increase in hypertensive crisis or during NE administration. In contrast, Ees reflects myocardial contractility and tends to significantly decrease in heart failure and acute myocardial infarction2,10. Under conditions of aging or disease not associated with hemodynamic stability, the absolute values of Ea and Ees may vary to some extent, but the ratio of Ea/Ees remains within the normal range7,9. However, in septic shock, Ea/Ees increases abnormally, with increasing Ea and decreasing Ees induced by the general use of NE and deteriorating myocardial contractility, respectively2,3. Despite some studies showing that NE may improve V-A coupling11,12, more studies seem to suggest the opposite13,14, or that the effects depend on the patient’s response to NE15–17. The basic effect of NE is mediated by the activation of α1- and β1-receptors, but the actual situation in septic shock is much more complex and varied, involving the interplay of multiple factors such as treatment duration, fluid resuscitation, heart rate (HR), MAP level, and combination agents3,17,18.

Given the controversial findings regarding the effect of NE on V-A coupling in septic shock and the lack of studies on the interaction with time and potential covariates, we conducted this pilot study. Introducing the time dimension allows for better discussion of therapeutic effects, dynamic hemodynamic changes, and disease progression.

Methods

In this prospective cohort study, data were collected from March 2025 to July 2025 in the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) of Peking University Shenzhen Hospital (Shenzhen, China). The institutional ethics committee of Peking University Shenzhen Hospital reviewed and approved the study protocol (approval number: IRB of Peking University Shenzhen Hospital [2025] 015th), which conformed to the ethical standards for medical research involving human subjects as laid down in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The protocol has been registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR) under the identifier ChiCTR2500099337 on March 21, 2025. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their next of kin before enrollment in the study. The study was designed and written according to the checklist Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology Cohort Study19.

Patients

Patients were included according to the following criteria: (1) age > 18 years; (2) diagnosed of sepsis and septic shock according to the “Surviving Sepsis Campaign: International Guidelines for Management of Sepsis and Septic Shock 2021”1; (3) use of NE to maintain blood pressure; and (4) patients were consecutively enrolled among those receiving pulse indicator continuous cardiac output (PiCCO) monitoring. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) septic shock for more than 12 h since diagnosis; (2) patients with acute myocardial infarction, acute pulmonary embolism, or a history of cardiomyopathy and heart failure; (3) patients who received intra-aortic balloon pump or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation; and (4) pregnant women.

Left ventricular-arterial coupling measurements

PiCCO monitoring was performed (Mindray, China) to calculate V-A coupling index, and the global end-diastolic volume (GEDV) and stroke volume (SV) were obtained by taking the average of three injections of 15 mL ice saline solution. Systolic blood pressure (SBP) was directly recorded as the real-time invasive arterial pressure, which was placed in the femoral artery. Ea was calculated as left ventricular end-systolic pressure to SV (ESP/SV), which was estimated as SBP/SV20, and Ees was estimated as SBP to left ventricular end-systolic volume (ESV). ESV was calculated as the difference between left ventricular end-diastolic volume (EDV) and stroke volume (SV), with EDV estimated as GEDV/4; thus, ESV ≈ GEDV/4 − SV21–24. V-A coupling was measured as Ea/Ees.

Data collection

Demographic information (age and sex), body mass index (BMI), source of infection, comorbidities, length of ICU stay, and 28-day mortality were collected. Procedures including invasive mechanical ventilation and renal replacement therapy during ICU stay, scores containing Sequential Organ Failure Assessment‌ (SOFA) and Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II), and laboratory indicators including lactate, total bilirubin, creatinine, and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) at baseline were recorded. Real-time hemodynamic parameters (HR, SBP, diastolic blood pressure [DBP], MAP, central venous pressure [CVP], cardiac output [CO], and SV), NE dose and duration, Ea, Ees, and Ea/Ees were recorded or calculated at 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 21, 24, 30, 36, 42, and 48 h after enrollment. The accumulated infusion volume (IV) and dose of other combined vasoactive agents at each time point were also recorded. The vasoactive inotropic score (VIS) was calculated to assess the total dose of vasoactive agents25.

Statistical analysis

The distribution of continuous variables was assessed primarily using graphical methods, including visual inspection of histograms. Variables that appeared approximately symmetric and bell-shaped were approximately normally distributed and are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD); otherwise, they are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages (%). The missing values of each parameter were less than 20%, so Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations (mice) was used to impute the missing data.

Linear mixed effects models (LMMs) were used to quantify the association of NE dose with Ea/Ees. A random-intercept LMM was fitted to account for the correlation of repeated measurements within the same participant, with participant included as a random effect and all explanatory variables modeled as fixed effects. Model assumptions were evaluated using standard diagnostic procedures, including visual inspection of residual-versus-fitted plots to assess linearity and homoscedasticity, Q-Q plots of residuals to examine the approximate normality of residuals, and inspection of the distribution of random effects. Given the repeated-measure design and the relatively small number of participants, these diagnostic results were interpreted with caution.

Model 1 was specified as a minimally adjusted model and included NE dose, time, and the interaction term NE × time to evaluate the primary association and its potential time-varying effect. Model 2 further adjusted for sex, age, BMI, CVP, and IV, together with the interaction terms NE × time and NE × IV. These covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance, biological plausibility, and their potential roles as confounders of the association between NE dose and Ea/Ees.

To test whether HR or MAP mediated the effect of NE on Ea/Ees, bootstrap-based mediation analyses (1000 resamples) were performed; a 95% confidence interval (CI) that crossed zero indicated no mediation effect. Moreover, MAP and HR were separately introduced into Model 2 as sensitivity analyses; the stability of the NE estimates under these additions confirmed the robustness of the primary results. All statistical analyses were conducted in R software 4.3.3. P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

As shown in Fig. 1, this study included 16 patients diagnosed with sepsis and septic shock. Table 1 summarizes the population’s basic characteristics. Briefly, 31.3% patients died within 28 days. Most patients were male. The included patients were critically ill at baseline, presenting with high lactate levels and SOFA and APACHE II scores. All patients accepted fluid resuscitation, high-dose NE (1.27 ± 0.89 µg/kg/min), and even other vasopressors (elaborated with VIS) to maintain MAP. The patients exhibited an increased Ea and a significantly decreased Ees, resulting in an extremely high Ea/Ees ratio at baseline.

Description of the vasoactive agent combination

A total of 184 NE dose values from 16 included patients were collected. All patients were administered NE at the beginning of enrollment, and six values of NE dose were reduced to zero in the middle or at the end of the process. Therefore, 178 NE usage values were collected, accounting for 96.7% of the data. Seven patients were administered either dobutamine (DB) or vasopressin (VP). Only 3 patients used metaraminol or methylene blue. In addition, 5 of the 16 patients used NE alone without a vasoactive agent combination, while 5 used another vasoactive agent combination. Four patients were treated with NE and two other types of vasoactive agents, while the other two used more than three combinations (Table 2).

Relationship between NE dose and left V-A coupling and its interaction with time and volume expansion

To evaluate the relationship between NE and V-A coupling, as well as its interaction with time and IV, an LMM was employed, and two models were developed (Table 3). In the unadjusted Model 1, each 0.1 µg/kg/min increase in NE dose was associated with a 0.140 increase in Ea/Ees. The time and interaction between NE and time (NE×Time) showed no significant effects.

After adjusting for sex, age, BMI, CVP, IV, and two interaction terms (NE×Time and NE×IV) (Model 2), the effect of NE remained positive and significant: each 0.1 µg/kg/min increase in NE dose corresponded to a 0.101 increase in Ea/Ees. None of the covariates—sex, age, or BMI—were independently associated with Ea/Ees. CVP showed a modest positive association, whereas each 1000 mL increment in cumulative IV volume was linked to a 0.360 reduction in Ea/Ees. The interaction terms NE × Time and NE × IV were not statistically significant, indicating that the relationship between NE and Ea/Ees did not change over time or with cumulative volume.

Mediation effects of MAP and HR on the association between NE and left V-A coupling

Bootstrap-based mediation analyses were conducted to explore whether HR or MAP mediated the effect of NE dose on Ea/Ees. The estimated indirect effects of HR and MAP were 0.103 (95% CI: −0.025 to 0.272) and − 0.106 (95% CI: −0.269 to 0.031), respectively. Because both 95% CIs crossed zero, neither HR nor MAP appeared to mediate the relationship between NE dose and Ea/Ees.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the association between NE and Ea/Ees. When HR was added to Model 2, each 0.1 µg/kg/min increase in NE dose remained significantly associated with a 0.094 increase in Ea/Ees (p = 0.002; 95%CI: 0.035 to 0.154); likewise, after including MAP, the NE estimate was 0.115 per 0.1 µg/kg/min increment (p < 0.001; 95% CI: 0.055 to 0.175). In both sensitivity analyses, an increase in NE dose was associated with a higher Ea/Ees, which is consistent with the results of the main analysis and confirm the robustness of the primary findings.

Discussion

In this study, we found that high-dose NE worsens V-A decoupling in septic shock. Elevated CVP tends to deteriorate uncoupling, whereas cumulative volume expansion alleviates it. The relationship between NE and Ea/Ees was time-independent and showed no interaction with volume.

It is important to note that our study population consisted of patients requiring intensive hemodynamic monitoring and high-dose vasoactive agents, who likely represent a more severe phenotype of septic shock. In early septic shock, NE may improve V-A coupling by restoring Ea in the setting of vasoplegia, thereby normalizing the Ea/Ees ratio26. Concurrently, it augments SV and CO in preload-responsive patients by decreasing venous capacitance and converting unstressed blood volume into stressed volume, which enhances venous return. These effects, complemented by improved coronary perfusion, the Anrep effect, and low-dose β-adrenergic inotropic stimulation, collectively enhance cardiovascular efficiency and coupling during the initial resuscitation phase27. Conversely, in the advanced phases of septic shock, escalated doses of NE may precipitate V-A decoupling, as evidenced by our findings.

This vulnerability to V-A coupling in severe phenotypes highlights the complex interplay between medically elevated afterload and sepsis-induced myocardial depression. Indeed, Guarracino et al. and Zhou et al. reported that the incidence of V-A decoupling in patients with septic shock is extremely high, at approximately 80%28,29. Therefore, it is reasonable to conclude that the Ea/Ees level may be a reliable prognostic index for predicting poor prognosis29–31. Specifically, both Ea and Ees are altered in patients with V-A decoupling, but Ees showed a significant decrease while Ea may remain within a relatively normal range23,32; the reference ranges of Ea and Ees: 2.2 ± 0.8 mmHg/mL and 2.3 ± 1.0 mmHg/mL, respectively33, which is consistent with our results.

The variation of Ea/Ees may be more meaningful for disease assessment as the hemodynamic status changes with therapy or disease progression. Several studies have found that the Ea/Ees variability is associated with prognosis, indicating that survivors tend to have a significant decrease in the Ea/Ees ratio after treatment21–23,34. V-A decoupling may be solved by optimizing the Ea/Ees ratio30,34,35; however, the heterogeneity among individuals results in different responses to treatment, leading to difficulties in normalizing Ea/Ees. Fluid resuscitation and vasopressors are commonly used as standard of care for sepsis and septic shock, but a variable response is observed among patients, with SV or CO not improving in about half, and Ea/Ees variance lacking in non-responders13,15,16,21. The significant intercept variance in the unadjusted model (Model 1) reflected the heterogeneity of the Ea/Ees response to NE among individuals, implying that the underlying mechanism requires further investigation. It is possible that poor treatment responsiveness and V-A decoupling are mutually dependent3.

In addition to the heterogeneous response to NE, the potential mediating roles of HR and MAP warrant careful consideration. HR and MAP are the two variables most immediately influenced by NE through β1- and α1-receptor activation, respectively. Theoretically, sustained tachycardia increases arterial stiffness and shortens diastole, thereby reducing SV and subsequently elevating Ea. However, this effect could be counterbalanced by the concurrent fall in SV, leading to a mismatch between Ea and Ees. Consistent with this concept, Morelli et al. and Pasetto et al. have demonstrated that selective HR reduction with esmolol or ivabradine improves V-A coupling36,37. Conversely, the relationship between NE dose and MAP is not strictly linear: at low doses, NE produces a predictable rise in MAP; nevertheless, the increase in MAP gradually declines or even stops when the NE dose is increased because the receptor becomes saturated, and the vasopressor effect of NE is attenuated4. In addition, the efficacy of NE on MAP may be affected by homeostatic imbalances such as acidosis and hypoxemia in sepsis and septic shock, with wide inter-individual variation17. Despite these well-described physiological links, neither HR nor MAP significantly mediated the association between NE dose and Ea/Ees. These findings suggest that the detrimental effect of NE on V-A coupling operates independently of its chronotropic and vasopressor actions and may instead reflect the direct modulation of intrinsic myocardial contractility or arterial tone.

To further test whether our core finding might be biased by unmeasured hemodynamic confounding or over-adjustment, we repeated Model 2 after separately adding HR and MAP. In both re-fits the point estimate for NE changed by < 15% (0.094 and 0.115 versus 0.101 in the primary model) and remained highly significant. These results indicate that the positive association between NE dose and Ea/Ees is neither confounded nor mediated by the two variables most directly altered by NE infusion, reinforcing our conclusion that high-dose NE independently aggravates V-A decoupling in septic shock.

As a traditional parameter reflecting right atrial pressure and venous return, CVP was considered a covariate. The CVP rose modestly but significantly with Ea/Ees, which is mechanistically reasonable: an elevated CVP reflects increased right heart filling pressure that are transmitted backward, raising intrathoracic and pericardial constraints, thereby stiffening the left ventricle and arteries. Such parallel increases in ventricle and arterial elastance decouple the V-A system.

Conversely, the cumulative intravenous fluid volume displayed an inverse relationship with Ea/Ees; however, this finding should be interpreted with caution. Although fluid resuscitation influences CVP, the relationship is not strictly positive. In early-stage or less severe ill patients with sepsis or septic shock, fluid resuscitation increases preload, lengthens initial myocardial fibers, and enhances myocardial contractility38, which subsequently decreases HR and improves MAP, CO, and Ees13. Once the threshold is exceeded, myocardial fibers become overstretched, contractility declines, SV decreases, and V-A decoupling exacerbates39. Most patients in our cohort had already reached the plateau of the Frank–Starling curve, where excessive fluid rapidly increases CVP without generating additional SV. Their high lactate levels, elevated SOFA and APACHE II scores, and markedly reduced baseline Ees all indicated critical illness and severely impaired myocardial contractility. These observations suggest either a narrow effective volume window or a poor response to volume expansion. While theoretical results predict that extra volume could attenuate V-A uncoupling, any benefit is canceled by the disproportionate rise in right-sided and pericardial pressures, ultimately worsening Ea/Ees in these preload-unresponsive subjects. Critically, the interaction term NE × IV was non-significant, indicating that the amount of fluid already infused does not amplify or attenuate the deleterious effect of high-dose NE on V-A coupling. These findings imply that clinicians should consider the early introduction of other vasopressors rather than rely solely on titrating NE and volume expansion.

The next challenge is the selection and timing of vasopressor therapy. Although NE has been regarded as the first-line vasopressor in the setting of septic shock, the timing of its initiation remains obscure. The latest meta-analysis demonstrates that early introduction of NE in septic shock is associated with reduced mortality40. When it comes to vasopressor combination, VP is endorsed as a second-line treatment in Sepsis-3 when NE alone cannot maintain hemodynamic stability1; however, data on its effect on Ea/Ees are scarce. Although White et al. pointed out that early adjunctive VP has the potential to reduce mortality, solid clinical evidence is still required41. Furthermore, in a previous study, an animal model demonstrated that VP can aggravate V-A decoupling more than NE12. Because the fall in Ees outweighs the rise in Ea in septic shock, restoring Ees is the priority, and DB is recommended as the first-choice inotrope for this purpose3,8,13. Beyond increasing cardiac output and inducing peripheral vasodilatation42, DB also lowered HR in the study by Guarracino et al., further favoring coupling13. Levosimendan has also shown promise for improving V-A coupling in patients with coronary disease43, but reports in sepsis are sparse and conflicting3,44. Until recently, only one meta-analysis concluded that inotropes may be beneficial for survival45, yet trials specifically targeting Ea/Ees are lacking. In our cohort, VP and DB were the most common adjuncts to NE, but the limited data prevented us from discussing the combination medication firmly. Future research will focus on the impact of vasopressor combination on V-A coupling.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center prospective pilot study with a relatively small sample size at the subject level. Although 178 repeated measurements were collected, these observations were derived from only 16 independent participants, and in repeated-measure analyses, the effective statistical power and precision are determined primarily by the number of independent subjects rather than the total number of observations. Although LMMs were used to account for within-subject correlation, the limited number of participants may have reduced statistical power and affected the precision, stability, and robustness of the multivariable model estimates. Therefore, the findings of this study should be interpreted with appropriate caution and validated in larger prospective studies. Second, selection bias exists. We estimate the Ea/Ees ratio with PiCCO monitoring, a technique commonly performed for the most critically ill patients (reflected by markedly elevated lactate levels and high SOFA and APACHE II scores), thereby excluding milder cases with lower NE dose and Ea/Ees. The general high-dose NE and extremely high Ea/Ees without lower data make it impossible to conclude an optimal NE dose and a more integrated relationship to Ea/Ees. Consequently, the observed relationship between NE dose and V-A coupling in our study may not be generalizable to patients in the early stages of shock or those with low vasopressor requirements. Third, Ea/Ees is calculated using the PiCCO parameters instead of the single-beat method by Chen et al.46. Although the single-beat method is non-invasive and ready to use, uncontrollable factors influence the image quality, and it is demanding on the operator’s skills. ESV was estimated as (GEDV/4-SV), which may be underestimated, but the inaccuracies can be ignored considering the agreement with ultrasound assessment of left ventricular function21,24. Finally, the current data are insufficient to determine the optimal timing and ideal selection for combination vasoactive therapy, warranting further investigation. To address these limitations, a large-scale randomized controlled trial is currently underway to further investigate the interplay between combined vasopressor therapy and V-A coupling.

Conclusion

This study preliminarily demonstrates that high-dose NE worsens V-A decoupling and that this trend remains unchanged over time. Elevated CVP modestly exacerbated uncoupling, whereas cumulative volume expansion tended to normalize Ea/Ees; however, these results must be interpreted with caution. The harmful effect of high-dose NE on V-A coupling is not modified by the infused volume. Integrating established septic shock management variables into our analysis will allow us to refine hemodynamic decision-making and tailor therapy accordingly. Future work should include a larger sample that enrolls milder patients to delineate the full dose–response curve of NE versus Ea/Ees and determine its optimal dose. The isolated and interactive effects of volume expansion on Ea/Ees, together with the optimal timing and combinations of vasoactive agents, warrant further investigation.
