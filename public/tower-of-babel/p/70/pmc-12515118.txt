Mismatch-introduced crRNA guided PCR-CRISPR/Cas12a platform improves EGFR point mutation detection in single tumor cell
Authors: Meng Wu, Fang Wang, Yu Wang, Ya-Xian Wu, Bo-Yu Tian, Xin-Yuan Ou, Qin-Chen Xu, Xiao-Yan Wu, Chao Han, Wan-Li Liu, Shan Xing
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDynamic monitoring of epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations is essential for the early identification of resistance and treatment adaptation. Single-cell heterogeneity analysis is crucial for precision cancer medicine, yet sensitive and specific detection methods for individual tumor cells remain challenging. Here, we develop a PCR-CRISPR/Cas12a platform enhanced by the incorporation of mismatched base in crRNA at specific site for single-cell point mutation detection. This platform demonstrated high specificity and sensitivity, detecting point mutation at a frequency of 0.1% and in as low as 1.02 ng of genomic DNA, which represents an improvement over the amplification-refractory mutation system PCR (ARMS-PCR). Notably, the accuracy of the platform is highly consistent with next-generation sequencing (NGS), as evidenced by Kappa test values surpassing 0.9. By utilizing a conical-pore membrane with optimized porosity for single circulating tumor cell (CTC) enrichment, our platform enables point mutations detection in individual tumor cells, offering potential enhancements in precision and reliability for EGFR mutation analysis. Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), as the most common type of lung cancer, has extremely high incidence and mortality rates worldwide [1, 2]. The advent of targeted therapy has revolutionized the treatment landscape for NSCLC, particularly with the identification of key driver genes [3]. Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-driven NSCLC, for instance, has shown promising improvements in prognosis when treated with EGFR-tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) [4–6]. The classical EGFR mutation subtypes encompass deletion in exon 19 (ex19 Del) and point mutations, notably L858R in exon 21 and T790M in exon 20 [7–9], and the selection of targeted treatment plans is precisely tailored to these specific mutation subtypes [5, 10]. To ensure precise guidance for clinical medication, EGFR mutation evaluation has become a standard diagnostic test for patients with NSCLC [11, 12].

Tissue biopsy, though the gold standard for EGFR mutation detection [13], suffers from its invasiveness, making repeated sampling challenging [2, 14, 15]. As a result, liquid biopsy has garnered increasing attention. By utilizing a minimally invasive method to obtain body fluid samples, liquid biopsy offers dynamic insights into the disease through the detection of circulating free DNA (cfDNA) [16, 17], circulating tumor cells (CTCs) [18], and extracellular vesicles [19, 20]. Among these, characterizing EGFR at the single-cell level in CTCs can offer a more comprehensive understanding of tumor heterogeneity, providing novel insights into dynamic EGFR testing and drug resistance [21–23]. However, CTCs are present in extremely low quantities in the bloodstream [24, 25]. To address this, they must first be isolated and enriched from blood, typically based on their physical and biological characteristics [26]. Furthermore, this scarcity necessitates highly sensitive and specific downstream analytical methods, particularly for studying single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) such as EGFR mutations in single-cell genomes. Currently, common methods for detecting SNVs in single cells include sequencing technologies, droplet digital PCR (ddPCR), and amplification-refractory mutation system PCR (ARMS-PCR) [27–29]. However, these methods are often associated with high costs and complex procedures, limiting their broad clinical application. Hence, a high-sensitivity, cost-effective, and user-friendly EGFR mutation detection technology for single tumor cell is urgently needed.

The CRISPR/Cas12a system, initially discovered in bacteria and archaea as an adaptive immune defense against foreign nucleic acids [30, 31], has revolutionized gene editing and gene therapy [32, 33]. More recently, its applications have expanded into molecular diagnostics, particularly for the highly sensitive detection of various biological targets when combined with PCR or isothermal amplification techniques [34–37]. The remarkable sensitivity of CRISPR/Cas12a makes it well-suited for EGFR detection at the single-cell level. However, Cas12a has limited capacity for recognizing single-base mismatches, which may cause it to be susceptible to interference from the wild-type background during mutation site detection, potentially leading to nonspecific outcomes. To enhance detection specificity, several methods have been reported. One involves using chimeric DNA-RNA structures to substantially improve Cas12a's sensitivity to single base mismatches [38], while another utilizes toehold-mediated strand displacement and branch migration techniques to minimize the interference from wild-type backgrounds [39]. However, these methods necessitate complex crRNA or additional primer design and currently lack a comprehensive and systematic design framework.

Here, we design a PCR-CRISPR/Cas12a detection platform to enhance specificity in EGFR point mutation detection. Incorporating principles derived from ARMS-PCR primer design [40], we introduce mismatch near the crRNA mutation detection site, thereby achieving high specificity in identifying single-base mutations. The integration of amplification with the Cas12a reaction enable high-sensitivity detection, with a high sensitivity threshold of 0.1%, surpassing that of ARMS-PCR. Furthermore, by leveraging the larger size and reduced deformability of CTCs compared to most hematopoietic cells [41, 42], we employed the physical capture microfluidic technology to isolate tumor cells and successfully detected EGFR L858R and T790M mutations at the single-cell level. This demonstrated the high potential of future precision cancer diagnostics based on CTCs.

Materials and methods

Reagents

All the oligonucleotides purified by high-performance liquid chromatography was synthesized by Ruiboitech (Beijing, China); the corresponding DNA sequences and modifications are shown in Supplementary Table 1. FnCas12a used in this study was synthesized and purified in our laboratory [43, 44], and the plasmid was purchased from Addgene (Massachusetts, USA; 171,666). The FITC anti-human EpCAM antibody and Alexa Fluor 594 anti-human CD45 antibody were purchased from Biolegend (California, USA; 324,204 and 304,060, respectively). A microfluidic chip for single cell separation was provided by Anfang Biotech (Guangzhou, China). Other chemical reagents were purchased from Guangzhou Chemical Reagent Factory (Guangzhou, China), and RNase-free water was from TIANGEN Biotechnology (Beijing, China).

Cell lines and NSCLC samples

The human NSCLC cell lines, NCI-H1975 (containing the EGFR L858R/T790M mutation) and A549 (EGFR WT) were stored in our laboratory. Both cell Lines were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Thermo Fisher, MA, USA; C11875500BT) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, ExCell Bio, Suzhou, China; FSP500), at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

A total of 43 tissue DNA samples of NSCLC were obtained from Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center (SYSUCC) between January, 2022 and December, 2023. All the lung cancer cases were clinically pathologically confirmed according to American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC, 8th edition). The next-generation sequencing (NGS) was detected by the Department of Molecular Diagnostics of SYSUCC, and the results indicated that there were 20 cases with the L858R mutation, 6 cases with the T790M mutation (among which 4 cases had both mutations), and 21 cases without either mutation. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of SYSUCC (D2021-005).

Preparation of crRNA

We synthesized a single DNA strand and its complementary strand, which contained a T7 promoter sequence and the target crRNA sequence. The two complementary DNA single strands were denatured by Heating to 95 °C for 10 min, followed by annealing to room temperature to obtain dsDNA, which served as a template for transcription. The crRNA was then transcribed at 37 °C using the HiScribe T7 High-yield RNA Synthesis Kit (New England Biolabs, MA, USA; E2040S). DNase Ι (New England Biolabs, MA, USA; M0303S) was used to remove the template DNA, and the crRNA was separated and purified though phenol–chloroform extraction and isopropanol precipitation. The crRNA was resuspended in RNase-free water, quantified using a NanoDrop Microvolume Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher, MA, USA), and stored at − 80 °C until use.

Microfluidic chip-based single-cell isolation

The H1975 and A549 tumor cell lines were spiked into whole blood from healthy individuals. The tumor cells were then isolated from whole blood using a microfluidic chip (Anfang Biotech, Guangzhou, China) combined with immunofluorescence staining. The chip consists of a polycarbonate frame and a central conical-pore membrane. The frame includes a sample inlet and two Luer ports on the top layer, and a matching chamber with channels on the bottom layer. The membrane, fabricated from Ormocomp nanoimprint resin, is sealed between the layers and contains an array of conical-pores (top diameter: 7 μm, bottom diameter: 16 μm; membrane thickness: 22 μm). The porosity is 71.06% when calculated based on the larger (bottom) pore surface; however, on the smaller (top) pore surface—which acts as the filtration interface—the porosity is 13.6%. Whole blood samples were loaded onto the chip and filtered by gravity through the membrane. Captured cells were identified based on positive EpCAM and negative CD45 expression via immunofluorescence staining and manually selected under a microscope.

DNA extraction and PCR-CRISPR/Cas12a detection

DNA was extracted using the Cell Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (TIANGEN, Beijing, China; DP304-03) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Prior to extraction, cultured cells were counted and collected by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 1 min. After cell lysis, DNA was captured on the spin column provided in the kit and purified by removing proteins and cellular debris through centrifugation. Purified DNA was quantified by NanoDrop Microvolume Spectrophotometer in RNase-free water, and then diluted to 10 ng/μL for subsequent use.

For genomic DNA extraction from individual tumor cells spiked into whole blood, the cells are first isolated using the microfluidic chip and then lysed by incubation at 60 °C overnight. The lysis products were mixed with DNA purification magnetic beads (Vazyme, Nanjing, China; N411-01) and incubated at room temperature for 10 min to allow the beads to bind to the DNA. Subsequently, the purified DNA was amplified using the REPLI-g Advanced DNA Single Cell Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany; 150,365), repurified with DNA purification magnetic beads, quantified and diluted to 10 ng/μL for further use.

For PCR amplification, the DNA samples were amplified using KOD-Plus-Neo Enzyme (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan; KOD-401). The PCR products were then added to the CRISPR/Cas12a reaction system at a dilution ratio of 1:50. The reaction was performed at 37 °C for 2 h, during which real-time fluorescence kinetic detection was performed.

Gel electrophoresis

Proteins were analyzed using polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE), with bands visualized through Coomassie brilliant blue staining. Nucleic acids were observed and analyzed via electrophoresis with a 1% agarose and a 12% TBE-urea PAGE denaturing gel. Agarose gel electrophoresis was performed in 1 × TAE buffer at 130 V for 30 min, followed by band visualization under ultraviolet Light. TBE-urea PAGE denaturing gels were run at 120 V for 1.5 h in 0.5 × TBE buffer and stained by The Rapid Protein High Sensitivity Staining Kit (Biosharp, Beijing, China; BL620A).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism V.9.2.0 software. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to analyze differences between two groups, while the ANOVA with Dunnett's multiple comparisons test was employed to analyze differences among three or more groups. To evaluate the performance of the PCR-Cas12 platform, the maximal Youden index value was considered the cut-off value for each mutation, and the Kappa test was used to assess the consistency between the PCR-Cas12 platform and NGS results. All tests were two-tailed, and three replicates were performed to improve the statistics, and P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Highly specific point mutation detection based on PCR-CRISPR/Cas12a

We have established the PCR-CRISPR/Cas12a detection platform, which integrates PCR amplification with CRISPR technology, to achieve highly sensitive and specific detection of EGFR L858R and T790M point mutations. And our platform can be used for detecting point mutations in tissue and single tumor cell samples. The detection methodology encompasses three key steps: (1) Acquisition of genomic DNA: Genomic DNA is directly extracted from tissue samples or obtained via single-cell whole genome amplification from individual tumor cells captured using an microfluidic chip; (2) Target sequence amplification: Primers specifically designed to amplify the L858R and T790M sequences are utilized, and PCR is employed to amplify the target regions; (3) CRISPR/Cas12a Detection: Guided by specific crRNA, the CRISPR/Cas12a system recognizes and binds to the mutation targets, activating the trans-cleavage activity of the Cas12a protein. This leads to the efficient cleavage of short DNA reporter probes that are labeled with fluorescent and quencher groups, ultimately resulting in the release of a detectable fluorescent signal (Fig. 1A).

A PCR-CRISPR/Cas12a detection process: Genomic DNA extracted from tissue samples and single tumor cells undergoes PCR amplification. The amplified target activates the trans-cleavage activity of the Cas12a protein, which subsequently cleaves the single-stranded reporter DNA (ss-DNA reporter), releasing a detectable fluorescent signal. B Tolerance of the Cas12a-crRNA complex in recognizing DNA targets: The Cas12a protein can be activated when the target is either fully complementary to the crRNA or contains a single base mismatch. Design of crRNA based on traditional method

Prior to constructing the detection platform, we expressed and purified the FnCas12a protein, approximately 150 kDa in size (Supplementary Fig. 1 A). We confirmed an increase in fluorescence signal only when both target and crRNA were present (Supplementary Fig. 1B). The limit of detection (LOD) for target detection by FnCas12a was calculated to be 1.04 pM (Supplementary Fig. 1 C, D). These results confirmed the enzymatic activity of the FnCas12a protein.

According to the CRISPR/Cas12a detection principle, a Protospacer Adjacent Motif (PAM) sequence is critical for recognition by the Cas12a protein. The first few bases of the crRNA that bind to the target constitute the seed region, which is essential for the specificity [45]. Therefore, the design of crRNAs needs to be based on the PAM and seed region (Fig. 2A). Through the Gene database of NCBI, we obtained the reference sequences for exons 20 and 21 of the EGFR gene in Homo sapiens (Gene ID: 1956). Since no natural PAM sequences were found near the T790M mutation, we modified the original sequence CAGCTC by replacing guanine and cytosine with thymine through PCR primer design, resulting in the sequence CATTTC to form a PAM (TTTC). Subsequently, a completely complementary crRNA, named crRNA-20T, was designed to target the T790M mutation (Fig. 2B).

To validate the rationality of our crRNA design, we synthesized both wild-type and mutant sequences. When detecting the T790M mutant target, crRNA-20T showed a significant increase in fluorescence signal, whereas no such increase was observed with the wild-type target (Fig. 2C). Under ultraviolet radiation, the visual fluorescence intensity of the system after 6-h CRISPR/Cas12a reaction was significantly stronger in the presence of the T790M mutant target, compared to the wild-type target and the RNase-free water control (Fig. 2F). Urea-PAGE analysis of the CRISPR/Cas12a reaction products guided by crRNA-20T showed complete cleavage of the T790M mutant target after 30 min, while the wild-type target remained largely intact as an un-cleaved band (Fig. 2G). These combined results demonstrated the high specificity of the crRNA-20T designed for the T790M mutation.

Then we identified PAM sequences (TTTG) in proximity to the L858R mutation and accordingly designed crRNA-21G and crRNA-21C, where the ‘G’ and ‘C’ represented the guanine and cytosine at the mutation site in the sense and antisense strands of DNA. However, upon validating the specificity of crRNA-21G and crRNA-21C for the L858R mutation, we found that crRNA-21G exhibited no significant increase in fluorescence signal for either the L858R mutant target or the wild-type target. In contrast, crRNA-21C demonstrated an increase in fluorescence signal in reactions with both the L858R mutant target and the wild-type target (Fig. 2D). When compared to crRNA-20T, both crRNA-21G and crRNA-21C showed significantly lower signal-to-noise ratios (SNR, defined as the ratio of fluorescence signals from mutant to wild-type targets) at the endpoint (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, after 6 h of CRISPR/Cas12a reaction with crRNA-21G, the visual fluorescence intensity was comparable to that of the RNase-free water control. Conversely, both the wild-type and mutant groups with crRNA-21C showed significantly higher fluorescence intensity than the control (Fig. 2F). However, no significant difference was observed between the wild-type and mutant targets, indicating that neither crRNA-21G nor crRNA-21C achieve the same level of detection specificity for the L858R mutation as crRNA-20T targeted for T790M. And we selected crRNA-21C to analyze its CRISPR/Cas12a reaction products. Silver staining revealed that neither the wild-type nor mutant targets were completely cleaved after 1 h, but both were completely cleaved after 2 h (Fig. 2H). This indicates that crRNA-21C does not differentiate significantly between wild-type and mutant targets and, therefore, cannot fulfill the requirements for specific detection of the L858R mutation.

Enhanced crRNA specificity for point mutation detection through additional mismatches

Due to the tolerance of the Cas12a protein, traditional crRNA design cannot always meet the specificity requirements for point mutation detection. Some studies have shown that, despite the presence of the seed region, the CRISPR/Cas12a system remains insensitive to single base mismatch [46]. However, its tolerance for two or more mismatches is significantly reduced [47, 48] (Fig. 1B). Therefore, we propose incorporating additional mismatches near the point mutation site in the crRNA to enhance the specificity. Introducing these additional mismatches reduces the recognition capability of crRNA for the wild-type target, while preserving its recognition of the mutant target, thereby further improving the specificity of CRISPR/Cas12a detection (Fig. 3A).

Based on the previous results, we selected crRNA-21C, which exhibited higher detection efficiency, for further screening and modification. The objective is to investigate the impact of introducing different types of mismatches at various positions on the specificity of crRNA for detecting the L858R mutation. We designated the cytosine (C) at the mutation detection site on crRNA as the origin (0 coordinate), with upstream positions towards the 5' end labeled as −1, −2, −3, and downstream positions towards the 3' end as + 1, + 2, + 3. We discovered that incorporating additional mismatches into crRNA resulted in varying degrees of improvement in SNR. Specifically, crRNA-21C-6U, which introduced a U/G mismatch at position + 2, showed the most significant improvement. Compared to crRNA-21C, crRNA-21C-6U exhibited an approximately 3.76-fold increase in SNR (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 3B). Real-time fluorescence kinetic analysis demonstrated that the CRISPR/Cas12a system mediated by crRNA-21C-6U exhibited significantly better discrimination between wild-type and mutant targets compared to that mediated by crRNA-21C (Fig. 3C). This difference in recognition efficiency was also observed under ultraviolet radiation, where the fluorescence intensity of the mutant target group was notably distinct from both the wild-type target group and the RNase-free water group after 6 h reaction (Fig. 3D).

To further analyze the reaction products mediated by crRNA-21C and crRNA-21C-6U, we performed gel electrophoresis. The results showed that, at the same endpoint, the group mediated by crRNA-21C exhibited significant cleavage for both wild-type and mutant targets. However, the group mediated by crRNA-21C-6U showed reduced cleavage efficiency for the wild-type target, with a noticeable uncut band compared to the mutant group (Fig. 3E). Grayscale analysis of the relative DNA content revealed that, under crRNA-21C mediation, the intensity of the uncut band in the wild-type group was 3.97 times greater than that in the mutant group. Similarly, under crRNA-21C-6U mediation, the intensity of the uncut band in the wild-type group was 5.58 times higher than that in the mutant group, consistent with the observed band results (Fig. 3F). These results demonstrate that the crRNA-21C-6U, selected through the screening of introduced mismatches, exhibits enhanced specificity for point mutation detection.

To further investigate the interaction between crRNA and DNA within the Cas12a protein, we predicted the structure of the ternary complex using AlphaFold3 (Supplementary Fig. 2 A). The results indicated that the introduction of additional mismatches made the binding of crRNA-21C-6U to the wild-type target looser, while the binding to the mutant target unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 2 C). However, the predicted binding of crRNA-21C to either wild-type or mutant targets showed no significant difference (Supplementary Fig. 2B). This finding indicates the possible mechanism by which the introduction of additional mismatches enhances specificity. Additionally, to verify whether our mismatch-introduction approach could be applied to screen for other highly specific crRNAs targeting point mutations, we designed a crRNA targeting another EGFR point mutation, G719S. Through screening, we identified crRNA-18A-5U, which showed 2.40-fold increase in SNR compared to before screening (P < 0.0001; Supplementary Fig. 3). The details were described in the supplementary appendix.

Optimization and performance of the PCR-CRISPR/Cas12a detection platform

To maximize the detection efficiency of the PCR-CRISPR/Cas12a platform, we optimized the ion composition within the CRISPR/Cas12a system and its interface with PCR. We found that the presence of Na+ and K+ affects the activity of Cas12a (Supplementary Fig. 4 A, B), whereas an appropriate concentration of Mg2+ and suitable pH conditions are conducive to optimal Cas12a protein performance. Specifically, the highest detection efficiency was achieved with 12 mM Mg2+ and a pH of 7.4, using Tri-HCl at a concentration of 2 mM (Supplementary Fig. 4 C, D). Considering that PCR system components might also impact Cas12a activity, the PCR products were added to the CRISPR/Cas12a system at a ratio of 1:50 for optimal results (Supplementary Fig. 4E).

We designed amplification primers targeting the L858R and T790M and successfully amplified the relevant genomic DNA regions from synthetic targets and tumor cell lines (H1975 and A549) (Supplementary Fig. 6). Using the established detection platform, we successfully distinguished the presence of 0.1% mutant targets within a wild-type background with the mediation of crRNA-21C-6U (Fig. 4B), whereas distinguishing mutant targets at even a 5% frequency was challenging when using crRNA-21C (Fig. 4C). Moreover, both crRNA-21C-6U and crRNA-21C demonstrated similar detection sensitivities, with LOD of 315.26 aM, and 438.16 aM (Supplementary Fig. 5 A, B). These results indicate that the crRNA selected through the introduction of mismatches exhibits higher specificity and comparable sensitivity. For the detection of the T790M mutation, crRNA-20T achieved an LOD of 1090.70 aM (Supplementary Fig. 5 C), and successfully distinguished mutant targets at a frequency of 0.5% within a wild-type background (Supplementary Fig. 5D).

We compared our PCR-CRISPR/Cas12a system with ARMS-PCR which is commonly used for point mutation detection. Our platform successfully detected the presence of point mutations in 37.5 ng of genomic DNA (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 4D), with a measured LOD of 1.02 ng (Fig. 4F). In contrast, ARMS-PCR required 12,000 ng of genomic DNA to detect point mutations (P < 0.001) (Fig. 4E). These results demonstrated that the PCR-CRISPR/Cas12a platform exhibited significantly higher detection sensitivity compared to ARMS-PCR. In addition, to evaluate the stability of the PCR-Cas12a platform, we detected 10 μM and 100 μM target five times under identical conditions. The relative standard deviations (SDs) from the mean were 3.07% and 2.41% for 10 μM and 100 μM target, respectively, when detected by crRNA-21C-6U (Supplementary Table 3). Similarly, the relative SDs from the mean value was 3.56% and 1.39% for 10 μM and 100 μM targets, respectively, when detected using crRNA-20T (Supplementary Table 4), indicating that the PCR-Cas12a platform had good reproducibility.

Detection of tissue sample and single tumor cell using the PCR-CRISPR/Cas12a platform

We recruited 43 patients with NSCLC and divided them into the EGFR L858R mutation group (n = 20) and the wild-type group (n = 23) based on their NGS results. DNA was extracted from the tumor tissues of these patients, diluted to a uniform concentration, and then detected using the PCR-CRISPR/Cas12a platform. The blank-subtracted fluorescence signal in the L858R mutation group (median, 8098) was significantly higher than that in the wild-type group (median, 265.4; P < 0.0001) (Fig. 4G). Based on the maximum Youden index, the optimal cut-off value was determined to be 1637 AU. Among the 20 cases with L858R mutations, 18 were successfully detected. The Kappa test yielded a k value of 0.9050 (Fig. 4I), indicating high consistency between our detection results and those of NGS. In addition, we selected samples with the T790M mutation and 12 wild-type samples from these patients, dividing them into the EGFR T790M mutation group (n = 6) and the wild-type group (n = 12) for detection using the PCR-CRISPR/Cas12a platform. The results showed that the blank-subtracted fluorescence signal in the T790M mutation group (median, 4722) was significantly higher than that in the wild-type group (median, 1515; P < 0.001) (Fig. 4H). Based on the maximum Youden index, the optimal cutoff value for T790M detection was determined to be 2377 AU. All T790M samples were successfully detected. The Kappa test revealed perfect agreement between our results and those obtained by NGS (k = 1.0000) (Fig. 4I). These results demonstrate that the PCR/CRISPR-Cas12a detection platform is suitable for tissue sample analysis and exhibits high reliability.

To further validate the feasibility of the PCR-CRISPR/Cas12a platform for single-cell analysis, a high-porosity conical-pore membrane was incorporated. Based on the physical characteristics of tumor cells, the blood sample was passed through the conical-pore membrane, where larger tumor cells were retained. Residual white blood cells were distinguished via CD45-based fluorescence staining, while tumor cells were identified using EpCAM staining. The tumor cells can be picked, followed by automated selection. Subsequently, the isolated single tumor cells undergo single-cell whole-genome amplification, and point mutations are detected using our PCR-CRISPR/Cas12a platform (Fig. 5A).

Subsequently, we introduced NCI-H1975 (containing the EGFR L858R/T790M mutation) and A549 (EGFR wild-type) human lung cancer cells into whole blood samples from healthy individuals to simulate CTCs (Supplementary Fig. 7 A, B). Using the conical-pore membrane and fluorescence antibodies CD45 and EpCAM staining, we were able to observe NCI-H1975, A549 (green), and white blood cells (red) under a microscope (Fig. 5B). We selected a defined number of tumor cells for detection: single NCI-H1975 cells (grouped as Ha-Hc), three NCI-H1975 cells (grouped as Hd-Hf), and five NCI-H1975 cells (grouped as Hg-Hi) were selected as the experimental groups, while five A549 cells were used as the negative control group. The results showed that for L858R detection, the fluorescence signals of experimental groups were significantly higher than those of the negative control group (P < 0.0001), except for group Hc and He (Fig. 5C). For T790M detection, the differences between experimental groups and the negative control group were statistically significant, except for the group He (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 5D). Overall, the PCR-CRISPR/Cas12a platform demonstrates the capability to analyze mutations at the single-cell level, laying a foundation for its potential application in CTCs detection.

Discussion

In this study, we enhanced the specificity of the Cas12a system for point mutation detection by introducing an additional mismatched base in crRNA at specific sites. By incorporating PCR, we constructed a highly sensitive PCR-CRISPR/Cas12a detection platform with the LOD of 315.26 aM for synthetic target sequences. Our platform exhibits high specificity and sensitivity, outperforming ARMS-PCR. It also demonstrates high reliability, with results highly consistent with NGS and high reproducibility, featuring a relative SDs of less than 5%.

Through our study, we significantly mitigated the specificity limitations of CRISPR/Cas12a detection by introducing additional mismatch in crRNA. According to studies, the CRISPR system functions by Cas12a protein forming a ternary complex with target DNA under the guidance of crRNA [45, 49]. The crRNA forms an R-loop structure with double-stranded DNA and recognizes the target specifically through base complementarity [50, 51]. Several studies have demonstrated that the seed region of the crRNA is the basis for its specific recognition [52, 53], yet we observed that this recognition pattern exhibits a degree of tolerance, particularly when detecting point mutations, where specificity is significantly diminished. We hypothesize that this tolerance may be associated with the interaction dynamics between mismatched bases [54]. Specifically, when the repulsive force between mismatched bases is small, it can still trigger Cas12a's cis and trans-cleavage activities. Drawing inspiration from the primer design principles of ARMS-PCR [40], we propose introducing an additional mismatch near the mutation detection site in the crRNA to diminish its recognition of non-specific targets, thereby enhancing detection specificity. By employing this strategy, we successfully screened a highly specific crRNA-21C-6U for the L858R mutation detection. And through structural predictions of the ternary complex formed by protein, target DNA, and crRNA, we found that compared to crRNA-21C, crRNA-21C-6U exhibited looser binding to the wild-type target, but showed no significant difference in recognizing the mutant target. Additionally, other studies also showed that the Cas12a protein exhibits significantly reduced tolerance to double-base mismatches compared to single base mismatches [34, 55]. However, existing studies still lack clear guidelines for the patterns of mismatch introduction and crRNA design.

Therefore, based on this analysis, we have summarized the crRNA design strategy for point mutation detection: (1) Design the crRNA based on the potential PAM sequence near the point mutation site, ensuring that the point mutation detection site falls within the crRNA seed region. This can be achieved through the careful selection of the original PAM or through PCR-introduced modification. (2) If the crRNA designed according to the potential PAM sequence cannot distinguish between the wild type and the mutant type (SNR < 2), utilize the mismatch introduction method to modify the original crRNA. An additional mismatch is introduced by incorporating a different type of base at positions −3 to + 3 relative to the crRNA point mutation detection site, and crRNAs with the highest SNR were screened from these modifications. Although there are currently no definitive rules for directly selecting specific mismatches to optimize crRNA design, some studies have proposed using deep learning to screen for high-specificity crRNAs [56–58]. By integrating deep learning approaches, we anticipate developing more straightforward, reliable, and efficient methods for crRNA screening.

Additionally, we employed a conical-pore membrane to isolate and enrich single tumor cells from blood based on their larger physical size and lower deformability [41, 42]. This approach enables the analysis of individual tumor cells and providing a novel platform for detecting point mutations at the single-cell level. The membrane’s porosity was optimized by systematically evaluating capture efficiencies using tumor cell lines across membranes with varying pore geometries; the selected design maximized cell recovery. The combination of the conical pore structure and optimized porosity enables efficient enrichment of CTCs while minimizing the risk of clogging during filtration [59].

During this study, we observed that as the number of tumor cells we picked decreased, the difficulty in detecting mutations increased. We consider this may be related to the mutation rate of the selected tumor cell Line. We sequenced the DNA sample of 5 NCI-H1975 cells, and the result showed that the mutation rates of L858R and T790M were 81.32% and 72.25%, respectively. Therefore, the negative results observed in our experiments may be due to the selection of wild-type cells. These results validate the accuracy of our detection platform when applied to individual cell. Experimental validation confirmed that our platform could detect point mutations in 1.02 ng of genomic DNA, surpassing ARMS-PCR [60]. Additionally, compared to other single-cell genomic point mutation analysis techniques such as NGS and ddPCR, our PCR-CRISPR/Cas12a platform is not only highly specific and sensitive but also rapid, convenient and cost-effective (Supplementary Table 5), making it more suitable for efficient and repeatable liquid biopsy applications.

However, this study has several limitations. First, the PCR-CRISPR/Cas12a platform was validated primarily for EGFR mutations (L858R/T790M); its utility for other mutations needs further testing. Second, single-cell analysis required whole-genome amplification, which may skew mutation detection efficiency due to amplification bias. Third, large-scale studies using blood samples from cancer patients are warranted to further validate CTC-based detection reliability.

Conclusions

In summary, we have successfully established the PCR-CRISPR/Cas12a platform, and by introducing mismatches into crRNA, we achieved high-sensitivity and high-specificity detection of EGFR L858R and T790M point mutations. The detection sensitivity of the modified crRNA-21C-6U for the L858R site reached 0.1%, representing a 3.75-fold improvement compared to the original crRNA. Based on this, we have summarized a feasible scheme that can be used for crRNA design and screening specifically for point mutation.

Furthermore, we have verified the reliability of the PCR/CRISPR-Cas12a platform’s detection results through DNA analysis of clinical tissue samples. The PCR-CRISPR/Cas12a platform can serve as a more convenient and cost-effective method for identifying various gene mutations in the clinic. Additionally, by integrating microfluidic technology, we have achieved the detection of individual tumor cells in peripheral blood and accurately distinguished mutation types in single cell, offering a technical foundation for future studies on precision diagnosis and treatment.
