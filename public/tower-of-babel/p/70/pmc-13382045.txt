Clinicopathological, multimodal treatment, and survival patterns for men with breast cancer in England and Wales: a population-based cohort study
Authors: Jemma M. Boyle, Diana Withrow, Sarah Blacker, Liyang Wang, Christine Delon, Jibby Medina, David Dodwell, Mark Verrill, Kieran Horgan, David A. Cromwell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundMale breast cancer (BC) is rare and understudied, and the incidence is increasing. This study evaluated clinicopathological, multimodal treatment and survival patterns for men with BC (versus women) in England and Wales as part of the National Audit of Primary Breast Cancer (NAoPri). This audit is commissioned by the Healthcare Quality Improvement Partnership on behalf of NHS England and the Welsh government.MethodsThis retrospective population-based cohort study used pseudonymised patient- and tumour-level data for all adults (≥18 years) who were newly diagnosed (as per ICD-10 codes C50 and D05.1) with BC between Jan 1, 2015, and Dec 31, 2021 in England and Wales, supplied by the English National Cancer Registries and Analysis Services (NCRAS) and Wales Cancer Network (WCN), respectively. Cancer registration data were linked to various national datasets. Risk-adjusted treatment patterns for men and women with oestrogen-receptor (ER) positive early invasive breast cancer (EIBC) were analysed using multivariable logistic regression. Flexible parametric survival models and competing risk analysis estimated 5-year overall and cause-specific survival.FindingsOf the 340,164 people diagnosed during the study period, 2463 men (England: 2346, Wales: 117) were compared to 337,701 women. Men were older with more comorbidities and increased frailty burden. Men had more locally advanced, higher-grade, ER-positive, HER2-negative disease. Men with ER-positive EIBC had more surgery after age 70 years, less frequently received neo-adjuvant chemotherapy (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 0.63; 95% confidence interval (95% CI) 0.44–0.90) and more often received post-mastectomy radiotherapy for intermediate- and low-risk disease (aOR 1.28; 1.05–1.56 and aOR 2.61; 2.19–3.12). Endocrine therapy use was equivalent (aOR 1.46; 0.92–2.32). Breast cancer-specific death was comparable for men and women, respectively (standardised cumulative incidence function [CIF] 3.6% [95% CI: 2.9%–4.5%] vs 4.6% [4.5%–4.7%]). Death from other causes was greater in men (standardised CIF 12.0% [10.8%–13.2%] vs 7.7% [7.6%–7.8%]).InterpretationThese findings show that men with breast cancer presented differently to women. Despite breast cancer-specific survival being comparable for men and women, there were notable differences in the management of men with ER-positive EIBC which require further exploration, including increased surgery among older men, reduced neo-adjuvant chemotherapy, and increased post-mastectomy radiotherapy for intermediate- and low-risk disease.FundingNone.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

A rapid literature review was undertaken following identification of a comprehensive systematic review including 59 studies (1946–2021). PubMed was searched using the terms “male breast cancer” for any additional relevant full text articles published between March 2021 and December 2023. Previous research on male breast cancer is limited due to its rarity, although the incidence is increasing. There is limited inclusion of men with breast cancer within clinical trials and much of the applied evidence-base is extrapolated from that of post-menopausal women. Most of the larger volume publications on male breast cancer come from United States (US) observational studies using Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) and National Cancer Database (NCDB) data. They cover a subset of the US population (limiting their generalisability), and potential sources of bias include incomplete recording of key tumour characteristics and poor capture of oncological treatments.

Added value of this study

This study provides a comprehensive picture of clinicopathological, multimodal treatment and survival patterns for men and women with breast cancer diagnosed between 2015 and 2021 in the English and Welsh National Health Service (NHS). The study analysed data from all NHS breast units (98–99% case ascertainment) and found that men with breast cancer presented with more advanced disease than women. The finding of similar increased breast cancer incidence with greater affluence in men prompts re-evaluation of traditional breast cancer aetiological precepts which have largely focussed on female-specific risk factors (e.g., hormonal contraception/hormone replacement therapy use, parity and access to screening). There were also notable differences in the management of oestrogen receptor (ER) positive early invasive breast cancer (EIBC) for men compared to women including: increased use of surgery among older men, reduced use of neo-adjuvant chemotherapy, and increased use of post-mastectomy radiotherapy for intermediate- and low-risk disease. After accounting for differences in patient and tumour characteristics, breast cancer-specific mortality for ER-positive EIBC was similar in men and women.

Implications of all the available evidence

The evidence shows that men with breast cancer present with more advanced disease than women, highlighting the importance of public awareness and education. Differences in the management of men compared to women with ER-positive EIBC, especially the novel findings of more frequent surgery for older men and increased post-mastectomy radiotherapy for intermediate and low-risk patients, suggest that new consensus evidence-based guidelines for men would be beneficial parallelled by epidemiological exploration of potential gender-based clinical decision-making.

Introduction

Male breast cancer (BC) accounts for less than 1% of all breast cancer cases in the United Kingdom (UK), with incidence increasing over time.1, 2, 3 Although male BC represents a small proportion of all breast cancer diagnoses, it is an important and understudied area. There are significant gaps in the understanding of the pathogenesis and optimal management, and the few guidelines for the management of male BC have been extrapolated from the evidence-base for post-menopausal women.4,5

Very small numbers of men with breast cancer are included in clinical trials. Much of the information regarding male BC has been derived from observational studies. These were predominantly United States (US) data, notably the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results Program (SEER) or National Cancer Database (NCDB) datasets and, on average, have included 1000 to 3000 men.6, 7, 8

Although the studies provide valuable insights, they have several limitations which restrict their applicability. First, case ascertainment in the SEER and NCDB is lower than achieved in English cancer registry data (reported as 98–99% for breast cancer diagnoses) and the characteristics of the people within these datasets differ in various ways from the national US population which limits the generalisability of the study findings.9, 10, 11 Second, there have been issues with the inconsistent or inaccurate recording of key tumour characteristics including staging, oestrogen receptor (ER) and HER2 status.12, 13, 14, 15, 16 Concerns have also been raised regarding data quality for the recording of oncological treatments such as systemic anti-cancer therapy (SACT), radiotherapy and endocrine therapy (ET).6,17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24 For England, linkage to bespoke routinely collected datasets including the SACT dataset, Radiotherapy Dataset (RTDS) and Primary Care Prescriptions Dataset (PCPD) addresses these gaps.25, 26, 27, 28 Finally, most of these studies analyse data which are at least 10 years old.

This retrospective cohort study was undertaken to provide real-world evidence on clinicopathological, multimodal treatment and survival patterns for men with breast cancer in England and Wales. It uses contemporary, population-based data from all hospitals providing breast cancer care in the English and Welsh National Health Service (NHS) with the aim of overcoming some of the limitations in prior observational studies within this area. The study also contrasts the results for men and women within the same population and timeframe.

Methods

Study design and ethics

This retrospective population-based cohort study was performed as part of the National Audit of Primary Breast Cancer (NAoPri). The NAoPri is one of ten national cancer audits delivered by the National Cancer Audit Collaborating Centre (NATCAN) (see www.natcan.org.uk). These audits are commissioned by the Healthcare Quality Improvement Partnership (HQIP) on behalf of NHS England and the Welsh government. The audit is a continuous process, with routine evaluation of the patterns of care and outcomes for people with primary breast cancer in England and Wales.

The aims of the audit are to provide regular and timely evidence to NHS breast cancer services about where and why patterns of care vary and to support local quality improvement (see www.natcan.org.uk/primary-breast). The audit uses routine national datasets, including all patients captured within the English National Cancer Registries and Analysis Services (NCRAS) and Wales Cancer Network (WCN). Case ascertainment for the English Cancer registration data has been reported as 98–99%.10

The study was exempt from NHS Research Ethics Committee approval as it involved analysis of pre-existing pseudonymised linked data collated for the purpose of service evaluation, as part of the NAoPri. Approval for the release and use of the national data by NAoPri without individual informed consent was granted under General Data Protection Regulation Article 6(1)(e) (public task) and Article 9(2)(j) (scientific research/statistics) by the National Registration Service (reference: NIC-709865-W9X6H) and the WCN.

Data sources

The study used pseudonymised patient- and tumour-level data for all people newly diagnosed with breast cancer between 1 January 2015 and 31 December 2021 in England and Wales, supplied by the NCRAS and WCN, respectively. Cancer registration data were linked to various national datasets.

For England, this included: Cancer Outcomes and Services Dataset (COSD), Hospital Episodes Statistics Admitted Patient Care (HES APC), National Radiotherapy Dataset (RTDS), Systemic Anti-Cancer Therapy (SACT) dataset and Primary Care Prescription Database (PCPD). For Wales, this included: Patient Episode Database Wales (PEDW) and Cancer Network Information System Cymru (Canisc), but not detailed SACT information or PCPD data. Linked mortality information (including vital status, date and cause of death) was provided from the Office for National Statistics (ONS) death register for both England and Wales. PCPD data were available from March 2018 and therefore analyses using these data were restricted to men and women diagnosed from 2018 onwards.

Further information on specific inclusion criteria for the NAoPri can be found in the 2024 State of the Nation Report and associated Methodology documentation.29

Study cohort

The study cohort included all patients (men and women) aged 18 years or over, newly diagnosed with breast cancer, defined using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th edition (ICD-10) codes as C50 (malignant neoplasm of breast) and D05.1 (intraductal carcinoma in-situ of breast).

The cohort was stratified according to stage as per the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification system and hormone receptor status. The UICC staging system is based on anatomical TNM criteria and does not incorporate other prognostic variables such as ER/HER2 status. Most analyses focused on patients with ER-positive early invasive breast cancer (EIBC) (Supplementary Figure S1). Clinicopathologic and treatment patterns are also described for ER-positive locally advanced breast cancer (LABC) (Supplementary Tables S2 and S3).

Definition of outcome variables

Patients were classified as undergoing breast surgery if they had a breast cancer operation (breast-conserving surgery [BCS] or mastectomy [with or without immediate breast reconstruction]) recorded within HES/PEDW within the first 12 months after diagnosis. Within HES/PEDW, these surgical procedures were identified using the relevant Office of Population and Censuses and Surveys, 4th edition (OPCS-4) codes. Patients were classified as having received immediate breast reconstruction when the date of mastectomy and date of reconstruction matched. Most patients (95%) had surgery within 3 months of the date of diagnosis. The definitive surgical procedure was reported meaning that patients who had BCS followed by a completion mastectomy were recorded as having received mastectomy.

Patients were categorised as having received neo-adjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) if chemotherapy started within 6 months of diagnosis and occurred before the date of surgery. Adjuvant chemotherapy was defined as chemotherapy which began within the 4 months after the date of surgery. Patients were defined as receiving post-mastectomy radiotherapy (PMRT) if the radiotherapy started within the 6 months after the date of mastectomy. For patients who received adjuvant chemotherapy, PMRT could be given up to 12 months after the date of mastectomy. Post-BCS radiotherapy was not reported due to the small numbers of men having BCS.

English patients were defined as receiving endocrine therapy (ET) if they had a PCPD record for an ET prescription within 12 months of diagnosis, having undergone surgery (thus corresponding to neo-adjuvant or adjuvant ET, not primary ET).26 No information on ET was available for Wales.

Definition of patient and tumour characteristics

Gender is recorded as either “male” or “female” in the data provided and is defined as “gender as self-declared”. We acknowledge that some people may not identify using these binary male-female genders. Socioeconomic status was derived from the Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD).30 Patients are allocated to an IMD quintile based on the national ranking of the area corresponding to their postcode with Q1 being the most deprived and Q5 the least deprived.

The Royal College of Surgeons of England Charlson Comorbidity Index (RCS CCI) was used to identify specific comorbidities and is based on ICD-10 codes within inpatient HES or PEDW records at diagnosis or in the two years prior to diagnosis.31 The Secondary Care Administrative Records Frailty (SCARF) index was used to measure frailty.32

The dataset included overall stage (TNM), tumour grade, ER status and HER2 status. Overall stage was categorised into non-invasive disease (stage 0), EIBC (stage 1A–3A), LABC (stage 3B and 3C), metastatic breast cancer (stage 4), and a stage unknown category. T- and N-category are predominantly pathological staging for those undergoing surgery and pre-treatment staging for those who have had neo-adjuvant chemotherapy. For PMRT analyses, patients were divided into three groups based on their risk of recurrence: low-risk (T1-2, N0), intermediate-risk (T3, N0 or T1-2, N1), and high-risk (T1-2, N2 or T3, N1-2). This is in accordance with national guidelines and previous work.5,33

Statistical analysis

All data preparation and statistical analyses were conducted using Stata version 17. Throughout all analyses, the level of statistical significance was set at 0.05. There was no formal study protocol. The work followed an initial analysis plan which was iteratively revised.

Descriptive statistics (numbers, percentages, 95% confidence intervals (CI)) summarised overall patient and tumour characteristics as well as treatment patterns for both ER-positive EIBC and LABC, according to gender. A post-hoc analysis summarised the cumulative number of male breast cancer cases for English Cancer Alliances and Wales using a heat map to show the geographical distribution.

Age at diagnosis was analysed as a continuous variable and was approximately normally distributed. Mean age was compared between the two groups using an independent samples Student's t-test. Categorical variables were summarised as frequencies and percentages and compared between groups using the Chi-squared test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. A pre-specified sensitivity analysis was undertaken to compare the tumour characteristics of men and women according to breast screening eligibility age-groups (<50 years, 50 to 70 years, ≥70 years). A pre-specified sub-group analysis evaluated differences between men and women in the burden of comorbidities and frailty, stratified by age (<70 years, ≥70 years).

Comparisons of treatment patterns between men and women were performed using multivariable regression models and accounted for potential confounders. Binary outcomes were analysed using logistic regression and categorical outcomes with more than two unordered categories using multinomial logistic regression, with results reported as adjusted odds ratios or relative risk ratios, respectively, together with 95% confidence intervals. P values were derived from parameter Wald tests.

Multivariable models included the categorical variables comorbidity index (RCS CCI), frailty (SCARF index), T-category, N-category, HER2-status and tumour grade. Age was included as a continuous variable, and the non-linear relationship was modelled using a restricted cubic spline. Smoothed residuals were plotted against age to ensure that the spline fitted the data. Model discrimination and calibration were assessed using the area under the receiver operating curve (ROC) and a plot of observed versus expected values, respectively (Supplementary Material). The presence of multicollinearity was assessed using cross-tabulations. No adjustment of the significance levels was made for multiple comparisons.

The percentage of patients who had surgery for ER-positive EIBC was examined graphically by gender and age, using symmetric nearest neighbour smoothing. To investigate the association between age and the use of breast surgery by gender, we calculated the marginal effect of age from the logistic regression model after accounting for other patient and tumour factors.34 The marginscontplot command was used to graph model-based adjusted predictions across age with 95% confidence intervals to provide a more clinically interpretable display of adjusted effects than regression coefficients alone. The interaction between gender and age was tested using a Wald test.

Due to small numbers, survival outcomes were only examined for patients with ER-positive EIBC. The unadjusted 5-year overall survival (OS) was calculated using the Kaplan–Meier method. Adjusted OS at 5 years was estimated using a flexible parametric survival model including age at diagnosis (continuous variable), and categorical variables for comorbidity index (RCS CCI), frailty (SCARF index), T-category, N-category, HER2-status and tumour grade. The model demonstrated moderate discrimination, with a Royston D statistic of 0.5037. This suggests that the patient and tumour factors included in the model had some ability to distinguish between patients with better and worse survival outcomes. However, survival is also influenced by additional factors not fully captured in the model.

The baseline hazard was modelled using a restricted cubic spline with 2 degrees of freedom. This was based on the assessment of models with differing degrees of freedom and selection of the specification with the best fit according to the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). A visual inspection of overall fit comparing actual and predicted survival was also undertaken. The proportional hazards assumption was formally tested using Schoenfeld residuals. This test is sensitive to minor departures from proportionality, and the global test showed statistical evidence of violation (p < 0.001). The large sample size may have increased sensitivity to small departures from proportionality. Visual comparison of Kaplan–Meier curves with Cox model–based predictions for each variable showed only minimal deviations and these were judged to not be clinically important (Supplementary Material).

Breast cancer-specific death and death from other causes were analysed as competing events using flexible parametric survival models. Flexible parametric survival models were fitted separately for breast cancer-specific death and death from other causes to give adjusted hazard ratios (aHR) with 95% CIs. Standardised cumulative incidence functions (CIF) at 5 years were derived to compare adjusted cause-specific mortality for men and women.35

Overall, 1547 males (96.2%) and 197,050 (96.3%) females had complete information for risk-adjustment variables. Due to the small (<5%), evenly distributed proportions of missing data between males and females, a complete case analysis was undertaken.36,37 A post-hoc sensitivity analysis was undertaken using “best-” and “worst-case” values for missing data which did not significantly change the results (Supplementary Material).

Role of the funding source

The funders had no involvement in the study design, data collection, data analyses, data interpretation, or the writing of the report.

As shown in Table 1, men were more likely to have a greater number of comorbidities (p < 0.0001) and increased frailty burden (p < 0.0001). More younger men (aged under 70 years) had at least one comorbidity (16.3% vs 9.0%, p < 0.0001) and mild frailty (22.1% vs 13.7%, p < 0.0001) compared to women under 70 years [not shown]. This difference was absent from people aged 70 and over and was unlikely to be clinically significant (respectively, 31.5% vs 29.0%, p = 0.045 and 40.8% vs 40.9%, p = 0.80 [not shown]). The increasing proportion of cancers among more affluent geographical areas was similar for men and women (p = 0.48).

Multimodal treatment patterns for ER-positive early invasive breast cancer (EIBC)

Table 3 summarises the treatment patterns for the 1609 men and 204,620 women with ER-positive EIBC. After adjustment for patient and tumour characteristics, men were more likely to undergo surgery (aOR 1.68 (95% CI: 1.34 to 2.10), p < 0.0001). A greater proportion of men than women had mastectomy as their definitive surgical procedure (96.0% vs 21.2%, aOR 125.1 (95% CI: 93.2 to 167.8), p < 0.0001) and mastectomy with immediate reconstruction was rarely recorded in men (among those who had mastectomy: 0.3% in men; 23.6% in women). Multimodal treatment patterns for ER-positive locally advanced breast cancer (LABC)

Supplementary Table S3 summarises the treatment patterns for the 213 men and 8934 women with ER-positive LABC. Overall, the patterns of treatment for men and women mirrored those observed for EIBC. Men were less likely to have breast-conserving surgery as their definitive breast surgery (aOR 0.013; 95% CI: 0.002 to 0.093, p < 0.0001).

Of the 172 men and 4286 women who had mastectomy as their primary surgery for LABC, men were more likely to have radiotherapy (aOR 1.56; 95% CI: 1.01 to 2.41, p = 0.047). The 5-year overall unadjusted survival rate for men with ER-positive EIBC was 76.6% (95% CI: 74.0%–78.8%), compared to 87.9% (95% CI: 87.8%–88.0%) for women. Adjusting for patient and tumour characteristics, reduced (but did not eliminate) the difference between men and women. The estimated standardised overall survival rates at 5 years were 84.5% (95% CI: 83.2%–85.8%) and 87.7% (95% CI: 87.6%–87.9%) for men and women, respectively.

In the competing risk models fitted for death from other causes and breast cancer-specific death, there was no association between being male and breast cancer-specific death (aHR 0.84; 95% CI: 0.66 to 1.06, p = 0.135) (Table 4). However, men had a higher risk of death from other causes than women (aHR 1.73; 95% CI: 1.52 to 1.98, p < 0.0001).

At 5 years, the standardised CIF for breast cancer-specific death with competing risk of death from other causes was 3.6% (95% CI: 2.9%–4.5%) for men and 4.6% (95% CI: 4.5%–4.7%) for women, with no significant difference in these values (−1.0%; 95% CI: −2.1%–0.1%). At 5 years, the standardised CIF for death from other causes was 12.0% (95% CI: 10.8%–13.2%) for men compared to 7.7% (95% CI: 7.6%–7.8%) for women, with a difference of 4.3% (95% CI: 3.2%–5.4%) (Table 4).

Discussion

This population-based study describes current clinicopathological, multimodal treatment and survival patterns for men and women with breast cancer in England and Wales. It confirms distinct differences in patient and tumour characteristics for men compared to women, as well as highlighting differences in surgical and oncological management for ER-positive EIBC. The findings show that differences in overall survival are attributable to deaths from other causes, and no difference is seen in breast cancer-specific mortality between men and women at 5 years.

It is a consistent finding throughout the literature that men with breast cancer are older at diagnosis than women.12,16,18,38 This finding may be partially explained by lead time bias in the diagnosis of male BC due to the absence of screening. Furthermore, although it is well cited within the literature that men are more likely than women to have a genetic component to their breast cancer (20% versus 5–10%), there are notable differences in the type of mutation, with men having a preponderance in BRCA2.4,12 Studies in women have shown that BRCA2 mutations are associated with a higher peak age at diagnosis compared to BRCA1.39 It is also possible that hormonal changes in older men are likely to contribute with declining testosterone and increasing oestrogen implicated in the pathogenesis of male BC.

The current study adds to this knowledge by providing a novel understanding of the interplay between age, comorbidity and frailty for men and women with breast cancer. Although men are older at diagnosis, it is comparatively younger men who are more comorbid and frailer than their female counterparts. This is important in understanding variation in treatment patterns between men and women.

An association was found between higher affluence and the incidence of male BC. Previous studies have demonstrated this association in women, with explanations including: reproductive factors, higher use of hormonal treatments, increased screening uptake, fewer barriers to accessing healthcare, and lifestyle factors.40,41 The findings in this paper allow speculation that gender-neutral factors might contribute more significantly to social class differences in the incidence of breast cancer.

Men presented more frequently with more advanced and poorly differentiated disease consistent with previous reports.22,38 This relative increase persisted outside of the 50–70 years age group which is the eligible population for national breast screening programmes in women in England and Wales. Possible reasons include: lead time bias in diagnosis due to the absence of screening available for women, poor public awareness of BC among men, and stronger genetic background influences.42 In addition, men have a reduced volume and central distribution of breast tissue compared to women which may allow smaller tumours to invade the overlying skin. Paradoxically, it could be suggested that the reduced breast volume should mean men are more likely to palpate their breast cancer at an earlier stage. This emphasises the potential role for male BC awareness and education.

In accordance with previous observational studies, the vast majority of men had ER-positive and HER2-negative BC.12,14,16,43,44 It has been postulated that the preponderance of luminal A type tumours in men is related to lower levels of circulating oestrogen, similar to post-menopausal women. However, the differences in tumour biology between men and women persisted across age-groups, suggesting additional contributing factors and possibly genetic differences (BRCA2 mutations tend to present with ER-positive disease).45,46

A novel finding was that older men (≥70 years) with ER-positive disease were more likely to have breast surgery than older women after adjustment for patient and tumour characteristics. Primary endocrine treatment is an alternative treatment option for ER-positive breast cancer, however, outcomes are inferior to treatment with surgery.47,48 For this reason, the inclusion of surgery is recommended as the standard of care for people with EIBC (irrespective of ER status) unless precluded by poor fitness or short life expectancy.5

A possible explanation is that healthcare professionals are more inclined to offer surgery to older men with ER-positive breast cancer than their female counterparts. This might be due to stereotypes and physician bias. For example, healthcare professionals may believe that men are more likely to refuse or be non-compliant with endocrine therapy. It might also reflect gender disparities in beliefs and preferences with one study citing increased refusal of surgery and adjuvant therapies in females with breast cancer relative to men.49 Access to healthcare resources may also differ between genders. For example, in England and Wales, older women are significantly more likely to live alone, and this lack of spousal support may contribute to treatment choices.50 Qualitative studies that explore patients' disease perceptions and preferences, and physicians' unconscious biases in proposing and recommending treatments, would be valuable.

A further novel finding is that, after stratification of PMRT for EIBC according to recurrence risk, men were more likely to receive PMRT for intermediate- and low-risk disease. To our knowledge, this has not been reported previously. In UK breast cancer guidelines, PMRT is not generally recommended for low-risk disease and can be considered for intermediate-risk disease.5 This might reflect closer post-mastectomy surgical margins in men or differing attitudes towards recommending radiotherapy, such as the belief that even a mastectomy in men does not provide wide enough margin clearance given the reduced volume of breast tissue. This aspect of care is of particular interest given the recently published SUPREMO trial where chest-wall irradiation did not improve outcomes for women with intermediate-risk EIBC.51

In this study, recurrence risk was largely based upon pathological staging. There are other high-risk factors that may be associated with the use of radiotherapy such as positive surgical margins and presence of lymphovascular invasion (LVI) for which data was not available. A recent meta-analysis concluded that the role of radiation in men, including which sub-groups might benefit, remains unclear, highlighting the need for better understanding.52 This is a research gap that would be interesting to explore through a survey of oncologists’ attitudes.

Use of NACT was less common in men than women, which might be explained by the preponderance in men of ER-positive HER2-negative disease, where lower response rates to NACT are usual and it is less likely that NACT will change surgical management from mastectomy to a breast conserving procedure. However, the findings persisted despite inclusion of only ER-positive patients and adjustment for HER2 status. This is in line with results from a previous study.20

Observational studies have shown evidence of survival benefits for men who receive adjuvant endocrine therapy.38,53, 54, 55 The rate of 97.6% in this study is high compared to a recent systematic review (average 58.1%, range 7.4%–95.0%).3,13 This is likely explained by better data quality as a consequence of the novel linkage to the Primary Care Prescription Database.26 It is important to note that endocrine therapy use is inferred from a prescription record and this may not assure compliance. There are studies suggesting high discontinuation over time due to side effects including sexual dysfunction.56,57 Most men received tamoxifen in line with European and UK guidelines.4,5,58

The analyses for LABC in men are limited by small numbers and the findings must be interpreted with caution. Men were almost twice as likely as women to present with LABC. There is little within the literature regarding the management of LABC in men. This study shows findings which mirror those of EIBC, with more frequent use of surgery, increased use of post-mastectomy radiotherapy and reduced use of neo-adjuvant chemotherapy.

For EIBC, our study supports the well-recognised observation that overall survival in men is worse than women.1,12,15,16,18,59 A novel finding from this study was the demonstration that there is no difference in breast cancer-specific mortality between men and women, with the difference in overall survival attributable to more deaths from other causes in men. There have been conflicting findings across studies for differences in breast cancer-specific survival.12 A recent US study using SEER data demonstrated worse 5-year breast cancer-specific survival in men across stage 1–3 disease. However, treatments were included in the risk-adjustment model and, unlike this study, it did not use competing risk analysis to account for death from other causes.60 As men are older at diagnosis and have a higher background mortality, not accounting for the competing risk of death from other causes could lead to over-estimation of breast cancer-specific death.

This study overcomes some of the limitations inherent to SEER- and NCDB-based studies.16 Strengths include the comparatively large, unselected population (with a contemporaneous comparable group of women) which is representative of current national “real-world” practice. There were approximately 350 new male BC cases per year in England and Wales, which is in line with Cancer Research UK (CRUK) statistics reporting around 390 new male BC cases per year across the whole of the UK.61

This study also used multiple routinely collected national datasets linked at patient-level to derive detailed information on surgical and oncological treatment patterns. This included novel and validated linkage to a national prescriptions dataset to examine the use of endocrine therapy. Finally, comprehensive risk-adjustment accounted for the differences in patient and tumour characteristics between men and women, including frailty which was absent in other studies.

This study also has some limitations. First, despite using national data for all patients diagnosed between 2015 and 2021, the numbers of men within some sub-groups (e.g., LABC, HER2 positive, triple negative) remain small. Small numbers also limit the usefulness of other analyses which would be of interest including differences between England and Wales, and analyses by ethnicity/race. Second, there remains a risk of residual confounding from variables that are not accounted for such as germline genetic data, genomics and patient preference. Third, we could not evaluate locoregional or distant recurrence as this information is not successfully collected within national datasets. Finally, endocrine therapy use is based upon prescription and does not necessarily infer compliance.

There are likely to be several broader factors contributing to the differences in approach to men with breast cancer. First, there is a paucity of dedicated national or international guidelines relating to the management of male BC. In England, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guidelines have limited reference to men separate to women, with tamoxifen use as first-line adjuvant endocrine therapy the only example where this distinction occurs.5 In 2020, the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) published a set of recommendations for male BC based on the results of 26 observational studies.4 However, this evidence was judged to be poor quality and final recommendations were based on expert opinion using Delphi consensus methodology. Focussed evidence-based guidelines would be beneficial, but this requires inclusion of men in clinical trials or well-designed observational studies, perhaps with international collaboration to maximise numbers. In addition, shared-decision making is crucial with respect to balancing the benefits and risks of different treatment options in relation to short- and long-term toxicities, quality of life, and survivorship.

Second, case volume across individual breast units is low with many units in England and Wales likely to diagnose and treat only 1–2 men per year. This prompts reflection on whether centralisation of care for men with breast cancer might be beneficial, but the rationale of better outcomes based on increased experience must be balanced against potential negative impacts such as travel burden.62

Finally, given the lack of screening for men, it is imperative that efforts are made to raise public awareness of male BC, with increased education regarding the signs and symptoms and promotion of regular self-examination.

This study has highlighted that the population of men with breast cancer in England and Wales is markedly different to the corresponding population of women. Although breast cancer-specific mortality for ER-positive EIBC is comparable, there are notable differences in the management of men compared to women including an increased use of surgery (both EIBC and LABC), reduced use of NACT (both EIBC and LABC), and increased use of PMRT for intermediate- and low-risk EIBC. These treatment differences may have important implications for short- and long-term toxicity, treatment burden, and survivorship. This warrants further work as well as consideration of consensus evidence-based guidelines for men and exploration of potential gender-based clinical decision-making.
