Strengthening WHO's South-East Asia Regional Office: from crisis to opportunity?
Authors: Swarup Sarkar, Diah Saminarsih, J.V.R. Prasada Rao, Kent Buse
Published: 2025

Recent events at the World Health Organization's (WHO) South-East Asia Regional Office (SEARO) laid bare weaknesses in governance that go beyond a single change in leadership. This includes an outdated leadership selection process, its Director being placed ‘on leave,’ and the second most populous country departing the WHO Regional Committee for another.1,2 This tests both WHO's credibility and SEARO's capacity to protect and advance the health of the region's population.

The Independent Panel for Pandemic Preparedness and Response3 identified structural tensions in WHO governance, with Regional Directors accountable both to the Director-General and to the member states that elect them. The Panel proposed two options: align with other UN agencies and appoint regional directors from headquarters, or retain the current system but strengthen accountability.

SEARO's most recent leadership contest illustrates why reform is urgent. Questions over the process governing Regional Director selection across all WHO regions were raised by over 100 experts prior to the SEARO election.4 The case of SEARO was particularly alarming with concerns about one family-based nomination, inadequate vetting, inappropriate campaigning, and political maneuvering.

The WHO legal team5 made welcome recommendations to address the problems, but these met resistance from governing bodies at headquarters and regional levels, exposing reluctance to reform.6 Weaker proposals were sent to Regional Committees last year, but little progress is evident. The absence of an independent and transparent process risks perceptions of bias and erodes trust in the organisation.

WHO's regional elections are unique among UN agencies. At their best, they embody regional ownership and responsiveness to local contexts. But ownership must be matched with accountability. To preserve the benefits of regional bodies while addressing the flaws in the selection of regional leaders, several reforms are needed. This includes full transparency in nominations, including public disclosure of qualifications and expertise. Second, independent external assessments of nominated candidates and public disclosure that candidates meet minimum criteria, before they can be shortlisted. Third, clear competency-based frameworks, which emphasise independence and integrity,4,7 would support more consistent external evaluations. Robust and transparent mid-term reviews of Regional Directors linked to measurable outcomes should be introduced.

Collectively, these reforms could strengthen confidence that regional leadership is both competent and committed to engaging equitably with all member states. Greater trust, in turn, can foster deeper regional cooperation, enabling swifter emergency responses and advancing WHO's broader agenda. At the same time, WHO faces a severe budget crisis and must find countries willing to step up (as China recently has), just as confidence in multilateralism is under strain. Reforms may create conditions for India and Thailand to assume greater responsibility.

Broader reforms could also help. The UN Joint Programme on HIV/AIDS demonstrated the value of the formal and structural engagement of civil society and people affected in UN governance processes. Even ad hoc engagement, as during negotiations of the Framework Convention on Tobacco Control, illustrate how civil society can influence the outcome and effectiveness of international health negotiations.8

Hence it is not surprising that independent experts and the WHO Secretariat recommended opening the nomination and vetting process for regional WHO directors beyond member states. They also called for more structured engagement of non-state actors throughout the process, including more open town halls in which the media and civil society actors could take part. These suggestions appear to have been blocked by the Executive Committee.6

WHO and its governing organs, including the Executive Board and the Regional Committees, should institutionalise meaningful civil society engagement as a core governance feature. Alongside greater transparency, meaningful engagement at the country and regional levels, beyond that currently offered by the WHO Civil Society Commission, can foster trust and confidence that WHO's global core technical mandate remains, to the extent possible, insulated from member state politics.

The departure of Indonesia, a major contributor to WHO, Gavi and the Global Fund, and a WHO prequalified vaccine producer, is consequential.7 It both weakens SEARO and raises questions of whether it has the critical mass and is fit to play the role originally envisioned for the semi-autonomous regional offices.

This moment provides SEARO the opportunity to support the wider organization in meeting increasing expectations to deliver on its vital mission through the structural reforms currently being implemented. WHO is navigating a transformed global health order—marked by geopolitical shifts, financial fragility, and pressure to refocus on its core mandate.9 Strengthening SEARO is a regional imperative; but must form part of broader efforts to ensure WHO remains independent, legitimate, and effective. The same principles that must guide SEARO reform—transparency, merit-based leadership, and accountability—are also the foundations for a revitalised WHO that is trusted to set global norms, safeguard health security, and convene meaningful dialogue across divides.

The stakes for SEARO could not be higher. The region faces pressing threats—pandemics, antimicrobial resistance, climate-related health risks—but also extraordinary opportunities: a young and growing health workforce, vibrant traditions of south–south collaboration, and technological innovation with potential for global impact in tackling old eliminable diseases as well as developing programmes for NCDs control at scale. A strong, credible SEARO could make an invaluable contribution for its members, including those affected by conflicts and economic challenges and those with limited international presence. Conversely a weak SEARO will further erode trust in multilateralism.

Regional leaders face a choice: allow existing shortcomings to continue, or embrace a selection process that is open, fair, and inclusive — one that respects regional autonomy while upholding the integrity of the WHO and its obligation to treat all member states equally. The reforms needed are within reach—what remains is the courage of member states to act. Should SEARO start the process of reform now, it could inspire upcoming committee meetings in other regions to put organisational reform on their agendas. Showing resolve is the best way to restore faith in multilateral institutions.
