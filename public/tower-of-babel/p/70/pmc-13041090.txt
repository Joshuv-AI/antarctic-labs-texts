ANGPTL8 regulates glucose metabolism in gestational diabetes mellitus mice, and proteomic analysis reveals the PI3K/Akt pathway plays its role in insulin-resistance HepG2 cells
Authors: Xinping Xie, Jianying Yan, Xiaoyun Chen, Lidan He, Jianbo Wu, Lin Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInsulin resistance (IR) is a pivotal determinant in the pathophysiological processes underlying gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM). Angiopoietin-like protein 8 (ANGPTL8) exhibits an ambiguous influence on glucose metabolism in the onset and progression of GDM. However, the functions of ANGPTL8 in the metabolic profiles of insulin-resistant (IR) HepG2 cells, glucose tolerance in GDM mice, and the possible underlying mechanisms require further exploration.MethodsANGPTL8 knockout and over-expressing HepG2 cell lines were established. Glucose uptake capacity in three clones (wild-type, ANGPTL8 knockout, and over-expressing) of insulin-resistant HepG2 cells was detected. Proteomic analysis of the IR HepG2 cells identified a cluster of relevant signaling pathways, and the effect of ANGPTL8 on metabolomic changes was evaluated. The GDM model was induced by a high-fat diet (HFD) and intraperitoneal injection of Streptozotocin (STZ) in pregnant mice. An oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) was performed in both GDM mice and ANGPTL8 over-expressing GDM mice.ResultsThe PI3K/Akt signaling cascade was stimulated by insulin resistance, and this activation could be effectively suppressed by inhibitors targeting PI3K, Akt, and GSK-3β. HepG2 cells over-expressing ANGPTL8 and exposed to high insulin concentrations demonstrated a marked enhancement in glucose uptake capacity, as opposed to their wild-type counterparts or ANGPTL8 knockout cells under IR conditions. The most substantial down-regulation was observed in lactate and branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs), which exhibited a positive correlation with IR. Promoted translation of ANGPTL8 was found in the liver, pancreas, and placental tissues of GDM mice. Significantly better glucose tolerance was observed in GDM mice transfected with m-ANGPTL8 compared with those that were not. Furthermore, the activation of Akt was observed in the primary hepatocytes of ANGPTL8 over-expressing GDM mice.ConclusionOver-expression of ANGPTL8 may increase glucose uptake capacity and influence the metabolic profiles in IR HepG2 cells, enhancing the insulin-mediated activation of the PI3K/Akt/GSK-3β pathway. ANGPTL8 may improve glucose tolerance in GDM mice. These findings might contribute to a new understanding of the roles of ANGPTL8 in glucose metabolism during the onset and progression of insulin resistance, providing novel insights for the clinical therapy of GDM.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-08738-6.

Background

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), defined as “glucose intolerance with onset or first recognition during pregnancy” [1], has become a significant public health issue and a focal point in modern obstetrics and gynecology owing to its prevalence as one of the most common metabolic disorders [2]. A large-scale study in 2013 applied new diagnostic criteria and reported that the prevalence of GDM in China was 17.5%, a rate that is still on the rise [3]. GDM may increase the risk of various serious perinatal complications, including hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, obstetric intervention, macrosomia, shoulder dystocia, birth trauma, neonatal hypoglycemia, preterm birth, and fetal distress [4, 5]. Women diagnosed with GDM are more prone to developing postpartum type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and cardiovascular disease (CVD); additionally, their offspring may face higher morbidity from obesity and T2DM in adulthood [6]. Although the exact etiology and pathogenesis of GDM are not fully understood, insulin resistance (IR) plays a crucial role in its development [7, 8]. While physiological insulin resistance is a characteristic of normal pregnancy leading to increased insulin secretion, women with GDM cannot adequately compensate for this resistance by increasing insulin production [8].

In gestational diabetes, various cytokines involved in glucose and lipid metabolism exhibit abnormal expression. Angiopoietin-like protein 8 (ANGPTL8), also known as betatrophin, is a protein abundantly expressed in human liver and adipose tissue. Several studies suggest that ANGPTL8 plays a vital role in glucose and lipid metabolism [9, 10]. Studies have shown that serum levels of ANGPTL8 increase in diabetic mice established via a high-fat diet (HFD), and its over-expression can alleviate hyperlipidemia, impaired glucose tolerance, and insulin resistance [11]. Other research has found that the serum concentration of ANGPTL8 in women with GDM is higher than in pregnant women on a normal fat diet (NFD) [12], indicating that ANGPTL8 may be involved in the onset and progression of GDM. Additionally, reports demonstrate that ANGPTL8 has high sensitivity in diagnosing GDM and may serve as an important predictor of postpartum type 2 diabetes [13]. These studies suggest that ANGPTL8 may play an essential role in the genesis and development of GDM, but its specific mechanisms require further investigation.

We conducted a study establishing an IR model in HepG2 cells and a GDM animal model in C57BL/6J female mice using HFD and streptozotocin (STZ). Our research aimed to explore the impact of ANGPTL8 on IR and GDM, as well as its mechanism in regulating glucose metabolism.

Materials and methods

Culture of hep G2 cells

HepG2 cells were kindly provided by Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. China (Catalog number: Procell CL-0103) and maintained in low-glucose (1.1 g/L, Gibco, USA) DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (ST30-2602, PAN-Biotech, Germany), mixed with 100 U/mL penicillin & 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Meilunbio, China) in a cell incubator (5% CO2, 37 °C) (Thermo Scientific, USA).

Establishment of ANGPTL8 gene knockout hep G2 cells

We utilized the CRISPR/Cas9 system to create a mutation in the ANGPTL8 gene by targeting exon 1. We employed a CRISPR/Cas9 design tool to construct sgRNA sequences. The specific sequences of the two single-guide RNAs (sgRNA1 and sgRNA2) were “GCCAGTTCTGGGCCGCCCATGGG” and “CTTTGTCAGCCGTCCCTCCGTGG”, respectively. These sgRNA sequences were synthesized as DNA oligonucleotides.

Establishment of ANGPTL8 gene over-expressing hep G2 cells

To generate ANGPTL8 over-expression cell lines, synthetic gene fragments containing the cDNA of ANGPTL8 were cloned into pMXS-IRES-BLAST/pMXS-IRES-puro expression vectors. These vectors, along with packaging vectors pCMV-VSV-G and psPAX2, were transfected into HEK293T cells using XTremeGene 9 transfection reagent to package retroviruses. Alternatively, lentiviruses were packaged by co-transfecting the expression vectors with pCMV-VSV-G into HEK293T cells. Subsequently, HepG2 cells were transduced with these viruses via spin-infection in the presence of polybrene to achieve over-expression of the ANGPTL8 gene. To maintain ANGPTL8 protein expression levels close to those in wild-type HepG2 cells, cells expressing the cDNAs were titrated with Doxycycline. This approach allowed for precise control over ANGPTL8 expression, facilitating the study of its functional roles.

In vitro insulin-resistant cell model

An insulin-resistant HepG2 (HepG2/IR) cell model was established using a high concentration of glucose. When cells reached 60–70% confluence, three HepG2 cell lines (wild-type, ANGPTL8 knockout, and ANGPTL8 over-expression) were rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and transferred to a hyperglycemic (5.5 g/L) DMEM medium for 24 h in an incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2. After high-glucose induction of IR, the HepG2 cell lines were co-incubated with insulin (100 nM) for 20–30 min [14].

Glucose uptake assay

Glucose uptake was measured using the fluorescent glucose analog 2-NBDG (MCE, USA). HepG2 cells were seeded in a 6-well plate at a density of 2.5 × 10^6 cells/well and cultured to establish the IR model as previously described. Cells were washed twice with PBS and then incubated with or without insulin (100 nM) for 30 min at 37 °C. 2-NBDG was then added to a final concentration of 50 µM and incubated for another 30 min. The reaction was terminated by removing the 2-NBDG-containing medium and washing the cells three times with ice-cold PBS. Intracellular fluorescence was measured using a microplate reader (Biosciences Accuri C6, Beckman Coulter, USA) at an excitation wavelength of 485 nm and an emission wavelength of 535 nm. Generally, the model is considered successfully established when the glucose uptake capacity of the IR model cells is significantly lower than that of the control cells.

Ganerally, when the glucose uptake capacity of the IR model cells are significantly lower than those of the control cells, the model is considered successfully established.

Proteomic detection and analysis

A high-intensity ultrasonic processor was used to extract proteins from Hep G2 and the Hep G2/ IR cells in lysis buffer (8 M urea, 1% protease inhibitor). The remaining fragments was eliminated by centrifuging at 12,000 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C. The protein concentration was then determined using the Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) reagent kit. Prior to digestion, the proteins were treated with dithiothreitol (DTT) for half an hour in the dark at 37 °C to reduce disulfide bonds, followed by alkylation with iodoacetamide (IAC) for a quarter at the same temperature. The proteins were then fused with millimolar Triethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB) to create an optimal environment for trypsin digestion. During this process, trypsin was added at a ratio of 1:50 to the protein to ensure optimal digestion, and the mixture was re-digested overnight at 37 °C at a ratio of 1:100. Following digestion, the peptides were then desalted and dried using the Strata XC18 SPE column. Additionally, the peptides were re-solubilized and labeled in 0.5 M TEAB. The mass spectrometry (MS/MS) was performed via a Q Exactive Plus instrument (Thermo Scientific, USA), which was interfaced with an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system. In the Orbitrap mass analyzer, complete peptides were checked with a mass resolution of 70,000 at an electrospray voltage of 2.0KV. A data-dependent scanning (DDA) tool with specific parameters was managed to collect data. To optimize the analysis, several parameters are set, including an automatic gain control of 50,000, a signal threshold of 5,000 ions/s, and a maximum duration of 200 s per cycle. To prevent the reduplicative scanning of precursor ions, the dynamic exclusion time for tandem mass spectrometry scans is set to 15 s. The acquired MS/MS data are analyzed using the MaxQuant search engine (version 1.5.2.8), which was designed for high-throughput proteomics data analysis. Gene ontology (GO) annotations are originated from the UniProtGOA database (http://www.ebi.ac.uk/GOA/). InterProScan software is employed to provide insights into biological processes(BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions(MF). Protein descriptions were glossed using the KEGG online service system. WOLFPSORT (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/) is processed to describe the subcellular localization of the annotated proteins. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the KEGG database, employing a two-tailed Fisher exact test. GO categories were filtered based on P values (P < 0.05), and the filtered P value matrix was converted using the formula X=-log10(P). Consequently, each functional category’s X value undergoes a Z transformation. All of the cluster members are visualized in a heat map.

Untargeted metabolomic analysis

Two Hep G2 cell lines(the wild-type and ANGPTL8 over-expressing, 1 × 107) were collected for comprehensive metabolic profiling using LC-MS/MS system (PTM Co., Ltd. Hangzhou, China) after the treatment with high glucose. With a addition of a methanol-water solution (in a 4:1 volume ratio) to the cell samples, the cells were vortexed vigorously for 3 min to ensure thorough mixing. Then the samples were spotted in liquid nitrogen for 5 min then on the dry ice for 5 min, and vortexed for 2 min after thawed on ice. After repeating this freeze-thaw circle 3 times, the samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm (4 °C) for 10 min to separate the supernatant from cellular debris. A 300µL of the supernatant, containing the extracted metabolites, was collected and composed in -20 °C for half an hour to further precipitate any remaining proteins. Then the samples were centrifuged again at 12,000 rpm (4 °C) for 3 min to remove any additional insoluble particles. Two aliquots of each sample were investigated under positive and negative ion conditions, respectively, using a T3 column for elution. The data acquisition was manipulated via the information-dependent acquisition (IDA) mode via Analyst TF 1.7.1 Software, located in Sciex, Concord, ON, Canada. The raw data file acquisited by LC-MS analysis was converted into mzXML format by ProteoWizard software for further processing. Peak extraction, peak alignment, and retention time correction were performed using the XCMS program. To ensure data quality, the “SVR” method was employed to correct peak areas, and peaks with a detection rate of less than 50% in each group were deserted. Metabolites were then identified. Putative identification was performed by matching the accurate mass and MS² fragmentation patterns against public databases (such as METLIN and HMDB) and AI-driven tools (metDNA). Crucially, the identities of all key metabolites reported in this study were unequivocally confirmed by comparing their experimental data against authentic chemical standards from our in-house library. This confirmation was based on a strict match of retention time, precursor ion accurate mass (< 5 ppm), and the MS/MS fragmentation spectrum. Unsupervised Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was operated by statistics function within R programming language (http://www.r-project.org) to identify any clustering or separation trends in the metabolic profiles. The Hierarchical Cluster Analysis (HCA) consequence of metabolites was performed, with results presented as heat maps to highlight clusters of metabolites, while Pearson correlation coefficients (PCC) were computed by the cor function in R to further assess the relationships between different metabolites. Both HCA and PCC were conducted by the Complex Heat map R package. Differential metabolites were determined based on two criteria, VIP (variable importance in the project) > 1 and P-value < 0.05 (Student’s t test). VIP values were calculated consistent with the results of Orthogonal Partial Least Squares-Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) results. Prior to OPLS-DA analysis, the data was log transform (log2) and mean-centered processed to normalize the distribution. To validate the model and avoid overfitting, 200 permutation tests were conducted. The KEGG Compound Database was processed to illustrate the annoated metabolites. Identified metabolites were illustrated by means of KEGG Compound database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/compound/), and annotated metabolites were mapped to the KEGG Pathway Database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html) to gain insights into the biological pathways and processes potentially affected by ANGPTL8 over-expression in HepG2 cells.

GDM animal model

Healthy 6-weeks-old C57BL/6J male and female mice were purchased from SPF BIO- TECHNOLOGY CO., LTD. (Beijing, China). These mice were housed at the SPF-level animal experimental center of Fujian Medical University in a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle, remaining the temperature ‌consistent‌ at 22 ± 1 °C. After one week of free access to chew and water, the female mice were randomly subdivided into two: high-fat-diet (HFD) group and normal-fat-diet (NFD) group (each consisting of 10 mice). The NFD group was fed normal feed provided by SPF Biotechnology Co., Ltd., as described as Yu Bai et al. [15], which contained at least 18% protein, at least 4% fat, no more than 5% fiber, no more than 8% ash, at least 36% carbohydrates, 1.0-1.8% calcium, 0.6–1.2% phosphorus, essential amino acids, vitamins, and trace elements. The high-fat diet al.so included 65% standard chow, in addition 15% lard, 10% egg yolk and 10% sugar[15]. After a week of dietary intervention, the mice were mated in a 1: 2 ratio; the next morning, the presence of vaginal mucus plugs was observed to confirm successful mating, marking the 0.5 day of pregnancy(GD 0.5).

To induce GDM, pregnant mice in the HFD group received daily intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of low-dose streptozotocin (STZ; 40 mg/kg body weight) for five consecutive days, from GD 3.5 to GD 7.5. STZ was freshly dissolved in 50 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 4.5) before each injection. Pregnant mice in the NFD control group received i.p. injections of an equal volume of citrate buffer. Return the mice to their home cages, provide free access to normal food and water. Repeat above steps on the next four consecutive days. GDM was confirmed by performing an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) at GD 8.5.

Female mice were euthanized on the GD18.5, and we collected their liver, pancreas, and placenta tissues for follow-up detection.

OGTT

Oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) was carried out at GD 0.5 and 8.5, along with weight measurement of the female mice on these same days. After fasting overnight (8:00 pM to 8:00AM ), the blood glucose was monitored via tail vein of mice before the mice were given a D-glucose (MCE, USA) solution by gavage. The amount given was 2 g/kg (400 mg/ml), and the blood glucose was determined at 30, 60, 90 and 120 min following the gavage. GDM was.

defined as fasting blood glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L or random blood glucose ≥ 16.7 mmol/L.

The nursing and operation were in accordance with the requirements of the Guide for the Management and Use of Laboratory Animals (8th edition, NIH), and the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University approved all experimental procedures to ensure the ethical treatment and welfare of the animals involved in the study.

Hydrodynamic tail vein injection

C57/B6J female GDM mice were underwent the hydrodynamic tail vein injection at GD11.5. The injections closely followed the method outlined by Kim YC et al. [16]. Specifically, pAAV8-m-ANGPTL8-Flag and the control viruses(Scramble AAV-8)were administered at a dose of 2 × 1013 genome copy/kg through intravenous injection using sterile saline, which was purchased from Genechem Co., Ltd. Shanghai, China (n = 4 per group). Each mouse received 20 µg/g body weight of the viruses, with the saline volume equal to 10% of their body weight. The injection must be finished in 8 to 10 s to ensure effectiveness.

A schematic of the GDM mouse model induction and experimental timeline showed in Supplementary Figure. S1.

The isolation of primary mouse hepatocyte

At GD18.5, primary mouse liver cells were isolated. GDM mice were euthanized and the portal vein was sequentially perfused with 30 ml of PBS and a 0.5 mg/ml solution of collagenase IV / dispase (Gibco, USA) mixture. The livers were dissected and cut into small fragments. Then these liver pieces were treated with another round of the collagenase IV /dispase mixture (1 mg/ml), and incubated at 37 °C for half an hour. The resulting suspension was collected, and 1 mg/ml of the collagenase II /dispase compound was added again, and the mixture was continuously shaken at 37 °C for another 30 min. The supernatant was then percolated through a 100 mm cell sieve and centrifuged at 4 °C for 5 min at 50 g. After centrifugation, the cell precipitate contained the liver cells. Finally, the cells were re- suspended in a 10% FBS-α-MEM medium for further incubate and analysis.

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR

The “ Fastpure Cell/tissue Total RNA Isolation kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) ” was processed to extracted total RNA. The concentration of total RNA was measured with ND5000 ultraviolet spectrophotometer (Bioteke, Beijing, China). The “ HiScriptⅢAll-in-one RT SuperMix Perfect for qPCR (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) ” was employed to reversely transcribed RNA into cDNA with Oligo (dT) and random primer. All of the instruments and reagents used in RNA extraction and reverse transcription were RNase-free. The cDNA was used for quantitative real-time PCR, using Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) along with SYBR Green (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) to detect the transcription levels of ANGPTL8, with β-actin as the internal control. The q-RT-PCR reaction conditions were as follows: 95 °C pre-denaturation for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of reaction, each cycle including 95 °C denaturation (10 s), 60 °C extension (30 s ), and final melting at 95 °C (15 s ), followed by incubation at 25 °C for several minutes. An Exicycler TM96 Quantitative Thermal Cycler was employed to perform the PCR reactions. The data were computed via 2-ΔΔCt transform. The primers were synthesized by Genscript (Sangon Biotech, China). The amplification of PCR products was performed using the following primers: ANGPTL8 forward, 5′-GCCTGTTGGAGACTCAGATGGA-3′, and reverse, 5′-CGCTGTCCCGTAGCACCTTC-3′; β-actin forward, 5′-CTCCATCCTG- GCCTCGCTGT-3′, and reverse, 5′- GCTGTCACCTTCACCGTTCC-3′.

Western blotting

Protein was extracted on ice with RIPA lysis buffer (Meilunbio, China). containing protease inhibitors (0.1 µg/ml chymotrypsin, 0.5 µg/ml trypsin, 0.6 µg/ml chymotrypsin A) and phosphatase inhibitors (sodium vanadate, 2mM). The protein concentration was quantified using BCA protein concentration kit. After denaturing in boiling water for 5–10 min, denatured proteins was separated by SDS-PAGE using polyacrylamide gels with a concentration ranging from 5% to 12% based on the protein size. Then the proteins were transferred to a PVDF membrane (Millipore Corp., Billerica, MA, USA) following approximately 2.5 h of electrophoresis. The membrane underwent a blocked step with skim milk at room temperature for 1 h and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with different antibodies as followings: rabbit anti-ANGPTL8 (1:1000; rabbit. no. AB180915, Abcam, UK), rabbit anti-PI3 Kinase p110α (C73F8) (1:1000; Cell Signaling, MA, USA), rabbit anti-Akt (pan) (11E7) (1:1000; Cell Signaling, MA, USA), rabbit anti-GSK-3β (D5C5Z) XP (1:1000; Cell Signaling, MA, USA), mouse anti-β-actin (8H10D10) (1:2000; Cell Signaling, MA, USA), mouse anti-LaminB1(1:20000; cat.no.66095-1-Ig, Proteintech, Wuhan, Hubei, China). After washed with TBST, the PVDF membrane was incubated with goat anti-rabbit or goat anti-mouse secondary antibody labeled with HRP (1:10000; no AB6721, Abcam, UK) at 37 °C for 60 min. Subsequently, the PVDF membrane was exposed to film using an ECL reagent for 5 min to visualize the protein bands in a dark environment and imaged with a Bio-red Pro-Analyzer Imaging System. The relative level of proteins was determined by densitometric analysis of Western blot bands using ImageJ software. The intensity of the proteins band was normalized to the corresponding β-actin and LaminB1 loading control band.

Immunofluorescent(IF)Staining

We detected the expression and distribution of ANGPTL8 by immunofluorescence staining method. The tissue sections from the liver, pancreas, and placenta of both HFD and NFD mice were fixed in 4% polyformaldehyde for 15 min. After washing the sections with PBS for three times to remove any residual polyformaldehyde, they were permeated with 0.1% Triton X-100 for half an hour to penetrate the cell membranes. The sections were then blocked with goat serum for a quarter at room temperature to prevent non-specific antigen binding, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with anti-ANGPTL8 antibody (1:1000; rabbit, product number AB180915, Abcam). After washing for three times with PBS, the sections were incubated with Cy3-labeled secondary antibody at room temperature for an hour in the dark. Then, the sections were stained with Aladdin (DAPI ) to color the cell nuclei. Eventually, the sections were sealed with a preservative and imaged at 400 × magnification using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus). The specificity of the anti-ANGPTL8 antibody was validated in ANGPTL8 knockout and over-expressing HepG2 cells (Supplementary Fig. 2-A and 2-B ). The mean fluorescence intensity of the IF images was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, USA). For each group, at least three randomly selected fields of view were analyzed. The region of interest (ROI) was defined based on the tissue morphology. The integrated density of the Cy3 signal (for ANGPTL8) was measured for each ROI. After subtracting the background fluorescence from an adjacent area without tissue, the corrected total cell fluorescence (CTCF) was calculated. The average CTCF value per group was then used for statistical analysis.

The flow cytometry(FCM)

The three sub-clones of differentiated Hep G2 cell lines (including the wild-type, the ANGPTL8 knockout and the ANGPTL8 over-expressing Hep G2 cells) were divided into control group and insulin-resistant model group. The control group was cultured with DMEM low-glucose (1.1 g/l, Gibco, USA) medium, and the insulin resistant model group was cultured with DMEM high-glucose (5.5 g/l, Gibco, USA) medium. After 24 h of incubation, the cells in both groups were incubated with 100 nM insulin for 20–30 min, and treated with 50 μm fluorescent glucose analogue 2-NBDG (MCE, USA) for 30 min ( performed as described in the“Glucose Uptake Assay”section of the article). The mean fluorescence intensity of the cells was detected using the flow cytometry (FCM) by Biosciences AccuriC6 (Beckman Cloulter, USA).

Statistical analysis

Results are presented as mean ± SEM. Unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test or One-Way ANOVA was used for comparisons. Analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 and GraphPad 8.0.1. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The presence of both insulin and high glucose in the medium greatly up-regulated ANGPTL8 mRNA expression in cultured HepG2 cells, while either compound alone had no significant effect (Fig. 1A).

Insulin time-dependently up-regulated ANGPTL8 mRNA levels

As shown in Fig. 1B, treatment with 100 nM insulin (in 5.5 mM glucose) gradually increased ANGPTL8 mRNA expression over 48 h. Furthermore, a significant increase in ANGPTL8 transcript levels was observed approximately 36 h after treatment with insulin and high glucose.

ANGPTL8 promotes the uptake of glucose in Hep G2 cells

FCM analysis estimated whether ANGPTL8 could ameliorate IR in HepG2 cells. As shown in Fig. 1C and 1D, over-expression of ANGPTL8 increased the glucose uptake capacity of insulin-resistant HepG2 cells and improved IR. Conversely, knockout of ANGPTL8 had no influence on glucose uptake in insulin-resistant HepG2 cells.

The protein level of ANGPTL8 was dramatically increased in liver, pancreas and placenta tissues in HFD (GDM) mice

Immunofluorescent staining showed that translation levels of ANGPTL8 in the livers, pancreases, and placentas were significantly increased in HFD (GDM) mice compared with NFD mice(Fig. 1E-H).

Over-expression of ANGPTL8 in the liver via AAV-8 improved glucose tolerance in GDM mice

OGTT was performed in GDM mice to assess the impact of ANGPTL8 on glucose levels. Consequence of OGTT assumed that significant decrease in blood glucose levels was detected in the group which ANGPTL8 were over-expressed in livers by AAV-8 compared with the GDM control group (Fig. 1K). Furthermore, FCM results in primary mouse hepatocytes showed that ANGPTL8 over-expression increased glucose uptake capacity, suggesting alleviation of insulin resistance (Fig. 1I J).

Proteomic analysis revealed the impact of the PI3K/Akt pathway on the IR process

Quantitative proteomics using TMT was employed to comprehensively investigate the impact of insulin on insulin resistance(IR)by protein changes in HepG2 cells. The study discerned 7,082 proteins, of which 7,018 were quantified. Proteins with a P-value less than 0.05 and an expression change exceeding 1.3 times or below 0.77 times were classified as differentially abundant protein (DAP). Ultimately, the study detected 109 up-regulated and 336 down-regulated proteins (Figs. 2A).

Discussion

Pregnancy is a unique period that affects various organs of the parent. The body adapts to metabolic changes, particularly in glucose metabolism, to support the fetus’s normal growth and development. One key adaptation is the regulation of insulin sensitivity [17]. From early pregnancy, insulin sensitivity increases with gestational age, promoting glucose absorption by fat tissue and ensuring adequate nutritional reserves for the fetus [17]. As pregnancy progresses, the secretion of placental hormones significantly increases, leading to a sharp rise in insulin resistance (IR) [18]. Clinically, insulin resistance means that higher than normal insulin levels are required to maintain stable blood glucose levels, prevent drastic fluctuations, and provide consistent energy for fetal growth [19]. To maintain blood glucose balance, the mother adapts to these changes through the proliferation and enlargement of pancreatic β-cells in a compensatory way, increasing insulin secretion in response to high glucose level [19]. However, when insulin secretion is insufficient to counteract the insulin resistance during pregnancy, the mother may develop impaired glucose tolerance, which can increase the hazard of developing gestational diabetes [8].

ANGPTL8, a protein produced by the liver, plays a dual role in glucose and triglyceride homeostasis in mice [10, 20]. Nevertheless, recent studies have highlighted significant controversy regarding the function of ANGPTL8, with its specific molecular mechanisms still unclear. Hu et al. [21]. had elucidated that the circulating concentration of ANGPTL8 in lately diagnosed type 2 diabetes cases was drastically elevated, and their serum ANGPTL8 level were positively related to post-oral glucose tolerance test blood glucose levels and postprandial serum insulin levels, but negatively related to insulin sensitivity in type 2 diabetes cases. Conversely, other studies have indicated that plasma ANGPTL8 levels are lower in obese type 2 diabetes patients [22], and human plasma ANGPTL8 levels are influenced by insulin secretion capacity [23]. Additionally, the function of ANGPTL8 in gestational diabetes remains to be elucidated.

To investigate the impact of ANGPTL8 on glucose metabolism in gestational diabetes (GDM), we explored the molecular mechanisms by which ANGPTL8 regulates glucose level. In this study, we used the human liver-derived Hep G2 cell line to induce insulin resistance by treating it with high concentrations of insulin (100 nM) in vitro. We found that the expression of ANGPTL8 was significantly up-regulated under conditions of glucose presence. Further, using flow cytometry, we observed that in the insulin-resistant condition induced by high glucose, cells over-expressing ANGPTL8 showed a significant increase in glucose uptake, whereas cells with ANGPTL8 knockout did not show a significant decrease in glucose uptake compared to control cells. Additionally, in GDM mice induced by a high-fat diet and STZ (streptozotocin), the translation of ANGPTL8 in the liver, pancreas and placenta was enhanced. Moreover, over-expression of ANGPTL8 in the liver of GDM mice improved glucose tolerance, and ANGPTL8 significantly raised the glucose uptake in the liver cells of GDM mice. These findings suggest that ANGPTL8 may have a crucial impact on glucose metabolism, potentially aiding in the alleviation of insulin resistance.

Our research has found that in the state of insulin resistance, glucose can induce the expression of ANGPTL8 in Hep G2 cells and enhance their insulin sensitivity and glucose uptake. This finding aligns with Hu et al..‘s report [21], explaining why ANGPTL8 expression in the liver of mice significantly increases during IR and re-feeding, as both blood glucose and insulin levels are elevated under these conditions. This also explains the inconsistent serum concentrations of ANGPTL8 detected by different authors in T2DM and gestational diabetes [22, 23]. We hypothesize that the reasons for this inconsistency are as follows: in the early stages of insulin resistance, when the body is mildly resistant to insulin, normal insulin levels are insufficient to reach a sufficient threshold in the liver to stimulate the high expression of ANGPTL8, resulting in low ANGPTL8 levels. As insulin resistance worsens, pancreatic β-cells continue to produce more insulin to counteract hyperglycemia, leading to hyperglycemia and hyperinsulinemia, which in turn increases ANGPTL8 expression. When insulin resistance continues to worsen to a certain stage, pancreatic β-cells cannot produce enough insulin to compensate for the worsening tissue insulin resistance (i.e., hyperglycemia and significant T2DM or gestational diabetes stage) [24], and the concentration of ANGPTL8 will gradually decrease. Therefore, the inconsistencies in ANGPTL8 expression among different populations are actually due to differences in individual insulin levels and the degree of insulin resistance.

Following the in vitro IR model established by treat the cells with high glucose, we screened the intracellular metabolites levels via the untargeted metabolomics by UPLC- MS/MS program. We have found through the metabolic profile analysis that, compared to wild-type Hep G2/IR cells, the over-expression of ANGPTL8 in Hep G2/IR cells resulted in significant changes in multiple metabolites, particularly a notable decrease in lactate, 2-hydroxybutyrate, branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs), aromatic amino acids (including tyrosine and phenylalanine), glutamate, and mannose. In recent years, lactate has received growing attention as a key regulator of insulin resistance and diabetes. Some studies suggest that high plasma lactate levels may be an early marker of insulin resistance and can be used to identify insulin resistance status [25, 26]. Other studies have found through metabolomics that branched-chain amino acids (such as leucine, isoleucine, and valine) are highly related to diabetes and glucose characteristics [27]. Additionally, several studies have found that aromatic amino acids are positively correlated with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) and the development of future diabetes [28, 29]. Moreover, the relationship between mannose and insulin resistance is also noteworthy. Mannose is a glucose-related metabolite primarily generated by liver. Numerous researches have demonstrated that serum mannose levels has positively correlation with insulin resistance and negatively correlation with insulin sensitivity [30, 31], which helps predict the onset of insulin resistance and diabetes. In our study, the significant reduction of metabolites such as lactic acid, 2-hydroxybutyric acid and branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) suggested that over-expression of ANGPTL8 may contribute to the alleviation of insulin resistance state [32], which could help delay the onset and progression of gestational diabetes mellitus. The mechanism by which ANGPTL8 contributes to these metabolite changes requires further study.

Furthermore, we discovered that insulin promotes the protein level of ANGPTL8 by triggering the PI3K/Akt/GSK-3β pathway, and ANGPTL8 expression is also inhibited by PI3K/Akt inhibitors. Over-expressed ANGPTL8 regulates this signaling pathway, thereby promoting insulin-mediated glucose uptake in HepG2 cells, a key mechanism for maintaining blood glucose homeostasis [33, 34]. Additionally, our findings indicate that even in insulin-resistant cells, the knockout of ANGPTL8 does not impact the expression of proteins in the PI3K/Akt/GSK-3β signaling. Liu et al.. ‘s previous study [35] showed that the function of ANGPTL8 in the regulation of glucose metabolism was dependent on the insulin-induced PI3K/AKT signaling, and they found that insulin influences ANGPTL8 expression by regulating this pathway. It is well established that acute activation of the PI3K/Akt pathway is predominantly mediated by phosphorylation rather than de novo protein synthesis. Consistent with this mechanism, our supplementary data demonstrated that ANGPTL8 also significantly elevated the expression of p-PI3K and p-Akt in the condition of insulin, with same changes in their total protein expression. These findings provide robust evidence that ANGPTL8 may trigger the rapid activation of the PI3K/Akt cascade to exert its protective effects.

Zhang et al..‘s research [36] suggests that ANGPTL8 may enhance insulin sensitivity by inducing insulin-mediated PI3K/Akt phosphorylation. These studies directly support our findings, indicating that high ANGPTL8 expression can enhance insulin sensitivity and glucose uptake by regulating insulin-mediated Akt activation, thereby improving glucose tolerance. Moreover, our proteomics KEGG pathway enrichment analysis results confirm that the PI3K/Akt pathway plays a role in this process, further supporting this conclusion.

In addition, our in vivo experiments revealed that the translation level of ANGPTL8 in the liver, pancreas, and placenta of GDM mouse models was significantly higher compared to non-GDM mice. Over-expression of ANGPTL8 improved glucose tolerance in GDM mice and enhanced their liver cells’ ability to take up glucose. This finding supports the potential role of ANGPTL8 in glucose regulation during gestational diabetes. Since Akt was significantly activated in the livers of GDM mice with ANGPTL8 over-expression, we hypothesize that AKT, as a key protein in the signaling pathway, also regulates glucose metabolism in these mice. However, this hypothesis requires further validation.

Finally, other authors highlighted the directions and prospects for future research. Recent studies have shown that the levels of ANGPTL8 in the circulation in hypertensive patients and mice and rats are significantly elevated, which is generally attributed to ANGPTL8’s role in inhibiting lipoprotein lipase (LPL) [37]. Another study suggests that ANGPTL8 may act as a negative regulator of pathological cardiac hypertrophy [38], and the ANGPTL8 family could potentially serve as a therapeutic target for preventing cardiovascular diseases [39]. Furthermore, it remains to be determined whether interventions targeting the ANGPTL8 triad can offer long-term health benefits in humans. These findings suggest that ANGPTL8 might be an insulin-sensitivity cytokine capable of regulating glucose metabolism, and it could represent a new approach to treating insulin-resistant diabetes, including gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM).

A consideration in interpreting our findings is the difference between the experimental models employed. The STZ-induced GDM mouse model is a well-established method for studying the consequences of severe hyperglycemia resulting from pancreatic β-cell damage and subsequent insulin deficiency. While this reflects a possible GDM pathology, it differs from the predominant human GDM phenotype, which is primarily characterized by insulin resistance. To explore the mechanism in a context of insulin resistance, we utilized a well-characterized in vitro model of insulin-resistant HepG2 cells. Intriguingly, we observed a consistent upregulation of ANGPTL8 in both the insulin-deficient in vivo model and the insulin-resistant in vitro model. This convergence of results across two pathogenically distinct models suggests that the regulation of ANGPTL8 may be a common downstream consequence of the metabolic dysregulation (e.g., hyperglycemia and/or hyperinsulinemia) present in both insulin-deficient and insulin-resistant states, thereby broadening the potential relevance of our findings to different facets of GDM pathology.

Regarding other limitations of this study, we summarize as follows: Firstly, it is not yet clear where ANGPTL8 specifically acts in the PI3K/AKT signaling. Specifically, which protein in this pathway ANGPTL8 directly interacts with and how it regulates the pathway through modifications still requires further research. Secondly, Zhang et al. [36]. found that after over-expression of ANGPTL8 for 60 days, its effect on improving glucose tolerance was not significant. This phenomenon may be owing to the gradual decrease in m-ANGPTL8 expression in the liver of diabetic mice following AAV-8 transfer. Additionally, since the treatment time does not align with the peak expression of ANGPTL8 in the liver, it remains uncertain whether over-expression of ANGPTL8 has long-term effects on gestational diabetes in mice. Lastly, we did not study insulin-resistant or GDM mice with ANGPTL8 knockout, leading to a significant limitation in the study of gestational diabetes animal models.

Conclussion

In summary, these experiments’ results presented in our report consistently indicated that ANGPTL8 enhances insulin-mediated glucose uptake by activating the insulin-mediated PI3K/AKT/GSK-3β pathway. Over-expression of ANGPTL8 in in-vitro RI model can raise the uptake of glucose, and the over-expression of m-ANGPTL8 in the livers of GDM mice might improve insulin sensitivity and alleviate insulin resistance. Our research is significant for managing insulin-resistant in GDM, suggesting that ANGPTL8 could serve as a potential anti-diabetic angent, implicating that ANGPTL8 might be a potential new medicine to reduce postprandial serum glucose level, also might represent a novel clinical treatment strategy.
