Disruption of a major facilitator superfamily transporter responsible for acetic acid efflux, increases the virulence of the insect pathogenic fungus Metarhizium rileyi

Introduction

Pests are globally widespread and pose a major threat to agriculture, adversely affecting crop growth and yields across nearly all ecosystems and farming systems. Among diverse classes of biopesticides, entomopathogenic fungi constitute a vital group owing to their high host specificity, potent insecticidal activity, safety to non-target organisms, and low risk of resistance evolution in target pests (Wang et al., 2017). However, because the time from natural infection to insect death is considerably longer than that of chemical pesticides, shortening both in vitro and in vivo growth cycles while maintaining insecticidal activity remains a key priority in the discovery of novel fungal biopesticides and the genetic improvement of existing strains.

In living organisms, the phospholipid bilayer forms a hydrophobic barrier for cells and organelles, whereas a variety of membrane-embedded transporters fulfill critical physiological functions—such as nutrient acquisition, metabolic waste excretion, intercellular communication, and energy metabolism—during fundamental growth and developmental processes (Rosado and Bayer, 2021). Among the secondary active transporters characterized to date, the major facilitator superfamily (MFS) represents one of the largest and most widely distributed superfamilies across the biosphere (Pao et al., 1998). They mediate the transport of a diverse range of substrates, including lipids, ions, carbohydrates, amino acids, peptides, and nucleosides (Niño-González et al., 2019). In contrast to ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters, substance transport mediated by MFS transporters is driven by transmembrane electrochemical and osmotic gradients rather than by ATP hydrolysis (Shilton et al., 2015; Jeckelmann et al., 2020). MFS transporter members are classified into 17 distinct families, with over one million sequenced proteins currently cataloged in the Transporter Classification Database (TCDB; http://www.tcdb.org) (Saier et al., 2021).

Numerous solute carriers in mammals—which are embedded in lipid bilayers—are well-established members of the MFS superfamily and play critical roles in both drug transport and the regulation of cellular resistance mechanisms, as exemplified by GLUT1, a protein involved in glucose transport (Pascual et al., 2004), and MFSD2A, a transporter responsible for docosahexaenoic acid uptake (Nguyen et al., 2014). In plants, members of the MFS transporter family fulfill essential functions in core growth regulatory processes, as illustrated by the well-characterized PHT1 and NRT2 families, which mediate phosphate and nitrate transport, respectively (Jacquot et al., 2020; An et al., 2023). In fungi, MFS transporters are implicated in sustaining growth across diverse stress conditions and modulating host-pathogen interactions. For instance, AaMFS19, which encodes an MFS transporter in the citrus pathogen Alternaria alternata, is modulated by kinases including Yap1, Skn7 and MAPK to mediate responses to oxidative stress and fungicide exposure (Chen et al., 2017). Salicylic acid (SA) serves as a key phytohormone involved in wheat defense against pathogen invasion (Qi et al., 2018). FgMFS1 (FGSG_03725), an MFS transporter in Fusarium graminearum, is significantly upregulated during infection in response to elevated salicylic acid (SA) levels, thereby facilitating successful host colonization (Chen et al., 2021). Although numerous MFS transporters have been identified, their functional characterization has progressed considerably more slowly than their structural analyses. In addition, MFS proteins may differ in substrate specificity and transport direction across species.

In this work, we obtained a M. rileyi strain with significantly enhanced growth and potent insecticidal virulence via disruption of MrMFS1 (NOR_04079), a gene encoding an MFS transporter. The molecular mechanism underlying this growth phenotype involves intracellular accumulation of acetic acid in conidia, which serves as a metabolically recyclable substrate that markedly promotes both conidial germination and mycelial growth—in vitro and during insect infection. The findings of this study provide novel insights into the important roles of MFS transporters in entomopathogenic fungi and offer valuable references for the development of hypervirulent fungal strains.

Materials and methods

Biological materials

The M. rileyi virulent strain CQNr01, isolated from naturally infected Spodoptera litura larvae, was used throughout the study (Chen et al., 2012). S. litura individuals were purchased from Keyun Biological Pesticide Co., Ltd. (Henan, China) and reared artificially in the laboratory at 25 ± 1 °C, 75% ± 10% relative humidity, and a photoperiod of 16: 8 (light: dark). E. coli DH5α and Agrobacterium tumefaciens AGL1 strains (Weidi Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), employed for vector construction and fungal transformation, were cultured in Luria-Bertani medium (LB).

Sequence analysis

By comparing existing transcriptome data with the NCBI public database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/GCA_001636745.1/) (Shang et al., 2016; Fan et al., 2023), genes encoding MFS transporters in M. rileyi were identified. The protein sequence of MrMFS1 was used as a query for homology searching against the NCBI database (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi), and protein sequences from species with the highest homology (protein sequence percent identity ≥50%, query cover ≥80%, and expect value < 1e−5) were downloaded. Subsequently, the Neighbor-Joining phylogenetic tree for the classification of the MrMFS1 protein was constructed using MEGA version 5 (Tamura et al., 2011) and visualized with iTOL (https://itol.embl.de/) (Letunic and Bork, 2024). Transmembrane helix prediction for the proteins was predicted using the DeepTMHMM Server v.1.0.44 (https://dtu.biolib.com/DeepTMHMM), and three-dimensional protein models were generated using AlphaFold2 (https://alphafold2.biodesign.ac.cn/).

Genetic transformation

A binary vector carrying a selectable marker (hygromycin resistance) was introduced into A. tumefaciens strain AGL1, and mutant strains harboring random T-DNA insertions were generated through co-cultivation with fungal blastospores. Briefly, M. rileyi was inoculated onto SMAY medium (40 g maltose, 10 g peptone, 10 g yeast extract, and 20 g agar per L), and blastospores were produced on approximately the second day. Subsequently, the spores were suspended in sterile distilled water to prepare a spore suspension, which was then mixed with A. tumefaciens harboring the target plasmid induced in liquid NIM medium (2.05 g K₂HPO₄, 1.45 g KH₂PO₄, 0.5 g NH₄NO₃, 0.15 g NaCl, 0.25 g MgSO₄·7H₂O, 0.01 g CaCl₂·2H₂O, 0.0025 g FeSO₄·7H₂O, 0.9 g glucose, 5 mL glycerol, and 5.33 g MES per L). The mixture was spread onto solid NIM medium (liquid NIM supplemented with 1.5% agar) overlaid with a microporous membrane. After two days of induction and co-cultivation, the microporous membranes were transferred to SMAY medium supplemented with cefotaxime and hygromycin for antibiotic selection. Finally, the grown transformants on the membrane were picked and subjected to molecular verification. Insertion mutants were selected based on antibiotic resistance, and the genomic insertion sites were mapped by thermal asymmetric interlaced PCR (TAIL-PCR) with specific primers (Table S1). TAIL-PCR was performed to amplify the flanking sequences of T-DNA insertion sites according to the standard three-round thermal cycling procedure (Liu and Whittier, 1995).

The gene in M. rileyi was deleted from the genome as reported (Lin et al., 2021). The flanking sequences of appropriate length (approximately 1000 bp) upstream and downstream of the gene were cloned and inserted into the corresponding regions of the hygromycin resistance marker in the knockout vector pzp-hph, respectively (Shao et al., 2015). Transformants were selected and verified for target site integration, and those designated for subsequent experiments were validated by Southern blot to confirm single-copy insertion. To achieve gene complementation, the genomic sequence—including the native promoter, open reading frame (ORF), and terminator region—was cloned by PCR and inserted into the pzp-Sur vector, which was constructed by substituting the hph expression cassette of pzp‑hph with the Sur expression cassette. The gene fused with GFP was expressed in M. rileyi for the investigation of protein subcellular localization. The target CDS sequence was amplified by high-fidelity PCR using cDNA as the template and then introduced into the pCAMBIA1303-TrpC—Hygro-gpdA-GFP vector (Miaoling Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China; Cat. no. P0823), which harbors a green fluorescent protein (GFP) tag and the gpdA promoter. Subsequently, the fused MrMFS1-GFP fragments were integrated into the genome of wild-type strains.

Gene expression analysis

To measure the expression levels of genes in the samples, total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Beyotime Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China; Cat. no. R0016), and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a kit (Mei5 Biotechnology Co.,Ltd., Beijing, China; Cat. no. MF011). For accuracy, the gene quantification experiment was conducted three times, with three technical replicates included in each run. The reaction mixtures were prepared with SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China; Cat. No. Q312), and the reactions were performed using a CFX Manager software (Bio-Rad). The Tub gene (GenBank accession number: AF399718.1) in M. rileyi was used as a reference gene, and the relative expression level of each gene was quantified using the 2−ΔΔCt method (Ingham et al., 2001).

Phenotypic analysis

Conidia were harvested to determine the germination rate, growth rate, and conidial yield. Briefly, the conidial suspension was prepared in 0.05% Tween 80 and adjusted to a final concentration of 10⁷ conidia/mL. A 50 μL aliquot of the suspension was evenly spread onto SMAY medium, followed by incubation at 25 °C. Starting from 6 h post-inoculation, 1 × 1 cm culture blocks were sampled every 6 h, and the number of germinated conidia per 100 was quantified under a microscope to determine the germination rate. For the growth rate assay, 3 μL aliquots of each suspension were spotted onto SMAY media, and fungal colony development was continuously observed and recorded. After complete conidiation on day 10, equal-sized culture plugs were aseptically excised from each strain using sterile cork borers and transferred into 50 mL tubes containing 10 mL of 0.05% Tween-80. The supernatant was then 10-fold diluted with the same solvent, and the conidial concentration in the resulting suspension was quantified to evaluate strain-specific conidial yield.

In addition, conidia were harvested from SMAY medium to compare fungal tolerance to various environmental stresses, including high salt concentrations (50 mM CaCl2, 1 M NaCl), oxidative stress (2 mM H2O2, 0.3 mM menadione), and cell membrane-disrupting agents (1% and 2% SDS). A 3 μL aliquot of each suspension was spotted onto SMAY medium supplemented with various exogenous stress-inducing agents, and colony development was monitored daily and documented by digital photography at 2 and 7 days post-inoculation. To prevent thermal degradation of heat-labile compounds during autoclaving, all reagents were filter-sterilized and aseptically incorporated into the molten agar medium after cooling to ≤60 °C. Moreover, 50 μL aliquots of conidial suspensions (10⁷ conidia/mL) were evenly spread onto SMAY medium. Subsequently, the inoculated plates were incubated in light-controlled incubators at designated temperatures. After 48 h, strain-specific developmental morphology was assessed microscopically. Conidia-coated SMAY plates were exposed to UV-C irradiation (1350 mW·m⁻²) for 0, 2, and 4 h, followed by incubation at 28 °C for 48 h. Conidial germination rates were then determined microscopically to assess strain-specific UV tolerance.

Drought resistance was evaluated as a proxy for strain shelf life under desiccation stress. Briefly, conidia were desiccated at 30 °C for 48 h, stored in the dark for 30 days, and subsequently resuspended in 0.05% Tween 80 to prepare conidial suspensions at defined concentrations (10⁷ conidia/mL). A 50 μL aliquot of conidial suspension was spotted onto SMAY medium plates and evenly spread. Conidial germination rates were quantified at defined time points to compare drought resistance across strains. All experiments described above were conducted in triplicate, with three technical replicates per run.

Microstructure of the cell wall

Surface morphologies of conidia and hyphae were examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) after fixation in 2.5% glutaraldehyde, dehydration in a graded ethanol series, mounting on silicon wafers, and gold sputter-coating. Conidial pretreatment for transmission electron microscope (TEM) followed the same protocol as that used for SEM, with minor modifications to accommodate ultrathin sectioning requirements. After chemical fixation, dehydration, resin embedding, and ultrathin sectioning, the ultrastructure of samples was examined by TEM.

Conidial surface properties

Conidia were harvested and separately suspended in distilled water and liquid paraffin to assess surface hydrophobicity. Additionally, conidia were harvested, washed three times with PUM buffer (2.22% K₂PO₄, 0.73% KH₂PO₄, 0.18% urea, 0.02% MgSO₄, w/v, pH 7.1), and resuspended in fresh PUM buffer to standardize the conidial concentration to 1 × 10⁷ conidia/mL. Aliquots (3 mL) of conidial suspension (initial concentration: n1) were mixed with 300 μL of hexadecane, incubated at 25 °C for 10 min to allow phase separation, and then chilled at 4 °C to stabilize the interface prior to quantification. After 30 min, the upper hexadecane phase was carefully aspirated, and the conidial concentration in the lower aqueous phase (n2) was determined by hemocytometer counting to quantify surface hydrophobicity. The hydrophobicity index of conidia for each strain was calculated as (n1-n2)/n1.

Glucan and chitin in fungal cell wall

Cell walls were prepared from fungal samples via ultrasonic disruption and subsequent centrifugation. The samples were then incubated in DB buffer (20 mM Tris, 50 mM EDTA, pH 8.0), washed three times with ice-cold deionized water (ddH₂O), and lyophilized. The lyophilized samples were suspended in 1 M NaOH (0.5 mg/mL, w/v) for 12 h. Subsequently, the pH of the supernatant was adjusted to 5.5, and the precipitates were collected and washed. The precipitates were redissolved in 3 M H₂SO₄ and hydrolyzed at 100 °C for 1 hour. Following cooling to room temperature, the pH was adjusted to 7.0, and the glucose content was determined using a glucose assay kit (Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China; Cat. no. BC2505). Dry cell wall samples were analyzed for chitin content using a colorimetric assay. Briefly, 25 mg of the sample was mixed with 5 mL of digestion solution (concentrated sulfuric acid: 5% hydrogen peroxide = 1: 4), followed by incubation at 55 °C with shaking for 1.5 h. 2 mL of the supernatant was transferred and centrifuged, followed by the addition of 2 mL of color reagent (prepared by mixing 50 mL of 2% salicylic acid in ethanol with 2.5 mL of 15% NaOH solution). The content of tested samples was calculated using the formula: [(A450 − 0.021) / 0.387 × V × 10–6] / m × 100%, where “A450” is the absorbance of the sample at 450 nm, “V” is the total volume of the reaction system, and “m” is the mass of the sample used.

Virulence assays

Bioassays were performed on third-instar S. litura larvae using topical inoculation and intrahaemocoelic injection, respectively. In the topical inoculation group, conidia were harvested from 10-day-old cultures grown on SMAY medium; a 5 μL aliquot of conidial suspension (1 × 10⁷ conidia/mL) was inoculated onto the insects’ surface. An equivalent volume of the conidial suspension (1 × 10⁷ conidia/mL) was injected into the haemocoel of a separate group of third-instar S. litura larvae exhibiting consistent physiological status to establish the intrahaemocoelic injection group. Each group comprised 50 insects, and the experiment was conducted three times. After injection, larval molting in each group was observed and recorded as an indicator of insect growth activity. In parallel, larval survival rates in the topical inoculation and intrahaemocoelic injection groups were recorded continuously (calculated as the number of dead larvae / total number of insects), and virulence differences among strains were analyzed based on LT50 values (time to 50% mortality of the tested insects). In addition, the relative fungal content within insects was quantified. Briefly, mixed genomic DNA from infected insects (the whole insect body was used for DNA extraction) was extracted to quantify the relative content of M. rileyi within the insects using qPCR. Generally, at least five insects were pooled per treatment group for DNA extraction. The Tub gene (Accession: AF399718.1) of M. rileyi was used to estimate fungal biomass relative to the actin gene (Accession: LOC111359844) of S. litura. The experiment was conducted three times, with three replicates established each time.

Formation and penetration assessment of appressoria

Cellophane membranes and locust wings were used as hydrophobic substrates to induce appressorium differentiation in vitro. Sterilized hindwings and cellophane were aseptically placed flat on SMAY agar plates; each substrate was uniformly overlaid with conidial suspension using a soft-bristled brush, followed by incubation at 25 °C. Similar to the quantification of conidial germination, the number of appressoria formed on cellophane and locust wings was continuously monitored and recorded. In the other group, after two days of cultivation, the hindwings were removed from the medium using sterile forceps. One portion was transferred to fresh SMAY medium, while the other was stained with Calcofluor White Brightener 2B (CFW). The original SMAY plates were retained in the incubator for further cultivation. Appressorium formation and subsequent fungal development on the wing surfaces were observed. Additionally, on day 7 post-inoculation, fungi on the SMAY medium (after hindwing removal) and on the transferred hindwings were observed separately to assess differences in infection structure formation and penetration ability.

Acid profiles and directional analysis of transport

The organic acid profiles in fungal samples were analyzed by liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS). The binding interaction between the target protein and a representative organic acid (acetic acid) was predicted using molecular docking simulations. Then, acetic acid was labeled with stable FITC fluorescence and co-incubated with conidia in SMY medium. At 24 h, a small aliquot of the sample was collected for fluorescence observation, while the remaining fungal material was centrifuged at low speed and washed repeatedly. The fungal sample was transferred to fresh SMY medium, incubated on a shaker at 25 °C for an additional 24 h, and the fluorescence of both the fungal mycelia and the medium was monitored again. Acetic acid transport and proton coupling were assessed by systematically varying the extracellular pH of the cultivation medium using HCl and NaOH. The extracellular and intracellular acetic acid concentrations were quantified by LC-MS following pH adjustment of the culture medium with HCl or NaOH across a defined pH gradient.

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance between groups was determined using a two-tailed Student’s t-test. Probability values (p-values) were calculated using Microsoft Excel 2010, and results were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. Statistical graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism version 8.0.2, and abstract graphics were created using the BioGDP online platform (https://biogdp.com/) (Jiang et al., 2025).

Results

Identification of MrMFS1 in M. rileyi

From a T-DNA insertional mutagenesis library of M. rileyi, a mutant strain (Nr-ac01) displaying significantly accelerated in vitro growth was isolated (Fig. 1A). Sequence analysis revealed that the T-DNA insertion site is located within the open reading frame (ORF) of a gene encoding a major facilitator superfamily transporter (709 bp downstream of the ATG start codon), which was designated as MrMFS1 (Fig. S1A). The MrMFS1 protein harbors a typical MFS1 PFAM domain (PFAM accession PF07690), spanning residues 76 to 414 as predicted by SMART, and is predicted to adopt 12 transmembrane α-helices by DeepTMHMM (Fig. 1B and C). The 12 transmembrane α-helices adopt the canonical 6 + 6 inverted repeat topology, a structural hallmark conserved across functionally characterized MFS transporters (Fig. 1D and E).

The MrMFS1 protein displays moderate evolutionary conservation, particularly within entomopathogenic fungal species of the genus Metarhizium. Most bootstrap support values for the internal branches of the phylogeny exceeded 90%, indicating high topological robustness of the inferred phylogenetic tree. Meanwhile, the MrMFS1 orthologs from the entomopathogenic genera Conoideocrella and Purpureocillium shared high sequence similarity, with bootstrap values exceeding 85%; phylogenetically, they resolved as two adjacent, well-supported clades closely related to the genus Metarhizium (Fig. S1B). Notably, the circular phylogenetic tree topology employed in this study represents only relative phylogenetic proximity. That is, strains within the same clade are inferred to be more closely related, whereas those in distinct clades are inferred to be more distantly related; absolute genetic distances cannot be estimated from branch lengths under this representation.

Most MFS proteins are localized to biological membranes, including the plasma membrane, mitochondrial inner and outer membranes, and thylakoid membranes of chloroplasts (Perland et al., 2017; Valifard et al., 2023). To explore the subcellular localization of mature MrMFS1, a recombinant MrMFS1-GFP fusion protein was expressed in M. rileyi under the control of the constitutive gpdA promoter. In contrast to the diffuse and uniform intracellular distribution of GFP observed in the control strain, MrMFS1–GFP fluorescence was predominantly localized to the hyphal periphery, consistent with plasma membrane localization (Fig. 1F)..

The spatiotemporal expression patterns of genes are closely linked to their functional specificity. In M. rileyi, MrMFS1 expression peaks during mycelial differentiation into conidia, and high transcript levels are also detected during the early stage of conidial germination (Xin et al., 2020) (Fig. 1G). During in vivo growth within the insect host, MrMFS1 expression displayed a similar biphasic pattern; upregulation was observed during conidial germination following host penetration, and again during the conversion of surface-associated hyphae into conidia (Boucias et al., 2016).

The ΔMrMFS1 strain exhibits accelerated growth, whereas no significant alterations in morphological or biochemical characteristics

The ΔMrMFS1 strain was generated and verified (Fig. S1A, C). Consistent with the phenotype of the random T-DNA insertion mutant Nr-ac01, the MFS1 knockout mutant also displayed accelerated growth in standard SMAY medium (Fig. 2A). Notably, conidial germination of the ΔMrMFS1 strain was significantly accelerated: over 50% germination occurred within 20 h, and full germination was achieved by 48 h—compared with approximately 50% germination at 36 h and complete germination at 60 h in the wild-type strain (Fig. 2B). Although vegetative growth was accelerated, conidial yield at harvest did not differ significantly between the ΔMrMFS1 mutant and the wild-type strain (Fig. 2C).

High-magnification SEM revealed that hyphae of both the wild-type and ΔMrMFS1 strains exhibited rough surfaces with irregularly distributed protuberances. Similarly, conidia displayed a textured surface featuring an organized array of fibrillar structures arranged in a knotted pattern (Fig. 2D). No differences were detected in conidial wall thickness or in the content of chitin and 1,3-glucan—the major structural components of fungal cell walls—between the ΔMrMFS1 and wild-type strains (Fig. 2E-G). The unchanged composition of chitin and β−1,3-glucan preserves the hydrophobic properties of conidia; hydrophobicity levels remained comparable between the wild-type and ΔMrMFS1 strains, ranging from 60% to 75% (Fig. 2H and I).

The MrMFS1 deletion mutant exhibits enhanced virulence against S. litura

Following infection with M. rileyi, larval locomotor activity was suppressed, and molting associated with growth was significantly impaired. In larvae inoculated with the ΔMrMFS1 strain, the proportion of individuals undergoing molting was significantly lower than in those inoculated with the wild-type strain, regardless of inoculation via topical application or intrahaemocoelic injection (Fig. S2). Throughout the bioassay period, the LT50 value for insects inoculated with the ΔMrMFS1 strain was <5 days, substantially lower than that of the wild-type strain (Fig. 3A). Moreover, the relative fungal biomass in insects inoculated with the ΔMrMFS1 strain was also higher than that in those inoculated with the wild-type strain (Fig. 3B). Similarly, this phenomenon was also observed in insects infected via intrahaemocoelic injection (Fig. 3C and D). Mycelial growth and conidia production on cadavers infected with the ΔMrMFS1 strain were accelerated relative to those infected with the wild-type strain (Fig. 3E). Notably, cadavers of insects killed by either the wild-type strain or the ΔMrMFS1 strain underwent mummification, accompanied by mycelia growth and conidiation on the surface.

Accelerated differentiation and formation of infection structures with the absence of MrMFS1 in M. rileyi

Appressoria are key virulence structures employed by both plant and insect pathogenic fungi to initiate host infection; their precise differentiation and development are indispensable for breaching the host cuticle or epidermis and establishing internal colonization (Talbot et al., 2019; Müller and Scheuring, et al., 2024). Both the wild-type and ΔMrMFS1 strains differentiated and formed appressoria on cellophane membranes and hindwings (Fig. 4A). However, relative to the wild-type strain, the ΔMrMFS1 mutant exhibited accelerated appressorium differentiation, and the density of differentiated appressoria per unit area of the inoculated surface was substantially increased (Fig. 4B). Moreover, the ΔMrMFS1 mutant demonstrated enhanced capacity for cuticular penetration and subsequent invasion of underlying host tissues. The ΔMrMFS1 mutant not only differentiated appressoria more rapidly and exhibited accelerated mycelial growth on wing surfaces, but also accumulated significantly greater fungal biomass and displayed a higher growth rate in the underlying medium following wing penetration (Fig. 4C).

After entering the insect host, entomopathogenic fungi typically differentiate into hydrophilic hyphal bodies to facilitate nutrient acquisition and subsequently undergo sustained proliferation until internal nutrients are fully depleted (Wang and St Leger, 2006). Here, M. rileyi undergoes rapid differentiation and produces blastospores that are several-fold to tens of times larger in size than conidia, thereby increasing the specific surface area and enhancing nutrient uptake efficiency. Subsequently, blastospores transformed into rod-shaped hyphal bodies and underwent rapid growth. During these morphological differentiation and developmental processes, the ΔMrMFS1 strain consistently outperforms the wild-type strain (Fig. 4D).

MrMFS1 deletion contributes to elevated stress tolerance and improved biocontrol potential in M. rileyi

In this study, the ΔMrMFS1 strain—characterized by an accelerated growth rate and markedly improved insecticidal virulence—was systematically evaluated for its stress tolerance. As a result, the deletion of MrMFS1 conferred reduced sensitivity to both oxidative and high-salt stress, accompanied by accelerated conidial germination and enhanced colony expansion (Fig. 5A). Regarding thermal adaptation, the ΔMrMFS1 mutant exhibits low-temperature tolerance (Fig. 5B), indicating increased physiological resilience compared with the wild-type strain under environmentally fluctuating conditions. Moreover, the ΔMrMFS1 mutant exhibits a significantly higher conidial germination rate following exposure to equivalent ultraviolet (UV) radiation intensities (Fig. 5C). A long shelf life is critical for fungal pesticides. After long-term storage under dry conditions, the germination and growth of the wild-type strain were significantly impaired, with only approximately 43% of conidia germinating by 48 h, whereas the ΔMrMFS1 strain exhibited a germination rate exceeding 80%, indicating a significantly extended shelf life for production compared to the wild-type strain (Fig. 5D and E).

MrMFS1 is a proton-coupled transporter that mediates the efflux of acetic acid

Metabolite profiling revealed no statistically significant differences in the intracellular concentrations of common monosaccharides and oligosaccharides between the ΔMrMFS1 mutant and the wild-type strain (Fig. S3). However, substantial accumulation of organic acids, particularly acetic acid, was observed in both hyphal and conidial cells of the ΔMrMFS1 mutant (Fig. 6A). Concurrently, extracellular acetic acid concentrations in the culture medium of the ΔMrMFS1 mutant were significantly reduced (Fig. 6B). These findings collectively indicate that the MrMFS1 protein probably functions as an efflux transporter for acetic acid. Subsequently, organic acid molecules were labeled with FITC and introduced into the culture medium. After cultivation, high fluorescence intensity was observed in both the culture medium and the mycelia of both the wild-type strain and the ΔMrMFS1 mutant. Then, hyphae were transferred to fresh conventional medium and incubated for 12 h. Thereafter, fluorescence intensity was detected in both the culture medium and the hyphal cells of the wild-type strain. In contrast, in the ΔMrMFS1 strain, fluorescence was retained exclusively within the hyphae, with no detectable signal observed in the culture medium, suggesting that acetic acid efflux is dependent on MrMFS1 (Fig. 6C). Under progressively increasing proton concentrations (induced by HCl treatment), MrMFS1 expression was downregulated in the wild-type strain, accompanied by intracellular accumulation of acetic acid. In contrast, intracellular acetic acid levels in the MrMFS1-deficient mutant remained stable despite increasing proton concentrations (Fig. 6D and E). Moreover, under low-proton conditions (with NaOH treatment), MrMFS1 expression was significantly up-regulated in the wild-type strain, accompanied by a marked reduction in intracellular levels of acetic acid. However, intracellular acetic acid content in the MrMFS1‑deletion mutant remained unchanged irrespective of variations in proton concentration (Fig. 6F and G). More importantly, the MrMFS1 overexpression strain exhibited constitutively elevated expression across all tested pH conditions; however, no statistically significant differences in expression levels were observed among the pH treatments, and expression in the overexpression strain consistently exceeded that of the wild-type strain by more than fivefold. Moreover, no statistically significant difference in intracellular acetic acid concentration was observed between the MrMFS1 overexpression strain and the wild-type strain across the tested pH conditions (Fig S4A, B). These results demonstrate that the MrMFS1 protein functions as a proton-coupled acetic acid transporter.

Discussion

Entomopathogenic fungi represent a critical class of biological control agents widely utilized in the field of biopesticides (Ferreyra-Suarez et al., 2024). In contrast to bacterial and viral pesticides, most entomopathogenic fungi possess a prolonged conidiation cycle—the process responsible for producing conidia, their primary infective propagules—thereby posing a significant bottleneck to the industrial application of numerous promising biocontrol fungal species. Thus, shortening the growth and reproductive cycle without compromising insecticidal efficacy represents a key priority in both the screening of novel entomopathogenic fungal strains and the genetic improvement of existing ones. Here, we identified a coding gene of the major facilitator superfamily, MrMFS1, from M. rileyi. Notably, unlike the canonical MFS transporters characterized in various fungi, disruption of MrMFS1 did not compromise the structural integrity or growth phenotype of the strain. Instead, it markedly promoted conidial germination and colony development. Furthermore, the accelerated growth phenotype of the ΔMrMFS1 mutant was consistently observed not only across a range of environmental stress conditions but also on host surfaces and within host tissues. That is, the ΔMrMFS1 mutant possesses significantly enhanced stress tolerance and insecticidal virulence compared with the wild-type strain. Thus, it can be concluded that the deletion of MrMFS1 results in a comprehensive enhancement of virulence in M. rileyi, generating a hypervirulent strain with improved fitness. This finding represents a notable and unexpected discovery. To our knowledge, no such phenotypic alterations have been reported in Metarhizium species or, more broadly, in most fungal pesticides.

The MrMFS1 protein characterized in this study is a typical MFS transporter localized to the cell membrane, as confirmed by protein sequence analysis and fluorescence localization assays. No obvious differences in cell morphology or in the content of chitin and 1,3-glucan, the major structural components of fungal cell walls, were observed between the ΔMrMFS1 mutant and the wild-type strain. The unaltered composition of these cell wall polysaccharides preserves the native hydrophobicity of conidia, thereby ensuring robust adhesion to the waxy, hydrophobic cuticular surfaces of insect hosts and maintaining full insecticidal efficacy under field application (Alon et al., 2022; Hajek and Eastburn, 2003). Expression of MrMFS1 in M. rileyi exhibits a temporally coordinated pattern with conidiation. MrMFS1 expression is markedly upregulated during conidial germination and the subsequent transition from germ tubes to mature conidiophores, as demonstrated under both nutrient-defined in vitro conditions and during active colonization of insect hosts. Here, pronounced morphological alterations are observed during conidiophore development and conidial maturation, suggesting a potential role for MrMFS1 in coordinating cell wall biogenesis or secondary metabolism during these critical developmental transitions.

Robust field adaptability is a prerequisite for the successful application of biopesticides. Given the frequent occurrence of extreme weather conditions in the field, it is critical for entomopathogenic fungi to tolerate diverse abiotic stresses, including temperature extremes, UV radiation, soil pH, salinity, and drought. Under standardized in vitro conditions, M. rileyi displays measurable mycelial growth at temperatures as low as 4 °C and reaches its maximum radial growth at 25 °C. This thermal growth profile closely matches the ambient temperature regimes experienced by Lepidoptera pests in subtropical agroecosystems, supporting the ecological compatibility and practical utility of M. rileyi as a biocontrol agent for targeted crop protection in these regions (Marchioro and Foerster, 2011; Matsukura et al., 2024). The ΔMrMFS1 mutant exhibits accelerated growth relative to the wild-type strain under standard in vitro culture conditions and further exhibits significantly enhanced tolerance to multiple abiotic stressors, including elevated temperature, high salinity, and oxidative stress. These findings suggest that the accelerated growth phenotype of the ΔMrMFS1 mutant arises from intrinsic perturbations in central carbon metabolism, rather than being affected by exogenous compounds. As a membrane-localized transporter protein, MrMFS1 deficiency likely compromises membrane integrity, thereby impairing its physiological function. Surprisingly, when exposured to high concentrations of SDS, a cell membrane–disrupting agent, the ΔMrMFS1 mutant still formed intact colonies, whereas colony formation by the wild-type strain was severely inhibited. Here, we hypothesize that MrMFS1 may be structurally compromised upon SDS exposure, thereby facilitating SDS influx and subsequent disruption of intracellular homeostasis. However, the absence of the MrMFS1 protein prevents SDS-induced membrane destabilization; the pore that would otherwise be induced by high concentrations of SDS fails to form, thereby conferring enhanced SDS tolerance to the ΔMrMFS1 mutant. This hypothesis requires experimental validation.

A critical consideration for the industrial application of entomopathogenic fungi is that enhancements in growth and abiotic stress tolerance must not compromise virulence—which must be preserved or, ideally, enhanced. In this study, accelerated growth of the ΔMrMFS1 mutant is also observed in insect hosts, accompanied by enhanced appressorium formation and accelerated blastospore development and differentiation within the hemocoel. Conidia with high cuticular infiltration capacity represent the primary infectious structures of entomopathogenic fungi. Following adhesion to the insect cuticle, conidia germinate and differentiate into appressoria, which penetrate the cuticle via a combination of mechanical pressure and exoenzymatic activity (Li et al., 2022). However, insects may partially mitigate this toxin -mediated pathogenic mechanism via cuticle shedding during molting. On one hand, molting enables insects to rapidly resume normal growth; on the other hand, it facilitates the removal of entomopathogenic fungi adhering to the cuticle surface. Deletion of MrMFS1 in M. rileyi accelerates appressorium differentiation and significantly increases the number of fungal cells penetrating the artificial induction matrix relative to the wild-type strain. These results indicate that the ΔMrMFS1 mutant exhibits enhanced invasive capacity, enabling more rapid host penetration and lethal action prior to completion of the host molting cycle. Upon entering the insect hemocoel, entomopathogenic fungi undergo morphological transition to hydrophilic hyphal bodies to facilitate nutrient acquisition, followed by sustained growth until internal nutrients are fully depleted. When introduced directly into the insect hemocoel via intrahaemocoelic injection, M. rileyi rapidly undergoes morphological differentiation to produce blastospores that are several-fold to over tenfold larger in size than conidia, thereby increasing the surface-area-to-volume ratio and nutrient uptake efficiency. Subsequently, the fungus differentiates into rod-shaped hyphae and undergoes rapid proliferative growth; notably, theΔMrMFS1 strain exhibits significantly enhanced hyphal extension rates relative to the wild-type strain.

We detected significantly elevated intracellular concentrations of acetic acid in both conidia and hyphae of the ΔMrMFS1 mutant relative to the wild-type strain. Subsequent fluorescence-based efflux assays further demonstrated that the ΔMrMFS1 mutant exhibits impaired acetic acid efflux from the cytosol. Moreover, these findings indicate that MrMFS1 protein functions as a proton-coupled acetic acid transporter in M. rileyi. Concomitantly, glycolysis and the TCA cycle—the central pathways of cellular energy metabolism—were significantly upregulated in the ΔMrMFS1 mutant, suggesting that acetic acid accumulation likely fuels enhanced metabolic flux and supports accelerated fungal growth.

Overall, our study demonstrates that disruption of MrMFS1 enhances fungal growth and developmental progression under diverse abiotic stress conditions as well as during host colonization—both in vitro and in vivo. In M. rileyi, MrMFS1 functions as a plasma membrane-localized transporter mediating proton-coupled acetic acid efflux; its deletion results in intracellular accumulation of acetic acid, which is subsequently assimilated as a carbon and energy source to support enhanced fungal growth. This study provides valuable insights into the physiological roles of MFS transporters in fungal pathogenesis and lays a foundation for the future development of highly virulent M. rileyi strains for insect control.
