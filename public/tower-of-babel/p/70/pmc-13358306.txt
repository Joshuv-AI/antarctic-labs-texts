Low-dose corticosteroids in severe pulmonary infection: a meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials
Authors: Joanne Lac, Sooyeon Han, Mahmood Ahmad, Harvey Starey, Soham Ganguly, Michael Tett, Oluwatosin Adeyemi, Jonathan J H Bray, Malik T Ahmad, Rui Providência
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposePulmonary infections are one of the leading causes of intensive care unit (ICU) admission and contribute to high mortality rates. This paper aimed to determine the effect of low-dose corticosteroids on outcomes in patients with severe pulmonary infections, including coronavirus disease of 2019, community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), pneumocystis pneumonia, sepsis, septic shock, and acute respiratory distress syndrome.MethodsWe systematically reviewed randomised controlled trials (RCTs) comparing low-dose corticosteroids (≤400 mg hydrocortisone-equivalent daily) to placebo or standard care in adults with severe pulmonary infections. The primary outcome was short-term mortality (≤90 days). Secondary outcomes included 28-day mortality, 30-day mortality, number of patients with at least one serious adverse event, length of hospital stay and ICU stay.ResultsWe analysed twelve RCTs including 4622 patients and 1203 events. The pooled OR showed a short-term mortality benefit: 0.83 (95% CI 0.70 to 0.98; p=0.029; I²=2.0%; number needed to treat: 37.84; Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE): low certainty). There was a reduction in length of ICU stay in patients with CAP, with a pooled mean difference of –0.78 days (95% CI –1.46 to –0.10, p=0.025). The use of corticosteroids did not significantly affect the length of hospital stay or severe adverse events. A significant difference remained in longer courses of steroids, whereas shorter courses had no significant difference (≤7 days vs >7 days; OR: 0.69; 95% CI 0.53 to 0.89; p=0.039 vs OR: 0.96; 95% CI 0.813 to 1.14; p=0.67; subgroup differences: p=0.03). Subgroup analyses in patients with CAP showed a significant benefit in short-term mortality (OR: 0.72, 95% CI 0.54 to 0.98; p=0.034; number needed to treat: 28.10).ConclusionWe found that low-dose corticosteroids significantly reduce 90-day mortality in severe CAP, although evidence for their benefit in other severe pulmonary infections and at shorter follow-up intervals remains inconclusive.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42024627881.Keywords: Pneumonia, COVID-19, ARDS, Bacterial Infection, Critical Care

Introduction

Pulmonary infections are a common cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, and can lead to serious complications such as sepsis or acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). International studies report that up to 70% intensive care unit (ICU) sepsis cases are due to pulmonary infections, including community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), COVID-19, and pneumocystis pneumonia (PCP).1,5 CAP cases continue to have significant mortality rates after hospitalisation, ranging from under 10% in patients under 60 to over 20% in those older than 60.6

While pulmonary conditions such as CAP, COVID-19, PCP, ARDS, and septic shock of pulmonary origin have distinct pathophysiology, they all share a critical feature in that the mortality and morbidity rates are driven by dysregulated inflammatory pathways, diffuse alveolar damage, and systemic consequences such as shock.7,9 Pathogen recognition activates both proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory mediators,10 and an imbalance in these mediators leads to overproduction of proinflammatory cytokines and subsequent organ damage.11 Therefore, while triggers may differ, the downstream pathophysiology relevant to corticosteroid action converges.

During an infectious episode, glucocorticoids are released into the systemic circulation to restore immune homeostasis, achieved by inhibiting transcriptional regulators of proinflammatory effectors.12 13 At high doses, glucocorticoids act via non-genomic mechanisms, leading to reduced cytokine production.14 Early randomised controlled trials (RCTs) evaluated the efficacy of high-dose corticosteroids in septic shock, but failed to demonstrate a benefit between treatment and placebo groups.15 16

Recent studies have transitioned to low-dose corticosteroid regimens. Evidence shows this approach provides medical advantages for certain respiratory conditions, including septic shock,17 severe CAP,18 and COVID-19.19 Guidelines currently endorse corticosteroid treatment (150 mg hydrocortisone-equivalent daily) for severe COVID-19 and conditionally approve it for septic shock and ARDS.20,22

The current recommendations for severe CAP treatment show variable approaches. The American Thoracic Society (ATS) and British Thoracic Society do not support corticosteroid therapy in severe CAP, but European and Latin American guidelines suggest corticosteroids (methylprednisolone, 0.5 mg/kg every 12 hours for 5 days) for patients with severe CAP showing signs of shock.23,25 South African guidelines recommend corticosteroids as an adjunct to antibiotics in CAP admitted to the ICU.26

Objectives

To evaluate the effectiveness of low-dose corticosteroids in reducing short-term (90-day) mortality, by treating critically ill adults with severe pulmonary infections such as COVID-19, CAP, PCP, sepsis of pulmonary origin, septic shock, and ARDS. Thus, we include the most recent RCTs to assist in informing future clinical guidelines on steroid therapy.27

Methods

This meta-analysis was conducted according to a pre-registered protocol on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO, ID: CRD42024627881).

Search strategy

We used OVID to search two databases (Embase and Medline) from January 1990 to December 2024, with no restriction on publication status, but including English-language articles only. Search terms used included “steroid”, “sepsis”, “pneumonia”, “covid”, “critical care” (full search can be found in the electronic online supplemental file 1 detail, ESM). We included only RCTs, with a population of adults with severe pulmonary infections (COVID-19, CAP, sepsis, septic shock, ARDS, and PCP). Adults must be ≥18 years, and the severity of the pulmonary infections must be defined as ‘severe’ in each study, with specified parameters (online supplemental table S1). Patients must be treated with low-dose corticosteroids (≤400 mg hydrocortisone-equivalent daily) and have a comparable group treated with placebo or standard care. Although no single agreed-upon definition of low-dose corticosteroids exists for severe pulmonary infections, this cut-off follows recently published systematic reviews and meta-analyses looking at mortality effects in severe pulmonary infection.27 28 This upper threshold enables us to capture the majority of studies employing a low-dose regimen. Our primary outcome was short-term mortality (≤90 days). Our secondary outcomes were 28-day mortality, 30-day mortality, number of patients with at least one serious adverse event (SAE), length of hospital stay and ICU stay. Studies must also report at least one of the primary or secondary outcomes listed. Studies in which the patient population was not limited to pulmonary sources of infection, but in which outcome data could be identified as a subgroup analysis, were also included. Studies were excluded if there was a mixed population of severity. SAEs were defined individually by each included study.1829,31 We also performed a manual search of reference lists of previous review articles, with the search completed on 8 December 2024. Two blinded investigators (JL, SH) screened articles for inclusion based on study title and abstract, and then relevant articles were retrieved for full text screening. All screened articles were able to be retrieved. This was reviewed again by two blinded investigators (HS, SG), and any conflicts were resolved by a third reviewer (JL).

Data extraction

Two investigators (SH, MT) extracted all variables using a standardised template, including study characteristics, population demographics, interventions, and outcome data.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Data were pooled by one investigator (JL), using the random-effects model; dichotomous outcomes (short-term mortality) were summarised as ORs, and continuous outcomes (length of hospital/ICU stay) were expressed as mean differences. For dichotomous data, the inverse variance method was used, and for continuous data, the combination method, Hedges’ g, was used.

The restricted maximum-likelihood estimator was used to derive between‐study variance (τ²). Meta-regression analyses were used to assess the effects of duration (as factor variables), and dose of steroids (as continuous variables). We also analysed duration by comparing short treatment duration (≤7 days) to long treatment duration (>7 days). For the dose of corticosteroid treatment, we compared >200 mg/day hydrocortisone-equivalent to ≤200 mg/day.

Appraisal of the evidence

We assessed the certainty of the evidence using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) domains: risk of bias, heterogeneity, imprecision, indirectness, and publication bias.34 This was done by JL and SH for each outcome.34

Two investigators (JL, HS) evaluated bias for each study using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool, with any discrepancies settled by a third investigator (SH). This tool assesses bias in five domains, and provides an overall grade. Risk of bias was only assessed as low for each study if all domains were deemed as low risk. A single domain listed as high risk meant that the overall risk of bias was deemed as high risk.

Heterogeneity was assessed using χ² (Cochrane’s Q) test and quantified by the I² statistic, with additional parameters (τ and H). We then categorised heterogeneity into four groups according to the Cochrane Collaboration definitions: 0–40% might not be important; 30–60% may represent moderate heterogeneity; 50–90% may represent substantial heterogeneity; and 75–100% considerable heterogeneity.35

Publication bias was assessed using Egger’s test and inspection of a funnel plot.

Sensitivity and subgroup analyses

Sensitivity analyses included leave‐one‐out diagnostics, influence analyses, and a separate analysis including only RCTs at low risk of bias.

We assessed subgroups for short-term mortality according to aetiology of respiratory infection: COVID-19, CAP and sepsis, ARDS, and septic shock.

Results

A total of twelve RCTs were included with 2324 patients in the low-dose corticosteroid treatment group and 2298 patients in the comparator group, giving a total of 4622 patients included in this meta-analysis.

Study selection

After screening a total of 282 records from Embase and Medline, twelve studies were selected for inclusion, as outlined in figure 1. During the full-text screening stage, 21 studies were excluded for various reasons, including mixed populations with varying severity of pulmonary infections, the use of corticosteroid doses exceeding 400 mg hydrocortisone-equivalent per day, and both groups taking corticosteroids (online supplemental table S2). Risk of bias and quality assessment

Out of twelve studies, six had low risk and five studies had some concerns,3037,40 the summary of which is shown in figure 2.41 One study had a high risk of bias due to a protocol deviation in Tomazini et al (2020) where 35.1% of the control group received the intervention.31 The summary of the GRADE quality assessment for all outcomes is shown in online supplemental table S3. Length of hospital stay and the number of patients with at least one SAE were rated as very low certainty. Short-term mortality, length of ICU stay, and short-term mortality in patients with CAP were rated as low certainty. This was mostly due to concerns about indirectness from two studies. Torres et al’s (2015) study involved recruiting patients across eight years with a 2007–2008 interruption, during which clinical practice may have changed.40 Bozzette et al’s (1990) study was conducted in the pre-Highly Active Antiretroviral Therapy era, during which baseline risk and ICU supportive care may have differed from current practice, limiting the applicability of these findings and downgrading the certainty of evidence for these outcomes.37

Primary outcome: short-term mortality (≤90 days)

A total of twelve studies involving 4581 participants were included. When comparing groups treated with corticosteroids versus placebo using a random-effects model, the pooled OR for mortality was 0.83 (24.95% vs 27.59%; 95% CI 0.70 to 0.98; p=0.029; I²=2.0%; NNT: 37.84; figure 3). When only including the six papers with low risk of bias, the OR was 0.85 (95% CI 0.69 to 1.06; p=0.15; I²=32.2%; online supplemental figure S1).

Heterogeneity was low and quantified with a τ² of 0.015 (τ=0.12) and I² of 2.0% (95% CI 0.0 to 59.2%). The χ² test yielded Q=11.23 (df=11, p=0.42). Leave-one-out diagnostics indicated that the findings were robust, with no single study unduly influencing the overall estimate (online supplemental figure S2). The Baujat plot demonstrates that no studies exerted undue influence on either heterogeneity or overall effect (online supplemental figure S3).

The funnel plot suggested absence of publication bias. However, Egger’s test for publication bias indicates potential plot asymmetry (–1.20 (95% CI –2.08 to –0.32), t=–2.661, p=0.024; online supplemental figure S4).

Cumulative meta-analysis demonstrated that the effect estimate stabilised over time as larger and more precise studies were added. The final cumulative estimate (OR: 0.83, 95% CI 0.70 to 0.98; online supplemental figure S5) showed lower heterogeneity (I² = 2.0%) than in earlier stages (I² up to 34%).

Secondary outcomes

28–30-day mortality

A total of six studies involving 1836 participants were included for 28–30 day mortality. When comparing groups treated with corticosteroids versus placebo, the pooled OR was 0.76 (95% CI 0.57 to 1.01; p=0.059; I²=0.0%; online supplemental figure S6). Heterogeneity was low and quantified with a τ² of 0.024 (τ=0.16). Three studies reporting only on patients with CAP showed a significant benefit in mortality with a pooled OR of 0.54 (76 (95% CI 0.35 to 0.85; p=0.0076; I²=0.0%; online supplemental figure S6)

Length of ICU stay

Three studies reported the ICU length of stay (n=1499), and only in patients with CAP. Our analysis found a pooled mean difference of –0.78 days (95% CI –1.46 to –0.10, p=0.025; online supplemental figure S7) showing that corticosteroid use had a significant reduction in ICU stay. There was moderate heterogeneity (I² = 57.8%).

Length of hospital stay

Five studies reported on hospital length of stay (n=1346), although one paper, Angus et al (2020), was excluded due to lack of reporting on the control group.30 The pooled mean difference was 0.95 days but was not significant (95% CI −2.58 to 4.47, p=0.60; online supplemental figure S8). The heterogeneity was very high (I² = 88.0%).

Number of patients with at least one serious adverse event

There were a total of 499 events recorded across four studies (n=1915). The pooled effect OR was 0.80 (95% CI 0.57 to 1.14, p=0.22; online supplemental figure S9), indicating no significant difference in risk of SAEs between corticosteroid and control groups. Heterogeneity was moderate (I² = 35.7%), and five papers were excluded due to inconsistencies in reporting on SAE.

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Aetiology

Analysis of the four COVID-19 trials showed no significant reduction in mortality (OR: 0.82, 95% CI 0.64 to 1.06, p=0.14; figure 3) and negligible heterogeneity (I² = 0.0%). Five trials focused on CAP and showed a statistically significant reduction in mortality (OR: 0.72; 95% CI 0.54 to 0.98; p=0.034; NNT: 28.10; figure 3) and negligible heterogeneity (I² = 0.0%). Three studies focused on sepsis, ARDS, and septic shock and showed no significant effect (OR: 0.97, 95% CI 0.81 to 1.17, p=0.77; online supplemental figure S10) with negligible heterogeneity (I² = 0.0%).

Steroid dose, type and duration

Meta-regression analysis on dose (mg/kg/day where possible) across ten studies (n=4068) showed no significant relationship between treatment dose and mortality effect size (regression coefficient: −0.0187; p=0.28), with low heterogeneity (I² = 10.25%). No significant difference was found in the eight studies using ≤200 mg/day of hydrocortisone-equivalent steroid (OR: 0.83; 95% CI 0.66 to 1.03; p=0.094; online supplemental figure S11), or for two papers using >200 mg/day (OR: 0.60; 95% CI 0.27 to 1.34; p=0.21; online supplemental figure S12).

Subgroup analysis found no significant effect of any specific steroid on mortality benefit. A summary of results for the effect of corticosteroid type in severe pulmonary infections can be found in online supplemental table S4.

Using meta-regression analysis across twelve trials (n=4681) with treatment duration specified as a factor variable, a short course of corticosteroids (≤7 days) was significantly associated with a higher log odds of mortality compared with longer courses (>7 days) (regression coefficient: 0.33, p=0.028), with negligible heterogeneity (I² = 0.0%).

In the seven trials where corticosteroid treatment was administered for ≤7 days, there was no significant benefit in short-term mortality (OR: 0.96; 95% CI 0.81 to 1.14; p=0.67; figure 4; online supplemental figure S13). However, in the five trials with longer treatment duration (>7 days), a significant benefit was found (OR: 0.69; 95% CI 0.53 to 0.89; p=0.039; figure 4; online supplemental figure S14), with significant subgroup differences (p=0.03).

Discussion

Summary of key findings and strengths

Our study showed a significant 17% reduction for the primary outcome of all-cause short-term mortality (≤90 days), with one life saved for every 38 patients treated. Certainty of evidence, as assessed by GRADE, was low for this outcome. A sensitivity analysis employing the leave-one-out method maintained the robustness of primary outcome results. Results also showed a significant reduction in length of ICU stay only in patients with CAP (mean of 0.78 days; low certainty), and no statistically significant impact on hospital duration or SAEs.

The overall mortality benefit should be interpreted with caution, however. Our subgroup analyses in patients with CAP showed a significant benefit in short-term mortality (OR: 0.72; p=0.034), but demonstrated no significant benefit in COVID-19, sepsis, ARDS, or septic shock cohorts.

Current guidelines for severe COVID-19 from the WHO recommend the use of systemic corticosteroids based on seven RCTs with a pooled mortality OR of 0.79 (95% CI 0.7 to 0.9).21 The result from our meta-analysis, including only four RCTs and suggesting a comparable (though non-significant) mortality reduction, is likely to differ due to differences in the inclusion criteria and lack of statistical power due to the smaller pooled sample. We did not include four of the papers included in the WHO analysis, specifically the Randomised Evaluation of COVID-19 Therapy (RECOVERY), GLUCOCOVID, DEXA-COVID19, and Steroids in Severe Acute Respiratory Infection (Steroids-SARI) trials.42,45 This was due to mixed populations of COVID-19 severity, high corticosteroid dose, and non-reporting of specific mortality results. Additionally, our meta-analysis includes a more recent paper.46 Many of the other excluded papers picked up in screening also address COVID-19 (online supplemental table S2). Of the nine papers, eight showed no significant benefit of steroids compared with no steroids. One study demonstrated a reduced mortality in the steroid group, but this was with the equivalent dose of 1250 mg hydrocortisone.

Meta-regression analyses examined dose as a potential moderator but found no significant effects (p=0.32). However, meta-regression analyses on treatment duration (>7 days vs ≤7 days) showed a significant association with mortality (regression coefficient: 0.33, p=0.028). This aligns with supplemental subgroup analyses where longer courses significantly reduced short-term mortality (OR: 0.69; 95% CI 0.53 to 0.89; p=0.039 vs OR: 0.96; 95% CI 0.813 to 1.14; p=0.67). This result must be approached with caution given the small number of longer-course RCTs (five) and variable tapering across studies.

Subgroup analysis of mortality by corticosteroid type did not find any significant differences. For hydrocortisone, there was significant between-study variation too (Q=11.66, df=5; p=0.04). Also, the evidence here for dexamethasone is only from two studies, limiting the reliability. Lastly, the prednisolone group had low heterogeneity, but a small sample size and low event rates could suggest imprecision.

Comparison with previous studies

Previous meta-analyses have individually explored the use of low-dose corticosteroids in CAP, COVID-19, PCP, ARDS, and sepsis.2847,50 This paper differs in that we pooled data from all relevant RCTs to evaluate the overall efficacy of low-dose corticosteroids across severe pulmonary infections. Moreover, previous meta-analyses pool data for both high and low-dose corticosteroids in their analysis which presents issues regarding the optimal dosage for clinical benefit.28 47 49 Our analysis incorporates the two most recent large-scale trials—Dequin et al (2023) and Meduri et al (2022).18 29

Our findings align with Smit et al’s (2025) meta-analysis on the use of adjuvant corticosteroids in CAP, which reported a reduction in 30-day mortality (OR: 0.72, 95% CI 0.56 to 0.94) compared to placebo.51 A substantial mortality benefit was observed only in patients with high baseline inflammation (C-reactive protein >204 mg). We demonstrated a similar benefit in the use of low-dose corticosteroids for severe CAP.

On the other hand, Briel et al’s (2018) meta-analysis found that adjunct corticosteroids in CAP led to a reduced hospital length of stay, but no significant reduction in 30-day mortality.52 This could be due to the inclusion of mixed severities of CAP, and averaging treatment effects across severities could dilute treatment effects seen only in severe subgroups.

Clinical implications

In this meta-analysis, we demonstrate that low-dose corticosteroids significantly reduced short-term mortality in the severe CAP cohort. Wu et al (2023) reported a risk ratio 0.61 (95% CI 0.44 to 0.85; p < 0.01) for 30-day all-cause mortality in severe CAP, which aligns closely with our finding of OR 0.83 (95% CI 0.70 to 0.98; p=0.029).28 However, the methodological differences with Wu et al (2023), which pooled data for both high-dose and low-dose corticosteroids, must be taken into consideration.28 An additional explanation for why the mortality benefit appears stronger in patients with CAP is that the treatment effect may differ by care setting. As we did not stratify patients depending on whether they were admitted to the ward or in the ICU, the markedly positive effect of corticosteroids in CAP could be inflated by ward-based cases, with evidence to suggest that corticosteroid regimens in ward settings may confer a greater clinical benefit as opposed to the ICU.53 Furthermore, different corticosteroids may have variable effects depending on the condition being treated. Hydrocortisone may prove to be more beneficial in CAP compared with other steroids due to its lower potency.54 Furthermore, hydrocortisone has significant mineralocorticoid activity compared with other corticosteroids, thereby regulating fluid balance and blood pressure—physiological effects that may be particularly relevant in the context of severe CAP.55 Therefore, future trials must endeavour to delineate the disease-specific profiles of different steroid types, as it may partly explain the apparent mortality benefit in CAP demonstrated in this paper, compared with other pulmonary infections.

The use of low-dose corticosteroids may confer a clinical advantage by offering an improved side effect profile compared with higher dosages, such as a reduced incidence of osteoporosis, infections, and cardiovascular complications.56 This study also found no increase in severe adverse events among the patients who took corticosteroids compared with controls, indicating it is an option for critically ill patients without the risk of causing significant harm.

Currently, guidelines from the European Respiratory Journal for the management of severe CAP suggest the use of corticosteroids (methylprednisolone 0.5 mg/kg every 12 hours for 5 days) only if shock is present, whereas findings from this paper suggest the adoption of routine low-dose corticosteroid therapy in severe CAP irrespective of whether shock is present or not.25 However, due to the lack of significant findings, this paper does not yet support any recommendation for the use of low-dose corticosteroids for other severe respiratory infections.

Limitations

The differences in the definition of ‘severe’ pulmonary infections may complicate the interpretation of pooled effects. In the severe CAP cohort, the difference in severity definitions includes the ATS criteria, Pulmonary Severity Index Class IV–V, and partial pressure of arterial oxygen/fractional inspired oxygen-based definitions. This not only increases heterogeneity in study populations as the definitions capture different populations, but it can also affect the pooled OR. However, without this leniency, imposing a post-hoc definition would have required excluding a substantial number of otherwise eligible high-quality studies, while also risking introducing selection bias, and thus we have gone with each system’s accepted definition of ‘severe’. Additionally, the lack of data on timings between hospital admission and initiation of the corticosteroid regimen adds another confounding variable, potentially explaining the difference in outcomes between the studies by Dequin et al (2020)57 and Meduri et al (2022)29.

Regarding the varied corticosteroid doses across the twelve included RCTs, all regimens fell below the predefined threshold of 400 mg hydrocortisone-equivalent per day. Within this range, corticosteroids exhibit a non-linear dose–response relationship, such that increases in dose produce diminishing additional anti-inflammatory effects, while adverse effects continue to rise.58 59 Consequently, moderate dose variation within this pharmacologically similar exposure range is unlikely to meaningfully alter the direction of the treatment effect. Rather, variability in treatment effect due to differences in dosage would be expected to increase inter-study variability, and bias pooled estimates toward the null.

During the risk of bias assessment, one high-risk study was identified (Tomazini et al (2020)).31 In this paper, 35.1% of the control group received the intervention, which may reduce the observed efficacy of the corticosteroid treatment. The leave-one-out analysis demonstrates that omitting this paper made no significant difference to the pooled OR for short-term mortality, where the OR was slightly reduced but the CI slightly increased.31

Future research directions

By including multiple severe pulmonary infections, we were able to assess whether corticosteroid effects were consistent across conditions with shared downstream inflammatory mechanisms. The finding that benefit was confined to severe CAP suggests important heterogeneity in treatment response, suggesting future studies should prioritise disease-specific investigation rather than routine aggregation of these populations. Other directions include directly comparing early versus delayed initiation of the regimen,60 extended versus short-course corticosteroid regimens,61 identifying the most effective choice of corticosteroid,25 and different tapering regimens.

Conclusions

This meta-analysis indicates that low-dose corticosteroids significantly reduce short-term mortality in adults with severe pulmonary infections, a result driven only by the severe CAP cohort. Longer courses (>7 days) of low-dose corticosteroids have an increased benefit on short-term mortality, but further investigation is required to confirm this finding. The significant decrease in mortality for severe CAP makes corticosteroids a promising cost-effective addition to standard treatment. This meta-analysis does not identify a single optimal corticosteroid dose. Rather, it supports the use of low-dose regimens (≤400 mg hydrocortisone-equivalent per day). Choice of agent, dose within this range, and duration should be individualised based on disease severity, comorbidities, and risk of adverse effects.
