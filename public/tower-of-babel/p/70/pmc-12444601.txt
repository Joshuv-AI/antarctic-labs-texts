Enhanced Carbon Sequestration Performance and Mechanism of Adsorption-Catalytic Bifunctional ZIF‑8 Coupled Microalgae
Authors: Luna Wang, Yan Gong, Yanming Miao, Zhifeng Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To support the development of a low-carbon economy and contribute to the achievement of carbon neutrality, this study synthesized bifunctional zeolitic imidazolate framework-8-based nanozymes (ZNZs) with dual functionality: CO2 adsorption and carbonic anhydrase (CA)-mimetic catalytic activity. The influence of ZNZs at varying concentrations on the growth rate of microalgae, chlorophyll and carbohydrate content, and ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase (Rubisco) and CA activity were explored. Results indicated that after the addition of 5 mg/L ZNZs, the growth rate of microalgae increased by 7%. Furthermore, the maximum chlorophyll and carbohydrate contents reached 4.55 and 43.59 mg/L, respectively, which were 12.6 and 4.2% higher than those in the control group. Additionally, Rubisco and CA activity (intra- and extracellular) increased by up to 21.26, 10, and 8.3%, respectively. These findings demonstrate that ZNZs enhance the photosynthetic efficiency of microalgae and increase microalgae’s affinity for CO2, highlighting their promise as a strategy for improving carbon capture and sustainable bioenergy production.

Introduction

Fossil fuels, including coal, oil, and natural gas, remain the primary sources of anthropogenic carbon emissions. In response to escalating global initiatives to mitigate climate change and the establishment of carbon neutrality targets, carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) technologies have gained prominence as essential strategies for reducing CO2 emissions from fossil-based energy sources. Microalgae have attracted considerable interest as efficient carbon-fixing organisms because of their high photosynthetic efficiency, resilience to environmental stress, and ability to utilize flue gases and wastewater as nutrient sources. They can be cultivated on marginal lands, such as saline–alkali soils and tidal flats, and can convert captured CO2 into valuable biomass, which serves as a feedstock for biofuels, animal feed, and nutraceutical products. Consequently, microalga-based CCUS offers a dual benefit of effective carbon fixation and resource utilization, representing a promising pathway toward the realization of a low-carbon economy and carbon neutrality. ,

Although microalga-based CCUS represents a promising strategy for carbon emission reduction, its practical implementation faces several challenges. The chemical properties of the microalgal culture medium and the dissolution characteristics of CO2 indicate low mass transfer efficiency from the gas-phase CO2 to the liquid phase (low equilibrium rate between CO2 and HCO3 –). , CO2 has a short residence time in the aqueous phase, and the affinity of ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase (Rubisco), which is the primary CO2-fixing enzyme in microalgae, for CO2 is inherently low. − Thus, the overall absorption and fixation efficiency of CO2 is low. Integrating CO2 adsorption materials into microalgal systems offers a viable approach to enhance carbon fixation efficiency, particularly at high CO2 concentrations (>20%), while minimizing carbon source overflow. This strategy holds considerable potential for applications in waste gas treatment and the utilization of microalgal biomass products.

Metal–organic framework materials (MOFs) can electrostatically self-assemble on microalgal cell surfaces, functioning as extracellular CO2 concentrators. When coupled with extracellular carbonic anhydrase (CA), these MOFs enhance the bioavailability and uptake of gaseous CO2 by microalgae. Enhanced Rubisco enzyme activity increases the carbon fixation efficiency of microalgae 1.9 times and considerably improves ability of microalgae to resist photoinhibition. Zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8), composed of Zn2+ metal centers coordinated with 2-methylimidazole linkers, forms a porous crystalline structure with tunable properties. Owing to its facile synthesis, adjustable pore size, low cytotoxicity, and excellent biocompatibility, ZIF-8 is often used as an efficient adsorbent for CO2. It can effectively capture CO2 within its porous network and convert it into dissolved inorganic carbon species, such as bicarbonate (HCO3 –), through physical and chemical adsorption mechanisms, thereby providing a bioavailable carbon source for microalgae, such as Chlorella. ZIF-8 possesses a zeolite-like sodalite (SOD) topology, featuring a robust structure with large cages (diameter of ∼11.6 Å) that are interconnected via narrow 6-ring windows (∼3.4 Å). This size range is ideal for CO2 (kinetic diameter ∼ 3.3 Å), allowing efficient entry without causing a significant decrease in selectivity. This structural characteristic enables ZIF-8 to create localized high-CO2 microenvironments in the algal solution, overcoming the resistance to gas–liquid mass transfer.

Compared to other metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) such as UiO-66 and MIL-101, ZIF-8 enables direct nucleation on algal surfaces through functional groups (−COOH/–NH2) under ambient aqueous conditions, eliminating the need for harsh conditions like high temperature or organic solvents. , The released Zn2+ icons acts as a cofactor for carbonic anhydrase, enhancing photosynthesis without inducing oxidative stress, unlike Fe3+-induced Fenton reactions. Additionally, the porous structure of ZIF-8 interacts synergistically with algal enzymatic systems to achieve integrated “adsorption–degradation” of pollutants, whereas other MOFs (Zr-based) often show single functionality or suppressed algal activity due to pore-size limitations or metal toxicity, such as from Cu2+. This unique biocompatibility and functional synergy make the ZIF-8-algae system more advantageous for environmental remediation and biomanufacturing applications. The ability of ZIF-8 to exhibit carbonic anhydrase (CA)-like activity primarily arises from the structural similarity between its framework and the active site of CA, as well as the catalytic role of zinc ions. The active site of natural CA contains zinc ions (Zn2+) coordinated with histidine residues, which activate water molecules to form a nucleophilic hydroxide (OH–), thereby catalyzing the CO2 hydration reaction. In ZIF-8, Zn2+ ions coordinate with imidazole ligands (analogous to histidine in CA), creating a similar coordination environment, thereby mimicking the active site of CA. This enables ZIF-8 to directly catalyze the CO2 hydration reaction without requiring additional modifications. Functional groups significantly enhance the CA activity of ZIF-8, with modifications further boosting its catalytic performance. Furthermore, ZIF-8 modified with hydrophobic valine exhibits CA-like catalytic activity and remarkable hydrothermal stability and potentially purifies high-temperature flue gas. Given the dual advantages of CO2 adsorption efficiency and enzyme-mimetic activity, the integration of such materials with microalgal systems warrants further investigation. This approach may facilitate the development of novel integrated CO2 capture and utilization platforms.

The objective of this study is to synthesize bifunctional ZIF-8-based nanozymes (ZNZs) with high biocompatibility, exhibiting efficient CO2 adsorption capacity and CA-like catalytic activity. The other objectives are to elucidate the interaction mechanisms between ZNZs and microalgae and to evaluate the potential of ZNZ–microalgae coupling in enhancing carbon fixation efficiency. By exploring this hybrid system, the study provides a technical foundation for the integration of CO2 capture and utilization with advanced materials in combination with biological systems. The findings have important implications across multiple domains, including environmental protection, ecological restoration, and sustainable clean energy development.

Material and Methods

Chemicals Reagent

2-Methylimidazole (>98%) was obtained from Aladdin Industrial Corporation (Shanghai, China). Zn­(NO3)2·6H2O (≥99.0%) was purchased from Cominno Chemical Reagent Co. (Tianjin, China). Methanol (≥99.5%) was supplied by Beichen Fangzheng Reagent Factory (Tianjin, China). The microalga species Chlorella vulgaris (FACHB-5) was acquired from the Freshwater Algae Culture Collection of the Chinese Academy of Sciences and cultured in BG11 medium obtained from Haibo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shandong, China). Rubisco and CA detection kits were purchased from Solarbio.

Synthesis and Characterization of ZNZs

The ZNZs were synthesized following a previously reported method. Briefly, Zn­(NO3)2·6H2O (1.5 g) and C4H6N2 (3.3 g) were completely dissolved in 70 mL of methanol. Then, 2-methylimidazole solution (70 mL) was slowly added to the zinc nitrate solution (70 mL). The reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature. The resulting white product was collected through centrifugation at 4000 rpm, washed three times with methanol, and finally dried under vacuum at 80 °C overnight.

The structural and morphological characteristics of the synthesized ZNZs and C. vulgaris cells were analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM; Zeiss SIGMA300). The crystalline structure of the ZNZs was determined through X-ray diffraction (XRD) using an XPert Pro MPD diffractometer. Specific surface area, pore size distribution, and nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms were measured with a surface area and porosity analyzer (Micromeritics ASAP 2460). An FTIR system (Thermo Nicolet iS5) was employed to identify the functional groups present in the ZNZs. The elemental composition and surface chemical states were analyzed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS; Thermo Fisher Nexsa).

The CA-like activity of the ZNZs was assessed using the esterase assay. In this method, the ZNZs catalyzed the hydrolysis of p-NPA (substrate) into p-NP, which exhibits a characteristic absorbance peak at 405 nm. A reaction mixture without ZNZs served as the blank control. The increase in absorbance at 405 nm over time was monitored using a UV–vis spectrophotometer, and the catalytic activity of the ZNZs was measured. The reaction system consisted of 26.7 mL of Tris-HCl buffer solution (0.1 mol/L, 26.7 mL, pH = 8.4), 3 mL of ZNZ solution (0.7 mmol/L, DMF as the solvent), and 0.3 mL of p-NPA solution (50 mmol/L, dissolved in acetonitrile). Absorbance measurements were recorded at 405 nm at predetermined time intervals of 1, 3, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 min at room temperature. The concentration of p-NP generated was calculated based on the p-NPA absorbance–concentration standard curve (Figure S1 in the Supporting Information). Catalytic activity was measured using the following equation 1 yt=1.497xt+0.0049,⁣R2=0.9971 where y t is the concentration of p-NP at time t, mmol/L; x t is the absorbance value of p-NP at time t.

Microalga Cultivation and ZNZs Addition

C. vulgaris cells in the exponential growth phase were inoculated into sterile BG11 medium at an initial cell density of 1.5 × 106 cells/mL. ZNZs were added to the culture medium at final concentrations of 0 (control), 5, and 10 mg/L (these concentration values were determined based on the results of preliminary experiments. Figure S7 in the Supporting Information). Each treatment was conducted in triplicate. Cultivation was carried out in a constant-temperature shaking incubator (HZQ-211, Yiheng, China) under the following controlled conditions: temperature of 25 °C, agitation at 150 rpm, light intensity of 4000 Lux, and a 12 h/12 h light–dark photoperiod.

Microalgal Growth

The growth curve of C. vulgaris was established on the basis of the relationship between cell density (1 × 106 cells/mL) and optical absorbance. Cell density was quantified using a hemocytometer under an optical microscope, while absorbance measurements were taken at 680 nm with a UV–vis spectrophotometer. The absorbance values were then converted to cell density by using a standard calibration curve (Figure S2 in Supporting Information). All experiments were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

Analysis of Photosynthesis

The maximum quantum yield of photosynthesis (F v/F m) was used in evaluating the photosynthetic activity of photosystem II (PSII). A chlorophyll fluorometer (AP110-C, AquaPen, Brno, Czech Republic) was employed to measure the photosynthetic parameters of C. vulgaris. Prior to measurement, algal samples were dark-adapted for 20 min for the activation of PSII reaction centers and oxidation of the electron transport chain. Upon exposure to a saturating pulse of light, the minimum fluorescence (F 0) and maximum fluorescence (F m) were recorded. The maximum quantum yield of PSII was then calculated using the following equation 2 Fv/Fm=(Fm−F0)/Fm

Intracellular Components

Pigments

Chlorophyll content was determined using a colorimetric method. A 5 mL aliquot of algal culture was centrifuged at 6500 rpm for 6 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was discarded. The resulting algal pellet was resuspended in 5 mL of methanol and incubated until the cells were completely decolorized (i.e., turned white). The mixture was then centrifuged again under the same conditions, and the supernatant was collected for pigment analysis. Absorbance readings at 653 and 666 nm were recorded using a UV–vis spectrophotometer (UV-5500, Metash, China). The concentrations of chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b were calculated using the following equations 3 chlorophylla(mg/L)=15.65OD666−7.34OD653 4 chlorophyllb(mg/L)=27.05OD653−11.21OD666

Proteins

Intracellular protein content was determined using a cell disruption method followed by colorimetric analysis. A 5 mL aliquot of the microalgal suspension was transferred into a centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 5500 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the resulting pellet was resuspended in 5 mL of PBS (pH 7.8) buffer solution. Ultrasonic disruption was performed in an ice–water bath for 15 min (25 kHz, 200 W, 5 s ultrasonic time, 10 s interval time). The homogenate was then centrifuged at 5500 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected for protein analysis. The protein extract was mixed with Coomassie Brilliant Blue dye, and absorbance was measured at 595 nm. The protein content was determined by calculating the sample concentration based on a standard curve (Figure S3 in Supporting Information) constructed using BSA as the reference standard.

Carbohydrates

Carbohydrate content was determined using the anthrone colorimetric method. A 5 mL aliquot of algal suspension was centrifuged and washed with deionized water, and the culture medium was removed. After the supernatant was discarded, 5 mL of distilled water was added to the algal pellet, and the mixture was heated in boiling water for 20 min carbohydrate extraction. The extract was then centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min, and the residue was removed. Then, 1 mL of the extract was mixed with 5 mL of the anthrone solution (prepared by dissolving 0.1 g of anthrone powder in 100 mL of 80% H2SO4). The mixture was reacted in boiling water for 10 min, and the absorbance was measured at 620 nm. Lastly, carbohydrate content was calculated using a standard calibration curve (Figure S4 in Supporting Information).

Cell Permeability

A 5 mL aliquot of Chlorella suspension was centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was collected, and absorbance values were measured at 260 and 280 nm with a UV–vis spectrophotometer. Variations in absorbance at these wavelengths were used to represent relative changes in nucleic acid and protein concentrations, serving as indicators of microalgal cell membrane permeability.

Enzyme Activity

Rubisco Activity

Rubisco activity was determined using a commercially available Rubisco activity detection kit (Solarbio, China). In this assay, Rubisco catalyzes the reaction between ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate and CO2 to produce 3-phosphoglyceric acid (PGA). PGA is subsequently converted to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate through the enzymatic action of externally supplied 3-phosphoglycerate kinase and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase, during which NADH is oxidized to NAD+. Algal cells were initially collected into centrifuge tubes, centrifuged, and the supernatant was discarded. Extraction buffer was then added at a ratio of 1 mL per 5 million cells. The cells were disrupted by ultrasonic treatment for 15 min (25 kHz, 200 W, 5 s ultrasonic time, 10 s interval time). The lysate was then centrifuged at 10,000g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected for analysis. Rubisco activity was assessed by measuring the absorbance at 340 nm before and after 5 min of reaction. 5 Rubiscoactivity(U/mgprot)=344×ΔA÷Cpr where ΔA = ΔA measurement – ΔA control; Cpr is protein concentration (mg/mL).

CA Activity

The intra- and extracellular CA activity of ZNZs in Chlorella cells were quantified using an esterase-based ultraviolet spectrophotometric method. Supernatants (0.1 mL) collected from centrifuged Chlorella suspensions at varying concentrations, or lysates obtained through the ultrasonic disruption of algal cell pellets, were used for analysis. The control group was 0.1 mL PBS (pH 7.8) buffer solution. Each sample was mixed with 0.7 mL of Tris buffer (50 mM, pH 7.5) and 0.2 mL of p-NPA aqueous solution in a quartz cuvette. The mixture was incubated in a 37 °C water bath, and absorbance at 405 nm was recorded before and after 5 min of reaction. CA activity was calculated on the basis of protein concentration.

Results and Discussion

Characterization Result

The morphological characteristics of the ZNZs were observed through SEM. As shown in Figure a, the ZNZ particles exhibited a regular dodecahedral structure with smooth surfaces and uniform particle size. The average particle diameter was approximately 90 nm. Figure b illustrates the interaction between ZNZs and C. vulgaris cells. Upon the addition of 5 mg/L ZNZ suspension, slight shrinkage of the algal cells was observed, and the ZNZ particles were visibly adsorbed onto the cell surfaces, indicating effective coupling between the nanozymes and microalgae.

Scanning electron microscopy images: (a) ZNZs; (b) the coupling of ZNZs and microalgae.

As shown in Figure a, XRD analysis confirmed that the synthesized ZNZs exhibited characteristic diffraction peaks consistent with the simulated ZNZ spectrum (CCDC no. 1429243). Distinct peaks were observed at 2θ values of 7.4°, 10.5°, 12.8°, 14.8°, 16.6°, 18.1°, 24.6°, and 26.7°, which corresponded to the (110), (200), (211), (220), (310), (222), (332), and (431) crystal planes, respectively. These results are in agreement with previously reported data, confirming the successful synthesis of the ZNZs. The nitrogen adsorption–desorption curve (Figure b) displayed a typical type I profile, indicative of microporous materials. The BET surface area was 1800.6 m2/g, and the BJH model determined the pore volume and average pore diameter to be 0.837 cm3/g, demonstrating the material’s high porosity and abundance of adsorption sites. The FTIR spectrum (Figure c) revealed characteristic absorption peaks. The band at 3138 cm–1 was attributed to the C–H stretching vibrations of the aromatic imidazole ring. The peak at 2934 cm–1 was assigned to the asymmetric stretching of saturated hydrocarbon C–H (CH2), and the peak at 1587 cm–1 corresponded to CN stretching within the imidazole ring. The presence of this characteristic vibrational peak further corroborates the structural integrity and distinctiveness of the imidazole ring. The peak at 1150 cm–1 belonged to the stretching vibration of aromatic C–N. The in-plane bending mode of the imidazole ring and the aromatic bending mode of sp2 C–H are assigned to the signals at 994 and 760 cm–1, respectively. The peak at 693 cm–1 is attributed to the out-of-plane bending vibration of the 2-methylimidazole ring. The characteristic absorption peak observed at 421 cm–1 corresponds to the stretching vibration of Zn–N coordination bonds within the ZIF-8 crystal framework. The prominent intensity of this peak strongly confirms the successful formation of stable chemical bonds between zinc ions and the nitrogen atoms of 2-methylimidazole (Hmim) ligands. The FTIR spectrum of ZNZs@Chlorella (Figure S5 in Supporting Information) exhibits the persistence of characteristic peaks at 1138 cm–1 (C–N stretching vibration), 994 cm–1 (in-plane bending of imidazole rings), and 420 cm–1 (Zn–N stretching vibration), confirming that ZNZs maintain structural stability in the Chlorella system.

Characterization of ZNZs: (a) XRD patterns; (b) BET nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherm; (c) FTIR spectrum; (d) XPS survey spectrum; (e) high-resolution XPS of C 1s; (f) N 1s; (g) O 1s; (h) Zn 2p. Plot of zeta potential of ZNZs and C. vulgaris (i).

XPS analysis further characterized the elemental composition and chemical states of the ZNZs (Figure d–h). The survey scan spectrum (Figure d) identified the primary elements present. In the high-resolution C 1s spectrum (Figure e), peaks at binding energies of 285.9 and 284.8 eV were assigned to C–O/C–N and C–H/C–C, respectively. This assignment is consistent with the molecular structure of ZIF-8, where C–O/C–N bonds originate from the functional groups of the imidazole ligands, indicating the coordination environment with Zn2+. This is consistent with previous reports on pristine MOF materials. The N 1s spectrum (Figure f) showed peaks at 399.4 and 398.8 eV, corresponding to NC and N–Zn. The peak at 399.4 eV corresponds to the tertiary nitrogen atom in the imidazole ring, which coordinates with the Zn2+ metal center to form the tetrahedral structure unique to ZIF-8. The signal at 398.8 eV is associated with the secondary nitrogen atom in the imidazole ring, playing a key role in enhancing the aromaticity and stability of the ligand framework. The O 1s spectrum (Figure g) indicated the presence of CO (532.2 eV) and C–O (531.5 eV) species. The CO bond likely originates from surface-adsorbed carbon dioxide or partial oxidation of imidazole ligands during synthesis or sample processing. In contrast, the C–O bond may be attributed to hydroxyl groups on the MOF surface or adsorbed water molecules. In the Zn 2p spectrum (Figure h), the peaks at 1044.7 and 1021.7 eV were attributed to Zn 2p1/2 and Zn 2p3/2, respectively. The difference between the two bands is 23.1 eV, which indicates the presence of Zn2+. The catalytic performance of the synthesized ZNZs is presented in Figure S6 of Supporting Information, confirming their high catalytic activity.

ZNZ Interaction with the Surface of C. vulgaris

The Figure i shows the zeta potential results under different pH values. ZNZs exhibit a positive charge within the pH range of 7–9, while the cell wall of Chlorella carries a negative charge due to its high content of carboxyl and phosphate groups. Consequently, the electrostatic attraction between the oppositely charged particles drives the initial interaction and binding between ZNZ and Chlorella. Furthermore, FTIR results (Figure S5 in Supporting Information) further confirm that ZNZs form a stable binding through coordination with the functional groups of the Chlorella cell wall (as indicated by the characteristic peak at 1650 cm–1).

Microalgal Growth and Photosynthesis Efficiency Profile

The effect of ZNZs at varying concentrations on the cell density of C. vulgaris is presented in Figure a. Throughout the cultivation period, cell density exhibited a consistent upward trend across all treatment groups. Notably, the groups exposed to ZNZs demonstrated higher cell densities than the control group (CK), indicating a growth-promoting effect. The treatment with 5 mg/L ZNZs yielded the highest cell density, reaching 11.64 × 106 cells/mL on the 12th day, which represents a 7% increase relative to the control. These results suggest that low concentrations of ZNZs can enhance microalgal growth. In contrast, the 10 mg/L ZNZs treatment group showed reduced cell density, likely due to the excessive release of Zn2+ ions. This elevated Zn2+ level inhibited the growth of C. vulgaris by disrupting cell division and proliferation processes. This phenomenon is consistent with previous research findings. ,

Effect of ZNZ concentration on (a) the growth curve of C. vulgaris and (b) the photosynthetic efficiency (F v/F m) of C. vulgaris.

As shown in Figure b, the maximum quantum yield of photosystem II (F v/F m) in C. vulgaris varied under different ZNZ concentrations throughout the cultivation period. The F v/F m values in the 5 mg/L ZNZ group consistently exceeded those of the CK, indicating enhanced photosynthetic activity. However, on the sixth day, the F v/F m value in the 10 mg/L group fell below that of the CK. This reduction may be attributed to excessive ZNZ adsorption on the algal cell surfaces at high concentrations, potentially leading to a shielding effect that hinders light penetration. By the final day of cultivation, the F v/F m value in the 5 mg/L ZNZ group reached 0.641, representing a 4.7% increase compared with the CK. This result further supports that low concentrations of ZNZs promote C. vulgaris growth and photosynthesis, whereas high concentrations may exert an inhibitory effect.

Changes in Intracellular Composition

Pigment Production

In the photosynthetic system, light-absorbing pigments absorbed incident light energy and transferred it to chlorophyll a at the reaction center, where the excitation of electrons initiated the photosynthetic electron transport chain. As illustrated in Figure a, the chlorophyll a content in C. vulgaris cells cocultured with 5 mg/L ZNZs reached 4.55 mg/L, representing a 12.6% increase compared with the CK. This enhancement can be attributed to the CO2-adsorbing properties of the ZNZs, which utilize their unsaturated metal active sites to capture CO2 and facilitate its conversion into HCO3 –. The elevated concentration of dissolved inorganic carbon (primarily in the form of HCO3 –) supports algal growth and stimulates chlorophyll biosynthesis. As a result, the increased pigment concentration contributes to improved photosynthetic performance in the microalgal cells. In addition, zinc ions in ZIF-8 are essential micronutrients for the growth and photosynthesis of Chlorella. Zinc is involved in the activity of several enzymes, including carbonic anhydrase, which plays a key role in CO2 fixation. Low concentrations of ZIF-8 release zinc ions that enhance chlorophyll synthesis. In contrast, high concentrations of ZIF-8 may inhibit chlorophyll synthesis and photosynthesis by inducing oxidative stress, disrupting the photosynthetic system, and damage to cellular structures. This finding further confirms the previous conclusion that the concentration of MOFs significantly influences cell development.

Effects of ZNZs at different concentrations on (a) chlorophyll a content, (b) chlorophyll b content, and (c) carbohydrates content in C. vulgaris.

Carbohydrate Content

As shown in Figure c, the carbohydrate content in C. vulgaris exhibited a dynamic pattern on the fourth, eighth, and 12th days of cultivation, initially decreasing and subsequently increasing. On the 12th day, the carbohydrate content in the treatment group with 5 mg/L ZNZs reached 43.59 mg/L. This fluctuation may be attributed to physiological and metabolic adjustments in the microalgae. During the early phase of cultivation, the rate of carbohydrate synthesis likely declined, while stored carbohydrates were actively consumed to support rapid cell proliferation and other vital metabolic processes. As the growth rate decelerated in the later phase, energy demands lessened, allowing the accumulation of carbohydrates within the cells. The excessive release of Zn2+ from 10 mg/L ZNZs may increase intracellular oxidative stress levels, thereby disrupting the activity of CA. CA plays a crucial role in the interconversion between CO2 and HCO3 –, and its impaired activity can weaken the CCM in C. vulgaris, subsequently inhibiting photosynthesis and carbohydrate synthesis.

Influence of ZNZs on Cell Permeability

Changes in cell membrane permeability influence the leakage of intracellular substances in C. vulgaris. When membrane integrity is compromised, cellular components such as proteins and nucleic acids can diffuse into the surrounding medium. These permeability changes can be quantitatively assessed by monitoring the release of intracellular proteins and nucleic acids, which exhibit characteristic absorbance peaks at 280 and 260 nm, respectively. As illustrated in Figure , on the eighth day of cultivation, the CK exhibited a 7.9% higher concentration of leaked nucleic acids and a 7.0% higher concentration of leaked proteins compared to the treatment group exposed to 5 mg/L ZNZs. This increase suggests a higher degree of membrane disruption and cellular apoptosis in the CK during this phase, resulting in the release of intracellular contents into the extracellular environment.

Effects of ZNZs on the Enzyme Activity of Microalgae

Rubisco Activity

Rubisco is a critical enzyme in photosynthesis, and its activity is directly linked to the photosynthetic rate. The influence of ZNZs on Rubisco activity was detected, serving as a basis for exploring the effect of ZNZs on the photosynthesis of C. vulgaris. As shown in Figure a, Rubisco activity in the CK was 10.96 U/mg protein, whereas a considerable increase to 13.29 U/mg protein was observed in the group treated with 5 mg/L ZNZs, representing a 21.26% enhancement compared with the control. This result suggests that ZNZs at an optimal concentration can promote photosynthetic activity in C. vulgaris. However, at a higher ZNZ concentration of 10 mg/L, Rubisco activity declined relative to the 5 mg/L treatment, indicating an inhibitory effect on the photosynthetic system at elevated ZNZ levels.

Effects of ZNZs at different concentrations on the (a) Rubisco activity, (b) intracellular CA activity, and (c) extracellular CA activity of the microalgae.

CA Activity

CA plays a vital role in the carbon-concentrating mechanism (CCM) of microalgae, catalyzing the reversible conversion of CO2 and HCO3 –. CA can act both extracellularly: outside the cell membrane or within the cell wall by accelerating the conversion of CO2 into HCO3 – for uptake and intracellularly within the chloroplast and mitochondria or outside the cytoplasm by converting HCO3 – back into CO2 for fixation by Rubisco in the Calvin cycle. As shown in Figure b, intracellular CA activity in the group treated with 5 mg/L ZNZs reached 0.099 U/mg protein, representing a 10% increase compared with the CK (0.09 U/mg protein). Similarly, Figure c shows that extracellular CA activity increased from 0.096 U/mg protein in the CK to 0.104 U/mg protein with ZNZ treatment, showing an 8.3% enhancement. These results indicate that ZNZs, as MOF materials, likely self-assemble on the surface of C. vulgaris through electrostatic and coordination interactions, enabling localized CO2 capture and enrichment. This enhanced CO2 availability supports improved photosynthetic performance by supplying additional inorganic carbon to the microalgal cells.

Conclusion

This study investigated the growth-promoting effects of bifunctional ZNZs, which possess CO2 adsorption capacity and CA-like catalytic activity, on C. vulgaris. The experimental findings demonstrated that the addition of 5 mg/L ZNZs enhanced microalgal growth and photosynthetic efficiency by 7 and 4.7%, respectively. Intracellular chlorophyll a and carbohydrate content exhibited considerable increases, reaching maximum concentrations of 4.55 and 43.59 mg/L, respectively, during the cultivation period. Meanwhile, Rubisco activity increased by 21.26%, whereas intracellular and extracellular CA activity increased by 10 and 8.3%, respectively. These improvements were attributed to the unsaturated sites of ZNZs, which effectively adsorbed CO2 and facilitated its conversion to bioavailable inorganic carbon, enhancing the photosynthetic capacity of C. vulgaris. This coupling strategy presents a promising approach for integrating CCUS technologies with microalgal cultivation, offering potential applications in environmental remediation, sustainable biomass production, and clean energy development. Future research will aim to further elucidate the regulatory mechanisms underlying ZNZ enhanced photosynthesis and biomass accumulation through integrated multiomics approaches, including metabolomics, transcriptomics, GO functional annotation, metabolic pathway enrichment analysis, and molecular interaction analysis.
