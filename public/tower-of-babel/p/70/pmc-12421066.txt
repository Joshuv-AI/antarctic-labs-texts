Acute Post‐Streptococcal Glomerulonephritis in a 22‐Month‐Old Toddler: A Rare and Uncustomary Age of Onset
Authors: Abrham Bire Akeberegn, Amanuel Anegagregn Bizuneh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAcute post‐streptococcal glomerulonephritis (APSGN) is an important immunological complication that follows Group A Streptococcus (GAS) infections. It usually affects children between 5 and 15 years of age and is rarely seen in children younger than 3 years, likely because their immature immune systems limit the exaggerated immune response. The condition typically presents with hematuria, edema, hypertension, and renal insufficiency. Diagnosis is supported by elevated antistreptolysin O (ASO) titers, low complement levels, and a recent history of GAS infection. Although the prognosis in children is generally good, APSGN continues to be a significant concern in regions where GAS is endemic. A 22‐month‐old girl, previously healthy, developed periorbital swelling, generalized edema, and tea‐colored urine after a recent upper respiratory tract infection. Laboratory tests showed proteinuria, hematuria, reactive ASO titers, and mild anemia, while imaging studies were unremarkable. Based on clinical and laboratory findings, APSGN was diagnosed. She was managed with furosemide, nifedipine, and ceftriaxone. Her condition improved markedly, with complete resolution of hematuria and proteinuria within 2 months. ASO titers returned to normal, and renal function was fully restored. This case demonstrates the uncommon occurrence of APSGN in children under 3 years of age and stresses the importance of including it in the differential diagnosis of nephritic syndrome in toddlers. The excellent outcome in this patient reflects the generally favorable prognosis of APSGN in pediatric populations. Early recognition and treatment remain essential, as GAS infections in infants, though less common, can still lead to post‐infectious complications such as APSGN.Keywords: APSGN, group a streptococcus, nephritic syndrome, pediatrics

Introduction

Acute post‐streptococcal glomerulonephritis (APSGN) is a significant immunological complication of infections caused by group A streptococci (GAS), primarily affecting the kidneys (Figure 1). It develops as a delayed sequela following pharyngitis or impetigo caused by nephritogenic strains of GAS, with a global incidence of approximately 470,000 cases annually and a mortality rate of 5000 deaths (1, 2, 16). APSGN predominantly affects children aged 5 to 15 years and is responsible for around 90% of post‐infectious glomerulonephritis [1]. However, it is rare in children below 3 years due to the immaturity of their immune systems, which likely reduces the ability to mount the exaggerated immune response characteristic of APSGN [2, 3].

The pathophysiology involves the deposition of immune complexes in the glomeruli, leading to inflammation and subsequent damage [4]. Clinical manifestations typically include gross hematuria (described as tea‐ or cola‐colored urine), edema (often periorbital), hypertension, and varying degrees of renal insufficiency [3, 4, 5].

Laboratory examination in cases of Acute Post‐Streptococcal Glomerulonephritis (APSGN) typically reveals mild anemia, proteinuria (less than 3 g/day), elevated blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine levels, an elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and low C3 complement levels [4, 5] Evidence of a recent Group A Streptococcus (GAS) infection, such as isolating the bacteria from the throat or skin lesions and elevated streptococcal antibodies, helps differentiate APSGN from other causes of acute glomerulonephritis (AGN) [5]. Treatment generally focuses on controlling hypertension and edema, while antibiotics are used to prevent the spread of nephritogenic strains to others [6]. Despite the potential for a severe presentation, the prognosis for children with APSGN is excellent, with over 90% recovering fully without long‐term renal impairment [2, 4]. Additionally, laboratory findings such as proteinuria, low C3 complement levels, and elevated antistreptolysin O (ASO) titers further confirm the diagnosis, underscoring the typically favorable outcome for pediatric patients [4].

In areas where GAS is endemic, APSGN remains a significant public health concern. Efforts to prevent GAS infections and early recognition of APSGN can significantly reduce morbidity and mortality [7].

Case History/Examination

A 22‐month‐old previously healthy girl presented with a 3‐day history of periorbital swelling, generalized body swelling, and tea‐colored urine. Her medical history was unremarkable, with no prior episodes of hematuria or family history of renal or visual abnormalities. Three weeks prior, she experienced symptoms of an upper respiratory tract infection (URTI).

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

Differential Diagnosis

Acute post‐streptococcal glomerulonephritis (APSGN): Characterized by hematuria, proteinuria, edema, and hypertension following a streptococcal infection. Diagnosis is supported by a history of preceding pharyngitis or skin infection, reactive ASO titers, and low complement levels [4, 8].

IgA nephropathy: Presents with recurrent episodes of gross hematuria, often following an upper respiratory tract infection. Unlike APSGN, complement levels are typically normal in IgA nephropathy [9].

Membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis (MPGN): causes persistent proteinuria and hematuria, often with low complement levels. MPGN can be distinguished from APSGN by its more prolonged course and histological findings on renal biopsy [10].

Hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS): Frequently follows a diarrheal illness caused by Shiga toxin‐producing E. coli . HUS is marked by the triad of hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury, which are not features of APSGN [11].

Lupus nephritis: Should be considered in patients with hematuria and proteinuria. A positive antinuclear antibody (ANA) test and other lupus‐specific serologies can help differentiate it from APSGN [12].

Treatment

The patient was managed with furosemide, nifedipine, and ceftriaxone. Blood pressure, renal function, and urine output were monitored serially. Edema resolved completely within a week, and the patient was discharged.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

The patient demonstrated significant clinical improvement over subsequent follow‐ups. Two months post‐treatment, hematuria and proteinuria resolved, ASO titers remained reactive, and serum creatinine decreased to 0.7 mg/dL. Complete blood count (CBC) returned to normal within 1 month and 2 weeks, while blood pressure stabilized after 1 month, leading to the cessation of antihypertensive medications. Follow‐up at the ninth month revealed no proteinuria or hematuria, along with normal blood pressure for age. Moreover, serum creatinine levels normalized to 0.6 g/L, and ASO titers became negative. No recurrence of hematuria was detected during follow‐up.

This case highlights the excellent prognosis of APSGN in children and underscores the importance of considering APSGN in toddlers presenting with nephritic syndrome, as the patient showed complete resolution of symptoms, including the normalization of laboratory findings, and no recurrence at six and 9 months post‐treatment.

Discussion

This case of Acute Post‐Streptococcal Glomerulonephritis (APSGN) in a 22‐month‐old girl describes a rare condition in children under 3 years of age. The low incidence in this age group is largely due to immature immune responses and the specific disease patterns of Group A Streptococcus (GAS), with fewer infections occurring in infants. However, the potential for APSGN to develop in younger children is increasingly recognized, as evidenced by reports of infants as young as 8 months old presenting with the disease [1, 3].

Epidemiological studies indicate that while GAS infections are less frequent in infants, they can still lead to post‐infectious complications such as APSGN [14, 15]. This is supported by a case of a 14‐month‐old child with APSGN following GAS pharyngitis [14]. Confirmed cases of APSGN have been documented worldwide, including an 8‐month‐old infant in Sicily who presented with the disease, with a clinical history, a positive throat culture, raised ASO titers, and low C3 levels [10].

Furthermore, a prospective study of 420 patients aged 3 months to 5 years with fever and signs of pharyngitis found that 14.7% had positive throat cultures for GAS, and 54% had elevated ASO titers, suggesting that true GAS pharyngitis can present in the first year of life [11]. Another study involving children below 3 years of age found that 29% of children over 2 years were positive for GAS, compared to 7% of controls, further emphasizing that GAS can cause up to 30% of pharyngitis in children aged 2 to 3 years [12].

The prognosis for APSGN is excellent in most pediatric cases, particularly when hypertension and renal function are closely monitored and appropriately managed. Antibiotics play a critical role in reducing the spread of nephritogenic GAS strains within communities [2, 12]. However, in resource‐limited settings, delayed diagnosis and treatment can increase the risk of long‐term sequelae, such as chronic kidney disease [3].

This case underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for APSGN in children presenting with nephritic syndrome, regardless of age, particularly in endemic regions. Early recognition, supported by targeted investigations such as ASO titers and complement levels, enables timely intervention and improves outcomes.
