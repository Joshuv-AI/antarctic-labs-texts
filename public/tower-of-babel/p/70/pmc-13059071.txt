Distinct Grey Matter Alterations in Untreated Mild to Moderate Chronic Low Back Pain: A Longitudinal Magnetic Resonance Imaging Study
Authors: Monica Sean, Samantha Cote, Alexia Coulombe‐Lévêque, Julia Huck, Marylie Martel, Ze Ming Liu, Guillaume Léonard, Kevin Whittingstall, Pascal Tétreault
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundChronic low back pain (CLBP) remains the leading cause of disability worldwide, yet lumbar structural abnormalities seldom account for reported symptoms. Prior neuroimaging studies often report reduced cerebral grey matter density (GMD) in CLBP but typically include patients with moderate‐to‐severe pain who are taking centrally acting medications, both of which can independently influence brain structure.Methods and ResultsThis longitudinal observational cohort study assessed GMD in mostly mild‐to‐moderate CLBP individuals not using centrally acting medications. High‐resolution T1‐weighted MRI scans were acquired over three timepoints (baseline, 2 months, and 4 months) from 27 CLBP participants and 25 matched healthy controls. Voxel‐based morphometry revealed significantly increased GMD in CLBP participants in the right middle frontal and temporal gyri, and left orbitofrontal cortex. Additionally, GMD in regions including the right inferior parietal lobule (rIPL) showed an inverse correlation with global pain severity across all timepoints (r up to −0.72, p < 0.0001). Participants with longer pain duration (≥ 5 years) showed lower GMD in the rIPL and reported greater symptom severity, independent of age.ConclusionsBrain structural alterations may be present even in mild, unmedicated CLBP and may vary depending on pain duration and intensity. Structural variation in the rIPL was consistently associated with pain severity, suggesting that this region may contribute to inter‐individual differences in symptom burden. Given the correlational nature of these findings, future well‐controlled longitudinal studies spanning the full spectrum of chronic low back pain severity and treatment exposure are warranted to clarify the temporal dynamics and clinical relevance of these structural differences.Significance StatementThis study demonstrates that individuals with mild, unmedicated chronic low back pain show increased grey matter density in frontal and temporal regions and a robust inverse association between pain severity and parietal grey matter. By identifying the right inferior parietal lobule as a neural marker linked to symptom intensity, these findings refine our understanding of pain‐related brain plasticity, highlight structural correlates present even in less severe clinical populations, and suggest novel targets for early intervention strategies in chronic pain.

Introduction

Across all chronic pain (CP) conditions, chronic low back pain (CLBP) represents one of the leading causes of disability worldwide (Vos et al. 2017). In 90% of CLBP cases, the pain cannot be linked to a specific musculoskeletal cause (Finucane et al. 2020; Kahere and Ginindza 2020; Kasch et al. 2022a; Maher et al. 2017; Nijs et al. 2024). Several studies have found no association between imaging abnormalities of the dorsal‐lumbar regions and the presence of pain (Brinjikji, Diehn, et al. 2015; Brinjikji, Luetmer, et al. 2015; Burgstaller et al. 2016; Hall et al. 2021; Kasch et al. 2022b). This is likely due to the fact that several morphological abnormalities in the spine can be present in individuals without pain; representing normal anatomical variations or nonpathological degenerative changes associated with aging that begin in early adulthood (Benoist 2003; Brinjikji, Luetmer, et al. 2015; Parenteau et al. 2021; Wocial et al. 2021).

The brain plays a central role in the perception and processing of pain, particularly in the development of CP (Apkarian 2008; Bushnell et al. 2013; Garland 2012). Several researchers suggest that CP may involve the persistence of pain‐related memories and/or alterations in the adaptive updating or attenuation of neural representations initially associated with injury (Apkarian 2008; Mansour et al. 2014; McCarberg and Peppin 2019; Phelps et al. 2021). Indeed, numerous studies using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) have reported structural brain abnormalities in individuals with CLBP in regions spanning from somatosensory integration (e.g., thalamus, ACC, Insula) to the limbic system (e.g., NAc, hippocampus, amygdala, PFC) (Apkarian et al. 2009; Baliki et al. 2011a; Biggs et al. 2022; Makary et al. 2020; Mansour et al. 2014; Phelps et al. 2021; Simons et al. 2014; Vachon‐Presseau 2018; Yi and Zhang 2011). Although recent chronic pain research has increasingly emphasized functional connectivity and network‐level dynamics, structural brain imaging remains highly relevant for characterizing longer‐term neuroplastic adaptations. Voxel‐based morphometry (VBM) provides a macroscopic index of grey matter density (GMD), thought to be reflecting regional differences in tissue composition influenced by synaptic remodelling, dendritic architecture, glial changes, and cellular metabolic state (Ashburner and Friston 2000; Douaud et al. 2006). GMD does not directly measure neuronal or glial density, indeed, histological studies have shown limited direct correspondence between MRI‐derived grey matter probability and cellular density (Eriksson et al. 2009a; Lockwood‐Estrin et al. 2012). Still, structural MRI has consistently identified reproducible grey matter alterations across chronic pain populations (Baliki et al. 2011b; Cauda et al. 2014; Neumann et al. 2023). Importantly, T1‐weighted MRI, the basis of VBM, is the most widely acquired brain imaging modality in clinical practice, making structural measures inherently more translatable and clinically scalable than functional MRI, which remains primarily research‐oriented. Moreover, structural alterations are not restricted to cortical regions but can also involve subcortical structures such as the thalamus, basal ganglia, and limbic regions (Apkarian et al. 2004a; Baliki et al. 2011b; Yuan et al. 2017), positioning VBM as a complementary and clinically applicable approach for understanding chronic pain as a distributed brain disorder.

Most studies on GMD have reported lower GMD in patients with CLBP (Apkarian et al. 2004b; Baliki et al. 2011a; Ivo et al. 2013; Schmidt‐Wilcke et al. 2006; Ung et al. 2014; Yu et al. 2021). These previous studies have mainly examined CLBP patients with moderate to severe pain or those taking centrally acting medications, which can affect brain structure (Puiu et al. 2016; Russell et al. 2018; Younger et al. 2011). For instance, even short‐term morphine use has been linked to lasting reductions in grey matter volume (Younger et al. 2011). This makes it difficult to determine whether brain changes are due to chronic pain itself or medication effects, and leaves open the question of whether similar changes occur in milder, untreated cases.

To address this, we conducted a longitudinal study of individuals with CLBP who had never used opioids, anticonvulsants, or antidepressants, along with healthy controls. Most participants reported mild to moderate pain (pain scores < 6/10) and showed better physical function (brief pain inventory interference scores < 30/70 and pain outcomes questionnaire score < 60/190) than is typically seen in the literature (Sean et al. 2024). Using high‐resolution T1‐weighted MRI across three visits in 4 months, our main goal was to identify differences in grey matter density (GMD) between medication‐free CLBP patients and controls.

Methods

Participants

All participants provided written informed consent for their participation in the study. Ethics approval was granted from the institutional review board of the Centre intégré universitaire de santé et de services sociaux de l'Estrie—Centre hospitalier universitaire de Sherbrooke (CIUSSS de l'Estrie‐CHUS) (Sherbrooke, Quebec, Canada; approval #2021–3861). This study was designed as an exploratory longitudinal pilot observational study and has been registered on Open Science Framework (OSF), under the name “Pilot project on brain and lower back imaging of chronic pain” (https://osf.io/p2z6y) and was conducted in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki. There was no patient and public involvement for the aims of this specific study.

Participants were recruited prospectively using posters at the CIUSSS de l'Estrie‐CHUS, local physiotherapy clinics, Facebook ads, and by word of mouth. Twenty‐seven CLBP patients and 25 HC aged 18 to 75 years old took part in this study between April 1st, 2021 and July 22nd, 2022. HC were matched with CLBP patients for sex and age. Most important inclusion criteria for the CLBP were: (1) ≥ 18 years old; (2) low back pain (≥ 4 months) with or without pain radiating to the legs or radiating to the neck; (3) average pain intensity of ≥ 3/10 in the 24‐h period before the initial visit and no high‐pain cutoff; (4) pain primarily localized in the lower back; (5) no history/no use of opioids, anticonvulsants or antidepressants but conservative non‐pharmacological care and over the counter analgesics were allowed. Exclusion criteria for HC were: (1) history of chronic pain; (2) pain at the time of testing; (3) an outstanding painful episode within 3 months of enrollment in the study. Most important exclusion criteria for the two populations included: (1) Low back pain associated with systemic signs or symptoms (e.g., fever, chills); (2) Involvement in litigation related to back pain, having a disability claim, receiving workers' compensation, or intending to apply for such compensation; (3) Presence of another comorbid chronic pain condition or neurological disorder; (4) Evidence of rheumatoid arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, acute vertebral fractures, fibromyalgia, or a history of spinal surgery or spinal tumours; (5) Current diagnosis of depression (Beck Depression Inventory [BDI] total score ≥ 20), psychiatric disorders requiring treatment, or diagnosis of depression within the past 6 months; (6) Use of therapeutic doses of antidepressants (e.g., tricyclics, SSRIs, SNRIs); low doses used for sleep may be permitted; (7) Presence of another significant medical condition such as uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, congestive heart failure, coronary or peripheral vascular disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, or malignancy; (8) History of acute myocardial infarction with residual cardiac arrhythmia; (9) Presence of severe cardiovascular, pulmonary, renal, hepatic, or endocrine disease, or severe bronchial asthma; (10) History of gastroduodenal ulcers or gastrointestinal bleeding; (11) Presence of intra‐axial implants (e.g., spinal cord stimulators or intrathecal pumps); (12) Current use (within the last 14 days) of recreational drugs, or a history of drug or alcohol abuse; (13) History of chronic opioid use for pain management; (14) Current use of high‐dose opioids, defined as > 50 mg morphine equivalent per day; (15) MRI safety exclusion criteria: presence of metallic implants (e.g., cochlear implants, pacemakers, vascular coils), brain or cranial abnormalities, extensive tattoos, or claustrophobia. Full list of inclusion/exclusion criteria are presented elsewhere (https://osf.io/p2z6y) (Sean et al. 2024).

Study Design

The study had an observational longitudinal design. All participants attended three sessions at the Centre de recherche du CHUS: baseline (BL), 2‐months (2 M) and 4‐months (4 M) after BL. During each visit, participants completed an array of pain questionnaires and underwent brain MRI. Participants were scanned three times over a four‐month period without any externally introduced treatment or intervention but were allowed to continue their usual pharmacological and non‐pharmacological treatments. Participants were instructed to report any major changes in their treatment regimen during the study period and were not allowed to participate in any other chronic pain study. Due to the exploratory nature of the study, no a priori power calculation was conducted. However, a post hoc power analysis based on our main outcome (GMD differences between HC and CLBP) indicated that we achieved a statistical power of 83%.

MRI Acquisition

MRI data was collected on a 3 Tesla (3 T) Ingenia scanner equipped with a 32‐channel head‐coil (Philips Healthcare, Best, Netherlands). A high‐resolution 3D gradient‐echo T1 weighted image was first acquired (flip angle of 8°, TR/TE of 7.9/3.5 ms, 1 mm isotropic voxels with a field of view (FOV) of 224 × 224 × 160 mm). The total scan time was 4 min.

Preprocessing of T1 Weighted Images

T1‐weighted images were converted from DICOM to NIFTI using the dcm2niix tool embedded into MRIcroGL software (https://www.nitrc.org/projects/mricrogl/). Voxel Based Morphometry (VBM) analysis was carried out using the default parameters within the FMRIB Software Library (FSL‐VBM) pipeline (Douaud et al. 2007). A visual summary is presented in Figure 1. Briefly, each MRI scan is processed to remove non‐brain tissue and to segment the brain into grey matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid. Next, grey matter images from all participants are aligned to a standard brain template (the ICBM‐152 atlas), ensuring that images from different groups are equally represented. The aligned images are averaged and used to create a custom, study‐specific template. All participant grey matter images are then non‐linearly registered to this new template. To account for any stretching or shrinking during registration, each voxel value is multiplied by the Jacobian determinant, preserving local volume changes. These images are then smoothed using a Gaussian kernel (typically with sigma values like 2, 3, or 4 mm) to make the analysis more robust (Coalson et al. 2018).

VBM and Statistical Analysis

FSL Randomize (Winkler et al. 2014) was employed to perform a voxel‐wise general linear model (GLM) analysis to assess group differences. Non‐parametric permutation testing (5000 permutations) was conducted on whole‐brain grey matter (GM) maps to generate t‐statistic maps comparing healthy controls (HC) and individuals with chronic low back pain (CLBP). Given that Randomize does not readily accommodate general repeated‐measures designs and that most prior studies have been limited to single time points or pre/post intervention comparisons, we opted against constructing a suboptimal repeated‐measures design matrix. Instead, we leveraged the longitudinal nature of our dataset by conducting three independent group comparisons across time points and identifying consensus regions across analyses, as detailed below.

To isolate brain regions with significant differences between HC and CLBP patients, four different thresholds were applied using AFNI (Cox 1996a) (Figure 2). First, in each visit (BL, 2 and 4 M), only voxels with independent t‐test score higher than 2.3 were kept from the randomized t‐test maps. Second, across these remaining voxels, we created an intersection map of only voxels present at each of the three visits (BL, 2 and 4 M) with independent t‐test score higher than 2.3 were retained. Third, this intersection map was subject to 3 different cluster sizes: 15 voxels, 30 voxels and 45 voxels, wherein voxels face must touch (first‐nearest neighbour clustering), meaning that clusters present at 45 voxels were retained. Finally, clusters present at each smoothing (sigma = 2, sigma = 3, and sigma = 4) were retained based on the approximate localization (z, y, and z MNI coordinates) of the cluster using Brainnetome Atlas (https://atlas.brainnetome.org/bnatlas.html). Clusters passing the four different thresholding were considered as the most reproducible and stable clusters. Average GMD values of these clusters were extracted at each visit (probability values ranging from 0 to 1, wherein 0 = no GM is present and 1 = filled with GM). Intracranial volume (ICV) was obtained using FreeSurfer (version 7.2) (http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/). ICV was compared between both groups using a Student's t‐test (unpaired). Sociodemographic data were also compared using a Student's t‐test (unpaired) or Chi‐square test with SPSS (version 28).

Thresholding's are based on (1) t‐stats maps obtained from randomize; (2) significant and overlapping voxels present at each visit; (3) clusters of at least 45 voxels and (4) clusters that were present in each smoothing kernels. BL = baseline, 2 M = 2‐months after baseline, 4 M = 4 months after baseline.

Pain Questionnaires and Composite Global Pain Severity Score

As mentioned before, pain and other behavioural questionnaires have been analysed and discussed in a previous article (Sean et al. 2024). Briefly, CLBP patients completed eight pain and behavioural questionnaires online using the platform “Research Electronic Data Capture” (REDCap). Data from the Brief Pain Inventory (BPI) short form subscales (BPI severity and BPI interference) are presented in Table 1. The measure “average intensity of pain in the last 24 hours” used here was also computed from the BPI severity score. Since a single questionnaire typically captures only one dimension of the pain experience, we developed a composite severity score based on all administered questionnaires, following several groups that have used similar approaches of creating a composite score in chronic pain research (Pilitsis et al. 2021; Rigoard et al. 2021; Vallejo et al. 2010). Similarly to our behavioural analysis of this dataset (Sean et al. 2024), we used a z‐score threshold approach for the construction of this severity index. More specifically, for each questionnaire score, a value of +1 was assigned when a participant's score exceeded the group mean by more than half a standard deviation (z‐score > 0.5). Conversely, a value of −1 was assigned when a score was more than half a standard deviation below the group mean (z‐score < 0.5). Scores falling within ±0.5 standard deviations of the group mean were assigned a value of 0. Various z‐scores threshold were explored previously (Sean et al. 2024), and the 0.5 value was deemed optimal for this dataset. This procedure was applied at each visit and for every participant. As such, a positive composite score reflects a classification of greater symptom severity, while a negative score indicates a classification of lower symptom severity. The questionnaires used to construct this global pain severity score were: Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS), Pain Disability Index (PDI), Brief Pain Inventory (BPI) short form, PainDETECT (PD), Pain Outcomes Questionnaire (POQ), State–Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI‐S/T), McGill Pain Questionnaire (MPQ) short form, Central Sensitization Inventory (CSI) short form.

Pain Severity Correlation Maps

To relate brain GMD and the severity score of each patient, we created an averaged correlation map using AFNI (Cox 1996b). For this analysis, only CLBP patients can be included as the pain severity score can't be computed for healthy controls. To do this, a 4D image was created, allowing the concatenation of the GMD maps of each patient. To obtain an averaged correlation map, the GMD value of each voxel was correlated with the severity score at BL across all individuals. This process was repeated at 2 and 4 M, allowing us to obtain an averaged correlation map at each visit. To determine the regions showing the most significant correlations between GMD and the severity score, we applied three different thresholds using AFNI (Cox 1996b) conceptually similar to the approach presented before (Figure 2). First, for each averaged correlation map (BL, 2, and 4 M), only voxels with a statistical p‐value ≤ 0.05 were retained. Second, from these remaining voxels, we created an intersection map by keeping only voxels present at all three visits (BL, 2, and 4 M). Third, this intersection map was subjected to a clustering analysis where only clusters of at least 45 voxels were retained. Voxels had to be contiguous (clustering based on the nearest neighbour). Clusters passing all three different thresholds were considered the most significant clusters.

Results

Participants

Fifty‐two participants (25 HC and 27 CLBP) were recruited in the study. Three CLBP participants dropped out after the first visit (unexpected pregnancy [n = 1], discomfort during MRI [n = 1], scheduling conflicts [n = 1]), and one dropped out after the second visit (move to a different city [n = 1]). All HC completed the three experimental visits. The 25 HC (15 women, 10 men) were aged 42 ± 14 years, and the 27 CLBP participants (13 women, 14 men) were aged 43 ± 16 years. All variables showed no significant difference between both groups.

Results from the pain questionnaires, for example from the last pain intensity in the last 24 h, BPIs and BPIi, showed that our sample consisted of patients with mild to moderate pain symptoms (Cleeland and Ryan 1994; Dworkin et al. 2012; Poquet and Lin 2016). Questionnaire scores and characteristics of the sample are presented in Table 1.

CLBP Patients Show Higher GMD

Initial VBM results showed differences in GMD between groups at each visit (BL, 2 and 4 M). Across all the statistically significant voxels, 76%, 81% and 71% of the voxels showed higher GMD in CLBP compared to HC, respectively at BL, 2 and 4 M when using a smoothing kernel of 4 mm as shown in Figure 3. Similar results were obtained when using different smoothing kernels (sigma = 2 mm and sigma = 3 mm; see Data S1 and S2).

Similar results were obtained when using different smoothing kernels (sigma = 2 mm and sigma = 3 mm; see Data S3 and S4).

CLBP Patients Do Not Show Stable and Reproducible Regions With Lower GMD

After applying the same methodological approach to evaluate the presence of significant regions with lower GMD in CLBP compared to HC, no voxels survived the full methodology (see Data S8–S10 for individual smoothing maps and cluster tables).

Pain Severity Is Inversely Associated With GMD

To examine the relationship between brain structure and clinical symptom burden, we performed whole‐brain voxel‐wise correlation analyses between grey matter density (GMD) and the composite global pain severity score at each timepoint for the 23 CLBP participants that completed all study visits (Data S11). At each of the three visits, from the voxels that showed significant correlations, more than 80% of these brain voxels showed a negative correlation (p < 0.05), indicating that higher symptom severity consistently was associated with lower GMD. After applying our selection criteria presented in method Section 2.7, two brain regions emerged as most strongly associated with symptom severity: the right inferior parietal lobule (rIPL) (Figure 5D) and the left lateral occipital cortex.

In these regions, participants with the highest severity scores consistently exhibited lower GMD. This inverse relationship was confirmed through linear correlation analyses at each of the three timepoints (Figure 5A–C for rIPL at baseline, 2 months and 4 months respectively), with coefficients reaching up to r = −0.72 (p < 0.0001), highlighting a strong link between symptom intensity and apparent grey matter atrophy in these areas. An exploratory analysis further examined the role of pain duration. Participants who had experienced pain for 5 years or more showed significantly lower GMD in both identified regions (Figure 5E for rIPL) compared to those with a shorter pain history (p < 0.05). These individuals also reported higher severity scores (Figure 5F) but did not differ in age (Figure 5G), suggesting that pain duration, independent of age, may contribute to grey matter alterations, potentially interacting with symptom severity. Almost identical results were observed for the left lateral occipital cortex (l‐LOC) where participants with the highest severity scores consistently exhibited lower GMD (Data S12).

We further examined potential sex differences in the relationship between GMD and pain severity within the two clusters identified from this analysis across the three timepoints. This yielded six possible correlations per sex (3 timepoints × 2 regions). In male participants, only one correlation reached statistical significance: the association between GMD and pain severity in the l‐LOC at the 4‐month timepoint (Data S13A and S14A). In contrast, female participants demonstrated four significant correlations across timepoints. Specifically, all associations were significant except for baseline l‐LOC and 2‐month rIPL (Data S13B and S14B). Overall, these exploratory findings suggest a stronger and more consistent relationship between regional GMD and pain severity in females compared to males. Given the reduced subgroup sample sizes, these results should be interpreted cautiously.

Between‐Subject Associations Drive GMD–Pain Severity Relationships

To determine whether the observed associations between GMD and pain severity reflected within‐subject change over time or stable between‐subject differences, we examined the relationship between changes in pain severity (ΔPain; baseline to 4 months) and changes in GMD (ΔGMD) within the identified clusters (rIPL and l‐LOC). Pain severity remained relatively stable across the three timepoints at the group level, with no systematic increase or decrease over the four‐month period. Importantly, correlation analyses between ΔPain and ΔGMD revealed no significant associations in either region (all p > 0.05), either at the group level or when separated by sex. These findings indicate that short‐term fluctuations in symptom severity were not accompanied by measurable structural changes in GMD over the study interval.

Discussion

This study examined whether individuals with chronic low back pain (CLBP), free from centrally acting pharmacological treatments, exhibit differences in grey matter density (GMD) compared to healthy controls (HC). We observed higher GMD in CLBP participants, particularly in frontal and medial temporal regions. These changes were present despite relatively low symptom severity and the absence of drugs such as opioids, anticonvulsants, or antidepressants treatments known to significantly impact brain structure.

Our findings differ from most previous MRI studies in CLBP, which often report reduced GMD, interpreted as cortical atrophy (Apkarian et al. 2004b; Baliki et al. 2011a; Ivo et al. 2013; Marcori and Okazaki 2019; Ung et al. 2014; Wang et al. 2017; Yu et al. 2021). Importantly, such studies have typically enrolled individuals with higher average pain intensities (~7/10) and greater functional disability, many of whom are medicated with centrally acting agents that can alter grey matter. For example, Younger et al. (2011) demonstrated that only 1 month of daily morphine use led to lasting grey matter reductions (Younger et al. 2011). By contrast, our mild, medication‐naïve cohort reduces these confounding influences and may represent a different stage along the chronic pain continuum.

Collectively, our findings differ from previous reports likely due to distinct patient characteristics; our CLBP cohort consisted of individuals with milder symptoms and no centrally acting medication (Sean et al. 2024). Chronic pain progression is dynamic (Baliki and Apkarian 2015; Mansour et al. 2014) and brain changes may reflect a unique neural phenotype associated with less severe pain. We observed increased grey matter density (GMD) mainly in the frontal and medial temporal lobes, regions repeatedly implicated in chronic pain development and maintenance (Apkarian et al. 2004b; Ayoub et al. 2019; Baliki et al. 2011a; Baliki and Apkarian 2015; Ivo et al. 2013; McCarberg and Peppin 2019; Ong et al. 2019; Stegemann et al. 2023; Thompson and Neugebauer 2019; Waisman and Katz 2024). These areas are crucial for learning, memory, and emotional processing (Jeneson and Squire 2012; McDonald et al. 2001), and persistent pain is thought to result from maladaptive memory retention and reduced flexibility in updating or modulating pain‐related neural patterns (Apkarian 2008; Apkarian et al. 2009; Biggs et al. 2022; Mansour et al. 2014; Phelps et al. 2021; Simons et al. 2014; Vachon‐Presseau 2018; Yi and Zhang 2011). Notably, Stegemann et al. (2023) showed that networks of prefrontal cells encoding fear memories are involved in the development and maintenance of chronic pain states (Stegemann et al. 2023). Furthermore, exaggerated recall of past pain can bias future pain expectations and increase the risk of chronicity (Baliki and Apkarian 2015; Berger et al. 2018; Houde et al. 2020; Jeneson and Squire 2012; McDonald et al. 2001; Salovey et al. 1993; Waisman et al. 2022; Waisman and Katz 2024; Williams et al. 2022). There is also evidence of elevated inflammatory markers such as interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β) in the prefrontal cortex and medial temporal lobe in chronic pain patients (Gui et al. 2016).

Also, our findings align with and extend prior work in healthy individuals demonstrating that the rIPL plays a key role in shaping individual differences in pain sensitivity. Specifically, Emerson et al. (2014) reported that GMD in the IPL was inversely correlated with experimentally assessed pain sensitivity in a cohort of healthy participants (Emerson et al. 2014). Individuals with lower GMD in this region were found to be more pain‐sensitive, suggesting that the IPL may modulate attentional and evaluative processes associated with pain perception. Our study builds upon these foundational results by revealing that the same region's GMD also inversely correlates with self‐reported chronic pain severity, suggesting a potential continuity between neural correlates of acute pain sensitivity and chronic pain burden. This pattern supports the idea that brain structure in the IPL may function as a trait‐like marker of pain vulnerability, where lower GMD could either predispose individuals to heightened pain experiences or result from persistent nociceptive input over time, or more likely, a dynamic interaction of both. The convergence of these findings suggests that structural differences in the IPL observed in acute settings may persist or even exacerbate in chronic pain populations, reflecting long‐term maladaptations of pain‐processing networks.

The rIPL has been implicated in attentional control, sensory integration, and body schema representation, functions that are frequently disrupted in chronic pain conditions. Prior studies have shown altered IPL engagement in pain modulation, shifting of attention away from nociceptive cues, and spatial discrimination of pain (Baliki et al. 2011; Cauda et al. 2014). Notably, our findings are consistent with prior structural brain imaging reports where IPL alterations have been identified in pain disorders such as fibromyalgia, chronic low back pain, and CRPS. Moreover, the observed effects appear to deepen with chronicity. Our subgroup analysis showed significantly reduced GMD in both the rIPL and occipital cortex in patients with pain lasting ≥ 5 years. This effect was independent of age, strengthening the interpretation that the brain alterations observed are due to the chronic pain process rather than normal aging. The lack of difference in age between long‐ and short‐term pain groups further underscores the importance of duration as a relevant modulator of pain‐related brain reorganization.

These structural changes are also highly consistent with the dynamic, plastic nature of brain adaptations in chronic pain. Several longitudinal studies have demonstrated that GMD loss in pain‐related brain regions can worsen over time, with the potential for partial recovery following successful treatment (Rodriguez‐Raecke et al. 2009, 2013; Seminowicz et al. 2011; Tétreault et al. 2018). Our findings reinforce this model by showing that even in a clinically mild‐to‐moderate chronic low back pain (CLBP) cohort, symptom severity and duration map onto progressive structural alterations. From a mechanistic standpoint, the parallels between brain regions involved in acute pain sensitivity (Emerson et al. 2014) and those associated with chronic pain severity in our study suggest shared vulnerabilities or common functional pathways that persist and become reinforced with time. This model supports the growing perspective that pain persistence reflects a cumulative impact of persistent nociceptive processing on brain networks related to attention, perception, and internal body mapping. Clinically, these findings suggest that individuals expressing lower GMD in the rIPL, even before chronic pain onset, may be at greater risk for pain persistence or higher symptom burden.

An additional and less anticipated finding was the inverse association between pain severity and GMD in the l‐LOC. Although the occipital cortex is traditionally conceptualized as a visual processing region, contemporary models frame chronic pain as a distributed brain network disorder rather than a dysfunction confined to classical nociceptive areas (Baliki et al. 2014; Hashmi et al. 2013; Tagliazucchi et al. 2010). Even acute pain can modulate functional brain networks that include occipital cortices (Zheng et al. 2020). The lateral occipital cortex participates in higher‐order visual processing, visuomotor integration, and body representation, and is functionally coupled with parietal and premotor regions involved in sensorimotor integration. Increasing evidence suggests that chronic pain is associated with altered visuomotor integration (Schabrun et al. 2017; Tsao et al. 2008) and body schema reorganization (Boesch et al. 2016; Lotze and Moseley 2007) reflecting maladaptive updating of internal models of the body. Structural alterations in occipital regions may therefore reflect network‐level adaptations within systems supporting spatial representation, attention to bodily signals, and integration of visual and somatosensory inputs. Within this framework, greater symptom burden may be associated with more pronounced reorganization of distributed visuospatial and sensorimotor networks, consistent with the view of chronic pain as a whole‐brain network phenomenon rather than a purely sensory disorder.

To clarify whether the observed GMD–pain severity associations reflected within‐subject progression or stable inter‐individual variability, we examined longitudinal changes in both variables. Pain levels remained within the low‐to‐moderate range and showed no systematic group‐level progression. Moreover, changes in pain severity were not associated with changes in GMD over the four‐month interval. This suggests that the inverse relationship between GMD and symptom burden is primarily driven by between‐subject differences rather than within‐subject structural adaptation over time. Accordingly, these findings are best interpreted as reflecting stable, trait‐like structural variability associated with chronic pain burden within an established CLBP population, rather than evidence of short‐term chronification or dynamic changes. This distinction strengthens the interpretation of rIPL and l‐LOC involvement as markers of inter‐individual variability in symptom severity rather than progressive neuroanatomical change.

The biological meaning of grey matter density (GMD) as measured by MRI is still unclear. Some studies have found no strong link between GMD and actual tissue measures like neuron or glial cell density (Eriksson et al. 2009b; Lockwood‐Estrin et al. 2012). However, others, such as Keifer et al., reported that GMD might relate to dendritic spine density, potentially reflecting the number of synapses (Keifer Jr et al. 2015). Another possible explanation is neuroinflammation. Positron Emission Tomography (PET) studies in chronic low back pain (CLBP) have shown increased levels of TSPO in several brain regions such as the thalamus and the back/leg regions of the primary somatosensory region S1 (Alshelh et al. 2022; Loggia 2024; Torrado‐Carvajal et al. 2021). Notably, these studies involved patients with mild average pain levels (around 3/10), similar to the patients in our study, suggesting that even mild chronic pain may be associated with subtle brain inflammation. However, recent human transcriptomic and molecular imaging studies have demonstrated that TSPO expression does not selectively index microglial activation but reflect the density of inflammatory cells rather than activation state (Nutma et al. 2023). Consequently, increases in TSPO signal in chronic pain populations should be interpreted as evidence of neuroimmune involvement or altered cellular metabolic state, rather than a direct marker of classical pro‐inflammatory activation. Changes in GMD might also reflect gliosis, a reactive process where glial cells (like astrocytes and microglia) grow or multiply in response to brain stress or damage. A recent review (Emvalomenos et al. 2024) highlighted gliosis as a possible mechanism involved in the transition from acute to chronic pain. However, it is important to note that T1‐weighted MRI cannot directly identify these biological processes. There is also a lack of post‐mortem or histological studies in people with chronic pain, which limits our ability to clarify the exact cellular changes underlying differences in GMD.

Given the well‐established influence of sex on pain perception, central sensitization, and brain structure, we conducted exploratory sex‐stratified analyses for both the primary group comparisons (r‐MFG, l‐OFG, r‐MTG clusters) and the pain severity correlations (rIPL and l‐LOC). Overall, the pattern of higher GMD in CLBP relative to healthy controls was preserved in both males and females across these principal clusters, suggesting that the observed structural differences are not driven exclusively by one sex. More pronounced sex differences emerged in the correlations between GMD and pain severity. Males showed only one significant association (4‐month l‐LOC), whereas females demonstrated four significant correlations across the six tested models, indicating a stronger and more consistent structure–symptom relationship in females. Although these analyses were exploratory and should be interpreted cautiously, this pattern is consistent with evidence of sex‐dependent neurobiological mechanisms in chronic pain and pain processing (Bartley and Fillingim 2013; Gupta et al. 2017; Labus et al. 2013; Mogil 2020), including greater engagement of cognitive–affective and mesocorticolimbic circuits in females (Reckziegel et al. 2021). These findings highlight the importance of considering sex as a biological variable and of conducting adequately powered, sex‐stratified longitudinal neuroimaging studies in chronic pain.

Strengths and Limitations

A major strength of this study is the focus on CLBP patients with no exposure to opioids, anticonvulsants, or antidepressants, avoiding medication‐related confounds and creating a cohort of patients with mostly mild/moderate symptoms. The longitudinal design, with three MRI sessions over 4 months, allowed us to implement a stringent repeated‐measures approach that reduced false positives, an uncommon strength in chronic pain MRI research.

Key limitations include the exclusive use of T1‐weighted MRI, which limits biological interpretation, as signal intensity is non‐quantitative and cannot distinguish between neuronal, glial, or extracellular changes. Additional modalities such as FLAIR or free‐water DWI could help detect neuroinflammation. The relatively small sample size also limits statistical power and increases the risk of both Type I and II errors, though repeated measures partly mitigated this. Replication in larger, multimodal studies is needed.

Conclusion

In individuals with mild, unmedicated chronic low back pain, we observed higher GMD in frontal and medial temporal regions compared to healthy controls, along with a consistent inverse association between pain severity and GMD in the right inferior parietal lobule and lateral occipital cortex. Longitudinal analyses indicated that these associations were primarily driven by stable inter‐individual differences rather than within‐subject structural changes over the four‐month interval. These findings suggest that regional structural variability may relate to differences in symptom burden within an established chronic pain population.

Interpretation remains limited by the use of T1‐weighted MRI, which does not permit direct inference regarding underlying cellular mechanisms such as synaptic remodelling, gliosis, or neuroimmune involvement. Future well‐controlled longitudinal studies spanning a broader range of symptom severity, duration, and treatment exposure, and ideally integrating multimodal imaging approaches, will be necessary to further characterize the neural correlates and clinical relevance of structural variability in chronic low back pain.
