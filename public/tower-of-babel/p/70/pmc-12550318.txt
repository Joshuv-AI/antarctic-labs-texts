Ornithomya palmae species nova (Diptera: Hippoboscidae) a new species of louse fly from the Canary Islands
Authors: Denise C Wawman, Borja Milá
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA new species of louse fly in the genus Ornithomya Latreille, 1802 (Diptera: Hippoboscidae) Ornithomya palmae sp. n. is described from the island of La Palma in the Canary Islands. Unlike other species of Ornithomya, this species has a series of horizontal ridges running across the scutellum. Otherwise, the most obvious differences between O. palmae sp. n. and the most closely related species Ornithomya chloropus Bergroth, 1901 are the arrangement of the wing veins and the lack of dark markings on the lateral gena and mesothoracic basisternum in the new species.

Introduction

The Hippoboscidae (Diptera) are a family of ectoparasites of birds (louse flies or flat flies) and mammals (keds) in which both sexes are obligate haematophages throughout their adult lives. The genus Ornithomya Latreille consists of at least 31 living species (Nartshuk et al., 2024) and one fossil species, although there are some species of uncertain status (Dick, 2018).

Unlike some species in other genera, the louse flies in the genus Ornithomya have full wings and are strong fliers. They are found on a large range of avian host species, with some species, such as Ornithomya avicularia Linnaeus, Ornithomya chloropus Bergroth, 1901, and Ornithomya fringillina Curtis, 1836, being generalists on a large range of hosts, and others such as Ornithomya biloba Dufour, 1827 being monoxenous or stenoxenous (Hill, 1962a; Hutson, 1984; Wawman et al., unpublished data). Members of the genus are known to vector a range of pathogens by various routes including Haemoproteus spp. By biting (Baker, 1957), trypanosomes when infected flies are ingested by their host (Baker, 1976) and, in all probability, Avipoxvirus by acting as a mechanical vector (Wawman et al., 2025b). Some species ranges are expanding and some are colonising new areas (Wawman, 2024, 2025) potentially increasing the risk that they will transmit diseases to new host species as they parasitise new hosts (Wawman et al., unpublished data).

Hippoboscidae have not been well studied in the Canary Islands, an archipelago situated in the Atlantic Ocean off the northwest of Africa (Fig. 1). Baez, 1978 reported seven species of Hippoboscid from the islands, five louse flies, Ornithomya chloropus, Ornithophila metallica Schiner, 1864, Crataerina acutipennis, Austen, 1926, Pseudolynchia canariensis Marquart, 1939, and Icosta minor Bigot, 1958, and two keds Hippobosca equina Linnaeus, 1758 and Melophagus ovinus Linnaeus, 1758. However, only two of these, O. chloropus and P. canariensis were reported from the island of La Palma. Ornithophila gestroi Rondani, 1878 has since been reported from Eleonora's Falcon Falco eleonorae Gené, 1839 on the island of Lanzarote (Gangoso et al., 2010) and Icosta pilosa Marquart, 1843 from Canarian Houbara Bustard Chlamydotis undulata fuerteventurae Rothschild and Hartert, 1894 on the island of Fuerteventura (Kehlmaier and Quaisser, 2013).

Map showing the location of the island of La Palma in the Canary Islands.

The aim of this article is to describe a new species in the genus Ornithomya, which was taken from the La Palma subspecies of the Canary Islands Chaffinch Fringilla canariensis palmae Tristram, 1889, a subspecies of small non-migratory passerine endemic to the island (Recuerda et al., 2021), during a study of these birds on the island of La Palma in the Canary Islands archipelago. It also aims to examine the potential value of using the lateral sidepiece of tergite 6 of females in the genus Ornithomya as a means of distinguishing the species by comparing these structures in sequenced specimens.

Materials and methods

Specimen collection

Louse flies were collected from Canary Islands Chaffinches Fringilla canariensis palmae on the island of La Palma in the winter of 2022, from different locations as shown in Table 1. The flies were collected while capturing Canary Islands Chaffinches for a long-term ornithological study, and were only found in this species. However, because we rarely capture other bird species in the nets, we are unable provide information regarding the host specificity of the flies. The three localities where the flies were collected differ markedly in habitat type, and include dense humid heath composed mainly of Erica canariensis Rivas Mart., Martín Osorio & Wildpret, 2011 and Morella faya (Aiton) Wilbur, 1994 (at Roque Niquiomo), and dry pine forest dominated by Pinus canariensis C. Sm. 2010 at two different elevations, 840 m (Fuencaliente) and 1825 m (at Roque de los Muchachos) (Table 1). The flies were preserved in 70 % ethanol for later examination, in individually labelled tubes, with a specimen code, date and location name.

Examination

The collected specimens were examined under a binocular light microscope, and photographed using a Leica M165 C microscope with a Leica DFC490 camera mount at Oxford University Museum of Natural History. Additional images of one wing under different lighting conditions were taken with a Leica APO stereo microscope using a GXCAM Hi Chrome SMIII camera (GT Vision). An Olympus TG6 camera held over one eyepiece of a Swift S306S stereo microscope was used to photograph the tergites and ventral abdomens.

Keys (Hutson, 1984; Maa, 1964; Nartshuk et al., 2024; Oboňa et al., 2022), the description of the genus Ornithomya (Latreille, 1802) and original species descriptions (Austen, 1930; Bergroth, 1901; Curtis, 1836; Dufour, 1827; Erichson, 1842; Ferris, 1930; Hutson, 1971, 1981; Maa, 1967, 1969; Macquart, 1844; Sharp, 1907), the description of a neotype of Ornithomya chloropus (Hill, 1964), and other references (Haeselbarth et al., 1966; Maa, 1964), as well as comparisons to museum specimens from western Europe and Africa, and previously sequenced specimens from the United Kingdom and Ireland (Wawman et al., 2025a), were used to try to establish the identity of the specimens. However, satisfactory taxonomic resolution beyond the level of the genus Ornithomya to a known species based on morphological features was not possible.

DNA sequencing

One middle leg was removed from each of the flies and preserved in a separate individually labelled tube in 70 % ethanol for sequencing. Upon reaching the laboratory, it was stored in a freezer at −80 °C, until it could be sequenced. The leg was disrupted under liquid nitrogen and DNA was extracted using the Qiagen Blood and Tissue kit, following manufacturer's instructions.

The cytochrome c oxidase I (COXI) gene fragments were amplified by PCR using primers LEPF1 (ATT CAA CCA ATC ATA AAG ATA TTG G) and LEPR1 (TAA ACT TCT GGA TGT CCA AAA AAT CA) (Integrated DNA Technologies, Belgium) (Hebert et al., 2004) at the manufacturer's recommended concentration of 0.5 μM, on an Applied Biosystems Veriti PCR machine, using the following cycling conditions, 5 min at 98 °C, 40x (30 s at 98 °C; 30 s at 60 °C; 30 s at 72 °C) then 7 min at 72 °C.

PCR products were visualised after separation on a 1 % agarose gel, purified using the Qiagen PCR Clean up kit according to manufacturer's instructions and then Sanger sequenced by Source BioScience, UK, using the same primers as for amplification.

The resulting sequences were compared against all sequences in both GenBank BLASTN (Zhang et al., 2000) and BOLDSystems v4 using their web-based alignment tools (Ratnasingham et al., 2024).

A phylogeny was produced from COXI sequences from GenBank and those for the female holotype and male paratype. Initial analyses used all available sequences for the louse flies that were of over 500 base pairs in length with no gaps. The package DECIPHER version 2.0 (Wright, 2014) in R version 4.3.1 (R Development Core Team, 2022) was used to align and trim the sequences. Sequences were removed if their alignments did not overlap well with the other sequences.

A tree showing the position of O. palmae sp. n. in relation to other louse flies species including different genera, was produced from the longest alignments. It was rooted through the COXI sequence from a moth, the Common White Wave Cabera pusaria Linnaeus, 1758, GenBank accession number OR369542. A second, unrooted tree showing the relationship to the other species in the genus Ornithomya was also produced. Both trees were plotted in MEGA version 12 (Kumar et al., 2024), using 1000 replicates of the standard boot strap method to produce a maximum likelihood neighbour-joining tree, with the Tamura-Nei model, uniform rates and the nearest neighbour (NNI) methods.

Other material examined

Other Ornithomya species, including various type specimens (holotypes, paratypes, etc.), from the collections at the Natural History Museum, London and Oxford University Museum of Natural History were examined for similarities to O. palmae sp. n. as many of the original species’ descriptions lacked the necessary detail and most species have no published DNA sequences. These included type specimens of Ornithomya comosa Austen, 1930, Ornithomya remota Walker, 1949, currently considered a junior synonym of O. parva Macquart, 1844, Ornithomya opposita Walker, 1849, and Ornithomya fuscipennis Bigot, 1885, as well as specimens of Ornithomya anchineuria Speiser, 1905, Ornithomya fur Shiner, 1868, Ornithomya inocellata Ferris, 1930, Ornithomya nigricornis Erichson, 1842, Ornithomya biloba Dufour, 1927, Ornithomya fringillina Curtis, 1936, Ornithomya chloropus Bergroth, 1901 and Ornithomya avicularia Linnaeus, 1758. A full list of the museum specimens examined can be found in the supplementary material S2.

Description

Summary

Ornithomya palmae sp. n. can be placed in the genus Ornithomya Latreille, 1802 because it shares the following features common to the genus: a dorsoventrally flattened body, covered with a tough exoskeleton, a small head with short antennae, humeral tubercules that project forward to reach the sides of the head, wings with few views, small halteres, bifid claws and empodia (Latreille, 1802). The fully functional wings have three crossveins posterior to the radial veins, with vein R 2 + 3 not adjacent to the costa, and wing membranes with microtrichia (Hutson, 1984).

The most obvious difference between O. palmae sp. n. and other species in the genus is in the structure of the scutellum. The male has a series of obvious horizontal ridges running from left to right across the whole length of the scutellum. In the females, these ridges are shallower, shorter and most obvious towards the posterior part of the scutellum but may be difficult to see at low magnification and under certain lighting conditions. Ridges such as these have previously been used in species descriptions (Bequaert, 1954; Walker, 1849) but their significance of is uncertain, and, as no fresh species were observed, it cannot be ruled out that they were caused by the fixation methods used, although no similar changes were seen in any of the other 4000 Hippoboscids in the genus Ornithomya preserved using the same methods, and they were present on the hard exoskeleton which is likely to be resistant to degradation by ethanol.

All three specimens of Ornithomya palmae sp. n. have palps and antennae of approximately equal length, six bristles arranged in a line along the posterior edge of the scutellum, although this is also a feature that is known to be unreliable (Hill, 1964; Hutson, 1984), and a similar overall pattern of wing venation. Wing veins R2+3 and R4+5 (following the notation of used by Hutson) run very closely to each other along their proximal two thirds, and the two distal cross-veins (between 1bc and 3r, and the unfilled vein between 2bc and 1 m) form a straight line.

However, O. palmae sp. n. would appear to exhibit some phenotypic variation in a range of features usually used to describe species of Hippoboscidae, despite the two specimens sequences having a 98.97 % match in the 583 base pair COXI DNA sequences used for “DNA barcoding” when aligned using the BLASTN feature in NIH GenBank (Zhang et al., 2000). For example, the ratio of distance between the points at which the wing veins R1 and R2+3 meet the costa to the distance between the points at which R2+3 and R4+5 meet the costa is different in the two females (holotype and paratype) and male paratype.

This species differs from the most closely related species Ornithomya chloropus in that it lacks the dark triangular markings on the gena (Bergroth, 1901; Hill, 1964) and has wing veins R2+3 and R4+5 that run very close to each other along their proximal and central thirds.

Discussion

A new species of louse fly Ornithomya palmae is described from the Canary Island of La Palma, which from “DNA barcoding” using a segment of the COXI gene was confirmed to be a separate species from its nearest relative Ornithomya chloropus. The phylogeny places O. palmae sp. n. in the same clade as O. chloropus, O. anchineuria and O. avicularia, with these and the other species of Ornithomya appearing in the same relative positions as in other recent phylogenies (de Moya, 2019; Petersen et al., 2007b; Yatsuk et al., 2023, 2025).

Differential diagnosis

The new species differs from all currently accepted Ornithomya species found in Europe and Africa as it has horizontal ridges across the scutellum, although the significance of these is uncertain. It should be noted, however, that this feature was described in Ornithomya remota (Walker, 1849) from Gough Island, Tristan da Cunha in the South Atlantic, which lies approximately 2810 km (1750 miles) from Cape Town in South Africa, and in Ornithomya synallaxidis Lynch-Arribalzaga, 1881 from Argentina (Bequaert, 1954), both currently considered junior synonyms of Ornithomya parva Macquart, 1844 (Bequaert, 1954), although the ridges are not mentioned in either the description of O. parva or that of other species subsequently synonymised with it, or in their subsequent redescriptions (Bequaert, 1954; Maa, 1964; Macquart, 1844).

It can be distinguished from all of the species in the O. biloba group - O. biloba, O. comosa Austen, 1930, O. fur Shiner, 1868, O. inocellata Ferris, 1930, Ornithomya roubaudi Seguy, 1938, Ornithomya cecropis Hutson, 1981, Ornithomya sorbens Hutson, 1971, and Ornithomya rupes Hutson, 1981 – by the lesser extent of the microtrichia in wing cells 1 m and 2 m and the absence of (or sparsity of) major setae on the scutellum away from its poster border (Austen, 1930; Dufour, 1827; Ferris, 1930; Hutson, 1971, 1981). Additionally, O. inocellata lacks tergites 2–6 and the ocelli are vestigial or absent (Ferris, 1930).

Ornithomya fringillina is smaller, usually only has four setae along the posterior edge of the scutellum and has a clear area in cell 3r close to the junction of vein R4+5 with the costa (Hill, 1962b; Hutson, 1984; Oboňa et al., 2022; Wawman et al., 2025a).

Ornithomya avicularia is usually larger, with eight setae along the posterior edge of the scutellum and has a large clear area in cell 3r close to the junction of vein R4+5 with the costa, and large clear areas between the tracts of microtrichia and cell 1 m due to the absence of microtrichia in those regions (Hutson, 1984).

Ornithomya papillosa Maa, 1964 has microtrichia almost completely covering wing cell 1 m and robust setae on the anal abdominal and lateral abdominal areas with basal papillae that are larger than the spiracles (Maa, 1964).

Ornithomya marginalis Maa, 1964 has 2 tracts of microtrichia in cell 1 m and 8 major bristles on the scutellum (Maa, 1964).

The original description of Ornithomya parva from “Brazil or Chile” refers only to its colour and the presence of black wing veins and that the legs are hirsute on the upper surface only (Macquart, 1844): O. palmae sp. n. has seta both the upper and lower surface of the legs. Bequaert illustrates a specimen of O. parva taken from Valparaiso, Chile, as having similar patterns of wing venation to some morphotypes of Ornithomya fringillina with a clear area in cell 3r, adjacent to the distal end of vein R4+5, which is not present in O. palmae sp. n. (Bequaert, 1954). The wing veins R2+3 and R 4 + 5 of O. parva are widely separated, unlike those of O. palmae sp. n., and the scutellum has four strong bristles along its posterior border and smaller ones scattered across its surface, whereas O. palmae sp. n. has six strong posterior scutellar bristles.

Examination of the female type specimen of Ornithomya remota Walker, 1849 currently standing under Ornithomya parva Macquart, 1844 at NHM, London (BMNH(E)251,391) as Ornithomya parva remota, reveals a fly of a similar size (wing 6.2 mm) to O. palmae sp. n., that also has six scutellar bristles and veins R2+3 and R4+5 that run more closely together than other species, O. parva remota, however, has distinctive very broad foreleg femurs, a single long strong seta on round tergite 6. In the original description of O. remota Walker states “Scutcheon transversed by a row of slight furrows, widening from the fore border to the hind border which is beset with a few stout black bristles” (Walker, 1849) but these were not easily visible in the specimen examined. The fly in NHM, London and another smaller specimen of Ornithomya parva (wing 4.0 mm) in OUNHM, from Baja, (? In Argentina) share the distinctive broad foreleg femurs that are not mentioned in any description (Bequaert, 1954; Walker, 1849), but which clearly distinguish these flies from O. palmae sp. n.

Ornithomya palmae sp. n. is very similar to O. chloropus but can be distinguished on the basis of the closeness of the longitudinal veins R2+3 and R4+5, which run closely together along the proximal two-thirds of their length in O. palmae sp. n. but are more widely separated in O. chloropus. Ornithomya chloropus also has dark triangular markings on the lateral gena, and often dark areas on the medial basisternum and is overall a much darker colour (Hill, 1964).

Variation in the morphology Ornithomya species

The few specimens of Ornithomya palmae sp. n. described show a degree of variation in some of the key features often used to distinguish species in the genus Ornithomya, most notably in the ratio of the distance between the junctions of wing veins R1 and R2+3, and R2+3 and R4+5 that has been used in some recent keys to the genus Ornithomya (Nartshuk et al., 2019, 2024; Oboňa et al., 2022). Inconsistencies in this ratio and in the position of the various wing veins have been reported previously for species in the genus Ornithomya (Bequaert, 1954). In Ornithomya palmae sp. n. the differences in wing morphology may be due to sexual dimorphism, or it may be that this species shows a high level of phenotypic variability. Ornithomya fringillina has been shown to have several morphotypes produced by the patterns of wing microtrichia (Smart, 1939; Denise C Wawman et al., 2025a). Wing lengths may also vary across the ranges cited for these species (Petersen et al., 2007a).

Similarly, chaetotaxy is known to be highly variable amongst the Ornithomya species, particularly in relation to the number of bristles on the scutellum (Bequaert, 1954; Hutson, 1984; Petersen et al., 2007a; Wawman et al., 2025a). Although the three specimens described all had six scutellar bristles, the two female specimens had a markedly different arrangement of setae on the lateral sidepieces of tergite 6, a feature reported in a the recent description of Ornithomya panovi (Yatsuk et al., 2025).

Although the two females of O. palmae sp. n. had similarly shaped tergites, sequencing cast doubt on the reliability of the use of the shape of tergite 6 of female flies in the determination of some species in the genus Ornithomya, a method which was previously described by T. C. Maa (Maa, 1962, 1967) but has not remained in widespread use in the Western Palearctic and Africa and does not appear in recent keys (Hutson, 1984; Nartshuk et al., 2024; Oboňa et al., 2022). The shape of tergite 6 may, however, be of use in determining the North American species (Levesque-Beaudin et al., 2020) although Maa stated that the only useful feature in separating the two North American species, Ornithomya bequaerti and Ornithomya anchineuria was the pattern of microtrichia on the wings (Maa, 1969).

The variability between features amongst individuals of the same species makes reliance on a small number of features unreliable. It may be that these species require a different approach, such as discriminant function analysis, to separate them morphologically.

We hypothesise that appearance may be of little relevance to species that live hidden amongst the feathers of their hosts and that they may rely on pheromones to identify a mate, rather than species specific physical characteristics.

Current issues in species delineation within the genus Ornithomya

This description of Ornithomya palmae sp. n., like most new species descriptions, is based only a small number (three) specimens, and consequently risks using features in the description that will, in future, found to be unhelpful for identifying this and other species. Further research with more specimens will be needed to clarify reliability of the various features, and the significance, if any, of the ridges on scutellum and its variable morphology in Ornithomya palmae sp. n., as ridges have not been documented in any currently accepted species, the only previous mentions of them being in the description of Ornithomya remota (Walker, 1849) and O. synallaxidis (Bequaert, 1954), which are now both considered to be junior synonyms of O. parva.

Variable morphological features in conspecifics in the genus Ornithomya are not the only problem in describing a new species. There are multiple issues even when a systematic approach to species definitions is used, combining consistent morphological features, comparisons of DNA sequences and phylogenetic analysis, that apply to many taxa, not just the Hippoboscidae. Many original species descriptions are very brief and not particularly useful, for example, those of O. fringillina (Curtis, 1836) and O. parva (Macquart, 1844) mainly describe the colour of the fly, which is very variable and can change with age, with little reference to the morphology. For some species the original descriptions are hard to find, particularly those when the names have been changed or those of species currently now considered junior synonyms, and may only be available as translations in other works, usually without any of original the illustrations, for example the description of O. synallaxidis is only readily available as Bequaert's translation (Bequaert, 1954).

Type specimens may have been lost, or not catalogued, and although neotypes have been designated and described for some species, such as Ornithomya chloropus (Hill, 1964), for most species they have not. In other cases, there seems to be confusion around type specimens. For example, a specimen (BMNH(E)251,391) currently standing under O. parva in Natural History Museum, London, and often cited as the holotype of O. parva, is more strictly that of Ornithomya parva remota Walker, 1949 or Walker's original O. remota. Whereas Macquart's O. parva was from “Brazil or Chile”, Walker's O. remota specimen originated from Tristan da Cunha, over 3300 km (2000 miles) away, and may yet turn out to be an endemic species of islands of the South Atlantic Ocean.

Over time museum specimens may also have been damaged, have faded, collected dust obscuring features, or lost wing microtrichia. Many have dried out to such an extent that some features, especially those on softer areas like the abdomen, have become distorted or hidden in folded tissue.

Modern molecular techniques, will hopefully help clarify these issues in time, but this would require sequencing type specimens, which may only be possible by using techniques used in archaeology to sequence ancient DNA (aDNA) which are currently expensive and require more analysis of the shorter sequences produced. Currently, only about one fifth of Hippoboscids have a published COXI sequence, and many of these are short or have large gaps. Full genome sequencing such as that undertaken by various projects, such as the Darwin Tree of Life Project (Blaxter et al., 2022) may help resolve issues in time, but relies on experts identifying species according to the currently accepted species list and consequently may miss cryptic species or those that have been inappropriately synonymised.

It is possible that the flies assumed to be O. chloropus or O. fringillina in previous studies of flies from the Canary Islands are actually the species described here. A published photograph of a Hippoboscid identified as O. chloropus, presumably taken from either La Palma or the neighbouring island of Tenerife (Baez, 1978), shows a fly with similar wing venation, but lacks enough detail to redetermine its identity. The formal description of this new species, Ornithomya palmae sp. n. may allow determination of preserved specimens from the region, which may yield further examples of O. palmae sp. n. or other species of flies that may have evolved on the islands along with endemic bird species.

Ecology and possible origins of O. palmae sp. n

All three specimens of O. palmae sp. n. were found on the same host species, and O. palmae sp. n. may have coevolved with the Canary Islands Chaffinch, Fringilla canariensis to become host specific. However, it is probable that it is a generalist species like the other Ornithomya in species group “a” (Dick, 2018) to which it is most closely related, especially as islands may lack host-specific Hippoboscids (Tomás et al., 2021). Further specimen collection will be needed to determine the range of host species used and other aspects of the ecology of O. palmae sp. n. such as whether it is limited to the higher altitude laurel and pine forests, or if it occurs throughout the island of La Palma, on other islands of the archipelago, or in mainland Africa or Europe.

The related host species the Common Chaffinch Fringilla coelebs Linnaeus, 1758 has been found to host three species of louse flies in Europe O. avicularia, O. chloropus, and O. fringillina in numbers that suggest that they are regular, rather than accidental, hosts of these species (Wawman et al., unpublished data) and it may be that the Canary Islands Chaffinches also play host to more than one species. However, island parasite communities are often species poor with host species that colonise islands hosting fewer species than their mainland counterparts as parasites “miss the boat” or fail to establish viable populations (MacLeod et al., 2010). For example, Macaronesian Blackcaps Sylvia atricapilla Linnaeus, 1758 lack host specific haemosporidians and have a lower prevalence of infection than mainland Blackcaps (Pérez-Rodríguez et al., 2013), and most of the species of passerines studied on the Azores have fewer species of feather mites than those on continental Europe (Rodrigues et al., 2015).

The only known specimens of O. palmae sp. n. were found at high altitudes (from 840 m to 1189 m) and it may be that it is adapted to colder conditions, as its closest relative O. chloropus appears to be (Hill, 1962a; Wawman, 2025). Ornithomya chloropus has not been reported from either North Africa nor the Iberian Peninsula, although it has been reported from the Azores (GBIF, 2023) and it may be that it the ancestors of O. palmae sp. n. followed the same route as the ancestors of the Canary Islands Chaffinch colonising the Canary Islands, via Madeira and the Azores (Recuerda et al., 2021) possibly arriving from northern Europe with its hosts. However, as it is probably a generalist species, O. palmae sp. n. may have arrived with birds from elsewhere migrating on the East Atlantic Flyway, or O. palmae sp. n. may have been present on other host species on the island of La Palma, remained as a glacial phase survivor, as O. chloropus did in the United Kingdom and Ireland (Hill, 1962a), and then parasitised the Canary Islands Chaffinch's ancestors when they colonised the island.

As Hippoboscids are known to transmit a range of diseases and to harbour a range of other pathogens (Baker, 1976, 1957; Bezerra-Santos and Otranto, 2020; Čisovská Bazsalovicsová et al., 2023; Farajollahi et al., 2005; Wawman et al., 2025b), the presence of a potential vector may have implications for the health of their hosts. Although the hosts are likely to be well adapted to pathogens currently circulating in the region, human activities or migratory birds could introduce a new disease potentially putting endemic species and subspecies in the region at risk.

Conclusion

There have been few studies of the Hippoboscids in this region and this study adds to our knowledge of a family of Diptera that has been infrequently studied until recently. The formal description of this new species, Ornithomya palmae sp. n. may allow determination of any preserved specimens from the region, which may yield further examples of O. palmae sp. n. and possibly lead to the identification of other new species, further contributing to our knowledge of this group of flies in the region. A comparison of the lateral sidepieces of tergite 6 of sequenced female specimens across the genus also showed that there was no consistency in this feature. It is likely that, as many species were described on the basis of a single specimen, differences due to sexual dimorphism and individual variation were missed.

Resumen

Describimos una nueva especie del género Ornithomya Latreille, 1802 (Diptera: Hippoboscidae), Ornithomya palmae sp. n., de la isla de La Palma en las Islas Canarias. A diferencia de otras especies de Ornithomya, esta especie presenta una serie de crestas horizontales que atraviesan el escutelo. Por lo demás, las diferencias más evidentes entre O. palmae sp. n. y la especie más próxima, Ornithomya chloropus Bergroth, 1901, son la disposición de las venas alares y la ausencia de marcas oscuras en la gena lateral y el basisternum mesotorácico en la nueva especie.
