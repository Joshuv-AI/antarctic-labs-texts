Concurrent Hypertrophic Osteoarthropathy and Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor–Induced Inflammatory Arthritis in a Patient With NSCLC: A Case Report
Authors: Isaac K.S. Ng, Kaung Myat, Salil B. Singbal, Victor G.H. Lee, Manjari Lahiri, Sen Hee Tay, May Shuen Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hypertrophic osteoarthropathy (HOA) is a prototypical paraneoplastic arthritis associated with lung cancer, specifically NSCLC. With the increasing use of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), ICI-induced inflammatory arthritis (IA) is another important differential diagnosis for joint pain in patients with lung cancer. We report, to our knowledge, the first case of a patient with stage 4 lung adenocarcinoma and paraneoplastic HOA who subsequently developed ICI-IA and cytokine release syndrome while receiving the PD-1 inhibitor, pembrolizumab. Differentiating between HOA and ICI-IA is essential because of their differing treatment implications, requiring careful clinical assessment with adjunctive biochemical (including cytokine profiling), radiological, and synovial fluid investigations.

Introduction

Lung malignancy is the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, of which NSCLC is the predominant subtype. In metastatic or incurable disease, the advent of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has led to improved survival outcomes. Hypertrophic osteoarthropathy (HOA) represents a prototypical paraneoplastic arthritis in lung adenocarcinoma, characterized by the “triad” of digital clubbing, periostitis, and painful arthropathy. With increasing ICI use, immune-related adverse events, including inflammatory arthritis (IAs), are becoming prevalent. We herein report a patient with metastatic NSCLC on pembrolizumab-based immunochemotherapy who sequentially developed HOA and ICI-induced IA.

Case Presentation

A 43-year-old Chinese man, a current smoker (30 pack-years), with no medical history or family history of malignancy, was newly diagnosed with having NSCLC in May 2025 when he presented with chronic cough, hemoptysis, fever, and night sweats. Chest radiograph and computed tomography scan of the thorax revealed a right perihilar mass with right hilar and mediastinal lymphadenopathy. Biopsy of the lung mass revealed poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma with mucinous features, with KRAS G12A detected and PD-L1 expression tumor proportion score at 3%. Staging positron emission tomography scan revealed an extraperitoneal nodule that underwent biopsy revealing poorly differentiated carcinoma. Given the metastatic disease, he was commenced on palliative immunochemotherapy comprising carboplatin, pemetrexed and pembrolizumab. After treatment cycle 1, he reported symmetrical polyarthralgia involving the hands, wrists, elbows, knees, and ankles, which had already developed 4 months preceding his cancer diagnosis but acutely worsened post-cancer treatment. At this juncture, laboratory investigations revealed leukocytosis (white cell count at 50.29 × 109/L) and elevated C-reactive protein level (184.7 mg/L), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (67 mm/h), and ferritin (4275 μg/L) (Table 1). At the initial cancer diagnosis, he already had leukocytosis (44.98 × 109/L) and elevated C-reactive protein level (145 mg/L), which were attributed to systemic inflammation and a near-leukemoid reaction from metastatic cancer. Rheumatoid factor, anticyclic citrullinated peptide, and antinuclear antibody were negative. Plain radiograph results revealed a thick periosteal reaction along the metacarpals, phalanges, radius, ulna, tibia, and fibula (Fig. 1B). The patient declined arthrocentesis. He was diagnosed with having HOA and treated incrementally with analgesia (paracetamol, tramadol), nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (etoricoxib), oral corticosteroids (including a short course of rapidly tapered “pulsed” dexamethasone), and intravenous zoledronate, with partial response. After 3 weeks, he re-presented with severe joint pains, worsening effusions over the elbows, knees, and ankles, and fevers up to 40°C. Hemodynamic results were normal. Infective workup was unremarkable. His ferritin levels rose to 5281 μg/L, and cytokine profiling revealed elevated interleukin (IL)-6 (predominant), IL-2 receptor (soluble), and IL-10 (Table 1). Musculoskeletal ultrasound confirmed active synovitis in bilateral ankles. He was then diagnosed with having ICI-induced Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events grade 2 IA with grade 1 cytokine release syndrome (CRS)1 caused by pembrolizumab. He was treated with one dose of intravenous tocilizumab 400 mg (8 mg/kg), an IL-6 receptor antagonist (IL-6RA), as guided by his cytokine profiling, with rapid improvement of fever, IA, and inflammatory markers; the corticosteroid was successfully weaned off. At the time of writing, the patient was planned to continue maintenance pemetrexed and pembrolizumab, with 4-weekly tocilizumab infusions to manage the immune-related adverse events.

Discussion

HOA is a rare condition characterized by the “triad” of digital clubbing, periostitis (periosteal reaction) of the tubular long bones, and painful arthropathies. It is often paraneoplastic, most often associated with NSCLC, especially lung adenocarcinoma. It can rarely be found in hereditary conditions, extrathoracic malignancies, or benign conditions (e.g., chronic suppurative lung disease, cyanotic heart disease, and hepatobiliary conditions) that cause intrapulmonary shunting or disrupt pulmonary vasculature,2 leading to the circulation of megakaryocyte and platelet fragments to the peripheries. This inflicts endothelial injury and tissue hypoxia and triggers the release of inflammatory cytokines and growth factors that cause neoangiogenesis, increased vascular permeability, fibrogenesis, and osteogenesis.2,3 In lung cancer, a hypoxic tumor microenvironment further triggers hypoxia-inducible factor-1α release, contributing to vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) production.3

On radiograph, HOA appears as a benign periosteal reaction with thin, uniform, or thick, wavy cortical bone thickening. In equivocal cases, bone scintigraphy is the most sensitive imaging modality to identify subtle periosteal reactions.2

Treatment of HOA requires addressing the underlying disease, in this case, lung cancer. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and opioid analgesics are initially used for pain relief, failing which bisphosphonate and octreotide injections can be considered,2 with demonstrated efficacy related to VEGF inhibition. Interestingly, a recent study revealed that use of a VEGF inhibitor (bevacizumab) in combination with standard carboplatin and paclitaxel chemotherapy in hypoxia-inducible factor-1α and VEGF-producing NSCLC with intractable HOA led to symptomatic relief with a reduction in plasma VEGF levels.3

Although our patient was initially diagnosed with having HOA, he had only a partial treatment response to standard anti-inflammatory medications and bisphosphonates. Subsequent clinical and sonographic findings of synovitis, fever, hyperferritinemia, and significantly elevated IL-6 levels suggested the development of ICI-IA and CRS. Unlike HOA, ICI-IA is characterized by clinical synovitis, which can be confirmed by musculoskeletal Doppler ultrasound revealing synovial thickening and enhancement with increased vascularity. Although HOA can present with joint effusions, these are caused by a sympathetic reaction to periostitis rather than true synovial inflammation.4 Arthrocentesis can confirm the nature of joint effusions—where a synovial white blood cell count more than 2000/mm3 suggests IA, whereas less than 500/mm3 is expected in HOA-associated effusion.4

Most ICI-IAs are seronegative and can occur after a single dose of immunotherapy.5 Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events grading is based on symptom severity and functional impairment.5 Treatment of moderate-to-severe cases involves corticosteroids (prednisolone 0.5–1 mg/kg/day), with consideration of disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs or biologics such as tumor necrosis factor-α inhibitors or IL-6RA.5 In our patient, tocilizumab (an IL-6RA) was selected to treat concurrent ICI-IA and CRS, with the latter predominantly driven by IL-6 signaling.6

Conclusion

In summary, we report, to our knowledge, the first case of concurrent HOA and ICI-IA in a patient with metastatic NSCLC. With the rising use of ICIs in advanced NSCLC, this “double whammy” of paraneoplastic HOA and ICI-IA may become more prevalent. Careful clinical assessment, with adjunctive biochemical, radiological, and synovial fluid investigations, can help distinguish between these two conditions to guide treatment decisions.
