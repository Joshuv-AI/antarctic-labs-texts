Sand burial and precipitation enhancement changed status of tillerous clonal plant Stipa bungeana as dominated species in sandy grassland
Authors: Yuying Shao, Saina, Lei Wang, Qingguo Cui, Xuehua Ye, Zhenying Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractsBackgroundSand burial and precipitation drive the community structure and function of semi-arid grassland. Stipa bungeana is a tillerous clonal plant that often forms a community in the sandy grassland, however, it is not clear how sand burial, precipitation enhancement and their interaction on importance of S. bungeana in the plant community. In the semi-arid desertification grassland in northern China, we conducted a two-years field experiment with simulated sand burial (no sand burial, 2 cm and 5 cm sand burial) and precipitation enhancement (ambient precipitation, 1/7 and 2/7 precipitation enhancement) treatments, and the morphological characteristics and important value of S. bungeana were determined.ResultsSand burial and precipitation enhancement promoted the growth of S. bungeana tillers, but reduced the size and aboveground biomass of S. bungeana tuft, thus affecting the status of S. bungeana in the community. Sand burial and precipitation enhancement also significantly reduced plant density and aboveground biomass of all other species except S. bungeana.ConclusionsThese results revealed that S. bungeana may lose its status as the constructive species, due to the accumulation of the negative effects of sand burial. Our findings may be helpful to better understand the process of desertification, and will contribute to grassland management and vegetation restoration in semi-arid desert areas.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07262-7.Keywords: Community structure, Constructive species, Desertification, Important value

Introduction

Sand movement caused by climatic drought and strong winds is a common phenomenon in arid and semiarid desertified regions such as Mu Us sandy grassland in Inner Mongolia, China [1–3]. In these regions, strong wind drives the local horizontal movement of sand, often burying plants growing in these environments [4–7]. Sand burial has a complex and multifaceted impact on plant survival and growth, since it can alter soil conditions (e.g., temperature, moisture, pH, oxygen levels, stockpile density, and nutrient status) and the light conditions of the buried portion of the plant and plant seeds [8–13]. For example, after being buried by sand, soil moisture changes due to the reducing evaporation, and soil nutrient also changes because sand burial affects the litter decomposition [14–17].

Effects of sand burial on plants are limited by many factors, such as the depth of sand burial and the characteristics of plants. Previous studies have shown that different depths of sand burial have different effects on plant growth. For example, Wang et al. showed that moderate sand burial promoted plant survival and growth [18]. Moderate sand burial can compensate for reduced photosynthetic rates by increasing leaf area [19, 20], and increased the number of ramets and biomass production by promoting rhizome connections [21], and produced adventitious roots for greater nutrient uptake [22]. However, sand burial of plants beyond the tolerance limit is fatal, attributed to the physical suppression of plant growth caused by deep burial as well as insufficient photosynthetic area and lack of oxygen supply to the roots [23–25]. Wang et al. showed that both medium-depth and deep sand burial had strong negative effects on plant growth [26]. In addition, the effect of sand burial on plants was species-specific, since different plants respond differently to sand burial [27]. Ye et al. found that sand burial increased the cover of Allium mongolicum, Pennisetum centrasiaticum, and Leymus secalinus; while it decreased the cover of S. bungeana and Corispermum chinganicum [28]. And studies also show that species from densely buried habitats show better survival rates than those from less buried habitats under the same burial pressure [29, 30].

Precipitation plays a crucial role in plant growth [31, 32], especially in inland arid and semiarid regions, where precipitation is always scarce and uneven. Due to global warming, a series of changes have occurred in precipitation and its distribution [33, 34], which may directly or indirectly affect the plant survival and growth. It is important to study the effects of changed precipitation on plant growth. The impacts of changed precipitation on plants always vary among different plant species and/or life forms [6, 28, 35]. For example, increasing precipitation significantly increased the important value and richness of annual plants, while the height, coverage, density and important value of perennial grass S. breviflora decreased in a desert steppe [36]. While in a typical steppe, the cover and density of perennial grass S. bungeana are significantly positively correlated with precipitation [37, 38]. Moreover, with increasing precipitation, shrubs have a greater ability to absorb water than perennial herbs, which increases their productivity [39].

The specific response of different plant species or different plant functional types in a plant community to external disturbance may change their status in the community. For example, our previous studies showed that sand burial increased the ANPP of rhizome grass and decreased the ANPP of perennial herbs, bunchgrass and annual herbs [7]. Thus, it is possible that Stipa bungeana, as a constructive species in the grassland in Mu Us sandy grassland, may change its status in the plant community under the strong influence of sand burial disturbance and climate change. S. bungeana is a perennial bunchgrass which widely distributed in northwest, north, southwest and northeast China, as well as in Russia, Mongolia and Japan [40]. It is a tillerous clonal plant, and often constructs a community with bunchgrass Cleistogenes squarrosa, rhizome grasses Pennisetum centrasiaticum and Leymus secalinus, and perennial forb Allium mongolicum. Although there have many studies on the climate adaptation of S. bungeana at the individual level [41–43], and at the community level [6, 7, 12, 28], the effects of external disturbance such as sand burial and climate change such as precipitation enhancement, and their interaction on importance of S. bungeana in the plant community is still no clear.

In the current study, we conducted a field experiment with simultaneous sand burial and precipitation enhancement treatments to test their effects on the importance of S. bungeana. We paid special attention to the following two questions: (1) How does sand burial and precipitation enhancement affect the individual growth of S. bungeana in the field? (2) What is the effect of sand burial and precipitation enhancement on the status of S. bungeana in the community? We test two hypotheses: (1) moderate sand burial might stimulate the growth of S. bungeana tiller (ramet), but sand burial might restrict the growth of S. bungeana tuft (ramet population) due to the negative effects of sand burial on its ability of tillering propagation. (2) sand burial in combination with precipitation enhancement might reduce the importance of S. bungeana in the community due to its promoting effects on other plant species. Our results will contribute to understanding the adaptability of clonal plants to environmental change, and to vegetation restoration and grassland management in semi-arid desertification areas in the context of climate and environmental change.

Materials and methods

Study site

This study was carried out at the Ordos Sandland Ecological Research Station, Institute of Botany, Chinese Academy of Sciences (OSES, 39º02′ N, 109º51′ E, 1350 m a.s.l.), located in the northeast Mu Us sandy grassland, Ordos Plateau, China (Fig. 1). The climate is temperate continental, with low rainfall, extreme seasonal and diurnal temperature variations, windy spring and cold winter. The mean annual temperature is 6.2 ℃, with an average minimum of −16.9 ℃ in January and an average maximum of 28.1 ℃ in July. The mean annual precipitation is about 350 mm, with large inter-annual fluctuations in the range of 161–664 mm, of which more than 80% is concentrated in June—September. The average potential evaporation volume is 2300 mm (data from OSES meteorological stations, 2005–2015).

Diagram of the study site and experiment design. S0, S2 and S5 are no sand burial, 2 cm and 5 cm sand burial, respectively. W0, W1 and W2 are ambient precipitation, 1/7 and 2/7 precipitation enhancement, respectively

Mu Us sandy grassland is the main body of Ordos Plateau, and the Kubuqi sandy desert is located in the northern part of the Ordos Plateau. Sand movements are frequent and large in this region, due to the large area of aeolian sandy soil, the perennial strong winds and surface erosion caused by overgrazing. Typical grassland is the dominant ecosystem in the eastern part of the Ordos Plateau, which is strongly influenced by strong wind and sand dynamics, and is dominated by perennial graminoids (herbaceous monocots), such as Stipa bungeana Trin. and/or S. grandis P. Smirn., Leymus secalinus (Georgi) Tzvel., Pennisetum centrasiaticum Tzvel., and Cleistogenes squarrosa (Trin.) Keng, with the perennial forbs such as Allium mongolicum Regel, Heteropappus altaicus (Willd.) Novopokr., Artemisia sacrirum Ledeb, and Ixeris chinensis (Thunb.) Nakai, and the annual forbs such as Chenopodium aristatum L., Corispermum chinganicum Iljin., and the herbaceous legumes such as Astragalus melilotoides Pall., Lespedeza davurica (Laxm.) Schindl., and Oxytropis gracilima Bunge as accompanying species.

Plant material

Stipa bungeana Trin. is a perennial, tillering plant of the genus Stipa in the Poaceae family. The culms grow to a height of 20–60 cm, with a flowering and fruiting period from June to August. It is widely distributed across warm-temperate and temperate regions of China and serves as an important forage grass. S. bungeana has a broad ecological range and always serves as the dominant species in forming natural plant communities co-occurring with A. mongolicum, C. squarrosa and other species on the Ordos Plateau. The plant identification was conducted by Dr. Xuehua Ye based on Flora of China [44]. The voucher specimen of S. bungeana was deposited in the Herbarium of the Institute of Botany, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Voucher No. 02094635).

Experimental design

In September 2010, a 100 m × 100 m area with a natural S. bungeana steppe plant community was closed as the experimental site in OSES, and then 54 quadrats (1 m × 1 m) with spaced distances of at least 4 m were selected. A factorial design with three sand burial treatments (no sand burial, 2 cm and 5 cm sand burial) crossed with three precipitation enhancement treatments (ambient precipitation, 1/7 and 2/7 precipitation enhancement per rainfall event) was used in this experiment. A total of nine treatments were set up and all quadrats were randomly assigned to one of these nine treatments, with six replicates in each treatment (Fig. 1). More details about the experimental treatments are provided in Ye et al. [28]. These treatments simulate realistic scenarios for this region, i.e., a slight overall increase in precipitation and predominantly sandy dynamics [45, 46].

Measurements of plant community traits

After two years of experimental treatments, the experiment was ended on September 6, 2012. Within each quadrat, species richness (number of species), plant cover (including total plant cover and plant cover of each species), plant height (maximum plant height to best reflect potential growth capacity) and density of each species were recorded. Then, all living aboveground biomass was separately harvested by species, and was dried to a constant weight at 65 °C and its dry weight was measured in the laboratory.

The frequency of each species is calculated as the number of quadrats where the species occurs divided by the total number of quadrats. And relative density, relative cover, relative biomass, relative frequency and relative height are calculated by dividing the density, cover, biomass, frequency and height of each species by the sum of which of all species in the quadrat, separately. Then the important value of S. bungeana was calculated to quantify the relative importance and ecological dominance of S. bungeana within the community, which was a comprehensive quantitative metric and also a core indicator in plant community analysis [47]. Ten leaves of S. bungeana were sampled from 3–5 plant tufts, and leaf length and leaf area were carried out using the image measurement method. The field-retrieved leaves of S. bungeana were placed on A4 paper, keeping the leaves flat, and digitized using a scanner. The processed image of the leaf to be measured was imported into Image J for measurement. Then, all the leaves were dried to a constant weight at 65 °C and weighed in the laboratory.

Statistical analysis

Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to examine the main and interaction effects of sand burial and precipitation on all traits of S. bungeana, as well as traits of the community. Multiple comparisons of the effects of sand burial or precipitation enhancement treatments were analyzed through Duncan’s test. The relationships between each trait were analyzed by univariate general linear models. All statistical analyses of the data were performed in IBM SPSS Statistics 26 (Armonk, New York, USA). Statistical graphs were plotted in GraphPad Prism 8.0.1.

Structural equation modeling (SEM) was employed to examine the direct and indirect effects of sand burial and precipitation enhancement on the important value of S. bungeana within the community, conducted using R 4.4.2. The path coefficients were derived using the maximum likelihood estimation technique. Subsequently, by building a model that incorporated all observed indicators and possible pathways, we established their hypothesized relationships. Finally, non-significant pathways were systematically removed to refine the model until the optimal model was obtained. For enhanced presentation, the structural equation model diagram was refined with Microsoft PowerPoint, and the standardized total effects were visualized using the ggplot2 package.

Sand burial had significant effects on the tuft size of S. bungeana tuft, with no significant effects on its aboveground biomass; while precipitation had no significant effects on both tuft size and aboveground biomass of S. bungeana tuft (Table 1). However, sand burial and precipitation enhancement had significant interactions on both tuft size and aboveground biomass of S. bungeana tuft (Table 1). Under ambient precipitation treatments, sand burial first increased and then decreased both the tuft size and aboveground biomass of S. bungeana tuft, while under 1/7 and 2/7 precipitation enhancement treatments, both the tuft size and aboveground biomass of S. bungeana tuft showed a gradually decreasing trend with the increase of sand burial depth (Fig. 2e and f).

Effects of sand burial and precipitation enhancement on the status of S. bungeana in the plant community

Sand burial significantly affected plant density and aboveground biomass (with or without S. bungeana), plant cover, special richness and Shannon–Wiener diversity index of the plant community, as well as plant cover of S. bungeana in the community; while sand burial had marginal effects on important value, plant density and aboveground biomass of S. bungeana (Table 1). precipitation enhancement had no significant effects on all the traits of the S. bungeana population and of the plant community, but precipitation with sand burial had a significant interaction on the aboveground biomass of S. bungeana in the plant community (Table 1).

Sand burial first increased and then decreased the important value of S. bungeana with ambient precipitation (Fig. 3); Under the condition of 1/7 precipitation enhancement or 2 cm sand burial treatments, sand burial or precipitation marginally decreased the important value of S. bungeana (Fig. 3).

Plant density of S. bungeana does not change significantly under the precipitation enhancement and/or sand burial treatments (Fig. 4a). However, the plant density of all species except S. bungeana was sensitive to sand burial (P = 0.000, Table 1), and under 1/7 and 2/7 precipitation enhancement treatments, it showed a significantly decreasing trend with the increase of sand burial depth (Fig. 4b). Sand burial decreased plant cover of the S. bungeana population with ambient and 1/7 precipitation enhancement, while it had no significant effects with 2/7 precipitation enhancement treatment (Fig. 4c). However, sand burial significantly decreased the total plant cover only under 1/7 precipitation enhancement treatment (Fig. 4d). Under ambient precipitation conditions, the aboveground biomass of S. bungeana showed a trend of first increasing and then decreasing; with 1/7 precipitation enhancement, it shows a gradually decreasing trend; and with the increase of sand burial depth, the aboveground biomass tends to be stable under 2/7 precipitation enhancement treatment (Fig. 4e). Under all three precipitation treatments, the aboveground biomass of all species except S. bungeana significantly decreased with the increase of sand burial depth, while precipitation had no significant effects on it under all three sand burial treatments (Fig. 4f). Factors influencing the importance of S. bungeana in the community

Important value of S. bungeana showed an extremely significant positive correlation with aboveground biomass per tuft, tuft size, plant density, plant cover and aboveground biomass of S. bungeana (Fig. S3). While importance value of S. bungeana showed a significant negative correlation with the plant density, aboveground biomass of other species, total plant density, and species richness in the community (Fig. S3).

The SEM showed that sand burial had a positive and direct effect on the important value of S. bungeana, but sand burial also had a negative indirect effect on the important value of S. bungeana via the negative effect on its plant cover (Fig. 5a). Both precipitation and sand burial had positive direct effects on the plant height of S. bungeana. However, the sand burial only had a positive and direct effect on the leaf area. In addition, plant density, cover and aboveground biomass of S. bungeana all have a positive direct effect on the important values (Fig. 5a). According to the total effect plot, plant cover of S. bungeana has the largest direct effect on its importance value and the aboveground biomass has the largest indirect effect on its importance value (Fig. 5b).

Discussion

The status of plant species in a community depends on their own growth and the growth of other plants in the community. When external disturbances occur, their status in the community tends to change because of the interspecific response of different plant species to the disturbances. Our results showed that sand burial and precipitation enhancement promoted the growth of a single S. bungeana tiller, which was reflected in the increase of leaf length, leaf area, leaf dry weight and plant height of S. bungeana, but decreased the tuft size and aboveground biomass of single S. bungeana tuft, and in turn affected the status of S. bungeana in the community, consistent with our hypotheses.

Sand burial may alleviate temperature fluctuation, improve soil fertility, enhance microbial activity, reduce water evaporation and enhance water retention capacity [12, 24, 48, 49]. A lot of studies showed that moderated sand burial could accelerate plant growth, especially the vertical growth of plants [50–52]. In the present study, sand burial significantly increased the leaf length, area, dry weight and also plant height of S. bungeana, indicating a positive effect of sand burial on the growth of S. bungeana tillers. While with no sand burial, the leaf length, plant height and leaf area of S. bungeana increased significantly with precipitation, consistent with our general understanding that water generally promotes plant growth in semi-arid regions. Previous studies have shown that precipitation enhancement significantly promoted the growth of perennial herbs [31, 53, 54], and also annual herbs [36, 37, 55].

However, as an aggregate of tillers, the responses of S. bungeana tuft to sand burial and precipitation enhancement are inconsistent. In the case of ambient precipitation, the tuft size and aboveground biomass per S. bungeana tuft show an inverted V-shape with the increase of sand burial, which further proves that plants can grow better under slight sand burial, which is consistent with the results of previous studies [56]. The negative effect of heavy sand burial on the growth of S. bungeana tuft may be attributed to the effect of heavy sand burial on the germination of tillers, which reduces the number of tillers and thus the size and biomass of S. bungeana tuft [49]. While precipitation enhancement significantly increased the aboveground biomass of S. bungeana with a marginal increase in tuft size under no sand burial treatment.

The balance between positive effects of shallow burial and negative effects of deep burial can change depending on the amount of precipitation. Slight sand burial accompanying nutrient input moderately promoted the growth of S. bungeana tuft in present study. However, with the precipitation enhancement treatment, the intensified leaching effect induced by the sand layer accelerates the translocation of nutrients from topsoil to deeper layers, suggesting the positive effect of nutrient input might be diminished. While the suppression of tiller production by sand burial might be little affected by rainfall, resulting a significant interaction between precipitation enhancement and sand burial in present study. The research results indicated that climate change (such as altered precipitation patterns) may exacerbate the detrimental effects of sand burial on grassland species in sand area.

Our results of SEM confirmed that the positive effects of sand burial and precipitation enhancement on the growth of S. bungeana tillers do not extend to the population level of this species. No direct or indirect effect was found between the growth characteristics of tillers and the population traits in S. bungeana in the present study. This may be because S. bungeana, as a tillerous clonal plant, expanded more by producing a large number of tillers, and the number of tillers plays a decisive role in the size of the plant. Additionally, sand burial directly affected the importance value of S. bungeana, which may be attributed to its influence on the importance of other species—our experiments demonstrated that sand burial significantly reduced both the density and aboveground biomass of other species within the community.

Sand burial also significantly decreased plant density and total aboveground biomass of other species except S. bungeana in the community, especially due to the negative effects of sand burial on annual herbaceous plants [6]. The effects of sand burial on S. bungeana itself and other plant species ultimately determine the importance of S. bungeana in the community.

Sand burial first increased and then decreased the important value of S. bungeana with ambient precipitation. This is because light sand burial promoted the growth (higher plant cover and aboveground biomass) of the tillering grass S. bungeana, while heavy sand burial inhibited its growth. And for other plant species, both light and heavy sand burial showed inhibitory effects. Under 1/7 precipitation enhancement treatment, sand burial marginally decreased the important value of S. bungeana in the present study. And under 2/7 precipitation enhancement treatment, the important value of S. bungeana kept a low lever under all three sand burial treatments. It is likely because the intensified leaching effect induced by increased precipitation weakened the facilitative effect of slight sand burial. Sand burial exerts comprehensive effects on plant habitats: it increases soil nutrients, raises surface soil temperature, and alters soil infiltration characteristics [57]. These modified habitat conditions differentially influence various plant species within the community. For shallow-rooted species like S. bungeana, while nutrient enrichment may promote growth to some extent, the enhanced water infiltration and topsoil nutrient loss under precipitation enhancement conditions ultimately inhibit their growth. In contrast, our previous studies have demonstrated that sand burial promoted the growth of some deep-rooted, non-dominant plants within the community, such as rhizomatous plants L. secalinus and P. centrasiaticum [6, 28]. As the duration of sand burial increases, the importance values of these species in the community might become progressively more significant. Such species-specific responses to sand burial and precipitation enhancement collectively drive changes in the importance value of S. bungeana within the plant community. Our results showed that sand burial and precipitation enhancement treatments were not conducive to maintaining the dominant position of S. bungeana. Considering that sand burials in nature tend to occur multiple times and have a cumulative effect, S. bungeana in wind-blown areas may lose its dominant status in the community due to frequent wind-blown sand activities.

Precipitation enhancement had no significant effects on species richness and the Shannon–Wiener diversity index in the present study, while severe sand burial significantly decreased them. This is consistent with previous studies, where high-intensity sand burial will reduce the soil air permeability and light, thus inhibiting seed germination and growth, and then reducing species richness [56, 58–61]. In view of the different responses of plants of different life forms to sand burial [28], some annual herbaceous plants may be the first to exit the plant community when the plant community is affected by sand burial, because sand burial may change the temperature and humidity condition of the topsoil, thus limiting the germination of seeds [12, 48]. However, with the accumulation of effects of sand burial, the tillerious clone plants such as S. bungeana and C. squarrosa in Mu Us sandy grassland may also withdraw from the community, due to the negative effects of sand burial on the germination of tillers and/or on photosynthesis of plants. As for the S. bungeana community, the withdrawal of S. bungeana as a founding species means a complete change in community structure.

To study the effects of sand burial on plant community structure, especially on the constructive species, may be helpful for us to better understand the process of desertification. At the same time, we are clearly aware that 2 years is a short experimental period, especially for perennials. For example, experimental treatments did not affect the plant density of S. bungeana tufts in the present study. In addition, our experiment is a one-time sand burial experiment, and cannot reflect the cumulative effect of multiple sand burials on plant growth. Subsequent studies need to pay more attention to the long-term and cumulative effects of sand burial on plant community structure and function.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrated that sand burial and precipitation enhancement had positive effects on the growth of S. bungeana tillers, but had negative effects on the growth of S. bungeana tufts. Sand burial and precipitation enhancement increased the leaf length, leaf area, leaf dry weight, and plant height of S. bungeana tillers, but heavy sand burial decreased the size and aboveground biomass of S. bungeana tufts, resulting the low importance value of S. bungeana. These results suggested that S. bungeana may lose its status as the constructive species in Mu Us sandy grassland, due to the accumulation of the negative effects of sand burial. Our results may be helpful to better understand the process of desertification. And more studies are needed to pay more attention to the long-term and cumulative effects of sand burial on plant community structure and function.
