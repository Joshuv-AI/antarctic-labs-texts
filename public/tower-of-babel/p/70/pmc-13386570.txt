Symptom burden, comfort, and self-care agency in diabetic hemodialysis patients: a cross-sectional study with mediation analysis
Authors: Hatice Ağralı
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHemodialysis patients with diabetes experience substantial symptom burden that is significantly associated with lower levels of self-care behaviors. While comfort-based nursing interventions have been shown to reduce symptom severity, the specific mechanisms through which symptom burden relates to self-care agency remain unclear. This study aimed to examine the relationships among symptom burden, comfort, and self-care agency in hemodialysis patients with diabetes, and to determine whether comfort mediates the association between symptom burden and self-care agency.MethodsA descriptive, cross-sectional study was conducted at a hemodialysis unit in Türkiye between May and December 2024. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Health Sciences at Süleyman Demirel University (Decision No: 75/20, Date: 04/29/2024), and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. A total of 125 hemodialysis patients with diabetes (mean age 66.31 ± 10.45 years) were recruited using convenience sampling. Data were collected using the Dialysis Symptom Index, Hemodialysis Comfort Scale, and Self-Care Agency Scale. Mediation analysis using the Baron and Kenny approach with bootstrap confidence intervals was performed.ResultsSymptom burden and self-care agency were significantly negatively correlated (r = − 0.402, p < 0.001), representing a moderate-sized association. Comfort showed no significant correlation with symptom burden (r = − 0.126, p = 0.160) or self-care agency (r = 0.117, p = 0.194). No significant mediating effect of comfort was observed in the relationship between symptom burden and self-care agency (indirect effect β = 0.005, 95% CI: −0.014 to 0.024). Symptom burden was directly and negatively associated with self-care agency (β = −0.407, p < 0.001). Taken together, these findings do not provide empirical support for the hypothesized mediation mechanism in which comfort serves as an intermediary in the pathway from symptom burden to self-care agency. The direct association between symptom burden and self-care agency was significant and unchanged after the inclusion of comfort in the model, further confirming the absence of a mediating role.ConclusionsSymptom burden was directly and negatively associated with self-care agency, independent of comfort levels, in diabetic hemodialysis patients. This relationship was not mediated by comfort. Healthcare professionals should prioritize direct, targeted symptom management interventions to improve self-care behaviors, rather than relying primarily on general comfort-enhancement approaches.

Background

Diabetes mellitus represents one of the most significant global health challenges of the 21st century, with its prevalence steadily increasing worldwide. According to the International Diabetes Federation’s data from 2025, approximately 588.7 million adults worldwide live with diabetes, with projections indicating this number will reach 852.5 million by 2050 [1]. Diabetic nephropathy, a major microvascular complication, is a leading cause of chronic kidney disease and often progresses to end-stage renal disease (ESRD), necessitating hemodialysis (HD) [2]. For patients with diabetes, the HD experience is compounded by the need to manage both metabolic and renal complexities, placing them at higher risk for severe physical symptoms [3].

The symptom burden in this population is substantial. Hemodialysis patients experience a high prevalence of distressing physical and psychological symptoms, including fatigue, pain, pruritus, sleep disturbances, anxiety, and depression [4–6]. A 2024 study confirmed the high burden in this population [7], and a systematic review emphasized the complexities of symptom management in hemodialysis patients, particularly those with comorbidities like diabetes [8]. For instance, over 80% of hemodialysis patients report significant fatigue, which severely limits daily functioning [9]. Having diabetes is an independent risk factor that exacerbates symptom burden, leading to poorer health outcomes and increased healthcare utilization [10]. This heavy symptom burden creates a significant barrier to effective self-management.

Self-care agency, a central concept in Orem’s Self-Care Deficit Nursing Theory, refers to an individual’s acquired ability to perform self-care activities [11]. In diabetic hemodialysis patients, self-care involves adherence to dietary and fluid regimens, medication management, blood glucose monitoring, foot care, and active participation in dialysis treatment. Strong evidence demonstrates that higher self-care agency is directly linked to improved clinical outcomes [12–14]. A 2025 study involving 306 hemodialysis patients found a significant negative association between symptom burden and self-care efficacy [15].

Katharine Kolcaba’s Comfort Theory provides a potential explanatory framework [16]. The theory posits that comfort is a fundamental human need encompassing relief, ease, and transcendence across physical, psychospiritual, sociocultural, and environmental contexts. Crucially, the theory proposes a specific mediation pathway: when patients experience distressing symptoms (an unmet need), their overall comfort is reduced; in turn, a reduced comfort state diminishes the patient’s capacity and motivation to engage in health-seeking behaviors, including self-care. Thus, comfort is theorized to serve as an intermediary—a mediator—in the pathway from symptom burden to self-care agency. Testing this mediation pathway empirically is the primary aim of this study. Studies applying this theory in the hemodialysis setting have shown that comfort-based nursing interventions can reduce symptom severity and improve well-being [17–20].

While the literature establishes links between symptom burden and self-care, and between comfort interventions and symptom relief, whether comfort actually mediates the relationship between symptom burden and self-care agency remains unknown. To address this specific theoretical and empirical gap, the present study proposes and tests a mediation model in which comfort serves as the potential intermediary mechanism. By investigating the relationships among these three variables, this study aims to generate evidence that can refine nursing theory and guide the development of more targeted interventions to improve self-care in this vulnerable population.

This study was guided by the following research questions: (1) What is the relationship between symptom burden and self-care agency? (2) What is the relationship between symptom burden and comfort? (3) What is the relationship between comfort and self-care agency? (4) Does comfort mediate the association between symptom burden and self-care agency in hemodialysis patients with diabetes?

Methods

Study design

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Health Sciences at Süleyman Demirel University (Decision No: 75/20, Date: 04/29/2024) and was conducted in strict accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. A descriptive, cross-sectional, correlational design was used to examine the relationships among symptom burden, comfort, and self-care agency. A mediation analysis approach was used to investigate whether comfort mediates the relationship between symptom burden and self-care agency.

Setting and participants

The study was conducted at a hemodialysis unit in a city hospital in southwestern Türkiye between May and December 2024. Participants were recruited using convenience sampling. Inclusion criteria were: (1) confirmed diagnosis of diabetes mellitus for at least one year; (2) receiving maintenance hemodialysis three times per week for at least 3 months; (3) aged 18 years or older; (4) able to communicate in Turkish; and (5) provided written informed consent. Exclusion criteria included severe psychiatric/cognitive disorder or severe sensory impairments preventing questionnaire completion.

Sample size was determined a priori using G*Power (version 3.1.9.7) for mediation models. Based on the recommendations of Fritz and MacKinnon (2007) [21] and assuming a medium effect size (f² = 0.15), informed by prior studies examining symptom burden and self-care in similar populations, a minimum of 104 participants was required to achieve 80% statistical power at a two-tailed alpha of 0.05. To account for potential missing data, the recruitment target was 135 patients. Of 135 eligible patients invited, 125 provided complete data (response rate: 92.6%).

Data collection

Data were collected by the primary researcher, who was not involved in the direct care of participants, to minimize coercion and response bias. Questionnaires were completed during hemodialysis sessions while patients were hemodynamically stable. The average completion time was approximately 20–25 min.

Instruments

A researcher-developed Demographic and Clinical Characteristics Form collected data on age, gender, education, marital status, diabetes duration, hemodialysis duration, comorbidities, and diabetes education status.

Dialysis Symptom Index (DSI)

Developed by Weisbord et al. [22], the DSI assesses the prevalence and severity of 30 common symptoms in dialysis patients on a 5-point Likert scale. Total scores range from 30 to 150, with higher scores indicating greater symptom burden. The Turkish version [23] has demonstrated good reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.83); in the present study, α = 0.91.

Hemodialysis Comfort Scale (HDCS)

Developed by Şahin Orak et al. [24] based on Kolcaba’s Comfort Theory, this 9-item instrument assesses physical, psychospiritual, and sociocultural comfort. Items are rated on a 5-point scale; scores range from 9 to 45. In our sample, Cronbach’s α = 0.85.

Self-Care Agency Scale (SCAS)

Originally developed by Kearney and Fleischer [25] based on Orem’s theory and validated in Turkish by Nahcivan [26], this 35-item instrument assesses perceived self-care capacity on a 5-point scale (range: 35–175). In the current study, Cronbach’s α = 0.89.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0. Descriptive statistics, Pearson correlations, t-tests, and one-way ANOVA were used. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d and partial eta-squared (η²). Mediation analysis followed the Baron and Kenny [27] approach supplemented by Hayes’ [28] bootstrapping method (5000 samples, 95% CIs).

Prior to the main analyses, we examined the associations between key demographic and clinical variables (age, gender, education level, diabetes duration, hemodialysis duration, and number of comorbidities) and the three main study variables. None of these variables showed a statistically significant correlation with all three main variables simultaneously; therefore, they were not included as covariates in the mediation model to maintain parsimony given the sample size. We acknowledge this as a study limitation. Regarding mediation testing: although the Baron and Kenny (1986) approach traditionally requires significant associations along each path, contemporary methodologists (Hayes, 2018) recommend evaluating the indirect effect directly via bootstrapping, as non-significant individual paths do not necessarily preclude a meaningful indirect effect. Accordingly, we proceeded with the bootstrapped mediation analysis.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 125 patients participated. The mean age was 66.31 ± 10.45 years (range: 36–88). The majority were male (56.8%) and married (73.6%). Most had primary school education or less (68.0%). Mean diabetes duration was 17.51 ± 8.22 years; mean hemodialysis duration was 5.19 ± 4.53 years. Hypertension (84.0%) and cardiovascular disease (48.8%) were the most common comorbidities. Only 20% had received formal diabetes education (Table 1).

Relationships among study variables

Pearson correlation analysis revealed a statistically significant, moderate negative correlation between symptom burden and self-care agency (r = − 0.402, p < 0.001; Cohen’s benchmark: moderate = 0.30–0.50). There was a weak, non-significant negative correlation between symptom burden and comfort (r = − 0.126, p = 0.160), and a weak, non-significant positive correlation between comfort and self-care agency (r = 0.117, p = 0.194) (Table 3).

Mediation analysis

Mediation analysis was performed to test whether comfort mediated the association between symptom burden and self-care agency (Table 4; Fig. 1). Symptom burden had a significant total association with self-care agency (β = −0.402, p < 0.001). When comfort was included in the model, the direct association between symptom burden and self-care agency remained significant (β = −0.407, p < 0.001). No significant mediating effect of comfort was observed in the relationship between symptom burden and self-care agency (indirect effect β = 0.005, 95% CI: −0.014 to 0.024). The proportion of the total effect mediated by comfort was only 1.2%. These findings indicate that comfort did not significantly mediate the association between symptom burden and self-care agency in this cross-sectional sample.

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the relationships among symptom burden, comfort, and self-care agency in hemodialysis patients with diabetes, and to determine whether comfort mediates this relationship.

Conceptual Interpretation of the Null Mediation Finding. The null mediation finding carries important theoretical implications that extend beyond the statistical result. Kolcaba’s Comfort Theory (2003) proposes a sequential pathway in which distressing symptoms represent unmet needs that reduce a patient’s comfort state, and this reduced comfort in turn diminishes the patient’s capacity and motivation to engage in health-seeking behaviors, including self-care. The present findings did not support this sequential pathway: neither the path from symptom burden to comfort (r = − 0.126, p = 0.160) nor the path from comfort to self-care agency (r = 0.117, p = 0.194) reached statistical significance, and the bootstrapped indirect effect was negligible (β = 0.005, 95% CI: −0.014 to 0.024). This suggests that, in this clinical population, comfort does not function as the intermediary mechanism through which symptom burden exerts its influence on self-care. An alternative conceptual interpretation is that comfort and impaired self-care may be parallel, independent consequences of symptom burden, rather than sequentially linked outcomes. That is, high symptom burden may simultaneously reduce comfort and impair self-care agency through separate pathways, without comfort serving as the mechanism connecting them. This interpretation is theoretically consistent with Orem’s Self-Care Deficit Nursing Theory (2001), which proposes that when the demands placed on an individual by illness and its symptoms exceed that person’s available self-care capacity, a self-care deficit arises directly. In this model, the pathway from symptom burden to impaired self-care is direct and does not require an intervening variable such as comfort. The observation that symptom burden explained a significant portion of variance in self-care agency in our data (r = − 0.402, p < 0.001) independently of comfort provides empirical support for this direct-effect model. Taken together, these findings suggest that Kolcaba’s Comfort Theory, while clinically valuable for guiding nursing interventions, may require adaptation when applied to populations with severe, chronic, and multidimensional symptom profiles. Specifically, the mediation pathway from comfort to self-care cannot be assumed a priori in this context but must be empirically tested. Future research employing longitudinal designs, larger samples, and multidimensional comfort measures will be essential to determine whether and under what conditions comfort functions as a mediating mechanism in this population.

The primary finding is that no significant mediating effect of comfort was observed. Instead, symptom burden was directly and negatively associated with self-care agency. This finding raises questions about the direct applicability of Kolcaba’s Comfort Theory in this specific population and context, and suggests that further empirical testing of the theoretical pathway is warranted. Our data suggest a more direct pathway: the presence of burdensome symptoms, regardless of the patient’s perceived comfort level, is associated with hindered self-care. This is consistent with recent research emphasizing the direct association of symptom clusters with patient outcomes and self-efficacy [15, 29].

It must be explicitly acknowledged that the lack of significant bivariate associations between symptom burden and comfort (r = − 0.126, p = 0.160) and between comfort and self-care agency (r = 0.117, p = 0.194) substantially limits the conceptual robustness of the proposed mediation model. The absence of these foundational associations means that the preconditions for a meaningful mediation mechanism, as theorized by Kolcaba (2003), were not empirically supported in this sample.

The non-significant paths from symptom burden to comfort (r = − 0.126, p = 0.160) and from comfort to self-care agency (r = 0.117, p = 0.194) are critical to interpreting the null mediation finding. Several explanations are plausible. First, the Hemodialysis Comfort Scale, while validated, may not capture dimensions of comfort most directly affected by the specific symptom constellation in this population. Second, patients who have lived with high symptom burden for many years may develop psychological adaptation mechanisms, maintaining stable perceived comfort despite fluctuating symptom severity. Third, self-care agency may be more directly governed by functional capacity and disease-specific knowledge than by general comfort states. Future studies employing longitudinal designs and multidimensional comfort measures are needed to clarify these relationships.

Several mechanisms could explain the direct association between symptom burden and self-care agency. First, the physical and cognitive energy required to manage a high symptom burden may leave patients with insufficient resources to engage in the complex tasks of self-care. Second, psychological symptoms such as anxiety and depression can diminish motivation, problem-solving skills, and self-efficacy [12, 30, 31]. Third, the unpredictable nature of symptoms in hemodialysis may be associated with disrupted routines, making consistent self-care challenging [32].

Our study also found a significant negative correlation between symptom burden and self-care agency, consistent with a large body of prior research [4, 5, 10]. The finding that patients who received diabetes education had lower symptom burden and higher self-care agency is particularly noteworthy, suggesting that structured educational interventions can empower patients to better manage their symptoms [33].

It is important to acknowledge that the quantitative relationships identified in this study do not fully capture the lived experience of symptom management for hemodialysis patients. Qualitative research has highlighted that patients experience symptoms not in isolation, but as interconnected clusters of distress, and that supportive relationships with healthcare providers and family members serve as critical resources for emotional resilience and coping [8]. These insights enrich our understanding of why symptom burden may directly be associated with impaired self-care agency, extending beyond what quantitative mediation models can reveal.

The complexity of symptom management in hemodialysis patients may itself contribute to cognitive overload, further undermining self-care capacity. Symptom simplification strategies—systematic approaches to reducing the cognitive burden of self-monitoring by identifying and prioritizing the most clinically significant symptoms—represent a promising direction for clinical nursing assessment [34]. Such strategies could serve as a practical implementation tool for the targeted symptom management approach recommended in this study.

Beyond traditional assessment methods, emerging computational approaches offer promising avenues for identifying patients at highest risk of severe symptom burden. Machine learning-based predictive models, such as those utilizing the XGBoost algorithm, have been proposed to classify symptom burden severity in hemodialysis patients, potentially enabling more precise, customized nursing interventions [35]. Integrating such data-driven tools into routine clinical practice could complement the evidence-based symptom management strategies recommended in this study.

Symptom network analysis and computational simulation modeling offer a sophisticated approach to identifying ‘central node’ symptoms—those most strongly interconnected with other symptoms and most influential in driving overall burden [36]. In our sample, fatigue, sleep disturbances, and pruritus emerged as particularly prevalent and severe. Targeting these central symptoms may produce cascading benefits across the symptom network, potentially yielding greater improvements in self-care capacity than generalized intervention approaches.

The capacity to engage in self-care is also shaped by a patient’s propensity to seek help and communicate symptoms effectively to healthcare providers. Help-seeking behavior (HSB) acts as a prerequisite for accessing support that enables symptom management. Validated tools such as the HSB-HD scale have been developed specifically for hemodialysis patients to assess these behavioral patterns, providing clinicians with a means to identify those who are struggling to disclose symptoms and take timely action [37].

The translation of evidence-based symptom management strategies into routine practice increasingly involves digital technologies. Digital symptom management interventions for patients with chronic kidney disease have demonstrated potential for enhancing patient engagement and self-management when designed in a theory-driven and patient-centered manner [38]. Future research should evaluate the effectiveness of such digital tools in directly reducing symptom burden and enhancing self-care agency in the diabetic hemodialysis population.

Finally, it should be acknowledged that the mediation model tested in this study is better understood as exploratory rather than confirmatory in nature. Given the cross-sectional design and the absence of significant individual paths, the present analysis cannot confirm or refute the theoretical pathway proposed by Kolcaba (2003). Rather, the findings highlight the need for longitudinal, theoretically driven studies with larger samples and multidimensional outcome measures to rigorously test this mediation hypothesis. The current study represents a first empirical step in mapping these relationships in a specific, underserved clinical population.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design does not allow for the determination of causality; longitudinal studies are needed to confirm the direction and temporal sequence of the observed associations. Second, the use of a convenience sample from a single center may limit generalizability. Third, all data were based on self-report, subject to recall and social desirability biases. Fourth, the mediation analysis was conducted despite the non-significant bivariate associations between comfort and the other two main variables. While contemporary bootstrapping methods (Hayes, 2018) do not require significant individual paths as a prerequisite for testing indirect effects, this represents a methodological limitation that should be interpreted with caution. The null mediation finding may reflect a true absence of a mediating effect, or may be attributable to the measurement properties of the comfort scale or insufficient statistical power to detect small indirect effects. Fifth, key demographic and clinical variables (age, comorbidities, duration of illness) were not included as covariates in the mediation model; their potential confounding influence cannot be ruled out.

Conclusions

This study provides new evidence on the relationships among symptom burden, comfort, and self-care agency in hemodialysis patients with diabetes. The key finding is that symptom burden is directly and negatively associated with self-care agency, and this relationship was not mediated by comfort. This finding raises questions about the direct applicability of Kolcaba’s Comfort Theory in this specific context and highlights the need for further empirical testing of the proposed theoretical pathway. Clinically, the results underscore the need for routine, systematic assessment of symptom burden and for targeted, evidence-based interventions to manage specific symptoms—particularly fatigue, sleep disturbances, and pruritus [39]—that most directly impair self-care capacity.

A sustainable pathway for systematic symptom assessment lies in the implementation of electronic Patient-Reported Outcome Measures (ePROMs), which enable real-time, patient-centered monitoring of symptom burden and self-care capacity. Evidence suggests that implementing ePROMs in chronic kidney disease settings is associated with benefits including more personalized treatment plans; however, important barriers such as digital literacy gaps and infrastructure requirements must be addressed [40]. Future implementation science research should evaluate the feasibility and effectiveness of ePROM-guided symptom management in diabetic hemodialysis patients.

While promoting comfort remains a core nursing goal, interventions must also equip patients with the skills and resources to overcome functional limitations. Structured patient education appears to be a key strategy. Future research should explore other potential mediators of the symptom burden–self-care relationship, such as self-efficacy, resilience, or social support, and test targeted symptom management interventions in longitudinal and experimental designs.
