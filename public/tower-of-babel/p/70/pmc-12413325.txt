Factors influencing satisfaction with preoperative education among elective surgical patients at Dessie hospital, ethiopia, 2024: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Sitina Yimam Getahun, Afework Edmealem, Ali Seid Eshetu, Endalew Minwuye Andargie, Yonas Fissha Adem
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPre-operative patient education is a key part of nursing consideration aimed at helping patients to clarify information about their operation, and what happens after surgery, based on patient need, level of knowledge, and patient condition. In every facet of healthcare, patient satisfaction is becoming a bigger priority. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the levels of patient satisfaction toward preoperative education and associated factors among adult elective surgical patients in Dessie Comprehensive Specialized Hospital.MethodsA facility-based cross-sectional study was employed among a total of 440 study subjects selected by a consecutive sampling technique in Dessie Comprehensive Specialized Hospital. Data were collected by using a pretested structured questionnaire and Data were checked, coded, and entered into Epi data version 4.6 and exported to SPSS version 26 for analysis. Both bivariable and multivariable binary logistic regression models were used to identify the factors associated with the outcome variable. Both crude odds ratios (COR) and adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with a 95% confidence level (CI) were computed, and a P-value < 0.05, in the final model, was considered statistically significant.ResultA total of 426 adult elective surgical patients participated in this study with a response rate of 96.8%. This study revealed that 82.2%, 95% CI: (78.2%, 85.7%) of patients had good satisfaction towards preoperative education. In this study, residents in urban areas (AOR = 4.303; 95% CI: 1.487–12.448), pre-operative education about surgery (AOR = 8.620; 95% CI: (3.672–20.235), insurance users (AOR = 6.165; 95% CI: 2.146–17.717), previous surgery (AOR = 15.416; 95% CI: 6.537–36.354), and patients’ duration of illness in days (AOR = 5.326; 95% CI: 1.839–15.430) were all significantly associated with satisfaction toward preoperative education.ConclusionPatient satisfaction with perioperative surgical services was higher than that reported in earlier studies conducted in Ethiopia. Health professionals need to emphasize the rural community, patients with a higher duration of illness, patients with previous surgery, and preoperative fear.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00423-025-03802-9.

Introduction

Preoperative education is delivered by healthcare professionals worldwide to help patients prepare for surgery. Patients can provide feedback about a hospital’s communication, educational efforts, and pain management procedures, as well as whether they received respect and dignity in their care [1]. Recently, the focus of patient education has evolved from illness pathophysiology and management to disease prevention, patient development, and family treatment, as well as the patient’s family’s participation in care management [2]. Preoperative patient education is defined as The provision of health-related information, emotional support, and the opportunity to acquire certain skills and abilities before surgery [3]. Preoperative patient education programs provide patients with sufficient information about the surgical procedures and potential difficulties, which may improve their satisfaction and reduce anxiety and depressive symptoms [4].

Preoperative patient education has been given in a variety of ways, such as through in-person meetings, one-on-one counseling, written materials, spoken teaching, and audiovisual presentations, or by combining some or all of these techniques [5].

Every year, more than 312.9 million surgical procedures are performed worldwide. Furthermore, a million or so patients may pass away from difficulties following surgery brought on by inadequate preoperative education, and seven million patients annually may experience health-related hazards as a result of postoperative operations [6]. A recent study conducted in 25 countries found that one in five African surgical patients undergoing all surgical procedures in a hospital developed preoperative abnormalities and of these, one in ten died after surgery due to inadequate preoperative education [7].

Patients also believed that death after surgery was the most critical and dangerous moment in their lives. Approximately 59.6% of surgical patients report experiencing preoperative anxiety related to the fear of undergoing anesthesia, 53.9% have a fear of death, 51.7% are anxious about potential postoperative pain, and 43.3% are concerned about their family’s well-being [8]. Nonetheless, the present study indicates that surgical patients in Ethiopia still experience anxiety, have low confidence in their postoperative care, report lower satisfaction with their medical experiences, and tend to have extended hospital stays due to unfavorable surgical outcomes [9]. Furthermore, there is a gap in the literature because no previous studies have been conducted to demonstrate preoperative teaching methods and related factors for patients who have undergone surgery in this context. Patients undergoing surgical procedures in developing nations like Ethiopia benefit from preoperative education, particularly in hospitals with limited resources [7].

Patients participate in preoperative education to reduce postoperative complications, increase patient satisfaction, reduce anxiety, and shorten hospital stays. These efforts reduce hospital stays and lower healthcare costs. It is very important to provide patients with proper instructions to help them after surgery and during recovery. On the other hand, preoperative education and training may improve patient satisfaction with the care they receive from their healthcare providers [10]. Patient satisfaction is increasingly important in all aspects of healthcare. Patient satisfaction surveys are essential for measuring the quality of service provided by healthcare systems [11]. However, patient satisfaction has significantly decreased in developing nations, particularly Ethiopia, despite being a key healthcare outcome and being closely linked to the utilization of health services [4].

However, patient satisfaction with preoperative education is a major public health issue in Ethiopia in general and in this study setting in particular. Assessing patient satisfaction with pre-operative education can help identify gaps in practice and provide direct evidence from people who have undergone surgery, which healthcare professionals can use to develop interventions to improve patient satisfaction and overall health. Therefore, this research was carried out to better understand the level of patient satisfaction towards preoperative education among adult elective surgical patients and its associated factors.

Methods and materials

Study design, area, and period

A facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted at Dessie Specialist Hospital, located in Dessie town, 401 km from Addis Ababa, the capital of Ethiopia, and 471 km from Bahir Dar, the capital of the Amhara region. The hospital has approximately 749 workers, including 548 healthcare professionals and 201 administrative staff. According to the second-quarter 2024 report from the Dessie Specialized Hospital Documentation and Archiving Department, the hospital has a total of 242 beds. It provides medical services to over 6 million people, many of whom are from nearby areas and the region, visiting for various medical needs. Dessie Comprehensive Specialized Hospital offers emergency, ART services, chronic care, surgical, dental, medical, pediatric, gynecologic, obstetric, and other services [12]. The study was conducted at Dessie Comprehensive Specialized Hospital from January 2024 to February 2024.

Source population

All adult elective surgical patients in Dessie Comprehensive Specialized Hospital.

Study population

All adult elective surgical patients in Dessie Comprehensive Specialized Hospital during the study period.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

All adult elective surgical patients in Dessie Comprehensive Specialized Hospital during the study period were included.

Exclusion criteria

Adult elective surgical patients who died during the operation, as well as those who experienced complications and were unable to communicate for interviews during the data collection period, were excluded from this study.

Sample size determination and sampling techniques

The sample size for the outcome variable was estimated using Epi Info version 7.2.5.0, employing the power approach method based on associated factors of satisfaction toward pre-operative education among adult surgical patients from previous studies [3]. Finally, a 10% non-response rate was considered. A 95% confidence level, 80% power, and ratio of non-exposed to exposed one were taken as an assumption for the power approach method for cross-sectional study. Therefore, the maximum sample size was calculated based on associated factors from a similar previous study and is 440 (Table 1). Study subjects were selected by consecutive sampling technique.

Data collection tool

Data was collected using a pretested and structured questionnaire developed after a review of different kinds of literature, documents, guidelines, and manuals related to the topic of interest [3, 4, 11, 13–16]. The tool consists of first is about socio-demographic characteristics (This part consists of 7 questions aimed to gather information about study participants’ background, the second part consists of 10 questions about patient and family factors, the third part consists of 16 questions about preoperative education, and the fourth part consists of 24 questions about patient satisfaction in preoperative education.

Data collection procedure

After preparing the questionnaire, three clinical nurses and one BSc nurse were scored by a supervisor to collect data. Each of them received two days of training on the meaning of each item in the questionnaire and on data collection techniques, such as greetings, obtaining consent, controlling data quality, and resolving ambiguous questions. To avoid re-interviewing patients who had multiple visits during the data collection period, data collectors asked and confirmed whether patients had been interviewed previously. Subsequently, data were collected through face-to-face interviews with patients at their exit (during discharge) and two hours post-operatively by the data collectors.

Operational definition

Patient satisfaction

It is a subjective response to the context, process, and outcome of a received service experience. The instrument includes questions about patient satisfaction and contains 24 questions. Each survey item was assigned a 5-point Likert scale: “Very dissatisfied” (1), “Dissatisfied” (2), “Neutral” (3), “Satisfied” (4), and “Very satisfied” (5). Then, the total score was computed and converted into percentages. According to the modified Bloom’s cut-off point, those who scored greater or equal to 80% were categorized as having good satisfaction towards pre-operative education, and those who scored less than 80% were categorized as having poor satisfaction towards pre-operative education [17].

Adult

People who have reached a certain age, as a rule, are eighteen and are considered by the law as having the ability to manage their affairs.

Wealth index

is a composite variable which will be calculated for urban and rural separately. All asset variables will be coded into binary variables. The first principal component analysis (PCA) will be generated from a group of asset variables for both urban and rural residents separately. Based on the first PCA, an aggregated score will be calculated for each of the interviewed patients, then, be grouped into quintiles, with quintile 1 representing the “poorest” and quintile 5 representing the “richest” [18].

Pre-operative education

Structured questionnaires that were adapted from earlier research on patients’ experiences of preoperative education in public hospitals in Addis Ababa were used. The questionnaire has 16 total questions and the mean was derived after computing each respondent’s response to the questions. According to the modified Bloom’s cut-off point, those who scored greater or equal to 80% were categorized as having adequate pre-operative education, and those who scored less than 80% were categorized as having inadequate pre-operative education [17].

Data quality control and assurance

The questionnaire was originally prepared in English. Later, he was translated into a local language in Amhalic, and conversely, translated into English to confirm the consistency. After the questionnaire was developed, three clinical nurses were recruited to collect data, and one registered nurse was recruited as a supervisor. The two-day training was provided to each of the values ​​of all elements of the questionnaire, welding methods, acceptance methods, quality monitoring methods, and data collection methods, such as how to solve ambiguous elements. Before data collection, a pre-test was conducted on 22 adult elective surgical patients at Boru Meda General Hospital to evaluate the consistency and clarity of the tool. To prevent repeated interviews with patients who returned for follow-up visits during the data collection period, data collectors verified whether each patient had been interviewed previously. Supervisors and principal investigators closely monitored the data collection process. The internal consistency of the tools used to measure both satisfaction with pre-operative education and the education itself was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. The Cronbach’s alpha values were 0.78 for pre-operative education and 0.85 for satisfaction with pre-operative education.

Data processing and analysis

Data completion and consistency were manually checked. The data were initially coded, checked, and entered into Epi-Data software version 4.06, then exported to the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) software version 26.0 for cleaning and statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics such as frequencies, medians, and interquartile ranges were performed to examine the distribution of participants within the variables included in the study. Multicollinearity among the explanatory variables was checked using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), and all values were less than 10.

Initially, bivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify variables for inclusion in the multivariable analysis. Variables with p-values of 0.25 or less from the bivariable analysis were selected as candidates for the multivariable logistic regression model. The model’s fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, which yielded a p-value of 0.859. To determine significant factors, multivariable binary logistic regression was conducted using the enter method. Statistical significance was set at a p-value of less than 0.05. The adjusted odds ratio with a 95% confidence interval was used to indicate the strength of the association between each explanatory variable and the outcome variable.

Factors associated with patient satisfaction towards pre-operative education among adult elective surgical patients

Both bivariable and multivariable binary logistic regression analyses were undertaken. In bivariable analysis, marital status, place of residence, educational status, payment status, preoperative fear, preoperative education, previous surgery, duration of illness, and surgical waiting time were fitted at a p-value < 0.25. Whereas, in the final multivariable analysis, place of residence, payment status, preoperative education, previous surgery, and duration of illness were significantly associated with the outcome variable at a p-value of ≤ 0.05 using the 95% CI.

Patients who were from urban areas were 4.303 times more likely to have satisfaction with preoperative education than patients who were from rural areas (AOR = 4.303; 95% CI: 1.487–12.448). Patients who have got adequate preoperative education about surgery were 8.620times more likely to have satisfaction towards preoperative education than those who have got inadequate preoperative education about surgery (AOR = 8.620; 95% CI: (3.672–20.235), Patients with insurance coverage are 6.165 times more likely to have satisfaction towards preoperative education than non-insurance users (AOR = 6.165; 95% CI: 2.146–17.717). Similarly, patients who had no previous surgery were 15.416 times more likely to have satisfaction with preoperative education than patients who had previous surgery (AOR = 15.416; 95% CI: 6.537–36.354). Moreover, Patients who had a duration of illness in days and months were 5.326 times (AOR = 5.326; 95% CI: 1.839–15.430), and 9.517 times (AOR = 9.517; 95% CI: (3.558–25.451) more likely to have satisfaction towards preoperative education than those who had a duration of illness in years respectively (Table 5).

Discussion

In the healthcare context, patient satisfaction is a subjective assessment of cognitive and emotional states that arise from the difference between a patient’s expectations of planned care and their perception of actual care. This study was aimed at assessing the proportion of patient satisfaction towards preoperative education and associated factors among adult elective surgical patients in Dessie Comprehensive Specialized Hospitals in Northeast Ethiopia. The study evidenced that 82.2%, 95% CI: (78.2%, 85.7%) of the patient had good satisfaction towards preoperative education among adult elective surgical patients. This study finding is in line with the finding of the study conducted in Addis Ababa (83.6%) and in Wolaita Zone (79.5%) [17, 19]. But it is higher than the findings of studies conducted in East Amhara Referral Hospitals, Gamo and Gofa zones, and Sohag University [7, 20, 21]. The reason for this difference could be the fact that medical personnel at Dessie Comprehensive Specialized Hospital are well trained, experienced, and committed, able to provide patient-centered preoperative education through open communication and emotional support to surgical patients at their own convenient time. This may increase patient satisfaction with preoperative education, which may also be due to differences in socioeconomic status, study setting, sample size, and patient/provider ratio across study areas. However, it was lower than the finding of the study done at the University of Gondar Teaching and Referral Hospital [22]. This difference may be due to various factors, such as differences in patient variables such as sociodemographic characteristics, differences in hospital settings, potential deficiencies in the provision of preoperative education and care in the hospitals investigated in this study, an increase in patient volume, increased involvement of well-wishers, etc.

In this study, patients who received preoperative education about surgery were 8.620 times more likely to have a positive satisfaction towards preoperative education than those who did not receive such education. This finding is supported by the results of studies conducted in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, and Wolaita Zone [17, 19]. A possible reason for this similarity is that patients who receive preoperative education from their healthcare providers have greater knowledge and understanding of the procedures and expectations associated with their surgery, which makes them better prepared. As a result of this instruction, they feel less nervous and uncertain, potentially improving their perception of the care they receive. Preoperative education also increases the likelihood that participants will feel empowered and involved in their care. They can be more engaged in the healing process and understand the importance of certain interventions [23]. A sense of ownership and increased teamwork with medical staff may also contribute to higher satisfaction.Patients from urban areas were 4.303 times more likely to be satisfied with preoperative education than patients from rural areas. This finding is similar to those reported in a meta-analysis study conducted in Ethiopia, Turkey, and Iraq [24–26]. A possible explanation for this gap is that residents in rural areas may have less access to basic health information about surgical services, and a lack of awareness and knowledge is more likely to cause fear. Additionally, rural patients may have fewer social networks and less access to support groups, which could lead to feelings of isolation and anxiety about surgery [27]. Furthermore, patients in rural areas may have a higher level of distrust in their healthcare providers regarding the value of preoperative education [28].

Consequently, this results in lower satisfaction among rural residents compared to patients from urban areas.

Patients with insurance coverage are 6.165 times more likely to satisfaction with preoperative education than non-insurance users. This finding is supported by the studies conducted in Wolaita Zone and North East Ethiopia [19, 29]. This could be because patients who receive treatment for free may view it as a gesture of kindness or support, which can enhance their overall experience and level of satisfaction with the preoperative education they receive. Furthermore, patients who do not have to pay for their medical needs may feel less stressed and anxious about the expense, which frees them up to concentrate more on the quality of care they receive.

Patients who had no previous surgery were 15.416 times more likely to have satisfaction with preoperative education than patients who had previous surgery. This study is similar to studies conducted in Gamo &Gofa Zones, and Turkey [21, 30]. This similarity could be because the patient’s past experience of suffering related to previous surgical trauma, hospital stay, and costs incurred could lead to low satisfaction with preoperative education.

Another factor that contributed to this outcome was that patients whose duration of illness in days and months were 5.326 times and 9.517 times more likely to be satisfied with preoperative education than those whose duration of illness was measured in years, respectively. However, contrasting results have been reported in studies conducted in Addis Ababa and Wolaita Zone, where the duration of illness was not significantly associated with satisfaction with preoperative education [17, 19]. This may be explained by the fact that patients with long-term illnesses often fear their diseased state and therefore see little hope of recovery. Additional stress may occur because chronic illness can change lifestyle, sense of self, and relationships with others. Consequently, this may lead to decreased satisfaction with preoperative education.

Strength and limitation of the study

The study’s results were derived through a cross-sectional analysis of the respondents, and as a result, the data were subject to response bias. Due to the cross-sectional nature of this study, it couldn’t ascertain cause and effect. To address the sample size adequately, this study uses a non-probability sampling method (consecutive sampling techniques), and the study result might not be equally sound as compared to probability methods. However, its important contribution as a source of knowledge about the associated factors of patient satisfaction towards preoperative education among adult elective surgical patients is one of its key strengths.

Conclusion

The current study revealed that a significant proportion of patients have good satisfaction with preoperative education among adult elective surgical patients in the study area. Place of residence, payment status, preoperative education, previous surgery, and duration of illness were significantly associated with patient satisfaction towards preoperative education among adult elective surgical patients. To address these findings, Dessie Comprehensive Specialized Hospital should organize and provide continuous refreshing training for healthcare providers on patient satisfaction towards pre-operative education so that they can share their knowledge with all surgical patients. Healthcare providers should provide multidisciplinary care, giving more emphasis to the rural community, patients with a higher duration of illness, patients with previous surgery, and preoperative fear. Future researchers should conduct this study by supporting qualitative research methods to explore detailed issues about facilitators and influencing factors for satisfaction towards pre-operative education among adult elective surgical patients.
