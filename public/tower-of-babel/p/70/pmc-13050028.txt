Investigating Candida species and associated bacteria from vaginal swabs and smears of symptomatic and asymptomatic adult women: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Fingani Annie Mphande-Nyasulu, Suchanun Meksang, Nitchakarn Noranate, Salisa Kongpanyakul, Paveenuch Kulalert, Pawatsan Nilchan, Prasert Trivijitsilp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•A unique cluster of Candida albicans and Nakaseomyces glabrata from Thailand.•Surface modifications observed on budding yeast cells.•A unique interaction between bacteria and Candida yeast cells is reported.•Differences in microbiome composition by age and between symptomatic and asymptomatic women are reported.•The highest prevalence of Candida colonization was observed in women aged 18-25 years.

Introduction

The composition of the vaginal community of microbes (vaginal microbiome) plays a crucial role in health outcomes. Different vaginal microbiome community types exist, with Lactobacillus-dominated profiles linked to vaginal health, whereas less diverse, non-Lactobacillus communities are associated with adverse outcomes, such as sexually transmitted diseases and poor reproductive health. [1]. Physiologic changes in the vagina may lead to colonization of yeast affecting the population of Lactobacilli, leading to vaginal candidiasis (VVC) [2,3]. Vaginal yeast infections affect approximately 75-80% of women in their lifetime, with a higher prevalence among those of childbearing age [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. Worldwide, approximately 25-30% of healthy women without predisposing factors have a diagnosis of VVC or recurring VVC (RVVC) [9]. It is estimated that 103-172 million women experience RVVC annually, with a global prevalence of 3871 per 100,000 women, and 372 million experiencing VVC in their lifetime [10]. Diabetes, immunosuppression, antibiotic use, contraceptives, and use of intrauterine devices have been linked to VVC [[11], [12], [13]].

Although Candida albicans is the predominant species, non-albicans species (including N. glabrata, P. kudriavzevii, and C. tropicalis) are among the commonly reported organisms. Species distribution may vary significantly within populations and geographical regions [14,15]. Given that vaginal microbiome community structures differ significantly between healthy and symptomatic women, and because VVC is associated with distinct, non-homogeneous bacterial shifts that can influence treatment efficacy, the co-analysis and simultaneous consideration of the vaginal mycobiome and bacteriome are recommended; hence, this study [1]. This study aimed to assess vaginal carriage of Candida species and associated bacteria in adult women in Thailand following two hypotheses. First, in symptomatic and asymptomatic individuals, there is a difference in the bacterial community, depending on the colonizing Candida species. Second, age affects the vaginal microbiome composition in symptomatic and asymptomatic adult women.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted at the outpatient department of King Mongkut’s Chaokhuntahan Hospital in Lat Krabang district, Bangkok, Thailand. The hospital serves students, staff, and people from surrounding areas. Adult women aged 18 years and above coming for routine Pap smear with or without symptoms of yeast infection were included in the study.

Ethics statement

Ethical permission was sought from King Mongkut’s Institute of Technology Research and Innovation Services to conduct the study under EC-KMITL_66_010, and written informed consent was obtained from adult women aged 18 years and above.

Study population

The study population comprised 101 pre- (aged 18-45 years), peri- (aged 46-55 years), and post-menopausal (aged >55 years) adult women, with an age range of 18-60 years, attending the outpatient clinic at King Mongkut’s Chaokhuntahan Hospital. Upon consent, a high vaginal swab, wet smear, and patient history were collected from symptomatic women (those presenting with symptoms, e.g. discharge, foul smell, and physical evidence of infection upon examination) and asymptomatic women (those who came to the hospital for routine check-up with no previous symptoms). The swab and wet smear slides were transported to the laboratory for further investigation.

Laboratory investigations

A culture from the swab was prepared on Saboraud dextrose agar with and without chloramphenicol (10 mg/l). The plates were incubated at 37°C under aerobic conditions for 48 hours, and the isolates were stored at −80°C in 25% v/v glycerol/tryptone soy broth. A Gram stain of the wet smear was carried out in duplicate using a standard procedure to confirm the presence of yeast and associated bacteria. The smear was analyzed using a light microscope at 10×, 20×, 40×, and 100× oil immersion objective (Nikon ECLIPSE Ci, Nikon Instruments Inc. 1300 Walt 87 Whitman Road Melville, NY, USA).

Identification of Candida species

The isolated Candida clinical isolates were plated on chromogenic agar CHROMagar Candida (VWR International PBI, Milan, Italy) and incubated at 38°C for 48 hours under aerobic conditions. Distinctive colored yeast colonies were identified according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

ABC genotyping for C. albicans

ABC genotyping was conducted on C. albicans clinical isolates confirmed by CHROMagar and metagenomic analysis. The Candida 25S rDNA gene was amplified using CAINT-L (5′- ATA 100 AGG GAA GTC GGC AAA ATA GAT CCG TAA-3′) and CA-INT-R (5′- CCT TGG CTG TGG TTT CGC TAG ATA GTA GAT-3′), and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed as previously published [16,17]. Gel electrophoresis of the PCR products was carried out using 2% agarose gel in Tris - Acetate - EDTA (TAE) buffer.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Five Candida isolates were selected for further analysis using SEM due to their morphology and/or growth patterns. A fresh culture was prepared from a glycerol stock of the original isolate, which was thawed and cultured in Tryptone Soy Broth for 1 hour at 37°C on a shaker at 150 rpm (New Brunswick Scientific Innova43 Incubator Shaker, Eppendorf, NJ, USA). The sample was spun down at 14,000 rpm for 1 minute, and the pellet was collected for SEM. The sample was fixed using 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer solution (pH 7.2) overnight, thereafter with 1% w/v osmium tetroxide in phosphate buffer solution for 1-2 hours. The yeast cells were placed on a filter disc and dehydrated using ethanol at serial concentrations of 30%, 50%, 70%, 95%, and 100% v/v. The sample was dried to the critical point and coated with gold sputter. The sample was then visualized using a SEM (JSM-IT500HR InTouchScopeTM, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan).

Identification and confirmation of Candida species and associated bacteria

For bacterial genomic DNA, the QIAGEN DNeasy Kit was used to extract DNA from 84 samples, whereas the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit was used to extract DNA from 27 samples, which showed Candida in their Gram stain. The DNA was quantified using the Nanodrop (Implen NanoPhotometer N50, Implen GmbH, Munchen, Germany). 16SrRNA V3-V4 regions and 18S rRNA regions were amplified using PCR for identification of bacterial and fungal species, respectively. Amplification of bacterial genomic DNA was conducted using V3-V4 primers, forward primer 341F (5′ CCT ACG GGN G GC WGC AG -3′) and reverse primer 805R (5′- GAC TAC HVG GGT ATC TAA TCC - 3′) [18]. The amplification of fungal gDNA was conducted using ITS1 and ITS2 primer sets: ITS1 forward 1737F (5′ - GGA AGT AAA AGT CGT AAC AAG G -3′) and ITS1 reverse 2043R (5′- GCT GCG TTC TTC ATC GAT GC - 3′); and ITS2 forward f/gITS7 (5′ – GTG AAT CAT CGA ATC TTT G – 3′) and ITS2 reverse 2409R (5′- TCC TCC GCT TAT TGA TAT GC - 3′) [19]. The DNA was amplified in a PCR reaction containing 10 µL of 2× PCR master mix solution (i-StarMAXTM II) iINtRON Biotechnology, Seongnam, Republic of Korea), 10 µM of forward and reverse primer, 1 µL of distilled water, and 3 µL of genomic DNA template. The PCR amplification was conducted in a thermal cycler (BIO-Rad, USA). Pre-denaturation was performed at 95°C for 4 minutes, followed by 25 cycles at 95°C for 5 minutes, 55°C for 30 seconds, and 72°C for 30 seconds for denaturation, annealing, and extension, respectively. Final extension was performed at 72°C for 10 minutes. Metagenomic amplification and sequencing metagenomic analysis were conducted on the samples to identify the Candida sp. and associated bacterial species isolated from the vaginal swab. Amplicon sequencing was conducted using Illumina technology, specifically targeting the 16S, 18S, and ITS genetic markers. The 16S rRNA V3-V4 regions, 18srRNA, and ITS regions were analyzed using the Illumina Novaseq X plus/Nova Seq 6000/Hi seq4000/Hiseq 2500/Miseq platform. 16SrRNA full length sequences were assembled using PacBio Revio/Sequel I and Sequel II.

Blast sequencing results and phylogenetic analysis

To determine relatedness between the Candida isolates and allelic diversity, evolutionary analyses were conducted in MEGA12 [20]. The Candida ITS1 and ITS2 sequences obtained from this study (27 isolates) and 31 Candida ITS1 and ITS2 sequences from the GenBank database (GenBank accessed in October 2024) were included in the analysis. Of the total 24 sequences, three were N. glabrata and 23 C. albicans and one was P. kudriavzevi. Evolutionary history was inferred using the neighbor-joining method [21]. The tree was drawn to scale, with branch lengths in the same units as those of the evolutionary distances used to infer the phylogenetic tree. The evolutionary distances were computed using the Poisson correction method [22].

Statistical analysis

A database was created in Microsoft Excel. Statistical analysis was carried out using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 30.0.0.0 [172]). A one-sample Student’s t-test was conducted with a 95% confidence interval (CI) and 1000 bootstrap using Hedges’ correction, which uses the sample SD plus a correction factor to estimate the effect of sample size. Pearson correlation was used to measure the relationship between the variables. Descriptive statistics were carried out to summarize the data.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

In this study, 39.6% (40 of 101) of women, aged 18-60 years, with a mean age of 35.6 years, were positive for Candida. The 18-25 year age group had the highest positive cases, 47.5% (19 of 40), whereas 12.5% (five of 101) were over 55 years of age (Table 1). A total of 45% of the women positive for Candida (18 of 40) were symptomatic, presenting with varying symptoms, including discharge and pelvic inflammation, whereas 55% (22 of 40) were asymptomatic (Table 1). Varying colors of discharge were observed among the symptomatic women: greenish discharge in seven of 18 (38.9%), yellowish discharge in nine of 18 (50.0%), whitish/grayish discharge in 10 of 18 (55.6%), and bloody discharge in two of 18 (11.1%). Interestingly, all symptomatic women were within the age range of 18-49 years (Table 1). Three women reported RVVC at recruitment, whereas one new case resulted in RVVC because the patient came back for treatment with the same symptoms for VVC within 12 months despite completing treatment after each consultation. Characteristics of isolated Candida species

Four Candida species were identified: C. albicans (32 of 40) (80%), 181 N. glabrata (five of 40) (12.5%), P. kudriavzevii (two of 40) (5%), and C. tropicalis (one of 40) (2.5%). Multiple species were observed in some patients: C. albicans + C. tropicalis in six of 40 (15%) and C. albicans + P. kudriavzevii in two of 40 (5%), using CHROMagar. Candida stellatoidae was identified in 15% (six of 40) using NGS (Table 1).

Five Candida isolates (F27-24 [C. albicans (asymptomatic)], F9 [N. glabrata (asymptomatic)], F21 [C. albicans/C. stellatoidae (symptomatic)], F2 [C. albicans (asymptomatic)], and F3 [C. albicans (asymptomatic)]) were selected due to microscopy observations and growth characteristics, and their morphology was further analyzed using SEM. Two distinct budding patterns, apical (polarized) budding (budding from two polar ends of the yeast cell) and non-apical budding (multiple budding sites), were observed (Figure 1). C. albicans isolates F27-24 and F21 showed a non-polarized budding pattern, with at least 1-5 budding positions on one or both ends of the cell. Similarly, three yeast cell surface patterns were observed. Isolate F9 and F27-24 exhibited a rough surface on budding yeast cells, whereas a hairy surface was observed on yeast cells interacting with bacteria in isolates F2 and F3. A smooth surface pattern for non-budding yeast cells for C. albicans and N. glabrata isolates was also reported (Figure 1). Hyphal networks were observed in F21, whereas no hyphae were observed in isolate F9 (N. glabrarta) as well as three other C. albicans isolates that were analyzed using SEM. A unique cluster of Candida isolates from Thailand

Of the 40 Candida-positive isolates, 27 (67.5%) were subjected to NGS of the ITS1 and ITS2 regions. Clustering of Thai Candida isolates using the ITS1 and ITS2 regions revealed C. albicans in 85% (23 of 27), N. glabrata in 11.1% (three of 27), and P. kudriavzevii in 3.7% (one of 27). Among the C. albicans, 30.4% (seven of 23), isolates F5, F14, F15, F16, F18, F26, and F27, were closely associated with C. stellatoidae (Figure 2). Sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis revealed three groups. Group 1, comprising C. albicans isolates from Thailand only (from the current study); group 2, comprising non-albicans species (N. glabrata and C. krusei) from Thailand and other geographical regions; and group 3, comprising C. albicans with close association with non-albicans species (N. glabrata) and C. stellatoidae from Thailand and other geographical regions (Figure 2).Figure 2Phylogenetic tree of Candida isolates constructed using ITS1 and ITS2 nucleotide sequences from this study (TH) and various geographical regions (country name) showing three clusters: group 1 (C. albicans [except F11 N. glabrata]) isolates, unique to Thailand; group 2 (Non-albicans [N. glabrata]) from Thailand (arrow) and other geographical regions; and group 3 (C. albicans with a close association to C. stellatoidae) from Thailand and other geographical regions.Figure 2 dummy alt text

Further analysis was conducted to understand the bacterial species associated with the identified fungal groups (group 1, group 2, and group 3). Commonly occurring bacterial species in women positive for Candida were grouped into 14 phylogroups. Group G1.3 Lactobacillus iners was present in all fungal groups. Compared with the fungal groups from the ITS1 and ITS2 phylogenetic analysis, Anaerococcus sp., Amygdalobacter sp., and Veillonaceae sp. were present only in group 1 (C. albicans isolates from Thailand) (Figure S1). Candidatus Sacharibactria, Bifidobacterium breve, and Streptococcus sp. (S. vaginalis, S. anginosus) were associated with group 3 (C. albicans, N. glabrata, and C. stellatoidae). Lactobacillus casei and L. crispatus (G1.2) were associated with asymptomatic women positive for Candida (Figure S1).

Symptomatic individuals positive for Candida show greater α diversity of bacteriome

Associated bacterial species in women positive for Candida and women negative for Candida, as well as the distribution of Ureaplasma species in adult women, were observed. A significant difference in vaginal bacterial composition was observed among women aged 18-25 and 26-35 years who were positive for Candida, with a correlation between age and associated bacteria at the 0.05 significance level and 95% CI (α 0.05 <0.038*, 95% CI −0.16 to 0.99). Similarly, a significant difference was observed between associated bacteria in women aged 26-35 years who were positive for Candida and those who were in the 46-55 years group (α 0.05 <0.016*, 95% CI −0.28 to −0.03). Among the 36-45 year group, a significant difference in associated bacterial species was observed between women who were positive for Candida and those who were negative for Candida (α 0.05 <0.016*, 95% CI 0.17 to −0.40). L. iners, Lactobacillus crispatus, and Streptococcus sp. were observed in the Candida-positive 36-45 years age group.

Among the women positive for Candida, symptomatic individuals had predominantly Gram-positive bacterial species, with 92.6% (23 of 25) Gram-positive and 7.4% (two of 25) Gram-negative, compared with asymptomatic individuals, who displayed 46% (19 of 42) Gram-positive species and 54% (23 of 42) Gram-negative species (Table 2, Figure S2). Five bacterial species were shared between the symptomatic and asymptomatic samples: L. iners, Mageebacillus indolicus, Veillonellaceae bacterium, (Fusobacteriales) Leptotrichiaceae, and Gordobibacter faecis (Figure S2). Lactobacillus crispatus was observed only in symptomatic individuals, whereas L. gasseri, L. jenseni, L. fornicalis, and L. mulieris were observed only in asymptomatic individuals. Symptomatic cases showed greater variation in the total number of bacterial species compared with asymptomatic cases, with 60% and 20% of the samples presenting with more than five bacterial species, respectively (Figure S4). C. albicans was the predominant species observed in symptomatic and asymptomatic individuals, 36.4% and 63.6%, respectively. On the contrary, N. glabrata and C. stellatoidae were predominantly observed in asymptomatic individuals.

Vaginal environments and virulence traits observed using Gram stain on wet smears

To visualize the presence of various fungi, bacteria, and parasites, as well as the state of the vaginal environment at the time of sample collection, wet smears were collected and stained with Gram stain (Figure 3). Five vaginal environments were observed among women positive for Candida. A healthy environment, comprising various bacterial species and Candida sp., L. casei–rich environment, L. iners–rich environment, L. iners and other pathogenic bacteria, and pathogenic bacteria–only environment without Lactobacillus sp. (Figure 3). To compare C. albicans– and non-albicans–colonized environment, a C. albicans–colonized vagina was designated community state type I (CSTI) and N. glabrata–colonized vagina as community state type II (CSTII). The CSTs were evaluated in terms of diagnosis at presentation and according to associated bacteria in the vaginal cavity (Figure 4). Among the CSTI and CSTII, healthy/Pap smear individuals represented 55.60% (15 of 27) and 66.7% (two of three), respectively. Although in women diagnosed with VVC/RVVC, 22.2% (six of 27) were among the CSTI and 33.3% (one of three) from the CSTII (Figure 4). Discussion

Symptomatic individuals who were Candida-positive show greater α diversity of bacteriome

This study used culture, microscopy, and molecular techniques to assess vaginal carriage of Candida species and associated bacterial species in adult women in Thailand. C. albicans, C. stellatoidae, P. kudriazveii, and N. glabrata were identified using NGS. Of particular concern is that all symptomatic cases presented were in the age range of 19-23 years. In this study, 90% of the women positive for Candida were new cases, with no record of VVC or RVVC, with C. albicans as the most abundant isolated species [23,24].

Candida colonization has also been reported to have a significant role in the balance between normal flora and pathogenic bacterial species in the vaginal microbiome. In this study, differences in the bacteriome were observed among women colonized by different Candida sp., C. albicans (CSTI), and N. glabrata (CSTII), as well as between symptomatic and asymptomatic women. These outcomes are similar to those reported in the study by Zhao et al., which reported that the VVC shaped the characteristics of the vaginal bacteriome [24,25]. Although the current study did not focus on VVC, the outcomes show bacteriome variation according to colonizing Candida sp. and the presence or absence of symptoms. Another unique finding of the study was the variation in proportions of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria in symptomatic and asymptomatic women. Previous studies have reported synergistic and antagonistic relationships between Candida sp. and Gram-positive bacteria [26], L. iners was the most predominant Lactobacillus species in women positive for Candida but was also observed in asymptomatic women in combination with pathogenic and non-pathogenic bacteria. A Gram-negative bacterium, L. iners, has been reported as a dominant community in non-pregnant women colonized with Candida sp., as well as in those with vaginal dysbiosis [23,24,26]. Gram-positive bacterial species, including S. anginosus, L. jenseni, L. mullieris, L. gasseri, L. pisttaci, and L. fornicalis, were all associated with asymptomatic women positive for Candida, confirming previous reports on synergistic interactions with C. albicans [27,28]. The outcomes of the study also identified Gram-positive bacterial species associated with symptoms in women positive for Candida. These include L. crispatus, Lacticasei bacillus, Acetivibrio sp., Ercella sp., Saccharofermentas sp., Arabiibacter massiliensis, Clostridiales bacterium, Parvimonas micra, Tissierellia bacterium, Amygdalobacter nucleates, A. indicium, Dialister sp., Atopobium sp., F. vaginae, Veillonellaceae bacterium, Sneathia vaginalis, S. sanguinegens, Coriobacteriacea bacterium, Paraeggerthella hongkongensi, Berryella wangjianwei, and P. marseille. Therefore, future research should focus on this association to further understand these outcomes. Interestingly, four Gram-positive bacterial species associated with symptoms were observed only in the group 1 Candida species that formed a unique cluster found in Thai population during phylogenetic analysis. Further studies will be conducted to characterize this unique cluster. Similarly, the vaginal environment has been reported to influence pathogen behavior, whether they exhibit pathogenic or commensal behaviors [28]. In the current study, Ureaplasma species were isolated from symptomatic (RVVC) women and one asymptomatic woman. Pathogenic bacterial species, such as Finegoldia sp., Anaerococcus sp., Dialister sp., and Prevotella sp., as well as Anaerococcus sp., were observed women positive for Candida. In women with RVVC, only Ureaplasma sp. and L. iners were identified from their vaginal smears. Elsewhere, U. urealyticum, U. homimis, and Prevotella sp. have been reported in pregnant women with adverse pregnancy outcomes [29,30]. Although this study did not involve pregnant women, the presence of such pathogenic species in women of reproductive age is a cause of concern [31].

Interactions between C. albicans and Firmicutes, Anaerococcus sp., Dialister sp., and/or Prevotella sp. were observed, with the bacteria attached to the surface of the hairy yeast cell, rather than the smooth cell surface. Fungal–bacterial interactions have been reported previously in oral, gastrointestinal, and blood infections and have been shown to influence disease progression [31,32]. From this outcome, the authors hypothesize that the morphology of the yeast cell could influence interactions with certain associated bacterial species in the vaginal microbiome. Whether these interactions could influence disease outcomes is yet to be determined. Interestingly, the yeast isolate that had bacteria attached to its surface was isolated from a healthy individual who visited the clinic for a routine health check-up (Pap smear). This interaction was not observed in yeast cells with smooth and rough surface morphology. The authors further hypothesize that the hairy yeast cell surface may be more conducive to bacterial attachment compared with the smooth or rough surfaces, thus warranting further investigation.

Age was observed as a determinant in the diversity of bacterial species in the vaginal isolates. The limited diversity among the 36-45 year-old (pre-menopausal) Candida-positive group and the increase in microbiome diversity in the 46-55 year age group (peri-menopausal and menopausal group) could be attributed to hormonal changes among the age groups [33]. Virulence traits, including hyphal formation and adhesion to epithelial cells, were observed in women with active Candida growth, irrespective of age. Encountered in symptomatic and asymptomatic cases, adherent species strongly suggest colonization may progress to clinical manifestations should the opportunity arise. Adhesion and invasion are greatly associated with pathogenic rather than commensal association. In the current study, L. iners was the most predominant bacteria in vaginal environments with active Candida growth, determined by the presence of hyphae, with no pathogenic bacteria observed. Similar findings were reported in adolescent women aged 15-19 years in South Africa, where women with Candida hyphae were less likely to have bacterial vaginosis [34].

The distinct cluster of some of the Candida isolates from this study could suggest strains that are specific to Thailand and not yet observed in other geographical regions. Clusters shared with isolates from other geographical regions suggest global circulation of these shared isolates, including Africa (Gabon, Egypt), Asia (China, India, Japan, Pakistan, Thailand), Europe (UK, Belgium), Middle East (Iran, Kuwait), Oceania (Australia), and South America (Brazil, Mexico).

Despite the success of the study, several limitations should be taken into consideration. There was a limited sample number, which could affect the interpretation of the results. Second, the study was conducted at one center; a multi-center study would be beneficial to compare outcomes.

Fungal groups identified in this study and their associated bacteria require further studies to understand their relationship.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study has shown that there is vaginal colonization with Candida species in symptomatic and asymptomatic adult women, with a unique cluster of Thai Candida isolates. Combined routine screening using smears with in-depth microscopy and molecular analysis could unravel unique relationships between the morphology of Candida yeast cells and associated bacteria, as well as the association between the colonizing Candida species, and the ensuing microbiome. Similarly, the authors hypothesize that certain Gram-positive bacteria are associated with symptoms in women colonized with Candida sp., thus opening up areas for further research.
