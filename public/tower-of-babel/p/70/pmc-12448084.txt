Millefeuille: The layering of the Martian atmosphere observed in forward scattering geometry
Authors: Nicolas Thomas, Miguel Almeida, Omar Mokhtari, Antoine Pommerol, Matthew Read, Bernhard Geiger, Lucie Riu, Manish R. Patel, Shane Byrne, Sarah Sutton
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We report on observations of the atmosphere above the limb of Mars in forward scattering geometry (~174° phase angle) using the Colour and Stereo Surface Imaging System (CaSSIS) aboard the European Space Agency’s ExoMars Trace Gas Orbiter spacecraft. These unique observations provide high-resolution (18 meters per pixel) measurements of light scattered by aerosols in the atmosphere. The observations are characterized by subkilometer-scale layering, ±20% variations in color of the individual layers, and high radiance (I/F > 1). Strong spatial (latitudinal) variability is also evident, suggesting highly inhomogeneous absorption and atmospheric heating. Models of the radiance, while likely to be degenerate, indicate good agreement with lower-resolution results inferred from other techniques. Mesospheric aerosol layers (strongly blue in CaSSIS color ratios) are also evident. The observed decrease in the CaSSIS RED/BLU color ratio with altitude is indicative of a decrease in particle radius.

INTRODUCTION

One of the key questions concerning the climate of present-day Mars involves our lack of understanding of the vertical distribution of aerosols in the atmosphere and how this is controlled seasonally and annually (1–4). A seasonal dependence of the dust load has been known for a long time (5, 6) with local measurements from landers confirming optical depth variations with season. Optical depths (τ) of between 0.4 and 1.5 were recorded over many Mars years in Gale Crater, for example (7). Dust storms (both regional and global) are also clear evidence of annual variability (8). Variability seen in global images acquired from Mars Express has recently been reviewed (9). Furthermore, it has been established that dust in the atmosphere is not distributed homogeneously with altitude but exhibits layering (10). Atmospheric dynamics are affected, as atmospheric aerosols (both mineral dust aerosols and icy condensates) interact with solar and planetary radiation [e.g., (11–13)], resulting in atmospheric temperature changes in response to changing atmospheric aerosol loads (8, 14). Nonequilibrium effects are also predicted (4). Compositional changes on annual timescales and spatial length scales at high altitude have also been reported (15). It has been suggested (7) that in specific seasons, ~25% of the column optical depth could be attributable to high-altitude ice particles.

Aerosol properties and their seasonal and spatial (horizontal and vertical) distributions are a fundamental input for weather and climate modeling because they can greatly affect the vertical radiative balance (16). Constraining the vertical distribution is becoming increasingly important, as Global Circulation Models (GCMs) become more powerful. Currently, the NASA Ames legacy Mars GCM has 24 vertical layers (17) with the layers increasing in thickness from ~10 m near the surface to ~5 km for the uppermost cells (~90 km). The effective vertical resolution in the mesosphere is therefore ~1/2 scale height. The vertical resolution used in the Mars Weather Research and Forecasting (WRF) model is about a factor of 2 higher in their multi-Mars year simulations (18). The Mars WRF includes radiatively active dust with authors finding in a prescribed scenario that dust radiation feedback results in nonlinear, spatially inhomogeneous variations in dust activity (18).

The need for better understanding of the atmospheric vertical profile has motivated the building of solar occultation instruments [e.g., (19–21)], which probe the atmosphere by studying the transmission through it as the Sun ingresses or egresses through the limb (22–24). Vertical profiles of extinction opacity covering Mars years 34 to 35 have been presented (25), where the dust vertical structure in the lower atmosphere was observed at a kilometer scale and mesospheric water-ice clouds observed above the dust haze coincided with elevated hygropause occurrences.

While this provides high signal-to-noise measurements, the Sun is not a point source and subtends (typically) 0.35° at Mars, which, in turn, corresponds to a vertical resolution of ~10 to 15 km from a 400-km orbit. Pinholes have been used within optical systems to decrease the effective size of the light source (19).

In principle, imaging systems are able to provide higher resolution observations of the Martian limb. However, allowable observational geometries constrain the ability of existing orbiter cameras to observe a well-illuminated bright limb while not endangering the instrument itself (e.g., by having direct unattenuated sunlight entering the optics). Several missions are also in Sun-synchronous orbits (e.g., Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter), which further constrain possible geometries. Here, we report optical observations of the Martian limb in near-forward scattering geometry with the Colour and Stereo Surface Imaging System (CaSSIS) from onboard the European Space Agency’s ExoMars Trace Gas Orbiter (TGO), which is in a non–Sun synchronous, roughly circular orbit about 400 km above the Martian surface (26). These observations are of significance because they show the vertical aerosol distribution at a spatial resolution of <40 m (scale of ~18 m per pixel) with very high signal to noise. This can be compared with lower resolution data (~1 km per pixel) acquired by, for example, the Mars Color Camera on the Indian Space Research Organization’s Mangalyaan spacecraft (27), while Mars Global Surveyor Mars Orbiter Camera wide-angle observations provide around 2 km per pixel at the limb.

The CaSSIS observations show layering with layer thicknesses of <1 km frequently seen and with large color variegation with altitude. In addition, they provide complementary observations to those of the solar occultation channels in the Nadir and Occultation for Mars Discovery (19) and Atmospheric Chemistry Suite (21) instruments on TGO. Here, we present the first observations and data analysis. A model calculation taking into account the observing geometry and estimated properties of the aerosols provides a reasonable fit to the data while also indicating that a substantially improved understanding of the atmospheric aerosol distribution is possible with further observations and analysis.

Particle size dependence in models

An example of a model reproducing the observations in the lower atmosphere reasonably well is shown in Fig. 3. This model result has been obtained by combining the forward SASKTRAN model with a fitting routine to allow iteration to the optimum solution. For the model shown, we constrained the aerosol density to change according to a scale height distribution only. The best-fit values of the scale height, the surface density, and the parameters of the log-normal size distribution were optimized through iteration. Comparison of this result with an identical model, which has been constrained to allow single scattering only (fig. S10), shows the importance of treating multiple scattering correctly in this geometry.

In the real case, there are more free parameters than can be investigated, covering, for example, the density of the aerosols at each height, latitude and longitude dependence, particle size, shape, etc. We used a single composition for each model. The wavelength-dependent refractive indices of dust in the SASKTRAN model have been used in most cases (fig. S9). However, the main characteristics of the model result in the color filters do not appear to be strongly influenced by the particle refractive index (fig. S8). The geometry of the observation has been accurately taken into account, and a brief sensitivity analysis showed a strong dependence of the radiance on the phase angle (as might be expected), indicating that future observations and associated exposure times must be carefully computed in advance.

The best fit to the data in Fig. 3 gives an aerosol scale height of H = 11.96 km [cf. Lemmon et al. (29)] with a surface number density of n0 = 1.51 #/cm3 and log-normal size parameters of r = 1.93 μm and m = 1.29 [cf. Chen-Chen et al. (30)]. The values are within the typical range found by other measurements. However, the fit with this simple model is by no means perfect, indicating deviations from the model assumptions. In particular, initial sensitivity studies show high sensitivity of the color ratio to particle radius as expected. Figure 4 provides a good illustration. The left panel shows a far superior result from the SASKTRAN code when compared to Fig. 3. Three effects have been included. First, the particle size has been reduced with altitude as shown in the right panel of Fig. 4. Physically, this appears to be sensible, and it makes a substantial change in the RED/BLU color ratio at high altitudes. Unlike in Fig. 3, the radiance in the BLU filter is now higher than in the RED and consistent with the observation. Second, a cutoff in the density distribution has been included at 53 km. Last, a series of peaks and troughs has been introduced into the density distribution (see right panel). Gaussian profiles have been used with a 100-m half-width. These changes to the density distribution produce very clear signals in the brightness distribution (e.g., at 44-km altitude), which shows that more sophisticated density and size distributions can be envisaged to produce a better fit. The high sensitivity of the model to particle radius and the relatively wide range of particle sizes found by other authors [e.g., (30)] when looking at time dependence suggest that altitude-dependent particle size variations would be a first-order explanation for the observations.

Latitudinal dependencies in vertical structure

The limb observation sequence (see the Supplementary Materials) produced five well-exposed datasets. They are shown in Fig. 5, scaled to the same radiance and with the altitudes matched in the center of the frame at ~31 km. The data were acquired between 07:13:45 and 07:17:49 UTC on 21 January 2024. The spacecraft was moving at around 3 km/s north-south at a roughly constant longitude. Hence, these datasets provide snapshots of the limb brightness every 200 km with the image width being ~3.6 km. In Fig. 5, the long axis of each image here is not perpendicular to the limb (which is a consequence of the inertial pointing of the spacecraft during the acquisition sequence), and it can be seen that the atmospheric layering is mostly parallel to the limb with only small deviations evident for specific layers. What is much more notable, however, is that there is a transition from a more gradually changing radiance distribution near the equator to distinct larger-scale layers as the spacecraft moved south (to the right). There are also more profound color changes with altitude in the more southerly acquisitions. This is illustrated in Fig. 6, which is a plot of I/F along the column of the rightmost (southernmost) dataset shown in Fig. 5. Over an altitude change of <5 km, the reflectance changes in some cases by more than a factor of 5. This should be compared to Fig. 1 (acquired at a lower latitude but in the same sequence) where changes are more gradual. Differences between the BLU filter data and the RED and NIR datasets are also evident (as might be deduced from Fig. 5). This indicates that while the vertical distribution of aerosols changes slightly even within one acquisition (corresponding to distances of the order of 3.6 km), there are substantial changes that can have a major impact on altitude-dependent energy deposition in the atmosphere on length scales of ~200 km and possibly less.

Limb observations necessarily imply that one is integrating along a line of sight in which the altitude is changing. However, if we assume that a layer at 40-km altitude is 1 km in vertical dimension, then it is trivial to calculate that the path length of a line of sight through the layer is 166 km, which, in turn, corresponds to nearly one-third of the path length through the atmosphere below 53 km (roughly the altitude of the sharp drop in signal seen in Fig. 3). Hence, the observed signal has a high probability of coming from the region of the atmosphere close to the minimum altitude of the line of sight. However, the changes in atmospheric structure with latitude seen here strongly suggest that nonuniform distributions along the line of sight (i.e., local density changes) need to be assessed, possibly in conjunction with the spatial information provided by the imaging capability.

Mesospheric (detached) layers

Figure 6 shows a weak but nonzero signal above 39 km and, in particular, a blue layer at around 40-km altitude. The signal-to-noise ratio is such that weak, and higher-altitude layers are evident throughout the entire dataset. Figure 7 shows an example in which four discrete layers can be seen (at 46.5, 51.0, 53.0, and 55.5 km above the limb). Layers at even higher altitudes may also have been present but, if so, they were out of the field of view for this observation sequence. Layers with an I/F of <0.01 can easily be detected, and longer exposure times (up to 30 ms) can be foreseen for future high-altitude observations. Figure 7 shows that the layers are strongly blue colored, and the two uppermost observed layers actually have little signal in the RED and NIR filters. Clancy et al. (15) demonstrated that mesospheric aerosol distributions are quite distinct from those at altitudes below 50 km. The higher-altitude aerosols had considerably smaller particle sizes and distinct dust and ice spatial and temporal distributions. The change in layer color with altitude seen in CaSSIS observations is broadly consistent with this interpretation. Fedorova et al. (22) observed detached layers with opacities in the range 0.01 to 0.15 at 45 and 60 km, which they assumed to consist of water ice and therefore were compositionally different from the lower-altitude, dust-dominated aerosol distribution. The CaSSIS observations would also support this and show that these layers can be <2 km in vertical extent.

DISCUSSION

We have shown that aerosols in the Martian atmosphere exhibit layers and fine structure over vertical length scales of <1 km. These structures can extend horizontally for tens of kilometers. Furthermore, these structures are occasionally substantially different in color (~20% in RED/BLU CaSSIS ratios) from their immediate surroundings. While a broad distribution of aerosols with altitude that can be modeled as a scale height distribution has been observed, more complex structures are also observed with fine structure superposed. The fine structure contributes to ~±10% variability with respect to a simple scale height model. The observed horizontal extent of the layers is much smaller than the line-of-sight column. Hence, local variability in aerosol properties may be much larger than indicated by the observation alone. Our modeling indicates that the observations, and specifically the color, provide unique insight into the particle radius at each altitude in the atmosphere. We also note that the color ratios may be influenced by particle shape (and, to a lesser extent, composition). As a result, parameter fitting to the data may require other constraints to limit degeneracy.

CaSSIS is able to obtain observations of this type approximately once a month with some limited possibilities to select the latitude and longitude of the limb position. We intend to build a database of these observations to try to gain more information about the properties and distributions and their spatial and seasonal variability.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data acquisition approach

CaSSIS is a medium-resolution (11.56 μrad per pixel) imager with four color filters (each covering 2048 pixels by ~256 pixels on the detector) operated in push-frame mode (31). The instrument has been well calibrated both in a relative and an absolute sense (32) and is photometrically stable.

Several constraints unique to these off-nominal observations needed to be addressed. Straylight (33) enters the aperture via reflection from multilayer insulation. Removal of this signal has proven effective for regular Mars imaging; however, for observations of the limb, it was decided to minimize the potential straylight contribution from the Martian surface by observing when the spacecraft is above the nightside hemisphere of Mars. A further constraint was to ensure that direct sunlight could never enter the aperture of CaSSIS close to the boresight while ensuring that a substantial fraction of the line of sight through the atmosphere at the limb would be illuminated directly by the Sun. Last, the spacecraft pointing modes only allowed for an inertial pointing but did allow the spacecraft to be rotated such that the x axis of the CaSSIS detector would be approximately perpendicular to the projection of the Martian surface, thereby maximizing the altitude coverage. Combined with the motion of the spacecraft in its orbit (ground-track velocity of ~3 km/s), this meant that by repeated imaging at roughly 1 image/s, CaSSIS would scan the limb with the same limb position passing through all of the color filters within just a few seconds, allowing assessment of the color variegation with altitude.

These constraints resulted in observations with a typical slant distance to the limb of ~1600 km and a phase angle ~174°—i.e., close to forward scattering geometry. On the other hand, we expected this geometry to be advantageous from a signal-to-noise perspective because of the properties of Martian aerosols. Aerosol particle radii [e.g., (34–36)] are in the range 0.2 to 2 μm, potentially varying strongly with altitude, and therefore strongly forward scattering (as can be demonstrated by simple Mie theory calculations; see figs. S5 to S7). The chosen geometry therefore almost maximizes the signal-to-noise ratio from the aerosols and provides constraints on the size parameters of particles in the layers through the color ratios.

The inertial pointing constraint meant, however, that the altitude of the center of the field of view changed fairly rapidly. In the specific case of the pass on 21 January 2024, the center altitude changed by 8 km within a 5-min period (see fig. S4). Multiple datasets with different exposure times were acquired during a pass so that an optimum exposure time could always be found within one dataset (see fig. S4). The approach has now been executed successfully several times. The results shown here focus on the pass from 21 January 2024. For this pass, the local time at the subspacecraft was 20:14 UT at the center of the observing sequence and changed by 14 min during the sequence. TGO was therefore definitively above the nightside hemisphere (Fig. 8 and fig. S1).

We note that CaSSIS observations of the surface of Mars under good illumination conditions use exposure times of ~1.3 ms (four times longer). Hence, the peak radiance of the limb is extremely high in this geometry with an I/F (“I over F” where I is the observed radiance and πF is the irradiance) near 1 (Fig. 1). The five image frame sequence acquired in this run resulted in complete color overlap for one frame only (256 pixels parallel to the limb by 2048 pixels perpendicular to the limb) at the center of Fig. 2. This figure, however, shows the other frames to allow the reader to see that the individual layers extend spatially across several frames. We radiometrically calibrated the image frames used to produce this figure following our standard processing with the geometry calculated using the SPICE toolkit (37). Manual adjustments to correct errors in spacecraft pointing and knowledge of the precise position of CaSSIS’s rotation mechanism were found to be unnecessary. (For Mars surface imaging, 2- to 3-pixel misalignments are occasionally found and are routinely corrected for by correlation using ISIS3.)

Models

The modeling of the radiance is challenging because CaSSIS is looking through an aerosol column that is only partially illuminated (a substantial fraction of the column, decreasing with altitude, is shadowed by the disk of Mars) and one that must be treated in 3D to account for the geometry. Furthermore, the unilluminated column does influence the signal because the particles therein both forward scatter and absorb photons produced in the illuminated part of the column. A simple calculation can be performed to estimate both the height at which the line of sight becomes optically thick (i.e., τ > 1) (32 to 38 km) and the reduction in the radiance resulting from the shadowing in the optically thin case. An example is shown in the Supplementary Materials. This has served as a check on our implementation of a more sophisticated 3D scattering code.

Some tools used for Earth observation can be adapted to this problem. We use the SASKTRAN RTM (38) code containing a Monte Carlo mode (39). The Monte Carlo integration allows the production of an unbiased estimate of the observed radiance. The “backward” Monte Carlo algorithm considers ray paths coming out from the observer, taking advantage of reciprocity. The radiance is calculated to a specific precision level of 0.1%. We consider a maximum of 25,000 photons per line of sight, and the maximum number of scattering orders is fixed at 10, which allows convergence to be achieved.

The geometry is fully spherical with a 1D atmosphere (i.e., no variation with latitude or longitude; the implications of which were discussed above). A density profile of the formn(z)=n0exp(−zH)is used as a starting point for modeling the radiance. Here, n0 is the number density at the surface, and H is the aerosol scale height. Similar approaches have previously been used (40).

For H, Vincendon and Langevin (41) found the scale height of dust particles to vary between 6 and 12 km depending on season using terminator observations acquired by OMEGA/Mars Express. Lemmon et al. (29) found that the vertical distribution of atmospheric dust at Gusev crater was consistent with uniform mixing with a measured scale height of 11.56 ± 0.62 km. Moroz et al. (42) found that the aerosol emission has two components: one corresponding to a scale height distribution with H = 10 km and stratified layers a few kilometers thick. The exponential component probably consists mainly of mineral dust and the layered one of icy particles. Hoekzema et al. (43) used High Resolution Stereo Camera (HRSC) measurements of dust in Valles Marineris and found H = 14.0 + 1.3/−1.1 km. Mishra et al. (44) found H = 14.08 km and H = 11.24 km at two different wavelengths in the visible using the Mars Colour Camera onboard the Indian Mars Orbiter Mission.

The particle size model follows a log-normal distribution with a mode radius, r, and a mode width, m. We treat the surface as Lambertian with zero bidirectional reflectance distribution function (BRDF) and a transmission of zero. This has relatively little importance because of the shadowing of the visible surface and the limited amount of scattered light reaching the part of the surface visible from the spacecraft.
