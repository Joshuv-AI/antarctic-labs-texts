Renin-angiotensin aldosterone system inhibitors usage among hospitalized patients with acute kidney injury: a retrospective study
Authors: Lingyi Xu, Qingqing Zhou, Linger Tang, Damin Xu, Li Yang, Xizi Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough not recommended for potential risk of worsening kidney dysfunction, continuation of renin-angiotensin aldosterone system inhibitor (RAASi) during an episode of acute kidney injury (AKI) is not rare in clinical practice, and the risk-benefit ratio for RAASi therapy should always be carefully evaluated. Limited data exists regarding the reasons for RAASi continuation during the acute course of AKI and its impact on kidney and overall outcomes.Materials and methodsThis retrospective study used electronic medical records from the clinical data warehouse of Peking University First Hospital to screen all adult admissions who developed AKI during hospitalization and were prescribed RAASi within 48 h prior to AKI onset between 1 January 2018 and 31 December 2020. Patients were further divided into RAASi discontinuers and continuers based on whether RAASi was withdrawn within 48 h after AKI onset. The primary outcome was non-recovery from AKI at discharge; the secondary outcomes were failure of full recovery from AKI and in-hospital mortality.ResultsA total of 321 patients were prescribed RAASi within 48 h prior to AKI onset, of which RAASi therapy was continued in 196 (61.1%) cases (RAASi continuers). Under-recognition of AKI was considered the main reason for the continuation of RAASi (143 cases, 72.9%), followed by the exclusion of RAASi as the culprit drug (31 cases, 15.8%), strong indications for RAASi (20 cases, 10.2%) and transient AKI (18 cases, 6.6%). The non-recovery rates were 58.6% and 29.6% in RAASi continuers and discontinuers, respectively. After fully adjustment, RAASi continuation was associated with an increased risk of non-recovery (RR, 1.727; 95% CI 1.179, 2.588) and failure of full recovery from AKI (RR, 1.427; 95% CI 1.062, 1.938) at discharge. A non-significant association was observed between RAASi continuation and in-hospital mortality in both unadjusted and adjusted model.ConclusionsUnder-recognition of AKI was predominant and might be the main reason for continuing RAASi after AKI onset. Continuation of RAASi during an AKI episode was associated with poor kidney recovery in the short term. Timely recognition of AKI should be improved, and a dynamic risk-benefit ratio evaluation for RAASi is required to achieve optimal management.Keywords: Renin-angiotensin aldosterone system inhibitor, acute kidney injury, recovery, mortality

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a severe but common complication among hospitalized patients characterized by a sudden deterioration of kidney function, with an incidence of approximately 10-15% among all hospitalized patients and over 50% in critically ill patients [1–3]. AKI is well known to be associated with higher mortality rates, more extended hospital stays, and increased healthcare costs [4–6]. The survivors are at increased risk for chronic kidney disease (CKD) and cardiovascular disease in the long run[7].

Renin-angiotensin aldosterone system inhibitor (RAASi) is commonly used in patients with hypertension, cardiovascular diseases and kidney diseases[8]. The long-term benefit of RAASi has been well demonstrated in large-scale clinical trials [9–11]. Among AKI survivors, RAASi therapy was also found to reduce mortality risk without increasing the risk of recurrent AKI [12,13]. However, due to its effect on intrarenal haemodynamics, RAASi therapy during the acute course of AKI may exacerbate kidney damage, particularly in combination with acute hypovolaemia, heart failure or sepsis [14–19]. In critically ill patients, exposure to RAASi during AKI was found to be associated with an increased risk of subsequent acute kidney disease but decreased all-cause mortality[20]. Among outpatients with CKD and progressive AKI, improved kidney function was observed after discontinuing RAASi therapy [21]. Thus, as a double-edged sword, RAASi therapy in AKI is of great concern, and clinicians should always weigh the benefit of mortality against the risk of poor kidney outcomes [22]. Although the comparative risk of restarting RAASi among AKI survivors have been increasingly studied, the effects of continuing RAASi therapy during an active AKI episode on renal and overall outcomes remains poorly characterized. In this retrospective study, the possible reasons for RAASi continuation among patients with new-onset AKI during hospitalization were analyzed, and the impact of this continuation of RAASi was evaluated, with non-recovery from AKI at discharge being the primary outcome.

Materials and methods

Study population

This retrospective study was conducted at Peking University First Hospital, China. All adult admissions between 1 January 2018, and 31 December 2020 were screened, and those who developed AKI during hospitalization and were prescribed RAASi within 48 h prior to AKI onset were included. AKI was defined and staged based on the KDIGO creatinine criteria [22], and AKI occurring within 24 h of admission was excluded as community-acquired AKI. Only the first qualifying hospitalization was included for patients with multiple hospitalizations. The flowchart of the study was presented in Figure 1. This study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Peking University First Hospital and was exempt from informed consent (2023-408). The present study was in adherence to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study flowchart.

Data collection

In-hospital data, including demographic data, admission and discharge data, diagnosis codes, laboratory values, surgery profiles, prescriptions and medical records, were obtained from the institution’s clinical data platform.

Baseline information included demographic characteristics (age, gender, body mass index [BMI]), chronic comorbidities (diabetes mellitus [DM], hypertension, heart failure [HF], coronary heart disease [CHD], cerebrovascular disease, chronic lung disease), laboratory tests (haemoglobin [Hb], albumin [Alb], serum creatinine [SCr], serum potassium, proteinuria) and blood pressure. Chronic comorbidities were identified according to admission diagnosis using the International Classification of Diseases-10th revision codes. The laboratory value referred to the first value recorded after admission. Proteinuria was defined as (1) urine protein ≥ 1+; or (2) urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR) ≥ 300 mg/g; or (3) urinary protein quantification ≥0.15 g/24 h. Changes in SCr, serum potassium and blood pressure during hospitalization were recorded. Uncontrolled blood pressure was defined as systolic blood pressure > 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure > 90 mmHg. Surgery profiles before AKI were extracted and major surgery was defined as any surgical procedure under general anaesthesia conducted in an operating room [23]. Medications that may affect kidney function included diuretics, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), contrast, proton pump inhibitors, nephrotoxic antibiotics (aminoglycosides, glycopeptides, sulphonamides, amphotericin, polymyxins), and anti-tumour agents. Use of medications was determined according to prescriptions. Information of hospitalization, including intensive care unit (ICU) admission and length of hospital stay, were also extracted. All the above data were automatically obtained from the data platform.

In addition, AKI aetiologies (pre-renal, renal, post-renal), critical illness before AKI onset (sepsis, shock, acute cardiac events), recognition of AKI (timely recognition, delayed recognition, unrecognition) [6], and reasons for continuation and restarting of RAASi were evaluated by two independent physicians according to medical records. Discrepancies were resolved by a third experienced clinician. Acute cardiac events referred to acute myocardial infarction (AMI), acute heart failure, hypertensive crisis and cardiogenic shock [24]. AKI recognition was defined as documentation or treatment adjustments related to AKI, such as records of increased SCr values or decreased urine output, adjustments of fluid therapy and medication according to kidney function, and nephrology consultation. Possible reasons for continuing and restarting RAASi were determined by a thorough review of medical records, and under-recognition of AKI was determined as the reason for continuing RAASi if: (1) AKI was not timely recognized; and (2) there were no obvious reasons for continuing RAASi in AKI, such as uncontrolled blood pressure, acute cardiac events and rapid kidney recovery from AKI.

RAASi use

Patients were further divided into RAASi discontinuers and continuers based on whether RAASi was withdrawn within 48 h after AKI onset. Restart of RAASi was defined as a new prescription of RAASi before discharge after withdrawal.

Clinical outcomes

The primary outcome was non-recovery from AKI at discharge, defined as a decrease of SCr by less than 25% from peak SCr or dialysis dependence at discharge [6]. The secondary outcomes were failure of full recovery from AKI, and in-hospital mortality. Full recovery from AKI was defined as the return of SCr below the threshold of AKI [6]; in-hospital mortality was defined as all causes of death until discharge.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented by frequencies (percentages), whereas quantitative variables were expressed as means (standardized deviations [SD]) or medians (interquartile ranges [IQR]). Student’s T-test, Wilcoxon rank test or chi-square test were used to compare groups. Missing data were not replaced by imputation.

The association between RAASi continuation and outcomes was evaluated using multivariable Poisson regression. Prespecified covariates and confounders for risk adjustment were selected based on clinical experience and literature, including age, gender, number of chronic comorbidities, baseline eGFR, initial AKI stage, major surgery before AKI, medications that may affect kidney function before AKI, and critical illness before AKI. Duration from AKI onset to the last SCr test was considered as a potential cofounder on kidney outcomes while length of hospital stay for mortality. Relative risks (RRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported. Sensitivity analyses on non-recovery from AKI and in-hospital mortality were performed to evaluate the robustness of the results: (1) RAASi discontinuers who restarted RAASi after withdrawal were excluded to eliminate the effect of RAASi re-initiation; (2) RAASi continuers who discontinued RAASi 48 h later after AKI onset were excluded to avoid the potential bias caused by delayed discontinuation; (3) both abovementioned populations were excluded.

All analyses were performed using SPSS software (SPSS 26.0, SPSS Inc) and R (version 4.3.2) within the R studio platform (version 2023.12.0 + 369), and statistical testing was conducted at a 2-tailed α level of 0.05.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

A total of 321 patients receiving RAASi therapy within 48 h prior to AKI onset were enrolled in the study (Figure 1). As shown in Table 1, the mean age was 67.6 (±15.5) years old, and 61.4% were male. Hypertension (91.3%), coronary heart disease (58.9%), and diabetes mellitus (52.6%) were the most common chronic comorbidities. The median SCr at admission was 95.4 μmol/L and eGFR was 62.0 mL/min/1.73 m2. Of these, 146 (47.7%) patients were detected to have proteinuria at admission. Approximately half of the patients (160/321, 49.8%) had uncontrolled baseline blood pressure at admission; 286 patients (89.1%) had concomitant use of other antihypertensive medications.

Most patients (87.5%) were initially classified as AKI stage 1, 9.7% as stage 2 and 2.8% as stage 3, respectively (Table 2). The pre-renal, intrinsic-renal and post-renal causes were observed at 89.0%, 29.3% and 1.9% of the study population. Use of medications that may affect kidney function was the main risk factor for AKI (81.9%), followed by acute cardiac events (36.4%) and major surgery (20.2%). Of the 263 patients exposed to medications that may affect kidney function before AKI onset, diuretics were the most common (68.5%), followed by NSAIDs (20.9%), nephrotoxic antibiotics (14.0%) and contrast (12.8%). 24.6% of patients had uncontrolled blood pressure at the onset of AKI and 7.8% developed newly onset hyperkalaemia after AKI. Less than half of AKI patients (40.5%) were recognized timely and 12.1% received renal referrals.

RAASi usage after AKI onset

In total, RAASi therapy was continued in 196 (61.1%) patients (RAASi continuers) within 48 h after AKI onset and discontinued in 125 (38.9%) patients (RAASi discontinuers). Compared with RAASi discontinuers, RAASi continuers presented with milder AKI and were less likely to be pre-renal. The proportion of major surgery, use of medications that may affect kidney function, sepsis and shock were also lower in RAASi continuers (all p < 0.05). It is worth mentioning that RAASi continuers were less likely to receive timely recognition, renal referrals and SCr monitoring than RAASi discontinuers (all p < 0.05). Besides, RAASi continuers had shorter length of hospital stay (15.9 days versus 22.2 days) and duration from AKI onset to the last SCr test before discharge (6.0 days versus 10.0 days) than RAASi discontinuers (all p < 0.05).

Reasons for continuation and restart of RAASi after AKI

Under-recognition of AKI was considered the main reason for the continuation of RAASi, as 18 cases (9.1%) had delayed recognition and 125 (63.7%) remained unrecognized until discharge (Table 3). Furthermore, RAASi was excluded as the culprit drug in 31 cases (15.8%). Twenty cases (10.2%) were identified as having strong indications for RAASi, and 18 cases (6.6%) underwent transient AKI (Table 3).

Among 30 RAASi discontinuers who restarted RAASi therapy before discharge at a median of 5.7 days after AKI onset, blood pressure control remained the main reason for restarting (16 cases,53.3%), followed by recovery from AKI (9 cases, 30.0%), cardioprotective effect (8 cases, 26.7%) and long-term renoprotection (2 cases, 6.7%) (Table 3).

Association between RAASi continuation and outcomes

During a median of 9.2 days of follow-up, the non-recovery rates were 58.6% and 29.6% in RAASi continuers and discontinuers, respectively. In the unadjusted model, RAASi continuation was associated with an increased risk of non-recovery at discharge (RR 1.982; 95% CI 1.383, 2.908). This association remained significant after adjusting for potential confounds (RR, 1.727; 95% CI 1.179, 2.588) (Table 4). The association remained consistent in the sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Table 1). Similarly, RAASi continuation was also associated with an increased risk of failure of full recovery from AKI (RR, 1.427; 95% CI 1.062, 1.938) after adjustment.

Discussion

In this retrospective study, we found that continuation of RAASi therapy after AKI onset was common (61.1%), with under-recognition of AKI being the primary reason. The continuation of RAASi was associated with an increased risk of non-recovery from AKI at discharge.

RAASi is commonly considered as a culprit drug for AKI because of concerns regarding altered renal haemodynamics [25]. Thus, discontinuation of RAASi during the acute course of AKI is recommended by KDIGO practice guidelines [22]. Despite limited data, the continuation of RAASi during AKI episodes is not rare in clinical practice [26,27]. In this study, RAASi therapy was continued in approximately three-fifths of patients. We were not surprised to find that the continuation of RAASi was associated with worse short-term kidney recovery, as RAASi could cause efferent arteriolar dilation, leading to decreased glomerular filtration [25]. Similar findings were also reported in a study of 19,074 critically ill patients with AKI that RAASi therapy was associated with an increased risk of acute kidney disease (AKD) after 7 days of AKI onset [20]. Despite the possible risk of limited kidney recovery, a protective effect on survival was also observed [20]. In this study, although not statistically significant given the limited number of events, a trend was observed for a decreased in-hospital mortality among RAASi continuers. Therefore, the risk and benefit of RAASi therapy should be carefully evaluated during the acute course of AKI. Apart from the potential decrease in mortality, renoprotective role of RAASi therapy has been proved in AKI caused by malignant hypertension or hypertensive crisis, as it can inhibit the overactivated RAAS system, avoid widespread organ damage, and preserve kidney function [28–31]; and cardioprotective effect has been observed in patients with AMI where lower incidence of adverse cardiovascular events were observed [32,33]. In these circumstances, continuing RAASi under close monitoring might be the optimal treatment. On the contrary, potential risks of continuing RAASi during AKI include hyperkalaemia, hypotension and worsening of kidney function, due to its mechanisms of reducing aldosterone production and potassium excretion, lowering blood pressure and affecting intrarenal haemodynamics. Among AKI caused by renal hypoperfusion or complicated with hyperkalaemia, timely withdrawal of RAASi, together with haemodynamic support or potassium-lowering therapy, might prevent further injury, leading to a quick recovery of kidney function and preventing from fatal emergencies [34]. Therefore, whether to discontinue RAASi is never a simple ‘yes or no’ question, and risk-benefit should always be carefully evaluated to achieve optimal management in individuals.

Unlike the short-term prognosis, the benefit of RAASi on long-term reduction in mortality among those who survived AKI has been clearly demonstrated [12,13,35–40]. In a study with 96, 983 patients who survived stage 2 or 3 AKI, RAASi therapy at hospital discharge was associated with improved survival without increased risk of recurrent AKI during 1-year follow-up [12]. A prospective study of hospitalized patients with AKI also found RAASi use at 3 months after discharge lowered the risk of all-cause mortality (HR 0.62 [0.46–0.84]) during a median of 4.9 years of follow-up without increasing the risk of recurrent AKI (HR 0.88 [0.69–1.13]) [39]. In addition, previous RAASi users who discontinued treatment after an episode of AKI had an increased risk of 2-year mortality compared with those who continued RAASi (HR 1.27 [1.15–1.41]) and no difference was seen in the risk of subsequent AKI [36]. Thus, initiating or restarting RAASi after AKI was recommended, although the timing was obscurely mentioned [41,42]. In this study, nearly one-fourth of RAASi discontinuers restarted RAASi at a median of 5.7 days after AKI onset, with uncontrolled hypertension and kidney recovery being the main reasons. Further studies are warranted to find the ideal timing and indication of RAASi after an AKI episode.

Timely recognition of AKI is the prerequisite for proper management. The under-recognition of AKI was prominent in developed or developing countries, resulting in poor outcomes [4–6,43–45]. A nationwide cross-sectional study conducted in China a decade ago found that the under-recognition rate of AKI was 78.8% (74.2% non-recognition, 4.6% delayed recognition) [6]. In this study, although the situation improved a bit, the recognition rate remained low, especially among those who continued RAASi. Given the lack of strong indication for RAASi use in most patients, it is reasonable to speculate that RAASi was not discontinued because clinicians did not identify the occurrence of AKI. A multicentric study also found that AKI patients who were under-recognized presented with a longer interval to RAASi discontinuation, a lower nephrology consultation rate, and a higher 30-day mortality rate [26]. Therefore, timely recognition is key to improving the prognosis, which might be achieved by utilizing clinical decision systems and automatic alerts in the future [2,46].

This study also had several limitations. First, as a single-centre study with a limited sample size, the result might not be applied to other settings. Second, causality could not be determined in this retrospective observational study. Third, our study may not provide robust evidence for the association between RAASi continuation and in-hospital mortality in AKI patients due to limited events of death, which requires further investigations. Fourth, it is worth mentioning that data requiring evaluations based on daily progress notes might be misestimated, including possible reasons for continuing and restarting RAASi therapy, and recognition of AKI, as clinicians may not always document their actions or thought processes accurately during busy routines. Fifth, the long-term outcomes could not be evaluated due to the lack of data after discharge.

Conclusions

Under-recognition of AKI was predominant and might be the main reason for not discontinuing RAASi after AKI onset. Continuation of RAASi was associated with an increased risk of non-recovery from AKI at discharge. Timely recognition of AKI should be improved, and a dynamic risk-benefit ratio evaluation for RAASi use is required to achieve optimal management.
