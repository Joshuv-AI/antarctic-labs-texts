A multilevel modelling approach to understanding antenatal care utilisation in Uganda
Authors: Moses Festo Towongo, Kannan Navaneetham, Gobopamang Letamo, Enock Ngome
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aims to investigate the factors influencing the adequacy of antenatal care service utilisation among women in Uganda, a country with the high maternal mortality rate in East Africa (336 deaths per 100,000 live births). One of the causes maternal morbidity and mortality is the underutilisation of antenatal care services, posing a significant challenge to achieving SDG 3.1, which aims to reduce maternal mortality to 70 deaths per 100,000 live births by 2030.MethodThe study utilised secondary data from the 2016 Uganda Demographic and Health Survey (UDHS), focusing on women aged 15 to 49 who had given birth in the five years preceding the survey. Data analysis involved univariate, bivariate and multilevel binary logistic regression models.ResultsThe findings from the adjusted model revealed that the adequacy of antenatal care services was 6.4% among women in Uganda. Moreover, individual and community-level factors influenced the adequacy of antenatal care service utilisation. Employment status emerged as a significant factor at the individual level, with employed women more likely to use antenatal care services adequately (AOR = 1.53, 95% CI: 1.13-2.08). Region of residence and community media saturation were influential at the community level, with women in the Northern and Western regions (AOR = 2.35, 95% CI: 1.34–3.43; AOR = 1.85, 95% CI: 1.29–2.67) and those in media-saturated communities more likely to utilise antenatal care services adequately (AOR = 1.85, 95% CI: 1.29–2.67).ConclusionsThe study underscores the limited utilisation of ANC services among women. Factors such as employment status, region of residence, and community media saturation play pivotal roles in determining the adequacy of antenatal care service utilisation. The government is urged to strengthen community media campaigns targeting underperforming regions which underutilizing antenatal care services, so as to improve service quality and access.

Introduction

Adequacy of antenatal care service utilization is key for combating maternal and infant morbidity and mortality [1]. Adequacy of antenatal care services refers to timing of first visit, number of visits, and adequacy of service content received by the pregnant woman during the gestation period [2]. It is critical to achieving Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.1, which calls for reducing maternal mortality to 70 deaths per 100,000 live births by 2030 [3]. Over the last two decades, the World Health Organization (WHO) has developed the focused antenatal care framework and then a revised framework that requires a woman to visit an antenatal care clinic at least four to eight times [1, 4]. Similarly, the government of Uganda through the Ministry of Health has adapted the WHO recommendations in their revised clinical guidelines [5, 6]. The main aim is intended to benefit both the mother and the unborn baby [1, 4]. Globally, maternal mortality has decreased by 40%, from 2000 to 2023 maternal deaths were estimated 700 death per 100,000 live births [7, 8]. Despite improvements, 260,000 women died during and after pregnancy and childbirth worldwide in 2023 [7–9]. Nearly 90% of maternal deaths in 2023 were in low- and middle-income countries. In 2023, 87% of maternal deaths occurred in Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. Sub-Saharan Africa had 70% of maternal deaths, and South Asia had 17% [7–9]. Recent studies have shown that in 2023 countries with highest maternal mortality associated with pregnancy ranged from 44.7% from Chad to 27.5% in Guinea, while Senegal, Comoros, Rwanda, Mauritania, Sao Tome and Principe, Gambia, and Ethiopia were rated low in female mortality [10]. Similarly, Uganda has high maternal mortality (336 deaths per 100,000 live births) in sub-Saharan Africa [11]. The common causes of maternal mortality among women across the globe are toxaemia, obstructed labour, sepsis, and haemorrhage [12, 13]. Meanwhile, in developing countries, hypertensive disorder and haemorrhage are among the leading causes of maternal mortality [14, 15]. Despite the known causes of mortality among women, studies indicated that only 3.3% of pregnant women in low- and middle-income countries attended ANC in the first trimester, 62.4% at least four times, and 2.9% at least eight times, contrary to WHO recommendations [1, 4, 16]. Tetanus toxoid vaccination coverage varied from 15.0% in Trinidad and Tobago to 91.8% in India [17, 18]. In Bangladesh, only 22% of women received all components of antenatal care [19], but in India, most women did, but only 31.4% received intestinal parasite drugs [20]. Similarly, studies in sub-Saharan Africa have shown that quality antenatal care services provision has varied across health facilities. The health facility readiness to provide quality ANC content ranged from a median of 19.9% to 84.9%, while the coverage of at least four ANC visits ranged from 24.8% to 75.8% [21]. In addition, a study in Ethiopia also showed that 20% of the women initiated their first visit to ANC in the first trimester, and a proportion of 53% received at least four antenatal care items [22]. Evidence suggests that the timing of the first visit to antenatal care services is influenced by maternal age [23–27], wealth index [23–25, 27], level of education [23–25, 27], parity [23, 25, 28], religion [23], residence, pregnancy intention status, and wanting more children [25], community ethnicity diversity and community distance to health facilities [28]. In relation to adequacy of antenatal care services, earlier studies showed that maternal age [29], residence, desired pregnancy, maternal occupation and contraceptive use [30], maternal education, husband’s education, and wealth index [29–31], media exposure [30–33], women autonomy and birth order [29], community literacy [29, 34] and community socioeconomic status [29, 31, 34], access to medical insurance and community ethnic diversity [31], and community-level health facility deliveries were the leading factors [31, 34]. Likewise, a study in West and Central Africa revealed that community socioeconomic status was associated with the utilization of quality antenatal care services [35]. In Uganda, several studies have been conducted on the utilization of antenatal care services [28, 36–53]. Of these studies [28, 37–40, 42, 44–47, 52], investigated ANC utilisation with respect to the timing of the first ANC visit. Moreover [36, 39–42, 46, 47, 50], focused on the adequate of antenatal care service utilization [40, 41, 43, 46–51] mainly focused on the content or quality of antenatal care. Adequacy of ANC service utilization acts as a gateway to safe delivery care and subsequently leads to early utilization of postnatal care services [1, 54]. Although in Uganda studies were conducted regarding antenatal care service utilization, these studies mainly focus on a single aspect of antenatal care services indicator making it difficult to infer their importance relative to other antenatal care indicators. In addition, some studies contained limited sample size and also fewer of the studies applied a multilevel analytic approach to analyze antenatal care service utilization which is multifaceted nature [55–58]. This study was set to explore factors influencing adequacy of antenatal care utilization in Uganda, mainly focusing on composite variable of timing of first visit, number of visits, and adequacy of service content of antenatal care service utilization using multilevel approach. The findings from this study will help the government of Uganda to monitor and evaluate its maternal healthcare programmes to save the lives of the mother and unborn baby.

Methods

Sampling design and surveys

This study used secondary data from the 2016 Uganda Demographic and Health Survey (UDHS). The women file was used to conduct this study. The study used a two-stage stratified cluster design based on the 2014 Uganda National Population and Housing Census (NPHC). A detailed description of the surveys can be obtained in the UDHS final report [11]. The study sample comprised a weighted sample of 9630 women aged 15–49 who reported having had their last birth in the five years preceding the survey.

Outcome variable

Adequacy of antenatal care (ANC) services was assessed based on four key ANC service utilization indicators: initiation of the first ANC visit during the first trimester, completion of at least four ANC visits, receipt of essential ANC services (weight and blood pressure measurements, urine and blood samples, provision or purchase of iron tablets, and administration of Fanisidar for malaria), all the six components of ANC content were computed their index ranged from 0 to 6. A woman who received at least six components of ANC was recorded 1 otherwise 0, and receipts of at least two doses of the tetanus toxoid vaccine were coded 1 and otherwise 0. A composite variable was developed by computing all the four components to develop an index which ranged from 0 to 4. Adequacy was measured by when women received all the four indicators of ANC services; otherwise, considered inadequacy.

Independent variables

This study explored the predictive power of various independent variables in relation to the adequacy of antenatal care (ANC) service utilization among women in Uganda. The variables included both individual- and community-level (contextual) factors, guided by Andersen’s 2007 Behavioural Model [59]. At the individual level, factors examined were age at last birth, educational attainment, employment status, religious affiliation, and parity, sex of the household head, household wealth index, and household size. Contextual-level variables comprised place of residence, region, and perceived community distance to health facilities, community socioeconomic status, ethnic diversity, and media exposure within the community. Statistical methods

The data was analysed using Stata version 17.0 software. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise key variables of individual and community level characteristics are presented as percentages. The chi-square test was used to examine the statistical association between adequacy of antenatal care service utilisation and covariates. The cut-off point for level of significance was set at p-value less than 0.05. All individual and community level variables which were found significant at bivariate model were subjected to multicollinearity diagnosis, and the test was done using variance inflation factor (VIF), the findings shows that multilinearity was absent among the variables (mean VIF = 1.61, Min VIF = 1.05, Max VIF = 2.37). The 2016 UDHS data are hierarchical; therefore, multilevel binary logistic regression modelling is appropriate to test the association between individual and contextual variables and the adequacy of antenatal care services. The multilevel binary logistic regression equation of the model is specified as follows [60]:

The probability that the ithindividual within the jthcommunity utilises antenatal care (ANC) services adequately is denoted by πij,while (1 − πij)represents the probability of inadequacy of ANC service utilization. Here, β0refers to the log-odds of the intercept, and β1through βnrepresent the coefficients for both individual- and community-level predictors. The variables X2ijto Xnijrepresent the explanatory variables at both the individual; age, employment status, religion, marital status, sex of the household head, wealth index, Household size and community levels: place of residence, region of residence, distance to health facility, socio-economic status, ethnicity diversity, and media saturation. The term uojcaptures the random effect at the cluster level, and eijrepresents the random error at the individual level. To examine how individual and community factors affect the adequacy of ANC service utilization. Overall, four models containing variables of interest were fitted for the outcome variable. The first model (Model 1) “Empty” model which contains no covariates, normally referred to as an intercept model only, it is used to decompose The model is used to examine the random effect of between-PSU variability. The inter-class correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated to establish if it is justified to apply multilevel analysis method by showing the level of variation between-PSU. Model 2 contained individual level factors only and it was used to assess how individual level characteristics affect the adequacy of ANC service utilization. The ICC was estimated to observe if there is any change in between-PSU variability upon adding the individual-level characteristics to the null model. Meanwhile, Model 3 contained community-level characteristics, excluding individual-level variables, to allow the assessment of the relationship between community-level variables and adequacy of ANC service utilization among women in Uganda. The final model (Model 4) incorporated both individual- and community-level variables to assess their combined effects on adequacy of ANC service utilization. The ICC in all models was calculated using the formula ICC = σ²u/(σ²u + π²/3), where σ²u represents the cluster-level variance. Fixed effects for both community and individual levels were shown as adjusted odds ratios (AORs), and the level of significance was measured at 95% confidence interval (CI)[60, 61]. Model adequacy was evaluated using the log-likelihood ratio, while model fit was assessed using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) [62]. To account for non-response on the sampled units in Uganda 2016 DHS dataset, we adopted the DHS methodology of approximating level-weights to normalize the data. The “svyset” module in the STATA was used to account for the complex sample design by taking into account the three pieces of design; weights, PSUs and the strata. Meanwhile at multilevel level logistic regression model, the approximated weights were applied to normalize the samples for individual and community-levels (PSUs). This method helped to prevent the problem of inflated type one error and large confidence intervals [63].

Results

Univariate analysis

A total weighted sample of 9630 respondents aged 15–49 years who gave birth in the five years before the 2016 UDHS survey was selected for this study. For analysis, a total weighted sample of 9,388 women who answered the questions correctly regarding ANC service utilization was used. Of this sample, 13.8% were adolescents. Over half (53.6%) of the respondents were aged 20–29 years. Only 5.5% were aged 40 years and older. The majority (82%) of the women were in the married and living together category. Only 13% of the respondents were previously married. Nearly three-quarters (73%) of the respondents belong to households headed by men. Nearly half (46%), of the respondents belonged to the higher parity (4+) group. The majority (67%) of the respondents belong to larger (5+) households. Only 30% of the respondents had secondary or higher education. The majority (60%) of the women had primary education. Over half (58.6%) of the respondents belong to the middle and rich categories of the wealth index bracket. Over two third (70.8%), of the respondents belonged to either the Anglican or Catholic denomination. Only 13% of the respondents were Muslims. Over three-quarters (77%) of the respondents reported having worked in the past twelve months preceding the survey. The majority (77%) of the respondents resided in rural areas. The respondents are almost equally distributed across the major regions (central, eastern, northern, and western) of Uganda. Over 60% of the respondents lived in areas deemed inaccessible to health facilities. At least half (50.5%) of the respondents belong to low socioeconomic status communities. The majority (57%) of the respondents belong to less diverse communities 51% of respondents lived in communities with less media saturation (see Table 2).

Bivariate analysis

Table 2 presents the percentage distribution of women by their sociodemographic characteristics and adequacy of antenatal care service utilization during the 2016 UDHS. Of the 9,388 women who utilized antenatal care services, only 608 (6.4%) utilized ANC services adequately. This proportion differed significantly according to age, parity, sex of household head, family size, and level of education, wealth index, employment status, and place of residence, region, and community distance to health facilities, socioeconomic status, and community media saturation. A total of 7.4% of women aged 20 to 29 years utilized ANC adequately compared to adolescents (4.8%). A proportion (7%) of married women utilized ANC adequately compared to their counterparts. A total of 7.3% of the women from female- headed households utilized ANC adequately compared to those from male-headed households (6.1%). A higher proportion (7.5%) of those who belong to households of 4 or fewer members utilized ANC adequately compared to their counterparts. Adequacy of ANC was recorded from women with secondary or higher education compared to those with primary or no education (5.7% and 5%, respectively). When compared to their counterparts, women in the rich category of the wealth index used ANC more extensively (8.7%). women who were employed 6.7% of the women utilized the ANC adequacy compared to those who were unemployed (5.3%). The 9.4% of women who resided in urban areas utilized ANC adequately compared to those in rural areas (5.5%). A proportion (7.5%) of women from the regions of central and western Uganda utilized ANC adequately compared to those from the eastern and northern regions (6.7% and 4.0%, respectively). Women who resided in communities that were considered accessible to health facilities utilized ANC adequately compared to those who resided in inaccessible areas (4.6%). Women of high socioeconomic status (8%) utilized ANC adequately compared to their counterparts. A higher proportion (7.9%) of women from media-saturated communities utilized ANC adequately compared to their counterparts (see Table 2).

Multivariate analysis

Random effects and model fitness

As shown in Table 3, the null model (model 1) shows a statistically significant variation of adequacy of ANC service utilization across the communities (=2.7, p<0.000), with 45% of the variation in the odds of utilizing ANC services adequately was attributed to community-level factors (ICC=0.45). After adjusting for individual-level factors (model 2), the variance in the odds of utilizing adequacy of ANC services was statistically significant (=2.3, p<0.001), with a variation of 41.0% in the odds of adequacy of ANC attributed to community-level factors. Adjusted to community-level factors, Model 3 shows a statistically significant variation in the utilization of adequacy of ANC services in communities (=2.1, p<0.000); in this model, 39% of the variation between clusters was attributed to community-level factors (ICC=0.39). The final model (Model 4), which is adjusted for both individual- and community-level factors combined, showed a statistically significant variation in the odds of utilizing adequacy of ANC (=2.0, p<0.000). In this model, the variation in the utilization of adequacy ANC services was 38%, which was attributed to both individual- and community-level factors. This shows that despite all factors being considered, the clustering effect is still significant in the final model. In terms of model fitness, the final model (Model 4), which included the individual and community-level factors, was the best-fitted model (see Table 3).

Discussion

This study sought to examine the level and determinants of adequacy of antenatal care service utilization among women in Uganda. The use of adequacy of antenatal care services can greatly contribute to the health of the mother and unborn baby [64–67]. Understanding the utilization of antenatal care services among women in Uganda is important to influence the efforts of the Ministry of Health and other stakeholders involved in designing interventions to improve the utilization of adequacy of antenatal care services. The findings from this study show that only a small proportion (6.4%) of women received adequacy of antenatal care services. These findings are contrary to Ugandan Ministry of Health guidelines, which require a woman to receive adequate of antenatal care services [5]. However, these findings are similar to other studies in low- and middle-income countries, which established that utilization of antenatal care services was low [16, 19–22]. This finding raises questions on the quality of antenatal care received by women in Uganda; the low use of antenatal care services could partially explain why maternal mortality in Uganda is very high in sub-Saharan Africa, Okonji et al., [10]. The low levels of adequacy of antenatal care service utilization could be explained by the influence of both individual- and community-level related factors such as women’s employment status, regional variations, and community access to media [20, 28–30, 38]. This study found that at the individual level, women’s employment status was a significant factor influencing the utilization of adequacy of antenatal care services. This finding is in line with other studies in sub-Saharan Africa that show that women’s occupational status was significant in influencing the adequacy of antenatal care service utilization [30]. Women’s employment status is linked with financial autonomy and improved socioeconomic status in the community. Women who are employed can afford to pay for transport and other indirect related costs associated with adequacy of antenatal care services compared to their counterparts [29, 30, 43, 52]. Economic inequalities among women may result in inadequate care for disadvantaged women, who are most likely to die during pregnancy, delivery, or postpartum [47]. In contrast, in Uganda, some studies by Sserwanja et al. [38, 39] found no association between women’s working status and adequate utilization of ANC access. However, women’s working status is an important component that provides women with financial autonomy to afford services regarding their own health and wellbeing in general terms [42, 43]. Furthermore, at the community level, the women’s region of residence was associated with the utilization of adequacy of antenatal care services in Uganda. Women who reside in the northern and western regions were more likely to utilize adequacy of antenatal care services than those who reside in the eastern and central regions, respectively. The findings from this study corroborated other studies from Bangladesh and Uganda that established similar regional variation with regard to the utilization of antenatal care services [19, 36, 38, 39, 47]. The major four regions showed sociocultural and economic differences. The central region, home to Kampala, is substantially developed; therefore, these findings are unexpected [36]. The northern region had been at war since 1988–2006. However, scholars have argued that due to the prolonged civil war, most nongovernmental organizations shifted their attention up north, including government programs on rebuilding for internally displaced persons. Furthermore, the northern region is also home to refugees from Democratic Republic of Congo and South Sudan, the presence of displaced persons camp offers accessibility to medical services to the communities around the displaced camps and also the presence of health worker in these camps also helps to educate and encourage women to attend antenatal care services [42, 58, 68]. Hence, these could have acted as a push drive to improve healthcare services in the northern region [38]. Data from 15 health institutions in the Eastern region revealed a 40% staff shortage, insufficient drugs and supplies, poor ANC counselling, and the failure to perform basic tests for the majority of clients in Eastern Uganda. A lack of staff and resources, along with poor service quality, may discourage pregnant women from using health centres, which could explain our study’s findings in Eastern Uganda [39, 49]. In contrast to prior findings that indicated the Western region to be more disadvantaged than the Eastern region due to migration, the findings in this study revealed that the Western region performs better than the Eastern region. The causes for improving maternal healthcare could be attributed to sustained government and other stakeholders’ participation in managing Ebola outbreaks and the surge of Congolese refugees, which may have aided in healthcare interventions as well [53]. Another interesting finding was the influence of community media saturation on the utilization of adequacy of antenatal care services. Women who live in highly media-saturated communities were more likely to use adequate of antenatal care services than their counterparts. Similarly, a study in Uganda found that during the prolonged war in Northern Uganda, radio was the common source of media used to access information by a large audience [33]. These findings were also consistent with earlier studies conducted elsewhere and within the country, which demonstrated a link between media access and maternal healthcare services [19, 38, 39, 64]. Media forms such as radio, television, and newspaper/magazines have been used to educate people about different programmes. For instance, mothers who watch television are more likely to be aware of pregnancy issues and the necessity of ANC [26]. For instance, across rural Africa, studies have shown that radio is one of the most common sources of media used to acquire information and is relatively cheaper compared to televisions and newspapers/magazines [32, 33, 37].

Strengths and limitations

This study aimed to assess the adequacy of antenatal care service in Uganda. Although similar studies were conducted, they mainly focused on the utilization of antenatal care services. This study also applied a multilevel analysis in which most of the studies in the Ugandan context used ordinary regression to analyse data collected using multistage sampling. Above all, in this study, the adopted approximated weights proposed by Elkasabi, Ren and Pullum [63] were applied to the model to normalize the samples for both individual and community levels. This method helped to prevent the problem of inflated type one error and large confidence intervals. Above all, the level-weights helps to overcome the problem of over-sampling, under-sampling and non-response [63]. The limitations of this study come with the secondary nature of the data, which is associated with recall bias. ANC-related information was only available in the surveys for mothers who had live babies. As a result, stillbirths and women who died during pregnancy or childbirth were excluded from the study. Besides, other sociocultural factors which were not controlled in this study may act as confounder. Above all, this study can aid the policy makers and other stake holders to come up with policies aimed at improving the adequacy of antenatal care service utilization among women in Uganda.

Conclusion

Although women in Uganda accessed antenatal care services, this study discovered that a smaller proportion of these women utilised ANC adequately. This study further found that the utilisation of adequacy of antenatal care services is influenced by both individual- and community-level factors. At the individual level, women’s employment status was found to be an influencing factor. Meanwhile, at the community level, women’s region of residence and media saturation in the community in which they reside are important factors influencing the utilisation of adequacy of antenatal care services. The government should prioritise employment creation for women, especially in the rural areas. The government should strengthen community media campaign to educate women about the importance of using antenatal care services during pregnancy. Regarding, regional variations, the government must target the underperforming regions to improve service quality and access to antenatal care services in the health facilities.
