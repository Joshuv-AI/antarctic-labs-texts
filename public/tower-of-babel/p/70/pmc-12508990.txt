Optimization of Operating Parameters Using Response Surface Methodology for CO2/CH4 Separation Using Polysulfone/NH2‑MIL-125(Ti) Mixed Matrix Membrane
Authors: Mustafa Alsaady
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The natural world and human civilization face serious threats from global warming caused by increasing anthropogenic carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions. In this study, we examine the optimization of operating parameters within mixed matrix membranes (MMMs) using NH2-MIL-125­(Ti), a metal–organic framework (MOF) dispersed within a polysulfone (PSf) polymer matrix to achieve efficient CO2/CH4 separation. Sustainable CO2 capture is the motivation behind this study, which aims to improve the membrane technology using the response surface methodology (RSM). RSM was applied (a statistical and mathematical technique) to explore the relationships among the operating parameters (temperature, feed pressure, and CO2 feed composition) that influence membrane performance and to develop an optimal model. The optimal combination of variables is determined when they are predicted individually in the optimized model, creating optimal conditions for high CO2 efficiency. The results of this study will enable the development of efficient and sustainable CO2 capture technology, promoting environmental sustainability.

Introduction

Membrane gas separation is of great interest to oil producers, refiners, chemical companies, and suppliers of industrial gases. , The capture of carbon dioxide (CO2) from gas streams is crucial in various applications, such as natural gas processing and carbon capture and storage (CCS) systems. , The development of efficient separation technologies plays a crucial role in efforts to reduce CO2 emissions. , Membrane technology has emerged as a promising alternative to conventional absorption and adsorption for gas separation due to its compact footprint, modularity, low energy demand, and ease of integration into existing systems. Unlike solvent-based absorption, membranes eliminate the need for regeneration and associated operational complexities. , At the same time, adsorption offers lower thermal energy requirements but faces challenges such as sorbent cost and sensitivity to impurities. ,

Polymeric membranes represent the most extensively employed materials in commercial gas separation processes, where gas transport is governed by the solution diffusion mechanism. , In this process, gas molecules initially sorb into the polymer matrix based on their solubility, subsequently diffuse through the polymer chains under a concentration gradient, and finally desorb at the permeate interface. The overall permeability is determined by the interplay between gas solubility and diffusivity within the membrane’s microstructure. Despite advantages, membrane processes often encounter permeability/selectivity trade-offs, fouling, and aging, necessitating multistage or hybrid configurations and material innovations. Advancements in mixed matrix membranes (MMMs) aim to address these challenges, enhancing performance for CO2 capture and natural gas purification. , Various researchers have incorporated various types of organic and inorganic fillers into mixed matrix membranes. MMMs are hybrid membranes that consist of inorganic fillers embedded in a polymeric matrix and offer unique advantages for enhancing gas separation performance by exploiting the synergistic effects of both mass transport components. − Advancements in MMMs aim to address these challenges, enhancing performance for CO2 capture and natural gas purification. Various researchers incorporated various types of organic and inorganic fillers into mixed matrix membranes. MMMs are hybrid membranes that consist of inorganic fillers embedded in a polymeric matrix and offer unique advantages for enhancing gas separation performance by exploiting the synergistic effects of both mass transport components. − ,,

In our previous research, we explored the development and optimization of MMMs comprising NH2-MIL-125­(Ti), a metal organic framework (MOF), integrated into a polysulfone (PSf) matrix for effective CO2/CH4 separation, as reported in the Journal of Membrane Science. The MMMs were fabricated via solution casting, followed by detailed characterization of their morphology and gas separation performance. The influence of MOF incorporation on gas permeability and selectivity was evaluated using single-gas permeation experiments. Findings revealed that the inclusion of NH2-MIL-125­(Ti) nanoparticles significantly improved the CO2/CH4 selectivity compared to pristine PSf membranes, while preserving adequate permeability. Morphological analysis confirmed uniform dispersion of the filler within the polymer matrix. Notably, the PSf/NH2-MIL-125­(Ti)-15% membrane exhibited remarkable performance, achieving a CO2 permeance of 19.17 Barrer and a CO2/CH4 selectivity of 31.95, underscoring its efficacy for applications such as natural gas purification and carbon capture. These MMMs demonstrated exceptional resistance to CO2-induced plasticization, with the PSf/NH2-MIL-125­(Ti)-15% membrane maintaining a stable performance up to 14 bar, in contrast to the pure PSf membrane, which experienced plasticization at 4 bar. This enhanced plasticization resistance is attributed to the incorporation of NH2-MIL-125­(Ti) into the PSf matrix. The combination of high CO2 permeability, superior selectivity, and robust plasticization resistance establishes the PSf/NH2-MIL-125­(Ti)-15% membrane as a promising candidate for advanced CO2 separation processes.

Several studies have investigated the pure gas separation performance of mixed matrix membranes (MMMs) incorporating NH2-MIL-125­(Ti). , For instance, Guo et al. reported the fabrication of PSf-based MMMs containing NH2-MIL-125­(Ti) fillers, which exhibit a marked enhancement in CO2 permeability along with a moderate improvement in CO2/CH4 selectivity compared to pristine polymer membranes. The membranes also demonstrated stable separation performance under elevated pressures with filler loadings up to 20 wt %. Similarly, Anjum et al. prepared NH2-MIL-125­(Ti)/Matrimid MMMs with filler loadings of 15 and 30 wt %, where the 15 wt % composite achieved an optimal CO2/CH4 selectivity of 50, underscoring the filler’s potential to improve gas separation efficiency.

However, studies focusing on the mixed gas separation performance of MMMs incorporating NH2-MIL-125­(Ti) remain scarce. Moreover, the influence of key operating parameters, such as CO2 feed concentration, operating pressure, and temperature, on the mixed gas separation behavior of these membranes has not been comprehensively explored. To bridge this knowledge gap, the present study systematically investigates the mixed gas separation performance of PSf/NH2-MIL-125­(Ti) MMMs using response surface methodology (RSM) to optimize operating conditions for achieving maximum CO2 permeance and CO2/CH4 selectivity.

This work is divided into two stages. In the first stage, membranes exhibiting optimal single-gas separation performance are reproduced, with key characteristics revisited to ensure consistency with previously reported findings. In the second stage, the operating parameters are statistically optimized using RSM, and the corresponding mixed gas separation performance is comprehensively evaluated. The outcomes of this study provide critical insights into parameter optimization for PSf/NH2-MIL-125­(Ti) MMMs, advancing their potential application in natural gas purification and carbon capture processes.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Polysulfone (PSf, M w ∼35,000; M n ∼16,000; Aldrich, USA) and N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, >99%, Aldrich) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. The synthesis of NH2-MIL-125­(Ti) nanofillers employed pivalic acid (99%), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF), 2-aminoterephthalic acid (98%), terephthalic acid (98%), acetonitrile (99.8%), and tetraisopropyl orthotitanate (97%), all procured from Sigma-Aldrich. All chemicals were used without further purification.

Synthesis of Filler

MIL-125 was synthesized following a solvothermal approach as previously reported. Briefly, 17.5 g of pivalic acid was dissolved in a mixed solvent system containing 125 mL of acetonitrile and 5 mL of tetraisopropyl orthotitanate. The resulting solution was transferred to a sealed vessel and heated at 100 °C for 3–4 days, yielding white crystalline solids. The crystals were collected via vacuum filtration, dried at 80 °C for 24 h, and ground gently to obtain a fine, white powder. Approximately 2.4 g of this powder was redispersed in a 50 mL mixture of DMF and methanol (1:1 v/v) and added to a solution containing 3 g of terephthalic acid dissolved in 75 mL of DMF. The reaction mixture was then subjected to thermal treatment at 100 °C for 24 h. The resulting solid was thoroughly washed with DMF to remove residual unreacted organic linkers, followed by methanol washing to facilitate solvent exchange, yielding highly crystalline MIL-125. The synthesis of NH2-MIL-125 followed the same procedure, except that 3.3 g of 2-aminoterephthalic acid was used in place of terephthalic acid.

Fabrication of Mixed Matrix Membranes

MMMs containing NH2-MIL-125­(Ti) were fabricated through a solution blending method. PSf was first dissolved into DMF with stirring for 18 h. The PSf/DMF solution was degassed at 25 °C overnight. Then, 5 wt % NH2-MIL-125­(Ti) particles were incorporated into the DMF solvent, and the resulting mixture was stirred and sonicated. 10 wt % of PSf/DMF solution was added to the NH2-MIL-125­(Ti)/DMF mixture to prime the particles. This was followed by mixing the solution for 30 min to homogeneously distribute the dispersed particles, which were then again sonicated for another 30 min. Then, the remaining bulk PSf/DMF solution was added to the primed PSf/NH2-MIL-125­(Ti) solution. The newest solution was stirred and sonicated alternately for 30 min. Then, the mixture was stirred vigorously for another hour. The solution was then cast onto a leveled Petri dish, after which the thickness was measured using a digital screw gauge, which ranged from 0.31 ± 0.22 μm. The fabricated MMMs were characterized, and gas permeation was performed.

Membrane Characterization

Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM)

The morphology of the prepared membranes was extensively characterized by using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) by the TESCAN CLARA (Czech Republic) model. This innovative imaging technique was used to take high-resolution images of both the surface and cross-sectional structures of the membrane, providing a comprehensive analysis of the physical properties of the membrane. A small portion of the membrane piece was cut, and it was suitable for SEM analysis. Each membrane sample was coated with gold to improve the clarity and resolution of the SEM images. A thin coat of gold was deposited on the samples to enhance their conductivity. This gold coating reduces the charging effects usually occurring during image if a nonconductive sample, such as a polymer, is under the beam. It minimizes the interference from these effects, leading to clear and sharp images that allow the microstructure and morphology of the membrane to be observed. The SEM analysis was performed under an accelerating voltage of 5 kV. The voltage was chosen to maximize the resolution and depth of field of the observations while allowing the fine details of the morphology of the membrane to be resolved. The SEM images provided insight into the membranes’ surface features, pore structures, and overall integrity.

Energy-Dispersive X-ray (EDX)

The compositions of the materials were analyzed by using energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopy. This method was used to characterize the elemental particles and MMMs and quantify the captured elements. Sample images of 10 μm of the EDX analysis were carefully performed to evaluate the distribution and concentration of elements. This enabled the details of the spatial distribution and homogeneity of elements in both the particles and the MMMs to be determined by sampling a 10 μm region.

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR)

The functional groups contained in the sample were analyzed by using the KBr method with an FTIR spectrometer (PerkinElmer Spectrum One, USA). For analysis, a small amount (about 1–2 mg) of the solid sample was finely ground with a large quantity (about 100 mg) of dry, spectral-grade KBr powder. The homogenized mixture provides a uniform sample dispersion in a KBr matrix. The mixture is compacted in a high-pressure hydraulic press, yielding a transparent pellet. The analysis was done in the transmittance mode with a spectral range of 400 cm–1 to 4000 cm–1. The collected interferogram data is then mathematically transformed into a spectrum via an FTIR spectrometer’s Fourier transform algorithm. The spectrum obtained shows absorption peaks at frequencies corresponding to the molecular bonds. The resulting spectra are corrected for the baseline to remove artifacts, and the peaks are searched against a reference database to identify analytes.

Single and Mixed Gas Permeation Performance

Initially, the NH2-MIL-125­(Ti)/PSf membrane, with an effective surface area of 2.0 cm2, was securely mounted in a stainless steel permeation cell by using a sealing gasket. Before testing, the entire permeation setup was evacuated under vacuum conditions overnight to eliminate residual moisture and entrapped gases from the membrane. The feed gas flow rate was maintained at 200 mL/min during the gas permeation experiments. Feed pressure and gas flow were precisely regulated using pressure regulators and mass flow controllers. All permeation measurements were conducted under steady-state conditions at 2 bar and 25 °C. Response Surface Methodology

The experimental design for the three responses (CO2 permeance, CH4 permeance, and CO2/CH4 selectivity) was conducted by using the Box–Behnken design mode in Design-Expert 13.0. The main factors (independent variables) were represented by A, B, and C for the temperature, feed pressure, and CO2 feed composition, respectively. The given ranges depend on the results of previous scientific work, with the following ranges: temperature, 30–50 °C; feed pressure, 2–8 bar; and CO2 feed composition, 30–70 volume%. The SEM images of the PSf/NH2-MIL-125­(Ti) MMMs (Figure c,d) confirm that there is a difference in the morphology from the pristine membrane. The facet with particles on MMMs shown in the top-view image proves that the NH2-MIL-125­(Ti) filler was properly incorporated into the surface of the MMMs. The cross-sectional image indicates that at least a few voids are formed inside the membrane, possibly due to the interaction of amine functional filler groups with the PSf polymer matrix. These functional groups enable hydrogen bonding, enhancing the adhesion of the filler to the polymer matrix. Enhanced interaction from the polymer folding back on itself helps reduce phase separation, ultimately benefiting the membrane’s structural stability and functional properties.

The amine functional groups are crucial in the advertisement of CO2 transport. The amine groups bind tightly to CO2, allowing the selective entry and subsequent diffusion of the CO2 gas. This contributes to higher gas pair selectivity by reducing nonselective voids in the MMMs. It allows the membrane to selectively separate CO2 via a mechanism different from that of other gases, such as CH4, through preferential absorption or adsorption, leading to its transport. Additionally, the NH2-MIL-125­(Ti) fillers enhance sorption via Lewis acid–base interactions and provide additional molecular sieving channels, synergistically improving permeance and selectivity compared to the pristine polymer phase. In contrast, other compounds are held back or rejected. The increased compatibility and selective interaction with CO2 enhance the performance of MMMs for gas separation applications.

ANOVA Results for Modeled Values for the Response Functions

The observed selectivity trend can be ascribed to the differing gas diffusion rates and affinities of CH4 and CO2 in the polymer matrix. CH4 has a larger molecular size and lower kinetic energy compared to those of CO2, which results in a lower diffusion rate of CH4 through the polymer. Furthermore, CH4 exhibits lower affinities to sorption sites in the Langmuir regions of the polymer matrix, which are more suitable for CO2 molecules because they are smaller and polar. Thus, CO2 is preferentially adsorbed and diffused in the polymer, which ultimately improves the membrane selectivity for CO2 relative to that of CH4. This behavior occurs because CO2 diffuses faster, whereas CH4, being more stable, experiences significantly greater resistance, resulting in the observed gas separation performance in terms of selectivity.

The PSf membrane with a filler shows a CO2 permeance of 19.1 Barrer and a CO2/CH4 ideal selectivity of 31.9. Significantly improved permeabilities and selectivity have been accomplished on the MMMs compared to the pristine PSf membrane. The PSf/NH2-MIL-125­(Ti) membrane retains a CO2 permeance of 19.1 Barrer at a loading of 15 wt % filler, which correlates to a 213% increase in gas transport behavior compared with the virgin PSf membrane. Simultaneously, the selectivity of the same membrane is improved by 266%. The addition of filler gave rise to substantially improved permeance and selectivity. Therefore, the PSf/NH2-MIL-125­(Ti) membrane outperforms those with higher filler contents for the desired separation process and is the most efficient composition for development.

Statistical Optimization of Mixed Gas Separation Using RSM

As PSf/NH2-MIL-125­(Ti) at a filler loading of 15 wt % exhibited the highest single-gas permeation performance, this membrane was selected for further evaluation of its binary gas separation performance using the RSM methodology. As mentioned in Table , the ANOVA results provide the basis for examining the impact of variables on the mixed gas separation performance. The ANOVA table assesses the statistical significance of the modeled values for the response functions while determining the contributions of individual factors and their interactions. Important statistics (e.g., F-value, p-value) underscore the model strength and robustness. If the F-value is high, the p-value is less than an acceptable threshold (typically 0.05). In that case, the variables significantly contribute to gas separation efficiency. The value of R 2 indicates the model’s ability to explain response function variability and suggests the possibility of making accurate predictions. This demonstrates the model’s great capacity for studying mixed gas separation dynamics and optimizing process parameters.

Figure b,c shows the effect of the CO2 feed composition on CH4 permeance. Initially, CH4 permeance increases with the increase in the CO2 feed composition but then decreases with a further increase in the CO2 feed composition. This outcome is primarily due to the increased concentration of CO2 on the membrane surface and in the permeate stream. The accumulation of CO2 creates a competitive environment that hinders CH4 adsorption by occupying a significant number of available adsorption sites on the membrane. As CO2 molecules dominate these sites, CH4 molecules face greater difficulty attaching to the membrane surface, reducing the overall CH4 adsorption rate.

Additionally, the high concentration of CO2 alters the diffusion dynamics within the membrane. Since CO2 molecules are smaller and have higher diffusivity compared to CH4, their presence slows the diffusion of CH4 by creating a physical barrier that CH4 molecules must navigate around. This combination of reduced adsorption and slower diffusion rates for CH4 results in a lower overall CH4 permeance, further emphasizing the dominant role of CO2 in controlling the gas transport properties of the membrane. This selective behavior is crucial for applications requiring enhanced CO2 separation efficiency, such as carbon capture and natural gas processing.

The experimental results demonstrate a clear inverse correlation between the operating temperature and CO2/CH4 selectivity for the PSf/NH2-MIL-125­(Ti) MMMs, as shown in Figure b. At lower temperatures, the membranes exhibit higher CO2/CH4 selectivity, which is primarily attributed to the solution diffusion transport mechanism. CO2, being more condensable and possessing a higher quadrupole moment, exhibits stronger interactions with both the polar sulfone and ether functionalities in the PSf matrix and the amine and carbonyl functional sites of NH2-MIL-125­(Ti). This results in enhanced CO2 solubility, favoring selective sorption and diffusion through both the polymer phase and the porous filler network. However, as the temperature increases, the CO2/CH4 selectivity decreases. This decline is attributed to higher temperatures, increasing the permeance of CO2 more significantly than that of CH4. The elevated temperature provides the CO2 molecules with more kinetic energy, enabling them to permeate the membrane more readily, thereby reducing the distinction between the permeation rates of CO2 and CH4. At the same time, CH4 permeance tends to decrease slightly or remain relatively unchanged, further narrowing the selectivity gap. This inverse relationship between temperature and selectivity highlights the trade-off between separation efficiency and operating temperature, emphasizing the importance of optimizing temperature and pressure conditions to achieve the desired balance between the CO2 and CH4 separation.

Conclusions

The current study applied RSM to optimize the CO2/CH4 separation using the PSf/NH2-MIL-125­(Ti) MMM. PSf/NH2-MIL-125­(Ti) MMM successfully achieved higher CO2 permeance and CO2/CH4 selectivity using 15 wt % NH2-MIL-125­(Ti) filler. The RSM results demonstrate the effects of operating parameters (temperature, feed pressure, and CO2 feed concentration) and their interactive effects on the CO2 permeance, CH4 permeance, and CO2/CH4 selectivity. Elevated feed pressure and increased CO2 feed concentration are critical factors in enhancing both the CO2 permeance and the CO2/CH4 selectivity. The optimization results showed that a low temperature (30 °C), high feed pressure (8 bar), and high CO2 feed concentration (70%) achieved the highest CO2 permeance and CO2/CH4 selectivity while maintaining the CH4 permeance at a certain acceptable level. Optimizing membrane technology for CO2 capture using an RSM provides an effective pathway toward sustainable environmental management. Laboratory-scale results demonstrate the reliability of this approach, offering a strong basis for scale-up while also considering economic feasibility and regulatory requirements.

Limitations and Future Directions

Despite their promising performance, PSf/NH2-MIL-125­(Ti) MMMs face notable challenges that limit their industrial translation. Issues such as moderate absolute permeance, difficulty in scaling up fabrication processes, and potential long-term stability concerns, including filler leaching, interfacial aging, and physical aging of the polymer matrix, must be addressed to achieve reliable, large-scale deployment. To overcome these hurdles, future work should leverage integrated DoE approaches enhanced by machine learning-based surrogate models, enabling efficient navigation of high-dimensional formulation and operating parameter spaces. Additionally, surrogate optimization applied to VPSA processes with flexible MOFs demonstrated effective multiobjective performance tuning under complex sorption phenomena. Adopting similar strategies employing surrogate models informed by DoE could substantially accelerate the development of PSf/NH2-MIL-125­(Ti) MMMs, guiding optimal formulation and operation while mitigating experimental and computational burdens.
