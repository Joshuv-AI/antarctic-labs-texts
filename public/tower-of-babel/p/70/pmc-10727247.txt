Association of High‐Deductible Health Plans With Health Care Use and Costs for Patients With Cardiovascular Disease
Authors: Ravi Gupta, Lin Yang, Jennifer Lewey, Amol S. Navathe, Peter W. Groeneveld, Sameed Ahmed M. Khatana
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBy increasing cost sharing, high‐deductible health plans (HDHPs) aim to reduce low‐value health care use. The association of HDHPs with health care use and costs in patients with chronic cardiovascular disease is unknown.Methods and ResultsThis longitudinal cohort study analyzed 57 690 privately insured patients, aged 18 to 64 years, from a large commercial claims database with chronic cardiovascular disease from 2011 to 2019. Health care entities in which all or most beneficiaries switched from being in a traditional plan to an HDHP were identified. A difference‐in‐differences design was used to account for differences between individuals who remained in traditional plans and those who switched to HDHPs and to assess changes in health care use and costs. Among the 934 individuals in the HDHP group and the 56 756 in the traditional plan group, switching to an HDHP was not associated with statistically significant changes in annual outpatient visits, hospitalizations, or emergency department visits (−8.3% [95% CI, −16.8 to 1.1], −28.5% [95% CI, −62.1 to 34.6], and 11.2% [95% CI, −20.9 to 56.5], respectively). Switching to an HDHP was associated with an increase of $921 (95% CI, $743–$1099) in out‐of‐pocket costs but no statistically significant difference in total health care costs.ConclusionsAmong commercially insured patients with chronic cardiovascular disease, switching to an HDHP was not associated with a change in health care use but was associated with an increase in out‐of‐pocket costs. Although health care use by individuals with chronic cardiovascular disease may not be sensitive to higher cost sharing associated with HDHP enrollment, there may be a significant increase in patients' financial burden.

In 2020, >30% of workers with employer‐based insurance coverage were enrolled in a high‐deductible health plan (HDHP). 1 Such insurance plans pair a high annual deductible with a low premium, generally with a health savings account (HSA) or health reimbursement account (HRA), which can reduce the burden of the higher deductible by covering expenses with tax‐exempt funds. By increasing cost sharing for discretionary care and making patients more cost conscious, HDHPs aim to reduce low‐value health care use. As a result, HDHP enrollment has been associated with lower inpatient and emergency department (ED) visits, 2 , 3 , 4 outpatient visits, 5 , 6 medication use, 5 , 7 and diagnostic imaging. 8 However, although some avoided care may be low value, HDHPs also reduce high‐value care, including preventive services. 9 , 10 Moreover, there are growing concerns that such plans may particularly burden patients with chronic illness. 11 , 12 , 13

Enrollment in HDHPs can magnify the financial burden faced by chronically ill patients who require a greater intensity of longitudinal health care services. 14 This trend will likely grow with increasing annual deductible levels for individual employer‐based coverage over the previous decade. 1 The increased cost sharing among chronically ill patients may lead to delayed and forgone care. 15 Cardiovascular disease is of particular concern because of its high prevalence and associated health care expenditures attributable to short‐term events and needed longitudinal care.

Previous studies have examined the effects of HDHP enrollment among people with cardiovascular risk factors on health outcomes, care use, and medication adherence. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 To our knowledge, no studies have examined the association of HDHPs with health care use and costs among patients with chronic cardiovascular disease, a population that is potentially more vulnerable to adverse health outcomes from reductions in needed care. Using a large national database of commercially insured patients, we examined the association between switching to HDHPs and health care use and health care costs among patients with chronic cardiovascular disease.

METHODS

Study Design

The current study data are not publicly available. This study was considered exempt from review by the institutional review board of the University of Pennsylvania, and subject informed consent was not applicable. This study follows the reporting requirements of the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology Statement. 20 The study cohort included beneficiaries from Optum's deidentified Clinformatics Data Mart Database, enrolled between January 1, 2010, and December 31, 2019. We considered individuals enrolled in a consumer‐driven health plan (CDHP) (HDHP paired with an HSA or HRA) to be enrolled in an HDHP.

As individuals who voluntarily choose to enroll in an HDHP may meaningfully differ from those enrolled in traditional plans, we first attempted to identify health care entities in which all or most beneficiaries switched from being in a traditional plan to a CDHP, as detailed in Data S1.

The HDHP group in our study included beneficiaries enrolled with health care entities offering traditional plans for a baseline period of 12 months and switching to offering HDHPs for a follow‐up period of at least 12 months. The index date was the plan type switch date. If an entity was estimated to have switched from traditional plans to HDHPs multiple times, we included only the first switch period. We limited the analysis to beneficiaries who switched in January of each calendar year. For individual‐level specificity, in the baseline year, all beneficiaries included in the HDHP group were required to not be enrolled in a CDHP and have a total annual paid deductible amount less than the minimum Internal Revenue Service HDHP deductible level. In the postindex date period, all of these individuals were enrolled in a CDHP. The control group included beneficiaries enrolled with entities that offered traditional plans throughout the study period. For the control group, the end of the first year of traditional plan enrollment was considered the index date. Control group beneficiaries included in the analysis were required to not be enrolled in a CDHP throughout the study period and not have a total annual paid deductible greater than the Internal Revenue Service limit. Beneficiaries in both groups were included in the analysis for a maximum of 3 years after the index date.

Study Population

The study population consisted of 18‐ to 64‐year‐old beneficiaries enrolled in traditional and HDHP plans, as previously described, who had a diagnosis of coronary artery disease, heart failure, peripheral artery disease, or stroke using International Classification of Diseases (ICD), Ninth Revision (ICD‐9) and Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) and Current Procedural Terminology codes before the baseline year (Figure S1 and Table S1). Individuals who were not insured through the same health care entity throughout the study period or had multiple health insurance plans concurrently were excluded.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes were the number of outpatient visits, hospitalizations, and ED visits; total out‐of‐pocket (OOP) costs; and total health care costs per patient per year. Outpatient visits were identified using Current Procedural Terminology codes for new or established patient visits and manual review of visit types (R.G.) (Table S2). Total OOP costs were the sum of an individual's copay, coinsurance, deductible, and prescription drug dispensing fees. Total health care costs were the sum of an individual's OOP costs and the estimated paid amounts. Estimated paid amounts were calculated from total standardized costs, which are based on standard pricing algorithms applied by Optum to reflect allowed payments for all provider services across regions. 21 All costs were adjusted for inflation to 2020 US dollars.

Secondary outcomes included the number of outpatient visits to cardiovascular disease–related specialists (cardiology, neurology, vascular surgery, or cardiothoracic surgery), cardiovascular disease–related hospitalizations, and cardiovascular disease–related ED visits per person per year. Cardiovascular disease–related hospitalizations and ED visits were identified using ICD‐9 and ICD‐10 codes for primary diagnoses (Table S3). We also examined the number of cardiovascular disease–related imaging and procedures per person per year using Current Procedural Terminology codes (Table S3). We analyzed OOP and total health care costs for outpatient care, hospitalizations, prescription drugs, and other services separately.

Given a possible change in patient behavior and health care use in the time leading to an entity's switch of plan types, we excluded outcomes and costs from the last month of the baseline year and the first month of the postindex period for both HDHP and control groups. Figure S2 displays distributions of the primary outcomes.

Covariates and Missing Data

Demographic characteristics, including age, sex, race, ethnicity, census division (health entity location), and household income, were obtained for each beneficiary. A few individuals were excluded because of missing sex or geographic location. We used multiple imputation (10 imputations) with fully conditional specification to account for missing race, ethnicity, and income data (Data S1). We also identified the presence of comorbidities based on the Elixhauser Comorbidity Index diagnostic codes. 22

Statistical Analysis

We described the beneficiaries who were in the HDHP or control group by first calculating summary measures of the proportion of beneficiaries in each group enrolled in a CDHP and the total annual amount paid in deductibles in the baseline and postindex periods. We then compared summary measures of baseline demographic variables, employer characteristics, and comorbidity prevalence between the HDHP and control groups using χ2 and Student t tests. To account for potential differences between beneficiaries in the 2 groups, we applied inverse probability treatment weighting using covariate‐balancing propensity scores.

We then used a difference‐in‐differences (DID) design to assess changes in outcomes associated with switching to an HDHP. We used a DID design in our analysis, as opposed to alternative designs, such as an interrupted time series model, because the latter typically requires data measured at higher temporal frequency 23 and a larger number of data points (ie, outcome rates) than were available in the data. After applying inverse probability treatment weighting weights, we fit a multivariable generalized estimating equations model with a negative binomial distribution and log link for all use outcomes (Data S1). The model accounted for within‐person repeated measures and used robust SEs. The relative and absolute DID values of the outcomes were calculated. A γ distribution was used for the cost outcomes given the skewed nature of health care cost (Figure S2). Preindex date parallel trends between the 2 groups for quarterly rates of the primary outcomes were assessed using an interaction term between a linear time trend and an indicator variable for treatment group assignment. Subgroup analyses included analyzing all primary outcomes for each cardiovascular disease subgroup and separately for whether individuals switched into an HSA or HRA after the index date.

All statistical tests were 2 tailed and used a P value of 0.05 as a threshold for significance. Summary measures are presented as means with SDs or 95% CIs or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). Multiple imputation was conducted using SAS, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc). All other statistical analyses were performed using Stata, version 17 (StataCorp LP). Data analyses were performed between April 19, 2021, and October 29, 2022.

RESULTS

Study Population Characteristics

A total of 934 individuals with cardiovascular disease were continuously enrolled in plans with health care entities that we identified as having switched from traditional plans to HDHPs, and 56 756 individuals were enrolled in plans with entities that did not switch plan type during the study period. The median total annual deductible amount paid was $138 (IQR, $0–$275) by individuals in the HDHP group and $0 (IQR, $0–$265) by those in the control group in the baseline period. After the index date, the median amount paid was $1345 (IQR, $436–$2028) in the HDHP group and $0 (IQR, $0–$234) in the control group. Among the HDHP group, after the index date, 18% switched into an HRA, 77% switched into an HSA, and 5% were enrolled in both CDHP types during their postindex date follow‐up.

The distribution of demographic, clinical, and employer characteristics for the study population is shown in Table 1. The mean age was 52 (SD, 8.6) years in the HDHP group and 52 (SD, 8.9) years in the control group (P=0.5). The proportion of individuals who were women was similar between the 2 groups (45.9% versus 44.2%; P=0.3). In the HDHP group, 13.5% of individuals were Black race, 13.4% were Hispanic ethnicity, 4.0% were Asian race, 66.4% were White race, and 2.8% had missing race and ethnicity data. The proportions of Black and Hispanic individuals in the control group were 12.7% and 12.1%, respectively, with the other groups in similar proportion to the HDHP group. The proportion of beneficiaries with household income <$50 000 was 18.7% in the control group and 20.6% in the HDHP group. The proportions of individuals in the race, ethnicity, and household income subgroups after multiple imputation are listed in Table S4. The proportion of individuals in each group with each of the other included comorbidities is listed in Table S5.

Primary Analysis

A total of 934 individuals in the HDHP group and 14 746 individuals in the control group were included after applying covariate‐balancing propensity score–based inverse probability treatment weighting weights to the study population. Overall, there was excellent balance of means and variances of the included covariates (Table S6). Trends of the primary outcomes in the baseline period did not differ statistically significantly between the 2 groups (Figure S3 and Table S7). In the DID analysis, there was no statistically significant change in outpatient visits (−8.3% [95% CI, −16.8 to 1.1]; P=0.08), hospitalizations (−28.5% [95% CI, −62.1 to 34.6]; P=0.3), or ED visits (11.2% [95% CI, −20.9 to 56.5]; P=0.54) associated with switching to an HDHP (Table 2). The mean number of outpatient visits per person per year in the baseline year was 5.9 (95% CI, 5.4–6.4) and 6.0 (95% CI, 5.6–6.4) for the control and HDHP groups, respectively (Figure 1). In the postindex date period, the mean number was 6.2 (95% CI, 5.6–6.8) and 5.8 (95% CI, 5.5–6.1) for the control and HDHP groups, respectively. Estimated mean hospitalizations and ED visits for the control and HDHP groups in the baseline and postindex date periods are listed in Table S8.

The mean annual OOP cost in the HDHP group increased from $1271 (95% CI, $1195–$1347) in the baseline period to $2210 (95% CI, $2092–$2329) in the postindex date period (Figure 2 and Table S7). In the control group, mean annual OOP cost was $1419 (95% CI, $1255–$1583) in the baseline period and $1437 (95% CI, $1265–$1610) in the postindex date period. Switching to an HDHP was associated with a 71.7% (95% CI, 52.9–92.8; P<0.001) increase in total OOP costs ($921 [95% CI, $743–$1099]) (Table 2). There was no statistically significant change in total health care costs associated with switching to an HDHP (−13.2% [95% CI, –36.7 to 19.2]; P=0.38). Secondary and Subgroup Analysis

There was no statistically significant difference associated with switching to an HDHP in cardiovascular disease–related specialist outpatient visits and hospitalizations (Table 2). Cardiovascular disease–related ED visits were significantly lower on the log scale (−45.2% [95% CI, −69.0 to −3.3]) but not on the absolute scale (−0.03 [95% CI, −0.07 to 0.003] visits per person per year). There were statistically significant increases associated with switching to an HDHP in OOP costs related to outpatient care (107.3% [95% CI, 83.0–134.8]) and prescription drug costs (26.8% [95% CI, 14.5–40.4]). There was no statistically significant difference in total health care costs related to inpatient or outpatient care. There was a significant decrease in prescription drug–related costs on the log scale (−30.9% [95% CI, −47.9 to −8.2]) but not absolute scale (−$1974 [95% CI, −$4080 to $132]).

There was no statistically significant change associated with switching to an HDHP in any of the cardiovascular disease–related imaging or procedures (Table S10). Among subgroups of cardiovascular disease and CDHP type (for individuals in the HDHP group), the DID estimates for the primary outcomes were similar to those in the overall study population (Tables S11 and S12).

DISCUSSION

In this study, commercially insured individuals with chronic cardiovascular disease experienced no statistically significant difference in outpatient visits, hospitalizations, or ED visits, following a switch to HDHPs, versus those who remained in traditional plans. Switching to an HDHP was, however, associated with a mean increase of $921 (95% CI, $743–$1099) in OOP costs per year, but no significant change in total health care costs. These results suggest that although HDHP enrollment did not change care use, including for immediate care services, it may increase financial burden among a chronically ill population.

Our study is the first, to our knowledge, to examine health care use among patients in HDHPs with established chronic cardiovascular disease. Prior studies of individuals with cardiovascular risk factors have noted lower medication adherence associated with HDHP enrollment. 17 , 18 , 24 Among individuals with diabetes, some studies have noted lower health care use 25 , 26 and greater ED visits associated with HDHP enrollment. 19 , 27 In a more chronically ill population, as in our study, certain types of care use may be less sensitive to cost‐sharing increases because of HDHP enrollment. Thus, there may be less capacity for individuals to reduce potential low‐value care. Given the reduction in medication adherence noted in some previous studies, one concern is that HDHP enrollment may be associated with a higher rate of adverse events. However, our study noted no significant differences in either all‐cause or cardiovascular disease–related hospitalizations and ED visits. Although we did not detect a difference in hospitalizations or ED visits, because the group of patients enrolled in HDHPs was relatively small and because these outcomes were rare, we may have been unable to detect smaller but meaningful differences in these outcomes. However, these results are similar to a previous study in which individuals with cardiovascular risk factors switching to an HDHP did not experience a significant increase in major adverse cardiovascular events. 16

This study offers some reassurance that HDHPs are not associated with adverse events in a chronically ill population. However, it raises concerns that HDHPs may exacerbate the financial burden faced by patients with chronic illness. Individuals with chronic cardiovascular disease have a particularly high financial burden. 28 In addition, financial stress is associated with higher incident cardiovascular disease and all‐cause mortality. 29 , 30 Although HDHP enrollment was associated with a substantial increase in OOP costs, total costs of care did not change significantly, suggesting a shifting of financial burden to patients with underlying illness who may be unable to avoid short‐term and longitudinal health care use. Although HDHP plans typically have lower premiums, as of 2020, the mean annual worker contribution to the premium for individuals enrolled in an HDHP was ≈$100 lower for those enrolled in HRAs and $300 lower for those in HSAs compared with individuals in non‐HDHP plans. 31 Therefore, it is possible that for many individuals with chronic cardiovascular disease, enrolling in an HDHP plan may lead to a substantial increase in financial burden that may not be completely alleviated by lower premiums. Moreover, premiums already constitute a major cost for patients with chronic cardiovascular disease. 32

Our findings are consistent with prior studies on chronic illnesses. 33 , 34 One study found that among patients with inflammatory bowel disease, who similarly need expensive longitudinal care, HDHP enrollment was associated with an increase in OOP costs but no change in total health care costs. 35 Other studies have found that although OOP costs increase among patients with chronic illness in HDHPs, care is not necessarily delayed or forgone, similarly suggesting a shifting of financial burden onto patients with higher baseline health care needs. 36 The present findings add to this literature and raise important questions for clinicians, employers, and policy makers about increasing cost sharing under HDHPs for patients with chronic illness.

Limitations

Our study had several limitations. First, this is an observational study in which insurance plan type was not randomly assigned and a causation cannot be assumed. Second, although we accounted for health care entity size and matched on individual‐level characteristics, we did not have any employer characteristics available and, therefore, were unable to account for employer‐level selection and factors such as employees' health status, which employers may have considered when requiring a switch to an HDHP. Third, our findings are not generalizable to publicly insured populations or individuals insured through the Affordable Care Act exchanges. Such populations may differ appreciably from those with commercial employer‐based insurance. Fourth, although we examined OOP costs, information about premiums paid by beneficiaries was unavailable.

CONCLUSIONS

Among commercially insured patients with chronic cardiovascular disease, switching to an HDHP was associated with no statistically significant change in health care use. Enrollment in HDHPs was associated with an increase in OOP costs and no change in total health care expenditures. These findings suggest that although health care use by individuals with chronic cardiovascular disease may not be sensitive to higher cost sharing associated with HDHP enrollment, there may be a significant increase in the financial burden experienced by this chronically ill population.
