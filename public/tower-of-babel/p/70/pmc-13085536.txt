The impact of increased serum ferritin levels in late pregnancy on pregnancy outcomes: a multi-center cohort study in China
Authors: Yue Zeng, Jie He, Guolin He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesWe aimed to explore the association between third trimester maternal serum ferritin (TT-SF) and adverse pregnancy outcomes.MethodsA prospective cohort study was conducted at 6 hospitals in China from May to December 2019, including 4003 pregnant women, to investigate the associations between TT-SF and obstetric complications, as well as maternal and neonatal perinatal outcomes. Logistic regression was used to screen for complications and outcomes associated with TT-SF. Mixed-effects models and interaction analyses were applied to control for the effects of center and population heterogeneity. The Youden index was used to determine the optimal TT-SF threshold for distinguishing outcomes.ResultsLongitudinal data indicate that the serum ferritin (SF) level in pregnant women decreases as pregnancy progresses, physiologically dropping from 75 ng/ml in the first trimester and 28.9 ng/ml in the second trimester to 21 ng/ml. Univariate logistic regression analysis shows that TT - SF is associated with low birth weight (LBW), preterm birth (PTB), and intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy (ICP). After adjusting for center effects and population heterogeneity, TT - SF remains independently associated with LBW and PTB (adjusted P < 0.05). The optimal cut - off value determined by the Youden index is 18.28 ng/ml, and density analysis confirms that this threshold can clearly divide pregnant women into two heterogeneous groups. Among them, the incidence of preterm birth in pregnant women with TT - SF higher than this threshold is 1.59 times that of those with TT - SF lower than the threshold, and the incidence of low birth weight is 4.5 times that of the latter. However, there is no statistically significant difference in the level of third trimester white blood cells (TT-WBC) in the third trimester between the two groups. Stratified analyses indicated that increases in TT-SF levels were significantly associated with an increased risk of LBW among women who initiated iron supplementation during mid-pregnancy and among those with supplementation durations longer than one month, whereas no significant association was observed with the daily dose of iron supplementation.ConclusionOur research findings highlight an often-overlooked aspect in clinical practice. Excessively high TT-SF levels may be associated with preterm birth and low-birth-weight infants, potentially indicating an increase in subclinical inflammation. At this stage, there is no observable difference in third trimester blood white blood cell (TT-WBC) levels. These findings suggest that TT-SF could serve as a marker for assessing the risk of preterm birth or low-birth-weight infants. Iron supplementation during pregnancy may exhibit distinct time-window effects and cumulative dose effects, and these findings provide insights for further optimization of iron supplementation strategies during pregnancy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-08910-y.

Introduction

Iron, as an essential trace element in the human body, is a critical component of hemoglobin, which plays a pivotal role in oxygen transport [1, 2]. Iron is directly involved in this physiological process. During pregnancy, maternal physiological changes, coupled with the demands of fetal growth and development, substantially increase the maternal iron requirement. Adequate iron levels are therefore crucial for both maternal and fetal health [1–3]. However, studies have shown that the overall prevalence of iron deficiency among pregnant women in China is 45.6%, indicating that nearly half of this population is affected. In late pregnancy, the prevalence rises to approximately 51.7%, which is markedly higher than the mid-pregnancy prevalence reported in Switzerland (31.8%) and the late-pregnancy rate in the United States (18.5%), highlighting the relatively severe burden of iron deficiency among Chinese pregnant women [4].

Iron deficiency during pregnancy is commonly associated with various obstetric complications, with iron deficiency anemia (IDA) being the most prevalent. Although the diagnosis and treatment of gestational IDA are relatively straightforward, they are often overlooked by healthcare providers, resulting in suboptimal management. Moreover, increasing evidence suggests that iron deficiency, beyond causing anemia, is also linked to a range of adverse pregnancy-related outcomes, including fatigue, diminished quality of life, pica, preterm birth, increased cesarean delivery rates, postpartum hemorrhage, and even maternal mortality. For the fetus, complications include elevated risks of low birth weight and small-for-gestational-age infants. Despite its high prevalence and potential consequences, iron deficiency in pregnancy remains underdiagnosed, under-researched, and undertreated.

Current research has largely focused on the association between maternal hemoglobin levels and pregnancy outcomes, generating a substantial body of evidence [5–9]. However, the relationship between maternal iron status and pregnancy outcomes remains inadequately explored. While iron supplementation has long been considered a safe and effective strategy to ensure adequate iron supply during pregnancy and to reduce the risk of IDA and its indirect consequences [10], emerging data suggest that elevated hemoglobin concentrations may also be associated with adverse outcomes [11, 12]. Whether similar associations exist with serum ferritin levels is still unclear. These findings underscore the need to re-evaluate current iron status indicators and to optimize iron supplementation strategies tailored to individual needs.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This study was embedded in the IRONWOMEN study, a population-based prospective cohort study from the first trimester onwards in six Class A tertiary hospitals in China between May 2019 and December 2019. This cohort was conducted across six tertiary hospitals located in four regions of China—East China, Southwest, Northwest, and Central South. Prospective data collection was carried out at the following institutions: West China Second University Hospital, Chongqing Maternal and Child Health Hospital, Hubei Maternal and Child Health Hospital, Affiliated Hospital of Yan’an University, Xiamen Maternal and Child Health Hospital, and Women’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Fig. 1). The ethics committee of the hospitals approved the protocol of this study. The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT 03961074). We obtained the written informed consent and anonymously analyzed the data from this cohort. The data were collected using two components: self-administered questionnaires (WeChat Official Account) and physician-administered questionnaires based on the hospital electronic medical record system (EMR). Information was extracted based on the active collection questionnaire: 1) basic demographic information: age, ethnicity, employment, education, household income, pre-pregnancy Body Mass Index (BMI), residence, medical insurance; 2) previous deliveries: time since last delivery, mode of last delivery, history of adverse pregnancy; 3) past medical history, family history, allergy history; 4) history of early pregnancy reactions, smoking and alcohol consumption. 5) Iron intake referred to pharmacological supplementation beyond dietary sources, based on each participant’ s iron supplementation regimen during pregnancy, including iron contained in multivitamin preparations. Information extracted based on EMR: (1) essential obstetrical characteristics: gravidity, parity, history of adverse pregnancy and childbirth, number of fetuses, and use of Assisted Reproductive Technology (ART); (2) Laboratory tests: Hemoglobin (Hb) and SF in the first (< 14 weeks), second (< 28 weeks) and third trimesters (≥ 28 weeks); (3) underlying diseases; (4) obstetric and perinatal outcomes and time of diagnosis. Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, we established this cohort. Inclusion criteria included: (1) pregnant women who registered at the survey site (hospital) during the survey period (May 1, 2019 - December 31, 2019); (2) planned delivery at the survey site (hospital); (3) pregnant women aged > 18. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) missing integrated and clear medical records; (2) not giving birth in the hospitals; (3) preexisting serious comorbidities before getting pregnant; (4) any confirmed current or past hematologic disorders; (5) use of assisted reproductive technology in the current pregnancy; (6) multiple gestation in the current pregnancy. In total, 5,551 women were enrolled in early pregnancy (Supplementary Table 1). Data on maternal iron markers in late pregnancy was available for 4,003 women. We excluded women with insufficient blood samples (n = 1,548), as well as those excluded based on the above criteria. The total population for analysis consisted of 3,360 women. All patient data relevant to this study were well recorded, with a missing data rate of less than 1% (Figure S1).

Exposures and outcomes

Given that fetal growth, development, and iron requirements peak in the third trimester, the primary exposure of interest was maternal iron stores, assessed by serum ferritin levels in late pregnancy. Obstetric and perinatal outcomes included: gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy (ICP), gestational hypertension (GH), and preeclampsia (PE); mode of delivery, postpartum hemorrhage (PPH ≥ 500 mL), postpartum admission to the intensive care unit (ICU), and hysterectomy; fetal distress (FD), intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), low birth weight (LBW, < 2500 g), macrosomia (> 4000 g), and preterm birth (PTB, < 37 weeks of gestation); amniotic fluid volume.

Laboratory procedures

Among women who completed follow-up to delivery, SF concentrations were measured in serum samples. Venous blood (3.5 ml) was drawn from a vacuum tube containing separation glue and centrifuged at 3000 r/min for 10 min at room temperature. SF was detected immediately after centrifugation. Serum SF in plasma was detected by ADVIA XPT automatic biochemical analyzer (purchased from Siemens Medical Diagnostics (Shanghai) Co., LTD.).

Results

TT-SF is an independent risk factor for both preterm birth and low birth weight

In univariate logistic regression analysis, TT-SF was significantly associated with the risk of LBW, PTB, ICP, and FD (Fig. 2and Supplementary Table 2). To assess the influence of study center, stratified analyses were conducted, revealing that TT-SF remained significantly associated only with LBW and PTB (Figure S2 and Supplementary Table 3). To eliminate the confounding effect of gestational age at delivery on the results, stratification by preterm birth status still showed a significant association between TT-SF and LBW, with no correlation with fetal sex. (Figure S3). Further analysis of FD was not feasible due to insufficient sample size for valid statistical inference (Figure S4 and Supplementary Table 4). To account for potential confounders, including center effects and known risk factors such as maternal age and pre-pregnancy BMI, we applied mixed-effects models to estimate the independent association of TT-SF with LBW and PTB. The results confirmed that TT-SF remained an independent risk factor for both outcomes after adjustment (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Table 5). We further examined whether the associations between TT-SF and LBW/PTB were modified by TT-HB or iron supplementation by including their interaction terms in the multivariable models. No significant interactions were observed, and neither TT-HB nor iron supplementation independently increased the risk of LBW or PTB (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Table 6). Taken together, these findings indicate that TT-SF is an independent risk factor for both low birth weight and preterm birth.

TT-SF exhibits an optimal discriminatory threshold that stratifies pregnant women into two groups with distinct pregnancy outcomes

To investigate whether there is an optimal threshold that can effectively distinguish the risk posed by TT-SF differences on the outcomes mentioned above, we employed the Youden index method, which identified an optimal TT-SF threshold of 18.285 ng/ml. Density analysis further indicated that TT-SF levels could categorize the population into two groups (Fig. 4A), representing potential differential subgroups, with no significant baseline differences between them (Supplementary Table 7). The group with TT-SF levels exceeding the threshold exhibited higher rates of LBW and PTB (Fig. 4B). Clinically, a TT-SF level of < 20 ng/ml is typically defined as ferritin deficiency, which is very close to the 18.285 ng/ml threshold. Even with the classic 20 ng/ml threshold, the high TT-SF group showed higher rates of preterm birth and low birth weight (Figure S5). The overall cohort showed a sensitivity of 0.831 for identifying LBW and 0.639 for identifying PTB, while the overall specificity was relatively low, at 0.485 and 0.482, respectively. The conventional cutoff (20 ng/ml) demonstrated a comparable discriminatory performance(Supplementary Table 8). Although inflammation is recognized as a primary cause of preterm birth, no significant difference in TT-WBC was observed between the two groups (Fig. 4C).

In the healthy population, SF levels gradually decline throughout pregnancy, reaching lower levels in the late stages. The risks of TT-SF for LBW and PTB outcomes varied across different iron supplementation strategy groups

To investigate the process and levels of SF changes during pregnancy, we extracted longitudinal data from the cohort without complications. The results indicated that SF levels gradually decreased throughout pregnancy, with levels of 75 ng/ml in early pregnancy, 28.9 ng/ml in mid-pregnancy, and 21 ng/ml in late pregnancy (Fig. 5A). The changes in HB followed a U-shaped curve, although the overall variation was minor (Fig. 5B). Additionally, iron supplementation during pregnancy slightly increased SF levels, but had no significant effect on the decreasing trend of SF AND HB levels over the course of pregnancy (Figure S6).

In the overall population, TT-SF was significantly associated with LBW and PTB in logistic regression analysis. After stratification by daily iron supplementation dose, no significant association between TT-SF and LBW/PTB was observed in either the low-dose or high-dose supplementation groups (Fig. 6A). Stratified analyses by timing of iron supplementation showed that TT-SF was significantly associated with LBW among women who received iron supplementation during mid-pregnancy, whereas no significant associations were observed in the early- or late-pregnancy supplementation groups (Fig. 6B). When stratified by duration of iron supplementation, TT-SF was significantly associated with LBW among women with supplementation durations of 1–3 months and 3–6 months, but not among those with supplementation durations of less than 1 month (Fig. 6C).

Discussion

Iron is an essential intracellular element that plays a key role in maintaining normal cellular function, particularly in oxygen transport and energy metabolism in red blood cells [1, 2, 13]. Absorbed iron is mainly incorporated into porphyrin-containing proteins and various functional enzymes, with hemoglobin and myoglobin responsible for oxygen transport and storage, and cytochromes involved in mitochondrial electron transport and energy conversion [14–16]. Therefore, by supporting oxygen delivery and energy metabolism, iron has an indispensable and fundamental role in the cellular life cycle. Because of the increased energy and oxygen demands of the fetus, iron requirements rise markedly during pregnancy, making this period a high-risk stage for iron deficiency [2, 10, 13]. Our longitudinal data show a physiological decline in serum ferritin (SF) levels as pregnancy progresses, which likely reflects increased iron utilization and expanded blood volume rather than an acute inflammatory response. Therefore, low ferritin levels are common in late pregnancy.

However, according to current reports, a decrease in ferritin during pregnancy is not always beneficial for pregnancy outcomes. First, as SF is an essential component for hemoglobin synthesis, insufficient SF leads to iron deficiency anemia [17]. Low HB levels in early pregnancy are associated with adverse outcomes such as low birth weight (LBW), preterm birth (PTB), small for gestational age (SGA), and stillbirth [18]. Additionally, SF itself, as a functional protein, may directly impact pregnancy outcomes, independent of hemoglobin. Studies have shown that low SF levels are associated with an increased risk of SGA [18]. Furthermore, a Cochrane review in 2012 suggested that daily iron supplementation during pregnancy could reduce the risk of low birth weight in infants [19]. These results indicate that both low SF and HB levels may lead to adverse pregnancy outcomes.

Given the physiological low iron levels during pregnancy and the relative safety of iron supplementation, most studies focus on the impact of decreased ferritin on pregnancy, often overlooking the potential harm of excessively high ferritin levels [18]. Recent studies have shown that iron deficiency is closely related to several adverse pregnancy outcomes, including low birth weight, preterm birth, and intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) [20–24]. Low SF levels are associated with an increased risk of SGA, while high SF levels are linked to PTB [20, 25]. Our study strongly supports this idea, showing that elevated TT-SF is independently associated with LBW and PTB, regardless of iron supplementation, and is not related to TT-HB levels, patient age, or pre-pregnancy BMI. Based on the accumulating evidence, a more plausible explanation is that TT-SF contributes to the development of PTB by activating certain inflammatory pathways. Common pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6, upregulate SF synthesis through the STAT3 pathway. When elevated SF is accompanied by increased CRP or IL-6 levels, it may serve as a surrogate indicator of an inflammatory cascade, which promotes cervical remodeling and activates matrix metalloproteinases, ultimately triggering spontaneous PTB [25–28].This mechanism can be understood as elevated ferritin being a byproduct of inflammation, with inflammation itself remaining the primary driver of preterm birth. From another functional perspective of ferritin, a more direct mechanistic explanation can be proposed: under non-physiological conditions, ferritin may exhibit pro-oxidative activity due to iron release. The released iron can act as a strong reducing agent, catalyzing the generation of free radicals. Exposure to reactive oxygen species may expand the pool of reactive iron, thereby exacerbating oxidative damage. Such oxidative stress can also activate the cascade of events associated with spontaneous preterm birth, ultimately leading to its occurrence [29, 30]. However, unfortunately, this study was unable to confirm a causal relationship between TT-SF and these pregnancy outcomes. Therefore, elevated ferritin may simply be a marker of preterm birth rather than a cause, but this requires further mechanistic investigation.

Furthermore, our study, along with others, found a significant correlation between elevated TT-SF and LBW. While preterm birth may contribute to lower fetal weight, TT-SF itself may independently reduce fetal weight, irrespective of gestational age. Some studies have suggested a relationship between elevated SF levels and SGA. Fetal weight is closely related to placental blood flow, and fetal growth restriction due to placental pathological factors has been widely studied [31]. However, no relevant pathological abnormalities have been reported in the placentas of patients with elevated SF levels. A hemodynamic hypothesis may explain this association: during pregnancy, the physiological reduction of SF levels and HB may actually benefit maternal health by reducing blood viscosity and increasing placental perfusion, promoting fetal growth. In contrast, elevated SF may increase blood viscosity [13], reducing placental perfusion, which could result in abnormal fetal growth. Similarly, this result can also be interpreted in reverse: there may be an underlying cause, such as impaired iron utilization by the fetus or mother. Although ferritin levels increase compensatorily, this cannot reverse the actual iron deficiency in the body, which may lead to lower fetal weight. While these hypotheses lack definitive supporting evidence, they remain plausible explanations. Overall, the direct relationship between SF and fetal weight requires further investigation to be confirmed.

A recent study published in the Cochrane Database summarized findings from 40 studies on iron supplementation during pregnancy [32]. The evidence consistently indicates that iron supplementation reduces the risk of maternal anemia and iron deficiency at term, and may also reduce the risk of iron-deficiency anemia at term. However, conclusions regarding the effects of iron supplementation on pregnancy outcomes, particularly fetal birth weight, remain controversial. Notably, this review did not comprehensively examine the impact of multiple dimensions of iron supplementation—including daily dose, timing of supplementation, and duration of supplementation—on pregnancy outcomes. These aspects may represent important directions for future research on iron supplementation strategies during pregnancy. Some of the findings of the present study provide preliminary insights into these issues. Of particular interest is the timing of iron supplementation. We observed that physiological iron levels tend to decline during mid-pregnancy, and that among women who received iron supplementation during mid-pregnancy, higher TT-SF levels were unexpectedly associated with an increased risk of LBW and PTB. This finding contrasts with our initial expectations. Mid-pregnancy represents a critical period for determining LBW, characterized by rapid placental structural and functional maturation, accelerated fetal weight gain, and highly active placental iron transport. In early pregnancy, iron is primarily utilized for maternal plasma volume expansion and erythropoiesis, whereas in late pregnancy fetal weight is largely established [11, 33]. Abnormal iron status during mid-pregnancy may therefore be more likely to disrupt fetal growth trajectories. Collectively, these findings suggest that the association between TT-SF and LBW exhibits a distinct biological time-window and cumulative exposure pattern.

In conclusion, although there is a large body of research on the relationship between hemoglobin and pregnancy outcomes, the direct relationship between ferritin and pregnancy outcomes remains unclear. To fill this research gap, this study, through a multi-center prospective cohort, clearly demonstrates that elevated TT-SF increases the risk of PTB and LBW. However, this study has several limitations. For instance, it failed to completely exclude the association between SF levels and underlying inflammatory status, nor to establish a causal relationship between low birth weight LBW and PTB, and did not incorporate complex scenarios such as multiple pregnancies and ART. Nonetheless, our findings suggest that the management strategies for iron intake during pregnancy should be re-evaluated, with particular emphasis on the time window for iron supplementation and cumulative exposure to iron supplements.
