Perinatal, neurodevelopmental and childhood health factors in patients with functional neurological disorder: a retrospective case-record study in a tertiary neuropsychiatry cohort
Authors: Bruce Tamilson, Jimmy T K Tse, Leo Nihoyannopoulos, Mahinda Yogarajah, Niruj Agrawal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFunctional neurological disorder (FND) is a complex neuropsychiatric condition. While psychological factors have been widely studied, less is known about the role of perinatal, neurodevelopmental and childhood/adolescent physical health factors.ObjectiveThis retrospective case record review aimed to examine the prevalence of perinatal, neurodevelopmental and childhood/adolescent physical health factors in patients with FND and their associations with FND subtypes.MethodsClinical records of 282 individuals diagnosed with FND were analysed for perinatal problems, neurodevelopmental problems and childhood/adolescent medical history. Functional symptoms were categorised into seizure, motor, sensory and cognitive subtypes.ResultsPerinatal problems (31.9%), neurodevelopmental delays (18.1%) and neurodivergent conditions (19.6%) were frequently reported in this cohort. Additionally, 48.2% had a significant physical illness during childhood or adolescence. Functional seizures were significantly associated with neurodevelopmental delays.ConclusionNeurodevelopmental and childhood/adolescent physical health problems were frequently identified in this FND cohort and may be relevant to the heterogeneity and complexity of symptom presentation. These findings support considering neurodevelopmental vulnerability as an associated clinical dimension in patients with FND, while not implying or precluding a mechanistic role.Keywords: FUNCTIONAL NEUROLOGICAL DISORDER, PAEDIATRIC NEUROLOGY, PSYCHIATRY

Introduction

Functional neurological disorder (FND) is increasingly recognised as a common condition across both general and specialist clinical settings. Although the psychological and sociocultural determinants of FND have been widely examined,1,3 comparatively little attention has been paid to perinatal, neurodevelopmental and childhood or adolescent physical-health factors, particularly those occurring early in life. Emerging evidence suggests that neurodevelopmental vulnerabilities, developmental delays and neurodivergent conditions such as autism and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) may increase susceptibility to functional symptoms later in life.4 5 Structurally mediated neurological conditions, such as epilepsy or immune-inflammatory disorders, may influence neurodevelopment of limbic, sensorimotor and salience networks, as well as interoceptive and autonomic systems, which are implicated in FND pathophysiology.6 7 Recurrent or severe childhood illness may also influence the development of illness beliefs, somatic vigilance and expectations of bodily threat, which can interact with predictive processing mechanisms implicated in FND.8

Clinically, many individuals with FND report histories of physical-health problems, subtle neurodevelopmental differences (such as developmental delays, traits of neurodivergent conditions) or chronic conditions.9 10 Yet these factors are rarely examined systematically in FND research. Existing studies report higher rates of neurodivergent conditions, autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and ADHD in FND populations relative to the general population,4 11 and children with perinatal problems are at increased risk for neurodevelopmental conditions.12 13 Taken together, these findings suggest that neurodevelopmental problems may be more common in people with FND. Whether perinatal factors are also over-represented in FND, and whether any such associations are direct or mediated via neurodevelopmental pathways, remains to be determined. Neurodevelopmental conditions, including ASD, have a highly multifactorial aetiology, with risk more strongly linked to complex polygenic architecture and heterogeneous perinatal influences rather than single perinatal exposures.

This study aims to describe the rates of perinatal, neurodevelopmental and childhood or adolescent physical health factors among individuals diagnosed with FND, using a retrospective case record review. It also examines how these factors relate to specific FND symptom subtypes, with particular attention to functional seizures (FS). The findings may help refine diagnostic formulations and inform future research on developmental and biological aspects of FND.

Methods

This study employed a retrospective case record review design. The study included all adult patients who were under the care of a tertiary neuropsychiatry service. This centre serves a population of roughly 3.2 million residents across the South West London and Surrey region in the UK; the median age within this population is ~40 years. Regarding ethnic composition, around 75% identify as white, 9% as Asian, 8% as black and 8% as Mixed or from other ethnic backgrounds. Between 20% and 50% of the population are migrants. Referrals to the clinic come from a range of sources, including neurologists, psychiatrists and general practitioners (GPs). As such, the sampling frame is broadly reflective of the general neuropsychiatric population in the UK.

All individuals included in the study were assessed by a neurologist and a neuropsychiatrist separately, with both clinicians confirming a diagnosis of FND. No exclusion criteria were applied. A total of 282 cases were identified from the current caseload as of June 2025 and included in the analysis. All patients were assessed in the neuropsychiatry clinic between 2022 and 2025. Data were extracted from two primary sources: the assessment reports (comprising multidisciplinary clinical documentation) and GP summaries. These records provided detailed information on perinatal history, neurodevelopmental problems, neurodivergent conditions, childhood/adolescent medical diagnoses and current FND symptom subtype (eg, seizures, motor, sensory, cognitive). Data were coded using a standardised pro forma designed by the research team to ensure consistent and systematic data extraction. This report focused exclusively on perinatal, neurodevelopmental and childhood/adolescent health factors. Each FND subtype was coded as a binary variable (present/absent), and participants could contribute to more than one subtype category.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise demographic characteristics and the frequency of perinatal, neurodevelopmental and childhood/adolescent health factors. Association between early-life factors and FND subtype was examined using a series of χ² tests of independence. Each predictor–outcome pair was analysed in a 2×2 contingency table format. For every significant association, ORs with corresponding 95% CIs were calculated to quantify effect size and direction of risk. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05. Because the analyses were exploratory, no adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied. Results were reported using Pearson χ² values and exact p values. We used logistic regression to explore whether perinatal problems were associated with FS and whether neurodevelopmental delays attenuated this relationship. ORs with 95% CIs were reported, using α=0.05. Analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (V.28). This study was conducted as a service evaluation under National Health Service governance procedures and no formal ethical approval was required.

Results

The study cohort comprised 282 adults: 79.8% were female (n=225) and 20.2% were male (n=57). The age of participants ranged from 18 to 82 years, with a mean age of 40.62 years (SD=14.41). The age distribution showed slight positive skewness (0.397), indicating a concentration of younger individuals with a few older outliers. In terms of ethnicity, the majority of participants identified as white (71.3%), followed by smaller proportions as Asian (6.0%), black (5.0%), Mixed (4.3%) and any other ethnic background (1.8%). Additionally, 11.7% of participants had no ethnicity recorded.

Functional neurological disorder subtypes

FS occurred in 213 cases (75.5%). Functional motor disorder (FMD) occurred in 109 participants (38.7%), and functional sensory disorder (FSD) in 55 (19.5%). FS and FMD showed no gender differences. FSD was more frequent in females (48; 21.3%) than males (7; 12.3%). Functional cognitive disorder was least common (24; 8.5%), mostly in females (22; 9.8% vs 2; 3.5%) (table 1). Most participants had a single FND subtype (157), mostly seizures (120). Two-symptom patterns occurred in 74 cases, mainly seizure–motor (35) and motor–sensory (21).

Perinatal, neurodevelopmental and childhood/adolescent physical health factors and FND

A range of perinatal, neurodevelopmental and childhood/adolescent physical health factors were reported among participants with FND (table 2). Across the sample, early-life physical health problems were common, with two-thirds (67.7%) experiencing at least one perinatal, neurodevelopmental or childhood/adolescent condition. Childhood or adolescent illness or head/brain injury emerged as the most prevalent domain (48.2%). Inflammatory, infectious or autoimmune conditions were also frequent (33.7%). Neurodevelopmental problems, including neurodivergent conditions, were reported in 32.3% overall. Perinatal problems affected 31.9% of the sample.

Perinatal problems and FND

In this cohort, nearly one-third (31.9%) had at least one documented perinatal problem (table 3). The overall burden was slightly higher in females (33.3%) than males (26.3%). Premature birth (birth before 37 weeks of gestation) was 8.2%. Umbilical cord prolapse, though infrequent in absolute terms (1.1%), was higher than population estimates (0.1–0.6%). Neonatal complications were broadly comparable to general population estimates. Twin or multiple births and post-term births accounted for 3.5%, which appears higher than population estimates. In contrast, obstetrical intervention variables, including prolonged labour and instrumental deliveries, were lower than expected population rates. Only 1.8% (5/282) of individuals who underwent a C-section had reported no additional perinatal problems.

Neurodevelopmental problems and FND

Across the sample, neurodevelopmental problems were common, with 32.3% of individuals showing at least one concern (table 4). Neurodevelopmental delays (including motor and/or language) affected 18.1%, while neurodivergent conditions such as ASD and ADHD were also significant at 12.1% and 10.3%, respectively. Males showed consistently higher rates in several domains, particularly motor delay (14.0% vs 7.6%), dyspraxia (5.3% vs 1.8%), and learning disability (mild/moderate) (12.3% vs 5.3%), contributing to a higher overall rate of any neurodevelopmental problem in males (38.6%) compared with females (30.7%).

Childhood/adolescent physical health conditions in FND

In this cohort, childhood and adolescent physical health conditions were common, with 48.2% reporting at least one significant illness or head/brain injury (table 5). Inflammatory or infectious conditions were the most frequent category overall (33.7%). Several sex-related patterns emerged. Females showed higher rates of congenital abnormalities (8.0%) and joint hypermobility syndrome (JHS) (8.4%). In contrast, males demonstrated higher frequencies of epilepsy (17.5% vs 8.4%) and asthma (31.6% vs 24.0%).

Psychological factors linked to neurodevelopment and FND

The above factors were analysed separately from neurodevelopmental variables, as they may represent psychological or psychosocial influences that either contribute to or result from neurodevelopmental vulnerability in individuals with FND. Significant maternal mental health problems during childhood were reported in 20.9% of the total sample, with a higher prevalence in females (22.7%) compared with males (14.0%) (table 6). Among those reporting maternal mental health difficulties, 11.3% (n=32) reported a common mental health disorder (depression, anxiety, Obcessive Compulsive Disorder or Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder). Alcohol dependence was reported by 5.0% (n=14). Serious mental illness (bipolar disorder or schizophrenia) was reported by 3.2% (n=9). Postnatal depression and neurodivergent conditions were each reported by 2.5% (n=7). Personality disorder and eating disorders were each reported by 0.7% (n=2). A substantial proportion of the cohort (69.5%) reported experiencing adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), again more frequently among females (72.4%) than males (57.9%). Emotionally unstable personality disorder (EUPD) was documented in 8.5% of participants, with the diagnosis primarily found among females (10.2%) and only a single male case (1.8%).

Statistical analysis

In exploratory analyses (no correction for multiple testing), associations were observed between perinatal problems, neurodevelopmental conditions and functional conditions (table 7). The largest effects were seen for the reciprocal association between ASD and ADHD, particularly ASD predicting ADHD (OR=10.87, p<0.001), and perinatal problems were associated with motor delay (OR=8.30, p<0.001). ADHD was strongly associated with language delay (OR=5.29, p<0.001), and epilepsy showed association with FS (OR=10.29, p=0.011).

Inter-relationships between perinatal problems, neurodevelopmental delay and FS

A strong association was identified between perinatal problems and neurodevelopmental delays (χ²(1) = 10.42, p=0.001), indicating that perinatal problems may co-occur with later neurodevelopmental delays. Binary logistic regression analyses were conducted to explore whether neurodevelopmental delay attenuated the relationship between perinatal problems and FS. In univariate models, perinatal problems were significantly associated with increased odds of FS (OR=1.96, 95% CI 1.03 to 3.70, p=0.039) and with later neurodevelopmental delay (OR=2.71, 95% CI 1.46 to 5.04, p=0.002). Neurodevelopmental delay was itself strongly associated with FS (OR=3.16, 95% CI 1.19 to 8.38, p=0.021). However, when both predictors were included in a multivariate model, the association between perinatal problems and FS was attenuated and no longer statistically significant (OR=1.71, 95% CI 0.90 to 3.28, p=0.104), while neurodevelopmental delay remained an independent and significant factor (figure 1).

In multivariate models adjusting for covariates, neurodevelopmental delay remained significantly associated with functional seizures (OR=3.16, 95% CI 1.19 to 8.38, p=0.021), while the association with perinatal problems was attenuated and no longer statistically significant (OR=1.71, 95% CI 0.90 to 3.28, p=0.104).

To account for potential demographic confounding, a fully adjusted bootstrap logistic regression model (1000 resamples)—including age, gender, ACE—showed that younger age (B = –0.043, p<0.001) and neurodevelopmental delays (B=0.857, p=0.038; OR=2.36) independently predicted FS. Perinatal problems, ACE and gender were not independently associated with seizure risk. The model demonstrated adequate calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow χ²(8) = 9.14, p=0.331).

Discussion

Across the sample, early-life health or developmental problems were common, with two-thirds (67.7%) reporting at least one perinatal, neurodevelopmental or childhood/adolescent health condition. To date, no published studies have specifically examined childhood/adolescent physical health factors to this extent—particularly perinatal problems—in individuals with FND, limiting direct comparisons. Perinatal problems were reported by 31.9% of the cohort. In the absence of FND-specific data, we compared our findings with general population prevalence rates. Twin and triplet pregnancies comprised 3.5% of our sample, which appears higher than the estimated 1–2% in the general population; however, the small number of cases limits meaningful statistical comparison.14 These groups are associated with elevated risks for neurodevelopmental and neuropsychiatric conditions,15,17 though again, literature focusing on FND is limited. Particularly, several perinatal patterns diverged from population norms. The rates of complicated labour were lower in our cohort. These unexpectedly low rates could reflect recall bias, information bias or underdocumentation, particularly given the presence of a substantial migrant population where early medical records may not be available or discussed with clinicians. Other perinatal outcomes, including rates of low birth weight and neonatal complications, were broadly consistent with general population statistics.14 18 This could suggest either a true lack of elevated risk among those with FND or be attributable to the biases described above. Direct comparison of C-section rates is challenging because our cohort was born mostly between 1960 and 2006, during which C-section rates rose gradually from approximately 4% in 1960 to around 19% by 1999.19 20 Similarly, rates of preterm birth varied over time and between countries, further limiting the validity of direct comparisons.

Neurodevelopmental problems, including neurodevelopmental delays and diagnosed neurodivergent conditions, were present in 32.3% of participants. Neurodevelopmental—motor, language, combined or global—delays were found in 18.1% of the sample. The limited body of literature specific to FND restricts direct comparison. In recent years, speech and language delay affects approximately 13–14% of children aged 2–2.5 years in the UK, while gross and fine motor delays are each seen in around 6–7%. Overall, around one in five children do not meet expected developmental milestones at this age. This is broadly comparable to our cohort. However, any direct comparison should be interpreted with caution, as prevalence varies by region, year, socioeconomic context and external factors, including the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic. However, in our cohort, some of these delays may reflect underlying neurodevelopmental conditions rather than isolated developmental delays. This aligns with findings from a recent meta-analysis by Bowler et al21, which documented delayed developmental milestones in neurodevelopmental conditions.21 In our sample, neurodivergent diagnoses such as ASD (12.1%) and ADHD (10.3%) were prominent. A meta-analysis by Tamilson et al11 found that approximately 10% of individuals with FND also had ASD.4 11 ADHD prevalence in FND populations has varied from 5% to 25%, depending on FND subtype and diagnostic method.4 22 Physical health problems were also more common in this cohort. Pal et al9, studying 68 paediatric patients with FND, found that 51% had a chronic physical illness.9 In our cohort, inflammatory or infectious conditions were the most prevalent (33.7%) and ASD was associated with higher odds of recurrent infections, consistent with previous studies.23 24 This is consistent with prior studies noting heightened immune dysregulation in neurodevelopmental conditions.25 26 However, non-biological factors, including differences in healthcare utilisation and access, may also contribute to this association. Males in the cohort demonstrated higher rates of epilepsy (17.5% vs 8.4% in females). Prior studies report mixed evidence for sex differences. For instance, Christensen et al (Denmark) reported that among localisation-related epilepsies, men were more affected (55% vs 45%), whereas idiopathic generalised epilepsies were more common in women (59% vs 41%).27 In our cohort, a significant proportion of those with epilepsy also had FS, consistent with prior findings showing that 5–10% of individuals with epilepsy may experience FS.28

Significant maternal mental health problems during participants’ childhood were reported by 20.9% of the cohort. Perinatal mental health problems are defined as mental health conditions occurring from 3 months prior to conception up to 2 years postpartum. In the UK, the prevalence of any perinatal mental health condition is approximately 25.8%, with common mental disorders (CMD) accounting for 25.3%.29 In contrast, the prevalence of maternal CMD in our cohort was 11%. This markedly lower rate is likely attributable to recall bias, information bias, under-reporting and limitations in historical documentation. The combined prevalence of severe mental illness, personality disorder and eating disorders in the general population is estimated at 1.2%.29 In our cohort, the combined prevalence of these conditions was 4.6%, representing a substantially elevated burden of more severe psychopathology. Literature supports the influential role of maternal mental health on child development.30 31 Prenatal stress is linked to increased risks of preterm birth and heightened physiological stress responses, while postnatal depression and anxiety may impair attachment, caregiving and developmental outcomes.31 EUPD was identified in 8.5% of participants, predominantly among females (10.2%) with only one male case (1.8%). This sex disparity aligns with population-level data.32 Rueber et al found elevated rates of personality pathology in patients with FS, with clusters resembling borderline personality profiles being especially prominent.33 However, this finding must be interpreted with caution in the context of the high prevalence of autism in FND. There is increasing evidence that autistic women are disproportionately misdiagnosed with EUPD due to overlapping features, combined with gendered diagnostic biases and under-recognition of autism in females.34 This raises the possibility that some cases classified as EUPD may reflect unrecognised or misattributed autistic neurodevelopmental profiles. A substantial proportion of participants (69.5%) reported at least one ACE, which is consistent with previous studies.2 Health and developmental issues are known to increase the risk of ACEs.35 Data from the US National Survey of Children’s Health (2019–2020) found that over one-third of children with two or more ACEs had a chronic health condition requiring specialised care.36 37

Several patterns of early developmental factors emerged in our data, highlighting interconnected pathways between perinatal events and later neurodevelopmental outcomes. C-section and neonatal complications were associated with an increased risk of ADHD, consistent with findings from Zhang et al, who reported in his meta-analysis that c-sections are associated with an increased risk of ADHD and ASD.38 However, that analysis did not account for the indications for C-section, limiting causal inference and raising the likelihood of confounding by indication. ADHD itself was linked to elevated rates of language delay, affecting approximately 30–40% of children with ADHD compared with 7–10% in the general population.39 An increased prevalence of JHS in FND has been reported. Nisticò et al reported that 55% of patients with FND, 44.4% with ASD and 30.8% of neurotypical controls scored above the Screening Questionnaire for Childhood Hypermobility (SQ-CH) cut-off, with both clinical groups scoring significantly higher than controls. Chen et al found that 74% of individuals with FND met criteria for JHS, compared with 45% without FND. However, JHS was present in only 6.7% of participants overall, well below general population prevalence (17.2%)40,43 likely reflecting underascertainment due to reliance on case record review. Premature birth was identified as a risk factor for ASD, with risk increasing in a dose-dependent manner relative to gestational age.44 45 In our cohort, ASD demonstrated associations with other neurodevelopmental conditions, including high comorbidity with ADHD (reported in 30–70% of cases in the previous studies),46 early language delay (present in 60–75% of autistic toddlers)47 48 and increased vulnerability to recurrent infections24 in keeping with existing evidence.

Neurodevelopmental delays in speech, motor and social communication domains are increasingly understood as reflecting atypical maturation and integration of distributed neural systems, including sensorimotor, language and socioemotional networks.49 50 Such delays are associated with reduced motor automaticity, impaired interoceptive integration and emotional dysregulation, all of which can increase susceptibility to functional symptoms under stress or attentional strain.51,55 Moreover, our findings are consistent with reports that developmental delays frequently co-occur with neurodevelopmental conditions including ADHD and ASD, both of which are overrepresented in FND populations and may exacerbate alterations in sensory processing and attentional bias.56

Perinatal problems were associated with later neurodevelopmental delay, and both were linked to FS in univariate analyses; however, this association with perinatal problems was attenuated after adjustment, while neurodevelopmental delay remained independently associated with seizures. There exists a substantial body of literature supporting the association between perinatal problems and the increased risk of neurodevelopmental problems.57,59 Furthermore, early childhood/adolescent illnesses and developmental disturbances are known to negatively impact neurodevelopmental trajectories.60,62 Individuals with neurodevelopmental conditions are at a heightened risk of experiencing atypical perceptual processes, including differences in theory of mind, alexithymia, interoceptive awareness and sensory processing.56 These perceptual differences are often sensitive to various forms of stress, physiological, physical or psychological, which can serve as potential triggers or exacerbating factors.63,65 Stress is particularly impactful when an individual faces a mismatch between demands and their capacity to cope. Under such conditions, especially in the context of atypical perceptual processing, the vulnerability to developing FND may be significantly increased.66 67

Childhood/adolescent illnesses, especially when recurrent, prolonged or medically significant, may also play a critical role in shaping illness-related cognitions and bodily awareness. Heightened interoceptive monitoring, reinforced symptom-focused behaviours and sensitisation of the stress response system may embed maladaptive illness narratives early in development.68 69 Even in the absence of overt trauma, these experiences may interact with underlying neurobiological vulnerabilities in ways that are relevant to functional symptom presentation.70

Taken together, these findings are consistent with integrative models of FND in which early biological adversity may be associated with later neural and psychological vulnerability, shaped further by developmental context and illness experiences, and influenced by biopsychosocial factors across the lifespan.6870,72 Consideration of these early risk factors may be informative for developmental formulation and clinical management in FND.

Strengths and limitations

This study offers clinical cohort analyses focusing specifically on perinatal, neurodevelopmental and childhood/adolescent health factors of FND. Additionally, data were extracted from multidisciplinary clinical assessments and GP summaries, enhancing the validity of the findings. The use of cross-tabulations, χ² tests and logistic regression provided robust statistical insight into associations between early neurodevelopmental factors and FND subtypes. Despite its contributions, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design introduces potential recall and reporting biases, especially for perinatal and early childhood data that rely on adult self-report or third-party accounts. Some participants may not have had access to detailed birth or developmental histories, likely leading to underreporting of certain risk factors. Without a matched control group, interpretation of frequency of early-life conditions in relation to FND is necessarily cautious, although comparisons with population estimates were undertaken where feasible.

Participants were drawn from a UK-based tertiary neuropsychiatric service, likely over-representing individuals with more severe, complex and chronic FND, including a higher proportion of FS and those with neurodevelopmental, perinatal and early-life physical health factors. UK referral pathways and long waiting times may further select for patients with entrenched difficulties. Individuals with more circumscribed adult-onset psychosocial precipitants may be under-represented, as these are often managed in primary care, general mental health services or neurology. Additionally, people with borderline learning difficulties, neurodivergent conditions, high anxiety or limited social support may be under-represented due to barriers to travel and engagement. These factors limit generalisability and should be considered when interpreting the findings. Future research should aim to replicate these findings in population-based samples, include matched controls and explore longitudinal pathways from early-life adversity to FND onset.

While the statistical analyses yielded several significant associations, there are important limitations that must be acknowledged. First, no correction was made for multiple testing. Age and gender were not included as covariates in the χ². Given known gender disparities in FND and neurodevelopmental conditions, and the wide age range in the sample, failing to control for these variables may have introduced confounding effects. Although many variables were treated as independent predictors, several are likely to be interrelated. For instance, perinatal problems, neurodevelopmental delay and psychiatric comorbidities often co-occur and may share overlapping aetiological pathways. Treating them as discrete, independent factors risks overestimating individual effects and underestimating complex interactions.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that perinatal, neurodevelopmental and childhood/adolescent physical health factors were commonly documented in this tertiary neuropsychiatric FND cohort. Over two-thirds of the cohort experienced at least one perinatal or neurodevelopmental or health-related vulnerability during childhood/adolescence. These findings can be understood within an allostatic stress–diathesis framework, in which perinatal problems and neurodevelopmental problems represent early biological vulnerabilities that increase sensitivity of stress-regulatory systems. Cumulative physical and psychosocial stress across childhood and adolescence may then contribute to allostatic load, leading to dysregulation of arousal, threat processing, interoception and motor control. FND symptoms may emerge when adaptive allostatic mechanisms become overwhelmed, reflecting the interaction between early neurodevelopmental vulnerability and later environmental stressors. This perspective integrates biological, developmental and psychosocial influences and supports a neurodevelopmentally informed biopsychosocial understanding of FND.

The observed associations between perinatal problems, neurodevelopmental delays and FS should be interpreted cautiously but suggest that early neurodevelopmental vulnerability may be relevant to later symptom presentation, alongside psychosocial influences across the lifespan.

Clinical implications

These findings underscore the potential value of considering a developmental perspective in the assessment of FND. Given the observed associations between perinatal factors, neurodevelopmental delays and FS, it may be useful to include early-life health and developmental history as part of a comprehensive clinical evaluation. Consideration of neurodevelopmental conditions such as ASD and ADHD may also be clinically informative in some patients, as these were commonly identified in the cohort and could be relevant to symptom presentation and management.
