Temporal and spatial dynamics of urban heat in the City of Córdoba, Argentina
Authors: Florencia Sangermano, María Lila Asar, Andrés M. Visintin, Elisabet Benítez, Francisco F. Ludueña-Almeida, Elizabet L. Estallo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionClimate change is expected to exacerbate the Urban Heat Island (UHI) effect in the coming decades by altering the structure and function of urban ecosystems and impacting human health.MethodsThis research assesses the dynamics of the UHI effect in the city of Córdoba, Argentina, by analyzing temperature trends over the past 19 years. Urban areas were classified into five classes using satellite imagery. Monthly average daytime and nighttime MODIS Land Surface Temperature (LST) were used to assess thermal differences across urban classes and temporal trends.ResultsDaytime urban cooling occurred during the winter and heating during the summer, while at nighttime, the city experienced heating year-round. Over 19 years, urban areas presented the highest significant increase in temperature during nighttime, with some areas also experiencing increases during both day and night. Shrublands and crops also showed an increase in LST, particularly during nighttime. Increasing trends in LST during the night overlap with 89.2 % of the population, 90.5 % of households, and 87 % of households with unsatisfied basic needs.DiscussionThe population of the city of Córdoba is highly exposed to the UHI, and the exposure is intensifying, potentially leading to heat-related diseases, spreading disease vectors such as mosquitoes, increasing energy and water consumption, and decreasing the overall quality of life for city dwellers.

Introduction

Land use and land cover change replace natural cover with a dense concentration of pavement, buildings, and impervious surfaces. These changes diminish water absorption capacity, altering hydrological cycles and energy balance, causing the Urban Heat Island (UHI) effect through increased heat accumulation and heat emissions [1]. The UHI is the difference between the air temperature (AT) within the city and the AT of its surroundings [2]. Warmer air in urban areas compared to the cooler air in nearby rural surroundings defines atmospheric urban heat islands (AUHIs), which could be measured by meteorological stations or mobile transects along cities [2,3]. When the phenomenon is monitored with remote-sensing data, we refer to it as surface urban heat island (SUHI), as the parameter studied is no longer AT but the land surface temperature (LST) [2,4,5]. The SUHI is a complex phenomenon that results from the interactions of multiple factors, including building density, height, and material, as well as the type and density of vegetation cover and the abundance of water, which modify the surface energy balance throughout the urban environment [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. The thermal infrared radiation retrieved from satellite observations provides a spatially continuous representation of the urban LST at the satellite overpass time. The difference between the urban and rural LST is known as the SUHI Intensity and is controlled primarily by differences in evapotranspiration and aerodynamic roughness [10,11].

The UHI changes the structure and function of the urban ecological system, affecting urban residents' health [12,13]. Moreover, the UHI affects ecological processes, such as survival, feeding, development, and reproductive rates of ectotherm organisms, including insects, which derive the heat they require from their environment [14]. The UHI has adverse effects on water and air quality [15], net primary productivity [16], and biodiversity [17]. In Córdoba, high summer temperatures have been associated with increases in respiratory infections, particularly in populations with poor living conditions [18], as well as increased mortality during summer heatwaves [19]. The UHI also relates to increased economic impacts, as it can significantly impact the level and pattern of energy demand, making cities hotter in summer and warmer in winter. Moreover, the UHI effect would increase energy demand associated with cooling during the summer, while a warming energy reduction would be evident in winter. Finally, cooling and heating are typically energy-intensive processes and can lead to high energy costs during extreme weather conditions [20]. Global climate change is expected to intensify temperature trends and increase the frequency of extreme heatwaves over the next few decades [19,21]. Numerous studies project that extreme heat waves will become more frequent in a warming climate, contributing to increased variability in surface temperatures [[22], [23], [24]]. Temperature variability, therefore, serves as a relevant indicator of both changing weather patterns and broader climate change. Trends in average surface temperature may reflect shifts in maximum temperatures, minimum temperatures, or both [25].

To minimize the UHI effect on human health and energy consumption, it is crucial to understand LST and AT behavior. Today, 56 % of the world's population (4.4 billion people) resides in cities, and the urban population is expected to double by 2050, with 7 out of 10 individuals projected to live in cities [26]. In Argentina, the proportion of urban dwellers was 92.6 % in 2024, with an annual growth rate of 0.47 % [27]. Understanding the spatial pattern, temporal trajectories, and populations affected by changes in surface urban heat is therefore of utmost importance. This work aims to characterize the spatial distribution and temporal dynamics of the surface urban heat island effect in Córdoba City, Argentina. Moreover, it assesses the spatial distribution of surface urban heat trends over the past two decades, using socioeconomic population data, to identify the exposure of sensitive populations to urban heat trends.

Materials and methods

Study area

This study focuses on the Capital Department of Córdoba, Argentina, which spans an area of 562 km² and has a population of approximately 1.5 million. Over 60 % of residents live in urban zones, making it the second-largest city in the country [28]. Between 1988 and 2019, the area experienced an extensive increase in agriculture and urbanization, at the expense of reducing horticulture, forest, and grassland land cover types [29,30]. Land cover changes affect the heterogeneity of the landscape, contributing to its degradation and impacting the provision of ecosystem services [30].

Córdoba experiences marked seasonality, with average annual maximum and minimum temperatures of 24 °C and 12 °C, and an average yearly rainfall of 800 mm [31,32]. The dry season generally occurs from April to September, while the wet season typically occurs from October to March.

Extraction of urban areas

Characterization of urban areas within the borders of the Capital Department of Córdoba, Argentina, was based on the analysis of medium-resolution satellite imagery. Sentinel-2 satellite images for 2022 containing low (<10 %) cloud coverage (143 images) were analyzed to remove remaining clouds and cloud shadows based on the Quality Assurance (QA) band bits 10 and 11. We then aggregated each band of the 143 images using the median operator. A built-up index at 10 m resolution was calculated based on the decomposition of visible bands into a Hue-Intensity-Saturation (HIS) color space. In our study area, urban regions present high intensity and low saturation. In contrast, non-urban areas exhibit high or low intensity, depending on the land cover type, and show overall high saturation. These differences in the response of urban areas to the HIS space allow for the identification of built-up surfaces. Based on this, a built-up index was constructed by subtracting the saturation image (S) from the intensity image (I) and dividing the result by their sum: (I-S)/(I + S).

Urbanization density (Figure S1.a in supplemental information) was calculated as the proportion of built-up areas within a 2 km radius moving circle (12.57 km2). The result was used to classify the region (Figure S1.b) as rural (<10 % built-up), low-density (between 10 and 20 % built-up), medium-density (between 20 and 30 % built-up), high-density (between 30 and 40 % built-up), and very high density (>40 % built-up).

Land surface temperature dynamics

We used MODIS Aqua (MYD11A2 V6) 8-day daytime and nighttime Land Surface Temperature (1 km resolution) to generate a monthly average LST time series. The QA band was used to extract the pixels deemed to be of “good quality”. After filtering good-quality pixels, we calculate monthly averages, producing a monthly time series spanning 2003 to 2022 (19 time intervals). Data gaps present in nighttime LST were filled through a harmonic interpolation, only if fewer than 3 consecutive months were missing.

To assess the typical surface urban heat dynamics throughout the year and across different levels of urbanization, we calculated monthly daytime and nighttime climate normals. Differences in land surface temperature (LST) between day and night across each urbanization category (rural, low, medium, high, and very high urbanization) were examined using a Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by a Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons.

Monthly LST anomalies were calculated to assess interannual trends using the Mann-Kendall Tau (MKT) correlation and significance estimator [32]. MKT is a nonparametric method for estimating monotonic trends, where 1 indicates a consistently increasing trend, zero indicates no change, and −1 indicates a consistently decreasing trend [32,33]. The nonparametric Theil-Sen slope estimator is robust to outliers [34] and determines the median slope among all possible pairs of observations over time. This metric was used to estimate the cumulative magnitude of change in LST over the 19-year period by multiplying the slope ( °C/month) by the total number of months (228). The result of the Mann-Kendall Tau significance test was used to map areas with significant increases in daytime, nighttime, and both daytime and nighttime temperatures. These areas are critical for urban heat island management, as increasing LST suggests an intensification of the SUHI effect.

To estimate the population affected by significant (p < 0.05) increasing temperature trends, we intersected the map of areas affected by significant increases in daytime, nighttime, and both daytime and nighttime temperatures with census maps depicting the total population, the number of households, and households with unsatisfied basic needs by neighborhood. Households with unsatisfied basic needs, as defined by Argentina’s National Institute of Statistics and Censuses [33], are those that meet at least one of the following criteria: they live in inadequate housing (e.g., rental rooms, hotels, or dwellings not intended for residential use), lack access to a toilet, are critically overcrowded (more than three people per room), have at least one school-aged child (6–12 years old) who does not attend school, or have more than four dependents per employed household member, and the head of the household has not completed third grade of primary school. To estimate the number of people affected by increases in LST, we weighted the census data provided at the neighborhood level by the proportion of each neighborhood affected by the temperature trends.

A land cover map of the city of Córdoba [28] at a 100 m resolution was used to characterize the land cover types most affected by the significant temperature increases. Land use – land cover classes were aggregated into urban, crops, shrubland, bare soil, and woody vegetation. Water and roads were excluded due to their small size compared to the LST pixel resolution.

Every analysis across datasets requires deciding on a minimum unit of analysis. For all analyses that required comparing the spatial pattern of low-resolution LST with other higher-resolution datasets, we performed it at the resolution of the dataset with a smaller pixel size. For this, LST was downscaled using the nearest neighbor resampling, ensuring no distortions in data values and assuming no temperature variation within a 1 km pixel.

MODIS and Sentinel data were pre-processed in Google Earth Engine (GEE), and the built-up index, urban classification, and trend analysis were performed in TerrSet2020 [34]. Statistical analysis was performed in R 4.2.1.

The nighttime LST in urban areas was significantly higher than in rural areas throughout the year (Figs. 1B and Fig. 3). Moreover, the level of urbanization relates to nighttime urban heat. High-density urbanized areas presented significantly warmer temperatures than lower-density areas (p < 0.05). However, there were no significant differences in nighttime LST between high and very high-density areas.

We observed significant (p < 0.05) increases in LST over the 19 years analyzed within the city of Córdoba. More than half of the study area (58.9 %) experienced a temperature increase between 2003 and 2022. Most areas showed increases in temperature at night (44.8 %), while 12.8 % experienced increases during both day and night (Fig. 4). Temperature increases were mainly found in urban areas (Table 1), with 82.7 % of the significant trends occurring in areas classified as Urban. We evaluated the average trend within each land cover type to see the effect of land cover on the magnitude of the LST trends over 19 years (Table 3, Fig. 5). We found that nighttime LST increased uniformly by an average of 1 °C during the study period. The increase was across urban areas, crops, shrublands, and bare soil. On the other hand, nighttime LST decreased by almost 1 °C in areas of woody vegetation. The magnitude of significant increases in daytime temperature was lower than for nighttime temperature. Given that the magnitude of change varied considerably within urban regions, we disaggregated the classes to their original land use classification (urban high, urban mid, urban low, and urban very low). With this disaggregation, it is evident that the highest magnitude of daytime increases occurred in high-capacity urban areas, with an increase of 0.77 °C during the analyzed period. This result aligns with the urban density analysis presented above (Table 2). Shrublands and bare soils experienced the second- and third-highest magnitudes of temperature increase during the daytime, respectively (0.61 °C and 0.46 °C). Discussion

Our research reveals that the city of Córdoba experienced urban cooling and UHI effects during the study period, with variations depending on the season, built-up density, and land cover type.

In the summer months, which correspond with the wet season, the agricultural fields surrounding the city of Córdoba are green and present higher vegetation moisture due to irrigation during the crop-growing season [35]. During this time, urban areas experience the expected positive urban heating effect, related to higher evapotranspiration in rural areas compared to the city due to increased water availability from irrigation and precipitation [36]. Our results on urban heating during the summer also agree with [37] for temperate areas, and with [38,39], which found that the magnitude of urban heating is more prominent in the summer months and varies with seasons due to changes in solar intensity, ground cover, and weather.

Daytime urban cooling in Córdoba happened during the dry season (fall, winter, and spring), agreeing with [40], who found an annual cooling in the city during the dry season between 2003–2016. Others [38,41] have also found negative urban heat during the daytime in Córdoba. Urban cooling is not uncommon in cities surrounded by croplands [36,41]. A pattern of urban cooling during the day and urban heating during the night has been reported in cities within dry ecosystems in Argentina [41]; however, seasonal differences were not assessed. The crop-growing season in Córdoba extends from September to May; thus, during the dry season, fields do not have live vegetation cover and become barren, leading to a decline in evapotranspiration (ET) across the landscape, compared to urban areas that continue being irrigated to maintain lawns [41]. Moreover, buildings create areas of shade, especially during winter when sun angles are low. This reduces the amount of incident solar radiation, which in turn decreases the energy absorbed by building materials, resulting in cooler surface temperatures [[42], [43], [44]]. The altered latent and sensible heat fluxes resulting from higher moisture and lower incident energy can lead to relatively cooler conditions in the city than in adjacent rural areas [36,45]. This urban cooling island effect (UCI) has also been reported in other arid areas [46,47].

Our trend analysis revealed that urban areas experienced a significant increase in nighttime temperatures, as well as both nighttime and daytime temperatures, over the 19-year period analyzed. Woody vegetated areas, although with small spatial coverage, experienced a significant decline in LST. Woody vegetation can regulate regional climate by maintaining water balance and evapotranspiration [48], and the replacement of woody vegetation by grasslands has been linked to increased temperatures [49]. Our results highlight the importance of maintaining and, ideally, increasing woody vegetation cover within urbanized areas to mitigate the increasing temperature trends.

Increases in daytime temperature occurred in mid-, high-, and very high-density built-up areas, with rural areas experiencing a decrease in LST of 1.23 °C. The disaggregation of the magnitude of daytime temperature trends by land cover indicates that daytime decreases in land surface temperature are predominantly in areas classified as low-capacity urban, crops, and woody vegetation, corresponding to our rural definition based on built-up density. The rural areas surrounding the urbanized city nucleus experienced an expansion of extensive agriculture between 1988 and 2019 [30]. The decreasing temperature trends in crops can be related to increased irrigation of extensive agriculture during the growing season. Thus, given that UHIs are defined as the relative urban heat in comparison with surrounding areas, the increase in agriculture in the region would lead to intensification of the UHI effect. Moreover, as with nighttime temperature trends, the effect of woody vegetation on temperature is also evident in daytime temperature trends, highlighting the importance of land cover in regulating both day and night temperatures. Future studies should evaluate the variability of these trends across seasons. This was not possible in this study due to the small temporal sample size, which is too small to infer the statistical significance of seasonal changes.

Urban heat and increasing LSTs in the city affect a large proportion of the population of Córdoba, including 84.7 % of households with unsatisfied basic needs. Urban heat causes health problems and economic impacts. Socially disadvantaged individuals are more vulnerable to the increased heat in urban areas, and households with unsatisfied basic needs often lack the resources to cope with extreme heat conditions [50].

Urban heat can impact air pollution [51,52], increase energy consumption for cooling [44], and increase water usage. Evidence of this is the increased morbidity rates in Argentina during the 2013–2014 heatwave [53]. Temperature increases can also affect the transmission of infectious diseases [54], with urban heat favoring the abundance of some mosquito species, such as Ae. aegypti [55,56], increasing the risk of Dengue transmission [57]. Córdoba has shown an increase in Ae. aegypti from 2009 to 2017 from January to March [58], corresponding to the daytime and nighttime UHI effect found in this study. Moreover, studies showed a stronger association between daily temperature ranges and respiratory infections during warm periods than during cold periods in Córdoba City, suggesting a higher risk of respiratory infections during the spring and summer months than during the fall and winter [18]. Climate change and its direct consequences, such as heat waves, are expected to intensify the effects of urban heat islands [19,59]. Similarly, extreme cold events may further amplify the impact of urban cool islands [60]. Prolonged exposure to extreme heat can aggravate a range of pre-existing health conditions by initiating pathological processes that may lead directly to fatal outcomes [19].

The UHI effect in Córdoba, combined with rising temperature trends, shows that the area is highly exposed to extreme heat. Additionally, these warming trends overlap with vulnerable populations. Therefore, policymakers and planning agencies should invest in urban planning strategies that reduce urban heat and promote behavioral practices to mitigate health impacts. Fu et al. [61] provide a comprehensive review of strategies that can be used to mitigate and increase community resilience to urban heat. Expanding urban greenery with woody vegetation through horizontal and vertical stratification of vegetation plantings [62] as well as vegetated roofing [63] can be considered long-term strategies to decrease urban heat. Cooling centers are important to provide refuge from heat during heatwave events. Cooling centers within neighborhoods are low-cost heat interventions that have been implemented in many cities worldwide, but are not available in the city of Córdoba. Cooling centers should be considered one of several adaptive strategies, as many people (especially from vulnerable populations) choose not to use these areas due to a lack of awareness of heat-related health impacts, inconvenience (e.g., low accessibility), or stigma [64]. Other strategies could include issuing warnings before heat waves with information on how to access cooling centers and providing transportation, offering energy assistance for residential cooling, promoting alternative working hours for outdoor workers, and ensuring access to drinking water (e.g., installing water fountains in public facilities) throughout the city. Any and all interventions should be co-designed through collaborations between the city planners, the research community, and the stakeholders to ensure success [65].

Climate change is expected to increase temperatures and alter the seasonal rainfall distribution in Córdoba, leading to drier winters and wetter summers. These changing patterns are likely to reinforce the SUHI effect. However, separating the impact of climate change from localized urban heating is a complex task that involves considering interactions across multiple scales, including local variables such as urban form, building materials, vegetation cover, and irrigation; regional agricultural practices like crop cycles, water-use efficiency, and evapotranspiration; and global influences such as atmospheric circulation and teleconnection patterns. Achieving a comprehensive understanding of these interconnected processes requires further interdisciplinary research. This study faces limitations due to data availability. Although MODIS LST offers coarse spatial resolution, making detailed analysis of urban temperature variability challenging, Landsat-derived mid-resolution LST data lacked enough cloud-free images to evaluate annual variability in urban and rural temperatures. Additionally, census data at the neighborhood level containing information on households with unsatisfied basic needs was only available for 2010 at the time of this research and may not reflect current vulnerability conditions. Health-related inequalities can be related to multiple socioeconomic factors beyond income level. For example, in Brazil, women and older adults experience higher rates of heat-related mortality [66]. To improve exposure assessments, future research should disaggregate census data by demographic characteristics.

Conclusions

The UHI effect is one of the most significant factors influencing urban climate worldwide. This study examined the temporal and spatial dynamics of urban heat in Córdoba, Argentina. It is the first to investigate this topic locally over 19 years of LST data, analyzing interannual and seasonal variations as well as identifying sensitive neighborhoods. The SUHI effect in Córdoba exhibits high temporal variability on a daily, yearly, and interannual basis. A SUHI is present throughout the year at night; however, during the daytime, the SUHI effect only occurs during the summer. In winter, the city becomes cooler than the surrounding areas. Moreover, the city has experienced rising temperatures since 2003, with some areas showing both diurnal and nocturnal trends. Results from this study will help city managers target mitigation strategies in neighborhoods that need them most.
