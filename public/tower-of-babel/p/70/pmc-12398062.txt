Strain-derived myocardial work indices in adult cancer survivors: results from an observational study and comparison with available reference ranges
Authors: Massimiliano Camilli, Federico Ballacci, Priscilla Lamendola, Marcello Viscovo, Giulia Tamburrini, Lorenzo Tinti, Ilaria Torre, Ludovica Amore, Stefan Hohaus, Filippo Crea, Gaetano Antonio Lanza, Francesco Burzotta, Giorgio Minotti, Antonella Lombardo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AimsAdult survivors of haematological malignancies are at increased risk of long-term cardiovascular sequelae. Several echocardiographic metrics have been tested to detect subclinical myocardial dysfunction before it progresses toward cardiac events. Myocardial work (MW) is a load-independent echocardiographic index that conjugates non-invasive arterial blood pressure and global longitudinal strain (GLS).MethodsSixty-three disease-free survivors of Hodgkin Lymphoma (HL) [49% male, median age 42 (33,0–50,5) years], without known cardiovascular disease or reported cardiological symptoms, were included in this observational study. Myocardial work data from cancer survivors were compared with those from healthy subjects recruited in the EACVI NORRE Study.ResultsMean left ventricular ejection fraction and GLS were, respectively, 57,0% (55,0–60,0) and − 18,0% (-19,2–17,1). Global work efficiency [GWE, 96% (94–97)], global work index (GWI, 1732 ± 340 mmHg%) and global wasted work [GWW, 78 (55–131) mmHg%] did not differ between male and female survivors; however, global constructive work (GCW, 2116 ± 386 mmHg%) was lower in males. Of importance, GWI and GCW were lower in cancer survivors compared to healthy subjects from the EACVI NORRE study (p = 0,0008 and p = 0,0324, respectively). When evaluating associations of MW indices with patients’ characteristics, only systolic blood pressure and ejection fraction were independently associated with both GWI and GCW at multivariable analysis.ConclusionFor the first time, this report provides values of MW indices in asymptomatic HL survivors without cardiovascular disease. GWI and GCW were significantly lower in HL survivors compared to healthy subjects. MW metrics might serve as valuable markers of subclinical cardiac dysfunction in this population.

Introduction

Adult cancer survivors are exposed to a greater risk of cardiometabolic comorbidities, cardiovascular (CV) events and mortality when compared to sex- and age-matched healthy subjects [1]. Heightened risk of CV disease in cancer survivors has been attributed to long-term toxicity of cancer therapies, shared risk factors between cancer and CV diseases, and biologic pathways related to the cancer itself [1; 2]. Large-scale studies highlighted a persistently elevated risk of CV disease that extended long beyond the duration of cancer treatment [1; 2]; in particular, long-term sequalae of blood malignancies treatment were associated with an increased risk of all incident CV diseases considered (ischaemic heart disease, non-ischaemic cardiomyopathy, heart failure, atrial fibrillation/flutter, stroke, pericarditis, venous thromboembolism) [3]. Moreover, and accordingly, cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) analysis revealed increased size and poorer function of both left ventricle (LV) and left atrium (LA), which was consistent with long-lasting cardiac consequences of chemotherapeutics, like the anthracyclines, that are cornerstone of treatment of many hematologic malignancies [3].

Given that anthracycline-related cardiotoxicity may take years before it culminates in symptomatic heart failure (HF) [4–6], there is an obvious need for imaging techniques to detect subclinical cardiac dysfunction in asymptomatic cancer survivors [7]. Non-invasive, strain-derived quantification of myocardial work (MW) should be considered in this setting; in fact, MW incorporates measurements of both speckle-tracking global longitudinal strain (GLS) and blood pressure (BP), thus obviating load-dependent biases that otherwise confound measurements of GLS and LV Ejection Fraction (LVEF) [8].

Here we aimed at evaluating MW values in asymptomatic adult survivors of Hodgkin lymphoma (HL) treated with an anthracycline-containing regimen. Results obtained from this population were then compared with those reported for healthy subjects recruited in the EACVI NORRE Study [8].

Methods

Study design

Patients with a history of Hodgkin Lymphoma (HL), treated at the Hematology Department and followed-up at the outpatient Cardio-Oncology service of Fondazione IRCCS Policlinico Universitario Agostino Gemelli of Rome, Italy, were asked to sign written informed consent to participate in an ambispective study. A retrospective segment of the study consisted of retrieving patients’ clinical data from the institutional electronic database, while an observational prospective segment consisted of elaborating MW data from an echocardiography planned as per clinical practice and guideline recommendations for surveillance of cancer survivors [9]. Serum cardiac biomarkers were also collected according to clinical practice and guideline recommendations [9].

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants had to meet the following inclusion criteria: i) history of HL treated with either three or six cycles of ABVD (Adriamycin, Bleomycin, Vinblastine, Dacarbazine) according to disease stage and risk factors ii), clinically confirmed complete response to treatment iii), at least one year duration of response after the last chemotherapy cycle, according to the definition of cancer survivors [9] iv), no reported cardiological symptoms. Moreover, patients were asked to complete the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ), in its short version including 12 questions (KCCQ-12). The KCCQ-12 is a validated health status measure for patients with overt or suspected HF. The KCCQ-12 contains four subdomains: Physical Limitation, Symptom Frequency, Quality of Life, and Social Limitations. Each subdomain provides an individual score from 0 to 100, with 0 and 100 denoting the worst and best possible health status, respectively. The mean of the four subdomain scores are presented as a summary score, with differences of ≥ 5 points being considered as clinically important [10].

Exclusion criteria were i) history of chest irradiation ii), reported cardiovascular symptoms or known cardiovascular disease iii), LVEF < 50% iv), high estimated left ventricular filling pressures and v), poor image quality at echocardiography. Eighty-three patients were screened, of whom 63 met the inclusion criteria.

All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study end points

The present study aimed to i) explore MW indices in asymptomatic adult HL survivors without cardiovascular disease ii), examine the influence of gender on MW indices ranges in this population iii), correlate MW indices with patients’ baseline characteristics iv), results obtained from our population were compared with those from the EACVI NORRE Study. The NORRE study attempted to provide reference ranges for indices of non-invasive MW from 226 heathy subjects with mean age of 45 ± 13 years and predominantly consisted of females (64,4%) [11].

Echocardiographic methods

All echocardiographic evaluations were performed by experienced operators using an ECG coupled GE Vivid E95, according to the American Society of Echocardiography/European Association of Cardiovascular Imaging recommendations [12]. For speckle tracking analysis, endocardial borders and myocardium of all segments from the apical views (four chambers, two chambers, and apical long axis) had to be clearly visualized throughout the whole cardiac cycle. Intra- and inter- observer variability assessments and reference values adopted in our echocardiographic laboratory have been reported elsewhere [13]. Images were then analysed offline using EchoPAC software v204 (GE Healthcare). As previously described, brachial artery-cuff pressure was measured 15 min after the end of echocardiographic evaluation with the patient lying in a calm and comfortable position [11].

Myocardial work analysis

Left ventricular speckle tracking strain analysis was performed semi-automatically by EchoPAC software v204 (GE Healthcare) in the three apical views and adjusted by the operator in terms of region of interest (ROI) width and positioning to optimize endomyocardial tracking. Markers for aortic and mitral valves opening and closure were required to set the beginning and the end of each main phase of the cardiac cycle. Brachial BP cuff was used to adapt the reference curve for left ventricular pressure estimation to time and amplitude. The software output displayed a series of indices which depicted the pressure-strain loop from various perspectives and provided a graphic representation (Graphical Abstract). The following main indices of MW were considered: global work index (GWI, total work performed by the heart from mitral valve closure to mitral valve opening); global constructive work (GCW, the work performed during shortening in systole added with work during lengthening in isovolumetric relaxation); global wasted work (GWW, the work performed during lengthening in systole added with work during shortening in isovolumetric relaxation); global work efficiency (GWE, constructive work divided by the sum of constructive and wasted work). The MW analysis was performed by two experienced operators (MC, PL).

Statistical analysis

Continuous data are presented as mean ± SD or median with interquartile range, as appropriate. The distribution of continuous data and residuals was evaluated by quantile-quantile plot inspection, and comparisons were then carried using the unpaired Welch’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test. Echocardiographic measurements were compared with distributions reported in the EACVI NORRE study using Welch’s unequal-variance t-tests. Intervals for GWI, GCW, GWW, and GWE were estimated using bootstrap methodology with 1000 resamplings. For each parameter we calculated the median and 95% confidence intervals using the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution.

Correlations between MW and other clinically relevant continuous variables (age, KCCQ score, NT-proBNP, systolic and diastolic BP, left ventricular ejection fraction, GLS, indexed left atrial volume, indexed left ventricular end-diastolic volume, average E/e’) were performed using Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlation. Parameters exhibiting significant correlations were used as predictors for a multivariable linear regression model to identify independent determinants of MW. Two-tailed P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Comparisons between our echocardiographic measurements and those from EACVI NORRE study were performed using Stata 18 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). All other analyses were performed using R 4.3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Overall population characteristics

Sixty-three adult HL survivors were included in our analysis. Patient enrollment strategy is presented in Graphical Abstract, while demographic, laboratory and imaging data are reported in Table 1. Median age was 42 (33,0–50,5) years and 49% (n = 31) of patients were male. There were some statistically significant differences between male and females with respect to haemoglobin count, echocardiographic and ECG indices, and KCCQ-12 Score (see also Table 1), but absolute values were within ranges of normality for either genders. Median NTproBNP was 50,0 (20,0–97,0) pg/mL, while median score for the KCCQ-12 was 81,6 (71,8–95,7). Median cumulative adriamycin dose was 200,0 (150,0-300,0) mg/m2. Mean follow-up was 4.2 (± 2.1) years.

Ranges for MW indices in adult HL survivors

Median GWE was 96% (94–97), mean GWI was 1732 ± 340 mmHg% and median GWW was 78 (55–131) mmHg%. These metrics did not significantly differ between males and females. Mean GCW was 2116 ± 386 mmHg%, with lower values being observed in males compared to females (2002 ± 409 versus 2226 ± 334 mmHg%, p = 0,045). Discussion

Cancer survivors’ surveillance results to be essential to prevent long-term cardiovascular morbidity, in particular late-occurring cancer therapy-related cardiac dysfunction (CTRCD) and HF [14]. Different imaging approaches, most of all involving echocardiography, have been proposed to document myocardial performance changes, from subclinical cardiac alteration to overt LV dysfunction [7]. It is known that two-dimensional LVEF does not represent a sensitive parameter in the detection of CTRCD, while GLS presents several limitations attributed to loading conditions, acquisition, image quality, technical issues and vendor-specific processing [15]. Myocardial work has the theoretical advantage of mitigating the load dependency of GLS, through the incorporation of blood pressure values and allowing longitudinal follow-up of myocardial performance without fluctuations in loading conditions [8]. While MW indices have been investigated during cancer therapy, in particular in breast cancer patients undergoing anthracyclines and human epidermal growth factor 2 inhibitors [16; 17], no reports are available in literature on the behaviour of MW parameters in adult survivors of haematological malignancies. In our population of 63 HL patients, we found that GWI and GCW resulted to be significantly lower in cancer survivors than those observed in heathy subjects from the EACVI NORRE population; this result should be discussed in view of the borderline-low median values of GLS detected in our population, highlighting the vulnerability of these patients and the additional opportunity offered by MW in cancer survivors’ surveillance. At the same time, the lack of difference in terms of GWW may be likely because this measure is affected by LV dyssynchrony, which was not prevalent in our population [17]. As well, in the absence of difference in GWW, the lack of difference in GWE should be partly expected.

Interestingly, the differences in GWI and GCW between HL survivors and healthy controls from the EACVI NORRE study reached statistical significance only in male patients. While this may suggest a potential sex-specific biological susceptibility—possibly related to sex differences in cardiac remodeling or myocardial reserve following anthracycline exposure—this interpretation should be approached with caution. Given the relatively modest sample size and the reduced number of participants within each sex-stratified subgroup, these findings may also reflect an issue of limited statistical power in subgroup analyses. Larger studies are warranted to determine whether the observed sex-related differences in MW indices are biologically driven or merely a result of sampling variability.

Univariable and multivariate analyses showed association between MW indices and baseline patients’ characteristics. Of importance, LVEF and systolic blood pressure had the closest association with investigated parameters. Of note, in the multivariable analysis, both GWI and GCW remained independently associated with systolic blood pressure and LVEF. Our results confirm evidence already available in literature. Many reports have verified the correlation between systolic blood pressure and MW parameters, due to its accounting in the MW calculation [8; 18]. Furthermore, as shown in a large sample of apparently healthy individuals from the STAges A/B and Determinants of Progression (STAAB) cohort study population, GCW and GWI directly correlated with LVEF values [19], as it was the case of our sample.

In our analysis, MW values did not exhibit correlation with health status, assessed by the KCCQ, and biomarkers of systemic congestion, such as NT-proBNP. However, the collection of this data further enriches the value of our work, proofing the absence of heart failure through laboratory and clinical data.

Limitations related to this report should be acknowledged. The number of patients enrolled in the study, as well as its single-centre nature, both represent the most important; on the other hand, the prospective segment of the study may strengthen results obtained. We decided to exclude a large number of cancer survivors followed by our clinic, introducing an obvious selection bias; however, we enrolled an highly-homogeneous population, with the same underlying hematologic malignancy, exposed to the same anti-cancer regimen, without cardiovascular manifestations and normal cardiac biomarkers. As well, HL patients exposed to radiation therapy (RT) were not included in the final analysis. While chest radiation is still largely indicated in the HL population, it notably induces direct fibrosis of the myocardium and epicardium, with increased cardiac stiffness and inevitable variations in MW evaluation [20]. However, further investigation is warranted in this setting. Finally, information on long-term outcomes of this apparently-healthy population is lacking, preventing the assessment of the prognostic implications of MW indices in these vulnerable patients.

Conclusions

Left ventricular mechanics, assessed by MW indices, may represent a valuable tool for precocious diagnosis of subclinical myocardial dysfunction in cancer survivors. For the first time, these results attempt to provide ranges of MW indices in an healthy population of cancer survivors previously exposed to anthracyclines. Our data were further compared with those obtained from the EACVI NORRE Study. We then analyzed correlations between MW parameters and survivors’ clinical characteristics.

Of importance, MW may facilitate CTRCD diagnosis in selected clinical scenarios, for example in those subjects with normal LVEF and borderline GLS, although its prognostic implications in this population should be still tested in larger studies. These parameters, when altered, may serve to guide specific cardiovascular surveillance and whether tested in dedicated clinical trials, to manage pharmacologic cardioprotection.
