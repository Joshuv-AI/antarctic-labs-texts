Subpulmonic Stenosis due to Systolic Anterior Motion of Elongated Papillary Muscle with Basal Chordal Attachment in a Patient with Congenitally Corrected Transposition of the Great Arteries
Authors: Homa Ghaderian, Sahar Asl-Fallah, Ali Hosseinsabet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
One of the complex congenital cardiac diseases is congenitally corrected transposition of the great arteries (cc-TGA). The most important feature of this anomaly is atrioventricular and ventriculo-arterial discordance. Several pathologies, such as subpulmonary obstruction, can accompany this anomaly. Several etiologies can cause subpulmonary obstruction. Echocardiography serves as an initial imaging method in assessing patients with this condition and in detecting accompanying anomalies. Here, we present a man with cc-TGA, accompanied by severe subpulmonic stenosis resulting from anterior systolic motion of an elongated papillary muscle with basal chordal attachment.

INTRODUCTION

One of the complex congenital heart diseases is congenitally corrected transposition of the great arteries (cc-TGA), where the morphological left atrium and morphological right atrium drain into the morphological right ventricle (mRV) and the morphological left ventricle (mLV), respectively. The mLV and mRV subsequently drain into the pulmonary artery and the aorta, respectively.[1] However, this condition may be associated with other congenital abnormalities such as Ebstein anomaly, ventricular septal defect (VSD), subpulmonary (left ventricular outflow tract [LVOT]) stenosis, and others.[2] Several pathologies can cause subpulmonary stenosis. Here, we present a man with cc-TGA and subpulmonary stenosis due to systolic anterior motion (SAM) of an elongated papillary muscle with basal chordal attachment.

CASE REPORT

The patient was a 30-year-old man with congenital heart disease who was hospitalized due to a prolonged fever lasting 1 month. On examination, he was febrile at 38.8°C, with bradycardia (heart rate = 50 bpm). His blood pressure was 125/79 mmHg, his respiratory rate was 20/min, and his oxygen saturation was 96%.

The electrocardiogram showed right axis deviation, a tall P wave, and complete heart block with a narrow QRS complex. The cell blood count revealed a white blood cell count of 12.3 × 103/mm3, with 82% neutrophils, and a platelet count of 152 × 103/mm3, without anemia. In addition, the C-reactive protein level and the erythrocyte sedimentation rate were 1.5 mg/dL (upper limit of normal range = 0.5 mg/dL) and 6 mm/h, respectively. The Streptococcus viridans was isolated once in blood culture, which was sensitive to penicillin and cephalosporins.

Transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiography (two and three-dimensional) revealed that atrial situs was solitus, ventricular looping was L-loop, the greater artery relation was L-malposition, accompanied by atrioventricular and ventriculoarterial discordance, and levocardia suggestive of cc-TGA. In addition, there was a high-muscular VSD (3 mm) with blood flow predominantly from the subpulmonary ventricle (mLV) to the systemic ventricle (mRV) in both systole and diastole [Video 1 and Figure 1]. Furthermore, there was dynamic subpulmonic (LVOT) obstruction due to SAM of an elongated papillary muscle with basal chordal attachment with peak pressure gradient = 145 mmHg, thickened, malcoapted and restricted motion of pulmonary valve cusps, moderate pulmonary valve stenosis (area by three-dimensional planimetry = 1.2 cm2) with severe pulmonary regurgitation accompanied by a small mobile particle (1–2 mm) on tip of anterior pulmonary valve cusp [Figure 2 and Video 2]. In addition, the function of the systemic ventricle (mRV) was mildly reduced, and there was moderate mitral and tricuspid regurgitation due to valve prolapse. The estimated mean pulmonary artery pressure, as determined by the pulmonary regurgitation profile, was approximately 18 mmHg.

Cardiac computed tomography angiography confirmed and delineated the anatomical structure of cc-TGA and an elongated papillary muscle with basal chordal attachment near the LVOT. It also demonstrated the absence of fixed stenosis in LVOT [Figure 3 and Video 3]. The patient was treated with intravenous ampicillin for 6 weeks as a possible case of infectious endocarditis, and the fever resolved during admission. The patient refused any other diagnostic and interventional procedure.

Multiplanar reconstruction of the subpulmonic region (*) in cardiac computed tomography showed no anatomical stenosis (a-c). It also revealed an elongated papillary muscle with basal chordal attachment that was probably responsible for the dynamic subpulmonic stenosis (arrow) (d). DISCUSSION

We aimed to present this case to demonstrate that SAM of the elongated papillary muscle with basal chordal attachment can be a cause for subpulmonic (LVOT) stenosis in a patient with cc-TGA. The subpulmonic (LVOT) stenosis in cc-TGA has been demonstrated in up to 50% of patients[3] and has different etiologies, including septal hypertrophy,[4] ventricular membranous septal aneurysm,[5] accessory tissue,[6] atrial and ventricular septal malalignment,[7] the mitral valve straddling,[8] and SAM of the mitral leaflet,[9] so its detection and its etiology is important in patients who are planned for corrective cardiac surgery or in the medical management of patients’ symptoms.

Cc-TGA cases with dynamic subpulmonic (LVOT) obstruction are rarely reported. In one case, dynamic obstruction occurred after tricuspid valve replacement due to mitral leaflet SAM and displacement of the interventricular septum toward the mLV, leading to episodes of hemodynamic instability that ultimately resolved with mitral valve replacement.[10] In another case, dynamic obstruction was caused by mitral leaflet SAM in the presence of mitral valve prolapse and a large aneurysmal membranous VSD prolapsing into the subpulmonic (LVOT) region.[11] In our case, the mechanism of dynamic obstruction, as mentioned above, was different.

The severity of the subpulmonic (LVOT) gradient detected in this patient was inconsistent with the gradient caused by severe pulmonary regurgitation alongside moderate pulmonary stenosis. In addition, that region was not narrowed, which did not support fixed stenosis. The VSD shunt was relatively vertical to the ultrasound beam and did not appear to participate in the recorded pressure gradient. In addition, the VSD shunt ran from the subpulmonary ventricle (mLV) to the systemic ventricle (mRV), indicating that the subpulmonary ventricle (mLV) had higher systolic pressure compared to the systemic ventricle (mRV) during systole, a difference that persisted into diastole.

Elongated papillary muscles with basal chordal attachment are involved in LVOT obstruction.[12] In our patient, SAM of an elongated papillary muscle with basal chordal attachment was the cause of obstruction in that anatomical region, because if there was SAM of the anterior mitral leaflet, it should be accompanied by highly eccentric posterolateral-directed mitral regurgitation that was in proportion to the severity of SAM and obstruction[13] In contrast, mitral regurgitation was not in that direction. The hemodynamic etiology of SAM in our patient may be attributed to the presence of an elongated papillary muscle with basal chordal attachment, accompanied by the hypercontractility of the subpulmonic ventricle (mLV) in the presence of pulmonary regurgitation, which is mediated by the Frank-Starling mechanism and augmented by decreased systemic vascular resistance resulting from infectious processes. In addition, the displaced interventricular septum toward the subpulmonary ventricle (mLV) is involved in the pathophysiology of obstruction. The absence of hypertrophy of the subpulmonary ventricle (mLV) indicated the lack of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. In cc-TGA, the SAM of an elongated papillary muscle with basal chordal attachment may be considered as a possible cause of stenosis of that anatomical region.

If it were possible, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging could better delineate the etiology of subpulmonic (LVOT) obstruction, not only by excluding fixed stenosis but also by providing the mechanism of the obstruction in that region in the absence of echocardiography artifacts, and better confirming the association between elongated papillary muscle SAM with basal chordal attachment and obstruction.

The subpulmonic (LVOT) stenosis is a predisposing factor for infectious endocarditis in this patient because it leads to turbulent flow, which predisposes the pulmonary valve to infectious endocarditis.[14] Regarding the possible diagnosis of infectious endocarditis in this patient, it should be noted that, by considering the 2023-modified Duke criteria, the presence of vegetation in echocardiography was considered a major criterion. The presence of a predisposition (in our case, congenital cardiac disease, significant valvular regurgitation, and LVOT obstruction), fever >38°C, and a positive blood culture at one time were considered as three minor criteria.[15]

In the echocardiographic evaluation of patients with cc-TGA, the probability of subpulmonic (LVOT) stenosis should be considered, and a comprehensive assessment of this region should be performed to identify its specific etiology, such as an elongated papillary muscle with basal chordal attachment, if present.
