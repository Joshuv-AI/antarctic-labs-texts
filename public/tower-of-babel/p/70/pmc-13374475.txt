Evaluation of the prognostic value of visual and quantitative CT analysis in myositis-associated interstitial lung disease
Authors: Sangmee Sharon Bae, Daniela Markovic, Augustine Chung, Jessica Channick, Andrea Oh, Tiffany De Leon, Fereidoun Abtin, Lila T Pourzand, Grace Hyun Kim, Jihey Lee, Sangwook Bae, Donald Tashkin, Jonathan Goldin, Christina Charles-Schoeman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate chest high-resolution CT (HRCT) images in idiopathic inflammatory myositis (IIM) using both quantitative CT and visual assessment.MethodsHRCT images from a single-centre longitudinal IIM cohort were reviewed both visually and by quantitative imaging analysis (QIA), which quantified the extent of ground glass (QGG), fibrosis (QLF) and total interstitial lung disease burden (QILD). Visual review identified ILD presence and pattern and excluded other comorbid disease processes. Linear regression assessed correlations between QIA measures and pulmonary function tests (PFTs). Trends of QIA and PFTs were analysed in patients with ≥2 measurements using mixed-effects models. Associations with all-cause mortality were evaluated using Cox regression.ResultsAmong 277 patients (mean age 51 years; 74% female; 77% dermatomyositis; 22% had antisynthetase syndrome), 168 had HRCT evaluable by both methods. ILD was identified in 51% by visual review. QLF and QILD had strong correlations with forced vital capacity (β=−0.7 and −0.59) and diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (β=−0.75 and −0.64) across all visits (p<0.0001 for all). Higher baseline QLF and QILD scores (HR 1.04, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.08) and HR 1.08, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.16) and worsening QLF and QILD trends in the first 12 months (HR 1.04, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.07 and HR 1.06, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.11) were associated with greater all-cause mortality (p<0.05 for all). Survival did not differ by the presence of visual ILD or by ILD pattern.ConclusionsIn this large, real-world IIM cohort, quantitative HRCT measures—particularly QLF and QILD—were strongly associated with lung function and predicted mortality. Higher baseline and early worsening of QLF and QILD were associated with increased mortality, whereas visual ILD presence and pattern did not predict survival.Keywords: Lung Diseases, Interstitial; Dermatomyositis, Adult Type; Polymyositis

Introduction

Interstitial lung disease (ILD) is a major cause of morbidity and mortality in idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM),1 2 and thoracic high-resolution CT (HRCT) plays a central role in the detection and evaluation of ILD.3 4 HRCT has been used to detect the presence of ILD, quantify its extent and identify changes in disease extent over time. Radiographic patterns of ILD have also been shown to be surrogates of important histological findings in idiopathic interstitial pneumonia and connective tissue disease-associated ILD (CTD-ILD).5,8

HRCT images are traditionally reviewed visually by a radiologist, which remains the reference standard for ILD diagnosis. Visual review offers pattern recognition and nuanced assessment of complex features that are interpreted within a clinical context, but is limited by subjectivity and interobserver variability and is time-consuming.9 Computer-assisted quantitative CT approaches are increasingly recognised for their ability to provide objective, reliable, reproducible quantification that is scalable to larger numbers and are sensitive to longitudinal change.9 10 Studies have demonstrated the utility of computer-assisted quantitative CT measures in myositis-associated ILD (MA-ILD), with associations reported across lung physiological measures and potential utility in identifying high-risk patient groups.11,14 The quantitative imaging analysis (QIA) in the present study employs a machine learning-based textural analysis algorithm that quantifies the extent of ILD-related parenchymal changes on CT, which has been validated in scleroderma-related ILD (SSc-ILD),15 and has also been employed as an outcome measure in clinical trials across diverse ILD subtypes.1316,21

Building on our prior study in antisynthetase syndrome (ASyS)-associated ILD, which focused on cross-sectional correlations between QIA and physiological outcomes,22 we hypothesised that quantitative CT metrics provide independent prognostic information beyond conventional visual scoring and clinical variables in MA-ILD. The present study extends the application of QIA to a broader real-world IIM cohort and evaluates both baseline and longitudinal QIA trajectories in relation to mortality. This represents a large-scale assessment of QIA as a potential prognostic biomarker in MA-ILD.

Methods

Patient selection

Patients with myositis consecutively recruited from a single quaternary referral centre between 2008 and 2023 were included. All patients with myositis met European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR)/American College of Rheumatology (ACR) classification criteria for at least ‘probable’ adult IIM and dermatomyositis (DM) subclass23 verified by chart review. ASyS was defined as possessing at least one antisynthetase antibody (anti-ARS ab) in the presence of at least one clinical feature: ILD, inflammatory myopathy, inflammatory arthritis, unexplained fever, Raynaud phenomenon or mechanic’s hands. Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) was defined as patients with anti-signal recognition particle (anti-SRP) or anti-3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase (anti-HMGCR) autoantibodies with proximal muscle weakness and markedly elevated creatine phosphokinase (CPK), or a muscle biopsy consistent with IMNM.24 Polymyositis (PM) was defined as patients who met EULAR/ACR criteria for PM subclass but not considered ASyS or IMNM.

Among patients with DM/PM/ASyS/IMNM in the cohort, those with at least one CT scan of sufficient quality for quantitative image scoring and/or pulmonary function test (PFT) outcome measures available for review were included in the current analysis (online supplemental figure 1).

Collection of clinical data

Baseline was defined as the date of the first HRCT scan available for review. For those who only had PFTs, baseline was determined as their first PFT after IIM diagnosis. We collected all available HRCT and PFTs done in the cohort as part of standard clinical practice until February 2025. Clinical data including demographics, medications, disease duration and comorbidities were obtained from patients’ charts and questionnaires. Date and cause of death that occurred until February 2025 were obtained from the chart and/or treating physicians. Laboratory assessments of CPK, aldolase, high-sensitivity C reactive protein (hs-CRP) and Westergren erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) were performed by the clinical laboratory using standard methods.

Myositis-specific abs (MSAs) were assessed using standardised lab protocols using either RNA immunoprecipitation or non-immunoprecipitation methods at commercial labs. Disease activity and damage were assessed using physician global myositis disease activity and damage scales by 100 mm visual analogue scale (VAS) and 5-point Likert scale.25 Serial PFTs, including forced vital capacity (FVC), total lung capacity (TLC) and diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide adjusted for haemoglobin (DLCO Hg), were retrospectively reviewed and reported as percentage predicted values (% predicted).

HRCT image analysis and ILD definitions

HRCT images were required to be thin-section (<1.25 mm), non-contrast, full inspiration images to be reviewed and scored. Scans were reviewed by three thoracic radiologists (AO, LTP, JG) and images not meeting sufficient quality or comorbid disease processes were excluded. Both visual and QIA were performed blinded to clinical outcomes.

Baseline HRCT scans were systematically re-assessed by two physicians (thoracic radiologist and pulmonologist) performing a consensus visual read independent of prior radiological reports. The visual read reported the presence of ILD, the presence of fibrosis and the overall ILD pattern. Presence of ILD was defined by radiographic findings consistent with ILD on HRCT showing at least one of the following features: (1) reticulation and fibrosis, (2) traction bronchiectasis, (3) honeycombing and/or (4) ground-glass opacification.26 Presence of ILD on the visual read (‘visual ILD’) was specified as either definite or possible ILD. Evidence of fibrosis was defined by the presence of reticulation, traction bronchiectasis/bronchiolectasis or honeycombing with architectural distortion and volume loss.27 ILD patterns were read according to 2018 and 2022 American Thoracic Society criteria for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis28 29 to describe usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP), non-specific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP) and organising pneumonia (OP) patterns. A mixed NSIP/OP pattern was noted, as described in previous studies.30 31

Computer-aided QIA was performed using the ‘QLF score’, a texture-based measure of disease extent derived from a supervised texture classification model.32,34 QIA scores were assigned as a percentage of the extent of ground glass (QGG), fibrotic patterns (QLF), honeycombing (QHC) and the quantitative total ILD (QILD) score that represented the sum of QGG+QLF+QHC. The term ‘QIA’ is used throughout to refer collectively to all four measures. The lungs were divided into three area-equivalent zones from apex to base (upper, middle and lower zones). Quantitative scores are expressed as a percentage of whole lung or of the zone of maximum involvement.

‘Clinical ILD’ was separately defined as ILD as documented by the patients’ treating physicians with HRCT reports demonstrating ILD findings. These patients may include those who were unable to provide HRCT images that met requirements for visual re-review.

Statistical analysis

Comparative analysis was performed between patients with and without ILD using Student’s t-test and Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables and χ2 tests for categorical variables. We used linear mixed-effects models to analyse the association between PFTs and QIA scores over time. Correlations were first explored in all patient visits with CT and PFT measures performed within 6 months of each other using linear mixed-effects models accounting for repeated measures and reported using a standardised regression coefficient (β). First 12-month and long-term (5-year) regression-based trends (slopes) of QIA and PFTs were calculated in patients with two or more measurements within the specified time frame. Associations between trends were reported as a standardised regression coefficient (β), representing the change in the dependent variable (in SD units) per 1 SD increase in the independent variable, facilitating a comparison of the relative importance of the predictors.35 Association strengths were classified as weak (<0.2), moderate (0.2–0.5) or strong (≥0.6). QIA scores were log transformed to fit model assumptions.

Associations with all-cause mortality were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards models, with baseline predictors modelled for time to death or last follow-up and results reported as HRs. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using tests based on scaled Schoenfeld residuals.36 For CT and PFT measures, baseline values as well as first 12-month trends were evaluated as predictors. The predictive value of QIA components over time with mortality was analysed using Cox regression with time-varying covariates to account for baseline and repeated measures over time. Models were further adjusted for demographics (age at disease onset and sex), ILD-specific MSA (positive anti-ARS ab, positive anti-MDA5 ab) and variables known to be associated with IIM mortality: cancer2 (ever vs never malignant cancers excluding non-melanomatous skin cancer), ever smoking37 38 and pulmonary hypertension (PH).39

All statistical testing was two-sided with a significance threshold of 0.05. Statistical analysis was performed using JMP Pro V.16.0 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA) and SAS V.9.4 (SAS Institute).

Results

A total of 277 patients were included in our analyses (online supplemental figure 1). Mean age was 51 years, 74% were females, 61% were white, 11% were black, 16% were Asian and 22% were Hispanic, and most patients had DM. At baseline, patients had a median disease duration of 1 year, moderate disease activity and damage per MD global VAS. A third of the cohort had either anti-ARS ab or anti-MDA5 ab (table 1). Forty-six per cent of patients within the cohort had CT evidence of ILD identified at some point in their disease per the treating physician’s clinical judgement (‘clinical ILD’).

Visual HRCT findings

Among the total cohort, 168 patients had CT images appropriate for review. On visual review, 86/168 (51%) had evidence of either definite or possible ILD (‘visual ILD’). Mixed NSIP/OP pattern was the most common ILD pattern (35%), followed by OP in 25% and NSIP in 18%. Fibrosis on the first available scan was present in 67% (table 1).

Patients with visual ILD were of similar age and sex distribution compared with those without visual ILD (table 1). The visual ILD group included more Asian and black patients, whereas DM/PM without visual ILD included more white patients. Anti-ARS ab and anti-MDA5 ab were higher in the visual ILD group, whereas anti-HMGCR, anti-TIF1r and anti-SAE abs were higher in patients without visual ILD. Patients with visual ILD were more often on rituximab, prednisone and less on methotrexate. Cancer and PH were more common in the visual ILD group. The visual ILD group had higher physician global damage scores, higher markers of inflammation (ESR, hs-CRP), more cough, dyspnoea and need for supplemental oxygen use (table 1).

Association of visual CT findings with QIA scores

The median QILD whole-lung score in the visual ILD group was 22 (IQR 12–37) (table 1). QIA scores were significantly greater in patients with visual ILD compared with patients without visual ILD (p<0.001 for QGG, QLF, QILD). The median QILD score was 25 (IQR 13–38) in the definite visual ILD group and 15 (IQR 10–28) in the possible ILD group, both of which were significantly higher than in patients without visual ILD (median 5 (IQR 4–11); p<0.01 for both comparisons). The zone of maximum ILD involvement was almost always the lower lung zone.

The visual ILD group had significantly worse % predicted FVC, TLC and DLCO compared with patients without visual ILD (table 1). Twenty-eight per cent of patients showed discordance between the visual ILD and PFT results, with 31% of those with visual ILD having a normal FVC (>80% predicted), and 25% of those without visual ILD having abnormal FVC (figure 1). However, QIA scores were very low in most patients with no visual ILD (figure 1). Eight patients with no visual ILD had QILD >10. On visual review, these scans demonstrated reticular markings that were attributed to dependent atelectasis, motion artefact or oedema related to heart failure.

Associations between QIA and PFTs

Higher FVC and DLCO Hg performed within 6 months of quantitative CT had strong associations with lower QLF, QILD scores (β=−0.59 to −0.75) and moderate associations with lower QGG (β=−0.37) in mixed-effects models (p<0.0001 for all, figure 2). We performed similar analyses including only patients with clinical ILD (online supplemental table 1), which showed similar moderate to strong correlations between QIA scores and PFTs (β=−0.41 to −0.70 for FVC, −0.32 to −0.70 for DLCO Hg, p<0.0001 for all).

Long-term QIA trends

As PFT measures are more readily available in the clinical setting, we hypothesised that short-term PFT changes would be predictive of long-term QIA trends. We selected patients who had at least two PFT measurements in the first 12 months and two QIA scores that spanned at least 1 year longer than corresponding PFTs to ensure that long-term slope encompassed a sufficiently longer period beyond short-term measurements. Mixed-effects models in this subset showed that the slope of PFT improvement in the first 12 months was weakly predictive of long-term improving QIA trends, particularly of QLF and QILD scores (β=−0.15 to −0.22, figure 3).

Long-term (5-year) QIA trends over time were also calculated for each baseline visual ILD pattern (online supplemental table 2). Patients with baseline OP (QILD 5-year slope mean −10.55% change per year) and NSIP/OP (mean −2.42% change per year) showed improving trends, while NSIP (mean 0.20% change per year) and UIP (mean 8.25% change per year) had stable to worsening trends.

Association of higher QIA with increased mortality

A total of 13 deaths (4.7%) occurred in the cohort (10 occurred in the ‘clinical ILD’ group) during a median follow-up time of 117 months (IQR 91–157). Baseline predictors of mortality were assessed using Cox models (tables2 3). Among patients with QIA performed (n=168), higher baseline QIA scores were significantly associated with increased mortality, with each 10% increase in baseline QILD corresponding to an 8% higher risk of death (HR 1.08, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.16, table 2). Associations with mortality remained similar in age-adjusted and anti-ARS ab-adjusted models. Trends remained, although no longer significant in models adjusted for cancer and PH. Among those with PFTs, lower baseline FVC and DLCO were also associated with increased mortality, although only the DLCO model (HR 0.95, 95% CI 0.91 to 0.99) and the cancer-adjusted FVC model (HR 0.95, 95% CI 0.91 to 0.99) reached statistical significance.

Early short-term changes were calculated as the slope of all QIA and PFT measurements obtained in the first 12 months using linear mixed-effects models (table 2). Increasing slopes in QLF and QILD scores in the first 12 months (meaning more rapid worsening in the first year) were associated with higher mortality (for every 10% increase in first 12-month slope, mortality increased by 4% for QLF slope and 6% for QILD slope). Associations remained significant in all adjusted models (age, anti-ARS ab, cancer, PH). In contrast, the first 12-month slopes for FVC and DLCO were not associated with mortality. Additional baseline predictors were assessed in Cox models, which showed higher baseline MD global damage scores, hs-CRP, presence of PH, cancer and cyclophosphamide use were associated with higher mortality (table 3).

Cox regression with time-varying covariates also showed that higher QIA scores from baseline throughout their follow-up period were associated with increased mortality (table 4). Patients with higher QILD by 10% corresponded with 12% higher risk of mortality (HR 1.12, 95% CI 1.03 to 1.21; p=0.007). QLF had HR 1.06 (95% CI 1.01 to 1.10; p=0.008), QGG had HR 1.12 (95% CI 1.01 to 1.23; p=0.03). Each QIA model was adjusted for potential confounders (age, sex, smoking, anti-ARS ab, anti-MDA5 ab, cancer, PH), which remained significant in all QLF and QILD models. Similar trends were seen when models were constructed only in patients with ILD (n=8 deaths) (online supplemental table 3).

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that quantitative CT-derived measures of ILD burden at baseline and early progression in fibrosis (QLF) and total ILD scores (QILD) are associated with increased mortality, whereas the presence of visually assessed ILD or visual ILD patterns on baseline images was not. These findings suggest that quantitative imaging metrics may capture prognostically relevant disease characteristics not reflected by conventional visual assessment.

The association between QIA and adverse outcomes such as mortality as observed is biologically plausible. QLF and QILD quantify the extent of fibrotic and interstitial abnormalities of the whole lung, enabling objective assessment of disease burden both cross-sectionally and longitudinally. Early worsening of these measures may reflect progressive fibrotic remodelling or structural lung damage that may be more closely linked to adverse outcomes. In contrast, visual assessment is less sensitive to subtle or diffuse changes,9 10 particularly in the early stages of disease progression.

These findings underscore the prognostic value of HRCT in MA-ILD. Jiang et al proposed a risk stratification scheme incorporating semiquantitative visual HRCT scores, ILD pattern (OP vs non-OP) and clinical features such as MSAs to identify patients at risk for acute/subacute progression of ILD,40 while Qiant et al employed deep learning to demonstrate that ground glass opacities (GGO) proportion is prognostic for RP-ILD in MA-ILD.14 Future studies should extend beyond rapidly progressive ILD (RP-ILD) to define radiographic features predictive of heterogeneous disease trajectories, treatment response and clinically actionable thresholds that can guide management across the spectrum of MA-ILD.

Visual review remains the standard approach for detecting ILD in the absence of universally accepted consensus criteria for MA-ILD. In our cohort, systematic re-analysis of HRCTs confirmed ILD (‘visual ILD’) in 51% of reviewed scans, consistent with the 30%–75% prevalence of ILD reported in prior IIM series.1 37 Mixed NSIP/OP pattern was the predominant radiographic pattern, followed by OP and NSIP, while UIP was uncommon. This predominance aligns with prior studies showing that NSIP/OP overlap is characterised by greater consolidation and subpleural ground-glass attenuation with peripheral and peribronchovascular distribution being less frequent, consistent with prior reports.3031 41,43 Together, these findings support the mixed NSIP/OP pattern as a defining radiographic feature distinguishing MA-ILD from other CTD-ILDs.

QIA scores demonstrated strong correlations with physiological measures, which has been demonstrated in prior studies. A recent report from our group in a separate ASyS-ILD cohort of 20 patients demonstrated that QILD scores were associated with FVC, DLCO, patient-reported and physician-reported outcomes of respiratory disease burden and health-related quality of life.22 Similarly, a South Korean cohort of 80 patients with MA-ILD showed strong associations between QILD and PFTs, with higher QILD scores predictive of death or lung transplantation.13 Collectively, these findings support that QIA captures a clinically meaningful representation of disease severity across physiological, patient-reported and prognostic domains.

In our current work, 28% of patients showed discordance between visual ILD and PFTs. Low FVC in patients without ILD may reflect suboptimal patient effort, pain, other restrictive disease, obstructive lung disease or respiratory muscle involvement, whereas preserved FVC in patients with visual ILD likely represents mild disease not yet causing ventilatory restriction. HRCT mitigates these limitations somewhat by directly assessing parenchymal abnormalities with high sensitivity, often detecting structural changes before physiological impairment becomes evident. In our prior study in ASyS-ILD, patient-reported dyspnoea scores and physician-reported pulmonary visual activity scale correlated more strongly with QLF and QILD scores than with PFTs,22 suggesting that QIA may better reflect patient and clinician perception of respiratory status. Nevertheless, HRCT is not universally accessible and carries radiation-related risks and therefore PFTs remain an essential measure of functional impairment in ILD. At present, optimal ILD assessment requires integration of both imaging and physiological measures pending further validation of quantitative imaging biomarkers in myositis lung disease.

As PFTs remain the primary surrogate of lung disease burden in clinical practice, we assessed the predictive value of early 12-month trends of PFTs for long-term (5-year) QIA trends. However, early FVC and DLCO trends correlated only weakly with long-term QLF and QILD trajectories. This likely reflects the heterogeneous and non-linear course of disease progression and treatment response in MA-ILD.

This study has several limitations. It was a retrospective analysis from a single quaternary referral centre, where heterogeneous immunosuppressive regimens and variable follow-up time may limit generalisability. Nonetheless, the real-world design of our study and long-term follow-up provide meaningful insight into IIM disease patterns encountered in clinical practice. HRCT and PFT assessments were not always done concurrently, and the timing was determined by physician judgement rather than standardised intervals, which introduces potential selection bias, as patients with more severe disease often underwent more frequent imaging. To minimise this limitation, we included high-quality external HRCT scans and restricted paired CT-PFT analyses to studies performed within defined time frames. The relatively small number of deaths limited adjustment for multiple confounders. Finally, although the QIA algorithm that we used is well validated in SSc-ILD and other ILDs,1316,21 it has not yet been validated in MA-ILD. Disease-specific radiographic features, such as confluent opacities that may blur the distinction between consolidation and fibrosis, underscore the need for prospective studies to refine and validate quantitative scoring in this population. Prospective, multicentre studies integrating QIA and radiomics approaches44 45 with clinical, physiological, autoantibody and molecular data are needed to confirm these findings and establish standardised imaging biomarkers for MA-ILD. Development of disease-specific QIA algorithms capable of distinguishing inflammatory from fibrotic patterns will be essential to improve risk stratification, monitor therapeutic response and guide clinical trial design.

In conclusion, quantitative CT-derived measures of disease burden at baseline and early progression of fibrotic abnormalities were associated with mortality in MA-ILD, whereas conventional visual ILD assessment was not. These findings support further investigation and external validation of quantitative imaging as a prognostic tool and potential endpoint in this patient population.
