Missed opportunities for tobacco dependence treatment among U.S. veterans referred for lung Cancer screening: An observational cohort study
Authors: Anne C. Melzer, Adam Kaplan, Megan Campbell, Hanna Lefchak, David Nelson, Deborah Pestka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTobacco dependence treatment (TDT) should be offered to all lung cancer screening (LCS) participants who smoke. We sought to examine the provision of TDT among patients referred for LCS.MethodsWe performed a multi-center, retrospective observational analysis of patients referred for LCS at seven regionally-affiliated Veterans Affairs Medical Centers and associated clinics from 9/2022–9/2023. We identified LCS referrals and tobacco use using administrative data. We extracted TDTs (±45 days from referral or Low-dose computed tomography [LDCT]) including medications and tobacco-related visits (TRVs). Outcomes included any pharmacotherapy, any TRV, and combined TDT (pharmacotherapy + two or more TRVs). Results were analyzed using generalized linear mixed effects models.Results9319 patients were referred for LCS, of whom 58.6 % currently smoked. Of those, 47.9 % resided in rural areas, 6.9 % were Black, 6.7 % were female; mean age was 67.7. Among those who currently smoked, 17.4 % received any pharmacotherapy, 13.6 % had at least one TRV, and 6.4 % had combined TDT. Participants in the LCS program were significantly more likely to receive medications around the time of the initial LDCT, with odds ratios (OR) < 1 for all comparison groups: interval scan OR 0.60 (95 %CI 0.48,0.76) no scan OR 0.65 (95 %CI 0.52,0.83), subsequent annual scan OR 0.59 (95 %CI 0.46,0.75). This pattern held for all three outcomes. Rural-residing individuals were less likely to have a TRV or receive combined TDT.ConclusionsWe found low rates of TDT and a concerning drop off in treatment after initial LDCT. Failure to address tobacco throughout the longitudinal process represents a missed opportunity.Primary funding sourceVeterans Affairs Quality Enhancement Research Institute (PII I50 HX003692–01) and a Health Systems Research Career Development Award (CDA 19–180).

Background

Lung cancer screening (LCS) with annual low-dose computed tomography scans (LDCTs) decreases lung cancer mortality through early detection of lung cancer. Because of the eligibility criteria selecting for individuals at high risk of lung cancer, many participants who receive LCS smoke. While about 14 % of adults smoke cigarettes (Wang et al., 2021), 50–60 % of LCS participants currently smoke (Cao et al., 2020). Most participants in LCS continue to smoke from year-to-year of screening (Fu et al., 2019). Therefore, tobacco dependence treatment (TDT) for LCS participants is strongly recommended by many policy makers (e.g. United States Preventive Services Task Force (Moyer, 2014), Veterans Health Administration (Scavella, 2022)) and professional societies (Fucito et al., 2016; Screening for Lung Cancer with Low Dose Computed Tomography, 2022; Wiener et al., 2022). Integrating TDT into LCS is expected to have wide-ranging health benefits, including lung cancer prevention (Cao et al., 2020; Baker et al., 2018; Clark et al., 2016). Modeling suggests that a 10 % increase in cessation among LCS participants who smoke would result in an 80 % increase in quality-adjusted life-years gained compared to LCS alone, meeting all thresholds for cost-effectiveness (Cadham et al., 2021; Meza et al., 2022). In our previous longitudinal, multicenter qualitative study of LCS participants, few patients recalled discussing tobacco cessation at all during the process (Golden et al., 2020) and did not feel that participation in LCS affected their intentions to quit smoking (Golden et al., 2022). Unless accompanied by TDT, LCS alone likely has little impact on cessation (Fu et al., 2019; Heiden et al., 2022; Slatore et al., 2014), unless a nodule of concern is identified (Styn et al., 2009).

Because it is longitudinal, LCS offers multiple opportunities to address tobacco use. Depending on age and smoking history at initiation, participants may receive annual LDCTs for upwards of 25 years. Prior data suggests that a minority of patients, about 15 %, are prescribed pharmacotherapy for tobacco treatment following an LCS referral (Heffner et al., 2021; Shen et al., 2021). Many have no documented discussion of smoking behaviors or treatment at their shared decision-making visit (Stead et al., 2016) (Slatore et al., 2014). Older individuals and Veterans who smoke often have high barriers to smoking cessation including high nicotine addiction, lower socioeconomic status, higher burden of comorbidity, high prevalence of mental health diagnoses, and more rural residence (Taylor et al., 2022; Jiménez-Ruiz et al., 2015; Melzer et al., 2021). Therefore, LCS programs should strive to serve the unique TDT needs of their participants.

Studies of LCS participants suggest that low intensity treatments are likely to be ineffective for boosting sustained cessation. Current guidelines recommend high intensity pharmacotherapy for individuals with comorbidities who smoke, which encompasses nearly all LCS participants (Leone et al., 2020). Pharmacotherapy should be combined with behavioral treatment for greatest success (Stead et al., 2016) (‘combined TDT’). Studies in LCS programs demonstrate a clear ‘dose-response’ relationship between program intensity and successful cessation (Cinciripini et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2023; Park et al., 2025). The Veterans Health Administration (VHA) has recommended that LCS be provided using formal screening programs and processes (Lung Cancer Screening, 2025; Smith et al., 2021), and has provided considerable central resources to rapidly scale uptake of LCS programs across the country (Slatore, 2024). Mandated program components include the use of proactive clinical reminders to prompt shared-decision making and referrals, LCS coordinators, and a national patient tracking database. These programs provide a means to impact key elements of LCS quality including tobacco treatment.

Objective

As part of a broader quality improvement initiative, we evaluated current practices in TDT for individuals referred for LCS across our regionally affiliated medical centers to identify potential targets for improvement. We were particularly interested in the prevalence and intensity of the treatments provided, and the impact of rurality and LCS processes on the provision of TDT.

Methods

Design

The Veterans Health Administration is divided into regional systems of care across the United States called Veterans Affairs Integrated Service Networks (‘VISN’). We performed a multicenter retrospective observational study of LCS participants within a Midwest VISN. This highly rural VISN encompasses the upper Midwest region of the United States (Minnesota, Western Wisconsin, North Dakota, South Dakota, Iowa, Nebraska). The evaluation was conducted in collaboration with the VISN Specialty Care Integrated Care Community and as part of a VISN-partnered Quality Enhancement Research Institute pilot. As such it was considered operations and deemed exempt from Institutional Review Board oversight and was conducted in compliance with medical center guidance.

Participants and setting

Participants included all individuals referred for LCS at a VHA medical center in the United States Midwest VISN from 9/2022–9/2023. Sites comprised the main VHA medical centers or the center's affiliated community-based clinics. These clinics provide primary care but refer patients to the LCS program located at the main medical center. One of the eight VISN sites performed no LCS exams and was excluded, as they generated no data. All VHA medical centers offer tobacco treatment, at a minimum through the VHA quitline and medications from primary care. Sites had implemented LCS for variable periods of time prior to the study period. LCS participation and ‘scan cycle’ (initial, subsequent annual or other interval follow-up, referred but did not complete exam) was defined using a combination of procedure codes and health factors. Health factors are tagged data fields generated by the VHA electronic health record (EHR). Health factors tied to the Lung Cancer Screening Platform (‘LCSP’) national patient tracking database identify and track an individual's progress through in LCS. Health factors are generated by the clinical reminder that is completed by the primary care provider during a visit when referring a patient for LCS. In some programs, the reminder generates a simultaneous imaging order. In other programs, the reminder prompts an LCS coordinator to review eligibility, and the coordinator places the imaging order, typically within one to two business days. VHA policy states imaging is to be scheduled within 30 days. We extracted health factors for initial enrollment (‘agrees to be screened’) and subsequent episodes (‘continue annual screening’) of screening. All but one participating site was using the LCSP, and most participants had LCS health factors. However, some LDCTs were ordered by primary care outside of a formal screening program. For participants without health factors, scans were identified and characterized using codes (Current Procedural Terminology code: S8032, G0297, 71271, and LungRADs (Lung-RADS®. AC of RC on, 2023)) alone. LDCTs were characterized as initial, subsequent annual, or ‘interval’ (i.e. an LDCT done for nodule follow-up or ‘incomplete LungRADS 0’ result on the prior LDCT). While there are unique scan titles/HFs for each step in the screening process some cases where the ‘round’ of screening was unclear were pooled with the ‘interval’ LDCTs. Clinician-level adherence to ordering procedures can be variable, such that these LDCTs are sometimes ordered as ‘nodule tracking’ or general LDCTs. Therefore, lacking the metadata to exclusively assign them to a screen ‘cycle’.

Participants with an enrollment or subsequent annual health factor but no completed image were categorized as ‘no LDCT.’

We extracted patient demographics (age, race, sex, rurality), site, LungRADS codes, and ordering clinician from the EHR. Ordering clinician was extracted from LDCT reports and was not available for patients who did not complete an LDCT. Rurality was defined based on Rural-Urban Commuting Area codes characterizing participants as urban/suburban and rural/highly rural (Rural-urban commuting area codes, 2020).

Measurements

Smoking status

Smoking status of LCS participants was based on the last tobacco health factor. Tobacco use and ‘pack years’ (average packs per day multiplied by years of cigarette smoking) are recorded in the LCS ‘tobacco pack year’ clinical reminder prior to LCS enrollment. Tobacco use status is also updated at least annually via a national ‘Tobacco Use’ clinical reminder and during certain clinical events, such as hospitalizations. Positive tobacco screens can encompass other products such as chewing tobacco, but assessments for LCS eligibility are limited to cigarette smoking as per the United States Preventive Services Task Force recommendation, where eligibility for LCS is based only on cigarettes. Given that all LCS participants should have smoked cigarettes, positive tobacco screens were assumed to represent smoking. Tobacco use was categorized as current, former or likely nonsmoker based on a previously validated method using the most recent smoking status and discounting aberrant data points (e.g. ‘lifelong nonsmoker’ in a patient with many measurements indicating current smoking). (Melzer et al., 2018) A small number of participants were listed as never smokers, suggesting they were referred erroneously for LCS. Analyses of TDT were restricted to current tobacco users.

Tobacco dependence treatment

We were interested in tobacco treatments initiated in relation to the LCS, including both visits and pharmacotherapy. We identified medications prescribed and tobacco-related visits with any clinician within +/− 45 days of LCS enrollment health factor date or, in the absence of health factors, completion of the LDCT. This timeframe was selected to identify care associated with the screening ‘episode’ but to also allow adequate time for the screening process to be completed.

Medications: We identified all prescriptions for tobacco treatment medications (nicotine patch, nicotine lozenge, inhaler, or gum, varenicline, bupropion, and combinations of medications). Combination Nicotine Replacement Therapy (NRT) was defined as prescriptions for both nicotine patch and short-acting nicotine. ‘Any pharmacotherapy’ was defined as a prescription for any United States Food and Drug Administration-approved cessation medication.

Visits: Tobacco-related visits were identified using combinations of codes found to have high specificity for identifying visits. Tobacco care is often combined with other care (e.g. with LCS shared decision-making visit, with other substance use disorder counseling). We used an inclusive definition including brief or greater than brief advice, behavioral counseling visits, and medication management visits. We first reviewed a nationally available VHA guidance document for coding tobacco-related visits to generate a preliminary administrative definition of a tobacco-related visit. In VHA, these visits can be completed by many clinician types (e.g. physicians, advanced practice providers, psychologists, pharmacists, social workers, nurses) using any approved treatment modality (in-person, phone, telehealth) offered clinically at that site. This preliminary definition combined visit codes and tobacco diagnoses to identify potential visits. To ensure >90 % accuracy of this definition to both identify and exclude the presence of a tobacco-related visit, we identified a random sample (n = 100) of LCS participants who smoke WITH identified tobacco-related visits and pharmacotherapy and a random sample (n = 100) WITHOUT identified visits and pharmacotherapy. We completed chart reviews on this sample to identify the presence or absence of a tobacco-related visit on the flagged date, or at any other time in the period of interest. Based on the results of the chart review, we further refined our administrative definitions and re-applied them to the cohort to generate the final assessment of tobacco-related visits. ‘Any tobacco-related visit’ was defined as at least one tobacco-related visit. Combined Treatment: ‘Combined TDT’ was defined as receipt of both pharmacotherapy and at least two tobacco-related visits. See appendix A for complete data definitions.

Statistical methods

Descriptive statistics are presented as frequency (percent) for categorical variables and mean (standard deviation) for continuous variables. We initially analyzed the binary TDT use outcomes using generalized linear mixed models including random effects for primary care providers (i.e. general physicians, advanced-practice nurses or physicians assistants providing general medical care) and for parent facility. We adjusted for and patient rurality, race, and sex. Predictor associations with a p-value <0.05 are deemed significant. We graphically display provider and facility random effects to show the variability within these higher-level constructs (see Appendix B for provider-level effects). Because provider had a small contribution to the fitted models and was systematically missing for patients who did not undergo LDCT, we excluded provider from the final models presented here. Analyses were completed using R version 4.4.2.

Results

We identified 9319 individuals referred for LCS across seven sites. Of those, 5464 (58.6 %) currently smoked. Individuals were predominantly older (mean age 68.4 years), white males with heavy tobacco use history. Nearly half (47.9 %) resided in rural or highly rural areas. Approximately a quarter of individuals did not complete their LDCT. Scans were distributed between initial, interval and subsequent annual LDCTs. Demographics were similar between participants who currently or formerly smoked. A small number of participants did not appear to have smoked in the past. Overall, 17.4 % of currently smoking participants received any pharmacotherapy, with variation between sites (range 13.9 %–24.6 %). For receipt of at least one tobacco-related visit, 13.6 % overall had a visit, with considerable between-site variation from 9.9 % to 70.8 %. Receipt of combined TDT with at least two visits and pharmacotherapy was low overall at 6.3 %, with between-site variation from 3.8 % to 20 %. Sites varied substantially in their provision of all treatments (Fig. 1). Participants were more likely to have a tobacco-related visit and receive pharmacotherapy or combined TDT with their initial completed LDCT than with any other step in the screening process, including subsequent annual scans. There appeared to be slight variation by sex, race, and rural residence. Of the pharmacotherapy prescribed, single agent NRT (short-acting or patch) was the most common (42 %). Short-acting NRT alone (23.6 %) followed by bupropion (22.1 %) were the most common agents. Combination NRT (12.3 %) and varenicline (14.7 %) were less commonly used. A minority of participants (8.7 %) received other combinations of agents (e.g. bupropion and NRT), which may also represent sequential rather than concurrent treatment. Sites varied in their choices of medications. For example, use of combination NRT ranged from 0 to 15 % and use of single-agent NRT from 30.8 to 60.4 %. Discussion

In this large, multisite, observational study of tobacco dependence treatment for LCS participants, we found considerable gaps in reach and intensity of care. Overall rates of treatment were fairly low, though very similar to past observational studies (Cao et al., 2020; Heffner et al., 2021; Shen et al., 2021). We included treatments that happened prior to the LDCT, which may have captured tobacco treatments not associated with the LCS. Even with this broader timeframe, treatment rates were low. To ensure we were capturing the many clinicians involved in TDT, we used a broad definition of tobacco-related visits to include all potential ‘touches’ in the tobacco treatment process. At several sites, the proportion of patients with at least one tobacco-related visit was very high, greater than 50 %. We explored potential reasons for this variation in a concurrent qualitative assessment (Pestka et al., 2025). We identified considerable variability in how tobacco is addressed in LCS, with some sites relying almost solely on primary care providers to discuss, prescribe or refer and other sites having LCS nurse coordinators address it routinely during shared decision-making visits. The between-site variation in TDT likely reflects differences in both LCS processes and documentation practices. For example, at some sites the LCS coordinators speak with every patient after primary care referral, offering an additional touch point to address TDT. Some of the largest LCS programs have the highest smoking rates and rely almost solely on primary care to address tobacco. Primary care providers may be less likely to code for tobacco counseling during a busy annual visit, particularly if the participant declines care. With a limited number of sites, we were unable to formally assess the association between these LCS/TDT processes and treatment uptake.

We found evidence of disparities in treatment for rural LCS participants for tobacco-related visits and combined TDT, which was an expected finding (Gaffney et al., 2022; Doogan et al., 2017; Talbot et al., 2019). Interestingly, we did not find a disparity in receipt of pharmacotherapy. In our prior qualitative work, LCS and primary care staff noted challenges in access to counseling resources that were more common at rural clinics with fewer resources. However, staff at these clinics also noted they were comfortable prescribing pharmacotherapy. We did see lower treatment rates for Black individuals that was not statistically significant, but we were likely underpowered for this finding. Increasing access to high-quality tobacco treatment including both pharmacotherapy and counseling is challenging for all clinics, but particularly in rural areas. Tobacco treatment can be delivered by phone or virtually. Centralized and integrated models can be highly effective in increasing access regardless of geography. LCS programs should pursue innovative care processes to ensure access to TDT for rural LCS participants, such as cross-training LCS staff and incorporating virtual care to expand the reach of tobacco treatment resources. However, these processes may be challenging to incorporate into programs due to the growing workload for LCS coordinators as screening rapidly scales to all eligible patients.

Unfortunately, we saw continued evidence that two of the most effective pharmacologic treatments—varenicline and combination NRT—remain underutilized, even in this population at high risk of lung cancer. Some of this low use is related to patient preference. Many patients are aware of previous concerns about mental health effects and decline to use varenicline. Unfortunately, many prescribers also remain concerned about use despite the removal of the previous black box warning (Skoloda et al., 2023). While the VHA has issued a national criteria for use supporting broad prescribing of varenicline, some sites continue to place barriers to the prescribing of varenicline, such as restricting it to certain prescribers or requiring a trial of nicotine replacement first. Clinicians and tobacco treatment leaders need to continue efforts to educate patients and staff and streamline prescribing options.

Finally, we found concerning gaps in TDT through the longitudinal screening process. Participants were far less likely to be counseled, prescribed medications, or participate in combined TDT with any LDCT after the initial scan. Treatment was also lower among those who ultimately did not complete an LDCT. We hypothesize that this at least partially reflects patients ‘opting out’ of two linked health-promoting interventions—cancer screening and tobacco cessation. The drop off in treatment after the first screen represents a missed opportunity but is not surprising. In previous studies of TDT in LCS in the U.S. and abroad, many interventions have focused on impacting the shared decision-making visit at the time of program enrollment (Williams et al., 2023). This can be an easier target to impact. Clinicians are specifically instructed to complete a discussion that addresses tobacco use. Subsequent interval LDCTs may be ordered somewhat ‘automatically’ with results reported through letters or secure messages rather than at a phone or in-person visit. Consistent with Centers for Medicare and Medicaid and Veteran Affairs guidance, clinicians are not required to repeat shared decision-making with subsequent annual screens but only need to ensure eligibility and desire to continue. A requirement for repeated shared decision-making would be a barrier to adherence to LCS follow-up. However, because the processes that prompt TDT participation with the initial screen are therefore less likely to occur with future scans, it is key that we identify mechanisms to address tobacco use throughout the process.

LCS staff and primary care providers have limited time to address tobacco use. Any intervention that increases their clinical burden will likely have low uptake. Technology-based options such as text messaging, patient portal messages, or interactive voice-response systems, may offer an alternative. These approaches have all been found effective in boosting cessation attempts in other patient populations (Nahhas et al., 2017; Rigotti et al., 2011; Fu et al., 2014; Rogers et al., 2018). Data suggests that patients are more motivated to quit shortly after their LDCT (Williams et al., 2022). Patients with upcoming or recent LCS exams who smoke could be contacted using one of these methods and connected to treatment. Such outreach interventions could capitalize on this increase in motivation that is otherwise typically not accompanied by a clinical visit. The VHA has noted the importance of integrating LCS and TDT and sponsored a multi-site clinical pilot incorporating tobacco treatment staff into LCS programs. A formal program evaluation is underway that will disseminate the findings of that pilot, offering insights into how LCS programs can better incorporate TDT.

Our study had limitations. We relied on administrative coding of tobacco visits, which may have missed some visits outside of the VHA or quitline referrals, though quitline referrals are low in number. Use of bupropion could be confounded by use for depression. Screen cycle was determined primarily based on health factors, so some annual LDCTs may have been mischaracterized as interval. However, the health factors specifying initial versus subsequent annual LDCT are highly specific and support our findings. We were unable to include participants who received LCS at outside imaging centers. It is possible that some individuals with ‘no scan’ completed imaging elsewhere. Participants were predominantly white male Veterans who enrolled in LCS at centers with a formal program, which may limit generalizability to other populations and settings. Our study has several strengths, including our refined definition of tobacco-related visits and inclusion of a large rural population.

In conclusion, we identified gaps in the provision of tobacco dependence treatment among LCS participants who smoke, with evidence of missed opportunities for treatment through the longitudinal process. Our future work will focus on identifying high-value processes for providing TDT for LCS participants and considering strategies for engaging patients in treatment from year to year.
