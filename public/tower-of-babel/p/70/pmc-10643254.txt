Do women's working hours inputs yield higher household economic welfare than men's?
Authors: Liming Chu, Qi Zhang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractTime allocation, which encompasses various aspects of the labor market and the household sphere, is a key factor affecting both genders in the employment and household spheres. Using the data from China Family Panel Studies Survey (CFPS) of 2014, 2016 and 2018, the paper examines the impact of working hours input on household economic welfare. The research results indicate that women's working hours input significantly contributes to the household economy and passes a series of robustness tests; the positive effect of women's working hours input on the household economy is stronger than that of men using the household savings rate to measure household economic welfare; and there are increasing working hours input and enhancing household economic welfare through the mechanism of increasing household income. Theoretically, time allocation is placed within the logical framework of the household microcosm. It suggests that individual working hours influence household economic welfare. Practically, the research findings provide useful policy insights: raising workers' wage levels, narrowing the gender labor gap, and improving working time systems and work arrangements to promote the equal development of both genders in the employment and household spheres.Keywords: Working hours, Household economic welfare, Gender differences

Introduction

China highly values the social status and employment participation of women on an equal footing with men. From the supply side, the labor force participation rate and working hours of Chinese women have always been among the highest in the world, indicating that Chinese women have actively participated in all areas of economic and social development and realized their self-development and self-worth through the labor market. From the demand side, as China's population ages and childlessness increases, there has been a transitional change in its labor supply-demand pattern. The demographic dividend has diminished and is evolving into a shortage of total labor supply. Economic development has pushed the boundaries of production possibilities outwards. Consumers' demands for products and manufacturers' demands for labor are increasing. Consequently, the simple supply of male labor has long been unable to meet market requirements.

In terms of labor supply, working hours systems such as “996” and “007” have become hot topics of discussion on the Internet, bringing the issue of working hours into the research focus of scholars. In the existing literature, scholars have extensively explored the relationship between working hours input and individual economic behavior [ [[1], [2], [3]]]. However, as an important part of time allocation research, there has been insufficient attention given to the household effects of working hours. Particularly from a gender perspective, how time allocation of both genders can achieve equal development in the employment and household spheres remains an important topic that needs to be addressed.

The paper is structured as follows. Sections 3, 4 present a review of the relevant literature and the theoretical hypotheses, respectively. Section 5 provides details of the methodology, variables, and data, and Section 6 introduces the estimation results and a pertinent discussion. Section 7 focuses on the conclusions and policy implications of the study. The last section focuses on future research and limitations of the study.

Literature review

Currently, there are numerous research topics on working hours, with a focus on the antecedents of working hours. Studies have indicated that divorce rates, productivity levels, technological progress, wage rates, marital status, number of children, husband income, and the level of human capital represented by education all exert effects on working hours [ [4,5]]. In terms of time allocation, men and urban women allocate more time in the labor market domain and leisure time, while rural women allocate more time in the household domain, with no gender difference in sleep time. It can be concluded that there are gender and urban-rural differences in time allocation [ [[6], [7], [8]]].

In terms of working hours consequences, there are fewer studies on related topics, mainly on the effects of working hours input on individuals, such as health, work-family balance and well-being. From a health perspective, when working hours input is limited to a certain extent, workers' disposable time is limited and their physical health and psychological emotions manifest as fatigue and negative work [ [9,10]]. In terms of work-family balance, working hours commitment may lead to conflict in labor roles; consequently, making it impossible to balance family and work. In terms of reality, work-family conflict is a very common social problem with a significant impact on individual workers, families and workplaces [ [11,12]]. The sense of role conflict is more prevalent in the female population, especially married women of childbearing age, and in urban areas of China, other factors are held constant, working women spend 20 h more per week doing housework than men [13]. Another study also finds that working women with social and family responsibilities still spend significantly more time doing housework than men despite working the same amount of hours as men [14]. The higher a woman's income in the labor market, the higher her time value, and the greater the economic loss caused by the interruption of her career due to childbearing. Therefore, the higher the opportunity cost of childbearing, the lower the willingness of high-income women to have children. Therefore, the time devoted to employment and its investment versus childbearing, raising children and maintaining a family has become a key factor in women's time allocation [15].If women's participation in the labor market is generally understood and encouraged by society, they will spend less time on household chores and more time working, thereby exerting positive effects on their lives [16]. Most studies mentioned above have focused on the association between working hours and workers' subjective well-being,the subjective feelings associated with working hours show mixed results [ [[17], [18], [19]]].It can be found that most studies related to working hours consequences focus on the impact of working hours input on individuals.

Although there are not many studies dedicated to the impact of working hours input on the economic welfare of households, some consensus has been accumulated at the level of theoretical analysis. It is found that labor participation time is a key determinant of rural household economic income and that non-farm employment of labor exerts significantly positive effects on changes in farm household welfare [ [[20], [21], [22], [23]]]. From a household perspective, the degree of household members' labor participation depends on the household structure and other members’ labor participation; consequently, the time and economic costs invested by different household members vary [24,25]. From a gender perspective, male and female labor forces have different behavioral characteristics, with differences in consumption, savings and risk preferences, and women tend to have higher bargaining power than men [26].

To sum up, scholars at home and abroad have studied working hours from multiple perspectives, leading to the conclusion that working hours are subject to macro and micro factors, the economic effects of working hours, the negative effects of “overwork” and the conflict between work and family. Nevertheless, there is still room for further development of the existing studies. Firstly, fewer studies are conducted on the effects of working hours, and the household effects of working hours input need to be deepened. Secondly, most of the existing studies related to the topic take farming households as research objects to explore the correlation between working hours and household economic welfare, which needs to be further verified from a gender perspective. Thirdly, the existing studies tend to analyze working hours as a single factor, and few of them refine it into an analytical framework of working hours effects.

Therefore, based on existing studies, the paper selects a micro-database and employs an instrumental variables approach to remove the endogeneity between working hours and household economic welfare, and conducts a general and mechanistic analysis on the problem of women's working hours input and household economic welfare. Attempts are made to answer the following questions. How large is the household economic welfare effect of women's working hours input in China? Are there different degrees of stratification? Is the pattern of the effect of working hours input on household economic welfare consistent for both men and women? Do household income and household consumption of men and women play the same role in it?

Compared with the established literature, the possible contributions of the paper are as follows. First, taking working hours as the research object, it analyzes the economic contribution of women's labor to the household in terms of labor value. Second, it refines working hours and analyzes the relationship between women's working hours input and household economic welfare in more depth. Third, it examines the impact of working hours input on household economic welfare from a gender perspective, which extends the exploration of the micro-mechanisms of the economic effects of working hours.

Theoretical hypotheses

Regarding the amount of working hours invested, both individual factors and the maximization of the utility of the household as a whole should be considered. According to the theory of time allocation, the value of the time factor for household production is reflected in its allocation outside the household, to the labor market for remuneration, and inside the household, for the production of goods by the household. These two components constitute the household production function. Workers, as individuals, consider the finiteness of time and leisure preference, and as family members, considers the utility of the household. Savings, as one of the main economic activities of a household, represent a household's balance of economic resources and reflect its economic strength. An increase in working hours input optimizes household economic welfare by transferring the household's economic resources from the present to the future so that future consumption can be satisfied. In terms of savings motivation, precautionary savings are a very important component of household savings, which is to prevent a sharp decline in consumption levels due to future uncertainty [27]. Household savings are generally the result of household income and expenditure, with the income mainly coming from labor for the vast majority of people but as working hours input increases, it does not mean that households will over-accumulate economic resources; therefore, the difficulty is to find the balance between over-accumulation and under-accumulation in the household economy [28]. The working hours may therefore exert positive and then negative effects on household economic welfare. Interpreting household economic welfare from a gender perspective reveals that women in the household have stronger motivation to save for their old age, and increased female working hours is the main source of discretionary income, which in turn drives up the household savings rate [29]. As a result, female saving preferences may make working hours have a differential impact on household economic welfare to varying degrees.

The time invested in labor is considered both from the perspectives of individuals and the maximization of the utility of the household as a whole. Time allocation theory suggests that men and women allocate their working time differently. The theory, with a focus on the allocation of time from a gender perspective, explores the allocation of time in the socio-economy. It highlights the impact of gender differences and comparative advantage in the socio-economy, providing guidance on gender differences in household welfare. Men's roles tend to be socially relevant, while women's roles and responsibilities tend to be related to the domestic sphere, reflecting the gendered division of labor between men and women. In this regard, men's professional and family roles are congruent and intertwined, as they are expected to forge successful careers while fulfilling their socially mandated responsibilities as breadwinners. Becker's time allocation model, which incorporates the time factor into intra-household analysis, reflects that men and women have different gender advantages in terms of returns to the labor market and the household sphere from the perspective of household utility maximization. Methodology, variables, and data

Data sources

The data used in this paper include the China Family Panel Studies (CFPS) and the China Statistical Yearbook of 2014, 2016 and 2018. In the process of processing the various types of data mentioned above, missing values and invalid data were excluded, truncated tails were processed, and weighting was also conducted to make the sample representative nationwide. The sample is based on the working age of the labor force. Given the advent of an ageing society, where the labor force has reached retirement age in a “retired but not retired” condition, the value of working hours is not affected by the retirement policy. Therefore, the age range of the labor force in the non-agricultural labor force sample is relaxed to 16–64 years old based on the considerations above.

Two levels of data are used in the paper. The first is personal information based on the adult questionnaire, including the gender, age, physical health, education level, marital status, political status, household status and working hours of respondents. The second is the household information based on the household questionnaire and family relationship questionnaire, including household size and household income. After the two parts of data were matched and merged, the final samples obtained were household data of 2014, 2016 and 2018, with macro and micro samples matched in accordance with the province of the worker and GDP per capita. After the missing value for each variable was removed, a final sample of 6610 valid samples was obtained.

Variable selection and description

Main variables

Household economic welfare indicators: the household savings rate of the respondents is selected as a proxy variable to measure. To measure the household savings rate, the studies by Chamon and Prasad (2010), Ma and Zhou (2014), and Su et al. (2016) [ [[30], [31], [32]]]are referred to. The household savings rate is defined in the following three ways. The study uses the first method to measure the explanatory variable, and the other two methods as robustness tests.

The first type of household savings rate is based directly on its economic meaning by subtracting household consumption expenditure from household income and dividing it by household income, denoted as savinga. The expression is as follows:

savinga=(household income - household consumption expenditure)/household income.

The second household savings rate, noted as savingb, is expressed as follows:

savingb = [household income - (household consumption expenditure-health expenditure - education expenditure)]/household income.

The third household savings rate, denoted savingc, is expressed as follows:

savingc = Ln (household income) - Ln (household consumption)

The key explanatory variable, working hours, comes from respondents' self-reported working time. Working hours are measured from three aspects.The continuous variable of labor time, the addition of the square term of working hours and the division of the working hours input into five time periods: (1,30] hours, (30,40] hours, (40,50] hours, (50,60] hours and (60, max] hours. Measuring working hours at each of the three levels provides a comprehensive picture of the impact of working hours on household economic welfare in terms of its trend, the extent of its impact and its specific relationship to it.

Control variables

In addition, this paper also controls factors that may affect workers’' level of household economic welfare, including three main categories: personal characteristics (e.g., age, household registration, education, health status, marital status), job characteristics (e.g., hourly earnings, whether or not a contract is signed), and household characteristics (e.g., household size, number of children in the household, number of older people, hours spent on household chores, health care expenditures). In addition, other factors (e.g., occupation, region and year fixed effects) are also controlled. The descriptive statistics for all variables are presented in Table 1.

Results and discussion

The impact of women's working hours on household economics welfare

The paper aims to analyze the impact of household economic welfare on women's working hours input. Given that the explanatory variables in the sample are continuous, the linear least squares (OLS) method was employed. In the regressions, in addition to using the main dependent variable of women's working hours, the control variables include variables such as personal characteristics, job characteristics and household characteristics, occupation, region and year fixed effects.

Although the regression results indicate that women's working hours input is significantly correlated with household economic welfare, the potential problem is that women's working hours input may be endogenous. On the one hand, there is the problem of omitted variables. For household economic welfare, unobservable variables (e.g., local customs and culture) may affect both women's working hours input and household saving behavior, thereby creating endogeneity. On the other hand, household economic welfare can also exert effects on how long women work.

To overcome the endogeneity problem, control variables were added into the regression analysis as many as possible using the instrumental variables method for two-stage estimation based on existing studies. In the paper, the average working hours of other women in the same industry (ivworktime) wasere selected as an instrumental variable. A two-stage regression (2SLS) was employed to address the endogeneity between women's working hours and household economic welfare. The reason for choosing the instrumental variable lies in that the working hours of the same gender in the same industry affects the working hours of the sample women, but not the economic welfare of the sample households. The instrumental variable is consistent with the conditions of ‘correlation’ and ‘exogeneity’.

The regression results based on the ordinary least squares and instrumental variables methods are shown in Table 2. Column (1) in Table 2 indicates the regression results under the definition of savings rate, with a positive regression coefficient of women's working hours, 0.342. Column (2) indicates the estimated results after using instrumental variables. As demonstrated by the results of the one-stage regressions, the coefficient of the average working hours of other female labor forces in the same industry is significant at the 1 % level, and the F-values of the first stage are all much greater than the critical values. According to the empirical value of Stock and Yogo (2005) [33], the critical value of the F-value greater than 10 % is 16.38. There is no problem of with selecting weak instrumental variables. Column (3) is the regression result with the squared working hours term added. The regression coefficient of the primary term of women's working hours is positive while the coefficient of the quadratic term is negative. The statistical results indicate that there may be an inverted U-shaped relationship between the two.

As can be seen from Table 2, first, women's working hours input is conducive to enhancing household economic welfare. According to the estimation results of the instrumental variables, the coefficient of women's working hours is significantly positive, with a coefficient of 1.122. An increase of 1 h per week in women's working hours means an increase of one percentage point in the household savings rate. The above regression results indicate that women's working hours input significantly contributes to household economic welfare. Those who work longer hours have a higher ability to save and invest. One motivation for saving is to guard against future uncertainties, for instance, low income, unemployment and poor health. The precautionary motive for saving can explain the fact that women's increased working hours input has led to higher incomes without a decline in savings rates. Since China's social and private insurance markets are still developing, there is a strong incentive to engage in precautionary saving. Studies have validated the existence of significant precautionary motives for saving behavior (Shi and Zhu, 2004) [34].

Secondly, an inverted U-shaped relationship exists between female working hours and household economic welfare, with a phased effects. It is calculated that women's working hours begin to dampen the household savings rate after the inflection point of approximately 102 h per week, well above the maximum national limit for extended working hours. This implies that the inflection point itself does not have real economic significance. It can be tentatively concluded that women's working hours make a positive contribution to household economic welfare at this stage. The regression results show that longer women's working hours have a stronger effect on the household savings rate. A working week of less than 30 h is used as the reference group. The four groups of 30–40 h, 40–50 h, 50–60 h and more than 60 h have an overall increasing impact on the household savings rate. The above regression results show that the impact of women's working hours input on household economic welfare has different stages. On the one hand, an individual increase in labor hours increases personal income; the total income of households rises significantly in the short term; more income means more savings. On the other hand, a clear habitual consumption effect can be found in households, and consumption levels show no significant changes in the short term. With both effects, household economic welfare is affected to the greatest extent in time periods longer than 60 h.

Third, household economic welfare is enhanced by personal income, household size and home ownership, but reduced by marital status and health care expenditures. Income level makes a significant positive contribution to household economic welfare, which is consistent with the relative income hypothesis, and the findings of Li and Huang (2015) [35]. Household size makes a significant contribution to household economic welfare, which is in line with the household saving demand theory and reflects the economic effect of household size. Home ownership significantly increases the household savings rate, which suggests that owning home equity is an important way to enhance household economic welfare. Increased health care expenditures increase household consumption expenditures, which in turn reduce household economic welfare. The ‘marriage effect’ of the unmarried labor force, which may increase savings rates to increase their competitive advantage in the marriage market, satisfies the competitive savings theory. This is because marriage is, to some extent, a risk-reducing institution where individual members insure each other against life shocks.

In terms of squared working hours, men's working hours input and per capita household income shows an inverted “U”-shaped non-linear relationship. It is calculated that men's working hours reaches an inflection point of about 77 h per week and start to have a dampening effect on per capita household income, with the extreme point being smaller than that of women's working hours and higher than the current average men's working hours. In terms of the interval of working hours, the impact of working hours input on per capita household income increases by 36.48 %, 47.55 %, 55.58 % and 65.86 % respectively in the male sample compared with that of 30 h shorter than weekly working hours, all of which are higher than that of women's working hours. The gap gradually increases. Therefore, it can be tentatively concluded that men's working hours also make a positive contribution to per capita household income at this stage.

Third, the impact of men's working hours input on per capita household consumption expenditures is negative, with the degree of impact being higher and then lower than that of women's, and the extreme value point being lower than that of women's. On average, both men's and women's working hours have a negative impact on household consumption expenditures, and the degree of impact of men's working hours on household consumption expenditures is higher than that of women's. Household consumption expenditures decreased by 2.33 % for each unit increase in working hours in the male sample and about 2.91 times as much as that in the female sample. In terms of the squared working hours term, men's working hours input and household consumption expenditures shows an inverted “U”-shape non-linear relationship. It is calculated that men's working hours reaches an inflection point of about 59 h per week and start to have a dampening effect on household consumption expenditures. The extreme value point is smaller than that of women's working hours, and slightly higher than the current average men's working hours. Therefore, it can be tentatively concluded that men's working hours have a positive effect on household consumption expenditures at this stage. In terms of labor hours, the male sample shows a gradual increase in the contribution of labor hours to household consumption expenditures compared with the 30-h week, especially in the range of 30–40 and 40–50 h, and to a greater extent than the female sample.

In summary, significant gender differences exist in the economic effects of working hours input on household economic welfare. The positive effect of working hours input on household economic welfare is greater for women than for men. Additionally, working hours input has an inverted “U”-shaped relationship with the household savings rate that measures household economic welfare, with the bands of 30–40 and 40–50 h per week having a greater impact on household economic welfare than weekly hours below 30 h. In addition, the mechanism by which working hours input affects household economic welfare also shows significant gender differences. The positive effect of women's working hours input on per capita household income is higher than that of men's, and working hours input has an inverted “U”-shaped relationship with per capita household income. Men's working hours input has a higher negative impact on household consumption expenditure compared with women's and an inverted “U”-shaped relationship with household consumption expenditures, with women having a higher impact on household consumption expenditures in the range of 30–40 and 40–50 h compared with weekly hours below 30 h.

Research conclusions and policy implications

Research conclusions

Using data from China Family Panel Studies Survey(CFPS) of 2014, 2016 and 2018, the paper empirically analyzes the impact of women's working hours on household economic welfare, explores gender differences in the impact of working hours on household economic welfare, and provides an important micro-level basis for assessing the economic consequences of working hours.

Firstly, it examines the relationship between women's working hours input and household economic welfare, finding that the continuous term, squared term and subinterval of women's working hours all contribute to the economic effect enhancement. In addition, the mean female working hours are 51.09 h, which is at the stage of economic effect enhancement. On average, an increase of 1 h per week in women's working hours input is associated with an economic effect of 1.12 %.

Secondly, it analyzes the effects of working hours input on household economic welfare from a gender perspective, finding that the effects of working hours input on economic effects are stronger for women than for men. Men's working hours input exerts positive effects on household economic welfare, with the degree of effects being higher and then lower than that of women. The extreme value point of men's working hours is lower than that of women. The extreme value point for maximizing household economic welfare for women's working hours input is 17.59 h longer than men's, compared to weekly working hours shorter than 30 h, with a degree of impact up to 4.21 % higher than men's.

Thirdly, the mechanism based on which working hours input influences the household economy was extensively analyzed, revealing that the effects of household economic welfare on working hours input for both women and men stems from its income-generating effects on the household. The effects of weekly working hours above 60 h on per capita household income were found to be as high as 50.23 % for women, while the positive effects of working hours input on per capita household income were higher for men. According to the gender analysis of the mechanism of the economic effect, the increase in the income effect of the male sample was also due to the income-generating effect of working hours input on households, with the effect of working hours input on household income being 1.12 times higher for men than women.

As can be seen from the above analysis, the gender differences in time allocation and income levels reflect the inconsistent pace of gender equality development in both the work and family spheres. The roles of sSocial and family should not be based on a gender-specific division of labor between men and women, but rather on proper identification of the roles of each sex and the possibility of having both a family and a career.

Policy implications

The research findings, which contribute to a proper understanding of the value of labor, strongly support the formulation of policies that promote household welfare and strike a rational balance between the market labor force and the household for both sexes. The policy implications of the paper include:

To promote high-quality economic development, the level of workers' wage income should be raised. High-quality economic development requires labor input as a strong support, which has a rich connotation, not only reflected in the stock and increase of labor force, but also in the production efficiency of labor force itself. There is a relatively slow and steady growth in the number of labor forces mainly due to the increase of labor efficiency. The significance of high-quality economic development lies in the improvement of labor efficiency, the creation of more material wealth and the increase of the overall social interest level. Under this premise, the fundamental way to raise the level of workers’ wage income requires an increase in labor efficiency.

The gender labor gap should be narrowed based on gender equality in employment opportunities. This can be achieved in the following two ways. Firstly, investment in human capital, with a particular focus on investment in education, should be increased. Investment in education can take the form of increased years of schooling and on-the-job training. Secondly, to protect the legitimate rights and interests of women in employment, society should try its best to create equal career choice opportunities and fair salary systems for both genders. The industry barriers caused by market segmentation affect access to resources and hinder the development of the labor market. Employment discrimination, family relationships, social ties and other factors widen the income gap. To solve the above problems, employment opportunities and income distribution equity should be promoted.

To build a family-friendly working environment, the working hours system and work arrangements can be improved in the following ways. Firstly, a flexible working hours system can be established. On top of the existing system of maternity leave, paternity leave and family care leave, a flexible working hours system should be set up to shorten the weekly or daily working hours of workers with family responsibilities, to meet the childcare needs of workers and other care needs of close relatives. Secondly, flexible working hours and flexible working arrangements should be implemented. In terms of working systems, enterprises are encouraged to implement flexible working hours and flexible working arrangements to effectively support women's childcare-workplace balance, and achieve their work-family balance by supporting enterprises from their aspirations. Thirdly, from the perspective of workers, the specific work objectives of improving labor efficiency, labor value and welfare and reducing involution should be defined in the practical work.

Further research could be conducted in the future to determine the consistency across age levels and different marital statuses or family structures with these findings.
