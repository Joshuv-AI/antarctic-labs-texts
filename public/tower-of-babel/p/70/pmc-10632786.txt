Knockout of transcription factor OsERF65 enhances ROS scavenging ability and confers resistance to rice sheath blight
Authors: Wenya Xie, Wenlei Cao, Shuaibing Lu, Jianhua Zhao, Xiaopin Shi, Xuanyu Yue, Guangda Wang, Zhiming Feng, Keming Hu, Zongxiang Chen, Shimin Zuo
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractRice sheath blight (ShB) is a devastating disease that severely threatens rice production worldwide. Induction of cell death represents a key step during infection by the ShB pathogen Rhizoctonia solani. Nonetheless, the underlying mechanisms remain largely unclear. In the present study, we identified a rice transcription factor, OsERF65, that negatively regulates resistance to ShB by suppressing cell death. OsERF65 was significantly upregulated by R. solani infection in susceptible cultivar Lemont and was highly expressed in the leaf sheath. Overexpression of OsERF65 (OsERF65OE) decreased rice resistance, while the knockout mutant (oserf65) exhibited significantly increased resistance against ShB. The transcriptome assay revealed that OsERF65 repressed the expression of peroxidase genes after R. solani infection. The antioxidative enzyme activity was significantly increased in oserf65 plants but reduced in OsERF65OE plants. Consistently, hydrogen peroxide content was apparently reduced in oserf65 plants but accumulated in OsERF65OE plants. OsERF65 directly bound to the GCC box in the promoter regions of four peroxidase genes and suppressed their transcription, reducing the ability to scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS). The oserf65 mutant exhibited a slight decrease in plant height but increased grain yield. Overall, our results revealed an undocumented role of OsERF65 that acts as a crucial regulator of rice resistance to R. solani and a potential target for improving both ShB resistance and rice yield.Keywords: cell death, OsERF65, peroxidase, rice, ROS, sheath blight

INTRODUCTION

Rice sheath blight (ShB), caused by the necrotrophic fungus Rhizoctonia solani, is one of the most devastating rice diseases worldwide (Cao et al., 2022; Li et al., 2021; Molla et al., 2020). The prevalence of ShB has significantly increased over the past decades, threatening rice production, accounting for 10%–30% yield loss annually and a reduction in grain quality (Molla et al., 2020; Zhu et al., 2019). Although cell death or hypersensitivity reactions in the host are beneficial for improving host resistance to biotrophic infection by limiting or cutting off the nutrient supply, they promote susceptibility to necrotrophy (Li et al., 2019; Mandal et al., 2019). R. solani, as a typically necrotrophic fungus, generally absorbs nutrients from dead cells or tissues for their reproduction, and during infection the pathogen can secrete large amounts of cell wall‐degrading enzymes, toxins and effector proteins to promote cell death (Bhaskar Rao et al., 2020; Oliver & Solomon, 2010; Singh et al., 2019). R. solani infection has been reported to cause the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and induce host cell death (Cao et al., 2022). However, mechanisms underlying the regulation of ROS production during R. solani infection remain largely unknown.

ROS are key signalling molecules that participate in plant growth, development and stimuli responses (Waszczak et al., 2018). In general, plant ROS content is maintained at a baseline level to ensure cellular homeostasis by balancing ROS production and scavenging (Choudhury et al., 2017; Qi et al., 2017). However, this homeostasis is easily disrupted under biotic and abiotic stress conditions, resulting in ROS accumulation in different cell compartments and promoting cell death (Mittler et al., 2022; Qi et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2022; Waszczak et al., 2018). Plant peroxidases (PODs) are antioxidant enzyme proteins that scavenge ROS and protect host cells from oxidative damage (Liu et al., 2021; Torres et al., 2006). Based on their functions, the plant PODs can be classified into three subgroups: classes I (ascorbate peroxidases), II (lignin peroxidases) and III (secretory peroxidases), in which the peroxidase III enzymes catalyse the metabolism of H2O2 by accepting electrons (Liu et al., 2021). In rice, the peroxidase III family genes Os05g04470 and Os10g39170 encode H2O2‐degradation enzymes and are directly regulated by the C2H2 transcription factor Bsr‐d1. Reducing the expression of these peroxidase genes enhances rice resistance to Magnaporthe oryzae (Li et al., 2017). OsPrx30 belongs to the class III peroxidase family, which mediates ROS scavenging to regulate resistance against rice bacterial blight (Liu et al., 2021). Four peroxidase genes (Os01g22249, Os03g02920, Os03g32050 and Os04g59210) have been reported to be directly upregulated by the bZIP transcription factor OsbZIP73; overexpression of OsbZIP73 significantly enhanced ROS‐scavenging ability (Liu et al., 2018). With regard to necrotrophic pathogen, two previous studies found that overexpression of rice gene OsPrx114 in carrot significantly enhances resistance to Sclerotinia sclerotiorum and Botrytis cinerea through the rapid removal of H2O2 during the oxidative burst response, increased expression of PR genes and enhanced lignin formation (Wally et al., 2009; Wally & Punja, 2010). Recent studies showed that the accumulation of antioxidant enzymes was apparently higher in resistant ShB genotypes but lower in susceptible genotypes, indicating that the antioxidant system and ROS homeostasis play an important role in regulating rice ShB resistance (Naveenkumar et al., 2022; Wang, Shu, et al., 2021). However, the underlying mechanism, such as how transcription factors modulate POD and the antioxidant system in response to R. solani infection, remains to be determined.

Transcription factors, such as WRKY, AP2/ERF and MYB, play pivotal roles in the regulation of pathogen resistance in plants (Wang, Niu, & Zheng, 2021; Wani et al., 2021; Xie et al., 2022). The rice AP2/ERF transcription factor superfamily can be divided into four subfamilies, AP2, ERF, RAV and DREB, according to the number of AP2/ERF domains and different functions. It has been established that ERF is the largest subfamily, consisting of 136 members in rice (Xie et al., 2022). ERF subfamily proteins typically possess one conserved AP2/ERF domain composed of about 60 amino acid residues and can specifically bind to C/AGCCGCC core motifs (Agarwal et al., 2006; Zarei et al., 2011). Increasing evidence substantiates the significance of ERF transcription factors in rice resistance to various pathogens (Hong et al., 2022; Xie et al., 2022). For example, OsERF83, OsERF922, OsBIERF3 and OsEREBP1 can be induced by M. oryzae infection or elicitor treatment. Overexpression of OsERF83 improved rice resistance to M. oryzae by upregulating several pathogenesis‐related (PR) genes (Tezuka et al., 2019). In contrast, OsERF922 is a negative regulator of rice resistance to blast through modulating PR genes (Liu et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2016). OsBIERF3 positively regulates plant resistance to both M. oryzae and Xanthomonas oryzae pv. oryzae by regulating cell wall biosynthetic enzyme‐related genes (Hong et al., 2022). OsEREBP1 can be phosphorylated by OsMPK12, enhancing its ability to bind to the GCC box at the promoter of PR genes and increase their expression for resistance to X. oryzae (Cheong et al., 2003; Tran et al., 2018). A recent transcriptome study revealed that at least 39 AP2/ERF‐encoding genes were up‐ or downregulated in rice leaves after inoculation with R. solani, suggesting that the AP2/ERF transcription factors play an important role in the interaction between rice and R. solani (Yuan et al., 2020). However, the roles of the OsERF transcription factors in regulating rice resistance to R. solani remain largely unknown.

In the present study, we systematically screened the rice ERF genes and identified OsERF65, which was strongly induced by R. solani infection and negatively regulated rice resistance to ShB by inhibiting ROS scavenging pathway. OsERF65 directly bound to the promoter and suppressed the expression of four peroxidase genes to modulate ROS homeostasis. The oserf65 mutant showed enhanced resistance to ShB but improved grain yield. Overall, the present study uncovered a regulatory function of OsERF65 in rice–R. solani interaction and provided a potential target for improving rice ShB resistance and grain yield.

RESULTS

Identification of OsERF65 gene in the rice response to R. solani

Over the past decades, many ERF proteins have been identified in the rice response to biotic stresses, while very few were documented in the interaction between rice and ShB (Xie et al., 2022). To mine rice ERF genes that respond to R. solani, we analysed previously published microarray data representing global transcriptional profiles in the susceptible variety Lemont and resistant variety YSBR1 after inoculation with R. solani (Cao et al., 2022). Over 30 OsERF genes were systematically analysed, of which six genes, OsERF65, OsERF83, OsERF86, OsERF91, OsERF93 and OsERF136, were significantly upregulated at 20 h postinoculation (hpi) in both Lemont and YSBR1 (Figure S1). To further validate the responses of these six genes to R. solani, we employed a reverse transcription‐quantitative real‐time PCR (RT‐qPCR) assay to detect their expression patterns. The results showed that OsERF65 was strongly induced, with the highest expression level in Lemont, while it was weakly induced in YSBR1 (Figure S2), suggesting that OsERF65 may contribute to the susceptibility of Lemont to R. solani infection. To investigate the mechanism underlying the rapid response of OsERF65 to R. solani infection, we analysed the cis elements in its 2‐kb promoter region in YSBR1 and Lemont. The results showed that there are seven cis elements (AATCA) and three GT‐1 (GAAAAA) cis elements (Figure S3) that were previously identified to respond to R. solani infection (Li et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2022), suggesting that the presence of these cis elements in the promoter region may facilitate a rapid response to R. solani infection. Protein sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis showed that OsERF65 consists of a conserved AP2/ERF domain of about 60 amino acids and is closely related to rice OsERF70 and Arabidopsis HRE1 and HRE2 (Figure 1a,b). HRE1 and HRE2 have been reported to be upregulated in hypoxic conditions, causing a reduction in energy production (Seok et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2011). OsERF70, also known as OsEREBP1, has been reported to regulate the expression of some pathogenesis‐related defence genes (Cheong et al., 2003). Accordingly, we focused on the role of OsERF65 in mediating the interaction between rice and R. solani.

OsERF65 protein localizes to the nucleus and acts as a transcription activator

Based on the putative nuclear localization signal (NLS) in the N‐terminal of the protein (Figure 1b), OsERF65 was predicted to localize in the nucleus. To validate this subcellular localization, the pM999‐OsERF65‐GFP construct and the control pM999‐GFP were developed and transiently introduced into rice protoplasts. The result showed that the OsERF65‐GFP signal was observed in the nucleus (Figure S4) and colocalized with the NLS‐RFP (red fluorescent protein) signal (Figure 2a), confirming that OsERF65 protein is localized in the nucleus.

To investigate the transcriptional regulatory activity of OsERF65, we transfected the vector pGBKT7 fused with the full‐length coding sequence (CDS) of OsERF65 and different truncated mutants, including OsERF65ΔN, OsERF65ΔAP2, OsERF65ΔC and the C‐terminal regions, along with the empty vector pGADT7, in the yeast strain AH109. The results showed that OsERF65 exhibited self‐activation activity, and the C‐terminal of OsERF65 acted as a potential transcriptional activator (Figure 2b). Furthermore, we employed a dual luciferase reporter assay to confirm this result in rice protoplasts. The construct of GAL4VP16 or OsERF65‐GAL4VP16 under the control of the CaMV 35S promoter was used as the effector, which was co‐introduced into rice protoplasts with the 4 × UAS‐LUC (luciferase) reporter construct, and REN (Renilla luciferase) was used as the control. The OsERF65‐GAL4VP16 effector construct exhibited higher activity of LUC:REN compared with the GAL4VP16 construct (Figure 2c). An RT‐qPCR assay was conducted to detect the expression pattern of OsERF65 in different rice tissues. The results showed that OsERF65 exhibited the highest expression in the leaf sheath (Figure 2d), which is the predominant infection part of R. solani. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that OsERF65 is a transcription activator and localizes in the nucleus.

OsERF65 negatively regulates rice ShB resistance

To explore the roles of OsERF65 in the rice–R. solani interaction, we generated two independent transgenic OsERF65 overexpression (OsERF65OE‐1, −4) lines in commercial rice variety XuDao3. The RT‐qPCR results showed that the transcript levels of OsERF65 were significantly overexpressed in both OsERF65OE‐1 and ‐4 lines compared with the XuDao3 wild type (WT) (Figure S5a). We also employed the CRISPR/Cas9 strategy to knockout the endogenous OsERF65 in the WT to generate oserf65 mutant lines. The 20‐nucleotide sequences in the second exon of OsERF65 were selected as the target site for Cas9 cleavage (Figure S5b). Two independent homozygous mutants (oserf65‐1, −2, with a 1‐bp deletion of an A and a G, respectively) were selected for further analysis (Figure S5c). The two mutants had truncated OsERF65 sequences, causing partial OsERF65 proteins in which the AP2/ERF domain was destroyed (Figure S5d). The two independent lines of OsERF65OE and oserf65 were used to determine the role of OsERF65 in response to R. solani.

All these transgenic plants were inoculated with R. solani in the field, and the lesion length was investigated at 7 days postinoculation (dpi). The two independent OsERF65OE lines showed significantly enhanced susceptibility to R. solani with lesion lengths of 14.1 ± 1.6 and 14.9 ± 2.1 cm, respectively, while the two independent oserf65 mutant lines displayed apparently enhanced resistance to R. solani with lesion lengths of 9.2 ± 1.4 and 9.5 ± 1.7 cm, respectively, compared with the WT of 12.0 ± 1.4 cm (Figure 3a,b). The results observed in the detached leaf sheath inoculation assay were consistent in the growth chamber (Figure 3c,d). Furthermore, we employed lactophenol cotton blue (LPCB) to stain the inoculation sites at 48 hpi and found that the density of hyphae in the OsERF65OE plants was significantly higher than that in the WT but was significantly reduced in the oserf65 mutants (Figure 3e). The biomass of R. solani in the inoculated part was further analysed. As expected, the relative abundance of fungal biomass in WT was significantly lower than that in OsERF65OE plants, but significantly higher than that in the oserf65 mutant (Figure 3f). Taken together, these data indicate that OsERF65 negatively regulates rice resistance to R. solani.

Transcriptomic analysis of WT and OsERF65OE plants in response to R. solani

To explore the OsERF65‐mediated resistance mechanism to R. solani infection, we performed a comparative transcriptome analysis using leaf sheaths from OsERF65OE lines and WT plants inoculated with R. solani. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that the transcriptome data could be classified into four clusters without intersection (Figure 4a). Based on the RNA‐seq data, we identified 7719 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in WT plants after inoculation compared to mock treatment. Among these DEGs, 4875 were upregulated and 2844 were downregulated (Figure 4b). Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis showed that the upregulated DEGs in WT plants were mainly associated with catalytic activity, oxidoreductase activity, cofactor, ion, heme binding, lyase activity, transporter activity and kinase activity (Figure S6a). Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis showed that the upregulated DEGs were mainly associated with metabolic pathways, including the biosynthesis of amino acids, secondary metabolites, citrate cycle, glutathione metabolism, phenylpropanoid, diterpenoid and flavonoid (Figure 4c). These findings suggest that rice requires energy, phytoalexins and antioxidant enzymes for defence against R. solani infection. Interestingly, the downregulated DEGs were mainly associated with photosynthesis and antenna proteins, and particularly with those related to the light‐harvesting complex in photosystem I and photosystem II (PSII) assembly (Figure S6b). These results are consistent with previous findings that R. solani suppresses plant photosynthetic systems, especially the PSII complex, to facilitate its colonization (Cao et al., 2022; Ghosh et al., 2017; Yuan et al., 2020).

On the other hand, total of 1810 (980 upregulated and 830 downregulated) and 1322 (856 upregulated and 466 downregulated) DEGs were identified in the OsERF65OE plants after R. solani infection and mock treatment, respectively (Figure 4b). KEGG pathway analysis showed that the 980 upregulated DEGs were mainly associated with diterpenoid biosynthesis, amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and the MAPK signalling pathway under the R. solani infection (Figure S6c). However, the 830 downregulated DEGs were mainly associated with phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and flavonoid biosynthesis (Figure 4d), suggesting the basal defence pathways in OsERF65OE plant response to R. solani infection were suppressed, which is the opposite to the response observed in WT plants (Figure 4c). GO analysis showed that 466 and 830 downregulated DEGs in OsERF65OE plants were associated with catalytic activity under both mock treatment and R. solani inoculation (Figures 4e and S6d), especially for the activities of peroxidase, oxidoreductase and antioxidant (Figure 4e), which have been well documented to participate in ROS scavenging (Liu et al., 2021; Torres et al., 2006). Increasing evidence suggests that ROS accumulation can lead to cell death and is beneficial to the infection of necrotrophic pathogens (Li et al., 2019; Mandal et al., 2019). We therefore focused on peroxidase genes, which directly function in scavenging H2O2, one of the important components of ROS. The heat map showed that the 19 genes involved in peroxidase and antioxidant activity were significantly downregulated in the OsERF65OE plants after inoculation of R. solani. In addition, genes related to phenylpropanoid (n = 10) and flavonoid biosynthesis (n = 6) were also downregulated (Figure 4f). RT‐qPCR assays verified the downregulated expression levels of those DEGs associated with peroxidase and antioxidant activity (Figures 4f and S7). Together, these results suggest that OsERF65 may inhibit the expression of genes associated with oxidoreductase and antioxidant activities to regulate the ROS levels during R. solani infection.

OsERF65 directly regulates the expression of four peroxidase genes

To further identify the candidate genes involved downstream of OsERF65‐mediated resistance to R. solani, we searched for the GCC box on the promoter of 19 peroxidase genes in DEGs. Results showed that 12 genes contained the core sequence of the GCC box in their promoters (Table S1). RT‐qPCR results showed that 11 of these genes were downregulated in OsERF65OE plants and upregulated in oserf65 mutants (Figures 5a and S8). We then employed the yeast one‐hybrid (Y1H) assay to validate whether OsERF65 binds to the promoters of the 11 genes. Y1H results showed that OsERF65 only bound to the promoters of Os01g0326300 (OsPrx13), Os07g0677100 (OsPrx110), Os08g0113000 (OsPrx117) and Os10g0109300 (OsPrx125) (Figures 5b and S9). We analysed the type of GCC box in the four peroxidase genes and identified the core sequences of AGCCGCC and CGCCGCC in their promoters (Figure S10). To confirm the interaction between the OsERF65 and the two types of GCC box binding sites, we performed an electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA). We chose 30 bp sequences containing AGCCGCC and CGCCGCC from the promoters of OsPrx125 and OsPrx110 as the probes, respectively. EMSA revealed that OsERF65 bound to the two types of GCC box, but not the mutated GCC box version (AGGCATC) (Figure 5c,d), consistent with previous findings that G2, G5 and C7 in the GCC box AGCCGCC are essential for the binding of ERF proteins (Magnani et al., 2004). In addition, we performed a new chromatin profiling method called transient and simplified CUT&Tag (tsCut&Tag) (Wu et al., 2022) to detect the binding of OsERF65 to the promoters of OsPrx13, OsPrx110, OsPrx117 and OsPrx125 in rice protoplasts. The GCC box motif is only found at the P1 position of OsPrx13, OsPrx110 and OsPrx125, as well as the P2 position of OsPrx13 and OsPrx117. The results showed that OsERF65 binding was enriched on the GCC box motif‐containing regions but not on the other regions of these promoters (Figure 5e). Finally, we analysed the function of OsERF65 on the four peroxidase genes through a transient assay using the LUC reporter driven by approximately 500 bp promoter of the four genes in rice protoplast (P OsPrx13 :LUC, P OsPrx110 :LUC, P OsPrx117 :LUC, P OsPrx125 :LUC). The results showed that LUC activities were apparently repressed by OsERF65 (Figure 5f). Collectively, these results indicate that OsERF65 can directly bind to the promoters of OsPrx13, OsPrx110, OsPrx117 and OsPrx125 genes and suppress their expression.

OsERF65 inhibits the ROS scavenging capability and promotes cell death

Given that many genes in the peroxidase family were downregulated in OsERF65OE plants after inoculation of R. solani, we hypothesized that OsERF65 regulates the rice R. solani resistance by affecting the ROS scavenging capability to promote cell death. Thus, we inoculated detached leaves of the OsERF65 transgenic plant and WT. We found that, compared with WT, the lesion area was significantly higher and lower in OsERF65OE plants and oserf65 mutants, respectively (Figure 6a,b). Consistently, 3,3′‐diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining showed higher content of H2O2 in the inoculation sites of OsEFF65OE plants and minimal levels in the oserf65 mutants (Figure 6c). A similar trend was found using the inoculated leaf sheaths of OsERF65 transgenic plants and WT in the field (Figure 6d). We also detected POD activity and H2O2 content using the inoculated leaf sheaths from the OsERF65 transgenic plants and WT in the field at 48 hpi of R. solani. The results showed that the POD activity was significantly lower in OsERF65OE plants and higher in the oserf65 mutants compared with the WT (Figure 6e). In contrast, the H2O2 content was significantly higher in OsERF65OE plants but lower in the oserf65 mutants (Figure 6f). These results suggested that the accumulation of H2O2 in OsERF65OE plants after inoculation could promote cell death, facilitating R. solani infection. Furthermore, we examined the catalase activity of the four peroxidases. Chemiluminescence assay results showed the four peroxidase proteins have significant catalase activity (Figure 6g,h). Considering that the OsERF65 belongs to the ethylene signalling pathway, we investigated the involvement of OsERF65‐mediated ShB resistance in ethylene signalling. We found that three critical genes, OsEIN2/OsACS1, OsACO1 and OsACO2, in ethylene biosynthesis had significantly reduced expression in OsERF65OE compared with WT after inoculation of R. solani (Table S2). The expression level of four POD genes downstream of OsERF65 were upregulated after the ethylene treatment (Figure S11), which suggests that OsERF65‐mediated ShB resistance is associated with ethylene signalling. Above all, these data suggest that OsERF65 functions mainly through suppressing the expression of POD genes and ultimately enhancing cell death to promote R. solani infection.

Oserf65 mutants showed enhanced yield and reduced plant height

ERF transcription factors have been reported to play diverse roles in plant development and immunity. To investigate the effects of OsERF65 on rice growth and development, we assessed the agronomic traits of its transgenic plants. The results showed that the OsERF65OE and oserf65 mutants had similar tiller number, panicle length, flag leaf length and flag leaf width, grain length, grain width and grain weight to WT, while an 8.22% and 10.37% decrease in plant height was observed in oserf65‐1 and oserf65‐2 mutants, respectively (Figures 7a–d and S12a–h). Interestingly, the oserf65 mutants showed significantly increased primary branch numbers (+11.67%–14.17%; Figure 7e), secondary branch numbers (+15.44%–17.19%; Figure 7f) and grain setting rate (14.35%–16.64%; Figure 7g). As a result, the grain numbers per panicle and plant yield of oserf65 mutants were significantly increased by 15.35%–16.64% and 13.84%–15.91%, respectively, compared with WT (Figure 7h,i). We therefore performed a field test for these lines and found that compared with the WT, the plot yields of the two oserf65 lines were increased by 7.69% and 9.14% (Figure 7j). Overall, these data indicate that OsERF65 is a promising target for improving both ShB resistance and grain yield.

DISCUSSION

As necrotrophic fungus, R. solani secretes many cell‐wall degrading enzymes and toxins to quickly kill cells and obtain nutrients for self‐reproduction (Oliver & Solomon, 2010). An increasing body of evidence from recently published studies suggests that injecting effectors into cells can trigger host cell death (Anderson et al., 2017; Li et al., 2019; Niu et al., 2021). In this study, we found that R. solani infection could rapidly induce OsERF65 expression, which further inhibited the expression of peroxidase genes, reduced the POD activity and inhibited ROS scavenging, resulting in local ROS accumulation and cell death to facilitate pathogen colonization and reproduction. In summary, we propose a working model whereby OsERF65 negatively regulates resistance to rice ShB (Figure 8).

Proposed working model of OsERF65 modulating rice resistance to Rhizoctonia solani. R. solani infection induced the expression of OsERF65. OsERF65 directly binds to POD genes and suppresses their expression, resulting in the excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the rice leaf sheath and triggering cell death, which promotes R. solani invasion.

OsERF65 functions as a transcriptional repressor in rice resistance to ShB

ERF transcription factors act as either transcriptional activators or transcriptional repressors in the plant response to the biotic stress or regulatory network (Xie et al., 2022). OsBIERF3 is a transcriptional activator that positively regulates rice resistance to fungal and bacterial diseases, but negatively regulates cold tolerance by upregulating a set of genes including PR, MAPK, CESA and cold‐responsive genes (Hong et al., 2022). Current evidence suggests that OsERF101 has transcriptional activation activity and positively regulates rice tolerance to drought stress by upregulating peroxidase activity and scavenging ROS (Jin et al., 2018). OsERF83 also functions as a transcriptional activator, enhancing rice resistance to M. oryzae by upregulating several PR genes (Tezuka et al., 2019). Although OsERF922 acts as a transcriptional activator, it can inhibit a set of PR genes and several genes involved in the phytoalexin biosynthetic enzymes by binding the GCC box sequence on their promoters (Liu et al., 2012).

In the present study, biochemical assays showed that OsERF65 played a transcriptional activator in GAL4 binding assays in yeast and rice protoplast by indirectly activating the downstream gene (Figure 2b,c), but served as a transcriptional repressor to directly suppress the expression of four POD genes (Figure 5e,f). We found that OsERF65 could directly bind to the GCC box sequences in the promoter of four peroxidase genes via Y1H, EMSA and tsCUT‐Tag‐qPCR assays (Figure 5b–e). Additionally, the expression of four peroxidase genes was significantly decreased in OsERF65OE plants but increased in oserf65 knockout mutants (Figure 5a). We hypothesize that OsERF65 can act as either a transcriptional activator or a repressor under different circumstances, depending on the presence of biotic or abiotic stresses, proteins and DNA interacting with OsERF65.

OsERF65 enhances rice susceptibility to ShB via inhibiting ROS scavenging

ROS are important for plants sensing abiotic and biotic stresses, and are necessary for cellular proliferation and differentiation. Nonetheless, ROS can yield detrimental effects based on their oxidative ability when they overaccumulate in cell compartments (Mittler, 2017; Mittler et al., 2022). Accordingly, the balance between ROS production and scavenging must be tightly regulated to protect the plant cell from oxidative damage (Mittler et al., 2022). The most stable form of ROS is H2O2, which antioxidative enzymes such as catalase (CAT) and peroxidase can scavenge (Mittler et al., 2022; Song et al., 2021; Waszczak et al., 2018). Overwhelming evidence substantiates that ROS are involved in plant disease resistance. For instance, ROS can enhance resistance to rice blast and leaf blight (Chu et al., 2022; Gao et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2021; Liu & Zhang, 2022), but enhance the plant susceptibility to necrotrophic pathogens such as S. sclerotiorum, B. cinerea and R. solani (Ding et al., 2020; Lopez‐Cruz et al., 2017; Wang, Shu, et al., 2021). Our previous study found that the resistant variety YSBR1 showed apparently stronger ROS scavenging ability than the susceptible variety Lemont after infection of R. solani (Cao et al., 2022). The ShB‐resistant rice lines/accessions exhibited significantly less H2O2 accumulation compared with the susceptible accessions at 48 hpi of R. solani (Oreiro et al., 2020). Another study has shown that enhancing rice tolerance to ROS can also enhance resistance to ShB by activating the rice antioxidant systems, such as ascorbic acid and glutathione, which can effectively reduce the oxidative stress caused by R. solani infection (Wang, Shu, et al., 2021). These clues suggest that decreasing ROS production will benefit the suppression of infection by necrotrophic pathogens.

In the present study, we characterized OsERF65, which negatively regulates rice resistance to R. solani by modulating ROS levels. OsERF65OE plants showed enhanced susceptibility to R. solani, whereas the oserf65 mutants exhibited increased resistance (Figure 3). GO analysis revealed that downregulated DEGs were significantly enriched in the peroxidase and antioxidant activity pathway (Figure 4e), with 19 peroxidase genes downregulated in OsERF65OE plants (Figure 4f and Table S1). We identified four downstream target genes that may be directly regulated by OsERF65 (Figure 5). The four POD genes belong to the class III peroxidase family, and chemiluminescence experiments showed that they all have the activity to decompose H2O2 (Figure 6h). In addition, DAB staining showed that H2O2 accumulated in the OsERF65OE plants after inoculation with R. solani but exhibited decreased levels in the oserf65 mutants (Figure 6c,d). POD activity and H2O2 content were subsequently examined. Reduced content of H2O2 and higher POD activity was found in the oserf65 mutant and the opposite findings were found in the OsERF65OE plants (Figure 6e,f). In summary, our results suggest that OsERF65 is induced by R. solani infection, which inhibits the expression of peroxidase genes and suppresses ROS scavenging, resulting in cell death and facilitating R. solani infection.

OsERF65 has potential for application against rice ShB and high grain yield

Rice ShB is one of the most devasting fungal diseases of cultivated rice worldwide and may result in up to 50% grain yield loss under optimal conditions (Molla et al., 2020; Wang, Shu, et al., 2021). Currently, chemical fungicides remain the predominant approach to control the disease, releasing chemical toxins into the environment (Li et al., 2021; Singh et al., 2019). Although many studies have attempted to document quantitative trait loci (QTLs) conferring rice ShB resistance, only a few QTLs have been identified, such as qSB‐9TQ and qSB11LE (Molla et al., 2020; Zuo et al., 2013, 2014). Due to the lack of major ShB resistance QTLs in rice, the development of an ShB‐resistant variety has been very slow. Improving resistance to pathogens and grain yield are the two major targets of rice breeding (Chen et al., 2019; Huang et al., 2010). In this study, we identified the transcription factor OsERF65. Interestingly, its knockout mutants significantly increased resistance against ShB without negatively affecting other agronomic traits (Figures 3 and S12). The plant height of oserf65 mutants was lower than that of the WT (Figure 7a,b), suggesting an ideal target for rice breeding to avoid lodging. Moreover, oserf65 mutants significantly increased the grain number per panicle and yield per plant (Figure 7h,i). In addition, the setting rate (Figure 7g) and the yield per plot (Figure 7j) were significantly increased in the oserf65 mutant. Although ERFs have been found to significantly regulate plant development, few studies have reported their function on grain yield. It is therefore essential to further elucidate how OsERF65 regulates rice branch development and ultimately increases grain yield. In summary, OsERF65 represents an ideal target for developing rice varieties with enhanced ShB resistance and higher grain yield through CRISPR‐cas9 associated technologies.

EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES

Experimental materials

The rice variety XuDao3 (japonica) was used as the WT plant in this study. It has been established that the rice variety YSBR1 exhibits high resistance to ShB, while Lemont displays high susceptibility to ShB (Cao et al., 2022). For the generation of OsERF65OE transgenic plants, the complete CDS of OsERF65 was amplified from Nipponbare cDNA, then inserted into the pCAMBIA1300U vector through the homologous recombination method. For the generation of the oserf65 mutants, one single guide RNA (sgRNA) targeting the second exon of OsERF65 was inserted into the pCAMBIA1305‐Cas9 vector. The transgenic plants were obtained through Agrobacterium tumefaciens‐mediated methods of genetic transformation based on a previously described protocol (Lin & Zhang, 2005). All primers used in this study are listed in Table S3.

Culture and inoculation of R. solani

The fungal isolate of RH9, with moderate pathogenicity, isolated from rice inoculated with R. solani AG1‐1A, was used to inoculate rice (Zuo et al., 2013). The method of inoculation of R. solani was performed as previously described (Cao et al., 2022). Briefly, RH9 was cultured on a plate containing potato dextrose agar at 25°C for 4 days. The mycelial discs were transferred into potato dextrose broth that contained sterilized toothpicks with an area of 0.02–0.03 cm2 until the hyphae covered the toothpicks. The toothpicks with hyphae were slightly inserted into the gap between the leaf sheath and stem to complete inoculation.

Phylogenetic analysis

For phylogenetic analysis, the protein sequences of ERF transcription factors were downloaded from the Rice Genome Annotation Project (RGAP) database (http://rice.uga.edu/) and the Arabidopsis Information Resource (TAIR) (https://www.arabidopsis.org/index.jsp) through gene accession numbers, then their sequences were imported into ClustalX2 software for sequence multiple alignments corresponding to the ERF domain. The neighbour‐joining method was used to generate the phylogenetic tree using MEGA 7 software.

Histochemical staining and observation

LPCB staining and preparation of inoculated leaf sheaths from the OsERF65 transgenic and WT plants at 48 hpi, and a light microscope (DMLS, 20–100×; Leica) was used for observation of the hyphal development. DAB staining of rice mutants, transgenic lines, and WT leaves and leaf sheath were conducted using a previously described protocol (Yang et al., 2017).

RNA‐seq and data analysis

The leaf sheath inoculated with R. solani and empty toothpicks at 24 hpi from WT and OsERF65OE‐4 transgenic plants were taken from multiple independent plants as one biological replicate, and three biological replicates were made in this experiment. RNA was extracted using the TRIzol reagent (Trans Gen). RNA sequencing was accomplished at Wuhan Benagen Technology Co., Ltd, in Wuhan, China, using the Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform. The read quality of the raw data was evaluated by the FastQC application and the low‐quality reads were moved. The clean sequence was mapped to the rice Nipponbare genome. Reads per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads values were calculated. DEGs were detected using DESeq2 with log2 fold change >|1| and false discovery rate (FDR)‐adjusted p < 0.01. The KEGG and GO analysis of DEGs was carried out by the Gene Denove online web server (https://www.omicshare.com/tools/). The original raw sequence data were deposited to the Sequence Read Archive of the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) with Bioproject ID PRJNA918425.

Gene expression analysis and measurement of fungal biomass

Inoculated leaf sheaths from WT and OsERF65 transgenic plants were sampled at 48 hpi. Total RNAs were extracted from the sample using the TRIzol reagent. The concentration of RNA was checked by an Ultra‐trace UV/VIS spectrophotometer (ND‐100, Hang Zhou MIU Instruments Co., Ltd). The Light Cycler 480 system was used for RT‐qPCR analysis. The gene expression levels were measured with three technical replicates and two biological replicates. The fungal biomass level was quantified using the expression level of the 18S rRNA gene and OsActin gene expression level as the control.

Yeast one‐ and two‐hybrid assays

For the Y1H assay, the peroxidase gene promoter containing the core sequence of GCCGCC‐box (c.500 bp) was cloned into the pLacZi vector, and the CDS of OsERF65 was inserted into the pB42AD vector. The resulting constructs were transfected into the yeast strain EGY48. The positive transformants were grown on SD/−Trp/–Ura plates containing 2% galactose, 2% raffinose, 1 × BU buffer, 0.08% X‐gal and 2% agar at 30°C for 3 days in the dark.

For the yeast two‐hybrid (Y2H) assay, the complete and different truncated CDS of OsERF65 were cloned into the pGBKT7 vector to generate the BD‐OsERF65 and BD‐ΔN, ΔAP2, ΔC and C‐terminal constructs. The resulting constructs and empty vector pGADT7 were transfected into the yeast strain AH109. The presence of the plasmids was confirmed by growth on SD medium lacking Leu and Trp (SD/−Leu/−Trp), then the positive yeast clones were suspended with sterile water and spotted onto plates containing SD/−Leu/−Trp/−His/−Ade medium. The self‐activation activity was observed after 3 days of incubation at 30°C.

Protein expression and EMSA

The complete CDS of OsERF65 was inserted into the pCold‐TF vectors (Xie, Ke, et al., 2021) and then was introduced to Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) cells. The competent cells were growth at 37°C for 2–3 h until OD600 = 0.4–0.5, and cultivated overnight at 16°C with addition of 0.2 mM isopropyl β‐D‐thiogalactoside (IPTG) for protein induction, then cells were collected by centrifugation at 4°C for His‐OsERF65 protein purification according to the manufacturer's instructions (GE Healthcare). For the double‐stranded DNA probe, equal amounts of forward and reverse strands, whose 5′‐ends were labelled with biotin, were mixed, boiled and then cooled to 25°C. Different amounts of unlabelled probes (20‐, 40‐ and 80‐fold of labelled probes) were incubated with biotin‐labelled probes at 28°C in binding buffer for 20 min using an EMSA kit (20148 Light Shift Chemiluminescent EMSA; Thermo Scientific). The samples were separated by nondenaturing electrophoresis using 6% native acrylamide gels in 0.5 × Tris‐borate‐EDTA (TBE) buffer under an electrical environment of 110 V for 1.5 h at 4°C. Then the gels were transferred to a nylon membrane (FFN10; Beyotime) in 0.5 × TBE under an electrical environment of 380 mA for 1 h at 4°C. The labelled double‐stranded DNA on the nylon membrane was detected by chemiluminescent assays according to the manufacturer's instructions (89880 Chemiluminescent Nucleic acid detection module kit; Thermo Scientific).

TsCUT&tag assay

The tsCUT&Tag experiment was performed as previously described (Wu et al., 2022) and operated according to the manufacturer's instructions (TD901‐01; Vazyme). First, protoplasts were isolated from rice leaf sheaths, and then the pM999‐GFP and pM999‐OsERF65‐GFP vectors were transiently expressed in the isolated protoplasts. Next, c.0.5–1.0 × 105 successfully transfected live cells were collected, immediately washed in wash buffer and immobilized on ConA‐Beads. The cells were then punctured with digitonin to allow binding of anti‐GFP (ABclonal) and IgG (Vazyme) antibodies, as well as the core hyperactive pG‐Tn5 transposase. Finally, the OsERF65‐binding DNA library was constructed for qPCR amplification using specific primers (Table S3).

Transient expression assays in rice protoplast

The transcriptional activity of OsERF65 was assessed in rice protoplast. Briefly, the CDS of OsERF65 was fused with GAL4DB‐VP16 as the effector. The reporter vector was 4 × UAS‐LUC, which contains 4 × UAS repeats for GAL4 binding (Cheng et al., 2015). The vector of 35S‐Renilla was used as a control to express the relative transcriptional activity.

To study the effects of the transcriptional activity of OsERF65 on its target genes, the CDS of OsERF65 was cloned into the GAL4DB vectors under the CaMV 35S promoter as the effector, the promoter of target genes containing the GCC box sequence was inserted into pGreenII‐0800 vector as the reporter, and the constructs were co‐transfected into rice protoplast according to the instructions (Xie, Ke, et al., 2021). The transcriptional activity was measured using the Dual Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega).

Measurement of POD activity and H2O2 content

To quantify POD activity, leaf sheath tissue was sampled from the OsERF65 transgenic plants and WT at 24 hpi with R. solani and put into liquid nitrogen immediately. About 100 mg of the sample was ground in liquid nitrogen into powder and mixed with 1 mL of 0.05 M phosphate‐buffered saline (pH 7.8) in a 2‐mL tube. After vigorously inverting and mixing, the tubes were centrifuged at 8000 g for 10 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was collected. POD catalyses the oxidation of specific substrates by H2O2, with characteristic absorption at a wavelength of 470 nm. POD activity was detected by spectrophotometric assay according to the manufacturer's instructions (Solarbio BC0095, Micro Peroxidase [POD] Assay Kit). POD activity (U/g) was calculated by the absorbance change of 0.01 at A470 in the reaction system per minute per gram of tissue.

For measurement of H2O2 content, 100 mg of the sample was ground in liquid nitrogen into powder, mixed with cold acetone, filtered to remove cellular debris, then centrifuged at 8000 g for 10 min at 4°C and the supernatant was collected. H2O2 was measured by spectrophotometric assay according to the manufacturer's instructions (Solarbio BC3590, Micro Hydrogen Peroxide Assay Kit).

Chemiluminescence assay

For the analysis of peroxidase activity kinetics, 5 μg of purified proteins from pCold‐OsPrx13, pCold‐OsPrx110, pCold‐OsPrx117, pCold‐OsPrx125 and pCold was transferred to a 96‐well white plate containing 100 μL of luminol/enhancer and 100 μL of stable peroxidase solution (Thermo, 89880F). Luminescence was immediately measured using a Spark multimode reader (Tecan). Three biological replicates were assayed for each sample.

Subcellular localization of OsERF65

The CDS of OsERF65 was cloned into the pM999‐GFP vector. The construct and the pM999‐NLS‐RFP vector were transiently co‐expressed in rice protoplasts and pM999‐GFP was the control. Protoplast was isolated from 2‐week‐old seedlings of WT plants in digestion solution for 4 h. For transformation, 5 μg of constructs was lightly mixed with protoplasts and an equal volume of polyethylene glycol 4000‐CaCl2, then the cells were incubated for 16 h at room temperature in the dark and collected according to the previous study (Xie, Ke, et al., 2021). GFP and RFP fluorescence were detected using a confocal microscope.

Hormone treatments

To investigate the effect of ethylene on the expression of OsERF65 and the four peroxidase genes, 4‐week‐old seedlings of WT plants were sprayed with 1 mM ethephon (Sigma‐Aldrich) dissolved in 0.05% Tween 20 (Tezuka et al., 2019). The leaf sheath was collected at 0, 4, 8, 12 and 24 h after the ethylene treatment. Leaf sheaths from plants treated with 0.05% Tween 20 solution were used as the control.

Evaluation of agronomic trait in field

The OsERF65OE transgenic plants and WT were cultivated in the paddy fields at Yangzhou University. Each replicate occupied 4 m2 and the width and length distances between two adjacent plants were 13 and 24 cm. Ten plants in the middle row were chosen for measurement. The agronomic traits of plant height, flag leaf length and width, tiller number, panicle length, primary and secondary branch number, grain width and length, grains per panicle, seed setting rate, 1000‐grain weight and grain yields per plot were measured at three independent replicate plots.
