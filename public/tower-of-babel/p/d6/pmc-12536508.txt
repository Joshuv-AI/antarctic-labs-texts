Legal questions of AI-generated immunological products for infectious diseases
Authors: Javier Zumárraga, Michael J McConnell, Daniel López, Antonio J Martín-Galiano
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTArtificial intelligence (AI) has potential to surpass human capabilities to reshape vaccine design. However, its integration raises legal, ethical, and intellectual property challenges. Transparency in AI-driven immunological products is crucial for regulatory approval and public trust. This article examines the “black-box” effect, liability concerns, and the need for human-interpretable reports summarizing the data processing and algorithm execution behind the AI-made decisions. This will ensure safety, accessibility, and regulatory compliance in the development of AI-generated vaccines and other immune therapeutics against infectious diseases.KEYWORDS: Black-box effect, explainable AI, immunoassay, machine learning, monoclonal antibody, reverse vaccinology, vaccine development, vaccine regulation

AI-designed vaccines may surpass efficacy of those designed by human experts

Immunization is one of the most cost-effective clinical interventions for improving life expectancy and reducing sequelae in allergic, infectious disease and oncological pathologies. However, generative AI has been strikingly less active in vaccine design than in other areas such as drug discovery,1 protein structure prediction (e.g. AlphaFold22) or semantics (e.g. ChatGPT3). Despite the continued extreme importance of infectious disease in global public health, AI-driven vaccine approaches have not yet been fully leveraged for some of the most pressing challenges, notably reduction of antimicrobial resistance, preventing zoonotic infections, and for pandemic response. Current vaccine development is limited by selection of classical antigens, difficulty to adapt to pathogen variability, long development times, durability of protection and safety issues. However, based on the well-documented success of AI in other scientific domains, AI may conceivably at least minimize these constraints by using all available data and high algorithm performance to efficiently identify vaccine candidates.

Many efforts in immunoinformatics, i.e. the study and modeling of immune system using computers, have focussed on epitope prediction, for both humoral and cellular branches of the adaptive immune responses. AI models are typically trained on curated datasets here containing known antigenic sequences, experimental immunogenicity data, and structural information, enabling them to predict and rank new vaccine candidates based on learned patterns. These approaches are combined in computational pipelines with additional evaluation modules or “filters” that assess parameters such as predicted safety (e.g., allergenicity, toxicity), immunogenicity, manufacturability, and conservation across pathogen variants (“strain coverage”), within the framework of the so-called reverse vaccinology (RV) paradigm, i.e. the rational identification of antigens using genome information and algorithms.4,5 Despite RV’s initial success in generating a proof-of-concept,6 many critical pathogens remain uncontrolled by licensed vaccines.7

Vaccine design and dosage regimen go far beyond antigen selection. Several steps in the process can be analyzed in formal molecular biology terms. For antigen and adjuvant selection, balancing adaptive humoral and cellular immune branches is often required for optimal efficacy. Epitope-based hybrid antigens require careful selection of linkers, epitope order and scaffold proteins.8,9 Other aspects such as rational point mutagenesis can enhance conformational stability, as exemplified by the introduction of prolines in the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein.10 The choice of vehicle, antigen dose, and administration regimen is often empirical. The optimal vaccine modality, e.g. nucleic acid vs. protein or viral vector vaccines, tailored to the pathogen and disease that is being prevented is difficult to anticipate. Another potential area in which AI could have significant impact is the identification, if required, of synergistic antigen combinations for multivalent vaccines that increase the breadth of the immune response and reduce the possibility of escape mutants. Finally, even when far from being a real option today by the intricate regulation of immune system, AI could even be used in the future to replace human subjects in the early phases of clinical trials by creating virtual patients.11

These challenges in vaccine development highlight the difficulty of vaccine design and underscore the necessity of identifying optimal products with the highest level of immunoprotection or strain coverage possible. Maximizing vaccine efficacy through design may exceed human cognitive abilities, even among experts. However, generative AI tools could revolutionize the field by automatically exploring the combinatorial complexity of vaccine possibilities to approximate the maximal efficacy and durability possible.12,13 AI may be able to gather and integrate information from multiple sources – such as curated databases, algorithm predictions and literature14–19 – and detect non-trivial connections between data from various stages of the vaccine design process. Thus, AI tools can assist in exploring these vast design spaces to prioritize candidates with higher probability of success, saving time and resources compared to traditional empirical approaches.

As there is potential that AI-driven models advance to surpass current research capabilities in the design of immunoproducts, large biotechnological companies, spin-offs, and public initiatives are expected to adopt these approaches. However, a number of fundamental concerns must be addressed prior to these becoming real-world tools beneficial for the population. Here, we briefly discuss key differences in regulatory and legal aspects between AI-generated vaccines and those classically designed by humans.

Black-box effect, human-interpretable reports and decision rational by experts

The relationship between AI and the scientific method is conceptually conflicting.20 This complicates, if not prevents, the traditional description of the “mode-of-action” of AI-based immunoprophylactic products in formal scientific terms, a required step for patent registration and application. AI results are often difficult to substantiate, i.e. the reasons for selection of each vaccine factor aligned with the body of knowledge and the basis for their combination, as the mathematical lattice behind its computational modeling remains opaque, even to top experts. This generates a “black-box” effect that clashes head-on with the empiricism of the scientific method.

To address this gap in AI-generated vaccine development, AI initiatives should prioritize the generation of human-interpretable reports that explain, as clearly as possible, the calculations and associative rules behind antigen selection, their fragmentation into immunologically meaningful units, e.g. epitopes, and their integration into the final vaccine formulation. These reports should provide complete supporting information of the selected combinations of stages that makes up the definitive vaccine candidate produced by AI, in both biological and statistical terms, and ideally complemented by self-explanatory graphics. Finally, yet importantly, methodological description should be precise enough to permit reproducibility by experienced users, according to scientific publication standards. One step in that direction is the establishment of explanatory AI approaches, which establish causal connections in the feature architecture.21

Keeping track of AI decision-making procedures can be arduous, as these may approach the human capability limits, but would help mitigate the “black-box” concern inherent to automated design of biological products. This transparency may also facilitate the authorization for progression to clinical trials of AI-designed antigenic formulations by regulatory authorities. In addition, it should be noted that vaccines are a unique class of pharmaceutical product, meeting the regulatory definitions of both a drug and a biological compound. Additionally, unlike many other therapeutics, they are administered to extensive numbers of healthy and, in many cases, vulnerable individuals such as children and the elderly. Transparency at all stages from development to marketing is therefore essential. Agencies require comprehensive documentation of algorithmic workflows to ensure that AI-generated formulations pertaining to public health are scientifically validated and aligned with clinical data. In all, regardless of the role played by AI in product design, to our understanding we must continue to evaluate safety and efficacy in controlled, well-conceived clinical trials as a final measure of product viability. An AI-human expert hybrid strategy would bring together the strengths of machine-driven insights and human oversight. This would have a profound impact on social stability across numerous medical fields, given the central role of immunoprophylaxis in maintaining public health and economy.

Intellectual property of AI-generated vaccines

AI’s role in innovation raises complex intellectual property (IP) questions. Traditional IP frameworks assign inventorship to humans, but AI-generated inventions pose unresolved legal challenges pending regulatory and judicial clarity. In vaccine development, this involves experts in immunology, microbiology, medicine and computational biology, yet AI discoveries blur the line between creator and tool. Patentability of AI-generated biomedical inventions remains uncertain and will likely require future court rulings and regulatory guidance to clarify inventorship, ownership, and protection boundaries.

In the healthcare sector, however, private companies may prioritize trade secret protection for AI algorithms and datasets utilized for training and testing. Thus, the public disclosure required by patents is blocked by this defense against potential industrial piracy. However, this strategy raises concerns about transparency of the vaccine design and development, as raised for biomedical products with major public health implications.22 Data protection and privacy are also cornerstones in AI-driven healthcare as training and testing large-scale datasets, often containing sensitive patient information, dictate performance of AI systems. These datasets may include the level of protection obtained (mortality reduction, amelioration of symptoms, no signs of disease …), side effects, and correlations with subpopulation characteristics. In this light, the General Data Protection Regulation in Europe imposes stringent data handling requirements, emphasizing consent, minimization, and anonymization.23 Similarly, China has a framework of standards at various levels covering data compliance, algorithms, cybersecurity, and ethics related to AI.24 This contrasts to the situation in the United States, which has no comprehensive federal rules dedicated to AI and privacy or Japan, which also lacks specific controls of input and output data.

Additionally, global collaborations, particularly during crises such as pandemics, must reconcile with these regulatory constraints with the urgent need for data sharing and immunoproduct innovation. Excessive pressure for intellectual property protection could ultimately hinder the global development of AI-based vaccines, making the balance between innovation and privacy compliance a critical issue.25 Standardized guidelines governing these aspects by drug regulatory agencies under the leadership of the WHO may be useful in addressing these challenges.

Legality for application in humans

After preclinical laboratory stages, the integration of AI into vaccine development, as in other healthcare fields, presents unique legal challenges that must be addressed to ensure its safe, equitable, and ethical application. Legal frameworks are evolving rapidly to accommodate the fast-moving character and complexities of these immunological innovations, which continue to improve over time.

The automated nature of AI models introduces technical constraints that complicates regulatory consistency,26,27 which have been responsibly considered by the principal official organizations. For example, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration has issued guidelines on AI-based medical devices, emphasizing the importance of transparency, reproducibility, and algorithmic accountability of the process followed.28 Similarly, the European Medicines Agency highlights the need for stringent validation processes, ensuring that AI-generated outputs are scientifically sound and clinically relevant.29 Japan has legislation in between the US and EU regulations regarding the use of AI-based medical devices.30 In addition, the Chinese National Medical Products Administration has established specific regulations for AI-based medical applications, underscoring the need for robust validation and risk assessment frameworks.31 Notably, significant legal differences between countries may arise, complicating global AI-driven vaccine deployment. To approach this relative disparity, the International Council for Harmonization of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use (ICH), works to develop global harmonization in pharmaceutical regulatory affairs, including IA-related issues potentially concerning vaccines. This organization currently has 17 governmental members and 23 other observer countries worldwide, as well as several large corporations, ensuring some degree of universality of criteria. However, this consensus should also assume the possibility of AI-modeled vaccines to be iteratively improved through reinforcement learning using new post-market data, which may require the adaptation of the legal framework to a dynamic post-market environment.

Performance and legality are interconnected. Safety breaches or efficacy concerns could lead authorities to reject patents or deny licensing for public use. Two aspects to consider are access inequality and liability for AI-designed vaccines. On the one hand, biases in training datasets could disproportionately compromise the desired vaccine effect in underrepresented populations, exacerbating healthcare disparities and raising legal and ethical challenges.32 On the other hand, AI-designed products may underperform, lead to misdiagnosis (immunoassays) or cause adverse effects such as self-reactivity (vaccines, monoclonal antibodies or passive immune sera). In these cases, it remains unclear whether legal responsibility would lie on AI developers, manufacturers, or healthcare providers. Existing product liability laws should be expanded to include shared accountability models or mandatory AI insurance policies,33 particularly for immunoprophylactic solutions.

Finally, as could not be otherwise, vaccinees have the legal right to be informed before being administered a bioproduct developed using AI models. Consequently, this may lead to refusal to be vaccinated, similar to what was experienced with nucleic acid-based preparations to prevent COVID-19 by Pfizer or Moderna.34 Moreover, this would provide ammunition to conspiracy, science denialisms and anti-vaccine movements who have used the artificial nature of the preparations as one of their main arguments from almost the beginning of vaccination era including the last pandemic. In this light, campaigns to improve public understanding of AI-generated immunological products are almost mandatory. For instance, life-saving impact, high quality design and the fact that they have passed all required safety checks, as much as traditional formulations,35 should be clearly underlined. Legal experts could support communication including appropriate media channels and informed consent processes. Besides, healthcare professionals may be also an important voice to maximize vaccination uptake on undecided populations.

Final conclusions

AI-driven innovations hold promising transformative potential for the development of immunological products. Collaborative efforts between policymakers, developers, and healthcare providers are essential to establish robust legal frameworks that address the challenges associated with AI-driven antigen design, as has been advocated for other AI-based healthcare products.36 Ensuring development transparency, regulatory harmonization, and equitable access will be key to fully realizing the benefits of AI in antigen-based prophylaxis, treatment and diagnosis areas.

Biography

Antonio J. Martín-Galiano is a tenured scientist at the Instituto de Salud Carlos III (Spain) with a PhD in Molecular Microbiology. His broad research interests encompass several topics related to infectious disease control. These include antibiotic resistance, virulome analysis, diagnostics, and, notably, vaccine development. He employs genomic sequences and protein structural data, all within a robust molecular framework. With extensive experience studying Streptococcus pneumoniae, one of the most common community pathogens, he has shifted his focus to priority nosocomial pathogens emphasized by the WHO, such as Acinetobacter baumannii and Klebsiella pneumoniae, as well as pandemic threats like SARS-CoV-2 and certain parasites. He effectively combines genomic and clinical metadata using machine learning techniques and tools developed in Python. Antonio has authored sixty-three research articles and was previously employed by The Spanish National Research Council and the Technical University of Munich. Outside the office, he enjoys writing, exploring social behaviours, listening to electronic music, and practicing meditation.
