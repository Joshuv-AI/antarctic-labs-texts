Reliability of AI-driven cephalometric analysis on CBCT: comparison with manual 2D cephalometry
Authors: Natalia Kazimierczak, Natalia Chwarścianek, Nora Sultani, Joanna Janiszewska-Olszowska, Tomasz Jankowski, Anita Nowicka, Zbigniew Serafin, Wojciech Kazimierczak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArtificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly utilized in orthodontics to automate cephalometric analysis. Despite promising results for AI-based landmark detection on 2D cephalograms, the reliability of AI analyses performed on 3D CBCT data by commercial systems remains unclear. The aim of this study was to evaluate the interchangeability between the results of AI-driven cephalometric analyses using two commercial software platforms (CephX and Invivo) on CBCT datasets and reference 2D cephalometric analysis following manual landmark identification.Materials and methodsThis retrospective study included 135 patients (mean age: 26.8 ± 10.1 years) who underwent concurrent CBCT and lateral cephalometric imaging. Analyses (Steiner and Ricketts methods) based on manual landmark identification served as the reference standard. For each variable, the mean difference (AI – reference) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and Bland–Altman plots were calculated. The intrareader repeatability of the manual reference was assessed with the ICC (3). For clinical interpretability, commonly accepted bands (angles ± 1–2°, linear ± 2 mm) were used.ResultsBoth AI systems showed systematic bias for key skeletal and dental measures. For CephX, SNA differed by + 3.40° (95% CI + 1.66 to + 5.14), SNB by + 5.88° (+ 4.33 to + 7.43), and ANB by − 2.48° (− 4.38 to − 0.58). For Invivo, SNA was + 5.07° (+ 2.40 to + 7.74), the SNB was + 4.40° (+ 2.64 to + 6.16), and the ANB was + 5.55° (+ 0.77 to + 10.33). Many parameters also had wide limits of agreement, indicating poor precision for individual patients. Several variables suggested proportional bias (greater discrepancy at larger values). The intrareader ICCs for the manual reference were 0.91–0.95. Overall, the 95% CIs for sentinel angles commonly exceed ± 2°, which argues against clinical interchangeability.ConclusionsIn their current form, the evaluated AI CBCT cephalometry tools do not match manual 2D cephalometry closely enough for standalone clinical use. Differences of 3–6° in the core sagittal angles are large enough to change the diagnosis and treatment plans. AI outputs should be reviewed and, when necessary, corrected by clinicians.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40510-026-00613-6.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Cephalometric analysis, CBCT, Orthodontic diagnosis, Intraclass correlation coefficient, CephX, Invivo

Background

Since its introduction in the 1930s, when Broadbent and Hofrath developed a standardized method to obtain lateral cephalometric radiographs, cephalometric analysis (CA) has played a pivotal role in orthodontics [1]. It enables a precise assessment of dentofacial morphology and provides a reliable diagnosis of malocclusion and its anatomical basis, thereby ensuring accurate diagnostics and treatment planning. Furthermore, CA allows for the morphometric evaluation of craniofacial growth, facilitates treatment monitoring, and supports the assessment of treatment outcomes [2]. CA is also a valuable tool in the field of orthognathic surgery, and, similar to its use in orthodontics, it enables precise treatment planning and evaluation of results [3].

Traditionally, CA is based on two-dimensional (2D) lateral cephalograms of the craniofacial region to obtain precise linear and angular measurements between bony and soft-tissue landmarks. The main disadvantages of manual cephalometric analysis include strong operator dependence, significant variability in identifying cephalometric points, and a labor-intensive process [4, 5]. Recently, AI has gained considerable attention in the medical sciences, especially in imaging diagnostics. Since orthodontic treatment planning requires radiological examinations, automatic cephalometric analysis is one of the most rapidly developing fields of AI application [6, 7]. Compared with manual analysis, AI-based automatic cephalometric analysis is operator independent and shows considerably less variability in the identification of cephalometric points [8, 9].

The first studies on the use of CT and CBCT in cephalometric analysis were published in the early 2000s. However, owing to their inefficiency and time-consuming procedures, these methods have not gained widespread popularity [10]. Recent advances in AI, including automated three-dimensional assessments of the skull, have revived interest in tomography-based cephalometric analysis. Multiple studies using AI for the automatic identification and analysis of cephalometric landamarks have demonstrated significant accuracy and efficiency compared with traditional analyses [11–18]. The authors also demonstrated the time efficiency of AI-based cephalometry: both automated 2D and 3D landmarking processes proved to be highly efficient, requiring less than one minute each [19]. However, conflicting results have also been published, showing that AI-driven 3D analysis cannot entirely replace manual measurements, emphasizing the necessity of human oversight to increase accuracy [20].

An exponential increase in the number of companies developing AI software for healthcare has been observed, reflecting a growing commercial outlook [21]. AI tools are now available not only to researchers but also as commercial online products. In orthodontics, systems such as Invivo7 Anatomage (Version 7.2.3, Osteoid, Inc., Santa Clara, USA) and CephX (ORCA Dental AI, Las Vegas, NV) have been introduced to provide CBCT-based cephalometric analyses. Low-dose CBCT-based cephalometric analysis could offer clinical benefits by providing detailed and varied radiological images from a single CBCT scan. Despite their growing commercial availability, these AI tools are typically proprietary and lack transparent methodological descriptions. Independent validation studies are essential to determine their actual clinical value and reliability.

The present study aimed to compare the agreement of the results of cephalometric analyses conducted automatically by AI software on CBCT images with a reference standard, defined as the results of standard cephalometric analyses performed on lateral cephalograms by an experienced reader. Two datasets—cephalograms and CBCT images—were derived from one patient cohort.

Materials and methods

The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Ethics Committee of anonymized for peer review (protocol No. 273/2025, Approval Date: 23.04.2025 for research involving human participants). Informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study.

Patients

The population of this retrospective diagnostic accuracy study initially consisted of 147 consecutive patients referred for both lateral cephalogram and cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) imaging at a private dental centerby orthodontists and dental surgeons between January 2023 and March 2025. The primary indications for both imaging modalities were preorthognathic surgery evaluation or complex orthodontic diagnosis. Both CBCTs and lateral cephalograms for each patient were obtained concurrently or within 1 week to ensure anatomical consistency. CBCT scans were taken with a large field of view (FOV) (18 × 13 cm). All images were anonymized by removing patient identifiers from DICOM files and cephalograms prior to analysis. The images were coded, and the evaluators were blinded to patient details to prevent bias.

The main exclusion criteria were as follows: scans not fully covering all relevant anatomical structures for cephalometric analysis, scans with severe motion artifacts or poor overall image quality affecting landmark identification, and patients with craniofacial syndromes or previous maxillofacial surgery and cephalograms with significant double borders of the mandible.

After the initial selection, 5 scans were excluded because of severe motion artifacts, and 7 patients were excluded because of insufficient scan range. The authors reviewed both datasets (CEPH and CBCT) of the remaining patients. Finally, 135 patients were included in the study group.

Image acquisition

All the CBCT and CEPH images were acquired via a Hyperion X9 PRO 13 × 16 (MyRay, Imola, Italy) machine. Standardized settings, marked as the “Regular” setting of the apparatus, were used for both modalities. For CBCT, the acquisition parameters were as follows: 90 kV, 36 mAs; CTDI/Vol, 4.09 mGy; and 13 cm FOV. CBCT images were reconstructed with a slice thickness of 0.3 mm. For lateral cephalograms, standardized patient positioning via cephalostat ear rods and the Frankfort horizontal plane orientation parallel to the floor was utilized. A fixed source-to-object and object-to-detector distance was maintained, and a calibration ruler was included in the images. Patient identifiers were removed from all images (both CBCT DICOM files and CEPH image files) to maintain anonymity, and images were coded for blinded analysis by the examiners.

In contrast, Invivo claims to perform a direct three-dimensional (3D) cephalometric analysis. Its AI engine is designed to identify landmarks in three-dimensional (X, Y, Z) space directly from the CBCT volume. However, upon closer examination, the analysis process revealed that the software automatically determines the mid-sagittal plane and projects all landmarks (for example, points A, N, B, and S), which are intended to lie within the mid-sagittal plane, onto this arbitrarily defined plane. As a result, it does not register the actual three-dimensional coordinates of these points. Consequently, the analyses we conducted were effectively two-dimensional and were performed within the arbitrary space established by the software as the mid-sagittal plane (Fig. 2).

The software subsequently automatically generated a list of available cephalometric analysis reports, including the identification of predefined landmarks and the calculation of angular and linear measurements pertinent to the Steiner and Ricketts analyses. Both analyses were selected for their clinical relevance and widespread adoption. The results of the cephalometric analyses were then exported from each program.

Ground truth 2D cephalometric analysis

The corresponding digital lateral cephalogram for each patient served as the reference standard (ground truth). All lateral cephalograms were semimanually traced and analyzed using a software for 2D cephalometric analysis Ortobajt (ver. 9.2.5, Ortobajt, Wrocław, Poland).

The analysis was performed by a single orthodontist with 12 years of experience in cephalometric analysis. To assess intraobserver reliability, 20% of the cephalograms were randomly selected and retraced by the same examiner after a 4-week interval.

For each image, calibration was performed via the software's tools on the basis of the scale marker. The predefined anatomical landmarks necessary for the Steiner and Ricketts analyses were then manually identified and digitized by the examiner (presented in Table 1) [22].

Characterization of the measurements performed in the study

Sample size and precision

This retrospective study included 135 consecutive patients. Because our objective was estimation of the magnitude of disagreement (bias) between the AI and the manual reference—not hypothesis testing—we based adequacy on the precision of the estimated mean differences.

For each variable, the 95% CI half-width around the mean difference is:

With n = 135 (√n ≈ 11.62), the study attains a half-width of approximately 2.0° whenever the SD ≤ 11.9°. In our data, key angular variables met this criterion (e.g., SNA SD ≈ 10.35°, yielding a half-width ≈ 1.74°), providing clinically interpretable precision relative to commonly cited bands of ± 1–2°. For linear variables, a half-width of ≤ 2 mm is achieved whenever the SD is ≤ 11.9 mm, which is typically satisfactory in cephalometric practice.

Statistical analysis

The primary objective was to evaluate the agreement between the cephalometric measurements derived from two AI-driven methods (CephX and Invivo) and those obtained via the manual reference standard. Agreement was assessed via Bland–Altman analysis [23]. Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum) were calculated for each cephalometric measurement obtained from the ground truth (cephalometric analyses conducted via an experienced reader) and the two AI methods (CephX, Invivo7). For each cephalometric parameter, the difference between the AI measurement and the reference measurement was calculated for every patient. From this set of differences, three key metrics were determined:

Mean difference (bias): The average of the differences, representing the systematic bias of the AI method relative to the reference. A positive value indicates average overestimation by the AI platform, whereas a negative value indicates average underestimation.

Standard Deviation (SD) of the Differences: A measure of random error or imprecision. A larger SD indicates greater variability and less consistency between the two methods across the patient sample.

Standard Deviation (SD) of the Differences: A measure of random error or imprecision. A larger SD indicates greater variability and less consistency between the two methods across the patient sample.

95% limits of agreement (LoA): Calculated as the mean difference ± 1.96 × SD of the differences. This interval defines the range where approximately 95% of differences between the two methods fall, assuming approximately normally distributed differences. Wider limits indicate poorer agreement and less reliability for individual patient assessment.

95% limits of agreement (LoA): Calculated as the mean difference ± 1.96 × SD of the differences. This interval defines the range where approximately 95% of differences between the two methods fall, assuming approximately normally distributed differences. Wider limits indicate poorer agreement and less reliability for individual patient assessment.

To assess the statistical significance of the systematic bias, the 95% confidence interval (CI) for the mean difference was calculated. If this CI did not include the value of zero, the bias was considered statistically significant at the level.

To evaluate the clinical relevance of the agreement, an a priori clinical equivalence margin was established as proposed in a recent paper by Pandis & Burzykowski [24]. Based on established literature and clinical consensus in orthodontics, a difference of up to ± 2° for angular measurements and ± 2 mm for linear measurements was defined as the maximum clinically acceptable discrepancy [25–27]. The 95% CI for the mean difference was compared against this equivalence margin. If the CI was fully contained within the margin (e.g., within − 2° to + 2°), the systematic bias was considered clinically negligible, and the methods could be deemed equivalent in terms of average accuracy. If the CI extends beyond or is located entirely outside the margin, clinical equivalence cannot be reached. Because multiple parameters were evaluated, we additionally report Bonferroni-adjusted confidence intervals for the mean differences (≈99.6%) as a conservative sensitivity analysis.

Finally, to investigate for proportional bias (i.e., a relationship between the magnitude of the measurement and the difference between methods), Bland‒Altman plots were generated by plotting the difference against the mean of the two measurements. To evaluate proportional bias (i.e., whether disagreement varies with measurement magnitude), we assessed the relationship between the differences and the averages in Bland–Altman plots. When a clear linear trend was present, we considered constant (horizontal) limits of agreement potentially misleading. Because differences in variability between methods may contribute to apparent trends, proportional-bias patterns were interpreted cautiously [28].

The concordance of the quantitative measures between the raters was assessed with an intraclass correlation coefficient of type 3 (according to Shrout and Fleiss) [29]). All the analyses were conducted in R software, version 4.5.1 (R Core Team); BA summaries with base stats; ICC with psych.

Using nominal 95% confidence intervals, mean bias was observed for many parameters (Table 5). However, because multiple parameters were evaluated, we additionally report Bonferroni-adjusted confidence intervals (approximately 99.6%) for the mean differences to support more conservative inference (Table 5). Under this adjustment, some parameters no longer show mean bias distinguishable from zero (e.g., ANB and Convexity for CephX), whereas clinically central measures such as SNA and SNB remain clearly biased, with mean overestimation of + 3.40° for SNA and + 5.88° for SNB. Regardless of statistical inference on the mean, clinical equivalence was not achieved for most key angular parameters because the confidence intervals extend beyond the pre-specified ± 2° margin.

For the critical SNA angle, which defines the anteroposterior position of the maxilla, CephX overestimated the measurement by an average of 3.40° (95% CI: 1.66° to 5.14°). Similarly, the SNB angle was overestimated by a mean of 5.88° (95% CI: 4.33° to 7.43°), and the ANB angle, which describes the jaw relationship, was underestimated by 2.48° (95% CI: − 4.38° to − 0.58°).

When assessed for clinical relevance against the predefined ± 2°/ ± 2 mm equivalence margins, the magnitude and variability of disagreement indicated that most parameters did not meet the criteria for interchangeability at the individual-patient level. While some parameters exhibited mean bias, the primary concern was the breadth of the limits of agreement, which frequently extended beyond the clinical acceptability thresholds, highlighting limited clinical reliability of the AI outputs when compared with manual tracing.

Furthermore, the precision of the CephX measurements was poor, as indicated by the exceptionally wide 95% LoA. For the SNA, the LoA was (− 16.89°, 23.69°), meaning that for an individual patient, the AI measurement could be nearly 17° lower or almost 24° greater than the manual measurement. This wide range of potential errors is clinically untenable for diagnostic or treatment planning purposes. Similar clinically unacceptable LoAs were observed for all other parameters, such as SNB (− 12.11°, 23.87°) and ANB (− 24.41°, 19.45°).

Visual inspection of the Bland–Altman plots (notably Panels 4A, 4B, and 4E) indicated a clear linear trend between differences and averages, consistent with proportional bias (i.e., magnitude-dependent disagreement). In such situations, constant (horizontal) limits of agreement may be misleading and should not be over-interpreted. Therefore, we complemented the standard Bland–Altman summaries with a regression-based Bland–Altman approach as described by Bland and Altman [23], which estimates the expected bias as a function of measurement magnitude and derives conditional limits using the residual variability.

To facilitate interpretation where proportional bias was apparent, we additionally provide regression-based Bland–Altman plots (difference-on-mean regression with regression-based limits of agreement) in the Supplementary Material (Figures S1–S3).

Like CephX, Invivo showed systematic differences versus the manual reference across multiple parameters. For example, SNA was overestimated by 5.07° (95% CI: 2.40° to 7.74°), and SNB was overestimated by 4.40° (95% CI: 2.64° to 6.16°). A particularly large and clinically alarming bias was observed for the facial axis, which was underestimated by a mean of 33.05° (95% CI: − 35.26° to − 30.84°).

Clinical equivalence was not achieved for most parameters. However, for Convexity and Facial Depth, the 95% CI for the mean difference did overlap with or was close to the ± 2 equivalence margin, suggesting that the systematic bias for these specific parameters may be less severe. Nonetheless, the wide LoA for these parameters, such as (− 7.91, 8.99) for convexity, still indicate poor individual precision, precluding their clinical interchangeability. For all other key diagnostic parameters, such as SNA, SNB, and ANB, the 95% CIs were well outside the equivalence margin.

The precision of the Invivo platform was also found to be inadequate for clinical use. The 95% LoA for SNA was (− 17.18°, 27.32°), and for ANB, it was exceptionally wide (− 34.39°, 45.49°). This level of random error makes the tool unreliable for individual patient diagnosis.

Intrareader reliability

The consistency of the manual ground truth measurements was confirmed through an intrareader reliability analysis. A randomly selected subset of 27 cases (20% of the total sample) was retraced and analyzed by the same experienced orthodontist after a 4-week interval. As shown in Table 7, intrareader reliability was consistently high across all parameters, with ICC values ranging from 0.91 to 0.95, indicating excellent repeatability of repeated measurements within the same reader. The descriptive agreement analysis demonstrated minimal systematic intrareader bias: mean differences were minimal across variables, and all 95% CIs for the mean differences were centered around zero. The LoA were narrow relative to the magnitude of the measurements and remained within the pre-specified clinically acceptable discrepancy thresholds (± 2° for angular measurements and ± 2 mm for linear measurements) for all analyzed parameters.

Discussion

The integration of artificial intelligence into dental diagnostics holds the promise of revolutionizing clinical workflows by enhancing efficiency and standardization. This study evaluated the agreement of CBCT based AI-driven cephalometric analysis of two commercial platforms against a manual reference standard performed by an experienced orthodontist. Our primary finding is that neither AI platform demonstrated sufficient agreement to be considered interchangeable with manual analysis for clinical orthodontic and orthognathic surgical planning. The analysis revealed two distinct and critical forms of error: statistically significant and clinically unacceptable systematic bias for most parameters and poor precision, as evidenced by an extremely wide 95% LoA.

The distinction between statistical significance and clinical relevance is paramount in method-comparison studies. Because multiple cephalometric parameters were analyzed, nominal 95% confidence intervals can overstate evidence for systematic mean bias when interpreted jointly. To address this, we provide Bonferroni-adjusted confidence intervals (approximately 99.6%) for the mean differences (Tables 5 and 6; Supplementary Tables S1–S2) while maintaining the prespecified clinical acceptability margins (± 2° for angular and ± 2 mm for linear measures) as the primary interpretive framework. Under this conservative adjustment, some parameters are no longer distinguishable from zero in terms of mean bias; nevertheless, clinically central sagittal measures (e.g., SNA and SNB) remain markedly biased, and limits of agreement are wide for many parameters, indicating poor precision at the individual-patient level. Consequently, irrespective of hypothesis testing, the observed magnitude of disagreement frequently exceeds the clinical thresholds that can affect treatment planning decisions.

Generally, the accepted error in landmark position between manual and automatic identification is interpreted as follows: distances less than 2 mm are considered correct, and those less than 4 mm are deemed acceptable; the differences observed in the boxplots of average measurement results are unequivocally unacceptable [30]. This indicates a lack of reliability that would be unacceptable in a clinical setting where precise measurements are paramount for diagnosis and treatment planning in orthodontics and orthognathic surgery. Although we have not directly evaluated landmark accuracy by measuring mean positioning errors, the ranges presented in Fig. 5 (box plots) indicate substantial discrepancies between the reference and AI analyses. Even most basic, key sagittal measurements, such as the SNA, SNB, and ANB angles, which define the sagittal relationship of the maxilla and mandible, all demonstrated poor agreement for both software packages. Moreover, both platforms occasionally produced implausible segmentations/contours extending beyond the CBCT field of view, which could lead to analyses partly driven by extrapolated rather than imaged anatomy (Fig. 1).

These results stand in contrast to some of the more optimistic reports in the literature, many of which have focused on the accuracy of AI in identifying specific landmarks on 2D cephalograms rather than evaluating the final calculated measurements from a 3D source [8, 9, 25–29].While studies have shown that AI can achieve landmark detection accuracy comparable to or even exceeding that of human examiners [17, 18], our study highlights that this does not automatically translate into reliable full-analysis packages, especially when dealing with the added complexity of 3D data. Our findings are more aligned with studies that urge caution, such as Bao et al. [20], who concluded that automated analysis was not yet accurate enough to completely replace human analysis, and Meric and Naoumova [31], who reported significant variability in the results from CephX on 2D radiographs, necessitating manual correction. A 2023 study by Serafin et al. [32] reported a mean difference of 2.44 mm between automated and manually performed digital identification of cephalometric landmarks in three dimensions. A meta-regression analysis revealed a significant relationship between publication year and mean tracing error. The authors concluded that recent advances in AI algorithms have led to substantial improvements in cephalometric landmark identification, demonstrating excellent accuracy. However, they assessed only the reliability of 2D cephalometry.

A central challenge in this evaluation is the comparison between a 3D imaging modality (CBCT) and a 2D modality (lateral cephalogram). Traditional 2D cephalometry is inherently limited by magnification, distortion, and superimposition of bilateral structures [33–35]. CBCT overcomes these limitations, but this also means that a direct one-to-one comparison of measurements is complex. Landmarks that are points in 2D are often represented as bilateral structures in 3D, requiring a clear and consistent method for deriving a single point for analysis. It is often unclear how commercial AI software performs this conversion. The manufacturers, particularly for the cloud-based CephX platform, provide little to no information on their methodology, whether they are performing a true 3D analysis or generating a synthetic 2D projection (e.g., RayCast or MIP) on which the analysis is then performed [33, 36]. This “black box” nature of the algorithms is a significant barrier to clinical trust and validation. The large standard deviations and clinically implausible mean values observed for some variables (e.g., a mean ANB of 0.26° ± 11.53° for CephX) suggest that the automated process is prone to gross errors that a human observer would immediately identify and correct.

This study has several limitations. First, the reference standard was analyzed on the basis of manual tracing of a 2D lateral cephalogram. While this represents the current clinical standard, it is itself subject to inherent limitations. A “true” gold standard might involve manual landmark identification on 3D CBCT datasets by multiple expert examiners, although this is a time-consuming process, and 2D analysis remains a reference standard. Second, the study is retrospective and is based on data from a single center using one type of CBCT machine, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Finally, AI software is in a constant state of development. Our results are a snapshot of the versions available during the study period, and future updates may improve performance, as shown in the abovementioned meta-regression [32]. The lack of versioning for the CephX platform makes tracking these changes over time particularly difficult.

While current findings indicate significant shortcomings of commercial AI cephalometric analysis of CBCT data, this technology still holds promise if it is rigorously improved and transparently validated. Until substantial reliability is demonstrated, clinicians should use AI-generated cephalometric results with caution, always verifying critical diagnostic measurements manually to safeguard patient outcomes.

Conclusions

In conclusion, while AI-driven automated cephalometric analysis of CBCT data is a field with immense potential, the commercial software packages evaluated in this study are not yet reliable enough for standalone clinical use. The observed systematic biases and, most critically, the poor precision (wide limits of agreement) present an unacceptable risk of diagnostic error and flawed treatment planning. Current 3D-based, AI, commercially available software cannot replace traditional cephalometric evaluation. These tools may serve as a preliminary step, but they cannot replace the critical evaluation and judgment of an experienced clinician. AI human oversight remains indispensable to ensure patient safety and optimal treatment outcomes.
