8,000 years of wild and domestic animal body size data reveal long-term synchrony and recent divergence due to intensified human impact
Authors: Cyprien Mureau, Léa d’Oliveira, Odile Peyron, Emilie Blaise, Audrey Renaud, Ana Balcarcel, Marine Jeanjean, Vincent Bonhomme, Laurent Bouby, Jérôme Ros, Allowen Evin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThis study explores how human and environmental influences shaped animal morphology over the last 8,000 y. By analyzing body size evolution in wild and domestic animals from the Northwestern Mediterranean, it reveals long-term synchrony in their trajectories until the Middle Ages, suggesting shared ecological constraints. In the last millennium, these trajectories diverged as human influence intensified, promoting unprecedented increases in domestic species body size, while wild species exhibited a trend toward decreased size. These findings underscore the long-term, dynamic interdependence between environmental change, human agency, and animal morphology, highlighting the deep and lasting prevalence of environmental influences on all species and, in the last millennium, the increasing impact of human activities.

Understanding the long-term interplay between human societies, environmental changes, and animal morphology is a fundamental question in evolutionary biology, bioarchaeology, and palaeoenvironmental sciences. Among the morphological traits preserved in the archaeological record, animal body size provides key insights into evolutionary trajectories and species responses to environmental and anthropogenic pressures. Body size is shaped by both intrinsic biological factors (e.g., genetics, metabolism) and extrinsic environmental pressures (e.g., climate, vegetation composition, interspecific competition, human selection), that change over time.

Life history theory posits that the size of organisms is a trade-off between growth, reproduction, maintenance, and immune defense (1) within the constraints of their ecological niche (2, 3). For example, stress during early development, such as poor nutrition, can have lasting effects on phenotypic traits including body size (4). In addition to resource availability (5), other key environmental drivers of body size variation include temperature (6, 7), precipitation (8), resource availability, predation (9), and disease (3).

Furthermore, artificial selection for larger or smaller animals has been widely documented in domestic species (10, 11), while human activities such as hunting and habitat fragmentation have been documented to influence body size evolution in wild fauna (12, 13). While body size change through time is a common trait of all organisms, disentangling the relative influence of environmental and anthropic influences remains challenging. These evolutionary drivers may act in combination or opposition, and their effects can vary in magnitude and direction across species and populations. Body size reduction has often been cited as a defining trait of the early domestication process (14). However, recent studies highlight that body size variation during domestication is neither uniform or unidirectional (15), highlighting the need for broader ecological and historical frameworks that integrate both wild and domestic taxa.

Bioarchaeological remains are unique diachronic evidence of past animal phenotypes, enabling the disentanglement of environmental and human factors that have shaped morphology throughout the Holocene. By tracing patterns of body size variation through time from a series of wild and domestic species, this approach provides key insights into artificial selection dynamics, environmental adaptation, and broader human–animal–environment interactions shedding light on the coevolutionary history of human societies and the animals upon which they relied. In bioarchaeology, the establishment of standardized biometric methodological protocols (16) has enabled archaeozoologists to compile large biometric datasets. These data provide essential biological proxies for reconstructing past animal populations and their body size evolution through time, providing valuable insights into the evolutionary mechanisms at play. However, studies spanning several millennia of morphological evolution are scarce, especially for wild species, and the integration of biometric approaches with palaeoclimate and palaeoenvironmental data remains in its infancy.

In the Western Mediterranean, bioarchaeological remains are available for domestic and wild taxa since the Neolithic transition, approximately ~8,000 y ago, when farming outposts were established by Impressed Ware pioneer groups from the Italo-Adriatic region. (17). These events marked a shift from a hunter-gatherer foraging model to a subsistence economy that was based primarily on agropastoral production (18) of four mammalian livestock species: sheep (Ovis aries), goat (Capra hircus), taurine cattle (Bos taurus), and pig (Sus domesticus)- taxa that had been domesticated ~2,000 y earlier in Southwestern Asia. These species remained integral to the human meat diet until the present day (19), alongside chicken (Gallus gallus), which were introduced into the Western Mediterranean around the 8th c. BC (20). Together with these key domestic taxa that were essential to human subsistence, four wild species, red deer (Cervus elaphus), brown hare (Lepus europaeus), European rabbit (Oryctolagus cuniculus), and red fox (Vulpes vulpes), are frequently recorded in archaeozoological assemblages from the region, although they are generally less abundant in post-Mesolithic contexts and have received comparatively little attention (19, 21). The rabbit (Oryctolagus cuniculus) represents a particular case due to its relatively late domestication, considered to have occurred through a gradual process beginning in Antiquity, to the start of the modern era (22).

The collection of a comprehensive archaeological, archaeozoological, and palaeoenvironmental dataset enables broader and more detailed investigations of long-term phenotypic change. Here, we therefore analyze these data in combination with environmental, climatic, and anthropic markers in order to interpret trends in body size evolution.

The climatic changes in the Mediterranean basin over the last 8 millennia have been reconstructed by several studies, revealing strong regional patterns, although these vary depending on the methodology or the proxies used (23). Most regional reconstructions of temperature and precipitation patterns through time have relied on pollen data (SI Appendix, Table S2), in addition to other proxies as speleothems (24). These syntheses have evidenced that, between the 42nd and 45th parallels, Western Europe was marked by specific climatic events including the cold “4.2 ka” and “Little Ice Age” (LIA) events (25).

The human impact on animal body size throughout the archaeozoological record can be assessed through three main parameters: 1) human demographic dynamics reflecting density of human settlement, 2) the evolution of regional flora from which human impact on vegetation can be inferred, and 3) changes in livestock composition impacting the way humans use both the animals and the landscape. Over the past three decades, Mediterranean France has yielded a large number of well-preserved and extensively excavated archaeological sites as a result of large-scale rescue and research operations, making it an exceptional region for documenting the long-term evolution of human demography (26). The estimation of forest opening by agropastoral societies, based on pollen records (SI Appendix, Table S3), also makes it possible to track how animals evolved in relation to environmental changes. For example, more open landscapes have been linked to less robust skeletons (27) and smaller body size (28) in large mammals. Pollen data can also be used to identify the human activities that led to regional landscape changes, particularly the pastoral pollen indicators (29) for husbandry practices, cereal pollen for agriculture, and pollen of Olea, Juglans, and Castanea (30) for arboriculture. In addition, changes in livestock composition can be quantified through the use of relative proportions of Number of Identified Specimens (i.e., NISP) in archaeozoological assemblages (SI Appendix, Table S1 and references there in) which provide unique insights into past animal husbandry. Remains from these archaeozoological assemblages have also been biometrically measured, providing a large morphometric dataset which we use here to track variation in body size through time.

This study leverages an extensive biometric dataset from Mediterranean France to investigate diachronic trends in skeletal size across five key domestic (sheep, goat, cattle, pig, chicken) and four wild species (wild rabbit, red deer, red fox, and brown hare) over the last 8 millennia. To provide a regional synthesis relevant to south-western Europe, we address the following questions:1.What is the pattern of body size evolution in wild and domestic animals over the last 8,000 y?2.Is the evolutionary trajectory between wild and domestic species similar, indicating common influences from human activities and/or environmental factors?3.What is the relative impact of human activities, climate change, and environmental conditions on the morphological variation of wild and domestic animals?

Similar Body Size Trends in Domestic and Wild Animals From the Neolithic to the Middle Ages

A total of 225,780 bones from 311 archaeological sites (SI Appendix, Table S1) and 81,211 linear measurements were analyzed. To compare body size across time and taxa, measurements were standardized using the Log Size Index [LSI, (31, 32)], a standard method in archaeozoology that expresses each value as the logarithmic ratio of a given measurement to a reference. The references used here correspond to the mean of each variable per each species of the entire dataset (SI Appendix, Table S4). This approach allows the detection of changes in body size (SI Appendix, Fig. S3), and body proportions (SI Appendix, Fig. S4), as well as population-level trends independent of absolute bone size differences (33).

To detect body size variation through time, regression analysis of LSI values were performed. They revealed nonlinear changes over time for all studied species (Fig. 1A), suggesting that environmental and anthropogenic pressures fluctuated over time within dynamic socioecological systems. Chronologically constrained hierarchical clustering identified four main phases of body size variation (Fig. 1A), which did not strictly align with the main changes in regional human demography (Fig. 2 B–D), agropastoral (Fig. 2 E and F), vegetation (Fig. 2 G and H), or climatic periods (Fig. 2 I–O). For each phase, the covariation across species in body size was assessed (Fig. 1B), as well as the correlation between species-specific body size patterns and archaeological, palaeoenvironmental, and palaeoclimatological proxies (Fig. 3) using Pearson correlation coefficients. This approach revealed a pattern of shared size trends among species, as well as some differences, especially in the timing of the shifts. However, LSI scaling further showed that the magnitude of these changes was substantially greater in domestic taxa, highlighting differential intensities of size evolution between wild and domestic populations (Fig. 1A). These long-term patterns must be interpreted in light of the specific regional palaeoenvironmental and archaeological contexts in order to build a large-scale understanding of the main patterns of human–animal–environment interactions.

Phase 1 (6,000 to 2,000 BC): A Common Neolithic Decline in Body Size for Domestic and Wild Species

During the Neolithic Period, the body size of all species, wild and domestic, was highly correlated and consistently declined through time (Fig. 1), suggesting a common response to anthropic and environmental influences.

This finding mirrors previous records across central (42, 43) and southern Europe (44, 45). In other European regions, such body size variations have been linked to shifts in species abundance, reflecting changes in pastoral practices (46, 47). Conversely, in Mediterranean France (Fig. 3), while the body size consistently decreased for all domestic animals during this period (Fig. 1A), the proportion of cattle remains increased during Phase 1 before decreasing in Phase 2 (Fig. 2A). In contrast, caprine remains exhibited the opposite trend. A parallel size reduction was observed in wild fauna, though less intense, suggesting that this phenomenon is not solely a result of domestic species adapting to the European environment (43).

Indeed, our results indicate a steady decline in body size for wild animals throughout the Neolithic period (Fig. 1A). Unlike domestic species, wild species were naturally present in the region before the Neolithic, and the observed trend may have begun earlier, at a time yet to be determined. A gradual decrease in the size of rabbits and red deer in Portugal, foxes in Israel and aurochs in Denmark, between 18,000 and 5,000 BC, has been considered likely due to rising mean temperatures during the Pleistocene–Holocene transition (48). In Mediterranean France, postglacial temperature increase concluded around 5,000 BC (26), leading to cooler summer (Fig. 2M) and drier conditions (Fig. 2 I–K) (49, 50). While temperature change cannot explain (Fig. 3) the linear decrease in body size for domestic species observed until the end of the 3rd millennium BC (Fig. 1A), body size for all species was positively correlated with mean annual precipitation (Fig. 3). This correlation with reduced precipitation may reflect changes in vegetation that impacted habitat and resource availability for both wild herds and domestic flocks.

Stability in arboreal pollen does not indicate significant deforestation; however, one of the most notable changes in the regional landscape is the transition from open coniferous forests during the Mesolithic to mesophilous forests and later dense coppices in the Neolithic (51), as evidenced by the observed increase in Quercus pollen (Fig. 1G). Additionally, the decreasing representation of carnivorans, both in terms of individuals numbers and species diversity, in regions of hominin-carnivore coexistence since the Late Pleistocene suggests that humans progressively outcompeted large carnivorans through increased hunting pressure (52, 53). This dynamic may have contributed to the reduction in the body size of wild species (54, 55).

Phase 2 (2,000 BC to 300 AD): The Impact of Major Climatic and Societal Transformations Upon Body Size

During Phase 2, spanning from the Bronze Age to the Roman Principate (Fig. 2D), the body size of species appeared partially decorrelated (Fig. 1B). The pattern of body size evolution is broadly similar across species, but with differences in timing and magnitude (Fig. 1A).

The reversal in the size trends at the beginning of Phase 2, that span from 2,000BC to 300AD coincides with a well-known climatic shift in the Mediterranean region during the “4.2 ka BP” event (Fig. 2O), characterized by a strong decrease in precipitation (56, 57). The temperature (Fig. 2 M and N) and precipitation (Fig. 2 J–L) proxies testify to climate changes but are poorly correlated with animal sizes (Fig. 3).

Domestic species sizes are positively correlated with the agro-pastoral and human demographic indicators at the expanse of the forest cover (Fig. 3). Indeed, after a phase of land clearance without deforestation during Phase 1 (58), increased agropastoral pressure (Fig. 2F) is associated with forest opening and fire activity (49, 59) during the second half of the 3rd millennium BC. These new management methods allowed agropastoralists to endure the severe aridity of this period (Fig. 2 I–K), potentially leading to the breakdown of previous socioenvironmental systems (Fig. 2B). Nevertheless, this trend seems to reflect a major reorganization of regional human settlements, particularly through population movement toward mountainous hinterlands, rather than a global collapse in human population (59). Throughout the last millennium BC, the increasing interaction of the human population of Southern France with other communities in the Mediterranean (60) led to an unprecedented exploitation of land (Fig. 2 E and F) thanks to a trading economy (61, 62) that implied a remarkable demographic growth (Fig. 2B).

It seems possible that the landscape opening resulted in larger plains and grasslands, also providing more suitable conditions for the red fox, which is reflected in its size evolution (63, 64). Moreover, the body size of present-day Iberian red foxes correlates with their proximity to agricultural areas and increased predation on domestic stock and carrion (65, 66). Both the forest opening and the expansion of agropastoral areas could have had a significant impact on this carnivorous species, without a noticeable effect on the wild herbivores included in this study (Fig. 3). Human societies living in diverse ecological contexts have deliberately changed their environments to meet their subsistence and cultural needs, actively transforming natural landscapes and managing wild plants and animals (67).

A change in domestic populations through the introduction of new lineages presents a compelling hypothesis to explain the observed increase in body size, that also reflects in changes in allometric relationship among skeletal elements for sheep and goat (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Some authors have linked the increase in caprines size to the introduction of new sheep and goat lineages in Europe (68, 69), during the second half of the 3rd millennium. Nonetheless, these morphological and genetic changes coincide with the rapid migration of Yamnaya-type “steppe” human populations (70, 71), whose arrival likely triggered other significant transformations, such as immunity challenges through the introduction of new infections, as well as new agropastoral techniques that were particularly advantageous for sheep and goats. These hypotheses warrant further archaeological and anthropological investigations. However, the differentiated response of domestic animals, particularly sheep and goats, suggests an effect of targeted human selection potentially paired with transregional gene flow, revealing how human agency began to decouple domestic animal evolution from purely ecological constraints.

Phase 3 (300 to 1,000 AD): A Global Decline in Animal Size Coinciding With Major Changes in Human Settlement

The third phase covers the chrono-cultural periods of Late Antiquity and the beginning of the Middle Ages. During this phase, body size evolution for all species was correlated, except for the red deer (Fig. 1B), suggesting again the impact of similar mechanisms on wild and domestic populations. Indeed, after reaching a maximum during the 1st and 2nd c. AD, body size for all species declined from the 3rd–5th c. AD to the end of the 1st millennium AD (Fig. 1 A and B). During this phase, anthropogenic and environmental factors were similarly correlated with body size in both wild and domestic animals, but the specific patterns of environmental and anthropogenic correlations (Fig. 3) differ from those observed in the Neolithic, indicating that different factors were influential in each period.

This new size trajectory occurred during an exceptional socioeconomic phase in the regional history. The human settlement inherited from the Roman period underwent profound upheavals, with a gradual decline in the number of archaeological sites from the 2nd c. AD onward (Fig. 2B). As the network of Roman cities declined, cereal farming devoted to urban supply shrunk in favor of smaller farms producing for their own needs. This shift resulted in fewer cereals among lowlands pollen records (Fig. 2E) and an increase in agropastoral indicators in the hinterland (Fig. 2F). This new land management system favored a wide variety of vegetal taxa cropped in polycultural systems (72), sheep and goat farming (Fig. 2A), but certainly no longer large animals (73).

Therefore, the observed common decrease in size, especially for wild species, cannot be attributed solely to the decline and fall of the Western Roman Empire. Late Antiquity (Fig. 2D) also corresponds to an “erosive crisis” (Fig. 2F) caused by high annual rainfall (Fig. 2 I–L), an unprecedented clearing of the landscape (Fig. 2 G and H) and the abandonment of Roman agricultural infrastructures that ensured land drainage (58). These abrupt changes are likely to have weakened the habitats of wild species, particularly in the highland hinterland, where both agricultural and pastoral indicators (Fig. 2F) reflect a new and continuing pressure from human settlements (74, 75). The confinement of wild mammal herds to more restricted areas could explain the reduction in their body size (76, 77).

Climate and disease proliferation are now widely recognized as key factors in the collapse of the Western Roman Empire (78). The climatic downturn associated with the Dark Age Cold Period, marked by cooler and wetter conditions (Fig. 2 I–O) placed substantial stress on food resource availability, a pressure further compounded by recurrent plague outbreaks. Proxies for temperature and hydrology also show strong correlations with changes in the body size of all studied animal species (Fig. 3). Moreover, both historical texts and zooarchaeological data document episodes of mass animal mortality between the 2nd and 10th centuries CE, framing this period as an age of both human and animal pandemics (79, 80). Increasing trade and connectivity facilitated the convergence of Old World disease pools, intensifying the risk of epizootics (81). As our understanding of these disease outbreaks remains limited, as does their impact on wild fauna, further research is necessary to better understand their potential impacts on animal body size. Such conditions may have influenced life-history trade-offs, favoring enhanced pathogen resistance at the expense of somatic growth.

Phase 3 marks the emergence of a new, shared trajectory in animal body size evolution and consistent correlations with diverse parameters of the biocultural environment. This alignment suggests that while anthropogenic pressures on animal populations persisted, their modalities shifted in response to changing ecological and potentially epidemiological regimes. These new conditions may have directly influenced the phenotypic expression of body size in both domestic and wild species.

Phase 4 (1,000 to 2,000 AD): Divergent Body Size Trajectories Between Wild and Domestic Taxa

The Phase 4 encompasses most of the Middle Ages and the Modern Era and marks a turning point in the evolution of animal size. From this last phase, wild species body size is strongly negatively correlated with that of domestic species (Fig. 1B) and both of them correlate differently with anthropogenic and environmental variables (Fig. 3). Indeed, while body size in wild species decreased steadily over the last two millennia, body size in domesticated species reached a minimum at the end of the 1st millennium AD, before increasing until the middle of the 2nd millennium AD (Fig. 1A).

The rabbit is a notable exception among wild species, with a significant size increase observed at the end of the Middle Ages (Fig. 1A), similar to domestic species. Rabbit domestication is considered as a gradual process starting between Antiquity and the late Middle Ages (22). Our findings show that large rabbits appeared in the 15th and 16th centuries and could correspond to the first domesticated morphologies in Mediterranean France, which coincides with contemporary English archival sources (82, 83).

An irreversible break in the expansion of agricultural land over forest occurred at the beginning of the 2nd millennium AD (84, 85). The Medieval Warm Period, characterized by higher temperatures (Fig. 2 N and O) and higher aridity (Figs. 1L and 2 I and J), particularly favored the agricultural exploitation of mountain areas (Fig. 2F). These major changes in the landscape greatly explain the inconsistencies found between temperatures and precipitation inferred from pollen (Fig. 2 K and M) and those reconstructed from other proxies. For wild species, these environmental changes increased habitat fragmentation (86) which may have caused their body size to decrease (28, 87). This period is also characterized by an intensification of hunting (88), which led to the decline of hare populations from the 16th c. onward and the disappearance of the red deer from Mediterranean France between the end of the Middle Ages and the 20th c. (89, 90). Since hunting pressure is correlated with a decrease in prey body size (91, 92), the increase in game hunting between the end of the Middle Ages and modern times could explain the size decrease observed in wild species.

The increase in body size of domestic species from the early 2nd millennium seems to have occurred very early in the Western Mediterranean, as this trend was not observed in other regions until the 13–17th c. AD (93, 94). The integration of livestock farming into a new techno-economic framework, along with changes in farming methods triggered by an increasingly open environment (Fig. 2 E–H), likely contributed to the increase of size in domesticated species. This trend predates the agricultural revolutions that accompanied the 19th-century Industrial Revolution and the late 20th-century mechanization. However, this recent period does not constitute a distinctly separate phase within our archaeological assemblage (Fig. 1A and SI Appendix, Fig. S3).

This period, spanning the last millennia, marks a bifurcation in the trajectory of animal morphological evolution, with wild taxa subject to increasing anthropogenic pressures (e.g., habitat fragmentation, hunting), while domesticated populations reflect increasing economic specialization and selective breeding under increasingly controlled management systems. This divergence exemplifies the growing asymmetry between natural and anthropogenic selection regimes, reinforcing the role of body size as an integrative marker of ecological and socioeconomic change.

8,000 y of Human–Animal–Environment Interactions

Our analysis demonstrates a strong alignment of body size trajectories across wild and domestic species until 1000 AD, suggesting that both groups faced similar constraints within their ecological niches. However, the greater amplitude of change in domesticates implies either that human selection for trait acted synergistically with natural selection or that domestic animals responded more strongly to environmental variability, possibly due to greater developmental plasticity or greater adaptability induced from human management.

Over the last 8,000 y, animal body size evolution cannot be attributed to a single ecological or anthropogenic driver. Rather, the strength and direction of correlations between size and analyzed proxies vary over time, reflecting shifting impacts from complex interactions between climate, land use, demography, and human intentionality. While human activity is widely recognized as a powerful evolutionary force (95, 96), our results demonstrate that natural selection prevailed as an evolutionary force on domestic animal morphology until the last millennium.

Given the consistent timing and direction of size shifts across wild and domestic species, factors such as the import of new genetic lineages (i.e., gene flow, new pathogens) or shifts in husbandry practices, (e.g., modified sex ratios, traits selection for meat or wool productions) cannot be considered as primary drivers. More likely, these human practices may have amplified the changes in domesticates relative to wild species, without introducing divergent trends prior to the last millennium.

From 1,000 AD onward, the degree of anthropogenic transformation—marked by extensive deforestation (Fig. 2 G and H) and significant shifts in floral assemblages (Fig. 2 E and F)—appears to have restructured ecological niches to such an extent that wild and domestic species were subjected to increasingly divergent selective pressures. These new environmental conditions likely promoted life-history trade-offs favoring reproductive investment and physiological maintenance in wild taxa, while selecting for increased body size and productivity in domesticates. Our results point toward pervasive anthropogenic processes shaping body size evolution, although these remain still incompletely understood. This study is a step toward generalization, to be tested in other regions with well-documented archaeological contexts.

Conclusion

This interdisciplinary study, integrating archaeological, zoological, vegetation, and climate data, demonstrates the deep and dynamic links between human societies and their environment. Over the past 8 millennia, the body sizes of wild and domestic animals have undergone significant changes, driven by intertwined environmental and anthropogenic factors.

Identifying and disentangling these influences on animal morphological evolution is particularly challenging for ancient populations, yet the strong and unexpected similarity in body size evolution between wild and domestic species over the first 6,500 y after the Neolithic introduction of agriculture in the North-western Mediterranean basin suggests that direct human impact on animal size remained secondary until ~1,000 y ago. If anthropogenic drivers did play a role during this period, they appear to have affected both wild and domestic animals in similar ways, likely through broad-scale environmental modifications such as deforestation or land-use change that affected their common ecological niche.

Climatic and environmental factors clearly shaped animal morphologies throughout prehistory while, in the last millennium, human–animal–environment interactions underwent profound shifts. Selective breeding intensified with the development of zootechnical methods and commercial livestock production, while human activities increasingly influenced wild species and global ecosystems. Notably, the stronger size shifts observed in domesticates suggest that environmental pressures alone are insufficient to explain recent trends. This points to the emergence of novel anthropogenic forces acting more directly on domestic populations, although the specific mechanisms, whether implicit selective pressures, intensified management, or sociocultural preferences, remain to be identified.

Body size is a sensitive indicator of systemic change, revealing both resilience and vulnerability within evolving human–animal–environment relationships. Understanding the relative influence of environmental and anthropogenic forces on animal morphology over the long term offers crucial insights into the deep history of domestication, ecological change, and the Anthropocene.

Materials and Methods

Archaeozoological Data.

The archaeozoological dataset is based on a compilation of taxon abundance and bone measurements. These data were acquired over nearly 30 y among archaeozoological assemblages found in 311 archaeological sites in Mediterranean France (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). The sites were dated from the beginning of the Neolithic in this geographical area, i.e., around 5,700 BC, to the modern period. The raw data were evaluated and corrected, through a systematic review of the record files, to obtain a standardized dataset of 225,780 bones and their 81,211 linear measurements. Additional information about the samples and the sites is available in SI Appendix, Table S1.

Measurements were acquired for all the taxa following the von den Driesch standards (16). Only measurements with more than five values were retained for the biometric analyses. The log size index (LSI) was calculated as the common logarithm of each measurement (X) divided by a reference measurement (Y), i.e., log10(X/Y) (31). In this study, the reference is the median measurement of the archaeological dataset corresponding to a LSI value of 0.0. It represents a regionally adapted comparison [see (33) for discussion about the choice of the standard].

Anthropogenic and Environmental Data.

Major changes in human settlement and activities were analyzed through a diachronic division of main chrono-cultural periods (Fig. 2D), and a demographic survey based on the number of recorded archaeological sites (Fig. 2B) compiled in (26). Archaeological and palaeoenvironmental records were classified in two biogeographical zones (BGZ) as defined in (26): BGZ I, i.e., eu/meso-Mediterranean coastal lowlands, and BGZ II, i.e., the supra-Mediterranean uplands whose altitude exceed 600 m (SI Appendix, Fig. S1).

By examining changes in the relative representation of the five domestic species within the archaeozoological assemblages (Fig. 2A), we tracked shifts in herd composition associated with new pastoral practices (97, 98). Their representation has been quantified through proportion of the bones of each species in global NISP (Number of Identified Specimens Per Taxon), a basic data item systematically recorded in French archaeozoological studies (99) and whose counting method has been standardized between the different authors. NISP percentages were calculated for 1,979 chrono-stratigraphic samples (CSS), i.e., groups of Stratigraphic Units defined following (100).

Pastoral activities have been calculated from low- and mid-altitude pollen records (Fig. 2 E and F and SI Appendix, Table S2) compiled in ref. 101: They are based on specific Pollen taxa (Asteroideae, Cichorioideae, Cirsium-type, Galium-type, Ranunculaceae, and Potentilla-type) that have been identified as being closely linked to pastured fields in mountain regions (29).

Climatic changes were reconstructed from different proxies including speleothems, or pollen records located in western Mediterranean area. Pollen-inferred climate parameters such as annual precipitation (Fig. 2K) and summer temperatures (Fig. 2M) corresponding to our study region have been extracted from the northern Hemisphere synthesis (23) (SI Appendix, Table S3). As human activities may induce a bias in the climate reconstructions based on pollen data, temperature signal has also been compared marine proxies from Mediterranean Sea (Fig. 2N) (25). Combined mean anomalies of d18O, d13C, and Mg/Ca from speleothems in Mendukilo cave, Northern Iberia (Fig. 2I) and in Corchia Cave, central Italy (Fig. 2J) were also integrated as a “palaeohydrological-trend” parameter (36). Periods of dry and wet phases (Fig. 2L) were assessed through major changes in vegetation cover (37) and storm activity investigated with a multiproxy sedimentological and geochemical study from lagoonal sediment core in the Gulf of Lion (38).

Tree pollen proportions (% AP, SI Appendix, Table S2) were calculated to evaluate forest changes in relation to the state of human occupation (Fig. 2 G–R) using the dataset compiled in ref. 101. Geomorphic data derived from alluvial and colluvial 14C dated records in mid-Rhone valley (34) were finally integrated (Fig. 2C) according to a correlation between landscapes erosion and agricultural/fire-setting activities on vegetation cover (102).

Plot Visualization and Statistical Analysis.

Diachronic analyses were performed with R language. Changes in mean values for each proxy through time (Fig. 2) were assessed using a smoothing method based on a local polynomial regression [function loess of the R package “stats” (103)]. This approach was selected as the most fitting model for all datasets, given their respective number of observations (104).

LSI curves by taxon and anatomical part (SI Appendix, Fig. S2) were designed using the R package “pataqu” (105). For each sample, 100 regression curves were calculated in order to take account dating uncertainties, ranging from a Terminus Post Quem (TPQ) to a Terminus Ante Quem (TAQ).

Mean coordinates of the 100 interpolated regression curves have been calculated in 100-y intervals for each taxon (function “fit_loess” from R package pataqu). Dissimilarity indices for the between-taxa LSI data have then been calculated, using the “coniss” method (Constrained Incremental Sums of Squares) (106) with the “chclust” function from R package “rioja” (107), to perform a hierarchical clustering constrained by chronological order (Fig. 1A). Finally, Pearson’s correlations (function “cor_mat” from R package “rstatix” (108)) were calculated between species body size variation (Fig. 1B) and between species body size and archaeological, palaeoenvironmental, and palaeoclimatological proxies (Fig. 3) for each “chronological cluster” obtained with the coniss clustering.
