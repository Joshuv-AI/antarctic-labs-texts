The crosstalk between MiRNAs and autophagy in leukemia: mechanisms and therapeutic insights
Authors: Alireza Faghiri Beyrami, Ali Akbar Movasaghpour Akbari, Leili Aghebati-Maleki, Sanam Dolati, Mehdi Yousefi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Autophagy, a process of cellular degradation and recycling, plays a biphasic role in leukemia; it acts either as a protective process that promotes the cancer cell to survive, or as a pathway that triggers cell death depending on certain conditions. MicroRNAs (miRNAs) regulate many important genes of autophagy, and these involve autophagy’s role in leukemia progression, drug resistance and therapy response. In this review, we will lay out the framework of how autophagy and miRNAs interact in that context and concentrate upon studies representative of how certain miRNAs such as miR-21, miR-155, or miR-29, regulate autophagy signaling pathways. We will also reflect on the potential of targeting the autophagy-miRNA axis to overcome drug resistance and enhance the outcome with treatment of leukemia. Although the utilization of miRNA-therapy and inhibitors have shown preclinical viability in leukemia, issues of delivery, specificity and toxicity create many obstacles. A greater comprehension of the molecular mechanisms underlying miRNA-mediated regulation of autophagy could lead to effective and novel therapeutic strategies for leukemia. This review highlights a clinical rationale to employ miRNAs and target autophagy modulation as a possible strategy to combat drug resistance leukemia.

Introduction

Leukemia is a type of cancer that affects the blood and bone marrow, marked by the uncontrolled growth of abnormal white blood cells. It is generally classified into four main types: acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), acute myeloid leukemia (AML), chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), and chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) [1, 2]. Autophagy is essential for cellular balance by breaking down and recycling cellular components. This process helps cells manage stressors such as nutrient deficiencies, metabolic imbalances, and infections. By removing damaged organelles and proteins, autophagy plays a key role in preventing diseases like cancer and neurodegenerative disorders, thereby preserving overall cellular health and the organism’s well-being [3]. Autophagy includes three main types: macroautophagy, microautophagy, and chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA). Each type plays a distinct role in the degradation and clearance of malfunctioning proteins, damaged organelles, and harmful pathogens, thereby maintaining cellular integrity and function. Macroautophagy entails capturing cytoplasmic materials by newly formed double-membrane vesicles that eventually merge with lysosomes for breakdown. In microautophagy, the lysosomal membrane can directly fold inward to capture cytosolic material for degradation. Chaperone-mediated autophagy uses chaperones to recognize cargo proteins containing a specific motif to bind to the lysosomal membrane [4, 5]. The role of autophagy in cancer is complex and controversial. Many clinical trials target autophagy inhibition, yet its impact varies. In some cancers, autophagy helps tumor growth and survival by recycling cellular components under stress and supporting resistance to chemotherapy. However, autophagy can also act as a cancer inhibitor by removing harmful cellular elements, maintaining genomic stability, and activating immune responses [5]. MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are short RNA molecules, usually around 22 nucleotides long, that do not code for proteins. They play a crucial role in controlling gene expression after transcription. miRNAs attach to complementary sequences on target messenger RNAs (mRNAs). This attachment can either block the mRNA from being translated into proteins or lead to its degradation, thereby regulating the gene’s expression [6]. miRNAs are fundamental to various biological activities, including organism development, cellular differentiation, programmed cell death, and metabolic activities. Their involvement in these essential functions highlights their adaptability and significance in biology [7]. Additionally, miRNAs are important in cancer diagnosis and treatment, as their expression patterns can predict patient responses to therapies and help in the early detection of different cancers (Fig. 1) [8].

Investigating the roles of autophagy and miRNAs in leukemia is highly important. These processes are deeply connected and critical in maintaining cellular balance and cancer progression. Autophagy, vital for cellular health, is often disrupted in leukemia, contributing significantly to both the progression of the cancer and its resistance to treatment [9]. miRNAs involved in regulating gene expression, have been recognized as important modulators of autophagy [10]. Thus, dysregulated miRNAs can either inhibit or stimulate autophagy, thereby influencing the survival and growth of leukemia cells [11]. Recent studies suggest that targeting the interaction between autophagy and miRNAs may lead to novel treatments for leukemia, enhancing the therapy effectiveness and overcoming drug resistance [12, 13]. Investigating the molecular pathways that control autophagy in cancer could provide new opportunities for targeting this process, enhancing the effectiveness of cancer treatments. Therefore, a deeper comprehension of the interplay between autophagy and miRNAs could uncover new molecular targets and lead to better clinical outcomes for leukemia patients.

This article is a literature review, not an original experimental study, and provides a conceptual overview of the roles of autophagy and miRNAs individually, within the context of leukemia pathogenesis, and we will highlight their interconnections and therapeutic targeting.

Molecular mechanisms of autophagy

One of the main trimmers for the degradation and recycling of cellular components is macroautophagy (hereinafter known as autophagy). During this process, the activation of autophagy-related (ATG) proteins results in the production of vesicles known as autophagosomes, with two membranes, and capture cargo before attaching to lysosomes to break down intracellular contents [14, 15]. The mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway plays a critical role in regulating autophagy, finally modulating the process in response to internal cellular stress and the availability of nutrients. This regulation ensures that autophagy is either suppressed or activated based on the cell’s metabolic needs and environmental conditions [16]. The autophagic process consists of five main stages: initiation, nucleation, elongation and maturation, fusion, and degradation [13]. Initiation stage: the mTOR plays a crucial role in this phase, where its activation by protein kinase B (Akt) and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling suppresses autophagy and, in contrast, its inhibition by 5′ AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) and P53 signaling promotes autophagy. During stressful conditions, mTOR is deactivated, which leads to the activation of the ULK1 complex (made up of UNC51-like kinase 1 (ULK1), FAK-family interacting protein of 200 kDa (FIP200), and Atg13) [17]. Nucleation stage: Beclin-1 plays a crucial role in forming autophagosomes, subsequently forming a complex with vacuolar protein sorting 34 (Vps34) and Atg14L named class III phosphatidylinositol 3 kinase complex (PI3KC3), which supports the nucleation phase and attracts proteins from the cytoplasm [18, 19]. Elongation and maturation stages: During these stages, two systems that resemble ubiquitin conjugation are necessary for facilitating the growth of the autophagosome membrane. The first system is characterized by the complex formed between the microtubule-associated protein light chain 3 (LC3) and phosphatidylethanolamine (PE). Atg4 cleaves LC3 at its C-terminal, resulting in the production of intracellular LC3-I, which is then conjugated with PE through the ubiquitin-like reactions mediated by Atg7 and Atg3. The lipid form of LC3, known as LC3-II, becomes incorporated into the membrane of the autophagosome [20]. The second system consists of the Atg12-Atg5-Atg16 complex, where Atg12 is conjugated with Atg5 through ubiquitin-like reactions mediated by Atg7 and Atg10. The Atg12-Atg5 conjugate noncovalently interacts with Atg16 to form a larger complex [21]. Fusion and degradation stages: During the fusion phase, autophagosomes combine with lysosomes to create autolysosomes, while in the degradation phase, the various materials contained within these autolysosomes are systematically broken down and dismantled, allowing for the recycling of essential nutrients and the removal of waste products (Fig. 2) [13, 22].

Autophagy in leukemia

Mechanism of autophagy in normal and leukemic cells

Autophagy, an essential cellular degradation process, is necessary for maintaining and properly functioning hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) involved in hematopoiesis. These stem cells are necessary for the production and differentiation of blood cells. Disruption of autophagy in HSCs can lead to various blood-related disorders, including leukemia, highlighting the importance of proper autophagic function for cellular health and disease prevention [23, 24]. Under normal conditions, cells activate autophagy in response to stressors like nutrient deficiency to produce essential metabolic precursors necessary for survival. This process is especially critical in neurons and other non-dividing cells, where it helps to remove damaged organelles and prevent the accumulation of toxic proteins [25]. In HSCs, autophagy regulates mitochondrial content, ensuring the cells’ metabolic needs and maintaining their differentiation potential. It also balances quiescence and activation in HSCs, which is vital for maintaining the stability of blood cell formation [24].

In leukemic cells, autophagy has a dual role. It can support cancer cell survival by providing metabolic adaptability and facilitating adjustment to the tumor microenvironment [26]. Conversely, autophagy can suppress tumor growth by breaking down cancer-promoting proteins and minimizing cellular damage [27]. For example, inhibiting autophagy in leukemic cells can increase their sensitivity to chemotherapy agents such as cytarabine, as it prevents the cells from relying on autophagy for survival. This indicates that modulating autophagy pathways could potentially enhance leukemia treatment [28]. According to findings, some autophagy-related proteins are involved in the metabolism and plasticity of cancerous cells, affecting how these cells respond to metabolic stress [26]. Specific autophagy mechanisms in leukemic cells, such as mitophagy, support tumor cell growth and survival by preserving the health of their mitochondria [25]. Targeting autophagy in quiescent leukemic stem cells (LSCs), which are frequently resistant to conventional treatments, has also been shown to have potential in current research. By blocking autophagy, LSCs can be stimulated into a more active state and become susceptible to therapy. Eliminating the LSCs that cause resistance and recurrence in cancer may assist in overcoming one of the major obstacles in leukemia treatment [26]. Autophagy functions similarly to a cellular recycling mechanism. It separates and reassembles cell components to assist cells in overcoming stressors such as infections, metabolic disorders, and lack of nutrients. Autophagy promotes general cellular and organismal conditions by eliminating impaired organelles as well as proteins, thereby assisting in avoiding diseases such as cancer and neurodegenerative disorders [23, 27].

The dual role of autophagy in cancer: survival vs. death

Autophagy aids in the survival and growth of cancer cells in the setting of tumor promotion, even under nutrient-deficient conditions. Insufficient nutrition causes these cells to activate autophagy, which breaks down unnecessary components and recycles them into vital elements, including lipids, nucleotides, and amino acids. Despite the absence of outside nutrients, the cells can still grow and reproduce because of this recycling mechanism, which maintains their metabolism and energy supply [29, 30]. Moreover, limited oxygen, low nutrition, and high tumor microenvironment (TME) acidity trigger autophagy in cancer cells. Autophagy initiates the process of mitophagy (the removal of damaged mitochondria) when oxygen levels are low (hypoxia). This lessens oxidative stress and stops the accumulation of dangerous reactive oxygen species (ROS), which aids in the survival of cancer cells [31, 32]. Furthermore, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) use autophagy in the tumor microenvironment to generate vesicles containing vital nutrients and amino acids. When absorbed by neighboring cancer cells, these vesicles form a powerful metabolic bridge between the CAFs and the cancer cells, promoting the development and survival of the cancerous cells [32, 33]. Besides nutrition recycling, autophagy also plays a role in controlling many signaling pathways that support the survival of cancerous cells. by activating autophagy, apoptotic signaling pathways can be suppressed, increasing cancer cell survival. This is accomplished via several mechanisms. As an example, pro-apoptotic factors can be degraded by autophagy, which lowers their availability and delays the beginning of apoptosis [29, 34, 35]. Moreover, autophagy can modulate the TME’s immunological response, which can influence the effectiveness of anti-tumor immunity and aid in cancer cells’ ability to evade the immune system. Autophagy may occasionally let immune-suppressive cells survive in the tumor microenvironment. Examples of these cells are myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and regulatory T cells (Tregs), which may hinder the activity of immune cells that fight tumors [36]. On the other hand, under some circumstances, autophagy can also promote cell death, which can inhibit the growth of tumors. This mechanism, called autophagic cell death, eliminates damaged proteins and organelles, preserving genomic stability and reducing the development of malignancies [37, 38]. It has been demonstrated that increasing autophagy causes cell death in glioblastoma, an aggressive form of brain cancer. Because these cancers can evade apoptosis or programmed cell death, they are typically resistant to conventional therapies. Nevertheless, scientists have found a method to force glioblastoma cells to self-digest till they die by boosting autophagy. This causes significant damage to essential cellular structures and processes, overwhelming the cells’ degradation mechanisms and finally resulting in the death of the cancer cells [39, 40]. Constitutively active Akt1 has been revealed to promote tumor development in vivo, simultaneously acting as an autophagy suppressor in vitro and in vivo, particularly in cells that lack the pro-apoptotic proteins Bak and Bax [41]. Furthermore, eliminating p62, a protein that autophagy breaks down, is crucial, as too much of it might result in tumor development [13]. Autophagy is essential in slowing the cellular changes that result in cancer because it preserves the function of mitochondria and lowers oxidative stress. Research has indicated that the absence of genes linked to autophagy, such as ATG5 and ATG7, leads to elevated levels of reactive oxygen species and genome instability, underscoring the significance of autophagy in inhibiting tumor growth [5, 42]. Additionally, autophagy improves the way tumor antigens are presented, which makes it easier for the immune system to identify and combat cancer cells. This procedure improves the immune system’s capacity to recognize and attack cancers. Moreover, pro-inflammatory factors (substances that might trigger chronic inflammation, an important indicator of risk for the growth of tumor cells) are broken down by autophagy, which aids in managing inflammation inside the body. Autophagy contributes to creating an environment within the body that is less favorable to the formation and advancement of cancer by lowering these inflammatory factors. Using these processes, autophagy supports the body’s innate defenses against cancer and directly contributes to tumor inhibition [5, 42]. Autophagy has dual hallmarks in human cancers that are context dependent. First, in its tumor-protective role, autophagy can buffer against both metabolic stress and therapeutic stress, as a mechanism of survival and therapeutic tolerance. Clinically, we have seen autophagy dependence in BRAFV600 mutant melanoma treated with MAPK inhibitors; the addition of the autophagy inhibitor hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) to dabrafenib and trametinib was noted to be feasible and had a high response rate, related to resistance from autophagy (BAMM trial) [43]. Preoperative HCQ with gemcitabine/nab-paclitaxel in resectable pancreatic cancer also demonstrates significant pathologic response and pharmacodynamic evidence of autophagy inhibition [44]. Early-phase combination studies of chloroquine with chemoradiation in glioblastoma indicate tolerability and possible signals of benefit based on a potential autophagy-related therapeutic tolerance (e.g., in patients with EGFRvIII positive disease) [45]. In contrast, tumor-suppressive autophagy limits tumor development through scavenging dysfunctional organelles and oncogenic proteins. Reduced BECN1 (Beclin-1) expression or monoallelic loss has been observed in multiple human tumors, leading to cancer development and a poor prognosis, suggesting the tumor-restraining role of basal autophagy [46, 47]. While in most tumors, increasing autoregulation is not considered as beneficial to cancer cells, in acute promyelocytic leukemia, retinoic acid and arsenic trioxide promote the autophagic-mediated degradation of the PML-RARA oncoprotein, resulting in a very high cure rate. This is one situation where elevating autophagy is destructive to the cancer cells being targeted [48]. In conclusion, autophagy has a dual function in cancer: it both aids in cell survival and encourages cell death. This makes it a challenging but potentially effective therapeutic target. We can create more targeted and efficient treatment plans by gaining insight into the specific factors that control whether autophagy functions as a friend or foe in cancer. Researchers are constantly exploring the many functions of autophagy to maximize its potential in the battle against cancer [49, 50].

Autophagy as a response to cellular stress in leukemia

Since leukemia cells develop quickly and inhabit an aggressive tumor environment, they frequently experience metabolic and oxidative stress. By removing damaged parts and providing the resources required for energy production and the synthesis of new cell components, autophagy aids in the survival of these cells. Many distinct genes and signaling pathways, including the Beclin-1-VPS34 complex and the ULK1 complex, which are essential for initiating and generating autophagosomes, precisely regulate this procedure [27, 51–53]. Another crucial part of autophagy’s role in leukemia is its interaction with the cell cycle. It is well-recognized that autophagy plays a critical role in controlling the cell cycle and preserving the delicate balance between cell division and apoptosis. This link guarantees that leukemia cells can withstand stress and prevent apoptosis in adverse situations [54]. Apoptosis is a crucial mechanism that maintains the health of cells and is closely correlated with autophagy. Because both autophagy and apoptosis have the ability to kill cells, as well as to inhibit each other, their connection is complex. The equilibrium between these two mechanisms determines leukemia cells’ fate during stress periods. For instance, autophagy can prevent apoptosis in leukemia cells during treatment, allowing them to survive and perhaps develop resistance to chemotherapy [35, 55, 56]. Furthermore, Drug resistance in tumor cells is one of the main obstacles in leukemia treatment. Such resistance is mostly due to autophagy. By breaking down and recycling cell components, autophagy aids leukemia cells in surviving the damaging impacts of chemotherapy. The cells can manage the stress produced through the treatment because this process gives them the resources they require to create fresh elements and generate energy [57].

MiRNAs and cancer

General functions of MiRNAs in cancer

miRNAs are little molecules that, depending on the circumstances and the genes they influence, can either promote cancer development or assist in slowing down it. miRNAs play a critical role in combating cancer by influencing drug resistance. For instance, miRNA-155 targets the FOXO3a gene, decreasing its expression and making breast cancer cells tolerant to chemotherapy medications like doxorubicin and paclitaxel. Other miRNAs, including miRNA-203 and miRNA-125b, also contribute to resistance to therapy in cancerous cells. Targeting these miRNAs could boost the efficacy of cancer interventions [58]. Additionally, miRNAs have a significant role in tumor angiogenesis, the process by which new blood vessels grow and give tumor nutrients. Angio miRs are a class of miRNAs that can either facilitate or obstruct this process. MiR-9, miR-200s, and miR-205, for example, contribute to the transformation of fibroblasts into cancer-associated fibroblasts, which promote the development of new blood vessels and tumors. Moreover, miRNAs such as the miR-17 ~ 92 cluster control genes like HIF-1α, which are essential for cancer cells to respond to low oxygen levels, facilitating the development of blood vessels and the growth of tumors [59, 60]. Additionally, miRNAs are essential in the tumor microenvironment by regulating the interactions between immune cells and tumor cells. Immune cells’ responses can be influenced by miRNAs released by tumor cells into their environment. For instance, based on the particular miRNAs involved, miRNAs might alter neutrophil activity, either aiding the tumor’s combat against it or encouraging its development. This demonstrates the complexity of miRNA interactions in the tumor microenvironment and emphasizes the possibility of using them as targets for cancer treatments [61]. Moreover, exosomal miRNAs can spread from tumor cells to other cells, boosting cancer cells’ metastasis and invasion of new regions. Exosomal miR-223, for instance, increases the extent of invasion of breast cancer cells via passing through the Mef2c-β-catenin pathway. This highlights the potential of exosomal miRNAs as indicators for monitoring the course of cancer and their critical involvement in cancer development [62]. Since miRNAs are non-invasive biomarkers, their circulation in the bloodstream represents a significant advancement in cancer diagnostics and prognostics. These miRNAs, which are persistent in bodily fluids including saliva, urine, and blood, can reveal important details on the existence, type, and course of certain malignancies. Their capacity to resist degradation relies on their encapsulation in exosomes or attachment to protein complexes [63]. Circulating miRNAs are helpful in diagnosing cancer because they can represent the molecular alterations occurring within the tumor. In addition, certain cancer kinds have more significant amounts of specific miRNAs, making them accurate cancer markers. These miRNA patterns are highly sensitive in differentiating between cancer patients and healthy persons. For example, miR-26a-2-3p and miR-574-3p have demonstrated efficacy in distinguishing between malignant and non-cancerous samples, indicating their potential utility in detecting biomarkers [61]. The various functions of miRNAs in cancer are also evident in how they control apoptosis and proliferation of cells. miRNAs that target several genes that regulate the cell cycle and survival pathways include those in the miR-17 ~ 92 cluster. For instance, miR-19 in this cluster stimulates the Akt-mTOR pathway, which supports cell proliferation and survival, by targeting the tumor suppressor gene Phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN). This demonstrates how miRNAs may affect important signaling pathways in cancer cells, assisting in the growth and management of tumors [60]. In conclusion, miRNAs have a significant role in drug resistance, blood vessel development, metastasis, and cell proliferation in cancer. They are present in exosomes in the circulation and can either promote or suppress malignancy. This makes them good candidates for novel therapeutic targets and helpful indicators for the diagnosis and prognosis of cancer. Research into the precise functions and processes of miRNAs will continue to advance our knowledge of cancer and aid in the creation of more effective management and treatment plans [8, 64–66].

Specific miRNAs involved in leukemia and their targets Researchers have discovered a number of significant miRNAs in leukemia. The following are certain miRNAs linked to leukemia and the targets they impact:

miRNAs are frequently expressed inappropriately in acute leukemia, which contributes significantly to the onset and progression of the cancer. AML benefits from the miR-29 family, which consists of miR-29a, miR-29b, and miR-29c, since it reduces the levels of DNA methyltransferases (DNMT3A and DNMT3B). Tumor suppressor genes are reactivated due to miR-29’s reduction of DNA methylation. Because of this, miR-29 is a potential target for therapies meant to correct aberrant patterns of DNA methylation in AML [67, 68]. Moreover, it has been discovered that in patients with AML, the expression of miR-29b is downregulated. Normally, miR-29b targets many cancer-related genes, including MCL-1 and CDK6, to help regulate cell proliferation and induce apoptosis. Low levels of miR-29b cause leukemia cells to proliferate and survive uncontrollably [69, 70]. However, in chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) cases, miR-29c is frequently detected at low levels. Normally, it targets TCL1, a gene that aids leukemia cell survival through participation in the AKT signaling pathway. Leukemia cells are able to proliferate because TCL1 is no longer restrained when miR-29c levels decline [71]. Low levels of miR-15a and miR-16-1 in CLL lead to a rise in BCL2, a protein that delays cell death. This upregulation promotes the survival of leukemia cells and advances the disease. By enabling cancer cells to undergo natural cell death, targeting the BCL2 pathway may offer an effective treatment option for CLL [72, 73]. MiR-181a and miR-181b target TCL1, a protein that aids in activating AKT kinase, to affect cell development and survival. The expression of TCL1 can be altered in ALL and AML by aberrant levels of miR-181, which alters the behavior of the cancer cells [74]. In AML, the expression of miR-181a and miR-181b is upregulated. These miRNAs target genes such as HOXA11 and HOXA7, which contribute to regulating cell growth and development [75]. Furthermore, in AML and CLL, high levels of miR-155 are detected. It targets many molecules, including SHIP1, which typically inhibits the PI3K/AKT pathway. miR 155 directly binds to and regulates SHIP1 (Src homology 2 domain containing inositol 5 phosphatase 1) a critical negative regulator of the PI3K/AKT pathway. SHIP1 hydrolyzes PIP₃ into PIP₂, thereby limiting membrane localization and activation of AKT. Consequently, the inhibition of SHIP1 by miR 155 promotes PIP₃ accumulation, AKT recruitment and phosphorylation, and enhanced AKT signaling [76, 77]. Leukemia cells are encouraged to proliferate and survive when miR-155 is excessively expressed [78, 79]. It has been demonstrated that MiR-223 targets NFI-A and Mef2c, two proteins crucial for the development of myeloid cells, and assists in regulating the production of granulocytes, a subset of white blood cells. When miR-223 levels are not appropriately controlled, it can lead to uncontrolled myeloid cell proliferation and development in AML [80]. Researchers have shown that targeting proteins, including BCL2 and MYC, promotes cell death and inhibits cell proliferation to function as a tumor inhibitor. Low miR-34a levels cause uncontrollable cell proliferation and survival in AML and CLL. Treatments that raise the levels of miR-34a may aid in inducing cell death and lowering the number of leukemia cells [81, 82]. Furthermore, leukemias such as ALL, AML, and CLL are influenced by miR-125b. It targets proteins like PUMA and BAK1 that cause apoptosis, preventing cell death. Elevated levels of miR-125b promote the growth and survival of leukemia cells. We could potentially decrease the number of leukemia cells and accelerate cell death by focusing on miR-125b or the pathways it impacts [83]. The genes HOXA9 and MEIS1, which are crucial for the formation of blood cells, have been affected by miR-196b. Inappropriate levels of miR-196b impair the normal expression of genes and aid in the development of leukemia in AML. Treatments aimed at restoring normal miR-196b levels may be able to address these problems and inhibit the proliferation of leukemia cells [84, 85]. It has been discovered that high levels of the miR-17-92 cluster, which is known to promote malignancy, are frequently observed in acute myeloid leukemia (AML). Numerous microRNAs in this cluster, including miR-17, miR-18a, miR-19a, miR-20a, and miR-92a, together target a number of tumor suppressor genes, including PTEN and E2F1 [86]. On the other hand, in AML, some microRNAs function as tumor suppressors. For example, miR-223 is often present in AML at low concentrations. It specifically targets the transcription factor E2F1, which is essential for regulating cell division. E2F1 overexpresses when miR-223 levels are low, causing uncontrolled cell proliferation [87]. Moreover, miR-9 has been shown to be aberrant in AML, and a worse prognosis is linked to its overexpression. miR-9 targets genes like MLLT3 involved in cell cycle regulation and apoptosis. As a result, leukemia cells are encouraged to proliferate and survive [88]. It has been indicated that Fludarabine-resistant CLL results from high levels of miR-21 in the illness. It specifically targets the PTEN tumor suppressor gene, which enhances the survival of leukemia cells by activating the PI3K/AKT pathway [89]. Moreover, inhibiting miR-21 with antisense oligonucleotides in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) mice has decreased leukemia cell proliferation and enhanced apoptosis [90]. Some miRs, for example, miR-21, have the opposite functional role based on the type of leukemia they are in. This is a context-dependent role (i.e. affected by genetic background, cell lineage, and tumor microenvironment) based on context-dependent targeting. In acute myeloid leukemia (AML), miR-21 is frequently overexpressed, promoting drug resistance by downregulating PTEN and increasing PI3K/AKT signaling to promote survival and autophagy [91]. Conversely, in chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) miR 21 can induce a more tumor-suppressive role depending on the environment. This appears to occur primarily through BCL2 or PDCD4 regulation, and is likely dependent on the specific signaling context and co-expressed miRNAs [92]. These differences in function highlight the need for leukemia subtype-specific evaluation when considering miRNAs as potential biomarkers or therapeutic targets [93]. In addition, miR-126 is often detected at low levels in AML. By targeting the cancer-related gene PIK3R2, restoring miR-126 levels can reduce the proliferation of leukemia cells [94].

How MiRNAs regulate autophagy-related genes

MicroRNAs are one of the crucial regulators within the autophagy pathway, influencing the expression of genes associated with autophagy. Wide ranges of miRNAs have been identified that affect autophagy by targeting genes essential to different phases of the autophagic process [95]. For example, miR-7 is recognized as a significant regulator of ATG5, which results in decreased expression of ATG5 and autophagy inhibition, reducing the invasiveness of hepatocellular carcinoma cells [96]. The formation of autophagosomes requires the participation of numerous proteins from the ATG family, and targeting various stages in this process can lead to distinct outcomes. The overexpression of ATG-4B mRNA, a key gene for autophagosome formation, has been associated with tumor progression and unfavorable prognosis in several aggressive cancer types [97]. In the context of cervical cancer, the downregulation of miR-34 leads to an increase in ATG-4B protein levels by stabilizing its mRNA, thus facilitating autophagy [98]. Beclin-1 is another key gene in the regulation of autophagy, primarily through its direct interaction with PI3KC3 [99]. Targeting Beclin-1 via miR-409 increases the chemosensitivity of colon cancer cells by inhibiting chemotherapy-induced autophagy. Similarly, a reduction in miR-191 level, coupled with an increase in Beclin-1 expression, has been noted in radiation-resistant lung cancer cells, indicating an increased level of autophagy in these cells [100]. The various miRNAs and their diverse targets are related to autophagy (Table 1). Additionally, microRNAs can modulate autophagy indirectly by influencing upstream signaling pathways that regulate the expression of autophagy-related genes (ATGs). The PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway has been recognized as an essential regulator of autophagy in various cancer types [101]. In the context of prostate cancer, miR-146 suppresses cell proliferation by downregulating PTEN, thereby promoting autophagy through the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway [102]. miRNA interactions shaping autophagy in leukemia. miRNA interactions influencing autophagy in leukemia. Disparate miRNAs could affect autophagy at the signaling nodes and/or the core machinery, resulting in context-dependent cooperation or antagonism. For example, in AML, miR-155 burns the SHIP1 bridge that ignites PIK3/AKT-mTOR and suppresses autophagy. Concomitantly, the miR-29 family can diminish ATG9A to spelunk autophagosome trafficking and flux, thus both driving coordinated down-regulation of autophagy [103, 104]. On the other hand, under hypoxic/innate-immune cues, miR-155 may also support autophagy by targeting Rheb/mTOR components, while miR-29 still impairs flux by downregulating ATG9A/TFEB, creating antagonistic influences that are likely dependent on the cell’s state and the relative abundance of the miRNAs’ targets. These examples show how miRNA networks can modulate autophagy in the same or opposite directions in leukemic cells [104, 105]. Through these indirect mechanisms, miRNAs can extensively influence autophagy by affecting the underlying signaling networks.

The impact of MiRNAs on autophagy pathways in chronic and acute leukemias

B-cell chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) is characterized by the accumulation of clonal B cells in the peripheral blood, bone marrow, and lymph nodes, making it the most prevalent form of leukemia in adults [106, 107]. The disease typically exhibits a low risk of progression and a relatively low mortality rate, with a 5-year relative survival rate nearing 90% [108]. Different studies indicate that microRNAs may serve as prognostic biomarkers in CLL [109]. Notably, miR-155 plays a significant role in regulating post-transcriptional gene expression in B cells and is associated with unfavorable clinical outcomes in CLL patients [110, 111]. In addition, CLL cases that exhibit deletions of the miR-34b and miR-34c show a more aggressive form of its [112]. The most common genetic alteration observed in chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), present in 65% of patients, is the deletion of the miR-15/16 gene, which acts as a tumor suppressor by directly inhibiting BCL2 [113]. Moreover, specific microRNAs have been identified as regulators of autophagy-related genes; for example, miR-130a has been demonstrated to reduce autophagic flux in primary CLL cells by targeting the ATG-2B gene [114].

Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is a type of hematological malignancies marked by an overproduction of immature lymphocytes and is the most prevalent cancer among children [115, 116]. ALL is categorized by its cellular lineage into T-cell ALL (T-ALL) and B-cell ALL (B-ALL), with B-ALL being responsible for 80%-85% of cases [117]. Numerous microRNAs are involved in ALL relapse, drug resistance, cell growth, differentiation, and apoptosis. For instance, the elevated expression of miR-124 is correlated with a poor prognosis and an increased likelihood of relapse in patients with both ALL-T and ALL-B [118]. Additionally, the overexpression of miR-100, miR-99, and miR-125b is associated with adverse outcomes and resistance to therapies such as vincristine and daunorubicin [118, 119]. Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a common hematologic malignancy in adults caused by genetic mutations and chromosomal abnormalities and characterized by immature differentiation of cells in the blood and bone marrow [120, 121]. Autophagy in leukemia treatment

The activation of autophagy in response to diverse chemotherapeutic agents can lead to either cell death or survival, thereby influencing the effectiveness or resistance to treatment [123]. Most chemotherapeutic strategies trigger autophagy in cancer cells as a defensive mechanism against stress-related damage [124]. Glucocorticoids (GCs) are frequently used in numerous chemotherapy treatments for lymphoid and myeloid malignancies, including acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL). Nevertheless, patients often exhibit resistance to GCs upon relapse [125]. The small-molecule antagonist GX15-070 (obatoclax), which targets antiapoptotic BCL-2 family proteins, has been shown to re-sensitize GC-resistant ALL cells by promoting autophagy within these cancer cells [126–128]. Despite the potential of Obatoclax to induce apoptosis and potentially autophagy in leukemia cells (including GC resistant ALL models), it did not fulfill its promise in earlier clinical trials. In a phase I trial involving subjects with relapsed/refractory AML or myelodysplastic syndromes, obatoclax exhibited limited antitumor activity with significant neurologic adverse effects related to the study treatment, such as somnolence, dizziness, and ataxia. Similar findings were observed in studies involving CLL and lymphoma, where clinical responses were limited and toxicities prevented further development of this drug. So although obatoclax is appealing mechanistically, it has not advanced into later stage leukemia therapy and is not currently under active clinical development [129, 130]. Additionally, in chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) cells, autophagy has been linked to cell survival and drug resistance [131]. Several autophagy-related genes are modified in CLL, suggesting that changes in autophagy contribute to the progression of the disease [132]. Researchers have indicated that using autophagy inhibitors in conjunction with chemotherapeutic agents like venetoclax can produce synergistic apoptotic effects, highlighting the protective role of autophagy against cell death in CLL cells [133]. Furthermore, numerous studies have shown the complex involvement of various microRNAs in influencing sensitivity to cytotoxic treatments in acute and chronic myeloid leukemias [134, 135]. For instance, in chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) cells, the downregulation of miR-21 has been found to enhance the expression of autophagy-related proteins, thereby increasing the sensitivity of leukemic cells to doxorubicin [136]. Combination of autophagy inhibitors with regulators of miRNAs enhance the efficacy of therapy. There is preclinical evidence showing that autophagy inhibition (e.g., chloroquine) can enhance chemotherapy in AML and miRNA modulation could rewire autophagy to change the response to drugs [137]. The chances of synergies occurring are high when miRNA intervention coordinates with pro-survival autophagy attenuation (e.g., miR-143→ATG7/ATG2B) and may work as adjuncts to chloroquine-associated late effects [138]. When a miRNA’s antileukemic effect occurs through pro-death autophagy (e.g., miR-455-3p→PPARα), chloroquine may compromise that effect. Therefore, thinking about directionality and where we address (upstream pathways/late phase flux) should influence our combination design [139, 140]. There is still a lot of clinical trial work to do. Therefore, by considering the role of autophagy in causing sensitivity or resistance of cancer cells to chemotherapy treatments, it is possible to use suitable inhibitors to create synergy with chemotherapy agents by improving the treatment process.

Therapeutic approaches involving MiRNAs and autophagy modulation in cancer

Clinical trials

To date, no therapeutic miRNA with an explicit/primary mechanism of autophagy modulation has advanced to cancer clinical trials. The miRNA agents that have progressed to human use are MRX34, (miR34a mimic), TargomiR (miR16 mimic), and cobomarsen (anti-miR155) primarily targeting pathways associated with proliferation, survival, and immune regulation; however, they may also indirectly affect autophagy, but again, autophagy was not specified as the primary mechanism of action for these miRNAs in their associated clinical protocols or reports. Several candidates have progressed from preclinical models into early-phase clinical trials. MRX34, a liposomal mimic of miR-34a, entered a phase I first-in-human trial (NCT01829971) in patients with advanced solid tumors. The trial showed dose-dependent repression of miR-34a target genes and indications of clinical benefit, but it was stopped prematurely due to severe immune mediated toxicities, including multiple patient deaths [141]. TargomiR (miR-16 mimic), delivered with EGFR targeted minicells, demonstrated acceptable safety and a MTD with disease control in some mesothelioma patients [142]. Anti-miR-155 (cobomarsen) had early evidence of pharmacodynamic activity but broader efficacy remains under evaluation [143]. In comparison, autophagy inhibitors have moved into clinical development specially targeting clinical use (e.g., chloroquine/hydroxychloroquine and, more recently, ULK1/2 inhibition with DCC-3116) although none has (to date) received oncology regulatory approval. Chloroquine/hydroxychloroquine (lysosomal inhibitors) have been used in combination with standard of care in a few cancers: In melanoma (BRAFV600) HCQ + dabrafenib/trametinib established a recommended dose; prespecified minimum 1-year PFS goal was not met [43]. In resectable pancreatic cancer: pre-operative HCQ + gemcitabine/nab-paclitaxel demonstrated improved pathologic response and on-target autophagy inhibition compared to chemo alone [44]. In glioblastoma: chloroquine + radio-chemotherapy was feasible with some signals in molecular sub-groups; however, retinal/cardiac risks limit the dose (NCT02378532) [45]. While next-generation inhibitors like the ULK1/2 inhibitor inlexisertib (DCC-3116) are right now in the clinic (Phase 1/2 alone or in combination with MAPK-pathway drugs), and efficacy readouts are advancing (NCT04892017). Also, exploring Apilimod (PIKfyve inhibitor) in B-cell malignancies utilizes the disruption of the endolysosomal function associated with autophagy [144]. Both strategies are making real clinical translation time. At the moment, autophagy inhibitors, especially HCQ/CQ, have the clearest history of clinical use; miRNA drugs have mechanistic proof of target modulation but have challenges in delivery and safety. Ultimately, the most important thing will be the selection of the correct disease contexts and combinations.

Safety considerations

Given the role of autophagy in cellular quality control in healthy tissues, inhibiting systemic autophagy could lead to accumulation of damaged proteins/organelles and tissue damage. In vivo, loss of essential autophagy genes (Atg5/Atg7) in neurons leads to the accumulation of ubiquitinated inclusions and subsequent neurodegeneration, indicating the physiological function of basal autophagy [145, 146]. Clinically, lysosomotropic inhibitors (chloroquine/hydroxychloroquine) have clinical risks of ocular (retinopathy) and cardiac damage (QT-interval prolongation, rare cardiomyopathy) that must be dose-limited and monitored [147, 148]. Conversely, miRNA therapeutics have shown on-target activity in early studies, but could be associated with specific risk for this class of drugs, including innate immune activation (TLR7/8), infusion-related reactions, and off-target gene modulation; importantly, the miR-34a mimic MRX34 program was terminated early due to serious adverse events resulting from an immune-mediated mechanism [141, 149]. Therefore, careful patient selection, dose and delivery system will be required to transition autophagy or miRNA modulation to the clinic.

Barriers to clinical application: delivery, safety, and specificity

In spite of the compelling biology, clinical applications are challenged by delivery (rapid nuclease degradation, poor bone marrow biodistribution, poor endosomal escape), safety (innate immune activation and vehicle-mediated toxicities), and specificity (the breadth of miRNA targeting increases the chance of off-target effects in normal tissues) [150]. Many of the early clinical trials underscore these challenges: MRX34, a mimic of miR-34a, was suspended for immune-mediated toxicities [141], while TargomiR using EGFR-targeted minicells was reported to have acceptable safety in a small study of miR-16, but had limited activity [151], and anti-miR-155 was reported to have pharmacodynamic activity but efficacy is still being analyzed [152]. As a group, successful applications of miRNAs and anti-miRNAs will likely require safer memes, targeted delivery, better endosomal escape and some form of patient selection, possibly based on biomarkers [153].

Microbiome considerations in drug efficacy

While direct bacterial biotransformation of autophagy inhibitors (e.g. chloroquine/hydroxychloroquine) in humans is not well-defined, CQ/HCQ can alter the gut microbial communities, and drug - microbiota interactions are known to affect the absorption, bioavailability, and host immune tone as well as modify antitumor efficacy and toxicity [154–156]. In contrast, current miRNA therapeutics are administered systemically, and therefore are unlikely to undergo direct gut microbial metabolism; nevertheless, the microbiome can be modulated and alter innate immune pathways (ex. TLR7/8 responsiveness) and ultimately affect oligonucleotide safety and activity [157, 158]. Future studies evaluating antibiotic exposure, microbiome profiles, and drug levels will provide important clarity regarding the effects [159].

Conclusion

As a fundamental cellular process, autophagy plays a dual role in leukemia cells. On the one hand, it contributes to cancer cell survival and drug resistance, and on the other hand, it acts as a tumor suppressor by increasing sensitivity to chemotherapy. This feature has made autophagy a potential target for new therapeutic strategies in leukemia, especially to overcome drug resistance, which is one of the main challenges of leukemia treatment. miRNAs play a key role in leukemia progression and treatment response by regulating the expression of autophagy-related genes and signaling pathways. Identifying key miRNAs and understanding their regulatory mechanisms can help improve treatment outcomes and reduce side effects. In sum, the interaction between autophagy and miRNAs has provided a new perspective for leukemia research and treatment, which can improve the effectiveness of treatments.
