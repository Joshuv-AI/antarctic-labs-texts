Multinational cost‐effectiveness analysis of empagliflozin for heart failure patients with ejection fraction >40%
Authors: Spyros Kolovos, Leana Bellanca, Harinala Groyer, Giuseppe MC Rosano, Alexandra Solé, Jennifer Gaultney, Stephan Linden
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsHeart failure is a chronic progressive condition, with considerable burden on patients' quality of life and economic burden for the healthcare systems. Before the approval of empagliflozin, there were no proven effective treatments for patients with heart failure with left ventricular ejection fraction (HF LVEF) > 40%. The aim of this study was to evaluate the cost‐effectiveness of empagliflozin + standard of care (SoC) compared with SoC alone for patients with HF LVEF > 40%, from the perspective of the healthcare systems of the United Kingdom (UK), Spain, and France, and to quantify the healthcare costs for these patients.Methods and resultsA lifetime Markov cohort state‐transition model was developed based on discrete health states defined by Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire‐Clinical Summary Score quartiles to track disease severity. Model inputs relied primarily on the EMPEROR‐Preserved trial data or obtained from published literature or country‐specific databases, as well as local guidelines for the requirements for the conduct of the economic evaluation of healthcare technologies. The total lifetime cost of receiving SoC per patient was £10 092, €15 765, and €14 958 in the UK, Spain, and France, respectively, which increased by £1407, €1148, and €1485, respectively, with the addition of empagliflozin to the SoC. Empagliflozin + SoC was associated with significantly reduced number of hospitalization for HF or cardiovascular death compared with SoC alone, which was a key driver offsetting its drug acquisition costs. The incremental cost‐effectiveness ratio per quality‐adjusted life year (QALY) gained was consistently favourable at £14 851, €11 706, and €15 447 in the UK, Spain, and France, respectively. Scenario analysis using the New York Heart Association functional class showed similar results. Probabilistic sensitivity analyses showed more than 50% probability for cost‐effectiveness for a willingness‐to‐pay (WTP) threshold of £/€20 000/QALY for the three countries.ConclusionsEmpagliflozin was found to be the first targeted treatment option that is clinically effective and cost‐effective for patients with HF LVEF > 40%. Prescribing empagliflozin with SoC to patients with HF LVEF > 40% is expected to improve clinical outcomes and patients' quality of life and substantially below accepted WTP threshold for the healthcare systems in the UK, Spain, and France.Keywords: Heart failure with left ventricular ejection fraction (HF LVEF) > 40%, Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, Cost‐effectiveness, Economic evaluation, Empagliflozin, SGLT2i

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a chronic progressive condition with high mortality and morbidity. 1 The most common symptoms of HF are dyspnoea, oedema, and fatigue, which affect considerably the quality of life. 1 Chronic HF can be categorized into two main phenotypes based on the left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF): LVEF ≤ 40% [HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF)] and LVEF > 40% (composed of HF with mildly reduced and preserved ejection fraction). 1 , 2 Of the total chronic HF population, the proportion of patients with LVEF > 40% range from 30% to 50% 3 , 4 , 5 ; however, the proportion can reach as high as 85% among elderly patients. 6 Regardless of the phenotype, patients with chronic HF experience acute episodes of worsening symptoms that may further impair the heart's ability to function, leading to increased disability and a heightened risk of death. 1

HF is one of the leading health problems causing significant clinical and economic burden on healthcare systems around the globe. 7 The worldwide prevalence of HF was estimated at 64.3 million in 2017. 8 As per Heart Failure Policy Network 2020 report, more than 15 million people, or around 2% of the European population, were estimated to be living with HF. 9 One‐year costs related to HF in the European Union were reported to be around €29 billion, with around 80% of total direct HF treatment costs attributable to hospitalizations. 10 , 11 , 12 HF patients accounted for 1 million inpatient bed days, which represents 2% of all English National Health Service (NHS) inpatient bed days and 5% of all emergency medical hospital admissions, with an average length of stay of 6–9 days and a 3 month readmission rate of 25%. 13 It is the leading cause of hospital admissions in the population above 65 years of age. 13 The economic burden of HF is likely to increase substantially over the next decade, potentially driven by ageing population and occurrence of comorbidity factors, with an estimated 46% increase in HF by 2030. 14 , 15

Before the approval of empagliflozin, there were no proven effective treatments for patients with heart failure with left ventricular ejection fraction (HF LVEF) > 40%. Clinicians only treated HF LVEF > 40% with a combination of therapies aiming to relieve symptoms and prevent or slow down further damage to the patient's heart. 1 Until recently, no class of drug/clinical trial had shown to increase survival or reduce hospitalizations in these patients. 1 , 16 , 17 , 18 Thus, there was a significant unmet need for evidence‐based, safe, effective, and cost‐effective treatment for patients with HF LVEF > 40%.

In the EMPA‐REG OUTCOME trial, empagliflozin first demonstrated a significant reduction in the risk of hospitalization for HF (HHF) (by 35%) and cardiovascular (CV) death (by 38%) compared with placebo in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and established CV disease. 19 , 20 Considering the advantages in patients with history of HF, empagliflozin was investigated for its effect on chronic HF with or without T2DM in the EMPagliflozin outcomE tRial in Patients With chrOnic heaRt failure (EMPEROR) trial programme. One of the trials of this programme, the EMPEROR‐Preserved trial, randomized 5988 patients with HF LVEF > 40% to receive empagliflozin (10 mg once daily) + standard of care (SoC) or placebo + SoC. Empagliflozin + SoC showed clinically meaningful and statistically significant reduction (21%) in the risk of CV death or HHF compared with placebo + SoC, 21 being the first clinical trial with positive results in these populations.

The clinical efficacy of empagliflozin has been established; however, the cost‐effectiveness remains an important consideration for the healthcare systems. Thus, the first objective of this study was to evaluate the cost‐effectiveness of empagliflozin + SoC compared with placebo + SoC (hereafter reported as SoC) for patients with HF LVEF > 40% based on the EMPEROR‐Preserved trial from the perspective of the healthcare system of the United Kingdom (UK), Spain, and France. The three countries were chosen to provide comprehensive information on cost‐effectiveness across different healthcare systems, which use economic evaluation evidence as part of their reimbursement decision‐making. The second objective was to quantify the healthcare costs of patients with HF LVEF > 40% receiving SoC, as estimated by the healthcare model given that, currently, there is limited information about this in the literature.

Methods

Model overview

An MS Excel‐based cost‐effectiveness model (CEM) was built based on the modelling approach that was previously accepted by the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) committee for empagliflozin + SoC for patients with HFrEF. 22 The model inputs were primarily derived from the pivotal EMPEROR‐Preserved trial data or obtained from published literature or country‐specific databases, where appropriate.

The model used a Markov cohort state‐transition approach based on discrete health states defined by Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ)‐Clinical Summary Score (CSS) quartiles to track disease severity (Supporting Information, Figure S1 ). Risk equations were used to estimate the rate of HHF, CV, and all‐cause (AC) mortality. In addition to HHF, the CEM included other adverse events (AEs) of interest and their impact on utility scores.

Model inputs

Patient population

The modelled patient population reflected the intention to treat (ITT) population of the EMPEROR‐Preserved trial. As per the trial's inclusion criteria, all patients were adults (aged ≥18 years) who had chronic HF with LVEF > 40% and New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional classes II–IV. The average age of population was 71.89 years, and 55.3% were male. Approximately half (49.1%) of the population had comorbid T2DM. Patients were receiving background optimized pharmacological treatment according to the SoC for HF LVEF > 40%, as detailed in the Supporting Information, Table S1 .

Treatment

Treatment options within the model regarded empagliflozin and various classes of background HF medications, including ivabradine (IVA), angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEi), beta‐blocker (BB), angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB), mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (MRA), and angiotensin receptor neprilysin inhibitor (ARNi), which were taken by patients in the EMPEROR‐Preserved trial (Supporting Information, Table S2 ).

Clinical efficacy

The clinical efficacy of empagliflozin + SoC and SoC was derived from statistical analyses data of the EMPEROR‐Preserved trial. 23 The probability matrices to track transition between health states were constructed directly from the EMPEROR‐Preserved trial data, by stratifying patients according to prior period health state (defined by KCCQ‐CSS) and calculating their observed proportions in the next period health state. The transition probabilities were calculated monthly, as per the model's cycle length (Supporting Information, Table S3 ).

The monthly rate of HHF events was estimated using a repeated‐measures Poisson model that was fitted to patient‐level data with generalized estimating equations (Supporting Information, Table S4 ). Parametric survival analysis was conducted to extrapolate time to AC death and CV death as a function of treatment and time‐varying KCCQ‐CSS health states, which in turn served as measures of disease progression beyond the EMPEROR‐Preserved trial duration. The analysis used Weibull parametric model for base‐case distribution for AC and CV death, as it gave the best fitted and the most plausible estimates for long‐term survival. Death attributable to non‐CV causes was calculated using the AC death and CV death risk equations derived from the EMPEROR‐Preserved trial. Similar to mortality survival analysis, the parametric survival analysis on the empagliflozin arm was performed for treatment discontinuation.

The AE rates for each of the two groups were derived from the EMPEROR‐Preserved trial by assuming a constant hazard (Supporting Information, Table S5 ). AEs with difference in event rates > 1% between the two comparator arms of the EMPEROR‐Preserved trial were included in the CEM, namely, urinary tract infection (UTI), genital infection, acute renal failure, hepatic injury, volume depletion, hypotension, hypoglycaemic event, and bone fracture.

Costs

All costs were expressed in 2021 Great British pounds for the UK, adjusted for inflation using the Eurostat Harmonised Indices of Consumer Prices data. 24 For Spain and France, the costs were expressed in 2021 Euros, adjusted for inflation using the Instituto Nacional de Estadistica (INE) 25 and the Institut national de la statistique et des études économiques (INSEE), 26 respectively.

Only direct medical costs—treatment acquisition, clinical event management, AE‐related management, and disease management—were considered. The treatment acquisition cost for empagliflozin and SoC therapies was extracted from the Monthly Index of Medical Specialities (MIMS) drug database for the UK, 27 from BotPlus Web database for Spain, 28 and from Prix publiés au JO for France 29 (Table 1 ). Country‐specific clinical event management costs for managing clinical events (HHF and CV death) were obtained from respective national databases or published literature (Supporting Information, Table S6 ). Expenditures associated with AE management were computed as weighted average costs based on the type of care received (outpatient visit, inpatient visit, or self‐treatment) and the unit cost of each visit type (Supporting Information, Table S7 ). HF‐related disease management costs associated with general physician (GP), cardiologist visits, and accident and emergency (A&E) referrals were computed based on the frequency of use and unit cost for each type of care. In addition, laboratory test costs and CV procedure costs were considered for France. Estimated monthly disease management costs are shown in the Supporting Information, Table S8 .

Health‐related quality of life

The utility values were accrued based on time spent in each KCCQ‐CSS quartile and events of HHF and AEs. A mixed‐effects linear regression model, with a random intercept to account for intra‐patient correlations in EQ‐5D responses, was used for the estimation of health state‐specific utility values (Supporting Information, Table S9 ).

As the utility value for KCCQ‐CSS quartile 4 derived from the trial was higher than the utility of general population (as reported by Sullivan et al. 2011, 30 Garcia‐Gordillo et al. 2016, 31 and Janssen et al. 2021 32 for the UK, Spain, and France, respectively), an age adjustment was done for the base‐case analysis. For the age adjustment, the utility for KCCQ‐CSS quartiles 1–3 was reduced by the relative difference between utility for KCCQ‐CSS quartile 4 as observed in the EMPEROR‐Preserved trial and the published utility of country's general population (70–79 years for the UK and Spain, and 64–75 years for France). The utility of the general population was then assumed as the utility value for KCCQ‐CSS quartile 4 in the model.

The impact of HHF on health‐related quality of life (HRQoL) was captured as one‐off decrements to those patients who experienced the event. Similarly, the disutility associated with AEs was captured as one‐off utility decrements to those patients who experienced the AE. The decrements associated with HHF were assumed to apply for 1 year, and decrements associated with AEs for 1 month (Supporting Information, Table S9 ).

Analytical approach

The base‐case analysis comparing empagliflozin + SoC and SoC alone was based on discrete health states defined by KCCQ‐CSS score quartiles. The model tabulated the cumulative number of clinical events experienced by the cohort, event rates per 100 person‐years, life years (LYs), quality‐adjusted life years (QALYs), costs, and incremental cost‐effectiveness ratios (ICERs). The annual discount rate applied to costs and health outcomes in the UK, Spain, and France was 3.5%, 33 3.0%, 34 and 2.5%, 35 respectively. The model was able to reproduce the EMPEROR‐Preserved trial results over the trial‐time period and was used to extrapolate results to a lifetime horizon.

A scenario analysis was explored using the NYHA functional class instead of KCCQ health states, which uses four classes to group HF symptoms based on physical activity level. Although it has been criticized previously, NYHA is a common measure of severity used in clinical practice and trials and has also been used in previous CEMs for HF. 36 , 37 The NYHA functional class demonstrated that most patients were in class II and often did not change their class during lifetime extrapolation. 38 Therefore, the KCCQ‐based approach was preferred for the base‐case analysis, and the health state transitions based on NYHA classes I–IV were included in the model to conduct alternative analysis and compare the model results against the KCCQ‐based approach. Finally, another scenario analysis was performed in which the model horizon was 10 years, in order to explore the cost‐effectiveness of empagliflozin on the shorter term.

One‐way deterministic sensitivity analyses (DSAs) were conducted to assess uncertainty in model parameters and identify potential drivers of the model's results. Probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) was conducted to reflect parameter uncertainty, by varying critical model parameters simultaneously and randomly using probability distributions. A thousand replications were run and the results were presented in the cost‐effectiveness plane and as cost‐effectiveness acceptability curves (CEACs).

Model validation

Several steps have been taken to test the validity of the CEM. The TECHnical VERification (TECH‐VER) checklist has been used to perform ‘black‐box’ tests, that is, ensuring that the model calculations are in line with a priori expectations. 39 The tests have been performed by a researcher that was not involved in the development of the CEM and included confirmation of parameters' plausibility, extreme value tests, and alignment between deterministic and probabilistic results. 39 Furthermore, a comprehensive validation of the observed event rates during the study period (average follow‐up time of 26 months) for HHF, CV death, and non‐CV death from the EMPEROR‐Preserved trial with the model‐predicted event rates over a 26 month time horizon was conducted. The estimates from the CEM predictions fell within the 95% confidence intervals of the observed values and, thus, considered valid (Supporting Information, Figure S2 ).

Compliance with ethics guidelines

This CEM was developed in accordance with the guidelines from the International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research (ISPOR)‐Society for Medical Decision Making on good modelling practices. The CEM is based on existing literature findings and completed clinical trials. Moreover, it does not involve any studies on human participants and animals directly performed by any of the authors.

Results

Base‐case analysis

The total healthcare cost of receiving SoC per patient was £10 092, €15 765, and €14 958 in the UK, Spain, and France, respectively, which increased by £1407, €1148, and €1485, respectively, with the addition of empagliflozin to SoC. Drug acquisition costs were higher in the empagliflozin + SoC arm, reflecting the additional cost of adding empagliflozin therapy to the existing treatment options considered in the SoC arm. Empagliflozin + SoC led to savings in some cost categories, due primarily to averted or delayed HHFs and CV deaths, and fewer AE costs (Table 2 ).

Treatment with empagliflozin + SoC was also associated with increased LYs (0.07 for the UK, and 0.08 for Spain and France) and QALYs (0.095 for the UK, 0.098 for Spain, and 0.096 for France) compared with SoC. For the empagliflozin + SoC arm, patients spent more time in KCCQ‐4 quartile state, which is associated with a higher utility compared with other states, as well as having fewer HHF events and longer survival.

Over a lifetime horizon, treatment with empagliflozin + SoC resulted in higher healthcare costs but with LYs and QALYs gained compared with SoC. Thus, treatment with empagliflozin resulted in an ICER of £14 851, €11 706, and €15 447 for the UK, Spain, and France, respectively (Table 4 ). Scenario analysis

For the scenario analysis using the NYHA functional class, the cost‐effectiveness results were similar to the base‐case analysis. The ICER per QALY gained for empagliflozin + SoC was £15 957, €12 928, and €17 133 for the UK, Spain, and France, respectively (Supporting Information, Table S10 ). For the scenario analysis using 10 years as model's horizon, the cost‐effectiveness results were similar to the base‐case analysis. The ICER per QALY gained for empagliflozin + SoC was £17 510, €14 007, and €19 379 for the UK, Spain, and France, respectively.

Sensitivity analysis

Deterministic sensitivity analysis

For all the three countries, the most impactful parameter on the ICER was the treatment effect of empagliflozin + SoC on HHF. Other impactful parameters included the disutility for HHF, the treatment effect of empagliflozin + SoC on CV mortality, inclusion of treatment discontinuation for empagliflozin + SoC, cost of treatment for HHF, and the KCCQ‐CSS fourth‐quartile utility value (only for France). Results of the PSA are summarized in the cost‐effectiveness plane (Figure 2 ). From the 1000 simulated cost‐and‐effect pairs, 73%, 75%, and 74% fell in the north‐east quadrant of the cost‐effectiveness plane for the UK, Spain, and France, respectively, indicating a high likelihood of empagliflozin + SoC being costlier but more effective than SoC alone in the three countries.

As shown in the CEAC (Figure 2 ), empagliflozin + SoC showed 50% probability of being cost‐effective at a WTP threshold of £16 200 per QALY gained for the UK, €9900 per QALY gained for Spain, and €15 500 per QALY gained for France. At £/€20 000 WTP threshold per QALY gained, treatment with empagliflozin had a 55%, 64%, and 56% probability of being cost‐effective in the UK, Spain, and France, respectively (Figure 2 ).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the cost‐effectiveness of empagliflozin or any other sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitor (SGLT2i), as an add‐on to SoC for the treatment of adult patients with HF LVEF > 40% compared with SoC alone in the UK, Spain, and France. Empagliflozin + SoC was associated with a significant reduction in the number of HHF and CV death, and similar non‐CV death events per 100 patient‐years, compared with SoC. Treatment with empagliflozin + SoC resulted in higher total healthcare costs compared with SoC over the lifetime horizon for the three countries considered. This increase in total cost was largely attributable to the addition of empagliflozin drug cost to the basket of SoC treatments and longer survival and treatment duration of patients treated with empagliflozin. Empagliflozin + SoC was also associated with gains in LYs and QALYs compared with SoC, as expected based on the favourable clinical efficacy of this treatment. Thus, empagliflozin is the first SGLT2i that shows a strong positive effect on clinical outcomes for patients with HF LVEF > 40% but also has a cost‐effective profile, as compared with SoC. This suggests that empagliflozin can fill in the unmet need for evidence‐based, safe, effective, and cost‐effective treatment for patients with HF LVEF > 40%, indicating a potential paradigm change in the treatment of HF.

The ICERs for empagliflozin + SoC compared with SoC were substantially below the commonly accepted WTP thresholds (e.g. £20 000 for NICE, and €20 000 for Spain and France), indicating that empagliflozin should be therefore also considered cost‐effective, where there is additionally no other treatment available, for patients with HF LVEF > 40%. The results of scenario analysis using the NYHA functional class were in line with the base‐case results. The DSA and PSA also showed that the results were largely robust to variations in model inputs.

Previous studies have shown that the economic burden for HF is high. 40 The total lifetime healthcare cost per patient identified in this study was comparable for the three countries considered (UK, Spain, and France). The small differences found in the total costs between the three countries are attributed to country‐specific prices included in the model. This study provided detailed information on which healthcare cost categories were considered and how the total healthcare cost was estimated to improve transparency on the healthcare burden related to HF LVEF > 40%. A systematic review summarizing the evidence on the total economic burden of HF in different countries demonstrated that the cost per patient reported in different studies is highly dependent on the country, follow‐up period, and cost categories included in the estimations. Because of this, the economic burden of HF often varied considerably. 41 However, in our study, we have used the same method to calculate the cost for each country, which showed consistent costs per patient across countries.

There is a lack of evidence on the cost‐effectiveness of SoC treatments for HF LVEF > 40% patients as a consequence that there are no targeted therapies for this indication. Nevertheless, there is some information on the literature about the cost‐effectiveness of empagliflozin, which are in line with the findings of this study. An economic modelling study from the perspective of the Australian healthcare system compared empagliflozin plus SoC to SoC alone among a hypothetical cohort of patients with HF with preserved ejection fraction and showed that empagliflozin had a high probability of being cost‐effective. 42 Furthermore, a recently submitted but unpublished study from the UK, Spain, and France showed empagliflozin to be highly cost‐effective in patients with HFrEF. A cost‐effectiveness analysis (CEA) performed based on the EMPA‐REG OUTCOME trial suggested that empagliflozin is highly cost‐effective in the UK to treat diabetes and high CV disease compared with placebo. 43 , 44 Empagliflozin has also been found cost‐effective in patients with diabetes and CV disease, compared with liraglutide, in Denmark. 45 A previous study has shown that empagliflozin in addition to SoC in HF patients with T2DM was a cost‐effective option for the UK, also providing long‐term health benefits to patients. 46 A recently published CEA showed subcutaneous semaglutide to be a more expensive treatment option compared with empagliflozin among patients with T2DM treated with metformin who required treatment intensification. 47 Overall, our study adds on to the existing evidence regarding the cost‐effectiveness of empagliflozin across various cardio‐metabolic therapy areas, and it is one of the first studies that provides evidence quantifying the healthcare costs associated with the SoC of HF LVEF > 40%.

The analysis has certain strengths. The analysis was based on the patient‐level data of the EMPEROR‐Preserved trial, a robust multinational phase 3 trial with ~6000 patients, which led to the approval of empagliflozin from the European Medicines Agency, the Food and Drug Administration, and other countries for treatment of patients with HF LVEF > 40%. 48 , 49 , 50 Disease progression was modelled using a patient‐reported outcome of KCCQ‐CSS, which is an established measure of health status in HF and a secondary endpoint of the EMPEROR‐Preserved trial, and has been accepted by NICE for two recent HF evaluations. 22 , 51 The equations for survival, HHF, and treatment discontinuation were adjusted in terms of KCCQ‐CSS quartile to capture change in KCCQ score on the health outcomes and distribute groups equally. Finally, the analyses demonstrated consistent results across three key European countries, suggesting that the economic burden is not affected by variations in the financial and organizational structures of the healthcare system and providing assurance of the generalizability of the present findings to a wider European perspective.

The study also has some limitations. First, time‐dependent data for drug utilization could not be obtained from the EMPEROR‐Preserved trial and, thus, drug use at the start of the trial was used. Second, costs of recognized but relatively mild AEs and low cost (e.g. polyuria and episodes of dehydration) associated with the use of SGLT2is were not incorporated into the model. These events generally lead to short episodes of care and low management costs. Therefore, they are expected to have only minimal impact on healthcare costs that would not significantly affect the findings. Also, the need to extrapolate long‐term clinical outcomes from a typical phase 3 not long‐term trial data (the median follow‐up time was 26 months in the EMPEROR‐Preserved trial) introduced some uncertainty. Though this kind of extrapolation based on phase 3 trials is common, no reliable external data source was available for estimating the rate of clinical events beyond the trial‐time period as these outcomes are closely tied to underlying SoC. However, sensitivity analyses varying the distribution of the risk equations did not affect the cost‐effectiveness of empagliflozin + SoC compared with SoC, even though there was some variation in the incremental health benefits. Finally, the analysis was conducted based on direct medical costs and the indirect costs (i.e. productivity loss) were not considered given the perspective of this analysis.

In conclusion, empagliflozin is shown to be the first targeted treatment option that is both clinically effective and cost‐effective for patients with HF LVEF > 40%. This is largely due to its impact on reducing HHF and CV deaths compared with the current SoC. Prescribing empagliflozin with SoC to patients with HF LVEF > 40% is expected to improve clinical outcomes and patients' quality of life, substantially within established WTP thresholds for the healthcare systems in the UK, Spain, and France.
