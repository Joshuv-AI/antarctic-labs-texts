Explaining the care process by companion midwife during childbirth and providing strategies to improve the companion midwife regulations: a study protocol
Authors: Narges Sadat Soltani, Samira Ebrahimzadeh Zagami, Leili Hafizi, Nahid Jahani Shoorab
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe World Health Organization emphasizes the importance of having a birth companion present to enhance care outcomes and satisfaction of mothers during labor and delivery. However, the care process provided by a companion midwife during childbirth is not well understood and existing challenges have not been identified. This study will be conducted with the aim of explaining the care process by companion midwives during childbirth and providing strategies to improve companion midwife regulations.MethodsThis qualitative study will use a grounded theory approach. The main participants will be companion midwives. Sampling will start with purposive sampling and continue through theoretical sampling based on emerging categories until data saturation is achieved. Data will be collected through in-depth, semi-structured interviews, field notes, and memos. Data analysis will follow the Strauss and Corbin (2015) method, which includes five steps: (1) identification of concepts (2), development of concepts based on their characteristics and dimensions (3), contextual data analysis (4), incorporation of the process into the analysis, and (5) integration of categories. In the second stage of the project, using the data extracted from the qualitative section, existing literature and the nominal group technique steps, solutions will be presented to improve the companion midwife program to be presented to policy makers and planners.DiscussionThis study is expected to contribute to a better understanding of care by companion midwives. Because, will identify the obstacles and facilitators of the implementation of the companion midwifery program in maternity settings.Ethical codeIR.MUMS.NURSE.REC.1402.130.Keywords: Companion midwife, Childbirth, Care, Process, Strategy

Background

Childbirth is an important life event because it not only signifies the beginning of a new life, but the process of birth itself holds great significance [1]. One successful and promising policy that promotes natural childbirth, improves pregnancy outcomes and enhances women’s experiences, is care by a doula [2]. This can be considered one of the most positive childbirth interventions in the last 20 years [3]. Care by a doula is a potential strategy to reduce unnecessary medical interventions during childbirth [4] and is an important yet underutilized resource in improving maternal health equity [5]. The term “doula” originates from Greek, meaning “female servant“ [6, 7]. Doula care includes emotional support (constant presence, reassurance, encouragement and praise), physical support (addressing thirst, hunger and pain), providing information about the birth process and coping mechanisms, respecting the woman’s decisions and assisting in establishing relationships with other caregivers [8, 9]. A doula does not perform any medical interventions in the delivery room and does not replace the role of the spouse or other family members who may be present. However, she provides continuous and uninterrupted emotional and physical support during labor. This continuity of care, provision of human presence and social support are unique to the role of a doula, distinguishing it from any other support model [10]. The World Health Organization (2020) has reported varying percentages of national policies or guidelines for the presence of a chosen companion during labor and birth in different countries without referring to the presence of a doula or companion in births in different regions of the world. The countries with the highest percentage of national guidelines for childbirth are America, Southeast Asia and Europe respectively with 76%, 73% and 72%, while Eastern Mediterranean countries have the least guidelines for attendance during childbirth that were reported as 19%. The World Health Organization emphasizes the presence of companions in childbirth for three reasons: creating pleasant experiences of pregnancy and childbirth, increasing the number of natural births, and improving the health of mothers and babies [11]. Iran is one of the few countries in the Eastern Mediterranean that has compiled and implemented regulations under the title of the regulatory regulation of companion midwife in 2009 (http://irmidwifery.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/4_5823522972412937019.pdf). In Iran, a doula is interpreted as a companion midwife, and with the conducted investigations, no documented statistics were found on the presence of companion midwives, and most of the studies done, investigated the effect of companion midwife on the outcome of childbirth [12–14]. The results of a meta-analysis (1999) conducted on 11 randomized clinical trials showed that continuous support during labor and delivery by trained individuals (doulas) led to a decrease in the duration of labor, reduced use of painkillers and oxytocin, decreased use of forceps and cesarean section [15]. The presence of a birth doula also has positive effects on women’s mental health during childbirth and postpartum, including reducing maternity anxiety, increasing self-confidence during childbirth, and decreasing postpartum depression [16]. Doulas are also people who help the mother start breastfeeding and build a bond with her baby [17]. Despite the confirmation of the positive role of the companion midwife on the outcomes of childbirth and the promotion of natural childbirth in Iran [12–14] and the emphasis of the World Health Organization (2020) on the fact that supporting women to have an elected or companion during labor and childbirth is a low-cost and effective intervention [11], but in Iran, despite the legality of the companion midwife in hospitals, it is not possible for a companion midwife to be present in all educational hospitals, and even their entry is prevented. According to the statements of Mohammad Khani, a member of the board of directors of the Tehran Medical System in May 1401, due to the conflict between the interests of midwives and gynecologists, regulation of companion midwives is not implemented in some hospitals [18]. Ahlemeyer and Mahon (2015) also reported that sometimes doula’s role as a member of the healthcare team in the hospital environment leads to a darkening of the relationship between midwives and doulas due to confusion about roles, motives and perspectives [19].

McLeish et al. (2017), in a qualitative phenomenological study exploring the experiences of doulas and mothers and how doulas communicate with midwives in the UK, identified three main themes: “doula as a complement,” “doula as a colleague,” and “doula as a challenge for midwives” [20]. Adams et al. (2021) reported in their study that the doula–physician relationship is a complex narrative involving power dynamics, mutual respect, and reciprocal responses. Since doulas hold no therapeutic role and adopt a holistic approach to birth, they may inadvertently create conflicts in the delivery room. The researchers emphasize that, given the importance of implementing continuous support programs for mothers, understanding the perceptions of midwives and obstetricians — and their relationships with doulas — constitutes a critical area of research, as these perceptions and roles directly influence how they interact with one another [21].

Care is a phenomenon that is expressed and experienced differently across cultures. Leininger describes care as a complex phenomenon encompassing 12 common dimensions: respect, concern, attention, help, active listening, presence, understanding, communication, protection, touch, comfort, and filial love [22]. Care provided by a companion midwife or doula aligns with the physio-social midwifery model — or, according to Wagner, “humanized care.” Companion midwives or doulas respect women’s autonomy and enhance their empowerment, confidence, and self-efficacy during childbirth [18].

Although the model of doula care is relatively well-defined, the actual process of care delivered by a doula (or companion midwife) remains unclear. Measuring care processes is a crucial component of efforts to improve the quality of care, as care systems may vary not only internationally but also regionally [23]. This study will therefore attempt to delineate the process of care provided by the companion midwife.

Among various qualitative research methodologies, grounded theory is employed when the phenomenon is socially embedded and the goal is to uncover social processes hidden within human interactions [24]. Moreover, grounded theory is among the most suitable methodological approaches for exploring less-understood areas related to social processes in human interactions [25].

Study aim

The process of care by Companion midwives during childbirth in Iran is not well understood and explained. By obtaining the results of this study, we can gain a deep insight into the experiences of Companion midwives in the delivery room and how they interact with obstetricians and midwives working in the maternity hospital. Therefore, the current research will be designed with the aim of explaining the process of care by Companion midwives during childbirth using the grounded theory method.

Methods/design

To conduct this study, the researcher will utilize a qualitative approach based on the grounded theory of Corbin and Strauss (2015). The aim is to understand and explain the care process provided by Companion midwives during childbirth, focusing on their interactions with other midwives, gynecologists, and pregnant women. The methodology will involve unstructured observations and open, in-depth interviews to gain a comprehensive understanding of the participants’ experiences.

This study seeks to discover the care process of Companion midwife during childbirth, which is affected by their interaction with the surrounding environment (doctor, midwife, ward officials, pregnant mother and her family. Upon receiving approval from the ethics committee and securing sampling permissions, the researcher will conduct interviews in Mashhad midwifery consulting offices, public and private hospitals, or other locations preferred by the participants.

To ensure data saturation, purposive sampling with maximum variation will be applied. For Companion Midwives, variation will be sought across key dimensions: age, years of experience, employment status, education level, and workplace setting (public vs. private hospitals). For pregnant women admitted to maternity wards, variation will be considered in terms of Number of pregnancy, type of delivery, duration of labor, birth agent. Midwives and hospital officials will be sampled according to their professional roles and positions.

Prior to each formal interview, the researcher will provide participants with a clear explanation of the study’s objectives and seek their informed consent. Once consent is obtained, a mutually convenient time and location for the interview will be arranged. Additionally, with prior coordination, the researcher will observe Companion Midwives during their presence in maternity wards or consulting offices. At times, the researcher will also enter the maternity ward under the role of a clinical trainer for midwifery students, participating in morning and evening shifts to facilitate naturalistic observation.

The researcher will aim to build trust with the participants by actively participating in midwifery procedures and patient care, while also observing the process. To document observations in the field, the researcher will take notes on key points, including the time and date, and will complete detailed notes promptly. If any questions arise during the observation, the researcher will seek answers through interviews and informal conversations. Key informants will be selected based on their relevance to the research objectives. Data collection will persist until a comprehensive description of the phenomenon of continuous care by Companion midwives is achieved.

The primary method of data collection in this research will be semi-structured in-depth interviews using open-ended questions. To enhance the depth and completeness of the interviews and gather richer data based on participants’ responses, exploratory and investigative expressions will be utilized. Interviews will be conducted once or multiple times, depending on environmental, time, and other factors. The length of the interviews will be determined by the interviewee’s willingness to continue and fully express their ideas.

Each interview will commence with a general question tailored to the type of participant. For instance, Companion midwives will be asked, “Tell me about your experience as a Companion midwife?” As the research progresses, questions will become more specific to delve deeper into specific concepts. Questions such as, “What kind of care do you provide to mothers in the delivery room?” or “What are your experiences collaborating with midwives and specialists?”

Throughout the interview, clarifying and probing questions will be used to further explore the participant’s responses. For example, questions like “Tell me more about this…” or “What do you mean by…” will be asked. Participants will also be given the opportunity to share any additional information or points they feel are important.

At the conclusion of the interview, participants will be encouraged to mention any topics they may have missed or wish to revisit. They will also be informed that they can reach out if they recall anything post-interview or have a change of perspective.

From other contributors Questions will also be asked. For example, the question to hospitalized mothers will be like this: “How was your experience of giving birth with a Companion midwife?” Or the specialists or midwives working in the hospital will be asked this question, “How are your experiences with the presence of a Companion midwife?”

These types of general questions allow participants to freely describe their understanding and experience. Questions in this field will also be asked of family members and other individuals who will likely be interviewed based on theoretical sampling. Another tool for data collection in this study will be field notes, used to document observations and data analysis. Observation is a suitable complementary method for collecting data and explaining unspoken behaviors. Observing behavior in the field provides an opportunity for the researcher to identify and understand the context that leads to its occurrence [26]. The researcher will take notes on what he sees, hears, thinks, and experiences (such as body language, facial expressions, etc.), which cannot be recorded through sound recording, in field notes. These notes will be recorded at the first opportunity and later used to inform the interview process. Additionally, any ideas that come to the researcher’s mind while reading interviews or codes will be written down. Interviews will be recorded using a tape recorder, with written permission obtained from participants before their voices are recorded. Immediately following the interviews, they will be transcribed word for word from the tape recorder. It may be necessary to modify the interview guide as the research progresses and categories are developed.

Data analysis

In this study, data collection and analysis will be conducted simultaneously from the beginning of the research. The interviews with the participants will be listened to multiple times to gain a general understanding. Subsequently, the interviews will be transcribed word for word. The data will be managed and organized using MAXQDA software version 2010. For data analysis, the coding paradigm of Strauss and Corbin (2015) will be utilized.

Due to the more systematic method of coding and analysis, as well as the importance of clarity of the research question, attention to the role of the researcher, and personal interest, the researcher has chosen to use the Corbin and Strauss (2015) method. Therefore, in this study, content analysis of the interviews will involve a continuous process of data comparison. This process will include five steps: identification of concepts, development of concepts based on their characteristics and dimensions, analysis of data for context, bringing the process into the analysis, and integration of categories [27].

Four criteria are used to evaluate the trustworthiness of the collected data based on Lincoln and Guba’s Criteria (1985) (Credibility, Dependability, Confirmability, Transferability ([28, 29].

Sample size and sampling method

In Grounded Theory, purposeful sampling (based on purpose) is used because the goal is to choose people who are a rich source of information. This allows them to actively participate in the study and help the researcher better understand their life and social interactions [27]. In qualitative research, it is not possible to determine the sample size beforehand; it is determined during the study. The end of sampling occurs when no new classes or suitable themes emerge, and the main classes are sufficiently developed in depth and variability [30]. In the present study, theoretical sampling is a part of the data collection and analysis process. It is used to fill the classes and develop the theory, continuing until saturation is reached.

In present study, data collection commenced on September 22, 2024 and will continue until data saturation is achieved. The researcher anticipates that data collection will be completed by January 31, 2025, based on the projected timeline and estimated sample size required for in-depth analysis. However, the final cessation of data collection will be determined qualitatively based on the attainment of thematic saturation, in accordance with qualitative research standards.

The second stage of the study (providing solutions based on the results obtained)

Researchers will utilize data from the qualitative section and other relevant studies to enhance Companion midwife regulations. The nominal group technique will be employed to gather input from experts in related fields, whose knowledge is pertinent to the issue at hand and aligns with the qualitative findings.

The nominal group technique is a structured approach that facilitates expert opinion sharing. This method ensures equal participation among all members, preventing any individual from dominating the group [31]. It involves problem identification, solution generation, and decision-making processes. Unlike a typical group discussion, this technique aims to achieve consensus among participants [32].

The steps of the nominal group technique include: initiating the meeting, silently generating and recording ideas individually, sharing and discussing ideas in a sequential manner, voting to prioritize key ideas, and deliberating on the selected ideas [32, 33].

Discussion

The review of the conducted research shows that there are no comprehensive qualitative studies, especially with the grounded theory approach, about the care process by a doula or companion midwife. The studies carried out are mainly in other countries and have been conducted only to examine the limited experiences of doulas without considering the background conditions and social background. In the limited studies that have been conducted using the qualitative method, the focus is on experiences rather than the discovery of the care process of doulas and companion midwives. These studies have examined some aspects of the perception and experience of mothers and midwives regarding doulas, but other individuals who interact with doulas or companion midwives have been less investigated. Obtaining the opinions of other people involved with doulas, such as gynecologists, department officials, expectant mothers, and their families, is also important [8, 20, 21, 34]. Considering the dominant role that obstetricians and gynecologists play in maternity care in many countries, this is an important area that needs more attention. In some studies, interviews were conducted only with midwives who had worked with doulas, and the opinions of other midwives were not taken into account [35].

The method of data collection in most studies was only interviews [8, 20, 21, 34, 35], even some interviews were conducted by telephone [20]. While, in order to fully investigate the interactions between individuals, observation is also necessary. Furthermore, many studies conducted abroad have focused on vulnerable women, such as immigrants and black individuals [20, 34]. In some studies, professional doulas were not utilized. Instead, individuals who were willing to become doulas for pregnant mothers were trained and then included in the study [10, 20]. Professional doulas were also people who did not have a university education, but had completed courses and obtained a doula certificate. In contrast, in Iran, a doula is considered a Companion midwife who holds a university degree in midwifery and has completed additional courses on topics like physiological childbirth and pain relief methods during labor. Therefore, it is true that she does the work of a doula, but she may be slightly different from a doula due to her knowledge of the birth process and her education, her experiences and understanding, and the challenges that may occur in her interactions. Therefore, it is important to monitor the care provided by Companion midwives closely.

In Iran, few studies have been conducted on birth attendants, mostly focusing on the benefits and advantages of using them [12, 14]. This highlights the importance of research in the present study, as no qualitative study has been conducted in Iran regarding this topic. Additionally, no qualitative study was found that examines the care process of a doula (Companion midwife).

In Grand Theory, observation is used alongside interviews to collect data, making it essential to observe people’s interactions. In the present study, interviews will be conducted with all individuals who interact with Companion midwife in any capacity, including ward officials, midwives, pregnant mothers, their families, as well as provincial and national officials.

Regarding providing a solution, no study has mentioned a written solution to improve the care provided by a doula (Companion midwife). Despite midwife regulations being available in Iran since 2009, they are not uniformly implemented in all hospitals. Therefore, the researcher aims to examine the care process provided by Companion midwives in Iran using Grounded Theory. By identifying existing challenges, the study will provide a solution to improve regulations using the nominal group technique.

This study is expected to enhance understanding of care by Companion midwives, identifying barriers and facilitators in maternity settings. The findings will assist policymakers and national managers in implementing Companion midwife program, which will not only benefit mothers through continuous support but also improve natural childbirth and delivery.
