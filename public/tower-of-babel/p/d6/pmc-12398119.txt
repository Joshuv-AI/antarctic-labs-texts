Comparison of chest radiograph and surface measurement to predict cavoatrial junction position of totally implantable venous-access port
Authors: Meng-ling Li, Di-tian Zhang, Peng-fei Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccurately predicting cavoatrial junction (CAJ) position is important for totally implantable venous-access port (TIVAP) application, which could reduce complications.MethodsClinical information of 117 breast cancer (BRCA) patients who underwent TIVAP implantation was collected. The length of the implanted catheter was determined by a chest radiograph method in the test group, as follows: total catheter insertion length was L, and the distance from the pre-puncture point to the right sternoclavicular joint was measured as L1; the distance from the right sternoclavicular joint to 1.5 vertebral bodies under the trachea carina in the chest radiograph was measured as L2; the preset catheter length was L = L1 + L2. The length of the implanted catheter was determined by a surface measurement method in the control group, as follows: total length of catheter insertion was L; the distance from the pre-puncture point to the right sternoclavicular joint was measured as L1; the distance from the third anterior intercostal space of the right sternoclavicular joint was measured on the body surface as L2; the preset catheter length was L = L1 + L2. CAJ positioning rate and complication rate were compared between the two method groups. Logistic regression analysis, receiver operating characteristic, and decision curve analysis were performed to evaluate the predictive value of the chest radiograph.ResultsFor BRCA patients, the chest radiograph was exhibited more accurately in predicting CAJ position. Subgroup analysis indicated a remarkably higher CAJ position rate in the chest radiograph method group regardless of age, while no significant difference between the two measurement groups for patients with BMI > 24.9 kg/m2 was observed.ConclusionThe chest radiograph method could more effectively predict the CAJ position than the surface measurement in the overall cohort. However, subgroup analysis revealed that this advantage was not statistically significant in patients with BMI > 24.9 kg/m2, suggesting reduced efficacy in high-BMI populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03150-x.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BRCA) constitutes the predominant malignancy among women worldwide, comprising 24.2% of all female cancer cases, and with a higher prevalence of 52.9% in developing nations [1]. Annually, about 300,000 women are diagnosed with BRCA in China [2]. Multi-course chemotherapy remains an essential arsenal in treatment, employing primary agents such as anthracyclines and taxanes [3]. However, the potent irritative nature of these chemotherapeutic agents predisposes patients to complications like peripheral phlebitis, vascular sclerosis, and in cases of drug extravasation even leads to severe local tissue necrosis [4–6], which escalates patient distress and healthcare expenditure. Thus, ensuring safe and precise chemotherapeutic delivery is of paramount importance.

Totally implantable venous-access port (TIVAP) has been widely used in tumor chemotherapy. TIVAP, a fully subcutaneous infusion system, consists of a catheter residing in the central vein and an infusion chamber surgically embedded below the dermal layer [7]. Its design permits long-term intracorporeal retention while remaining concealed beneath the skin [8]. The device has garnered widespread adoption for tumor chemotherapy, assuring secure and enduring venous access for patients necessitating prolonged infusion therapy [9]. By channeling medication directly into the central vein, TIVAP circumvents the aforementioned adverse effects [8]. Originating in the United States in 1982 with surgical implantation via the jugular vein, the method has since evolved towards minimally invasive interventional techniques, gaining traction in clinical settings due to its associated low infection risk, ease of maintenance, and extended device longevity [10, 11].

The position of the catheter tip within the infusion port is critical for operational safety [12]. Accumulated clinical evidence underscores a strong correlation between catheter tip placement and the emergence of complications such as occlusions, catheter-related thrombosis, dislodgement, phlebitis, and arrhythmias [13]. Arrigo reported that primary or secondary malposition of the catheter tip was associated with an elevated thrombosis risk [14]. In a retrospective analysis of 5,400 cases, Pittiruti et al. compared the complications (such as arterial puncture and pneumothorax) under different insertion methods [15]. Contemporary studies and expert consensus advocate for the catheter tip’s optimal placement at the cavoatrial junction (CAJ) [13]. Hence, pinpointing the TIVAP catheter tip’s accurate placement at the CAJ has garnered considerable attention.

Current methodologies for catheter tip placement encompass surface measurement, which estimates catheter length by measuring from the puncture site to the third intercostal space, and the preoperative chest radiograph method, which determines catheter length through preoperative imaging [16, 17]. This study proposes a comparative analysis between the preoperative surface measurement and the chest radiograph positioning methodologies for predicting CAJ position. The objective is to delineate a more refined approach to infusion port implantation, minimizing port-related complications, enhancing the device’s lifespan, and safeguarding infusion port utilization.

Methods

Patients

From February 2023 to March 2024, female BRCA patients who underwent TIVAP implantation in the Fujian Longyan First Hospital were included in this study according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria as follows: inclusion criteria: [1] the age is 18–75 years old; [2] the infusion port is placed for the first time (via the right internal jugular vein approach); [3] the catheter can be maintained regularly in our hospital; [4] the illness requires the establishment of an infusion port channel. Exclusion criteria: [1] patients with absolute contraindication of indwelling catheter in infusion port; [2] patients with coagulation dysfunction or platelet count < 20 × 109/L; [3] patients with severe infection; [4] patients with right BRCA after an operation, right cervical lymph node swelling and compression, and right neck and chest with recent radiotherapy plan; [5] chest radiograph examination has not been conducted before catheterization; [6] the imaging signs needed to be observed on the chest radiograph are vague and difficult to distinguish; [7] thoracic deformity; [8] patients who have undergone thoracotomy before; [9] patients with heart diseases, such as valvular heart disease, atrial fibrillation, pulmonary heart disease or patients who may affect the change of P wave after cardiac surgery. Besides, patients were randomly divided into a test group receiving chest radiograph method (n = 59) and a control group receiving surface measurement (n = 58). Patients were dynamically randomized using the minimization method with stratification by age (≤ 50 years vs. >50 years) and BMI (< 25 kg/m2 vs. ≥25 kg/m2). The allocation sequence was generated by an independent statistician. Group assignment was concealed until after enrollment.

Ethical approval and informed consent

The study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee, and written informed consent was provided by all patients before the operation.

Procedures of TIVAP implantation

In the test group, the preoperative chest radiograph positioning method was used to predict the catheter insertion length in the infusion port: the total catheter insertion length was L, the distance from the pre-puncture point to the right sternoclavicular joint was measured as L1, and the distance from the right sternoclavicular joint to 1.5 vertebral bodies under the trachea carina in the chest radiograph was measured as L2, and the preset catheter length was L = L1 + L2 (Fig. 1A) [1]. local anesthesia at puncture point, subcutaneous tunnel and subcutaneous capsule; [2] puncture the internal jugular vein under the guidance of ultrasound, remove the syringe after blood return, and send the guide wire for 15–20 cm; [3] remove the puncture needle, and then fix the guide wire; [4] incision of the capsular bag, the width of the incision is about 2.5 cm, and the depth is cut to the fascia of pectoralis major, and the distance from the skin is about 0.5 ~ 1 cm; [5] bluntly separate the subcutaneous tissue of the capsular bag, with the bottom about 5 cm away from the incision; [6] make a 0.5 cm transverse incision at the puncture point, confirm that the catheter sheath is firmly connected with the dilator, and then slowly rotate along the guide wire and push it into the blood vessel, leaving more than 2 cm outside; [7] release that locking device of the catheter sheath and the dilator assembly, and simultaneously remove the guide wire and the dilator; [8] feed the catheter to a pre-measured length L through a catheter sheath hemostasis valve, and position the catheter tip position in combination with ECG during the operation; [9] remove the catheter sheath, and you can withdraw blood, that is, determine whether the catheter is in the blood vessel, and clamp the back end of the catheter; [10] subcutaneous tunnel: push the tip of the tunnel needle from the venipuncture site to the incision of the capsular bag; [11] connect the rear end of the catheter to the thread opening of the barb of the tunnel needle to ensure that the catheter completely covers the thread opening; [12] slowly pull the catheter out of the subcutaneous tunnel to the incision of the capsular bag through the tunnel needle to ensure that the catheter does not slip out; [13] adjust the catheter through the small incision at the puncture point, determine the correct length of the catheter entering the blood vessel through the scale, and locate the tip position in combination with ECG during the operation; [14] cut the catheter to an appropriate length at 90, leaving enough length to cope with the movement of the body and connect the injection base; [15] sleeve the catheter lock into the catheter, ensure that one end of the developing line faces the end connected with the injection base, push the catheter to connect the injection base connecting handle to just pass through the second joint, and horizontally deduce the catheter lock until it is flush with the injection base; [16] put the injection seat into the position of the bag and adjust the catheter; [17] use a non-invasive puncture needle to connect the syringe, percutaneously puncture the diaphragm of the injection seat, withdraw the blood for confirmation, and inject 20mL of normal saline to confirm that the position is correct and there is no blockage or leakage, and then insert the needle to fix the port body after operation; [18] skin cleaning, incision suture and dressing; [19] record the operation information, catheter implantation length and chest X-ray catheter position in the maintenance manual of implantable drug delivery device for later maintenance; [20] take a chest X-ray to confirm the catheter tip position within 24 h after TIVAP implantation. All chest radiographs were acquired using a standardized protocol: (patient positioning: supine posteroanterior position; fixed imaging parameters: 121.0 kV, 1.75 mAs).

Evaluating indicators

CAJ positioning rate

Radiographic markers of the tip position of the catheter on the X-ray chest film. The upper segment of the superior vena cava (SVC): from the upper border of the origin of the right main bronchus to a rib height within the range of the right main bronchus. The middle segment of the SVC: overlaps with the position of the right main bronchus. The lower segment of the SVC: from below the right main bronchus to 1.5-2 vertebral body units below the carina, with reference to the upper right border of the cardiac silhouette. CAJ: 3–4 cm below the carina, equivalent to a range of 1.5-2 vertebral body units (vertebra + intervertebral disc), regarding the upper right border of the cardiac silhouette. Right atrium: below the junction of the SVC and the atrium and not exceeding the midline. Right ventricle: below the junction of the SVC and the atrium and extending beyond the midline. Radiology experts assess and record the position of the catheter tip on each chest film according to the above criteria, and record the number of cases in which the catheter tip is located in the -S segment, S segment, and + S segment. S segment: the catheter tip is located in the lower 1/3 segment of the SVC (including CAJ), defined as accurate positioning; -S segment: above the S segment (including the upper-middle segment of the SVC and the brachiocephalic vein segment), defined as catheter too short; +S segment: below the S segment (including the right atrium and right ventricle), defined as catheter too long. For clear interpretation of brachiocephalic vein: This segment encompasses the left brachiocephalic vein (which courses anterior to the aortic arch) and the right brachiocephalic vein (which descends more vertically). These veins serve as the primary tributaries of the SVC, ultimately converging to form the SVC. Anatomical Characteristics: (1) Relatively smaller caliber: The brachiocephalic veins exhibit a significantly narrower lumen compared to the distal (lower) segment of the SVC. (2) Reduced blood flow velocity: Hemodynamic flow within these veins is slower than that observed in the distal SVC. (3) Relatively superficial location: They are situated in proximity to the thoracic inlet. (4) Greater mobility: These veins, particularly the left brachiocephalic vein as it traverses the aortic arch, possess a degree of mobility influenced by respiratory movements and neck motion. (5) Branching patterns and angulation: The junction where the brachiocephalic veins merge into the SVC may exhibit angulation. Furthermore, the left brachiocephalic vein itself demonstrates an inherent curvature as it crosses the mediastinum.

Catheter malposition rate

Catheter malposition is one of the common early complications of TIVAP. Insufficient catheter depth, that is, the catheter not reaching CAJ, can increase the risk of catheter malposition, kinking, or bending, especially in patients with chronic cough, constipation, and frequent vigorous activities. As the catheter tip is located in other veins outside the superior vena cava, it may affect venous blood flow or the direction of fluid infusion during infusion, leading to local discomfort symptoms in patients. It can also cause turbulent flow within the blood vessels and increase the risk of thrombus formation. Statistical analysis

Continuous variables with non-normal distribution were reported as the median [interquartile range], while some normally distributed continuous variables were reported as mean (± standard deviation). Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies accompanied by corresponding percentages. The disparities of continuous variables between the two groups were assessed utilizing the Mann-Whitney U test or Student’s t-test. The discrepancies in categorical variables were analyzed employing the Chi-square test. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistical significance. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 22.0 software and R (version 4.3.1). We performed power analysis using G*power software version 3.1.9.7 based on the sample size (59 samples in the test group and 58 samples in the control group), alpha level of 0.01, and power level of 0.95, yielding an effect size of 0.791.

Multicollinearity refers to the situation in a linear regression model where, due to the exact or high correlation among explanatory variables, the model estimation is distorted or difficult to estimate accurately. We performed collinearity analysis to evaluate multicollinearity among variables, and those with a variance inflation factor (VIF) > 5 were considered to be collinear [18]. To evaluate the clinical value of the chest radiograph, we performed logistic regression analysis, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) with area under the curve (AUC), and decision curve analysis (DCA).

Results

Participant characteristics

The baseline characteristics of BRCA patients receiving TIVAP are shown in Table 1. For all patients, the mean age of patients was 49.291 years. They had a mean height of 156.316 cm and a median weight of 55.000 kg (mean BMI, 22.858 kg/m2). Regarding location in CAJ, the proportion of cases was significantly higher in the test group (66.102%) than in the control group (31.034%) (P < 0.001). As for the occurrence rate of thrombosis, the test group had a slightly lower proportion than the control group (22.034% vs. 27.586%). In addition, due to the limited number of patients experiencing malposition (2 patients) in the control group, and the absence of such an event in the test group, this variable was not included in the analysis. These findings suggested that the test group exhibited a more accurate rate for CAJ prediction and a lower incidence of complications.

Chest radiograph method independently predicted CAJ

To determine the independent value of measurement, the multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed. Since L was calculated by L1 + L2, and BMI was calculated by weight (kg)/height2 (m2), L1, L2, height, and weight were removed to avoid collinearity and potential bias in this analysis. The variable L is defined as the exact sum of L1 and L2. Similarly, BMI is calculated directly from weight and height. This creates deterministic, perfect linear relationships among these variables within the dataset. If L, L1, and L2 were all included in the same regression model, they would exhibit perfect multicollinearity because L is completely redundant given L1 and L2 (or vice versa). Including both BMI and its components weight and height would also result in perfect or extreme near-perfect multicollinearity. For validation, we performed collinearity analysis. Initial collinearity analysis of L, L1, L2, height, weight, and BMI showed extremely high VIF values, confirming multicollinearity. Exclusion of L1, L2, height, and weight resolved this issue, with the retained variables (L and BMI) showing VIF values below 5 (Table S1). As shown in Table 2, the group was the only independent factor for predicting the position of CAJ (P < 0.05). Compared with the control group, the chest radiograph method in the test group was more likely to be localized in the CAJ position (odds ratio = 4.263, P < 0.001).

Subgroup analysis

To examine heterogeneity in more depth, subgroup analyses were conducted. From Table 3, the location of CAJ was accurately assessed for 57.143% and 33.333% of patients with BMI > 24.9 kg/m2 in test and control groups, respectively (P > 0.05). Besides, the occurrence ratio of thrombosis was 14.286% in the test group, less than that of the control group (33.333%). Among patients ≤ 24.9 kg/m2, the location of CAJ accounted for 68.889% in the test group, superior to the control group (30.435%) (P < 0.05). However, the difference in thrombosis rate was not significantly different between the two groups with a test vs. control ratio of 24.444% vs. 26.084% (Table 4).

ROC and DCA curves

To confirm the predictive value, we conducted ROC and DCA analyses. The AUC value of the group (test vs. control) was 0.693 in predicting the CAJ position in all participants (Fig. 2A). The corresponding DCA result is shown in Fig. 2B. The AUCs were 0.710 and 0.619 for CAJ prediction in the BMI ≤ 24.9 kg/m2 group and BMI > 24.9 kg/m2 group, respectively (Fig. 2C and Fig. 2E). In addition, the group (test vs. control) presented a better net benefits for patients with BMI ≤ 24.9 kg/m2 group than the BMI > 24.9 kg/m2 group (Fig. 2D and Fig. 2F). These revealed that the group (test vs. control) performed well in predicting CAJ position, particularly in those with BMI ≤ 24.9 kg/m2 group.

Discussion

The escalating number of cancer patients has led to an increase in chemotherapy prescriptions, thereby driving the demand for TIVAPs [19]. Nonetheless, misplacement of the catheter tip remains a persistent issue [20]. Complications arising from catheter misplacement encompass the proximity of the catheter tip to the vein wall, impeding infusion/aspiration, inducing venous thrombosis or perforation, and complications from over-insertion of the catheter tip, such as malignant arrhythmias, hemothorax, pleural effusion, damage to the right heart valves, or even the walls of the right atrium or ventricle, mediastinal fluid accumulation, and cardiac tamponade [21, 22]. Herein, we compared the accuracy of CAJ position prediction and complication occurrence rate between the chest radiograph and surface measurement groups.

The important highlight of this study was the superior value of the chest radiograph positioning method in predicting the location of CAJ than the surface measurement method in the overall cohort. Compared to traditional surgical methods, real-time image guidance can enhance the technical success rate of venous access ports and reduce the incidence of surgical-related complications [23]. However, the effectiveness was influenced by some clinical factors. Wallace et al. found that the positional catheter tip movement was positively related to BMI [24]. Besides, after controlling for covariates, Larson et al. found that the probability of false-positive results on chest radiographs increased with the rise in BMI categories [25]. Further analysis revealed that visceral fat is one of the primary causes of image noise in chest radiography results [26]. Subgroup analysis showed no significant association of group (test vs. control) with CAJ position among patients with high BMI. However, the group (test vs. control) demonstrated a better efficacy in patients with BMI ≤ 24.9 kg/m2 group than in the all-population group, followed by BMI > 24.9 kg/m2 group. Similarly, Sahinkaya et al. found that the accuracy of Peres’ height formula in predicting CAJ position significantly decreased in patients with high BMI [27]. The authors speculated that high BMI may affect predictive efficacy through factors such as the accumulation of chest fat.

Except for BMI, age is also considered as an important factor for mammographic density [28]. A retrospective study found that calcification in locations such as the pleura significantly increases with age in individuals over 50 years old [29], which may affect the results of the chest radiograph. In children, a similar correlation was also found between image noise and age [30]. Moreover, Qiao et al. observed that the morphology of the aorta and the relative distances between its branches change with age [31]. This could lead to deviations in traditional surface landmark-based localization in the elderly population. However, our results found that the predictive values of the chest radiograph method were significantly better than surface measurement methods in both young and old patients. It suggested that the position of CAJ may not be influenced by age, and the impact of imaging noise caused by aging on the determination of CAJ position is not significant.

Moreover, the observed difference in thrombosis rates between the test and control groups (22.034% vs. 27.586%; absolute risk reduction: 5.552%; relative risk reduction: 20.126%) carries substantial clinical implications. This finding suggests that the preoperative chest radiograph method may mitigate the thrombosis risk, a major cause of catheter dysfunction and treatment interruption. Patients with BRCA inherently face elevated thrombotic risks due to malignancy-associated hypercoagulability, chemotherapy-induced endothelial injury, and foreign body reactions. Given the critical consequences of thrombotic events (e.g., mandatory anticoagulation, chemotherapy delays, and increased healthcare burdens), adopting the preoperative radiography method could enhance treatment safety and continuity, particularly in high-risk subgroups. While further validation is warranted, these results underscore CAJ-optimized catheter tip positioning as a key strategy to reduce device-related morbidity.

For strengths, the power analysis calculated with G*power confirmed that the study (sample size, effect size, alpha level, and power level) was well-designed to increase the evidence level of our study. In addition to comparing the rate of CAJ position and complication between the test and control group, we validated the predictive of the method using ROC and DCA curves to select key sub-populations, providing a direction for studying the role of the chest radiograph method. For limitations, the underlying mechanism of why the chest radiograph predicated CAJ position more accurately in BMI under 24.9 kg/m2 group requires further investigation. Besides, this study was not blinded, which may introduce bias into the evaluation process; however, three design elements minimized its impact: (1) Objective primary endpoint: Tip-CAJ distance was measured on radiographs using anatomical landmarks and digital tools, minimizing subjectivity; (2) Blinded endpoint assessment: Radiologists measuring outcomes were unaware of group allocation; (3) Procedural standardization: Surgical steps were protocolized to reduce inter-operator variability. Although the sample size achieved a satisfactory effect size, it is likely due to the small sample size that complications other than thrombosis—such as malignant arrhythmias, hemothorax, or pleural effusion—are not included in the analysis due to their low incidence rates. Finally, the study was conducted exclusively on female patients; however, TIVAPs are not used solely in females.

In conclusion, we found that the chest radiograph method could effectively predict the location of CAJ, which may be affected by high BMI. The application of the chest radiograph method in assessing catheter length in TIVAP could be beneficial in improving the rate of proper positioning of the infusion port catheter tip at the CAJ, thereby reducing complications such as thrombosis, arrhythmias, and catheter displacement. In the future, we will modify the chest radiograph method to improve its effectiveness in TIVAP.
