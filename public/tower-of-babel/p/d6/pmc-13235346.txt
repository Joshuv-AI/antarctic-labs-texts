Are avocados good for the brain? Most likely yes, in spite of their lack of effect on cognitive performance in a well-conducted 6-month randomized controlled trial
Authors: Emilio Ros
Published: 2026

As population ages, cognitive decline and dementia are becoming leading causes of disability worldwide. Given the paucity of effective treatments, there is a pressing public health need for efficient preventive strategies of cognitive decline, a pre-dementia stage. Although with a lesser degree of evidence than established dementia risk factors such as low education, visual impairment, diabetes, hypertension or social isolation, evidence is accumulating on the relationship between dietary factors and cognitive functioning [1]. Cohort studies suggest that consumption of healthy foods and adherence to plant-based dietary patterns, such as the Mediterranean diet, are associated with reduced risk of cognitive disorders [[2], [3], [4]]. Mediterranean-type dietary patterns are reputed as both heart-healthy and environmentally sustainable [5]; additionally, evidence from prospective studies suggest a favorable association with cognitive decline, but no clear link with all-cause dementia or Alzheimer’s disease, the most frequent cause of dementia [2,3,6].

The evidence base from observational studies favoring brain health is relatively strong for increased consumption of some paradigmatic plant foods, such as fruits and vegetables, in general [7], at young ages [8,9], or in adulthood [10]. Fruits and vegetables are often categorized together in cohort studies, but the inverse association with cognitive impairment is maintained when fruits are considered alone [8,10]. These plant foods are rich in antioxidants, particularly polyphenols, bioactives that may reduce inflammation and oxidative stress, supporting brain health [11]. Results from randomized controlled trials (RCTs), however, have been inconsistent, probably due to limitations in study design, like participants’ characteristics and study duration. For slow-developing conditions such as cognitive decline, meaningful exposures to candidate protective nutritional interventions should be years rather than months, making such studies difficult to conduct.

Few scientifically-sound, well-powered, and relatively long-lasting nutritional trials with outcomes on cognitive function have been conducted in older adults. Two RCTs testing dietary changes [12,13] and another two examining the cognitive effects of a whole food (walnuts) [14] and a cocoa extract [15], both rich in neuroprotective polyphenols, deserve to be mentioned. A 3-year trial of the Mediterranean-DASH Intervention for Neurodegenerative Delay (MIND diet), a hybrid of the Mediterranean diet and the DASH (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension) diet, failed to show benefits for global or domain-specific cognitive function [12]. A similar outcome (no benefit) was reported for the 2-year Walnuts and Healthy Aging (WAHA) study (14) and the 2-year cognitive sub-cohort of the COcoa Supplement and Multivitamin Outcomes Study (COSMOS) [15]. On the other hand, a PREDIMED cognition sub-study lasting 4.1 years on average had a positive result for total cognition, memory and executive function of the Mediterranean diet supplemented with extra-virgin olive oil or mixed nuts, again polyphenol-rich foods [13]. Thus, only the longer lasting study, PREDIMED, which was conducted in older persons at high cardiovascular risk, hence prone to impaired cognition, disclosed improvement and even reversion of age-related cognitive impairment.

In a well-designed, adequately powered and properly conducted RCT published in this issue of the journal, a 6-month supplementation of the usual diet of middle-aged overweight or obese individuals with one daily avocado, a fruit rich in salutary nutrients and bioactives, failed to show any cognitive benefit [16]. Authors argue that increased adiposity in their participants might have mitigated the effect of the avocado diet on cognition, as has been postulated for LDL-cholesterol changes. This is unlikely, because no interaction of body mass index with cognitive effects has been described in other large nutrition intervention trials [[12], [13], [14], [15]], even though overweight and obesity are risk factors for cognitive impairment and dementia [1].

Should we conclude that avocados play no role in the promotion of brain health? No, avocados are probably good foods for the brain to a similar extent than other fruits and vegetables, for which, among common food groups, there is the highest strength of observational evidence for cognitive benefit across the life course [7]. According to a recent meta-analysis of 10 RCTs, avocado diets reduce the potency of two major cardiovascular risk factors, blood pressure and LDL-cholesterol [17], which are also established risk factors for cognitive decline and dementia [1]. There is also sound epidemiological evidence that the frequent consumption of avocados confers cardiovascular benefit. In the 2 large seminal prospective cohorts of women and men of the Nurses’ Health Study and the Health Professionals Follow-up Study, multivariable-adjusted inverse associations between avocado consumption and incident cardiovascular disease were observed after 30 years of follow-up [18]. Given the close link between cardiovascular disease and neurodegeneration, with vascular disease of the brain likely being a pathogenic contributor to age-related cognitive dysfunction and dementia [19], it can reasonably be inferred that if a food is good for the heart, it is also good for the brain.

Yet, even with long-term RCTs, it is difficult to prove that single foods, no matter how rich in salutary nutrients like avocados are, can improve cognition. Because of the synergy of its food components in reducing cardiovascular risk factors and fostering neuroprotection, a whole plant-based diet approach, with a dietary pattern high in fruits and vegetables and low in ultra-processed foods, is better suited to be associated with cognitive benefit [[1], [2], [3]]. Another strategy with more possibilities of success is the multi-domain approach, intervening concomitantly to modify several lifestyle aspects, such as diet, physical activity and other modifiable risk factors. Converging evidence from multimodal trials performed on the long-term (2 years and beyond) in the last decade suggests that individuals at higher risk of cognitive decline and dementia are those who might obtain more benefit from preventive strategies [20], signaling population groups to target in future interventions. Further studies are needed to better delineate the optimal timing for interventions to improve cognition. Regarding when to intervene across the life course, it is reasonable to do it as early as possible to imprint a long-lasting healthy lifestyle, but the positive results of the PREDIMED cognition study, with a mean participants’ age of 67 years at inception [13], suggest that it is never too late to change dietary habits and obtain significant benefits for brain health.
