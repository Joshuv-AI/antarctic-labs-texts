Utilization of sugarcane bagasse ash as green adsorbent for Fuchsin basic dye removal
Authors: Jay R Patel, Nikita J Ambegaonkar, Ronak Patel, T S Rajaraman, Pratik Patel, Piyush Patel, Naina H Tahilramani, Sunil R Patel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSugarcane bagasse is generated in huge quantities worldwide which can be converted into high-end valuable products. A major portion of bagasse is converted into Sugarcane Bagasse Ash (SCBA) by burning it at a high temperature in industries. Proper utilization of SCBA instead of landfilling can reduce the harmful environmental effects. In this paper, application of bagasse ash as a green adsorbent for the removal of Fuchsin Basic (FB) dye was investigated. SCBA was characterized using XRD, FTIR, SEM-EDS, BET for insights into its properties. Parameters like contact time, pH, temperature, initial concentration and adsorbent dosage were varied and their impact on dye removal was studied. A maximum removal around 78% (for 10 mg/L dye concentration, 3 g/L adsorbent dosage, 7 pH, 60 min and 25 °C) was achieved using SCBA. Adsorption equilibrium was investigated and Langmuir model fitted best (qmax = 9.1706 mg/g and kl= 0.1690 L/mg, R2 = 0.9939) as compared to Freundlich and Temkin models. Additionally, kinetics studies revealed that Pseudo second order (R2 = 0.9998) was superior over other kinetic models. Thermodynamic study confirmed the endothermic adsorption of FB on SCBA and FB removal of 50% was observed even after four cycles indicating reusability of adsorbent after regeneration. Use of SCBA directly as adsorbent without excessive pretreatment promises to be an attractive option.Keywords: Waste utilization, Sugarcane bagasse ash, Dye removal, Green adsorbent, Dye-laden wastewaterSubject terms: Pollution remediation, Chemical engineering, Green chemistry

Introduction

With increasing population and industrialization, waste generation has surged, making wastewater a major concern. Industrial effluent from pharmaceuticals, paper and pulp, chemicals, refineries, textiles, and food etc. contain toxic compounds detrimental to plant and animal life. Dye-laden wastewater, particularly from textile, leather, and tanning industries, significantly contributes to environmental pollution, affecting human health and surroundings. Methods like advanced oxidation processes, photocatalysis, microbial degradation, adsorption, membrane filtration etc. are available to treat such wastewater. Out of these techniques, adsorption has proved to be a promising method for the dye removal with biosorbents and adsorbent synthesized from waste material garnering increased attention.

Fuchsin Basic (FB) is one such dye which is carcinogenic and mutagenic in nature1–4. FB is a triamino triphenylmethane dye which finds wide application in textile, cosmetic and pharmaceutical industries5. It is also used as a staining agent in medical laboratories. Wastewater containing FB can thus be released into environment from these industries and laboratories1. The allowable limit of FB in wastewater varies by regions and countries and it is highly dependent on local legislation. As per EPA, the general colourant limit is < 1 mg/L in wastewater to avoid any harmful effects to the environment6. Considering the carcinogenic and toxic nature of FB dye, adopting conservative benchmark is reasonable and precautionary. Exposure to FB bearing wastewater affects human health and can cause tissue necrosis, quadriplegia, increased heart rates, jaundice, headache, dizziness etc.1,7. This necessitates development of treatment techniques to remove FB from water. Various green adsorbents like pistachio nutshell8, waste eggshell membrane9, Euryale ferox Salisbury seed shells10, Leaves Prunus Cerasefera11, Mussel shells12 etc. have been reported in literature for FB removal.

Sugarcane bagasse ash (SCBA) is another waste which has been widely reported for its use as an adsorbent due to its wide range of binding sites13. It has been reported that around 100 million tonne per annum bagasse is generated in India14. By burning 1 ton of sugarcane, nearly 25 kg of SCBA is obtained as waste from industries15. This SCBA is usually landfilled which raises certain environmental concerns16,17. Utilizing this huge amount of SCBA can help to reduce the quantity of waste to be disposed and thus harmful environmental impact can be minimised. Many studies have been carried out with SCBA as adsorbents for dye removal. Siddiqui et al. reported bagasse carbon based composite material (SBC/Al(OH)3) as adsorbent for methylene blue and methyl violet18. Garg et al. used SCBA as sorbent with removal efficiency of methylene blue around 55.2% in Ultraviolet (UV) environment19. de Santana et al. applied SCBA to wastewater bearing Acid Red 27 dye and maximum adsorption was found out to be 15 mg/g20. Many other such studies are available in literature for utilizing bagasse/ash as adsorbent21–24.

Although other adsorbents have been used for FB removal, most studies with green adsorbents like pistachio shell, eggshells etc. require physical processing and some form of preliminary treatments (crushing, chemical treatment, calcination etc. which requires excessive energy or additional material) before using them as an adsorbent12,25,26. Herein, FB removal using SCBA as adsorbent is reported. The objective of this study is to check the effectiveness of SCBA (which is available in large quantities in sugar industries in a country like India) directly obtained from industry, as a sustainable and eco-friendly adsorbent for FB dye removal and to study adsorption behaviour of FB onto SCBA. The direct use of SCBA (after simple water washing and drying) as an adsorbent without excessive pretreatment (crushing/grinding/chemical treatment/calcination at high temperature) is explored in this study. To the best of authors’ knowledge, removal of Fuchsin basic dye using waste SCBA as an adsorbent has not been reported till date. The current work also incorporates studies on effect of various parameters on adsorption, isotherms, kinetics, and thermodynamics along with reusability of adsorbent for fuchsin basic dye removal.

Materials and methods

Materials

Bagasse ash was obtained from sugar industry based in Ankleshwar (Gujarat). In sugar industries, waste bagasse was burnt as fuel to meet-up the heat requirements. After burning, left-out residual ash (SCBA) was used further as an adsorbent after washing with water twice, drying in oven (70 °C) and sieving through 120 μm sieve.

Fuchsin Basic dye was obtained from Astron Chemicals. Distilled water was used to prepare solutions. To change pH of solution, Analytical grade Hydrochloric acid (Finar) and Sodium Hydroxide (Finar) solutions were used. Nitric acid (Finar) was used as desorbing agent.

Characterization

Water washed and dried bagasse ash was characterized using various analytical techniques for insights into their composition, morphology and other properties. Functional groups were identified using FTIR - Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy - (Perkin Elmer, SP-65). The SCBA sample was mixed with KBr in 1:100 ratio to prepare a pellet (200 mg pellet with 198 mg KBr and 2 mg sample). X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were obtained to know the structural properties of the sample using Bruker AXS D8 Focus P-XRD instrument with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.1546 nm) as X-ray source in the 2ϴ range from 5° – 80° with current and voltage of 40 mA and 40 kV respectively. Surface area of adsorbent was determined using BET analysis (Anton Par, Autosorb IQ-XR-XR). Morphology and elements of the ash was studied with the help of Scanning Electron Microscopy Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (SEM-EDS) (Jeol, JSM-6390LV, Oxford X-Act) with samples coated with Palladium.

Adsorption

A solution of Fuchsin basic red dye with initial concentration of 10 mg/L was prepared in which fixed quantity of SCBA was added as an adsorbent. Stirring with the help of magnetic stirrer was provided for certain period of time at room temperature. After that, sample was filtered and analysed in UV- Visible Spectrophotometer (LI2704) at 546 nm wavelength corresponding to the peak absorbance of Fuchsin basic dye. Absorbance was noted down and was used to calculate the percentage removal as per the formula given in Eq. (1), where Co and Ct are initial and final concentrations (mg/L) respectively:

While conducting the experiment, 100 mL of dye solution (of particular concentration) was added with 0.1 g of adsorbent in a 250 mL capacity beaker for adsorbent dosage of 1 g/L. Similarly, for other dosages, adsorbent quantities were varied (0.2 g, 0.3 g, 0.4 g) for treating 100 mL of dye solution. Parameters like SCBA dosage, pH, temperature, initial concentration and time were varied to study their effects on dye removal. The different SCBA dosages taken were 1 g/L, 2 g/L, 3 g/L and 4 g/L and the adsorption time was varied from 0 to 80 min. pH was varied as 3, 5, 7 and 9 and temperature was varied as 15 °C, 25 °C, 35 °C and 45 °C. Initial concentration was varied from 10 mg/L to 40 mg/L and dye removal was calculated using spectroscopic data. While examining the effect of one parameter, other parameters were kept constant. All the experiments were repeated thrice for better accuracy and average values were taken as results. This is represented as error bars in the graphs.

For the condition of 3 g/L adsorbent, 7 pH, 10 mg/L initial concentration of solution and 25 °C (due to neutral condition of pH, room temperature, and minimal dye usage), Pseudo First Order (PFO), Pseudo Second Order (PSO) and Intra Particle Diffusion (IPD) kinetic studies were carried out. Moreover, isotherms (Langmuir, Freundlich and Temkin) were also fitted for various initial concentration of dye solution and constants were determined. Error analysis was also conducted for prediction of best fit model. Regeneration and reusability of SCBA was also studied by contacting 0.05 N nitric acid solution with used SCBA and cycles were repeated multiple times.

Results and discussion

Characterization of SCBA

Figure 1 depicts the FTIR spectra of the SCBA sample. The broad peak around 3100–3600 cm−1 can be attributed to the presence of vibration bands of both free and bounded hydrogen to OH − groups in the surface of the ashes20. The peak at 1620 cm−1 could be related to the presence of carbonyl groups from conjugated hydrocarbons. Between 2830 and 2965 cm−1 , peak showed stretching corresponding to asymmetric and symmetric vibrations of -CH2 group27. Peaks around 1100 and 790 cm−1 can be assigned to asymmetric and symmetric vibrations of Si-O-Si. Additionally, the peak around 1100 cm−1 can also be attributed to -C-O-H enlargements and –OH deformation. Peak around 465 cm−1 can be assigned to the flexion vibration O-Si-O27.

FTIR Spectra of SCBA sample.

X-Ray diffraction pattern of SCBA shown in Fig. 2 revealed that it predominantly possessed the quartz phase of silica. Additionally, wide scattering peaks (humps) were observed which formed between 15° and 30° on 2θ axis indicating the presence of some amorphous silica28.

XRD spectra of SCBA sample.

Nitrogen adsorption and desorption was allowed on the surface of SCBA to determine its surface area. Surface area of SCBA was 38.465 m2/g and that of pore volume was 9.326 × 10−2 cm3/g with average pore radius of 4.84898 nm. Pore diameter in the range of 2–50 nm size represents mesopore and thus SCBA adsorbent (9.69796 nm) represents a mesoporous structure29.

SEM analysis of SCBA is shown in Fig. 3. The images reveal that the particles exhibit a variety of forms, including rod like structures (20–30 microns in length) intermingled with irregularly shaped branched tree-like fibrous structures. This diversity in particle morphology is indicative of the influence of combustion temperature on mixed composition during the formation of SCBA30. Further, the particles appear as large agglomerates with a rough and uneven surface texture. This roughness in the SEM images suggests a high degree of porosity, which is consistent with the mesoporous nature (pore diameter of 2–50 nm) indicated by the BET results (pore diameter ~ 10 nm)29. Higher magnification reveals that agglomerates are composed of smaller irregular shaped particles that are loosely bound together leading to uneven surfaces. This uneven surface can contribute to high surface area thereby enhancing adsorption31.

SCBA as an adsorbent

SCBA was used as adsorbent to decolorize Fuchsin Basic (FB) dye solution. The Effects of various parameters like pH, temperature, dosage, contact time, initial concentration etc. were studied in detail and the study related to isotherms (Langmuir, Freundlich and Temkin) and kinetics (PFO, PSO and IPD) were also conducted.

Effect of contact time and adsorbent dosage

Figure 4 shows the effect of contact time on the FB dye removal from 10 mg/L solution for different dosages (1 g/L to 4 g/L) of SCBA. For all dosages, it can be observed that the dye removal percentage increases rapidly to a high value in the initial period. This is due to the availability of sufficient active surface sites on the adsorbent in the initial period of 15–20 min. However, after sufficient contact time, the adsorption rate reduces and gradually attains the equilibrium. Similar results have been reported in literature where adsorption rate declines with contact time, ultimately achieving equilibrium12. In the present study of FB adsorption on SCBA, the equilibrium was attained at around 45 min.

Dye removal (%) with time for various dosages (1 g/L, 2 g/L, 3 g/L, 4 g/L) at initial concentration of 10 mg/L at 25 °C and 7 pH.

For higher dosages, a sudden rise in removal percentage (70% in 15 min for 4 g/L) is observed as compared to a relatively gradual rise for lower dosages (45% in 15 min for 1 g/L) as shown in Fig. 4. During the initial period, more active sites are available at higher dosages leading to faster dye removal as compared to lower dosages. However, as the contact time is increased, even the lower dosages of 1 g/L and 2 g/L were able to exhibit almost similar FB removal after equilibrium attainment. Over 75% removal efficiency was achieved within 45 min for all dosages. The equilibrium removal rate at 45 min for 1 g/L, 2 g/L, 3 g/L and 4 g/L were 75.00%, 76.3%, 76.81%, and 77.10% respectively. Almost similar equilibrium removal rates for dosages varying from 1 g/L to 4 g/L indicate that after a certain contact time, the capacity to remove dye is not significantly affected by the dosage of SCBA. For higher dosages, more active sites are available but it is balanced by agglomeration of particles due to excess adsorbent and that can decrease the usage of active sites available for adsorption20,29. 3 g/L dosage was chosen for further studies to balance the benefits of fast initial adsorption with efficient use of the adsorbent.

Effect of pH

Experiments were conducted at different pH to check the effect of acidic and basic medium on the adsorption. The pH was adjusted using 0.1 M HCl and 0.1 M NaOH solutions. Figure 5depicts the effect of pH in the range of 3–9 on the adsorption of FB with initial concentration of both 10 mg/L and 20 mg/L. It is clearly visible that the FB removal was favoured in the basic medium as compared to the acidic medium. It has been reported that the point of zero charge (PZC) of sugarcane bagasse ash is in the range 7-832,27. Since the surface of adsorbent is positively charged when the pH of the solution is below PZC, the positively charged cationic groups of FB dye experience electrostatic repulsion leading to decreased attachment of dye molecules on the SCBA surface. On the other hand, basic medium provides the negative surface charge to the adsorbent due to the hydroxyl groups. This results in increased affinity between the dye molecules and SCBA surface, thereby enhancing the adsorption efficiency. The equilibrium removal percentages under pH 3, 5, 7 and 9 for 10 mg/L solution are 41.88%, 56.90%, 77.53% and 82.9% respectively. Similar trend is observed for 20 mg/L FB solution with highest removal observed in the basic medium of 9 pH and lowest removal observed under a pH of 3. This result of better removal in basic medium matches with other studies of FB dye removal11,12.

Effect of pH on FB solution (10 mg/L and 20 mg/L) treated with 3 g/L adsorbent at 25 °C.

Effect of temperature

Adsorption experiments were carried out at different temperatures (15 °C, 25 °C, 35 °C and 45 °C) to observe the effect on the FB dye removal. Figure 6 indicates that the adsorption of FB on SCBA adsorbent increased with rise in temperature. The equilibrium removal percentages for FB increased by 5.36% when the temperature was increased from 15 °C to 45 °C and the adsorption capacity was also elevated from 2.51 mg/g to 2.688 mg/g with the temperature variation of 15 °C to 45 °C. This could be due to the fact that at higher temperatures, the kinetic energy of the dye molecules is increased along with the reduction in the viscosity of the solution leading to enhanced diffusion of dye molecules into the pores of the adsorbent33.

Effect of temperature on 10 mg/L solution of FB treated with 3 g/L adsorbent at 7 pH at equilibrium condition.

Effect of initial concentration

Adsorption experiments were also conducted at different initial concentrations of FB dye solution (10 mg/L, 20 mg/L, 30 mg/L, and 40 mg/L). As seen in the Fig. 7, the adsorption capacity reduced at higher initial dye concentrations. As the initial concentration of the dye increased from 10 mg/L to 40 mg/L, the adsorption capacity also reduced. The equilibrium FB removal percentages decreased from 76.81% at 10 mg/L to 49.50% at 40 mg/L. This could be attributed to the fact that at low dye concentration, the available active sites in the adsorbent (3 g/L dosage) were sufficient for the adsorption. As the dye concentration increased, the available active sites were no longer sufficient for the removal of excess dye molecules present in the solution. These dye molecules competed for the limited active sites resulting in decreased removal percentage of FB.

Effect of initial concentration of FB dye treated with 3 g/L adsorbent, 7 pH and 25 °C.

Regeneration and reusability of SCBA

For 3 g/L adsorbent dosage and 10 mg/L initial concentration solution, regeneration of SCBA was studied. 0.05 N nitric acid (100 mL) was used as desorbing agent and the used adsorbent was contacted with it in a 250 mL beaker. After proper stirring for 1 h, the contents were then centrifuged for phase separation. SCBA was further washed with distilled water and then contacted again with 10 mg/L dye solution for 45 min. This cycle was repeated four times and regeneration and reusability was studied by measuring concentration of FB in solution. Figure 11 represents the percentage removal of dye in each cycle. From the graph it was observed that for first and second cycle, nominal change in % removal was observed (76.81% to 72.29%). For the third and fourth cycles, drop in % removal (61.94% and 50.02% respectively) indicates loss of adsorbent sites after multiple usage. It is evident from this study that SCBA can be used multiple times with efficient adsorption of FB dye. This is consistent with other studies reported in literature which also stated reduction in removal percentage due to blockages of active sites by dye molecules45,46.

Conclusions

The current work provides new insights for utilization of sugarcane bagasse ash. SCBA, obtained from sugar industry, was directly used for successful removal of fuchsin basic dye. In the application of SCBA as adsorbent, dye removal around 78% was obtained for 10 mg/L dye concentration, 3 g/L adsorbent dosage, 7 pH at 25 °C. Dye removal was enhanced under basic conditions and at higher temperatures. Among Langmuir, Freundlich and Temkin isotherms models, the Langmuir model exhibited best fit with experimental data (qmax = 9.1706 mg/g and kl= 0.1690 L/mg, R2 = 0.9939). Moreover, pseudo second order (with R2 = 0.9998) proved to be the superior kinetic model over pseudo first order and intraparticle diffusion models. Reusability of SCBA for multiple cycles highlights its sustainability as low-cost adsorbent for the treatment of dye-laden wastewater. The application as green adsorbent showcased positive results, indicating that SCBA from industry can be directly used as a promising alternative to conventional adsorbent materials. Considering the huge quantities of SCBA generated in tropical countries like India and Brazil, its direct utilization as an adsorbent without excessive pretreatment offers a sustainable alternative for dye removal. Pilot plant studies, Life Cycle Assessment (LCA), and Techno-Economic Analysis (TEA) can be a way forward to assess feasibility and environmental impact for real-world applications to promote circular economy principles.
