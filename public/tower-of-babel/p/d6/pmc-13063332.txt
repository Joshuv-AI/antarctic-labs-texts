Manic switching from depression is affected by adult hippocampal neurogenesis
Authors: Jieun Song, Woonryong Kim, Yoojin Park, Ja-Myong Lee, Jong-Hoon Kim, Dong-Hyuk Park, Heon-Jeong Lee, Ki-Soon Shin, Hyun Jung Kim, Dongho Geum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMany individuals who initially present with depression later develop manic or hypomanic episodes, yet the biological processes accompanying this delayed shift remain unclear. Here, we examined whether perturbation of the adult hippocampal neurogenic niche is associated with time-dependent shifts in mood-relevant behaviors. Using Nestin-CreER::Rosa26-FloxedSTOP-DTa (Nestin-DTa) mice, we induced partial ablation of Nestin-positive adult neural stem cells (aNSCs) in the hippocampus by tamoxifen administration and assessed phenotypes at 4 and 8 weeks post-ablation. Neurogenic-lineage populations in the dentate gyrus were quantified by immunohistochemistry using stage-specific markers. Partial aNSC ablation reduced the Nestin-positive pool and BrdU-labeled proliferation, followed by a compensatory expansion of proliferative progenitors and DCX-positive neuroblasts. At 4 weeks, Nestin-DTa mice exhibited anxiety-like behavior and increased forced-swim immobility. By 8 weeks, anxiety-like measures normalized, and mice displayed increased exploratory/locomotor activity and reduced forced-swim immobility, accompanied by sustained elevation of progenitor and immature neuronal compartments. These findings provide a tractable model in which disruption of the adult neurogenic niche is associated with a transition from depression-like behavior to a hyperactive, mania-like phenotype over time.KEYWORDS: Bipolar disorder, mood-state transition, adult neural stem cell, hippocampal neurogenesis

Introduction

Mood disorders, including major depressive disorder (MDD) and bipolar disorder (BD), impose a significant global health burden by contributing to disability and increased mortality. Although both conditions are characterized by recurrent depressive episodes and share overlapping neurobiological substrates, BD is distinguished diagnostically by the occurrence of at least one manic or hypomanic episode (Bowden 2005). A major clinical challenge is the delayed diagnosis of BD, frequently complicated by manic switching, in which individuals initially presenting with depressive symptoms later develop mania or hypomania (Li et al. 2012; Musliner and Ostergaard 2018). While clinical predictors for this transition have been reported (Nestsiarovich et al. 2021), the fundamental neurobiological mechanisms underlying the delayed emergence of mania remain unclear.

The pathophysiology of mood disorders is closely linked to pathways governing brain plasticity and cellular resilience. Oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and dysregulated insulin signaling have been implicated in BD-related neurobiological changes (Data-Franco et al. 2017), and these processes converge on the adult hippocampus. The hippocampus is a key brain region for mood regulation and is one of the few areas that supports adult hippocampal neurogenesis (AHN), the continuous generation of new neurons from neural stem cells (NSCs) (Eisch and Petrik 2012; Anacker and Hen 2017; Kim et al. 2017). The neurotrophic hypothesis posits that chronic stress and depression impair AHN, leading to reduced hippocampal volume and subsequent depressive symptoms, including cognitive deficits (Levy et al. 2018). Consistent with this, reduced activity of neurotrophic factors, particularly brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and its receptor TrkB, has been observed in patients with BD (Kim et al. 2010; Soontornniyomkij et al. 2011). These findings strongly suggest that compromised stem cell maintenance and subsequent defects in AHN are central to the neurobiology of both depressive and bipolar pathology.

The importance of AHN is further underscored by its role in treatment response. A key finding in preclinical research is that the efficacy of most conventional antidepressant treatments, including pharmacological agents and electroconvulsive therapy, relies on their ability to promote the proliferation and maturation of new hippocampal neurons in animal models (Madsen et al. 2000; Malberg et al. 2000). This restoration of neurogenesis is often viewed as necessary to reverse the deficits associated with the depressive state. However, while AHN promotion is linked to depression resolution, the fate of this restored or compensatory neurogenesis during the shift toward mania remains a critical, unresolved question. Manic episodes, which can be triggered by stress or even antidepressant use (Salvadore et al. 2010), are associated with hyperactive states that may reflect aberrant plasticity or even a pathological overcompensation by the neurogenic niche.

A major translational limitation is that many existing animal models of BD reproduce isolated depressive- or manic-like phenotypes, but do not capture the dynamic cyclicity and the key transition of manic switching characteristic of the human condition (Choi and Han 2015; Beyer and Freund 2017). Here, we present a novel animal model of BD using Nestin-CreER::Rosa26-FloxedSTOP-DTa (Nestin-DTa) mice. Inducible ablation of adult neural stem cells (aNSCs) in this model produces an initial depressive-like phenotype that subsequently transitions into a manic-like state after eight weeks, thereby enabling experimental interrogation of state transition. We hypothesize that this behavioral switch is driven by compensatory hyper-proliferation of progenitor populations in the adult hippocampus following initial aNSC loss. This model provides a platform to elucidate cell-intrinsic mechanisms by which aberrant AHN dynamics may mediate transitions between depressive- and manic-like states, offering fundamental insights into the BD pathophysiology.

Materials and methods

Generation of Nestin-CreER::Rosa26-FloxedSTOP-DTa (Nestin-DTa) mice

Nestin-CreERT2 transgenic mice (tamoxifen-inducible Cre recombinase; Jackson Laboratory, Stock No. 016261) were crossed with Rosa26-Floxed-LacZ-Diphtheria Toxin Fragment A (DTa) reporter mice (Jackson Laboratory, Stock No. 007900) to generate Nestin-CreER::Rosa26-FloxedSTOP-DTa mice (hereafter referred to as Nestin-DTa), as previously described (Brockschnieder et al. 2004). All mouse lines were maintained on a C57BL/6 background. In this system, administration of tamoxifen (TAM) induces Cre-mediated excision of the floxed STOP cassette, thereby permitting the constitutive Rosa26 promoter to drive selective DTa expression in Nestin-expressing neural stem/progenitor cells. Consequently, this mechanism enables conditional ablation of aNSCs upon TAM treatment.

Sibling offspring were collected for experimental use and individually genotyped by PCR following established protocols (Campbell et al. 2004). Male mice aged 12 weeks were utilized for all experiments. The specific number of animals used in each assay is indicated in the corresponding figure legends. All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Korea University (IACUC No. KOREA/2025-0014).

Drug treatment

Tamoxifen (TAM; Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No.T5686) was dissolved in a vehicle solution consisting of ethanol (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No.1.00989.1011) and sunflower seed oil (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. S5007) at a final concentration of 10% ethanol and 90% sunflower seed oil using sonication. TAM was administered via intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection at a dose of 180 mg/kg once daily for five consecutive days in 12-week-old mice to induce aNSC ablation.

To label proliferating cells, the thymidine analog 5-bromo-2′-deoxyuridine (BrdU; Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. B5002) was prepared in sterile saline and administered as a single i.p. injection (100 mg/kg). BrdU injection was performed 24 h after the final TAM treatment. Brains designated for the analysis of cell proliferation were rapidly isolated one hour following the BrdU injection for subsequent immunohistochemical analysis.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical analyses were performed as previously reported (Kim et al. 2007; Hussaini et al. 2013). Briefly, the mice were perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Biosolution, Cat. No. BP031) prepared in PBS, and brains were post-fixed in PFA for 24 h. Fixed brains were then cryoprotected in 30% sucrose (Junsei, Cat. No. 31365S0350) before being sectioned serially at a thickness of 40 μm using a cryostat, and subsequent immunostaining was performed by the free-floating method. For BrdU staining, sectioned brain slices underwent an antigen retrieval process as previously reported (Hussaini et al. 2013). Sections were incubated with primary antibodies prepared in blocking buffer. The following primary antibodies and dilutions were used: anti-BrdU (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. B2531, 1:1000), anti-DCX (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Cat. No. sc-271390, 1:1000), anti-Nestin (Millipore, Cat. No. MAB5326, 1:250), anti-Sox2 (Millipore, Cat. No. AB5603, 1:100), anti-calretinin (Swant, No. CR7697, 1:500), anti-NeuN (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. ABN78, 1:500), anti-Tbr2 (Abcam, No. ab23345, 1:500), anti-Mcm2 (BD Biosciences, Cat. No. 610701, 1:500), anti-PCNA (DAKO, Cat. No. M0879, 1:500). After several washes with PBS, sections were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, Cat. No. A-21207, A-21203, A-21206, A-21202) for 30 min, washed, and mounted. Images were captured and analyzed using a confocal microscope (LSM800; Zeiss).

Behavioral test

Learned safety test

The learned safety test was performed as outlined in Supplementary Fig. 1a. A shock chamber (24 × 24 cm) with a stainless-steel rod floor was used. Mice were habituated to the chamber for 10 min prior to training. During training (Days 1–3), mice were exposed to two types of unpaired events: (i) tone presentations delivered without foot shock (conditioned stimulus; CS, 80 dB, 20 s) and (ii) scrambled foot shocks delivered in the absence of the tone (unconditioned stimulus; US, 0.5 mA, 2 s). The order and timing of CS and US presentations were arranged such that the CS did not predict the US. On Day 4, safety memory recall was assessed by quantifying freezing during a preCS baseline period followed by the CS period, and freezing responses were scored using an automated system (SmartEye, Ver. 1.0).

Open field test

An open field test was conducted in a novel open box (40×40×30 cm) under dim light conditions (40 lx). Animals were transported to the experimental room and left uninterrupted for approximately 10 min before the experiment. They were placed in the center (20×20 cm) of the arena and allowed to explore for 10 min. Both the duration of time spent in the center and the frequency of center visits were recorded. EthoVision3.0 software (Noldus) was used to record and analyze the movements. The field was cleaned with 70% ethanol between subjects.

Elevated plus maze test

The elevated plus maze included two open arms (30×5×2 cm) and two closed arms (30×5×30 cm) extending from a center area (10×10 cm). The maze was elevated 50 cm above the floor. All experiments were conducted under dim light conditions (40 lx). Animals were transported to the experimental room and left uninterrupted for approximately 10 min before testing. Animals were placed in the center area with their heads pointing toward one of the open arms. Anxiety-like behavior was assessed by the percentage of time spent in the open arms and the number of entries in the open arms. An arm entry was recorded when the animals reached the distal part of the arm. Each session lasted 10 min. The apparatus was cleaned between sessions with 70% ethanol and dried. All experiments were recorded with a video camera and analyzed using EthoVision 3.0.

Forced swim test

Animals were transported to the behavior testing room and allowed at least 10 min of acclimation before testing. A clear cylindrical glass container (50×20 cm) was filled with water. Each animal was placed in the container, and movements were recorded from the time the animal entered the water until it ceased swimming. All recorded images were analyzed using EthoVision 3.0. A heat pad and a dry, warmed canister were prepared in advance to prevent hypothermia and used to recover mice after testing.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Prism v10.0 (GraphPad Software). For comparisons between two independent groups, two-tailed unpaired Student's t-tests were used as appropriate. For time-course data analyzed across repeated time blocks (e.g. immobility time per 1-min block in the forced swim test), two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was performed with Genotype as the between-subject factor and Time block as the within-subject factor, using the Geisser–Greenhouse correction when sphericity was not assumed. When block-wise pairwise comparisons were performed, p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using Tukey's method. Data are presented throughout the manuscript as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Ablation of adult hippocampal neural stem cells in Nestin- DTa mice

To investigate the contribution of AHN to mood regulation, we generated Nestin-DTa mice in which Nestin-expressing aNSCs can be ablated by TAM administration (Figure 1(A)). Experimental analyses were performed at 4 and 8 weeks after TAM treatment in 12-week-old mice (Figure 1(B)). To verify the efficacy of aNSC ablation, we quantified Nestin-positive cells in the dentate gyrus (DG). Nestin-DTa mice exhibited a marked reduction of Nestin-positive aNSCs compared with wild-type (WT) controls (Figure 1(E,F,K); t(6) = 8.404, p < 0.001), indicating that a substantial fraction of the aNSC pool was eliminated following TAM induction. We next assessed proliferative activity at the end of TAM treatment using BrdU labeling. Consistent with aNSC ablation, the density of BrdU-positive proliferating cells was significantly reduced in Nestin-DTa mice (123.8 ± 21.43 cells/mm2) compared with WT controls (408.5 ± 40.07 cells/mm2) (Figure 1(C,D, G-J, L); t(6) = 6.266, p < 0.001). Together, these results demonstrate that conditional aNSC ablation led to a robust suppression of proliferating progenitor populations in the adult hippocampus.

To determine whether hippocampus-linked behavior was concomitantly affected, we evaluated learned safety using an established safety-learning paradigm (Supplementary Fig. 1) (Pollak et al. 2008). Whereas WT mice exhibited a significant reduction in freezing during the safety cue (CS) relative to the preCS period, Nestin-DTa mice failed to show this CS-associated suppression of freezing (t(20) = 5.888, p < 0.0001), indicating impaired safety learning following disruption of the neurogenic niche. Collectively, these findings indicate that hippocampal neurogenesis was ablated to an extent sufficient to induce measurable deficits in hippocampus-linked behavior.

Increased anxiety and depression in Nestin-DTa mice four weeks after aNSC ablation

We next examined anxiety- and depression-related behaviors in Nestin-DTa mice at 4 weeks after TAM administration, a time point at which perturbation of the neurogenic niche is expected to impact DG circuit function. Anxiety-like behavior was assessed using the open field test and elevated plus maze. In the open field test, Nestin-DTa mice displayed reduced center exploration in the open field, reflected by a lower percentage of time spent in the center and shorter center duration per entry, consistent with increased anxiety-like behavior (Figure 2(A–C); t(18) = 2.242, p < 0.05; t(18) = 2.447, p < 0.05). In the elevated plus maze, Nestin-DTa mice spent less time in the open arms and entered the open arms less frequently than WT controls (Figure 2(D–F); t(17) = 2.525, p < 0.05; t(17) = 3.634, p < 0.01), while total distance traveled was comparable between groups, indicating preserved locomotion (Figure 2(G); t(17) = 0.681, p = 0.505).

Depression-like behavior was evaluated using the forced swim test. Nestin-DTa mice showed increased total immobility compared with WT controls (Figure 2(H); t(13) = 2.829, p = 0.0142). To evaluate temporal dynamics, immobility time per 1-min block (Figure 2(I)) was analyzed using a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with Genotype (WT vs. Nestin-DTa) as the between-subject factor and Time block as the within-subject factor, applying the Geisser–Greenhouse correction (ϵ = 0.5874). This analysis revealed significant main effects of Genotype (F(1, 13) = 8.006, p = 0.0142) and Time block (F(2.350, 30.55) = 6.989, p = 0.0021), with no significant Genotype × Time block interaction (F(2.350, 30.55) = 0.8499, p = 0.4535). Collectively, these results indicate that partial aNSCs ablation is associated with increased anxiety-like behavior and increased depression-like behavior, as reflected by elevated immobility in the forced swim test, at 4 weeks after TAM administration.

Compensation of transient amplifying progenitors, neuroblasts, and immature neurons in the hippocampus of Nestin-DTa mice after aNSCs ablation

To define cellular events accompanying the depressive-like phenotype, we analyzed the composition of neurogenic lineage population in the DG 4 weeks after TAM-induced aNSC ablation. Consistent with a compensatory response within neurogenic niche, Nestin-DTa mics exhibited an increase in PCNA-positive proliferative cells compared with WT controls (Supplement Fig. 2; t(7) = 4.821, p < 0.01). Notably, despite persistent depletion of the Nestin+ and Sox2+ aNSC compartment at this time point (Figure 3(A,B)), downstream progenitor populations were markedly expanded. Specifically, Mcm2-expressing type 2a transient amplifying progenitors (TAPs) were increased in Nestin-DTa mice (408.1 ± 34.32 cells/mm2) relative to WT mice (213.8 ± 27.32 cells/mm2) (Figure 3(C,D,N); t(9) = 3.850, p < 0.01). Type 2b TAPs (Mcm2+ DCX+) were similarly elevated (Figure 3(E,F,O); t(9) = 5.383, p < 0.001). In parallel, DCX-positive neuroblasts (NBs) were robustly increased in Nestin-DTa mice (1247 ± 98.36 cells/mm2) compared with WT controls (439.8 ± 35.57 cells/mm2) (Figure 3(E–H, P); t(6) = 7.718, p < 0.001). Consistent with this expansion, calretinin-positive immature neurons were also increased (Figure 3(I, J, Q); t(4) = 3.052, p < 0.05), and the transitional DCX+/calretinin+ population was elevated (Figure 3(G, H)). The lineage hierarchy and marker expression across neurogenic stages are summarized in Figure 3(M).

Despite this marked amplification of progenitor and immature neuronal compartments, terminal maturation appeared to be limited at 4 weeks, as indicated by a reduction in BrdU+/NeuN+ double-positive cells (Supplementary Fig. 3). Together, these data suggest that partial aNSC depletion triggers a compensatory expansion of downstream neurogenic populations, while effective maturation and/or circuit integration of newborn neurons may lag at this stage. Based on these findings, we next assessed behavioral and cellular outcomes after an additional 4-week interval (8 weeks after ablation).

Switching from depressive-like behavior to a hyperactive phenotype eight weeks after aNSC ablation

We re-evaluated mood-related behaviors in Nestin-DTa mice at 8 weeks after TAM treatment using the open field test, elevated plus maze, and forced swim test (Figure 4). In the open field test, the reduced center exploration observed at 4 weeks was no longer evident at 8 weeks, and Nestin-DTa mice exhibited center-related measures comparable to WT controls (Figure 4(A–C)). Specifically, center duration did not differ between groups (Figure 4(B); t(23) = 0.7900, p = 0.4376), and the average center duration per single entry was also comparable (Figure 4(C); t(23) = 0.9461, p = 0.3539). In the elevated plus maze, Nestin-DTa mice displayed increased open-arm exploration relative to WT controls (Figure 4(D-F)): open-arm duration was increased (Figure 4(E); t(23) = 2.519, p < 0.05), and the number of open-arm entries was also increased (Figure 4(F); t(23) = 3.086, p < 0.01). In addition, total distance traveled was increased in Nestin-DTa mice (Figure 4(G); t(23) = 2.630, p < 0.05), indicating elevated exploratory and locomotor activity.

In the forced swim test, Nestin-DTa mice displayed reduced immobility over the 5-min session (Figure 4(H); t(23) = 3.058, p < 0.01). To capture temporal dynamics, immobility time per 1-min block (Figure 4(I)) was analyzed using a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with Genotype as the between-subject factor and Time block as the within-subject factor (Geisser–Greenhouse correction; ϵ = 0.7854). This analysis revealed a significant main effect of Genotype (F(1, 23) = 9.350, p = 0.0056), with no significant main effect of Time block (F(3.142, 72.26) = 0.7875, p = 0.5100) and Genotype × Time block interaction (F(3.142, 72.26) = 2.339, p = 0.0778). Consistent with the block-wise pattern, Tukey-adjusted post hoc comparisons between genotypes at each time block identified significantly reduced immobility in Nestin-DTa mice during blocks 4 and 5 (block 4: adjusted p = 0.0013; block 5: adjusted p = 0.0055), whereas differences in blocks 1–3 were not significant (adjusted p > 0.05). Collectively, these behavioral data indicate that depressive-like behaviors were alleviated and that Nestin-DTa mice exhibited a hyperactive behavioral phenotype at 8 weeks after aNSC ablation.

Overgrowth of neuronal progenitors and immature neurons beyond recovery eight weeks after aNSC ablation

To relate behavioral state changes to neurogenic progression, we examined neurogenic lineage populations at 8 weeks after aNSC ablation using stage-specific markers spanning type 2 TAPs through mature neurons (Figure 5). Nestin-DTa mice showed elevated neurogenic output relative to WT controls, with increased abundance of type 2 TAPs populations. Although Mcm2+ type 2 TAPs were not increased in Nestin-DTa mice compared with WT controls (Figure 5(A, B, J); t(16) = 2.113, p = 0.0507), Tbr2+ type 2 TAPs were elevated (Figure 5(A-D, K); t(15) = 3.722, p < 0.01). In addition, the number of DCX-positive NBs was increased (Figure 5(E, F, L); t(8) = 3.012, p < 0.05), and the DCX+ population exhibited prominent neuritic processes consistent with ongoing neuronal development. Calretinin-positive immature neurons were elevated (Figure 5(G, H, M); t(7) = 6.775, p < 0.001), and NeuN-positive mature neurons were restored and/or increased in Nestin-DTa mice compared with WT controls (Figure 5(G, H)). Although these marker-based analyses demonstrate a robust expansion of progenitor and immature neuronal compartments at 8 weeks, they do not directly resolve whether newborn neurons differ in maturation state or circuit integration between 4 and 8 weeks. This limitation is addressed in the Discussion, where we outline plausible time-dependent cellular and circuit-level mechanisms that may underlie the behavioral transition despite broadly similar lineage-stage marker categories.

Discussion

In this study, we examined how perturbation of the adult hippocampal neurogenic niche relates to mood-relevant behavioral phenotypes in mice. Using Nestin-DTa mice, TAM administration induced partial ablation of Nestin-positive aNSCs in the hippocampus. Four weeks after ablation, Nestin-DTa mice exhibited increased anxiety-like and depression-like behavior alterations, accompanied by marked changes in neurogenic-lineage populations. Notably, proliferative progenitors and immature neuronal compartments remained expanded at later time points, and behavioral phenotypes shifted toward increased exploratory and locomotor activity by eight weeks post-ablation. Together, these findings support an association between altered AHN dynamics and bidirectional changes in mood-related behaviors in this model.

Impaired hippocampal neurogenesis and related structural alterations have been reported in mood disorders and in preclinical models. Clinical imaging studies have described decreased hippocampal volume in MDD (Campbell et al. 2004; Videbech and Ravnkilde 2004), whereas evidence linking hippocampal volume to BD remains less consistent. In animal models, depressive-like behaviors have frequently been linked to reduced hippocampal cell proliferation and neuronal differentiation (Kim et al. 2005; Sahay and Hen 2007), and related observations have been reported in selected BD-relevant paradigms, including prenatal stress models and BDNF haploinsufficiency models (Lemaire et al. 2000; Magarinos et al. 2011). Antidepressant treatment increases hippocampal neurogenesis in several rodent models (Malberg et al. 2000; Kodama et al. 2004), and blocking neurogenesis can attenuate antidepressant-like effects in specific contexts (Santarelli et al. 2003). In our Nestin-DTa model, anxiety-like changes were robust at four weeks (Figure 2), and forced-swim immobility was increased, while the progenitor and neuroblast compartments were expanded (Figure 3), indicating that lineage-stage marker abundance alone may not predict behavioral state. A parsimonious interpretation is that functional outcomes depend on the maturation, survival, and circuit integration of adult-born neurons, and that the neurogenic niche may be transiently dysregulated during the post-ablation period.

Notably, compensatory (or ‘reactive’) increases in adult hippocampal neurogenesis have been described in other settings where the neurogenic niche is perturbed, including seizure activity and brain injury, in which progenitor proliferation and immature neuron production can increase substantially (Parent et al. 1997; Kernie and Parent 2010; Neuberger et al. 2017). Such responses have been proposed to reflect recruitment of quiescent precursor pools and/or altered division dynamics of the remaining stem/progenitor population, together with changes in niche-derived signals, including inflammatory and trophic cues, and activity-dependent feedback that bias lineage output. In this context, the expansion of progenitors and immature neurons observed after partial aNSC depletion in Nestin-DTa mice may represent a homeostatic attempt to restore neurogenic output, while the functional consequences of this response are likely to depend on the timing and fidelity of maturation and circuit integration.

At eight weeks after aNSC ablation, we observed sustained expansion of progenitor and immature neuronal compartments (Figure 5), coinciding with behavioral changes suggestive of heightened arousal and increased activity (Figure 4). Prior studies indicate that manipulating the activity of newborn neurons can influence stress- and depression-related phenotypes (Tunc-Ozcan et al. 2019). Altered hippocampal excitability and plasticity have also been reported in patient-derived neuronal models of BD and in animal models that capture aspects of mania-like behavior (Walton et al. 2012; Hagihara et al. 2013; Mertens et al. 2015). Although our data do not directly establish that expanded immature neuronal populations drive the hyperactive phenotype, the temporal dissociation between cellular composition and behavioral state suggests that qualitative features of adult-born neurons, such as maturation stage, recruitment properties, and integration into dentate gyrus circuits, may influence behavioral outcome. In this context, the sustained enrichment of immature neuronal compartments at eight weeks could plausibly bias dentate gyrus network recruitment and plasticity, thereby contributing to increased exploratory and locomotor activity.

Importantly, while our model exhibits a transition from depression-like to hyperactive, mania-like behaviors, it remains unknown whether the underlying mechanisms are etiologically equivalent to those driving BD in humans. Accordingly, we present Nestin-DTa mice as a novel experimental platform to study mood-state transitions resembling key features of BD cyclicity, rather than as a definitive etiological model of BD. Several limitations should be considered. The behavioral tests capture only selected aspects of anxiety-, depression-, and mania-like behaviors; thus, our findings do not establish a causal relationship between AHN and complex affective states or fully model the clinical spectrum of BD. In addition, although we quantified neurogenic lineage-stage populations, we did not directly assess neuronal maturation, functional integration, or circuit-level excitability across 4 and 8 weeks after ablation, which would be required to mechanistically link cellular dynamics to behavioral changes. Future studies incorporating expanded behavioral paradigms, mature adult-born neuronal labeling strategies, and direct circuit readouts will be important to test the proposed scenarios and define how neurogenic-stage dynamics relate to state transitions in this model.
