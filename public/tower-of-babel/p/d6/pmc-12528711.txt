Double-layer silicon-based optical phased array transceiver based on cascaded phase control
Authors: Yaoyuan Zhang, Rui Wang, Ming Wei, Ruitao Jiang, Hongdong Zhang, Jer-Shing Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLight detection and ranging (LiDAR) is of central importance for modern artificial intelligence (AI) assisted automated intelligent optical devices. Silicon-based optical phased arrays (OPAs) have attracted widespread attention due to their rapid and efficient scanning capabilities. However, the complex waveguide routing structure has limited the spacing between elements of traditional two-dimensional (2D) OPAs, limiting the achievable angle range of 2D scanning. The traditional planar architecture struggles to meet high-density integration demands. Transitioning to the third dimension to leverage spatial degrees of freedom represents the only viable path toward future ultra-high integration. Consequently, we present a dual-layer optical phased array design employing a hierarchical architecture with cascaded phase shifters. This configuration strategically positions space-intensive waveguide routing in the lower layer while putting the 2D antenna array in the upper layer, achieving unprecedented spatial efficiency. The optimized layout enables an ultra-compact 5 μm antenna pitch, supporting far-field beam steering over ± 18.1° scanning angles. The primary technical challenge lies in interlayer coupling and the manufacturing of bilayer fine structures. However, recent advances in micro/nanofabrication technology have demonstrated the capability to meet our design specifications at current process nodes. Our design thus posts a new research direction for highly efficient, compact, and wide-range 2D beam control platforms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-19751-8.Keywords: Optical phased array, Silicon photonics integration, Micro-nano structure design, Waveguide components, Beam controlSubject terms: Nanoscale devices, Optoelectronic devices and components, Silicon photonics, Optical techniques

Introduction

With the rapid popularization of AI, an increasing number of unmanned manipulation devices are silently revolutionizing people’s lives. Among these intelligent devices, utilizing beam scanning to gather surrounding scene information is the pivotal step to achieving autonomous operation. Additionally, beam scanning devices represented by LiDAR have extensive applications in fields such as autonomous driving and aerial surveying1–3. Therefore, how to realize efficient, fast, and low-cost beam scanning is of great significance in practical applications4,5.

Traditional beam scanning typically involves a series of discrete components for mechanical scanning. However, this scanning method is constrained by slow speed, large volume, and high costs, making it challenging to achieve large-scale applications. In recent years, laser scanning technology based on OPAs has received widespread attention due to its excellent solid-state beam steering capability. Among the emerging OPA technologies, silicon-based OPAs have efficient compatibility with complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) technology6–9, enabled by utilizing a silicon-on-insulator platform, thereby achieving commercial-scale integration. Consequently, OPAs based on silicon photonics platforms have rapidly become a focus of research10–12.

The OPA scanning technology, as the name suggests, employs control over the optical phase to achieve beam steering for scanning purposes. Its primary output module consists of a group of antenna elements arranged in a one-dimensional13,14 or two-dimensional array15,16. By individually controlling the phase of each element, different phase retardations between array elements are chosen for different steering requirements, thus enabling the scanning functionality of far-field diffracted beams. This technology offers multiple advantages, such as small size, low cost, fast scanning speed, and so on.

Currently, the 2D arrangement of OPAs is constrained by antenna size and the complex waveguide routing within the array aperture, making it difficult to achieve scanning ranges greater than 10° for large-scale arrays17–19. There are some reports20,21 targeting the reduction of the phase shifter dimension, and the compact array arrangement is realized by reducing the internal complexity of the aperture by utilizing the off-aperture phase shifter to cross-control the transverse and longitudinal phases. However, this method uses a single phase-shifter to control the phases of multiple antennas, which reduces the maneuverability and accuracy of the far-field beams relative to the independently phase-controlled OPA. Therefore, individual phase control is required for each antenna in the high-precision beam control scenarios. Thus, how to reducing the element size within the array aperture is challenging. Exploring new solutions in space appears to be important to address this issue.

In this paper, we propose a double-layer 2D OPA layout scheme based on the cascade phase shifter. The high-efficiency interlayer coupler combined with the cascade phase-shifter principle is utilized, which can ultimately realize an ultra-compact layout of the 2D OPA. This scheme is by far the most compact one capable of realizing independent phase control of antennas. Building upon the conceptual design of the overall structure, we provide a systematic description and optimization of the silicon photonics devices involved, including optical antenna, interlayer coupler, phase shifter, and so on. Using the 16 × 16 antenna array as an example, we conduct simulation demonstrations of this model. Additionally, we characterize and analyze the scalability and manufacturing feasibility of this structure.

Double-layer array overall structure

Figure 1a illustrates the schematic diagram of the overall structural layout. To maximize spatial utilization, we have devised a double-layer OPA structure. In this configuration, the waveguide routing structures occupy larger spatial areas, and the external aperture phase shifter array is located in the lower layer. The antenna array and internal aperture phase shifter are situated in the upper layer, with inter-layer energy transfer facilitated by the single-mode interlayer couplers. Employing the cascaded phase shifting principle enables 2π phase control for each antenna. Compared to traditional structures, this design not only compresses the spacing between array elements but also doubles the expansion of waveguide routing space.

The schematic diagram of the OPA structural layout. (a) The overall structure. (b) The antenna array at the upper layer. (c) The waveguide array at the lower layer. (d) Part of the active integral array.

The specific transmission scheme is as follows: In the lower layer, a 1550 nm wavelength external laser signal is coupled into the chip’s internal bus waveguide through edge coupler 1(detailed information in supporting information S1). The light path is divided into 128 uniform routes via the Multi-Mode Interference (MMI) splitter array, with a phase controller set on each path to achieve a 2π phase change. Within the antenna array, due to the inevitable high crosstalk of linear waveguides, we employ a sinusoidal waveguide structure for internal signal transmission to minimize inter-element spacing (detail information in supporting information S2). Similarly, maximizing internal spatial utilization of the array is achieved through dual-port injection. It’s worth noting that while sinusoidal waveguides effectively reduce crosstalk on internal waveguide transmission paths, this structure is not suitable for long-distance transmission. Therefore, outside the array, a large number of S-shaped bends are required to achieve low-loss transition spacing. Additionally, due to differences in propagation paths, inevitable inconsistencies in terminal power will occur. To ensure uniform antenna radiation, power tuning of different paths is necessary.

Furthermore, to achieve the flexible switching between transmission and reception modes of the OPA, we utilize an optical switch to implement the free selection of transmission and reception functions through switch selection. In the reception mode, to enhance signal sensitivity, we employ coherent reception methods. This involves using the reference light input from edge coupler 2 and coherent processing with the received signal. The combined signal then undergoes on-chip detection, converting it into electrical signals for information processing. It’s important to note that the optical switch operates by controlling phase differences between optical paths to achieve constructive or destructive interference of signals for switching. Due to manufacturing errors, it’s challenging to precisely determine the switch state during execution. Therefore, we incorporate a power monitor to achieve accurate control in this structure.

Part of the active internal-aperture structure of the OPA is depicted in Fig. 1d. After the optical signal enters the array through S-bends, precise routing for each path unit is achieved via low-crosstalk sinusoidal waveguide routing within the array, as shown in Fig. 1c. It’s important to note that at this stage, the signal on each path is the sum of energy from two antennas. This signal needs to be transmitted to the upper layer via interlayer couplers. After that, the signal requires reasonable allocation through directional couplers (detailed information in supporting information S2). Optical signals from the branch paths are routed directly to the antennas, while signals from the main path need to pass through U-shaped bends before reaching the antennas.

Within the array, the half number of antennas need to be implanted with poly-silicon resistors for 2π phase control of the sub-arrays. The sub-array structure, as depicted in Fig. 1b, has a size of 5 μm × 10 μm, with a 5 μm spacing between antennas. Additionally, air gaps are present between sub-arrays to prevent chip overheating caused by the compact spacing of dense phase shifters. In the following sections, we will discuss in detail the phase control system.

Array phase control system

The core principle of OPAs lies in controlling the phase variation of each antenna to achieve arbitrary beam steering in the far field. Therefore, how to implement an efficient and adaptive phase control system is crucial. In our structure, by using the theory of cascaded phase shifting, combining the external-aperture phase shifter with the internal-aperture phase shifter enables efficient phase control for each antenna. At the same time, it provides greater freedom for compact arrangement of internal array components. The specific principle is as follows:

As shown in Fig. 2a, we divide the phase control into two levels. The first-level structure is located outside the aperture and consists of half the number of antennas. This phase control denoted as Φ1, governs the overall phase control of the subsequent array elements, enabling arbitrary phase difference control outside the aperture. The second-level structure is inside the aperture, where the phase control Φ2 is responsible for individual antenna phases, allowing for arbitrary phase difference control between antenna elements within the array. By properly cascading the two-level phase structures, arbitrary phase differences ΔΦ between antennas can be achieved, thereby enabling arbitrary beam control in the far field of the antennas.

External phase shifter

For the external phase shifters of the array, we continue to use the well-established technique of metal-induced thermal phase modulation. This involves placing a metal heater with a high resistivity above the waveguide. By applying different voltages, we control the temperature of both the metal and the waveguide, thereby altering the effective refractive index of the waveguide and inducing phase changes. The specific structure and temperature distribution are shown in Fig. 2b. The TiN metal layer, located above the waveguide, is responsible for heating. To prevent chip overheating caused by simultaneous operation of the phase shifters, we have etched air cooling slots between the waveguides. Additionally, we have conducted simulation and comparative analysis of the temperature required for achieving a 2π phase shift and the rise and fall times for different lengths of heated waveguides, as shown in Fig. 2c.

Internal phase shifter

For the internal phase shifters of the array, we control the phase by utilizing embedded poly-silicon resistors, as shown in Fig. 2d. Within the U-shaped waveguide, we embed the rectangular poly-silicon resistors. Temperature control is achieved by applying voltage to the resistors via copper wires. In the 180° bending region, direct bending will cause large optical losses due to the small radius. Therefore, we have made some structural adjustments to minimize the optical loss. The inner ring of the waveguide is designed with circular structures of r1 = 0.65 μm and r2 = 0.55 μm, while the outer ring adopts an elliptical design with semi-major axes lengths of a1 = 1.3 μm and a2 = 1.2 μm. As a result, optical losses of 0.65dB and 0.41dB are achieved respectively, with an overall optical loss of less than 1.4dB for the entire structure. Furthermore, we simulated the temperature distribution of this structure during the realization of a 2π phase shift, as shown in Fig. 2e, while Fig. 2f displays the temperature rise and fall curves for different regions at this temperature.

Interlayer coupler system

With the further advancement of silicon photonics integration research in recent years, it has become increasingly challenging to meet the requirements for high-density integration in some scenarios using 2D photonic integration alone. Therefore, it is both effective and necessary to explore 3D integration for photonic-electronic structures in the future8,22. The traditional interlayer signal transition scheme involves utilizing waveguide coupling principles, where dual-layer waveguides are placed closely, and signal coupling is achieved through a certain length of coupling distance23. However, in our structure, we need to achieve efficient and rapid coupling over short distances. At the same time, inevitable signal crosstalk occurs when waveguides are placed close. Therefore, the method is not suitable for our structure.

Previous research24, reported on U-shaped interlayer couplers to reduce the number of repeaters and equalizers over short communication distances. This work achieved efficient interlayer optical signal transmission, but the size was relatively large and limited to multimode structures. However, direct coupling at the connection of the bilayer structure inevitably leads to significant energy loss. Therefore, we place right-angled triangular sloped surfaces at the connection points to achieve a smooth transition of the signal. As depicted in Fig. 3c, the right-angled triangular structures in the transition region are labeled 1, 2, 3, and 4 respectively. Their respective lengths of the right-angle sides are denoted as a and b, the width of the vertical waveguide is denoted as w, and its height is h. We optimize the relevant parameters using the particle swarm optimization algorithm to improve transmission efficiency, as shown in Fig. 3b. Dielectric nanoantenna radiation system

Antenna unit structure design

As the terminal of the OPA, the rational design of the antenna is crucial. Since the far-field radiation of the array is formed by the mutual interference of each antenna, the size and diffraction efficiency of the antenna to some extent determine the compactness and overall radiation intensity of the array. In the reception mode, further consideration must be given to the aperture ratio of the antenna area within the array. Therefore, in the design process, we need to take all these factors into account. Due to the poor focusing ability of traditional linear grating structures when guiding and emitting lights, this phenomenon is more pronounced in the design process of small-sized units. Therefore, in this paper, we still adopt a non-linear focusing grating antenna. Based on our previous work25, further optimization and improvement of the antenna are discussed as follows:

Expandability and manufacturing characterization

Expandability

Firstly, we systematically optimized several waveguide components as detailed in the paper. The design principles we employed are not only applicable to our specific model but can also be extended to various scenarios requiring compact and efficient or high conversion efficiency structures. For instance, the single-mode layer-to-layer rapid coupling structure can be adapted to other single-mode scenarios, exploring new degrees of freedom in 3D space to achieve further integration of waveguide structures. The highly efficient edge couplers and low crosstalk sinusoidal waveguide structures are also suitable for applications with stringent spatial requirements.

Furthermore, we only conducted research using a 16 × 16 array as an example due to limited computing resources. In reality, our double-layer structure with a spacing of 5 μm between elements, can support even larger array designs, up to 24 × 24 arrays. Such dimensions are already sufficient for conventional application scenarios. For larger-scale application needs, additional waveguide routing layers can be added according to design requirements. In summary, our design approach also exhibits high scalability for large-scale arrays.

Finally, in our paper, we focused only on waveguide structure designs using silicon material. However, this approach can be equally applicable and insightful for OPA systems based on materials such as silicon nitride waveguides or other materials.

Manufacturing characterization

All our structures are developed based on CMOS processes26,27. For the fabrication of the double-layer structure, direct bonding using oxides can be employed. As for the layer-to-layer coupler, wet etching can be utilized to construct the connecting slopes. In terms of manufacturing technology, current high-precision etching processes can achieve a technological node of 10 nm. Therefore, the fabrication capabilities are adequate for manufacturing our designed structures.

Based on the current manufacturing process, the width tolerance of the conventional waveguide structure is between ± 5 nm, and the height tolerance is between ± 3 nm. Manufacturing errors will have an impact on the phase through the waveguide, which further affects the performance of the array far-field, and will lead to a decrease in the main lobe power in the far-field, and a rise in the side lobe power, and additionally cause a bias impact on the beam pointing control accuracy.

Based on this, during the manufacturing process, phase errors are inevitably introduced. We analyzed the effects of phase errors that may be introduced to the far field of the array structure. We applied random phase errors to the 16 × 16 antenna array ranging from − π to π, as shown in Fig. 6a. The specific far-field diffraction results are shown in Figs. 6b,c.

According to the simulation results, we observe that under random phase errors, the far-field energy scatters and exhibits strong background noise. Therefore, algorithmic calibration of the antenna array’s initial phase is necessary before conducting experimental tests28,29.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we have designed a 3D-integrated silicon-based OPA transceiver. By utilizing the cascaded phase shifting principle and integrating the layer-to-layer coupler, significantly reduces the spatial occupancy within the array, greatly improves the spatial scanning range in transmitting mode, and achieves higher energy conversion efficiency in receiving mode. In our research, we first optimized the key functional components used in the OPA. Based on this, we conducted the simulation analysis using a 16 × 16 array as an example. The results show that our structure can achieve a far-field scanning range of 18.1°×18.1° with a spacing of 5 μm × 5 μm between ultra-compact 2D array elements and an array area of 80 μm×80 μm. Furthermore, it achieves a beam FWHM accuracy of 0.98°. This scheme is by far the most compact one capable of realizing independent phase control of antennas. The analysis indicates that our structure can be adapted for larger-scale arrays, providing a new research direction for the compact and efficient design of OPAs. Based on this, utilizing this solution can achieve wide-angle, high-precision beam control, thereby having good application prospects in the fields of LiDAR and optical communications.
