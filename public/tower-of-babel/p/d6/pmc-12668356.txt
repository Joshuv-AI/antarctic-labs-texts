Effect of unified transdiagnostic treatment on negative affect, anxiety, and difficulties in emotional regulation in obese children: A randomized controlled trial
Authors: Shoaleh Amani, Nahid Bahadoriyan Lotf Abadi, Maryam Maadi Esfahan, Abbas Masjedi- Arani, Amir Sam Kianimoghadam, Mohsen Rostami
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Obesity is common among children and adolescents and leads to unpleasant consequences for them. Unified transdiagnostic therapy as an emotion-based therapy has many applications in the field of emotional disorders, and its effectiveness has been investigated in various fields. Since obesity also involves emotional issues, this research is intended for the purpose to investigate the effectiveness of this treatment on negative affect, anxiety, and emotion regulation problems in obese children.MATERIALS AND METHODS:The present study is a controlled clinical trial and was conducted in 2023. Forty children with obesity have been selected using the convenience sample method and randomly assigned to two experiential and control groups in a randomized controlled trial and assessor blinded study. For 8 weeks, the experiential group participated in a transdiagnostic treatment session lasting approximately 2 months; each session will last for 90 min. Participants from both the experiential and control groups completed Positive and Negative Affect Schedule for children, Multidimensional Anxiety Scale for Children, and Emotion Regulation Checklist as a pretest and posttest. Participants completed study questionnaires before treatment, immediately after, and at a follow-up 2–4 months later. A statistical method was used for the analysis of variance.RESULTS:In the experiential group, anxiety, negative affects and difficulties in emotion regulation significantly decreased (P < 0/05). In the control group, these changes were not observed.CONCLUSION:The results have shown that a unified transdiagnosis therapy is an efficient in reducing negative effects, anxiety, and difficulties in emotion regulation. Since childhood obesity has unpleasant psychological consequences, psychological interventions to reduce its effects should be considered.

Introduction

In addition to adults, obesity is also common in children, and its prevalence increases in children aged 5 to 9 years, and 11.6% of them live with obesity.[1] High monthly income, being an only child, and parental obesity are among the risk factors of obesity in children at this age.[2] According to the World Federation of Obesity and other organizations, including the American and Canadian Medical Associations, obesity is a chronic progressive disease.[3] Childhood and adolescent obesity are associated with the risk of premature mortality[4] and are often associated with mental disorders.[5] Several factors play a role in the etiology of childhood obesity, including genetics,[6] social and environmental factors,[7] and psychological factors.[8]

Childhood and adolescent obesity can lead to a wide range of unpleasant physical and psychological consequences,[9] and psychological disorders occur more often in these people.[10] Obesity is associated with higher scores of negative affects (NAs) such as anger, hatred, fear, and shame.[11] NA reflects overall negative condition and indicates that people with higher NA are more anxious and upset and have a negative view of themselves.[12] According to the affect regulation model of binge eating, patients binge eat to reduce NAs and use food as a means of distraction to relieve it.[13] NA correlates with neuroticism and is a personality trait that refers to the tendency to react negatively to life events.[14] Personality traits play a significant role both as risk factors and as protective factors in causing overweight and obesity, and neuroticism, as one of the five big personality factors, is one of the risk factors related to obesity.[15]

Previous research has shown that obese children are more likely to have anxiety symptoms than their peers.[16] Many data show that there is a relationship between obesity and symptoms such as anxiety and depression.[161718] Obesity itself may be associated with the risk of anxiety and depression in children and adolescents.[19] Anxiety is a feeling of fear or worry and is associated with a physically stimulated central nervous system.[20] Among obese children, significant anxiety is associated with increased physical dissatisfaction and psychological distress of parents, as well as decreased quality of life.[21]

One of the dominant explanations for eating behavior is that there is a form of escape from negative emotions.[22] Obese people have more difficulties in emotion regulation and lack planning strategies and emotional awareness.[23] In obese individuals, one of the factors involved in overeating is the suppression of emotional expression.[24] Emotion regulation refers to the automatic and controlled processes through which people influence the type of emotion they experience, when they have it, and how they experience and express it.[25] If people effectively use emotion regulation skills, unhealthy behaviors related to obesity will decrease and can help prevent and treat obesity.[26] One of the interventions that target problematic emotion regulation attempts is the Unified Protocols for Treatment of Emotional Disorders.[27]

The Unified Protocol for the Trans diagnostic Treatment of Affective Disorders (UP) is an emotion-focused cognitive behavioral intervention that targets the underlying cause of all anxiety, depression, and related disorders, that is, impaired emotion regulation.[28] A recent systematic review has shown that UP can significantly reduce anxiety and depression.[29] It seems that emotion regulation can be considered as a mediating factor through which UP can reduce anxiety and depression.[30] UP consists of five core modules or components that target the mood characteristics, specifically neuroticism, which underlies all anxiety, depression, and related disorders.[28] The results of various research studies such as Bullis et al.,[31] Kennedy et al.,[27] Steele et al.,[32] Zirakbash et al.,[33] and Ghaderi et al.[34] have shown that unified transdiagnostic treatment is effective in improving psychological components such as emotion regulation, NAs, and emotional disorders such as anxiety and depression.

As mentioned above, among the factors that can be effective in obesity are anxiety and NAs, as well as difficulty in regulating emotions. Considering the high prevalence of obesity in children and its unpleasant consequences, it is necessary to take measures to prevent and treat it. Unified transdiagnostic therapy is among the interventions that have been effective in the field of emotion regulation. Since until now the study has not focused on the effectiveness of this treatment in the field of childhood obesity, the purpose of the present study is to investigate the effectiveness of unified transdiagnostic treatment on NAs, anxiety, and difficulty in emotional regulation in obese children.

Material and Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a randomized controlled and single-blind design to assess the effectiveness of unified transdiagnostic treatment. An impartial researcher performed the randomization process without any knowledge or involvement in the treatment, and a simple method of randomization was used. Initially, adequate arrangements were made with the Education Department in Isfahan Province and children whose overweight was diagnosed by an endocrinologist and met other inclusion criteria were included in the study.

Study participants and sampling

Participants will include obese and overweight children. The inclusion criteria will be (1) obesity and overweight (BMI>95th), (2) children aged 7 to 15 years with overweight and obesity, (3) not receiving psychological services outside of the treatment session, (4) having primary education, (5) completing the informed consent, and (6) outside of therapy, parents who do not receive psychological care. The criteria for exclusion of children will be as follows: (1) parents’ dissatisfaction with the child’s attendance to treatment sessions and (2) participating in therapeutic programs related to a therapy method.

G*Power (http://g-power,apponic.com) has been used to determine the sample size. With an effect size of 0.45, an alpha of 0.05, and a power of 0.8, a total of 44 respondents in two equal sized groups were needed.[35] Considering the possibility of dropping, 46 children were included in the study. In total, 46 obese children were including to the study and were considered eligible. Six participants declined participation. Subsequently, 40 participants were randomly allocated to intervention and control waiting lists.

Data collection tool and technique

A set of study questionnaires were completed by participants prior to treatment, immediately following the treatment phase pretreatment and at follow-up 2–4 months.

After making the necessary arrangements with the Education Department of Isfahan province, explanations were given to the parents of children who had been diagnosed with overweight by an endocrinologist and met other inclusion criteria pertaining to the objectives and method of the study. The respondents have given their consent after agreement. Positive and Negative Affect Schedule for Children (PANAS), Multidimensional Anxiety Scale for Children (MASC), and Emotion Regulation Checklist (ERC) were also administered to them before the intervention. Subsequently, intervention and control waitlists have been randomly divided into two groups of respondents. Respondents from the intervention group received 8-session unified transdiagnostic treatment. Although no therapy was given to the waiting list participants, only UP intervention was administered after completion of a data collection.

Measures

Demographic characteristics

Age, body mass index, level of education, and weight are included in the demographic characteristics questionnaire.

Negative affect

For children, negative effects have been measured using the PANAS. The PANAS is a self-reported adjective checklist and 5 Likert-type scale that contains 10 items for measuring positive and negative affects.[36] Its internal consistency scores are between 0.80 and 0.86, which shows that it has good internal consistency.[37] The Persian version of the short form of PANAS-C has suitable psychometric properties and can be used to evaluate positive and negative emotions in Iranian children and adolescents, and Cronbach’s alpha for the original and short forms of this scale was 0.85 and 0.81, respectively.[38]

Anxiety

Anxiety was measured using the Multidimensional Anxiety Scale for Children. The MASC is a self-report questionnaire comprising 39 items with four main subscales.[39] The internal reliability and the 3-week and 3-month retesting reliability of this scale were satisfactory to excellent.[40] In addition, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.79 in Iranian populations, the MASC has good psychometric properties.[41]

Emotion regulation

The ERC was used for measuring the regulation of emotion. ERC has 24 items and is a 4-point Likert scale. The ERC includes two dimensions of Emotion Regulation and Emotion Lability/Negativity.[42] Shields and Cicchetti (1997)[43] reported good internal reliability for the Emotion Regulation (α = 0.83) and Emotion Lability/Negativity (α = 0.96) subscales. This questionnaire has good validity and reliability among Iranian children and adolescents, and its Cronbach’s alpha is reported as 0.86.[44]

Ethical consideration

Informed consent was acquired from their parents or legal guardians to participate in the study.

(Ethics code: IR.SBMU.MSP.REC.1400.521).

Statistical analysis method

The research data were analyzed by a researcher who was not involved in delivering the treatment. SPSS 24 software (IBM, SPSS Statistics 24) has been used to analyze the collected data. Research data were analyzed using descriptive statistics (i.e., mean and standard deviation) and inferential statistics (including repeated-measures ANOVA).

Interventions: Unified transdiagnostic treatment

Participants attended eight face-to-face sessions based on Barlow Unified Diagnostic Therapy book. The eight manualized treatment modules described by Barlow were divided into a number of sequential therapy sessions. The details of the sessions are listed in Table 1.

Depending on the anxiety scale, these encrustations can be visual or reality during or outside the session. Before the confrontation, the therapist explains the logic of the encounter and its barriers to the client; this allows the client to respond to emotions more efficiently while anticipating possible events. During these sessions, clients are helped to apply the skills learned during treatment, such as relaxation exercises and mindfulness, to facilitate the treatment process and remove obstacles.8. Furthermore, a significant interaction between group and time was observed for the variables of NA, anxiety, blaming others, self-blame, perspective-taking, rumination, acceptance, planning, reappraisal, and positive focus (P < 0.01), indicating differences across stages and between the experimental and control groups for these variables. However, no significant interaction between group and time was found for catastrophizing and planning (P > 0.05), showing no differences across stages or groups for these variables.

However, for anxiety, perspective-taking, and catastrophizing, FFF values did not indicate significant differences between the groups (P > 0.05), showing no meaningful group differences for these variables.

Discussion

This trial aims to explore the effectiveness of a single transdiagnostic treatment for obesity in overweight children using both randomized and supervised trials. This trial is especially important considering the effectiveness of UP for anxiety, NA, and emotional dysregulation in this population. The results of our study have shown that the average scores of NA, anxiety, and difficulty in regulating emotions in the post-test and follow-up were higher in the experimental group than in the control group. The findings of this research show that unified transdiagnostic therapy is effective in NA, anxiety, and emotional dysregulation in obese children. The findings of this research are consistent with the study of Todd J. Farchione et al.,[45] who showed that UP leads to a significant improvement in negative and positive affect levels. As a transdiagnostic intervention program, UP focuses on major emotional disorders in psychopathology to change a wide range of outcomes, including positive and negative affect.[46] UP uses techniques that moderate people’s emotions.[32] On the other hand, emotion regulation can affect people’s positive and negative affects.[47] So, it can be concluded that this treatment can have an impact on NAs by using effective emotion regulation strategies.

Another finding of this study is that this intervention is effective on anxiety. Masaya Ito et al.[48] showed that UP is an effective approach for patients with anxiety disorders. Also, the findings are consistent with the results obtained from Judith M. Laposa et al.’s[49] study, which shows that this intervention has led to a significant reduction in general anxiety, worry, social anxiety, and panic. It is also consistent with the study of Jacqueline R. Bullis,[31] Alberto González-Robles,[50] and Julia García-Escalera.[51] The improvement of this variable in these children after the intervention may occur due to several factors. According to UP models, the root and basis of emotional disorders such as anxiety is the temperamental tendency of people to experience high levels of negative emotions (i.e., neuroticism) and high emotional response, which leads to problematic and ineffective regulation of emotional experiences.[52] The findings indicate that emotion regulation strategies are among the determining factors in the onset and persistence of anxiety disorders.[53] It seems that this intervention leads to the reduction of anxiety or prevents it from occurring due to the techniques used to regulate people’s emotions.

The last finding of this research is that UP can affect the difficulties of emotional regulation. This finding is in line with the results of the Ghasemzadeh et al.,[54] who showed that this intervention can improve emotion regulation in mothers of children with type 1 diabetes. It is also consistent with Judith M. Laposa’s study.[49] In explaining this finding, it can be said that UP is an effective emotion regulation-based intervention that focuses on emotion dysregulation, which plays a central role in all types of psychopathology and aims to increase maladaptive emotion regulation skills and develop efficient skills.[46] But this protocol has not been effective on the catastrophizing variable. This finding is inconsistent with the study conducted by Mazaheri et al.[55]

Limitations and recommendations

In view of its provisional nature, the study has certain limitations. In particular, the sample size was very low and could have prevented us from detecting substantial differences in some of our analyses. It is recommended that this study be further investigated with a larger sample size in the future. Second, the population of this study was limited to the schools of one province and cannot be generalized to other cities. It would be better to expand the study sample in future studies. Third, most of the measures in this study were self-reported. Therefore, known biases associated with self-report methods were not avoided in this study. Different results may have been obtained using other data collection tools.

Conclusion

In sum, the present study provides preliminary evidence for the effectiveness of UP on anxiety, NAs, and emotional dysregulation in obese children. The current results require further study through replication. More future work is needed to understand more precisely how these results are achieved. Finally, it is suggested to follow up the results of treatment with transdiagnostics on a long-term basis. In the intervention group, there were several significant improvements for self-reported negative emotions, anxiety, and difficulty in emotion regulation, but not in the control group. Future research should seek to replicate the findings in a larger sample.
