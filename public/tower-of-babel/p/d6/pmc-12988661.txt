The Combined Effect of Platelet-to-HDL-Cholesterol Ratio and Glycemic Metabolism Status on the Prognosis of Patients Undergoing Percutaneous Coronary Intervention: A Large-Scale Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Zhiqiang Yang, Zhifan Li, Yanan Gao, Ang Gao, Xue Li, Yong Wang, Kefei Dou, Xiao Wang, Tingting Guo, Hong Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Platelet-to-HDL-cholesterol ratio (PHR), an emerging inflammatory biomarker, shows promise as a predictor in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and coronary artery disease (CAD). However, the association between PHR and glycemic metabolism status and their combined effects on the prognosis of CAD patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) remains unclear.MethodsA total of 31,761 CAD patients hospitalized for PCI were included. The primary endpoint was defined as major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs). According to the median PHR levels, patients were grouped into higher levels of PHR (PHR-H) and lower levels of PHR (PHR-L) and further divided by glycemic status into four groups: PHR-H/T2DM, PHR-H/Non-T2DM, PHR-L/T2DM, PHR-L/Non-T2DM.ResultsDuring the 1-year follow-up, 2258 (7.1%) MACEs occurred. Elevated PHR conferred 17% excess risk for MACEs. A 1-unit increase in LnPHR was associated with a 26% increased risk of MACEs in the T2DM cohort, and a 27% increase of MACEs risks in the non-T2DM cohort. Compared to those in PHR-H/T2DM group, patients in PHR-L/T2DM, PHR-H/Non-T2DM, PHR-L/Non-T2DM had significantly decreased risk of MACEs [adjusted hazard ratio (aHR): 0.83, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.73–0.96, P =0.009; aHR: 0.81, 95% CI: 0.69–0.95, P =0.009; aHR: 0.70, 95% CI: 0.60–0.83, P <0.001; respectively]. Cox regression analysis also indicated the highest risk of MACEs among patients in PHR-H/T2DM group than in other groups (P for trend <0.001). Restricted cubic spline analyses revealed positive linear associations of PHR with MACEs, all-cause death, and unplanned target vessel revascularization, while a significant non-linear relationship between PHR and myocardial infarction. Additionally, subgroup analysis indicated consistent effects on MACEs within diverse subsets.ConclusionElevated PHR levels combined with T2DM were associated with worse clinical outcomes after PCI at 1-year follow-up. The PHR may serve as a valuable predictor for identifying high-risk patients post-PCI, particularly in diabetic cohorts.Keywords: platelet-to-HDL-cholesterol ratio, inflammation, glycemic metabolism status, coronary artery disease, percutaneous coronary intervention, prognosis

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) represent the predominant contributor to global mortality, imposing substantial pressure on the medical system.1 Inflammation plays a pivotal role in the development of atherosclerosis, and further leads to the progression of coronary artery disease (CAD).2 Accumulating evidence has established the clinical predictive significance of inflammation indicators among CAD individuals, and anti-inflammatory therapeutics hold the potential benefits in hindering the progression of CAD.3,4

It is well-known that platelets are involved in the process of hemostasis and thrombosis. Recently, platelets have been recognized as modulators of inflammation, and participate in the progression of atherosclerosis.5 Besides, elevated circulating platelet levels demonstrated a dose-dependent correlation with incident type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) development,6 emerging as a potential modulator in the pathogenesis of T2DM.7 Thus, platelets are recognized as sensitive indicator for quantifying the pathophysiological of metabolic and CVD. However, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) possesses benefits in anti-thrombotic, anti-inflammatory, and cardioprotective properties.8 There was an inverse correlation between HDL-C concentrations and cardiovascular (CV) risk, and this association has predictive validity in the population of T2DM.9 Therefore, serum platelet count and HDL-C are critical indicators of inflammatory changes and hemorheology. The platelet-to-HDL-C ratio (PHR) is a novel inflammatory biomarker integrating hematologic and lipid parameters, and may serve as a useful tool for the screening of patients with diabetes/prediabetes.10 Furthermore, previous investigations revealed that high PHR levels were related to CVD mortality, and the incidence of heart failure (HF).11,12

Inflammation is regarded as a common pathogenesis of atherosclerosis and T2DM, and T2DM is one of the independent risk factors of CVD. Therefore, PHR may be a potential predictor in patients with T2DM and CAD. However, there is currently limited evidence regarding the prognostic value of PHR in CAD patients with impaired glycemic metabolism status. Whether PHR and T2DM had a synergetic effect on the prognosis remains unknown. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the relationship between PHR and glycemic metabolism status and to further evaluate their synergetic effect on the prognosis of CAD patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), addressing a critical gap in personalized cardiovascular risk management.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

This exploratory retrospective analysis was derived from a single-center prospective observational study conducted at a single tertiary cardiovascular center. During the three-year study period (2016–2018), 41,090 consecutive CAD patients undergoing PCI were enrolled at Fuwai Hospital, National Center for Cardiovascular Diseases, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (Beijing, China). The exclusion criteria were as follows, (1) in-hospital death; (2) missing platelet and HDL-C data; (3) missing other crucial baseline data; (4) hepatic and kidney dysfunction; (5) malignant tumor. Following rigorous screening, the final analytical cohort comprised 31,761 eligible subjects. These participants were stratified into four comparative groups based on median PHR values and T2DM status: PHR-H/T2DM (n =5988), PHR-L/T2DM (n =5454), PHR-H/Non-T2DM (n =9898) and PHR-L/Non-T2DM (n =10,421).

A systematic follow-up protocol involving scheduled telephone consultations or outpatient visits was implemented at 1-, 6-, and 12-month post-PCI. The primary endpoint was defined as major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs), a composite outcome incorporating all-cause death, myocardial infarction (MI), and unplanned target vessel revascularization (TVR) within 12 months after index PCI. Secondary endpoints were individual MACEs components at 12 months’ follow-up. Event prioritization followed clinical severity hierarchy (death > MI > revascularization) in cases of multiple concurrent events. All endpoint adjudication underwent blinded verification by at least two independent cardiovascular specialists. All endpoint events were independently adjudicated by two cardiovascular specialists who were blinded to the patient’s PHR, group allocation, and glycemic status. Any discordance or uncertainty was resolved by consensus with a third senior cardiologist, whose decision was final.

This investigation was approved by the Ethics Committee of Funai Hospital (No. 2021-1603), and the study protocol was conducted in strict accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Due to the non-interventional nature of this retrospective analysis and implementation of rigorous de-identification protocols for all patient data, the institutional ethics committee granted a waiver of individual informed consent requirements. The study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Treatment and Procedure

All PCIs were conducted by experienced interventional cardiologists following contemporary clinical practice guidelines. The choice of equipment, interventional strategies, and anticoagulation during PCI was at the discretion of individual physicians. Quantitative coronary angiographic analyses were performed by at least 2 independent cardiologists. Perioperative antiplatelet management involved administration of a loading regimen comprising aspirin (300 mg) and a P2Y12 receptor antagonist (clopidogrel 300 mg or ticagrelor 180 mg) before PCI, provided patients had not received adequate pre-treatment with dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT). Post-PCI antiplatelet therapy consisted of daily aspirin (100 mg) combined with either clopidogrel (75 mg once daily) or ticagrelor (90 mg twice daily), maintained for at least 12 months.

Data Collection

Baseline information on demographic features, cardiovascular risk factors, laboratory tests, PCI characteristics, and medication at discharge were systematically collected from the electronic medical system. Venous blood sampling was collected from all patients within 24 hours post-admission, and with a fasting state (>8 hours). The complete blood count, lipid profiles, and other biochemical parameters were measured at the core laboratory in Fuwai Hospital. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was derived through the application of the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation.

Definitions

The PHR was mathematically derived from standardized laboratory measurements using the formula: PHR = platelet count (×109/L) / HDL-C concentration (mmol/L), where platelet quantification was performed via automated hematology analysis and HDL-C levels were determined enzymatically. CAD confirmation was established through invasive coronary angiography, defined as ≥50% luminal diameter stenosis in ≥1 major epicardial coronary artery. T2DM was diagnosed according to American Diabetes Association (2023) criteria, requiring either: (1) Classic hyperglycemic symptoms with random plasma glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L (200 mg/dL); (2) Fasting plasma glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dL) from venous sampling; (3) HbA1c ≥6.5% measured by NGSP-certified methods; (4) 2-hour postprandial glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L during 75g OGTT; or (5) established clinical diagnosis with ongoing glucose-lowering therapy.13 The diagnosis of hypertension and dyslipidemia is defined according to guidelines.14,15 Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) /height (m)2. Chronic total occlusion (CTO) lesion was determined as thrombolysis in myocardial infarction (TIMI) flow grade of 0 in a native coronary artery for more than 3 months.

Statistical Analysis

Quantitative parameters were characterized using distribution-dependent descriptors: normally distributed variables as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed data were summarized with median (interquartile range, IQR). Categorical parameters were expressed as absolute counts with proportional frequencies. Continuous variables were compared by one-way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis test among groups, whereas categorical comparisons employed a chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. The PHR was analyzed both as a continuous variable and as a categorical variable dichotomized at the median. The median was chosen as a clinically intuitive and data-driven cutoff for initial risk stratification, aligning with methodologies used in prior study.16 Given the right-skewed distribution of the PHR, the natural logarithm of PHR (LnPHR) was used in all continuous variable analyses to approximate a normal distribution. The correlation between PHR and glucose (fasting blood glucose, FBG) or inflammatory (C-reactive protein, CRP) parameters was evaluated by Pearson correlation analysis. Temporal patterns of MACEs and other endpoints were visualized through Kaplan-Meier estimators, with intergroup survival differences assessed via stratified Log rank testing.

Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression quantified risk associations, presenting adjusted hazard ratios (aHR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) for baseline PHR levels and glycemic metabolism status. Covariate selection in models followed a hybrid approach combining factors with p <0.1 in univariate regression analysis and established cardiovascular prognosticators. Four progressively adjusted models are as follows, Model 1: Unadjusted; Model 2: demographic-adjusted (age and sex); Model 3: adjusted for variables in Model 2 as well as hypertension, dyslipidemia, HF, stroke, prior MI, prior PCI, prior coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), anemia, triglyceride (TG), low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (LDL-C), eGFR, CRP, left main/three-vessel disease, CTO, in-stent restenosis (ISR), number of lesions treated, number of implanted stents ≥3, and total stents length >60mm; Model 4: comprehensively adjusted for variables in Model 3 as well as β-blocker, ACEI/ARB/ARNI, insulin, and oral hypoglycemic agents.

Linear or non-linear associations were examined using restricted cubic splines (RCS) with three and four knots, and were comprehensively adjusted in Model 4. To address potential confounding from acute-phase variability, we conducted a sensitivity analysis confined to patients with chronic coronary syndrome (CCS). Prespecified subgroup analyses were performed to assess differential associations between PHR and glycemic status with MACEs. These stratified interaction assessments were represented through forest plots, incorporating the following stratification variables: age, sex, BMI, acute coronary syndrome (ACS), hypertension, TG, LDL-C, and left main/three-vessel disease.

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 25.0 (version 25.0; Chicago, United States) and R software (version 4.4.1; Vienna, Austria). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Between January 2016 and December 2018, a total of 41,090 patients with CAD who underwent PCI at Fuwai Hospital were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 9329 patients were excluded due to in-hospital death (n =27), missing platelet and HDL-C data (n =4647), incomplete baseline information (n =516), hepatic and renal dysfunction (n =3117), or malignant tumors (n =1022). After exclusions, 31,761 eligible patients were enrolled in the final analysis. A detailed flow of patient inclusion and exclusion is presented in Figure 1.

The enrolled cohort had a mean age of 60±10 years, and was predominantly male (76.3%). During the 1-year follow-up, 129 (0.4%) all-cause deaths, 131 (0.4%) MIs, and 1998 (6.3%) unplanned TVRs. In total, 2258 (7.1%) MACEs occurred.

Baseline Characteristics

Study participants were stratified according to the median PHR levels and glycemic metabolism status. This classification yielded the following four groups: PHR-H/T2DM (n =5988), PHR-L/T2DM (n =5454), PHR-H/Non-T2DM (n =9898) and PHR-L/Non-T2DM (n =10,421). Comparative analyses of the four groups revealed significant divergences in baseline characteristics (Table 1). Patients in the PHR-H/T2DM group were more likely to be accompanied by hypertension, dyslipidemia, HF, CAD family history, prior MI, and anemia. Besides, they had significantly higher levels of white blood cell, TG, FBG, and CRP, while lower in total cholesterol (TC), HDL-C, LDL-C, and left ventricular ejection fraction. Patients with high PHR and T2DM had more CTO lesions treated, and a higher proportion of stents implanted ≥3, and total stent length >60 mm. As for medications, all of the enrolled patients received DAPT therapy, more patients in the PHR-H/T2DM group were treated with β-blocker, angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEI)/angiotensin II receptor blocker (ARB)/angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitor (ARNI), insulin, oral hypoglycemic agents, and Nitrate.

Predictive Value of PHR and Different Glucose Metabolism Status on MACEs

Over 1-year follow-up, the prevalence of MACEs in the low and high PHR groups was 6.5% (1026/15,875) and 7.8% (1232/15,886), respectively. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed a heightened risk trajectory for MACEs in patients exhibiting either T2DM or elevated PHR (All Log-rank P <0.001) (Figure 2). In unadjusted analysis, elevated PHR conferred 21% excess risk for MACEs (crude HR =1.21, 95% CI 1.11–1.31, P <0.001). This association persisted robustly following comprehensive adjustment in Model 4 (aHR: 1.17, 95% CI: 1.07–1.28, P <0.001) (Table 2).

In T2DM patients, higher levels of PHR were associated with an increased risk of MACEs, both as a continuous variable (crude HR: 1.30, 95% CI: 1.09–1.55 per 1-unit increase in LnPHR, P =0.004 and aHR: 1.26, 95% CI: 1.04–1.53 per 1-unit increase in LnPHR, P =0.019) and a categorical variable (crude HR: 1.20, 95% CI: 1.05–1.36, P =0.007 and aHR: 1.19, 95% CI: 1.04–1.37, P =0.012) in univariate and multivariate analysis. Meanwhile, PHR in patients without T2DM had similar results as patients with T2DM (Table 2).

Synergistic Impacts of PHR and Glycemic Metabolism Status for 1-year Clinical Outcomes

The stratified analysis revealed differential cardiovascular risk profiles across the combined effects of high or low PHR and with or without T2DM, and the incidence of MACEs was 8.7% (523/5988), 7.4% (402/5454), 7.2% (709/9898), and 6.0% (624/10,421), respectively (Table 3). Besides, there were significant differences in MACEs, MI, and unplanned TVR among the four groups (P <0.001, P =0.028, and P <0.001, respectively). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated a synergistic risk interaction between PHR-H and T2DM, with the PHR-H/T2DM group exhibiting the most pronounced risk trajectory for MACEs, MI and unplanned TVR (Log-rank P <0.001, Log-rank P =0.028, and Log-rank P <0.001) (Figure 3).

To address potential confounding from acute-phase inflammatory responses, this sensitivity analysis restricted the cohort to patients with CCS, thereby excluding those presenting with ACS (Table S1). Discussion

This large-scale retrospective study provides novel evidence that the PHR synergizes with T2DM to stratify cardiovascular risk in patients undergoing PCI. We demonstrate that the concomitant presence of high PHR and T2DM identifies a distinct high-risk phenotype with the worst prognosis, significantly beyond the risk associated with either factor alone. Specifically, we found that PHR was an independent predictor for MACEs in CAD patients undergoing PCI. Compared to T2DM patients with higher PHR, the other three groups had significantly lower risks of MACEs, MI, and unplanned TVR. To our knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively evaluate this synergistic interaction and its implications for post-PCI outcome prediction.

Platelets, developed from megakaryocytes in the bone marrow, not only play an important role in thrombosis and hemostasis, but also regulate the process of inflammation, contributing to the development of atherosclerosis. Platelets play an important role in the development of atherosclerosis and CVD.17 Activated platelets can release pro-inflammatory and pro-angiogenic mediators, such as chemokines and cytokines. They interact directly or through mediators with endothelial cells, stimulating the expression of adhesion molecules like intercellular adhesion molecule-1 and vascular cell adhesion molecule-1. This facilitates the recruitment of leukocytes to the arterial wall and promotes the development of foam cells—a key characteristic of atherosclerosis.18 High levels of platelet count are not only associated with the development of CVD in the general population, but also independently associated with worse outcomes in STEMI patients.19,20 Besides, increased platelet activation was related to lipid disorders, increased the risk of atherosclerosis, thrombotic events, and adverse CV outcomes in CAD patients.21,22 A recent study found that the pathological state of platelets in T2DM patients increased the risk of CV events.23 Therefore, abnormal inflammatory responses and metabolic disturbances may jointly contribute to the risk of CVD onset and progression.

HDL-C, as a “good cholesterol”, has anti-inflammatory and anti-thrombotic properties.8 The mechanisms of reducing inflammatory responses include promoting reverse cholesterol transport, inhibiting the oxidation of LDL-C, clearing circulating pro-inflammatory factors, and so on.24 In addition, HDL has been shown to have an impact on coagulation by inhibiting platelet activation, decreasing platelet aggregation, and reducing thrombosis.25 A lower level of HDL-C was associated with a higher incidence of adverse CV events.26 Our RCS analysis revealed a non-linear relationship between PHR and MI, which may reflect that HDL-C will loss protective function when below a specific threshold. As demonstrated by a cohort study, a U-shaped relationship between HDL-C and MI or all-cause death.27 This observation, while requiring confirmation in larger studies, underscores that the PHR-MI relationship may not be monotonic and highlights the complex biology integrating inflammation and lipid metabolism.

PHR, derived from two of the most routine, low-cost, and universally available blood tests, and was initially considered a useful biomarker for predicting metabolic syndrome (MetS), increased with the severity of MetS, suggesting that PHR may serve as a potential indicator for CV risk.28 Other studies further investigated the association of the PHR with other diseases, such as depression,11 hyperuricemia,29 chronic obstructive pulmonary disease,30 and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease.31 In addition, elevated PHR levels were associated with an increased risk of stroke,32 HF,12 and CVD mortality.11 A cross-sectional study found that higher PHR levels are associated with the stenosis of coronary arteries in CAD patients.33

T2DM, a common chronic metabolic disease, increases the CV risk in patients with stable or acute coronary syndrome. Patients with DM have worse clinical outcomes after coronary revascularization with either PCI or CABG.34,35 Santos et al found that poor glycemic control was correlated with a higher incidence of stent failure, increasing the risk of CV events.36 Chen et al found that PHR has the potential to screen patients with diabetes/prediabetes, and the possible mechanisms include, chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and lipid metabolism disorders.10

A cohort study further explored the effect of PHR on mortality in CAD patients with or without T2DM and found that higher PHR levels with T2DM were associated with higher all-cause mortality and cardiac mortality.37 Besides, PHR levels may serve as a valuable predictor for identifying high-risk individuals. Currently, no studies have investigated PHR for predicting comprehensive CV outcomes in CAD patients undergoing PCI. Our study provides strong evidence to support a synergistic adverse effect of elevated PHR and T2DM on clinical outcomes in CAD patients undergoing PCI, revealing that PHR is more strongly associated with glycemic metabolic status and that patients with elevated PHR and impaired glucose metabolism have worse 1-year outcomes, including MACEs, MI, and unplanned TVR. Besides, the sensitivity analysis demonstrates that the prognostic utility of the baseline PHR and its synergistic effect with T2DM is not driven solely by the acute inflammatory response in ACS patients. The association remains strong and clinically significant in stable CCS population, and this strengthens the validity of PHR as a robust risk-stratification tool across the CAD spectrum. Therefore, PHR is an easy obtained, convenient composite biomarker that integrates inflammatory and metabolic pathways and can stratify CAD patients at high cardiovascular risk.

FBG, a cheap and convenient measurement, can rapidly monitor blood glucose, and a previous study revealed that the CVD burden is attributable to high FBG.32 Consistent with previous study,33 we also observed a positive correlation between PHR and FBG, suggesting its potential utility in stratifying patients, particularly those with T2DM, based on FBG levels. However, PHR in non-T2DM patients was not significant correlated with FBG in our study. As illustrated by Chen et al that PHR was positively associated with diabetes/prediabetes,10 which suggesting the dependence of PHR on disorders of glucose metabolism. Insulin resistance and chronic hyperglycemia in patients with T2DM can activate inflammatory pathways (stress-activated Jun N-terminal kinases and the transcription factor NF κB),38 which may further increase PHR. However, in non-T2DM individuals with relatively normal glycemic metabolism, this association is weakened. The correlation analysis provided mechanistic insight that PHR elevation is more specifically linked to established dysglycemic pathways rather than to normal metabolic variation. Besides, a significant positive association between PHR and CRP was observed, highlighting PHR’s inherent inflammatory nature.

Based on the above findings, PHR may serve as a potential therapy target for patients with T2DM undergoing PCI, and no specific treatment for managing the level of PHR until now. Our subgroup analysis indicated that patients with a BMI <28kg/m2 showed a trend of reduced risk of MACEs. Therefore, well management of weight may improve the long-term outcomes in CAD patients undergoing PCI. Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs), with semaglutide as a prominent example, was initially for the treatment of T2DM. Furthermore, it also demonstrates a good efficacy in weight management.39,40 GLP-1 RAs exert their effects through dual central and peripheral mechanisms: centrally, they modulate brain regions controlling appetite to regulate hunger and energy expenditure via neurotransmitter and peptide release, while peripherally, they improve glycemic control by enhancing insulin secretion, reducing glucagon release, delaying gastric emptying, and regulating gut hormones.41 A randomized controlled trial has shown that for CVD patients with a BMI of ≥27 kg/m2 and without diabetes, treatment with GLP-1 RAs can reduce the incidence of adverse cardiovascular events.40 Subgroup analysis revealed that the protective effect of GLP-1RAs on cardiovascular events was consistently observed in both diabetic and non-diabetic patients, as well as in obese and nonobese patients.42 Interestingly, previous numerous studies found that GLP-1RAs have anti-inflammatory effects.43–45 Therefore, based on the above considerations, we speculate that GLP-1RAs might have a cardiovascular protective effect on high-risk patients in our study, and this requires further study to confirm.

Chronic inflammation has been demonstrated to participate in the progression of CVD, and anti-inflammatory therapy has an effect in halting the progression of CVD.4 There were several anti-inflammatories, such as colchicine, canakinumab, and so on. Previous studies have found that colchicine can significantly reduce hs-CRP and IL-6 levels, making plaque more stable.46,47 Low-dose colchicine (LoDoCo) series trials found that compared with the control group, CAD patients who took 0.5mg of colchicine daily had a significantly reduced risk of CV events, whether in stable CAD or MI patients.48,49 Early initiation of colchicine use is beneficial for MI patients.50 Given the pro-inflammatory profile of the elevated PHR and the established CV benefit of colchicine in CAD, we hypothesize that patients with both high PHR and T2DM might derive particular benefit from targeted anti-inflammatory therapy. This hypothesis requires dedicated investigation to evaluate the efficacy and optimal usage of colchicine in this population.

This is the first large-scale study to specifically investigate the synergistic prognostic effect of the PHR and T2DM in patients undergoing PCI. While PHR has emerged as an inflammatory marker in cardiovascular and metabolic research, its combined impact with glycemic status on post-PCI outcomes had not been previously explored. Our work directly addresses the interplay between metabolic dysfunction and inflammation in determining residual cardiovascular risk after revascularization—a critical and clinically relevant gap. There were several limitations exist in our study. First, the level of PHR was just measured at one time after admission without monitoring their dynamic changes in long-term outcomes or determining risk stratification thresholds. Second, our study is only based on a single center, although Fuwai Hospital is a national center for cardiovascular diseases, potential bias may exist. Besides, a large number of patients were excluded, which might have introduced a selection bias. This likely results in a conservative underestimation of the adverse prognosis associated with the PHR-H/T2DM phenotype in an entire PCI population. The generalizability of our findings to diverse ethnic and geographic populations needs to be further validated. Third, our study lacks data on glycosylated hemoglobin A1c, an indicator evaluating the average glucose level of the last 2–3 months. Therefore, we cannot use PHR to assess the prognosis of patients with prediabetes. Fourth, given the retrospective, registry-based design and the implementation of a consensus-driven adjudication model, a formal Cohen’s κ statistic was not calculated. Clinical endpoints were determined using a standardized, tiered adjudication process with blinded review, which is a widely accepted and rigorous method in large-scale registry studies. Finally, the study patients without assessing the Synergy Between PCI With Taxus and Cardiac Surgery score, without further evaluating the relationship between PHR and the severity of CAD.

Conclusion

In this large-scale real-world observational study, elevated PHR synergizes with T2DM to significantly worsen prognosis in PCI patients, driven by intertwined metabolic, thrombotic, and inflammatory pathways. The simplicity and accessibility of PHR make it a pragmatic tool for risk stratification, particularly those with concomitant T2DM. Future research should focus on translating these findings into personalized therapeutic strategies to mitigate the disproportionate cardiovascular burden in diabetic populations.
