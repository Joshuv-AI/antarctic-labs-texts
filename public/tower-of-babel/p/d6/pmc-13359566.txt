Safety and efficacy of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors combined with albumin-bound paclitaxel and fluorouracil-based agents as second-line therapy in biliary tract cancers
Authors: Xiao Wang, Ying Zha, Pengen Li, Shumin Fu, Lin Xu, Weimin Jiang, Jianbing Wu, Ye Mao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBiliary tract cancers (BTCs) are a group of highly aggressive malignancies with limited therapeutic options. Gemcitabine plus cisplatin (GemCis) remains the standard first-line regimen; however, the efficacy of currently recommended second-line therapies remains unsatisfactory. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting programmed cell death protein 1 or its ligand (PD-1/PD-L1) have demonstrated antitumor activity in a subset of patients with BTC, while albumin-bound paclitaxel (nab-paclitaxel) has shown efficacy across multiple solid tumors. This study aimed to evaluate the real-world safety and efficacy of a second-line combination regimen comprising PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, nab-paclitaxel, and fluorouracil-based agents (capecitabine or S-1) in patients with advanced BTCs.MethodsThis retrospective study included patients with advanced BTCs who received second-line therapy with a combination of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, nab-paclitaxel, and fluorouracil-based agents (capecitabine or S-1) at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University between January 2019 and May 2025. Tumor response was assessed according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1, and treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) were graded using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0. The primary endpoint was progression-free survival (PFS); secondary endpoints included overall survival (OS), objective response rate (ORR), and TRAEs.ResultsA total of 36 patients were enrolled, including 17 (47.2%) with gallbladder cancer, 17 (47.2%) with intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, and 2 (5.6%) with extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma. According to RECIST version 1.1, no complete responses (CR) were observed; four patients (11.1%) achieved a partial response (PR), 19 (52.8%) had stable disease (SD), and 13 (36.1%) experienced progressive disease (PD), yielding an ORR of 11.1% and a disease control rate (DCR) of 63.9%. The median progression-free survival (PFS) was 6.8 months (95% confidence interval [CI]: 4.2–10.2), and the median overall survival (OS) was 10.8 months (95% CI: 7.2–16.2). No significant differences in PFS (6.8 vs. 7.9 months, P = 0.55) or OS (11.1 vs. 10.8 months, P = 0.68) were observed between patients who had received prior immunotherapy and those who had not. The most common grade 3–4 TRAEs were chemotherapy-related myelosuppression, decreased appetite, and elevated total bilirubin. One patient developed hand-foot syndrome, and another experienced immune-mediated pneumonitis. No treatment-related deaths were reported.ConclusionThe combination of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, nab-paclitaxel, and fluorouracil-based agents (capecitabine or S-1) demonstrated manageable toxicity and modest clinical activity as a second-line therapy for advanced BTCs. This regimen may represent a feasible treatment option for patients who progress following first-line therapy. Moreover, continued administration of this immunotherapy-based combination beyond initial progression may confer survival benefits in select patients.

Introduction

Biliary tract cancers (BTCs) comprise a heterogeneous group of malignancies arising from the biliary epithelium and are anatomically classified into gallbladder cancer (GBC), intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA), and extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (eCCA), the latter further subdivided into perihilar and distal types [1]. As the second most common primary hepatic malignancy, BTCs account for approximately 3% of all gastrointestinal cancers. Despite their relatively low incidence, the absence of specific clinical manifestations often leads to delayed diagnosis, and most patients present with advanced or unresectable disease, rendering them ineligible for curative resection [2]. Consequently, the overall prognosis remains poor, with a five-year survival rate below 20% [3]. Even among patients undergoing radical resection, recurrence occurs in 50–70% of cases, and the survival benefit conferred by adjuvant chemotherapy is modest.

Based on the phase III ABC-02 trial, the combination of gemcitabine and cisplatin (GemCis) was established as the standard first-line chemotherapy for advanced BTCs, achieving a median overall survival (OS) of 11.7 months [4]. More recently, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), particularly those targeting programmed cell death protein 1 and its ligand (PD-1/PD-L1) such as nivolumab and pembrolizumab, have shown promise in restoring T cell activity and enhancing antitumor immune responses. The phase III TOPAZ-1 trial confirmed the efficacy of combining immunotherapy with chemotherapy, demonstrating that durvalumab plus GemCis significantly improved long-term survival, with a 24-month overall survival rate of 23.6% compared to 11.5% with chemotherapy alone [5]. A phase III randomized controlled trial (KEYNOTE-966) evaluated the efficacy of pembrolizumab combined with GemCis versus chemotherapy alone as first-line treatment for advanced BTCs. The median OS was 12.7 months for the combination therapy group compared to 10.9 months in the control group, with a 24-month overall survival rate of 24.6% versus 19.2%, respectively. These results confirm that pembrolizumab combined with chemotherapy can provide durable survival benefits in patients with advanced BTCs [6]. Despite these advances, therapeutic options for patients who progress after first-line treatment remain limited. The phase III ABC-06 trial established FOLFOX (folinicacid, fluorouracil, and oxaliplatin) as a second-line option, yielding modest improvements in OS and progression-free survival (PFS) compared with active symptom control alone; however, the median OS of 6.2 months underscores the substantial unmet clinical need [7].Moreover, continuation of platinum-based therapies in second-line treatments following first-line platinum-based regimens may increase the risk of resistance and reduce potential therapeutic benefits. Currently, there is no definitive clinical data to confirm whether immunotherapy can maintain efficacy across different lines of treatment.

Albumin-bound paclitaxel (nab-paclitaxel) exerts antitumor effects by stabilizing microtubules and inducing apoptosis through cell-cycle arrest [8]. Its nanoparticle formulation enhances intratumoral drug delivery while mitigating systemic toxicity and has demonstrated efficacy across various solid tumors, including breast cancer [9]. A phase II clinical trial demonstrated that nab-paclitaxel combined with S-1 exhibits manageable toxicity in patients with advanced BTCs, with some patients deriving clinical benefits. However, further validation is needed to confirm its survival advantage [9]. A phase III randomized trial (SWOG S1815) compared the efficacy of gemcitabine combined with nab-paclitaxel and cisplatin (GAP) versus GemCis. Although the trial failed to show a significant improvement in OS, PFS, or objective response rate (ORR) in the GAP group (negative results), these findings suggest that the addition of nab-paclitaxel to the GemCis regimen does not provide clear additional benefits in first-line treatment of biliary tract cancers. This highlights the need for further exploration of other combination strategies, including immunotherapy-chemotherapy regimens [10]. Given the challenges of limited efficacy in chemotherapy and restricted benefits from monotherapy with ICIs, combination therapies present a potentially viable option.

However, the efficacy and safety of combining PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors with nab-paclitaxel and fluorouracil-based agents (capecitabine or S-1) as a second-line therapy for BTCs remain unclear. Therefore, this retrospective study aimed to evaluate the real-world safety and efficacy of this immunotherapy-based combination regimen in patients with advanced BTCs who experienced disease progression following first-line therapy.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective, single-center, observational study included patients with advanced BTCs treated at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University between January 2019 and May 2025. Eligible patients had experienced disease progression following first-line therapy with GemCis and subsequently received second-line treatment comprising a PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor in combination with nab-paclitaxel and a fluoropyrimidine-based agent (either capecitabine or S-1). Failure of first-line treatment primarily refers to disease progression confirmed by imaging following treatment with the first-line GemCis regimen, or premature discontinuation of the regimen due to intolerance of GemCis-related toxicities, such as severe bone marrow suppression, nephrotoxicity, neurotoxicity, or hypersensitivity reactions.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The principal inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) histologically confirmed BTCs, with immunohistochemical (IHC) staining used as necessary to distinguish it from hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC); (2) age between 18 and 75 years; (3) an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0–1; (4) documented disease progression or intolerance following first-line GemCis therapy; (5) no prior exposure to taxane-based agents (including, but not limited to, paclitaxel, liposomal paclitaxel, nab-paclitaxel, and docetaxel); (6) at least one measurable lesion as per the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1.

Key exclusion criteria included: (1) severe comorbidities, such as significant hepatic dysfunction or renal insufficiency; (2) history of hypersensitivity or severe adverse reactions to any taxane drug; (3) pregnancy or lactation; (4) uncontrolled conditions, including symptomatic brain metastases; (5) incomplete baseline data; (6) interruption of treatment or failure to complete the planned therapy.

Treatment regimen

Patients received a combination regimen of a PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor with nab-paclitaxel and either capecitabine or S-1. The treatment protocol involved administering the PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor at a dose of 200 mg intravenously on day 1 of each cycle. Nab-paclitaxel was given at a dose of 125 mg/m² intravenously on days 1 and 8. Capecitabine was dosed at 1250 mg/m² orally twice daily on days 1–14 of a 21-day cycle, or alternatively, S-1 was administered orally twice daily on days 1–14 of a 21-day cycle, with body surface area (BSA) adjusted doses: 40 mg for BSA < 1.25 m², 50 mg for BSA 1.25–1.49 m², and 60 mg for BSA ≥ 1.5 m². Capecitabine is commonly chosen for patients; however, for elderly patients, those with poor performance status, or individuals unable to tolerate the side effects of capecitabine-particularly hand-foot syndrome, diarrhea, or cardiotoxicity-S-1 is preferred as an alternative.

Treatment was continued until one of the following occurred: radiologically confirmed disease progression, unacceptable toxicity, or patient withdrawal. Dose modifications were allowed based on predefined criteria and individual tolerance. Dose adjustments for capecitabine/S-1 are determined based on the type and severity of treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs), graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0. Dose reductions are categorized into Grade I (75% of the original dose) and Grade II (50% of the original dose). For PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, treatment is temporarily suspended in cases of specific immune-related adverse events(irAEs), and permanently discontinued for severe reactions. Tumor response was assessed using computed tomography (CT) scans every 6–8 weeks and evaluated according to RECIST version 1.1. TRAEs were graded according to CTCAE version 5.0.

Study endpoints and assessment

The primary endpoint of this study was PFS, defined as the interval from treatment initiation (second-line treatment) to the first documented disease progression, as determined by RECIST version 1.1, or death from any cause. Secondary endpoints included OS, ORR and TRAEs. OS was defined as the time from treatment initiation (second-line treatment) to death from any cause. ORR is the combined rate of complete (CR) and partial response (PR) in treated patients. TRAEs were defined as adverse events that occurred during treatment and were considered either intolerable or related to the study regimen.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.1) and IBM SPSS Statistics (version 27.0). Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Differences between groups for categorical variables were assessed using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. PFS and OS were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, with comparisons between survival curves performed using the log-rank test. The reported P-values are intended solely for descriptive analysis and are not used for confirmatory hypothesis testing. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were applied to identify prognostic factors. Variables with a P value < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were entered into the multivariate model to construct the prognostic prediction model. Model parameters were estimated using the maximum likelihood method, and results were presented as hazard ratios (HRs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 36 patients who received second-line therapy comprising PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors in combination with nab-paclitaxel and fluorouracil-based agents (capecitabine or S-1) were included in this study (Fig. 1). Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The median patient age was 56 years (range, 42–75), and 15 patients (41.7%) were male. Nine patients (25.0%) had an ECOG performance status of 0. The cohort consisted of 17 patients (47.2%) with GBC, 17 (47.2%) with iCCA, and 2 (5.6%) with eCCA. All patients included in the study exhibited proficient mismatch repair (pMMR) status. Twenty-one patients (58.3%) had received prior immunotherapy as part of first-line treatment. In the second-line setting, 22 patients (61.1%) were treated with camrelizumab and 11 (30.6%) with sintilimab. All patients presented with metastatic BTCs at enrollment, with intrahepatic metastases observed in 31 patients (86.1%). Non-regional lymph node metastasis was the most frequent site of distant spread (44.4%). The median number of treatment cycles administered was 4.5 (range, 2–11).

Survival outcomes by prior immunotherapy exposure

Patients were stratified into two cohorts based on prior exposure to immunotherapy in the first-line setting: the prior immunotherapy group (n = 21) and the immunotherapy-naïve group (n = 15). In the prior immunotherapy group, PR was observed in 2 patients (9.5%), SD in 13 patients (61.9%), and PD in 6 patients (28.6%), yielding an ORR of 9.5% and a DCR of 71.4%. In the immunotherapy-naïve group, PR was achieved in 2 patients (13.3%), SD in 7 patients (46.7%), and PD in 6 patients (40.0%), resulting in an ORR of 13.3% and a DCR of 60.0% (Table 2B).

The median PFS was 6.8 months (95% CI: 3.8–16.2) in the prior immunotherapy group and 7.9 months (95% CI: 4.0-10.9) in the immunotherapy-naïve group, with no statistically significant difference observed between the two groups (P = 0.55). The median OS was 11.1 months (95% CI: 4.7–18.8) in the prior immunotherapy group and 10.8 months (95% CI: 8.0-NA) in the immunotherapy-naïve group, with no significant difference between groups (P = 0.68) (Fig. 4C and D).

Prognostic analysis

Prognostic factors for PFS and OS were analyzed using Cox regression models. Multivariate analysis revealed that CA125 levels greater than 10.46U/ml were independently associated with poorer PFS and OS. Additionally, total bilirubin, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and prior immunotherapy exposure were identified as significant prognostic factors for PFS, while CA199 showed prognostic significance for OS (Table 3).

Adverse events

TRAEs are summarized in Table 4. The majority of patients experienced grade 1–2 TRAEs. The most common TRAEs of any grade were vomiting (91.7%), anemia (80.6%) and pain (77.8%). The most frequent grade 3–4 AEs included chemotherapy-induced myelosuppression, elevated total bilirubin, and decreased appetite. Myelosuppression primarily manifested as anemia (2.8%), leukopenia (11.1%), neutropenia (16.7%), and thrombocytopenia (2.8%). A total of four patients experienced irAEs,, including one case of hand-foot syndrome, one case of immune-related pneumonia with concurrent hypothyroidism, and two cases of hypothyroidism. No treatment-related deaths were reported.

Discussion

BTCs are associated with poor prognosis, and no established standard of care currently exists following the failure of first-line therapy. Although immunotherapy has emerged as a cornerstone treatment for various malignancies, its efficacy as monotherapy in BTCs remains limited compared with other solid tumors, largely due to the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [11]. While ICIs have shown promise in a highly selected subset of BTCs patients, there remains an urgent need to develop combination strategies that can effectively modulate this resistant microenvironment. This study evaluated the safety and efficacy of a novel second-line regimen combining PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors with nab-paclitaxel and fluorouracil-based agents (capecitabine or S-1) in patients with advanced BTCs. Our findings demonstrate that this combination achieved encouraging efficacy with a manageable safety profile. The median PFS was 6.8 months, the median OS was 10.8 months, the ORR was 11.1%, and the DCR was 63.9%. Compared with the phase III ABC-06 trial, which established FOLFOX as a second-line treatment option, our regimen yielded a longer median OS (10.8 vs. 6.2 months) and a superior 6-month OS rate. It is worth noting that the ABC-06 protocol utilized oxaliplatin as a sequential platinum agent following cisplatin-based therapy failure. In this study, the re-administration of a different platinum-based drug following prior use of another raises concerns about potential resistance. Although research suggests that the resistance mechanisms of oxaliplatin and cisplatin are not entirely overlapping-for instance, oxaliplatin forms larger and more hydrophobic DNA adducts-there remains a possibility of cross-resistance between the two agents [12].

The observed survival benefit with our regimen may be attributed to the synergistic effects of combining chemotherapy with immunotherapy. The landmark TOPAZ-1 trial established that the combination of immunotherapy and chemotherapy demonstrates efficacy in advanced BTCs, even in unselected biomarker populations [13]. BTCs are typically characterized by an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, where abundant immunosuppressive cells and factors significantly hinder treatment efficacy [14, 15]. The tumor microenvironment (TME) of BTCs is enriched with various immune cell subsets, which often exhibit suppressive functional states. Furthermore, BTC-particularly iCCA-features a stromal-rich composition and dense extracellular matrix barriers, which further impede immune cell infiltration and drug delivery, posing significant challenges to therapeutic efficacy [16]. However, emerging evidence suggests that BTCs may not entirely be an “immune desert.” The PD-1/PD-L1 axis plays a critical role as an immune checkpoint pathway, negatively regulating T cell function and suppressing anti-tumor immune responses [17]. Notably, CD8+ PD-1-EOMES-T cells have been identified as a strong immune predictor of long-term survival in iCCA. Over 85% of patients with high densities of these T cells achieved long-term survival, emphasizing that preventing T-cell exhaustion is crucial for successful immunotherapy [14, 18]. This underscores the rationale for deploying PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, which are specifically designed to reverse the suppression of such potent T cells.

In the phase II KEYNOTE-158 trial, pembrolizumab as second-line therapy in PD-L1-positive (CPS ≥ 1) advanced BTCs demonstrated an ORR of 5.8%, a median PFS of 2.0 months, and a median OS of 7.4 months, with a favorable safety profile and only 11% grade 3–4 TRAEs [19]. More importantly, combining ICIs with chemotherapy, as demonstrated in the phase III TOPAZ-1 and KEYNOTE-966 trials, significantly improved outcomes, extending median OS to approximately 12.8 months [5, 20, 21].

Nab-paclitaxel has demonstrated significant efficacy across various solid tumors, including breast and pancreatic cancers, primarily by inducing microtubule stabilization and subsequent tumor cell apoptosis [22]. Its unique nanoparticle formulation offers superior tissue compatibility compared to conventional paclitaxel [23]. As a nanoparticle agent, nab-paclitaxel leverages endogenous albumin’s natural transport mechanisms-such as gp60 receptor-mediated transcytosis across endothelial cells-to enhance tumor targeting. This selective drug delivery to tumor tissues potentially improves therapeutic efficacy while minimizing systemic adverse effects [24, 25]. Nab-paclitaxel has the ability to specifically remodel myeloid cell populations within the tumor microenvironment. Studies have shown that nab-paclitaxel treatment can amplify mast cells and pro-inflammatory macrophages, both of which play key roles in initiating and sustaining anti-tumor immune responses [26]. The pilot NAP-CAPABIL study demonstrated that capecitabine combined with nab-paclitaxel achieved a median PFS of 5.7 months and median OS of 12.1 months as a second-line regimen for advanced BTCs, with a grade 3–4 adverse event rate of 50% [27].

The KEYNOTE-966 study demonstrated that combining pembrolizumab with GemCis significantly prolonged OS compared to GemCis alone. The median OS was 12.7 months for the combination therapy, achieving both statistical and clinical significance in improving outcomes [6]. As evidenced by the KEYNOTE-158 trial, pembrolizumab has demonstrated promising antitumor activity in patients with microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) or deficient mismatch repair (dMMR) advanced solid tumors, including those originating from the biliary tract [19]. Although the subset of BTCs patients with dMMR, MSI-H, or high tumor mutational burden (TMB-H) is small (estimated at < 5%), accumulating evidence supports the immunogenic nature of BTCs [28]. Beyond its direct cytotoxic effects, nab-paclitaxel may enhance antitumor immunity through multiple mechanisms. It induces immunogenic cell death (ICD), leading to the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) such as high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1). This process promotes dendritic cell (DC) maturation and facilitates the formation of T cell-antigen-presenting cell (APC) clusters, thereby priming a robust T cell-mediated immune response [29–31]. Moreover, the unique nanoparticle properties of nab-paclitaxel enable it to be conjugated with PD-L1 antibodies via pH-sensitive linkers, forming targeted nanoparticles that enhance tumor-specific drug delivery [32–34]. However, nab-paclitaxel has also been reported to upregulate PD-L1 expression on tumor cells, potentially serving as a mechanism of immune escape. This provides a strong rationale for combining nab-paclitaxel with ICIs, which can counteract such adaptive resistance and restore immune surveillance [35–37]. This synergistic approach has demonstrated clinical benefit across multiple cancer types. For instance, the combination of nab-paclitaxel with a PD-1 inhibitor has been shown to significantly prolong survival as a later-line treatment in non-small cell lung cancer [23, 38]. Notably, a case report documented a patient with iCCA who achieved tumor downstaging with nab-paclitaxel plus cisplatin and a PD-1 inhibitor, subsequently becoming eligible for surgical resection [39].

This study included patients treated with different PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors. Some studies in early breast cancer have shown that PD-L1 inhibitors may provide better improvement in PFS compared to PD-1 inhibitors; however, the incidence of TRAEs of any grade is generally higher with PD-L1 inhibitors [40, 41]. That said, safety profiles vary depending on treatment context, tumor type, and therapy regimen (monotherapy vs. combination), and clinical decisions must be tailored to specific indications and patient conditions. For fluoropyrimidine-based treatments, this study primarily included patients receiving capecitabine and S-1. S-1 optimizes the pharmacokinetics and toxicity profile of 5-FU through multiple mechanisms, whereas capecitabine relies on tumor-localized enzyme expression for targeted activation [42]. In metastatic colorectal cancer, S-1 has demonstrated non-inferiority and potential equivalence to capecitabine in terms of PFS, OS, and ORR [43]. However, the efficacy and toxicity profiles of these agents in biliary tract cancer remain to be fully established. Given the pharmacological and metabolic differences between PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors and fluoropyrimidine-based treatments, future studies focusing on subgroup comparisons could help clarify their differential efficacy and further optimize treatment strategies.

We further analyzed survival outcomes across different tumor subtypes and found no significant differences in OS or PFS among the three BTCs types. This is noteworthy, as previous studies have reported substantial heterogeneity in treatment response among anatomical subtypes, with GBC often exhibiting poorer responses to certain chemotherapies compared to cholangiocarcinomas [44, 45]. Analysis of genomic and transcriptomic data from the Tempus database revealed that specific molecular alterations-such as IDH mutations, FGFR fusions, and KRAS mutations-are closely associated with the immune microenvironment characteristics of different BTCs subtypes. These findings suggest that molecular profiling could provide important insights into tumor-specific immune dynamics and potentially guide tailored therapeutic approaches [46]. The consistent efficacy observed with our regimen across subtypes suggests that the combination of nab-paclitaxel and immunotherapy may overcome the biological variations associated with anatomical origin. This could be attributed to the broad immunomodulatory effects of nab-paclitaxel, which may activate shared immune mechanisms across BTCs subtypes.

Additionally, our prespecified subgroup analysis revealed that prior exposure to ICIs in the first-line setting did not significantly impact survival outcomes with our combination regimen in the second-line. Previous immunotherapy treatments may induce or expose mechanisms of resistance in tumors through various pathways. A key factor contributing to immunotherapy resistance is the immune-suppressive state of the TME. For instance, dysregulation of interferon (IFN) signaling pathways can weaken anti-tumor immune responses, thereby limiting the efficacy of ICIs (such as PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors) [47]. Tumor cells themselves employ molecular mechanisms to evade immune recognition and destruction, including defects in antigen presentation (e.g., loss of HLA class I molecules), mutations in key genes within the interferon-γ pathway (e.g., JAK1/2), and downregulation of PD-L1 expression, all of which have been shown to be closely associated with resistance to immunotherapy [48]. Interestingly, the results of this study suggest that continuing immunotherapy after resistance could still provide survival benefits for patients. This may stem from the immunosuppressive mechanisms within the BTCs tumor microenvironment (such as overexpression of immune checkpoint molecules) that limit the sustained efficacy of immune therapies. However, the addition of nab-paclitaxel in the second-line treatment regimen might alter the tumor microenvironment, reactivating immune responses and rendering previously “ineffective” immunotherapy beneficial again [49]. Nab-paclitaxel may achieve this by clearing suppressive immune cells, exposing new tumor antigens, or reshaping the microenvironment to favor immune activity, thus creating new conditions for immunotherapy to exert its therapeutic effect. However, it is important to note that this study’s findings were based on a small sample size and a retrospective design. Future prospective studies are needed to refine these observations and better identify the patient populations most likely to benefit from such combination strategies.

Based on the data from this study, the survival outcomes and toxicity profile of this treatment regimen show promising signals warranting further investigation. Compared to the ABC-06 trial, which reported a Grade III-IV TRAEs rate of 69%, this study observed a lower rate of adverse events (31%). The Grade 3–5 toxicities associated with FOLFOX in the ABC-06 trial were primarily chemotherapy-induced, such as neutropenia (12%) and fatigue (11%), with three cases of chemotherapy-related deaths reported. In contrast, the TRAEs in this study were mainly chemotherapy-induced myelosuppression, including anemia (11.11%) and neutropenia (16.67%), which could be effectively managed through preventive measures and symptomatic treatment. A total of four irAEs were noted, including hypothyroidism, hand-foot syndrome, and immune-related pneumonitis. No treatment-related deaths were reported. These findings suggest that this regimen may offer both potential efficacy and manageable safety, highlighting its feasibility for further clinical validation.

This study preliminarily demonstrated that the PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors combined with albumin-bound paclitaxel and fluoropyrimidine-based regimens exhibited promising activity and manageable safety in second-line treatment for advanced biliary tract cancers, offering a novel exploratory approach for clinical practice. However, inherent limitations must be acknowledged: as a small-sample, single-arm retrospective study, and without direct comparison to FOLFOX, the recommended second-line chemotherapy regimen, this combination therapy should not be considered standard treatment. Instead, its primary value lies in providing a preliminary hypothesis and data reference for the design of future prospective randomized controlled trials. Further evaluation of this regimen must be conducted within the framework of rigorously designed clinical trials or as an individualized treatment option after thorough assessment and management. In addition, all participants in this study were advanced-stage biliary tract cancer patients, who often presented with complex symptoms caused by the disease itself or previous treatments. In this clinical context, certain irAEs, such as fatigue, appetite loss, or mild dyspnea, may be confounded with disease progression or general physical deterioration. Future prospective studies should incorporate more systematic and standardized tools for proactive monitoring and assessment of adverse events to more accurately depict the safety profile of this treatment regimen in real-world settings.

Conclusion

The combination of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors with nab-paclitaxel and fluoropyrimidine-based drugs as a second-line treatment regimen has demonstrated manageable toxicity and potential clinical efficacy in advanced BTCs, suggesting that it could serve as a viable treatment option for patients who progress following first-line therapy. Furthermore, continuing immunotherapy after the development of resistance may still offer certain survival benefits to patients.
