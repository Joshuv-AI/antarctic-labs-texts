Variability analysis of a low-cost paper dipstick nucleic acid extraction method for wastewater surveillance using gage repeatability and reproducibility
Authors: Shruti Ahuja, Avani Kulkarni, Kiran Kondabagil, Siddharth Tallur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microbial concentration remains a key challenge in developing low-cost, point-of-use wastewater monitoring assays for pandemic preparedness, primarily due to sample heterogeneity and low pathogen load. Paper dipstick-based methods are promising for simplified and low-cost RNA capture and isolation from sub-milliliter sample volumes. However, given the manual nature of the method, variability is a major concern. To evaluate variability introduced by the method type, operator handling, and wastewater samples, we performed a multi-operator two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and ‘evaluating the measurement process’ (EMP) gage repeatability & reproducibility (gage R&R) study. Two-way ANOVA was used to determine whether differences in Ct values across samples, operators, and their interaction were statistically significant. To further assess the source of variation and quantify the repeatability and reproducibility of the assay, a comprehensive EMP gage R&R analysis was conducted. We detected pepper mild mottle virus (PMMoV) and bacteriophage Phi6 in wastewater samples collected from a sewage pumping station at IIT Bombay campus. PMMoV is abundant in human feces, and a reliable control for normalizing pathogen loads in wastewater by population size, while Phi6 spiked in the wastewater samples is a common surrogate for enveloped viruses such as SARS-CoV-2. Our study demonstrates that dipstick method detected changes in PMMoV load associated with change in the population density due to the campus summer break with the total variation lower than the acceptable gage R&R limit of 30%.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43492-x.

Introduction

Wastewater surveillance provides real-time insights into spatio-temporal trends in the spread of infectious diseases and collecting comprehensive epidemiological data for pandemic preparedness and rapid response1,2. Building on the success of population-level poliovirus detection through wastewater monitoring3, wastewater based epidemiology (WBE) has been extended for the identification of fecal indicators4, antimicrobial-resistant bacteria5, illicit drugs6, pharmaceuticals6, and human health biomarkers7 over the past few decades. In recent years, the importance of WBE has been highlighted in monitoring the COVID-19 pandemic and its recurrent surges and localized outbreaks8–10. Wastewater surveillance offers a cost-effective, and early-warning approach to track community-level viral circulation, even in the absence of clinical testing11,12. However, the existing wastewater surveillance workflow is laborious, requires sophisticated laboratory instruments and skilled workforce, hindering its scalability in resource limited setting13,14. Furthermore, environmental and demographic factors contribute to variability in wastewater data; therefore, normalization using wastewater flow rate or fecal indicators such as PMMoV is recommended by the CDC to account for dilution and variability in human waste contributions15–19. Other major challenges in implementing an end-to-end surveillance system include low pathogen load, pathogen concentration, and nucleic acid extraction, sample-to-sample variability, and sample heterogeneity.

To simplify the wastewater surveillance workflow, paper-based dipstick method is very promising for nucleic acid extraction from microorganisms in wastewater20–22. Using this method, the authors have previously demonstrated the capture and isolation of nucleic acids from different viruses such as SARS-CoV-2, PMMoV, and Phi6 from wastewater samples20. This method includes thermal lysis, followed by nucleic acid capture using paper dipsticks, leveraging the favorable nucleic acid binding and release kinetics of a cellulose matrix. However, the manual handling steps involved in the method impact assay variability. Therefore, it is important to quantify the contributions of different sources of variation in the assay performance, and assess suitability of the assay for determining changes in viral load in wastewater epidemiology.

ANOVA is one of the most widely used statistical tools in biological research to examine the statistical significance of differences in mean between experiment groups. Two-way ANOVA, in particular, enables simultaneous evaluation of the effects of two independent variables and the potential interaction between them on the output response. This approach is well suited for biological experiments, where variability may arise from multiple factors such as sample source or differences in operator technique. However, while ANOVA is useful in identifying whether differences between experimental groups are statistically significant, it does not quantify the relative contribution of each source of variability, nor does it evaluate the consistency of the measurement system itself. These limitations necessitate the use of additional tools, such as Measurement system analysis (MSA) and gage repeatability and reproducibility (gage R&R), particularly in protocols where manual steps MSA employs various statistical tools to quantify variability and evaluate the accuracy, precision, and stability of a measurement system (MS). Gage R&R provides a detailed analysis of variability by isolating and quantifying the effects of the measurement system, operator handling, and sample characteristics. Table 1 highlights the use of gage R&R analysis in quantifying variability across biological assays and analytical instruments23–28. However, most of these gage R&R studies use the AIAG gage R&R approach developed by the Automotive Industry Action Group (AIAG), primarily used in the automotive industry and other manufacturing sectors29–31. AIAG gage R&R is suitable for manufacturing environments where the primary concern is the consistency and accuracy of measurement systems used for quality control and process optimization32,33. On the other hand, evaluating the measurement process (EMP) gage R&R provides a comprehensive analysis of variability, including interactions between different factors34,35, which is crucial for understanding how various sources of variability affect the assay performance in biological contexts.

Particularly, we performed a crossed EMP gage R&R study, wherein each operator performed the dipstick method on each sample in triplicates under similar conditions36,37.

Materials and methods

Wastewater samples and pre-processing

The viruses and the methods used to capture them in this study are described in Table 2. The viruses chosen include bacteriophage Phi6, a popular surrogate for enveloped RNA viruses such as SARS-CoV-239, and pepper mild mottle virus (PMMoV), a common indicator to evaluate fecal pollution in water samples and assess water quality. PMMoV is found in high concentrations in human feces, making it a reliable control organism for normalizing pathogen loads in wastewater samples relative to population size40. Raw, untreated grab wastewater samples were collected from the sewage pumping station at the IIT Bombay campus, Mumbai, India, covering both residential and academic areas, as per the CDC guidelines41.

Dipstick preparation and method of use

The dipstick preparation followed the methodology described by Mason & Botella (2020)46. After thermal lysis of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${1}\,\hbox {mL}$$\end{document} of the sample at 60 °C for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${10}\,\hbox {min}$$\end{document}, nucleic acid capture was achieved in three steps:Step 1: Binding of the nucleic acids to the exposed region of the dipstick (portion of the dipstick that is not coated with wax)Step 2: Washing of exposed region of the dipstick to remove impuritiesStep 3: RNA elution in reverse transcription master-mixPhotographs of dipsticks with detailed dimensions are shown in supplementary material Fig. S2. The dipstick assay followed the methodology detailed by Ahuja et al.20 and Mason & Botella (2020)46. Briefly, the dipstick method uses the favorable nucleic acid affinity and release kinetics on cellulose matrix. Whatmann grade I filter paper (GE Healthcare) was used as the dipstick substrate selectively binds and retains high–molecular weight nucleic acids, whereas smaller molecules such as amplification inhibitors and other impurities, either do not bind or are removed during the washing step. Nucleic acids are released during the elution step through mechanical agitation of the dipstick (compression and bending), which facilitates desorption from the cellulose matrix. In addition, salts present in the reverse transcription master mix promote nucleic acid release. The cellulose paper dipstick assay with thermal lysis therefore enables rapid, nucleic acid extraction and purification without the need for specialized equipment.

The first two-way ANOVA test was performed to assess the effect of dipstick method and operator (A and B) on Ct values that served as the continuous dependent variable. Additionally, the second two-way ANOVA test was conducted to evaluate potential variation in the performance of the simplified dipstick method across different wastewater samples and operators. The analysis generated output including the sum of squares, degrees of freedom, mean squares, F-ratios, and p-values for each main effect and interaction term, allowing for evaluation of the statistical significance of each factor.

Following the two-way ANOVA tests, we conducted a multi-operator study combined with a crossed gage R&R analysis using the EMP approach to evaluate the accuracy and precision of the dipstick method in pathogen detection from wastewater samples. This analysis focused on variability across operators, samples, and interactions. The input parameters included part (wastewater sample with unknown concentrations of PMMoV), operator (technicians A, B and C), and measurement (threshold cycle: Ct value obtained through RT-qPCR serving as the primary quantitative measure for gage R&R analysis). Additionally, the sigma multiplier (process width) was set to 6 standard deviations (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pm 3$$\end{document} sigma). This is typical in EMP studies to capture a broad range of natural process variation in assay performance47. Residual Maximum Likelihood (REML) was applied to estimate the variance components, as it effectively manages unbalanced data and offers robust variance component estimates in biological assays48.

The variance components assessed included repeatability, reproducibility, and interaction variation49. Repeatability (intra-operator variability) or equipment variation corresponds to the within-assay variation in the variance components. A measurement is considered repeatable if a single operator consistently obtains similar results when measuring the same sample multiple times. Reproducibility (inter-operator variation) or appraiser variability is analogous to between-operator variability in the variance components and measures precision across different operators. If multiple operators achieve consistent results using the same dipstick method on the same sample, the measurement is considered reproducible50. Additionally, we evaluated product variation (or sample variation), which explains the variability due to factors like dipstick manufacturing, sample heterogeneity, and differences in viral load across samples. Operator-sample interaction or interaction variation, which accounts for the variability introduced by the operator and sample combination, was also assessed. The details of the workflow and terminologies for two-way ANOVA and EMP gage R&R in JMP® Statistical Discovery software (version 17.0.0, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) are provided in supplementary material section 5.

Discussion

The Gage R&R variability analysis demonstrates minimal spread in Ct values for the three operators while detecting PMMoV and the process control bacteriophage Phi6 as shown in Fig. 3a and b, emphasizes minimal operator-to-operator variability. For bacteriophage Phi6, the consistent Ct spread seen across the samples was as expected, as the spiked concentration of bacteriophage Phi6 was the same across all samples. The low variability in operator-sample ID interaction for PMMoV further demonstrated minimal influence of specific operator handling on sample variability. However, the interaction variation for Phi6 was comparatively higher, largely caused by the handling of operator C as demonstrated in the parallelism plots shown in Fig. 2d, which show a slight deviation in Ct values for sample IDs 3 and 5. High repeatability variation seen for Phi6 suggests room for further optimization of the method and dipstick design.

The primary source of variability for PMMoV detection is attributed to fluctuations in PMMoV concentration due to change in population51,52 across the different samples collected over time (before and during summer break). Given that all samples were collected from the same academic campus catchment, both before and during the summer break, major dietary and regional differences that are often cited as drivers of PMMoV variability are unlikely to change substantially over the study period. This high product variation is expected, given the dynamic nature of wastewater viral loads as population density and activity change. The total gage R&R being less than 30% indicates acceptable variability35, demonstrating the assay’s reliability in consistently detecting target pathogens, even with moderate operator and sample variation. This makes the dipstick assay suitable for field applications in conjunction with portable, field-deployable sensors53–57, addressing the critical research gap in establishing low-cost end-to-end wastewater surveillance systems.

According to EMP guidelines, the gage R&R study is classified into four classes34,35 wherein the first class has a measurement system variation less than 10% and the measurement system is considered acceptable. The second class has a measurement system variation less than 30% and the measurement system is considered conditionally acceptable, with room for improvement. The third and fourth classes have measurement system variation greater than 30% and are considered unacceptable. The dipstick method has gage R&R values below 30%, indicating the method is acceptable for field applications. However there is potential to further enhance its performance. Variability can be minimized by automating process steps or by designing devices such as centrifugal microfluidic disc58,59 based chips for liquid handling and processing. This would allow for automated capture and elution of nucleic acids from a dipstick affixed to the chip surface, without the need for rigorous compression. Additionally, based on our observations, we recommend incorporating a matrix recovery process control into each sample to enhance quality assurance across multiple operators, in accordance with CDC guidelines41. This involves spiking a non-target virus (an internal process control, such as Phi6) into the wastewater samples prior to processing to assess viral loss during extraction and monitoring procedural variability. Measuring the recovery of an internal control of known concentration across multiple operators would enable in determining operator to operator variability and help confirm proper functioning of the simplified dipstick protocol. Furthermore, a no-template control and a sterile water blank processed through the complete dipstick workflow should be routinely included to identify potential contamination introduced during sample handling or processing.

Conclusion and future work

This study demonstrates the utility of two-way ANOVA and gage R&R analysis as a robust method to validate a biological assay when applied to complex and heterogeneous environmental samples such as wastewater. While two-way ANOVA is a powerful tool for detecting whether fixed factors such as sample or operator significantly influence an assay’s outcome, it has inherent limitations. Specifically, ANOVA does not quantify the proportion of variability attributable to each factor, nor does it evaluate the overall capability, precision, or consistency of the measurement system. It can detect statistically significant differences even when their practical impact is minimal, especially in highly sensitive systems such as qRT-PCR. Whereas, gage R&R allows for a detailed assessment of both repeatability and reproducibility by systematically isolating and quantifying variations due to the measurement system, operator handling, and the samples themselves.

The gage R&R analysis suggested that the variability was primarily attributed to within-sample repeatability, followed by operator-sample interaction and negligible due to operator-to-operator handling, indicating that the dipstick method consistently produced stable results across different operators and samples. This highlights the robustness of the simplified dipstick method. Unlike ANOVA and t-tests, which are primarily used to determine statistical significance between experimental conditions, gage R&R provides a nuanced understanding of how both the measurement system and human factors influence assay performance, making it indispensable for quality control and method validation in biological research. This shows that gage R&R extends beyond just the dipstick method evaluated here.

Our work shows that it can be widely applied in laboratory assays and field-deployed diagnostic methods to ensure the reproducibility of results across different settings, operators, and batches. This is particularly relevant when developing products or assays that will be used in decentralized or resource-constrained environments, where manual methods and operator variability may have a larger impact. The acceptable variability in the simplified dipstick method to isolate and purify nucleic acid highlight the field applicability of the test. With up-scaled manufacturing the dipstick method can be integrated with a field-portable RT-qPCR devices, or rapid detection techniques like loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP), and a lateral flow assay to ensure on-site wastewater monitoring, making the setup more accessible in low-resource or remote areas.
