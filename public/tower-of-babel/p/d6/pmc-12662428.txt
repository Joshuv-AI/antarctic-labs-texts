Genetic association of lipid-lowering drug target genes with cholecystitis: A summary-data-based Mendelian randomization analysis
Authors: Kai Zhao, Yubo Zhao, Zhening Yan, Ruihuan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
There were some evidences to suggest the correlation between circulating lipid levels and cholecystitis, but no evidence had been indicated the causal relationship between lipid-lowering drugs and cholecystitis. To investigate this, we employed drug target Mendelian randomization (MR), summary-data-based MR (SMR), and genetic colocalization analyses to assess the association between lipid-lowering drugs and cholecystitis. In this study, we used 2 sets of genetic tools to proxy lipid-lowering drugs: elevated high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (CETP), decreased low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDLR, HMGCR, NPC1L1, PCSK9, APOB, and ABCG5/ABCG8), and decreased triglycerides (LPL, PPARA, ANGPTL3, and APOC3); the expression quantitative trait locus of target genes from the eQTLGen consortium and Genotype-Tissue Expression project V8. Then, the causal effects of these lipid-lowering drugs genetic proxies on cholecystitis were estimated using a variety of MR, SMR, and colocalization as sensitivity analyses. Collectively, in the MR results, we found that the significant causal effects between genetically proxied ABCG5/ABCG8 enhancement and HMGCR inhibitors were associated with a reduced risk of cholecystitis. The results of SMR and heterogeneity in dependent instruments tests indicated that the expression of ABCG5/ABCG8 and HMGCR in multiple tissues were associated with cholecystitis. In conclusion, our study provides genetic evidence demonstrating a causal relationship between the enhancement of ABCG5/ABCG8 gene proxies and the use of HMGCR inhibitors with a reduced risk of cholecystitis. These findings support the potential reuse of lipid-lowering drugs in patients with cholecystitis and could inform the development of effective treatment strategies for this population in clinical practice.

1. Introduction

Bile is a digestive fluid produced in the liver cells and stored in the gallbladder, playing a crucial role in the digestion of fats in the small intestine.[1] However, bile buildup can cause the gallbladder to swell, potentially leading to cholecystitis, an acute or chronic inflammation of the gallbladder wall.[2] In the United States, acute cholecystitis affects over 200,000 people annually, most commonly, acute cholecystitis caused by gallstones.[3] Approximately 1% to 4% of people with gallstones develop cholecystitis, acute cholecystitis, or gallstone-induced pancreatitis.[4] Research has shown a significant correlation between the etiology of cholecystitis and factors such as diet and lifestyle, including obesity, a high-fat or low-fiber diet, and rapid weight loss.[5] In addition, cholecystectomy is the gold standard treatment for acute cholecystitis.[6] Although the mortality rate of patients with acute cholecystitis is <10%, the mortality rate of postoperative cholecystitis and acalculous cholecystitis patients is as high as 23% to 40%.[7] Therefore, studying early intervention and prevention strategies for cholecystitis remains crucial for reducing the social health burden. A previous study found a correlation between low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and gallbladder disease.[8] Besides, a recent Mendelian randomization (MR) study suggested potentially protective effects of circulating DHA and omega-3 concentrations on cholelithiasis and cholecystitis.[9] These findings suggested associations between circulating lipid levels and gallbladder disease. In addition, statins reduce the secretion of cholesterol in the bile by inhibiting the biosynthesis of cholesterol in the liver, ultimately reducing the concentration of cholesterol in the bile.[10] It has been shown that long-term statin use is associated with a reduced risk of cholecystectomy after gallstones.[11] In addition, previous animal studies have shown the positive effects of statins in preventing gallstone formation.[12,13]

However, there was no evidence indicating that lipid-lowering medications can reduce the risk of developing gallbladder disease. Yet, due to the existence of confounding factors and limitations with regard to feasibility, cost, and ethics, it is difficult to adopt randomized clinical trials to make causal inferences.[14]

In recent years, drug target MR utilizes genetic instruments within or near target genes to simulate potential effects of drug targets and can be employed to predict opportunities for drug development and repurposing.[15] Since genetic variants are allocated randomly at conception and before the onset of disease, they can help mitigate confounding factors and reverse causation. This approach is akin to the randomization of genetic alleles in randomized clinical trials, while simultaneously mitigating potential biases from confounding factors and reverse causation.[16,17]

In this study, we conducted a drug target MR analysis to estimate the causal effects of lipid-lowering drugs on the risk of cholecystitis. Our goal was to assess whether genetically mediated lipid-lowering drugs influence the risk of cholecystitis. Additionally, these findings may support the reuse of lipid-lowering drugs as potential preventive strategies for cholecystitis in future trial designs and offer valuable guidance for their clinical application.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design

The study framework is outlined in Figure 1. MR relies on 3 key assumptions: genetic variation is associated with risk factors; genetic variation is not related to confounding factors; and genetic variation affects outcomes only through risk factors. For this MR study, 11 lipid-lowering drugs were included in the analysis, with detailed information provided in Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q762. To investigate the potential impact of lipid-lowering drugs on cholecystitis, we used the negative beta to represent the reduction in outcome values resulting from lower levels of corresponding gene expression. Additionally, this study adhered to the guidelines outlined in the STROBE-MR statement.

Study frame of the drug target Mendelian randomization study. HDL-C = high-density lipoprotein cholesterol; HEIDI = heterogeneity in dependent instruments; LD = linkage disequilibrium; LDL-C = low-density lipoprotein cholesterol; MAF = minor allele frequency; MR = Mendelian randomization; SMR = summary-data-based MR; TG = triglycerides.

2.2. Genetic proxies for lipid-lowering drugs

For drug target MR analysis, we selected commonly used and novel lipid-lowering drugs based on the most recent guidelines for the treatment of dyslipidemia. Utilizing the DrugBank database (https://go.drugbank.com/), we identified the pharmacological target genes for these drugs. According to their primary pharmacological actions, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), LDL-C, and triglycerides (TG) were chosen as biomarkers due to the capacity of lipid-lowering drugs, these target genes were further categorized into elevated HDL-C (CETP), decreased LDL-C (LDLR, HMGCR, NPC1L1, PCSK9, APOB and ABCG5/ABCG8), and decreased TG (LPL, PPARA, ANGPTL3, and APOC3) (see Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q762).[18] The genome-wide association study (GWAS) data of biomarkers come from the Global Lipids Genetics Consortium, which is the largest GWAS meta-analysis of lipid genetics known to us, including up to 187,167 European participants.[19] The detailed material information and data download link can be found in Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q762. Variants located within 1 Mb on either side of each target gene, associated with biomarkers at a genome-wide significance threshold (P < 5E-08) and a minor allele frequency > 0.01 were selected. To enhance statistical efficiency and account for the gene region size, single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were clumped using a more permissive linkage disequilibrium (LD) threshold of r² < 0.1 within a 1000 kb window.

2.3. Outcome datasets

We obtained the primary outcome data from the R11 version published by the FinnGen consortium (cases: 5237; controls: 397,583).[20] The replication outcome GWAS data was conducted by Sakaue S el al. (cases: 9820; controls: 461,431).[21] Additionally, we used coronary heart disease (CHD), which includes 60,801 cases and 123,504 controls, as a positive control outcome because the benefits of lipid-lowering drugs for CHD are well established.[22] The GWAS summary data are limited to individuals of European ancestry and does not overlap with exposure data samples. All cases were defined by the ICD-10 diagnostic code, the detailed data information can be found in Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q762 and original literature.

2.4. Primary MR and colocalization analyses

Before performing the MR analysis, the alleles for the exposure and outcome data were harmonized, and palindromic SNPs were excluded. Additionally, IVs that were absent from the outcome dataset were also excluded. We calculated the magnitude of the genetic predictors for each exposure through F-statistics (Beta2/SE2).[23] Usually, F-statistics > 10 are considered not subject to weak instrument bias. Additionally, to mitigate the effects of horizontal pleiotropy and heterogeneity on the MR results, the MR-radial outlier test was performed to identify and exclude outliers.[24] Additionally, the MR-PRESSO method was used to detect and correct potential pleiotropy and outliers.[25] When the P-value > .05, it indicates the absence of horizontal pleiotropy. In MR-Egger regression, the intercept term is a helpful indication of directional horizontal pleiotropy, when P-value < .05, it indicates the existence of horizontal pleiotropy.[26] Furthermore, we used a Cochran Q test to examine the heterogeneity, with a P-value < .05 indicating evidence of heterogeneity.[27] Furthermore, the I²GX is used to quantify the heterogeneity between instrumental variables (IVs). The higher the I² value, the greater the difference between the effect estimates (for example, >50%). We applied the random effects inverse variance weighted (IVW) method (This method assumes that there was no pleiotropy in the IVs and also considers heterogeneity between IVs) as the main MR analysis to obtain the estimate of the causal association, and utilized MR-Egger, weighted median, and weighed mode methods as sensitivity analysis.[26,28–30] The Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust the P-value threshold for significance in the MR analyses to 4.55E-03 (.05/11). P-values <4.55E-03 were considered significant, while P-values between 4.55E-03 and .05 were considered nominally significant. To determine the direction of the effects, we used the MR Steiger directionality test to assess causal direction. When the results of MR analyses were significant, we used a bayesian colocalization analysis to test the potential for such genetic confounding.[31] The basic hypothesis for colocalization in the same genomic location is H3: both traits have a causal genetic variant, but not the same variant and H4: these 2 traits have the same causal variant. The PP.H4 > 0.8 is an effective cutoff value for the colocalization evidence. H4/(H3 + H4) represents the probability of colocalization conditional on the presence of a causal variant for the outcome. Analyses were executed in R software (4.3.0, R was developed by statisticians from the University of Auckland, Auckland, New Zealand) using the TwoSampleMR, RadialMR, MR-PRESSO, meta and coloc packages.

2.5. Supplementary analyses

To assess the robustness of the main analysis, we performed SMR and heterogeneity in dependent instruments (HEIDI) tests to evaluate the association between cis-expression quantitative trait locus (cis-eQTLs) for each drug target and cholecystitis. The cis-eQTL data were obtained from the eQTLGen consortium (including 31,864 whole blood samples) and Genotype-Tissue Expression project (GTEx) V8 (including 49 tissues and covers 15,201 samples).[32,33] For each gene, the SMR analysis was conducted only on top SNPs (the lowest P-values). The HEIDI test utilized multiple SNPs within a given locus to distinguish druggable genes associated with cholecystitis risk, determining whether these associations were due to a common genetic variant rather than genetic LD.[34] When P-SMR < .05 and P-HEIDI > .05, it indicates that the SMR analysis results are significant and that the associations are not driven by genetic LD.[34] In addition, this study adopts multi-SNP-based SMR test, which integrates all SNP information that meets the P-value threshold in the region and conducts multi-SNP-based SMR analysis.[35] Furthermore, the use of the genomic locus visualization module has significantly enhanced our understanding of genetic regulatory mechanisms. Below the locus distribution map, a heat map displays chromatin states in specific regions across different cell types or tissues, with data sourced from the Roadmap Epigenome Consortium. These epigenomic annotations help identify functional elements such as promoters, transcriptional regions, and enhancers, while revealing their potential cellular or tissue-specific characteristics. We performed the SMR and HEIDI tests using the and the SMR portal (https://yanglab.westlake.edu.cn/smr-portal/).

3. Results

3.1. Primary MR and colocalization analyses

For the primary analyses, a total of 2 to 156 SNPs were selected as proxies for the genetic variation of 11 lipid-lowering drugs (Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q762). The F-statistics indicated that the MR estimates were less susceptible to weak instrument bias (Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q762). The I²GX value > 0.5 indicates potential heterogeneity among the IVs (Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q762). Therefore, this study uses the random effects IVW model to deal with the heterogeneity between the IVs. The main IVW-MR analyses found suggestive pieces of evidence for the association between genetically proxied ABCG5/ABCG8 enhancement (odds ratio [OR] = 0.328; P = 5.47E-06, PP.H4 = 1), CETP inhibitors (OR = 0.808; P = 2.65E-06, H4/(H3 + H4) = 0.51), HMGCR inhibitors (OR = 0.614; P = .001, H4/(H3 + H4) = 0.92) and APOC3 inhibitors (OR = 0.820; P = .001, H4/(H3 + H4) = 0.91) were associated with reduced risk of cholecystitis data from the FinnGen study (Table S4a, Table S8a, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q762, Fig 2). Moreover, the forest plots, scatter plots, leave-one-out plots, and funnel plots were shown in the Figures S1–S4, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q763. In replication analysis, we found the association between genetically proxied ABCG5/ABCG8 enhancement (OR = 0.035; P = 7.13E-108, PP.H4 = 1) was associated with reduced risk of cholecystitis data from the replication study (Table S4b, Table S8b, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q762). Although the causal effect of HMGCR, CETP, and APOC3 were not significant, the effect values were consistent with the direction of the primary MR analysis results (Table S4b, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q762). Furthermore, the positive control result showed that these lipid-lowering drugs genetic proxies were associated with a reduced risk of CHD (Table S4c, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q762, Fig 2). Cochran Q test, MR-PRESSO, and MR-Egger intercept showed no evidence of heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy (Table S5–S7, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q762). The results of sensitivity analysis were basically consistent with the direction and effect size of the IVW results. The Steiger directionality test results suggested IVs affected the outcomes through exposure (Table S9, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q762).

Associations between genetically proxied lipid-lowering drugs and outcomes. CI = confidence interval; nSNP = number of SNPs; OR =odds ratio.

3.2. Supplementary analyses

The results of the SMR and HEIDI tests showed that the expression of ABCG5/ABCG8 and HMGCR in multiple tissues were associated with cholecystitis, which provided additional evidence for our results. The results of the study indicated that increased expression of ABCG8 in subcutaneous tissue (FinnGen: OR = 0.812; P-SMR = .003; P-HEIDI = .118), increased expression of ABCG8 in whole blood tissue (FinnGen: OR = 0.668; P-SMR = 9.38E-08; P-HEIDI = .074), and increased expression of ABCG5 in subcutaneous tissue (Sakaue S et al: OR = 0.777; P-SMR = 7.62E-05; P-HEIDI = .147) were all significantly associated with a reduced risk of cholecystitis. Additionally, decreased expression of HMGCR in lung tissue (FinnGen: OR = 0.779; P-SMR = .037; P-HEIDI = .141) was also associated with a reduced risk of developing cholecystitis. Full results of the SMR and HEIDI tests are shown in Table S10, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q762. Notably, the results of the multi-SNP-based SMR test also showed that increased expression of ABCG8 in subcutaneous tissue (FinnGen: P-SMR-multi = 2.85E-03; Sakaue S et al: P-SMR-multi = 1.11E-07) was associated with a reduced risk of cholecystitis. 4. Discussion

This study provides genetic evidence demonstrating a causal relationship between the enhancement of ABCG5/ABCG8 gene proxies and the use of HMGCR inhibitors with a reduced risk of cholecystitis. In contrast, the association between proxy genes for other lipid-lowering medications and cholecystitis is either of low intensity or not statistically significant. To validate the robustness of these findings, the study employed various genetic tools to identify potential therapeutic targets for cholecystitis, offering new insights into treatment strategies for the condition.

Accumulation of bile leading to gallbladder distension is one of the causes of cholecystitis.[2] However, ABCG5/ABCG8 play a crucial role in the secretion of biliary cholesterol, as well as in cholesterol absorption and excretion.[36] They work synergistically to facilitate the excretion of cholesterol and phytosterols, thereby influencing the levels and metabolism of cholesterol in the body.[37] A previous animal study demonstrated that pioglitazone can elevate the levels of ABCG5/ABCG8 in the liver of guinea pigs, thereby exerting a significant ameliorative effect on cholesterol gallstone disease.[38] Additionally, a previous MR study has indicated that drugs targeting the ABCG5/ABCG8 proteins and modulating the distribution of sterols/cholesterol may exhibit a causal effect in reducing the risk of cholelithiasis and cholecystitis.[39] This may provide evidence for our findings.

Furthermore, in the present study, we observed a potential protective effect of HMGCR inhibitors. HMGCR inhibitors belong to statins and are a widely used class of lipid-lowering drugs in clinical practice.[40,41] There was evidence that HMGCR was associated with inflammatory pathways and that HMGCR inhibitors reduce the risk of developing rheumatoid arthritis.[42–45] This may explain why the HMGCR inhibitors we observed in this drug target MR study reduce the risk of cholecystitis, but the mechanism is still unclear. Previously, studies had shown that HMGCR was one of the candidate susceptibility genes for gallstones, and HMGCR inhibitors prevented gallstone formation and cholecystic damage.[38,46] Moreover, studies have shown that all statins except pravastatin significantly inhibit bilirubin uptake and do not damage the liver at different concentrations, suggesting that the use of statins is relatively safe.[47] However, further research is needed to elucidate the exact molecular mechanisms involved in this correlation.

Our research presents several advantages. Firstly, comparing to developing new drugs, reusing old drugs is more economical and time-saving. Secondly, it used genetic variants mimicking lipid-lowering drugs to examine the effect of drugs by drug target MR. This approach overcomes limitations for observational studies, including reverse causality and potential confounders. Thirdly, our study utilized 2 sets of genetic instruments to proxy drug exposure, used the colocalization analysis, and employed multiple sensitivity analysis to enhance the robustness and validity of the research results and conclusions. Fourthly, when selecting IVs, screen for genetic variations in drug target genes associated with LDL-C, HDL-C, and TG to replace lipid-lowering drugs and ensure the effectiveness of genetic instruments.

However, this MR study had several limitations. First, although our results suggest that genetic proxy ABCG5/ABCG8 agonists and HMGCR inhibitors reduce the risk of cholecystitis, specific factors such as drug dosage, metabolic profile, mechanism of action, as well as individual differences are still uncertain, which may limit our ability to comprehensively and accurately assess the actual effects of drugs. Second, the primary inference from our results were relevant to the prevention rather than treatment of cholecystitis, as the risk factors for disease onset are not always the same as those for disease severity or prognosis. Thus, this study may be more meaningful in providing direction on the causal relationship of drugs. Third, potential horizontal pleiotropy may affect the effect of MR analysis. We used SNPs near the gene encoding the drug target as an instrumental variable, which minimized the possibility of pleiotropy. Additionally, since our study primarily involved individuals of European descent, further investigation and validation are needed to generalize these findings to other ethnic groups. Therefore, additional research, including clinical trials and experimental studies, is essential to address these limitations. Despite these limitations, our study provides valuable insights into the use of lipid-lowering drugs in clinical trials of cholecystitis.

5. Conclusion

In conclusion, our drug target MR analysis found strong evidence to suggest that ABCG5/ABCG8 agonists and HMGCR inhibitors as causal protective factors for cholecystitis. This study supports the reuse of lipid-lowering drugs in patients with cholecystitis and helps to develop beneficial treatment plans for the population with cholecystitis in clinical practice.
