Red blood cell metrics as predictors of cardiovascular risk in anemic versus non-anemic heart failure patients
Authors: Samira Arami, Bahare Gholami Chaboki, Arsalan Salari, Fatemeh Baharvand, Seyed Mehdi Mousavi, Zahra Ahmadnia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic heart failure is a growing health concern globally, with rising prevalence and considerable social and economic impact as a leading cause of death. Although significant progress has been made in cardiovascular disease treatment over the last two decades, this condition continues to pose a significant clinical challenge. The connection between red blood cell parameters and major cardiovascular complications in patients with chronic heart failure remains poorly understood. Assessing these metrics could enhance our ability to predict long-term cardiovascular outcomes in this patient population.Materials and methodsIn this single-center, retrospective analysis of a prospective cohort, 419 chronic HF patients (mean age 63.70 ± 11.67 years) were enrolled between 2023 and 2024 and categorized into anemic (n = 251) and non-anemic (n = 168) groups based on WHO hemoglobin criteria. Baseline demographic, clinical, and hematological parameters (MCV, MCH, MCHC, RDW, Hb, HCT) were recorded. Patients were monitored over 12 months for major adverse cardiovascular events, encompassing cardiovascular mortality, non-fatal myocardial infarction, non-fatal stroke, or cardiac rehospitalization. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 16, with significance defined as p < 0.05.ResultsAnemic patients had a higher prevalence of diabetes, hyperlipidemia, prior CABG, and smoking (all p < 0.05). They also exhibited significantly lower MCH, MCHC, Hb, and HCT, and higher RDW (p < 0.05). Correlation analyses revealed significant associations between various RBC indices and hematological parameters in both groups. However, in the multivariable analysis, none of the RBC indices including hemoglobin, hematocrit, MCH, and MCHC were independent predictors of rehospitalization or all-cause mortality (all p > 0.05). Hyperlipidemia showed a non-significant trend toward increased all-cause mortality (OR: 1.675; 95% CI: 0.954–2.942; p = 0.073).ConclusionAlthough anemia and aberrant red blood cell indices correlate with an unfavorable clinical profile in heart failure patients, they did not independently predict short-term major adverse cardiovascular events in this study population. This implies that the prognostic significance of these standard hematologic parameters may be constrained in advanced heart failure.

Introduction

Heart failure persists as a predominant contributor to global morbidity and mortality, with a multifaceted pathophysiology shaped by hemodynamic disturbances and comorbid conditions [1, 2]. Approximately 64 million cases worldwide are affected by heart failure. Affected patients necessitate ongoing medical management, including regular clinical assessment, hospital admissions, and comprehensive therapeutic regimens. Therefore, chronic heart failure represents a significant economic burden on healthcare systems worldwide, with global costs projected to reach $400 billion by 2030 [3].

It is estimated that by 2030, the prevalence of this disease, especially in less developed countries, will increase by up to 46%, with the number of affected individuals exceeding 6 million [1, 3, 4]. In Iran, official data from the Ministry of Health and Medical Education report that cardiovascular diseases account for 33% to 39% of national fatalities [5]; Additionally, heart failure is present in 25% of admissions to cardiac departments across Iran [6]. Ahmadi and colleagues, utilizing data from the National Iran Myocardial Infarction Registry, further identified Guilan province as having the second highest prevalence of heart failure nationally at 51.7%, following Khuzestan province [4]. The growing incidence of heart failure presents fundamental challenges for patients, caregivers, clinicians, and healthcare administrators [5].

In order to control the economic burden imposed by cardiovascular diseases, especially heart failure, it is crucial to estimate the severity and prognosis of the disease. It is possible that this can be achieved by focusing on hematological characteristics [6–8]. Anemia and hematological parameters have been considered for estimating the severity and prognosis of cardiac diseases like heart failure [9, 10]. Mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), and mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC) are standard red blood cell indices obtained from complete blood counts, providing information on erythrocyte size and hemoglobin content. Research has linked elevated mean corpuscular volume with poorer outcomes in cardiac patients [11, 12]. Nevertheless, evidence concerning the prognostic utility of MCH and MCHC in coronary artery disease remains limited and inconsistent [13, 14]. While current guidelines emphasize the role of intravenous iron therapy in iron-deficient heart failure patients, the prognostic utility of routine red blood cell indices—such as MCV, MCH, and MCHC—in predicting cardiovascular outcomes remains less clear, particularly in anemic versus non-anemic subgroups [15].

Considering that chronic heart failure is a disease with increasing prevalence and significant social and economic importance among the causes of mortality worldwide, despite advances in the treatment of cardiovascular diseases over the past 20 years, this serious condition remains an unresolved clinical challenge. To control the economic burden imposed by cardiovascular diseases, especially heart failure, estimating the severity and prognosis of the disease is crucial, which may be achievable through attention to hematological parameters.

The association between red blood cell indices and major adverse cardiovascular events in chronic heart failure patients remains inadequately characterized. Assessing these parameters could enhance prediction of long-term cardiovascular outcomes in this patient group. Accordingly, we designed a study to examine the relationship between red blood cell indices and major adverse cardiovascular events in anemic and non-anemic heart failure patients presenting to Dr. Heshmat Hospital in Rasht between 2023 and 2024.

Materials and methods

Study design

The present investigation constitutes a retrospective analysis of a single-center prospective cohort study. The research involved chronic heart failure patients attending the clinic at Dr. Heshmat Hospital in Rasht during 2023 to 2024. Participants were classified into anemic and non-anemic categories based on initial assessment of red blood cell indices. The study was performed in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration, and informed consent was acquired from all subjects prior to enrollment.

Study population

Eligible participants included all adults over 18 years of age diagnosed with chronic heart failure who visited the hospital clinic during the research period. Anemia was defined as hemoglobin levels below 12 g/dL for women and under 13 g/dL for men [16]. A convenience sampling approach was employed for patient recruitment.

Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) Acute heart failure; (2) Hemoglobin under 9 g/dL or hematocrit below 30%; (3) Documented hereditary hemoglobinopathies; (4) Severe renal dysfunction; (5) Chronic inflammatory disorders; (6) Active malignancy or myelodysplastic syndrome; (7) Recent hemorrhage or blood transfusion within the preceding three months.

Data collection

Following provision of written informed consent, eligible patients were enrolled. Baseline demographic information—including age, gender, occupation, educational attainment, and marital status—as well as the date of the initial clinic visit were recorded. Blood specimens were collected for analysis of red blood cell indices. These parameters were evaluated using standardized biochemical techniques in the hospital’s clinical laboratory.

Follow-Up and outcomes

Patients were followed for 12 months’ post-enrollment. During follow-up, major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) were assessed via telephone interviews conducted by the research team. MACE was defined as the occurrence of non-fatal stroke, non-fatal myocardial infarction, cardiovascular-related death, or hospital readmission due to cardiac causes [10].

Statistical analysis

Normality of continuous variables was determined via the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Normally distributed data were summarized as mean and standard deviation; non-normal data as median and interquartile range. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. Group comparisons for continuous variables employed independent t-tests for normal data and Mann-Whitney U tests for non-normal data. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. Multigroup comparisons for continuous variables utilized ANOVA for normal data and Kruskal-Wallis tests for non-normal data. Correlations between normally distributed continuous variables were assessed with Pearson’s coefficient; Spearman’s coefficient was used for non-normal variables. Logistic regression modeling was applied, when appropriate, to estimate odds of clinical outcomes between anemic and non-anemic individuals while controlling for potential confounders. All analyses were performed with SPSS version 22.0, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Ethical consideration

The study protocol received approval from the Ethics Committee of Guilan University of Medical Sciences, Rasht, Iran (Ethics Code: IR.GUMS.REC.1402.301). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Results

The baseline characteristics of the total, anemic, and nonanemic groups are listed in Table 1. A total of 419 patients (252 men and 167 women, mean age: 63.70 ± 11.67years) who completed follow-up and reached the end points were enrolled in this study and divided into anemic (n = 251) and nonanemic (n = 168) groups according to different levels of hemoglobin. Here, we showed that HF patients with anemia were older, included more males, and had higher RDW levels, but the MCH, MCHC, hemoglobin, and HCT were significantly lower. Regarding comorbidities, hypertension was more common in anemic patients (60.2% vs. 47%, p = 0.06), diabetes mellitus and hyperlipidemia were significantly higher in the anemic group (p = 0.008 and 0.034, respectively). Previous CABG was more frequent in anemic patients (12% vs. 4.8%, p = 0.01), while cigarette use was also higher in the anemic group (20.7% vs. 13.1%, p = 0.04). The incidence of MACEs in the anemic group was higher than that in the nonanemic group, but it was not statistically significant. No significant differences were observed in other baseline parameters (all had p values > 0.05) (Table 1).

Discussion

This study investigated the role of red blood cell (RBC) indices as predictors of cardiovascular risk in anemic and non-anemic HF patients. Our findings indicate that while anemia and specific RBC indices are associated with a higher burden of comorbidities and certain hematological abnormalities, they did not independently predict MACEin our cohort over a 12-month follow-up period.

Our findings diverge from studies conducted in other cardiovascular populations. For instance, research in non-anemic patients with Acute Coronary Syndrome (ACS) has identified elevated MCV and MCH as independent predictors of long-term MACEs [12, 17, 18]. The proposed mechanism suggests that macrocytic erythrocytes, with their impaired deformability, may hinder microcirculatory flow and reduce antioxidant capacity, thereby exacerbating ischemic injury. Interestingly, in that same ACS cohort, MCHC was not a significant prognostic marker, a finding consistent with our results. The authors hypothesized that higher hemoglobin concentration within smaller cells (high MCHC) might facilitate capillary transit and oxygen delivery, potentially offsetting adverse effects. However, in STEMI patients with anemia, another study found that MCV was not an independent predictor for in-hospital bleeding and mortality, and the worse outcome was not determined by the type of anemia but by atrial fibrillation, older age, diabetes, and other chronic noncardiovascular illnesses [19].

Consistent with previous literature, we found that anemic HF patients were older and had a higher prevalence of comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, and a history of coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) [1, 10, 19]. As expected, the anemic group exhibited significantly lower levels of hemoglobin, hematocrit, MCH, and MCHC, alongside higher RDW, a known marker of anisocytosis and poor prognosis in HF [1, 20]. The higher, though not statistically significant, incidence of all-cause mortality and acute myocardial infarction in the anemic group aligns with the established view of anemia as a marker of disease severity in HF.

Our correlation analysis revealed complex relationships between RBC indices and other parameters. The negative correlation between MCV, MCH, MCHC, and RDW in both groups suggests that dysregulated erythropoiesis and red cell size heterogeneity are common in HF, regardless of anemia status. Interestingly, in the non-anemic group, MCH and MCHC showed a significant negative correlation with HCT, and MCHC was positively correlated with Hb. This may indicate that in patients with preserved hemoglobin levels, the quality and concentration of hemoglobin within red cells become more relevant and could reflect subtle underlying nutritional or inflammatory states not captured by hemoglobin levels alone [12, 13].

The central finding of this study, however, is derived from the multivariable logistic regression analysis. After adjusting for potential confounders, none of the hematological parameters—including hemoglobin, HCT, MCH, and MCHC—were independent predictors of rehospitalization or all-cause mortality. This contrasts with some studies that have found prognostic value in MCV or MCHC in acute coronary syndromes [11–13]. This discrepancy may be attributed to the different patient populations (chronic HF vs. acute coronary syndrome), the advanced stage of heart failure in our cohort (as evidenced by a low mean LVEF of 24%), or the overwhelming influence of the underlying severe cardiac dysfunction itself, which may diminish the relative prognostic weight of hematological parameters. Furthermore, hyperlipidemia showed a strong trend towards increased all-cause mortality, hinting at its persistent role in cardiovascular risk, even in a population with advanced HF.

In summary, while anemia and abnormal RBC indices are prevalent and associated with a worse clinical profile in HF patients, their utility as independent predictors for short-term MACE in a general HF population may be limited. The pathophysiology of HF is multifactorial, and the contribution of erythrocyte metrics to risk stratification may be overshadowed by more dominant factors such as ventricular function, neurohormonal activation, and comorbid conditions.

Our findings do not contradict current guidelines regarding iron therapy in heart failure, as we did not assess iron status or intervene with iron supplementation. Rather, our study suggests that conventional RBC indices may have limited independent prognostic value in advanced HF, where factors such as ventricular dysfunction and comorbidities dominate risk stratification.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its single-center, retrospective design may limit generalizability and introduce unmeasured confounding. Second, the relatively short follow-up period and sample size might have been insufficient to detect smaller effect sizes. Third, we lacked data on the specific etiology of anemia, background heart failure therapy, and advanced iron or inflammatory markers, which could provide deeper pathophysiological insight. Finally, outcome assessment via telephone interview, while practical, may be subject to recall bias.

Also, our study found limited independent prognostic value for isolated red blood cell indices, this underscores the complex, multifactorial nature of risk in advanced heart failure. Future research should explore the utility of composite biomarkers that integrate information across different pathophysiological pathways. For instance, the RDW to eGFR ratio (RGR) combines a marker of erythrocyte heterogeneity and systemic inflammation (RDW) with a key indicator of renal function (eGFR), reflecting intertwined cardio-renal-anemic interactions. Preliminary studies in other cardiac populations suggest that such combined indices may offer superior predictive power for adverse outcomes compared to single parameters [6–8]. Our study, by design, did not evaluate these novel composites, which represents a limitation but also a clear direction for future investigation to refine risk stratification in heart failure.

Conclusions

In this study, heart failure patients with anemia exhibited a poorer clinical profile and altered red blood cell indices. However, over a one-year follow-up period in this cohort, these hematological parameters were not independent predictors of major adverse cardiovascular events, specifically rehospitalization or all-cause mortality. Our findings indicate that conventional RBC indices are not independent prognostic markers for short-term MACE in patients with advanced heart failure. Nevertheless, these results do not diminish the importance of evaluating and managing anemia—especially iron deficiency—in clinical practice. Adherence to established guidelines for anemia management remains essential, as targeted iron therapy may still improve patient-centered outcomes. Furthermore, the prognostic utility of these indices may vary in less advanced stages of heart failure or when integrated with other biomarkers.
