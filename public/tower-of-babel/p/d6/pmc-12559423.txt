Correlation of three wear testing methods and surface roughness analysis in 3D-printed crown materials
Authors: Anastasiia Grymak, Kai Chun Li, Andrew B Cameron, Sunyoung Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe objective of this in vitro study was to evaluate and correlate the wear resistance of three additively manufactured dental restorative materials using three wear test methods. ball-on-disc, block-on-ring, and linear reciprocation. Three polished or glazed restorative materials were divided into four groups and subjected to wear testing under 49 N and 70 N loads, submerged in artificial saliva to simulate 48 months of in vivo wear. Vertical loss, step-height, surface roughness and friction coefficient were recorded. Correlation and statistical analyses were conducted. Ball-on-disc and reciprocative methods exhibited an overall weak positive correlation, while reciprocation vs. block-on-ring had weak to negative correlations. Three-way ANOVA indicated a significant effect of material type on vertical loss values (P < 0.03). Reciprocation caused higher surface roughness for Crowntec Plus and VarseoSmile Plus (P < 0.05), but not for MFH C&B (P > 0.05). Friction coefficient remained clinically acceptable across all methods. Increased load (49 N to 70 N) minimally impacted wear but affected surface roughness. Materials exhibited varying wear performance based on the testing method used. Weak correlations were observed across wear tests in most instances, with increases in load having minimal impact on wear outcomes and variable impact on surface roughness depending on the testing method.Keywords: Wear, In vitro testing, Correlation, 3D-printing, Additive manufacturing, Block-on-ring, Reciprocation wear, Ball-on-disc, Dental wearSubject terms: Biomaterials, Biomedical materials

Introduction

Wear resistance of dental materials are often investigated using chewing/wear simulators with antagonists such as a stainless steel ball which is designed to simulate human enamel1,2. Due to the difficulties in replicating the complex nature of oral environment, in vitro studies frequently yield varied wear outcomes, often further complicated by different methodologies used in studies2,3.

Wear testing encompasses two primary mechanisms: two-body wear, where the antagonist directly contacts the material and, three-body wear, which introduces abrasive particles between the surfaces4. Various testing methods such as chewing simulators, pin-on-disc, reciprocative wear, block-on-ring, and ball-on-disc are included in the wear testing, and ISO/TS 14569-2 (2001) references relevant equipment for these. Nevertheless, there are several studies which employed testing methods outside ISO standardization or adapted certain ISO elements within non-standardized methods (e.g., CS-4.8, SD Mechatronik, Feldkirchen-Westerham)5,6.

These inconsistencies significantly affect the evaluation of critical wear characteristics such as the coefficient of friction (CoF) or surface roughness which are the key parameters that guide the dental material choice7,8 CoF is a measure of amount of friction existing between two surfaces, and therefore, a lower value is preferred as surface roughness, wear and overall performance would be affected conversely otherwise9. As a reference, the CoF between enamel/enamel has been reported to reach up to 0.910 while enamel against ceramics varies between 0.28 and 0.711–13. Surface roughness values below 0.2 μm are also desirable to minimize plaque accumulation, wear rates and surface friction14.

Wear tests also differ in experimental parameters such as load, medium, number of cycles, material shape, surface treatment, and the choice of antagonist material – all of which directly affect wear outcomes15,16. Clinically, occlusal forces vary substantially depending on tooth location and patient factors, with average masticatory forces around 10–120 N and maximum bite forces in the posterior region often exceeding 200–360 N17,18. In vitro studies, including chewing simulations, have shown that increasing applied load—especially in the presence of abrasive media—significantly accelerates wear of composite materials19. This indicates that laboratory tests with lower loads may underestimate clinical wear, particularly in high-load posterior applications. This variability underscores the absence of a universally accepted testing approach, highlighting a critical gap in the reliable assessment of wear resistance for dental materials. Moreover, the number of cycles were not clearly defined in wear testing methods and have been scarce since the 1980s2,20. Additionally, the 12 wear simulators outlined in ISO 14569-2, (2001) providing general guidelines for test performance21. Therefore, there is a need to investigate the wear produced through commonly employed methods and to determine whether correlations exist between various wear testing procedures.

These complexities in wear assessment are further compounded by the introduction of new materials, such as those produced through additive manufacturing (AM). AM materials can have different filler composition22 and processing protocols such as polymerization processes23, printing orientation22–24, layer thickness25, and post-processing techniques23 which may significantly influence wear characteristics, mechanical properties and surface roughness. Due to these variable characteristics and manufacturing processes involved in the AM industry, these are additional confounding factors when considering results from different wear tests26–28. Previous research often reports that wear resistance of AM materials by comparing them to conventionally or subtractive manufactured polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) resins28–34. However, the findings from these studies may not be directly comparable due to variations in wear testing methodologies, the number of cycles, antagonists, printing techniques, and the types of 3D-printed materials used.

It remains unclear how the wear of materials with varying mechanical properties and manufacturing methods (e.g., build orientation, layer thickness, type of 3D-printer) translate to long-term clinical behavior22,35. It is expected that AM materials will deteriorate over time, with their mechanical properties diminishing due to masticatory forces, leading to increased wear and surface roughness32. Surface treatment such as glazing is often recommended to protect the outer layer of restorations, however, this is not expected to be a long-term solution as once the glaze deteriorates in conjunction with the ongoing process of chewing, the surface roughness of remaining restoration would increase significantly, potentially undermining the long-term durability of the restoration32.

Considering the number of different wear tests available, this study aimed to assess any correlation among three wear testing methods – ball-on-disc, block-on-ring, and reciprocation by comparing vertical loss, the CoF and surface roughness of three different AM materials. While these tests are not included in ISO 14569-2 (2001), they were selected based on their ability to offer a more standardized and controlled environment for evaluating material wear resistance following standardizations (ASTM G99-17, ISO 20808 and ASTM G133) and performed on a single wear testing machine. The null hypothesis in this study was that no significant correlation would be observed among different wear tests.

Materials and methods

Three commercially available 3D printed resins (VarseoSmile Crown Plus, Bego, Germany; Crowntec, Saremco, Switzerland; NextDent C&B MFH, NextDent, The Netherlands) normally used to produce permanent (VarseoSmile Crown Plus, Bego and Crowntec, Saremco) and temporary (NextDent C&B MFH) indirect restorations were investigated (Table 1). Artificial saliva (Biotène Dry Mouth Gentle Oral Rinse, Haleon, United Kingdom) was used as the testing medium, and stainless-steel material with various configuration according to different wear tests was used as the antagonist.

Specimen preparation

All specimens (n = 120) were designed using AutoCAD 2024 (Autodesk Inc.) and manufactured with a 3D digital light processing (DLP) printer (Asiga Max UV, Asiga) with a layer thickness of 50 μm (Fig. 1). The block-on-ring (BoR), (n = 40) configuration included a ring (7 mm × 40 mm) with a stainless-steel block (8 mm × 4.5 mm × 10 mm) as the antagonist. The BoR specimens were printed at a 90-degree build angle to match the lattice structure with the contact surface of the antagonist. The ball-on-disc (BoD), (n = 40) configuration consists of a disc (30 mm × 10 mm) paired with a standardized stainless-steel ball (ø 6 mm). The BoD specimens were additively manufactured with flat print orientation on a printing bed to align with the lattice structure of two other testing methods to achieve same parallel structure. Printed specimens were post-processed as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Reciprocation (Recip), (n = 40), configuration included rectangular blocks (10 × 10 × 4 mm) following ISO 14,569–2, at a layer thickness of 50 μm and build angle of 0◦, with a stainless-steel block (8 mm × 4.5 mm × 10 mm) as the antagonist. All blocks were embedded in epoxy resin (EpoFix Kit, Struers, Denmark) for specimen stability32 (Table 1).

Diagram of the experimental procedures. Red arrow: antagonist; Green arrow: load- 49–70 N; Purple arrow: tested material; Blue arrow: direction of wear test.

The specimens were stored away from direct light and tested within seven days of manufacturing32. The specimens were sequentially polished with silicon carbide paper at P1200, P2000 and P4000 grit on a polishing machine (TegraPol 21, Struers) with a continuous water supply and abbreviated as ‘NG’. Half of the specimens (n = 60) were surface treated with a thin layer of glaze (Dreve, NanoVarnish) using a glazing brush, and cured as per the manufacturer’s instructions with a light polymerization unit (EyeVolution MAX, Dreve) for 7 min and abbreviated as ‘G’. The glaze thickness ranged between 15 and 20 μm according to the measurements done using surface profilometer after the wear test.

Experimental methodology

The BoD, BoR and three-body-wear contact tests were conducted using a universal testing machine (UFW200 Universal Multi-Function Wear Test System, NeoPlus) according to the ISO 20808:2016, ISO 7148-2:2012/ ASTM G99-1736, and ISO 14569-2:2001 including ASTM G133, respectively. All tests were done in wet conditions in artificial saliva (Biotène Dry Mouth Gentle Oral Rinse, Haleon, United Kingdom)37 simulating the wear behavior in the oral cavity and making the results more clinically relevant21,36,38,39. Each material was divided into four groups (n = 5 per group), based on the combination of loads (49–70 N) and surface treatments (polished or glazed).

The BoD experimental method employed vertical loads of 49–70 N, a sliding speed of 0.1 m/s, a wear radius of 3 mm, and a sliding distance of 1000 m at a controlled temperature of 27–30 °C. This study refined previous approaches by calculating the number of cycles according to the average length of stroke in natural dentition36. The wear testing spanned 53,000 cycles, corresponding to approximately four years of in vivo use, as specified by ISO 20808:201638. The BoR method used the same testing conditions as the BoD except for the sliding speed of 1 Hz or 60 RPM. The BoR testing lasted for 8,286 cycles which also equated to approximately four years in vivo (ISO 20808:201638; DeLong et al. (1985).

Reciprocative wear data of vertical loss, CoF and surface roughness readings were used for method comparison with other two methods and to determine correlations between methods.

The vertical loss (µm) and CoF were recorded simultaneously and obtained from a universal wear testing machine software (UWFSOFT-V1, NeoPlus). The surface roughness in terms of Sa (areal average roughness), Ra (linear-based) (µm), and step height (recorded deepest point in the specimen according to its surface) (µm) were recorded from four quadrants of each specimen with one Sa measurement and three readings for Ra and step-height per quadrant, using a confocal laser microscope (CLSM) (LEXT OLS5100, Olympus). Antagonists were also evaluated for their surface roughness, and randomly selected antagonists further went assessments for any wear and scratch occurrence under CLSM.

To assess linear relationships and to correlate the wear testing data, Pearson’s correlation for data that have passed the normality test and Spearman’s rank for the data that did not pass the normality test correlations methods were applied.

Collected data for all testing methods, including vertical loss, volumetric loss, step height, surface roughness, and CoF, was statistically analyzed using SPSS (SPSS v27.0; IBM Corp). The vertical loss and surface roughness for the reciprocative method were obtained from a previous study32. A one-way ANOVA was used to compare between three methods for every group. Normality was tested for using Kruskal Wallis. Mann-Whitney U tests were instead conducted for non-normally distributed data. Three-way ANOVA was used to determine the interaction between three wear testing methods, which included dependencies of materials, load and surface types.

Results

Vertical loss

Figure 2a; Table 2 present mean vertical loss results. Certain groups consistently showed significant differences in vertical loss between specific testing methods. For example, across all three materials, significant differences were observed between BoR and BoD in the 49N_NG groups, as well as between BoD and reciprocation in both 70N_G and 70N_NG groups. In contrast, no significant differences between testing methods were observed in the 49N_G group for both VarseoSmile plus and MFH C&B. In the Crowntec Plus material, significant differences were observed in all groups between at least two testing method pairings. The three-way ANOVA revealed that material type had a significant main effect on vertical loss values (F = 3.48, P < 0.03). Significant interactions were found between material type, glaze, load, and method, suggesting that the effect of material type on vertical loss varies depending on the combination of these factors (P < 0.05). However, the effect of load alone was not significant (P > 0.05).

Step-height

The results did not show comparable statistical significance between methods for step height as observed for vertical loss. For instance, significant differences in vertical loss were found across all testing methods in the VarseoSmile Plus 49N_NG group, whereas no such differences were noted for step height (Fig. 2b). For MFH C&B 49N_G, this pattern was reversed. All MFH C&B groups exhibited at least one significant difference between testing method pairings. The three-way ANOVA revealed a significant interaction between material, method, and load (F = 2.46, P < 0.001), emphasizing the importance of the combined effects of these factors on wear performance. Overall, vertical loss remained consistent across materials for BoR and BoD, whereas reciprocation showed a trend of higher vertical loss and step height for the high filler content materials (VarseoSmile Plus, Crowntec Plus) and for the low filler content material (MFH C&B). Notably, only step height was higher for MFH C&B, while vertical loss was lower compared to the high filler content materials.

The remaining results for BoD vs. BoR exhibited moderate to negative correlations ranging between r = 0.53 and r = -0.56. BoD vs. reciprocation exhibited an overall positive trend, though the results showed only weak to moderate positive correlation in MFH C&B. VarseoSmile Plus had moderate to strong correlations in 49N_G (r = 0.67, P > 0.21) and 70N_G (r = 0.83, P > 0.08) groups. Crowntec Plus exhibited a mostly negative correlation with only one moderate positive correlation observed in the 70N_NG group (r = 0.75, P > 0.15).

Surface roughness

The mean Ra (µm) values are presented in Fig. 4b; Table 3. The surface roughness values measured in the BoR group are significantly higher than those measured in the BoD group across all three materials. Surface roughness was above 0.2 μm after the wear testing. These Ra (µm) results were compared with those from our previous study’s reciprocation data33. It was found that reciprocation had a greater degree of surface roughness (Ra) compared to other simulation methods across all testing groups for Crowntec Plus and VarseoSmile Plus (P < 0.05), but not for MFH C&B materials (P > 0.05).

Discussion

This study evaluated the correlation between three engineering-based wear testing methods – BoD, BoR and reciprocation using AM restorative dental materials under varying load and surface treatments. The null hypothesis was rejected as indicated by significant differences between testing methods, materials and loads. The observed differences highlight the complexity of correlating wear testing methods. Therefore, in vitro results should be considered with caution when comparing results from studies using various testing methods and when extrapolating the data to determine the long-term clinical performance of dental materials.

The size of filler particles in resin-based restorative materials affects material properties such as flexural strength, resin flowability, hardness and modulus elasticity. An increase in the filler particle size can also have a negative, non- impact on the polymerization efficiency which will be associated with a higher CoF. This can then lead to increased internal shear stress within the polymer matrix affecting the overall mechanical properties40. Considering these, the different filler content of the materials was expected to influence wear in the current study. However, the impact of material composition on wear was only observed with the reciprocation method, where the increased filler content materials resulted in higher wear41. This inconsistency in the results further highlights the importance of establishing a universal wear testing method so that reliable data can be used when assessing AM materials.

The current study employed surface roughness measurement Ra. It serves as effective indicator to evaluate AM materials as they can have peaks, valleys and often non-uniform surfaces during the layer-by-layer manufacturing process. These measurements are also aligned with industrial recommendations and the integration of standardized techniques as outlined in device guidelines42,43. Ra measurements are especially beneficial as they can be done ‘along’ and ‘across’ the build layers to give further insights into the surface characteristics of AM materials. For all materials Ra data showed significant differences between testing methods but not between groups. Repeated measurements of surfaces showed that reciprocation tended to produce lower Ra values compared to BoR and BoD, suggesting that reciprocative wear is 2-directional more aggressive and leads to smoother surfaces. The surface roughness of Crowntec Plus did not differ significantly between different wear testing methods and thus indicating stable surface quality. Therefore, materials with similar wear behavior across different testing apparatus may be preferable to test their predictability and consistency in wear performance.

The different loads used in the current study did not result in significant changes in wear or surface roughness across all materials when tested using the BoR method. However, variations in surface roughness were observed with the BoD method, where non-glazed and glazed materials exhibited almost identical roughness. In contrast, the other two methods showed decreased roughness in non-glazed materials compared to their glazed counterparts. This can be explained by the increase in surface area in relation to the constant load resulting in reduced stress on tested material. As a result, the applied forces progressively became lower than the yield stress of the material, leading to a decrease in abrasion44. The differences in contact areas between the testing methods may provide insight into various clinical scenarios, such as worn dentition, localized occlusal loading, or material behavior under different wear patterns. According to the current study, it can be stated that differing loads up to 70 N have a minimal impact across methods, regardless of surface changes. However, load considerations should remain important when standardizing across methods, especially if minimizing surface roughness is a priority. Nevertheless, loads above 70 N could affect wear rates and material deformation. Further research with higher and more varied loads may reveal different correlations across testing methods and offer insights into wear behavior under stronger masticatory forces. Notably, the biting forces are typically observed in the first molars and can reach up to 300 N in healthy young adults45.

The current study showed that glazing generally improved wear resistance, as the glaze layer materials remained intact longer than non-glazed ones and demonstrated more consistent performance over time during the wear test. Glazed and non-glazed materials exhibited varying responses to deformation depending on the method of wear testing. The BoR method showed no signs of material deformation, whereas the reciprocation testing led to visible deformation and glaze chipping32. The MFH C&B material glazed groups exhibited significantly higher step values in the BoD testing method. This may be due to the relatively high speed of the BoD test which may have accelerated glaze wear by causing chipping and fretting of the material, potentially adding approximately 15–20 μm to the wear value. While results had a high standard deviation, due to the small vertical loss, these differences were clinically negligible even when considered with the increased variability.

Previous research has shown a poor correlation between in vitro wear testing methods and clinical outcomes, with no consistent connection across methods6,7,46–48. Those studies that reported correlations included a limited number of restorative materials (two or three) and were limited to one type of load and surface treatment46,48. Furthermore, the clinical data used for comparative analyses in these studies has been criticized for inaccuracies and inconsistencies, as they were derived from studies that followed different protocols47,48.

Recent clinical study have used VarseoSmile Plus material to measure vertical loss over 24 months49. The study revealed that 35% of restorations had wear beyond 0.5 mm and were classified as fracture, meanwhile other restoration wear was varying between 0.11 and 0.35 mm. In our in vitro assessments, the choice of method could potentially be assigned to the specific type of wear being analyzed, for example, the BoR method tends to simulate flatter wear, while the BoD and Recip method represents higher cusp-like stress conditions, while BoD method is exposed to more surfaces, Recip method is concentrated in very small area.

VarseoSmile Crown Plus demonstrated lower-than-clinical wear in our simulation: even under Recip method, vertical loss remained below the clinical extremes with highest vertical loss of at 70 NG 145.1 ± 107 μm). Establishing these relationships between test methods and clinical outcomes is important because it helps identify which laboratory protocols best replicate intraoral wear patterns. This supports the selection of testing methods that are more predictive of real-world performance and ultimately assist clinicians in material selection based on anticipated functional loading conditions. Furthermore, this may suggest two key points: first, in vitro protocols may underestimate the actual in vivo wear, especially in high-load posterior regions; second, while VarseoSmile Crown Plus shows comparatively favorable wear resistance among printed materials, but clinical use may best be restricted to low-load or interim applications until durability under full occlusal stress is confirmed.

Previous research indicated that wear values for resin-based materials placed in posterior teeth should not exceed 50 μm over a 1-year period from 6 to 18 months3, which translates to roughly 200 μm over four years. In the current study, vertical loss measurements from BoR and reciprocative methods exceeded this threshold while BoD data consistently remained well below. While it is acknowledged that wear may not progress linearly over time, this discrepancy suggests that the choice of testing method could misrepresent a material’s long-term wear behavior. As such, multiple testing methods including greater testing variability should be used for broader estimation until clinical comparisons or more advanced testing methods become available. Finally, the current research highlights the lack of ISO standardization and guidelines, and further research is required.

The limitations of this study include the use of only two loads, which may not fully represent the range of biting forces (up to 300 N) encountered in patients45. Expanding the study to include additional testing loads could provide more comprehensive insights. Another limitation was the inability to record volumetric loss for the BoD and BoR tests due to the significantly larger testing surface areas compared to the reciprocative wear test. The CLMS used in the current study was unable to detect measurements below 5 μm, which may have influenced the overall results. Additionally, while changes in the surface roughness of the ball antagonist could not be measured, its small dimension made it impossible to assess volumetric loss accurately.

The lack of a universal correlation between testing methods and clinical wear data highlights the need for more refined testing approaches as wear testing is time-consuming and resource intensive. Therefore, future research should focus on developing predictive algorithms that integrate material properties, testing conditions and clinical data to model long-term wear. Such models could provide more accurate estimates of material longevity and help to bridge the gap between laboratory testing and clinical outcomes. By comparing the predictions with clinical outcomes and factoring in load types, material properties, and testing methods, we can estimate how many chewing cycles a material can withstand. This approach would ensure that laboratory results align with real-world performance and would improve our understanding of material durability based on chemical composition and mechanical properties.

Different test methods on AM materials resulted in varying degree of wear and surface roughness. Certain test methods may exhibit greater degree of wear due to different material.
