Capillary gingival hemangioma in a cat
Authors: Se Eun KIM, Na-Young LEE, Jeong-Seop OH, Dae-Yong KIM
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 12-year-old spayed female American short-haired cat presented with a palatal gingival mass located between the right maxillary third incisor and the canine teeth. The mass was dark red and had a narrow attachment to the gingival margin of the canine tooth. The mass was completely removed by marginal excision and the histopathological diagnosis was a capillary hemangioma. The mass did not relapse until 1 year later; however, the tooth was extracted because of cervical resorption of the right maxillary canine immediately adjacent to the mass resection site. This report presents a rare case of the gingival hemangioma in a cat and the possibility of a causal relationship between the occurrence of external cervical tooth resorption and hemangioma resection.Keywords: capillary hemangioma, cat, external tooth resorption, gingiva

Hemangiomas of dogs and cats are benign tumors originating from the vascular endothelium and mainly include capillary and cavernous types, although arteriovenous types are occasionally reported [7, 11]. These lesions can manifest in various anatomical locations, such as bones, heart, kidneys, liver, skin, spleen, and tongue, potentially leading to severe bleeding secondary to anemia when located in certain areas [5].

In cats, the oral cavity is the fourth most common site for tumors [1]. A previous study on oral tumors in cats reported that nearly 90% of oral tumors were malignant, with more than 60% squamous cell carcinomas and 12% fibrosarcomas [14]. Benign lesions that occur in the oral cavity of cats include gingival hyperplasia, odontogenic fibromas, feline-inductive odontogenic tumors, eosinophilic granuloma complexes, amyloid-producing odontogenic tumors, and feline pyogenic granulomas. There are few reports of hemangiomas of the oral mucosa in cats, especially in the gingiva, and reports of oral hemangiomas in dogs are rare [9].

Tooth resorption is a common dental disease in cats that results in the loss of tooth structure and can be classified into internal and external resorption, depending on the anatomical location. The specific etiology of tooth resorption in cats has not yet been identified; however, external cervical root surface resorption is known to be caused by minor trauma (e.g., mastication and vascularization) to the cementum [2]. This report describes the case of a cat that was diagnosed with a gingival hemangioma and subsequently developed a tooth resorptive lesion at the same location after treatment.

A 12-year-old, 4.11-kg spayed female American short-haired cat was presented for health screening. The patient had undergone extraction of the left maxillary canine and third premolar had been extracted 2 years prior because of tooth resorption. The owner recently reported gingival redness and swelling on the palatal side of the right maxillary canine, without loss of appetite or pain. A nonanesthetic oral examination revealed severe dental calculus in the bilateral maxillary premolars (calculus index [CI] 3) and gingival inflammation (gingival index [GI] 3) [6, 12]. The mandibular cheek teeth and canines showed mild calculi (CI 1) and gingival inflammation (GI 1). Mild calculus and gingival inflammation were found in the right maxillary canine, and a dark red spherical gingival mass with a diameter of 0.5 cm was found between the right maxillary canine and third incisor (Fig. 1A). The mass was slightly displaced to the palatal side of the right mandibular canine, with normal occlusion. There were no abnormalities such as gingival hyperplasia, stomatitis, or lymph node enlargement.

Blood profiles including complete blood count (CBC) and serum chemistry were within the normal range except for a slight increase in creatinine (1.8 g/dL; reference range: 1.0–1.6 g/dL). No significant abnormalities were identified by thoracic radiography or abdominal ultrasonography. Dental examination under general anesthesia revealed several missing teeth including the right maxillary second premolar, left maxillary second and third premolar, left mandibular third premolar and right mandibular third premolar, and tooth resorption was observed in the right maxillary third premolar tooth (stage 4c). There was no evidence of inflammation in the oral mucosa, except in the gingiva adjacent to the calculus. The gingival mass between the right maxillary third incisor and the canine, was slightly mobile, with a narrow attachment to the palatal gingival margin of the canine without periodontal pathology. Intraoral radiography revealed that the bilateral mandibular third premolars had stage 5 type 1 tooth resorption and the right maxillary third premolar had type 2 tooth resorption. There was no evidence of osteolysis between the right maxillary third incisor and canine.

After professional dental cleaning, the gingival mass was excised using a diode laser (K4 Eco; Hulaser Inc., Seoul, Korea). Sutures were not required as the attachment site on the gingival margin was narrow (Fig. 1B). For histopathological examination, the gingival biopsy specimen was fixed in 10% neutral phosphate-buffered formalin, processed routinely, cut into 3-µm thick sections, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Histopathological examination revealed that the gingival mass was polypoid, well-circumscribed, and consisted of vascular spaces of various sizes filled with erythrocytes (Fig. 2A). They were lined with a single layer of uniform endothelial cells with inconspicuous nuclei, and mitotic figures were rare (Fig. 2B). The mass was diagnosed as a capillary hemangioma, and it was confirmed that the resection site had completely healed on reexamination 2 weeks after surgery. At the 6-month follow-up, the owner noted that the patient had mild salivation around the right maxillary canine. There were no apparent abnormalities or obvious clinical signs of pain, and the mass did not recur (Fig. 1C).

The patient visited again 1 year after gingival mass resection for an annual periodontal status follow-up. The owner reported increased calculus formation on the patient’s remaining cheek teeth and general gingival redness. On the gross nonanesthetic oral examination, the hemangioma had not relapsed. However, a small and slit defect was found in the cervical region of the palatal surface of the right maxillary canine adjacent to the mass resection site (Fig. 1D). When the defect was examined using a dental explorer, the patient showed a pain response and bleeding was observed from the lesion.

To accurately identify the defect, a dental examination was performed under general anesthesia. Preanesthetic examinations, including CBC, serum chemistry, and electrolyte levels, were within the normal range, except for mildly elevated creatinine (1.76 g/dL). Tooth resorption, which had not been identified in the previous year (Fig. 3A), was observed at the cervical root of the right maxillary canine on intraoral radiographs (Fig. 3B). No additional changes were observed in the alveolar bone or gingiva at the location of the prior hemangioma. The right maxillary canine was diagnosed with stage 2 external root resorption, and extraction was planned because the defect was exposed to the oral cavity and bleeding occurred during probing. After professional dental cleaning, the right maxillary canine was extracted and the alveolar socket was closed with a single-layered gingival flap using 5–0 polyglecaprone 25 (Monocryl; Ethicon Inc., Somerville, NJ, USA) after alveoloplasty with a round bur. The extraction site healed without complications after 2 weeks.

Oral hemangiomas in humans can occur on the gingiva, palatal mucosa, lips, and salivary glands and are often of the capillary type. Periodontally, gingival hemangiomas develop from the interdental gingival papilla and progress toward the adjacent teeth [2].

There are few reports of oral hemangiomas in cats, and there has been a previous report of multiple hemangiomas on the lateral surface of the tongue in a 2.5-year-old Siamese cat [7]. Pyogenic granulomas, a relatively common type of benign oral proliferations in cats, can histologically mimic hemangiomas because they are accompanied by vascular proliferation. However, pyogenic granulomas usually occur as a soft-tissue masses at the site of mechanical irritation in patients with malocclusion, and the most consistent histological feature is extensive ulcerative granulation tissue containing blood vessels and collagen fibers. Feline pyogenic granulomas also show lymphoplasmacytic inflammation, similar to feline chronic stomatitis, and may present as lobular capillary hemangiomas with thick outward granulation tissue [8]. Recent studies have suggested that pyogenic granuloma and lobular capillary hemangiomas should be considered separate lesions because they show clinical and pathological differences in the pattern of capillaries and inflammatory infiltrates [13, 15]. The hemangioma in this case was a spherical reddish mass that occurred on the gingival margin without mechanical irritation, and the visual aspect and location of occurrence were similar to those of epulis. Histopathological examination revealed capillaries with a thin mucosal epithelium without granulation tissue or cells involved in chronic inflammation. Therefore, the patient was diagnosed with capillary hemangioma rather than epulis or pyogenic granuloma.

A leading theory explaining tooth resorption in cats is that all cats are affected by surface resorption and that very small resorption sites usually heal with the formation of cementum-like tissue; however, if these sites do not heal, tooth resorption occurs. [2]. In humans, external cervical tooth resorption occurs due to damage to the cervical attachment apparatus. Inflammatory cytokines, interleukin-1 and tumor necrosis factor-alpha are mediators of osteoclastic bone resorption, and root resorption can occur when inflamed fibrovascular connective tissue exists in the cervical region where the periodontal ligament is not intact [3]. There are also case reports on occurrence of tooth resorption when reactive gingival lesions such as pyogenic granuloma or peripheral giant cell granuloma are present [4, 10].

The patient in this case showed external cervical resorption at the adjacent site 1 year after hemangioma removal on the palatal side of the right maxillary canine. There was a history of salivation near the site at the 6-month postoperative follow-up, and tooth resorption was observed at the same site at the 1-year follow-up; therefore, the possibility that tooth resorption was related to the gingival hemangioma could not be excluded. However, this patient already had the left maxillary canine extracted several years earlier due to tooth resorption; therefore, tooth resorption may not be related to capillary hemangioma. Dental radiography was required to confirm the cause at the 6-month postoperative follow-up; however, it was not performed because of the owner’s concern about anesthesia owing to the age of the patient, which was a limitation in this case.

Hemangioma is a tumor that can occur in any part of the body where blood vessels are distributed, but it is well known that it rarely occurs in the oral cavity of cats and generally has a good prognosis with surgical removal. Herein, we report a case of a hemangioma at the gingival margin of a cat with subsequent tooth resorption at an adjacent site. Although gingival hemangioma is rare and difficult to generalize from a single case, it may be necessary to monitor tooth resorption in cases of hemangioma on the gingival margin at the time of occurrence and even after surgical removal, as vascularization and tooth resorption are closely related.
