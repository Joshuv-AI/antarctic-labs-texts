Rheumatic Multivalvular Disease in Older Patients
Authors: José Martín Alanís-Naranjo, Kevin David Aragón-Ontiveros, Julio Cesar Rivera-Hermosillo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Rheumatic multiple valvular heart disease remains undertreated and underestimated in older patients; many are unaware of their diagnosis until they experience complications. This case series presents 3 older patients with undiagnosed rheumatic multiple valvular heart disease who presented with complications, describing their valvular assessment and therapeutic outcomes.

Multiple valvular heart disease (MVHD) is a highly prevalent condition, affecting 30%-34% of older patients with valvular heart disease (VHD).1,2 As a result of population aging, degenerative valve disease (63%) has overtaken rheumatic heart disease (RHD) as the most common cause of elderly MVHD (18%-22%).1, 2, 3 Nevertheless, in registries of RHD, 44.7%-50.4% of elderly patients have MVHD.1,4 The most common valvular lesion is mitral valve disease (MVD), especially mitral stenosis (MS), followed by aortic and tricuspid valve disorders.2,5Take-Home Messages•Older patients with RHD are unaware of their diagnosis until they experience complications.•Screening for early intervention and beginning antibiotic prophylaxis at a young age can reduce the impact of clinical RHD in older patients.•The Heart Team’s assessment should guide intervention in older patients with rheumatic multivalvular disease.•Control of risk factors and treatment of comorbidities could improve outcomes, reduce operative risk, and simplify the procedure for older patients undergoing intervention.

RHD remains prevalent among populations with limited access to primary health care, especially in remote areas where cardiologic diagnostic tools are scarce and awareness is low. Now, many older patients living with RHD are unaware of their diagnosis and the sore throat/ acute rheumatic fever that preceded RHD.5,6 In the elderly, symptoms are often minimized and attributed to "getting older." In addition, the contribution of comorbidities to symptoms often confounds the picture of rheumatic VHD.4

Consequently, in many RHD-endemic areas, most elderly patients seek medical attention once they develop severe rheumatic VHD and experience complications such as heart failure, atrial fibrillation (AF), pulmonary hypertension, stroke, systemic embolism, and infective endocarditis.5 The long-term complications of RHD continue to burden low- and middle-income countries. However, in these countries, studies are primarily on pediatric patients, and information on older patients is limited.7

Herein, we present a case series describing the clinical presentation and echocardiographic characteristics of 3 older patients with rheumatic MVHD presenting with complications.

Case Series

Three previously asymptomatic older patients without family or personal history of cardiovascular disease presented with new-onset symptoms. As a result of living in rural remote areas, they reported poor access to health care throughout their lives. The patients underwent clinical assessment and echocardiography. Case 1

An 83-year-old woman presented with palpitations and progressive worsening dyspnea. AF was diagnosed on admission, as well as moderate aortic regurgitation (AR) and mixed MVD (Figure 1, Videos 1 and 2). Due to the patient severe mitral regurgitation, the patient was not a candidate for percutaneous mitral commissurotomy (PMC), so the Heart Team decided to perform mitral valve replacement (MVR), which was done without complications 3 months later. Follow-up transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) showed normal prosthetic valve function without progression of AR.

Case 2

A 72-year-old woman experienced progressive dyspnea and lower limb edema. The patient was diagnosed with moderate AR, severe tricuspid regurgitation (TR), and severe mixed MVD. (Figure 2, Video 3, Video 4, Video 5). Given the concomitant severe TR and the fact that the patient was not a candidate for PMC due to the severe mitral regurgitation, the Heart Team decided to perform MVR and tricuspid annuloplasty. Four months later, the patient underwent surgery without complications; follow-up TTE showed a mitral mechanical valve with good position and function, mild TR, and no progression of AR.

Case 3

A 65-year-old woman with acute ischemic stroke was admitted to the hospital. A heart murmur and AF were also discovered. Moderate MS, moderate TR, left ventricular ejection fraction of 34%, and spontaneous echocardiographic contrast were detected (Figure 3, Videos 6 and 7). Due to the high likelihood of left atrial appendage thrombus formation due to spontaneous echocardiographic contrast, the Heart Team decided to perform MVR with additional left atrial appendage ligation. Six months later, the patient underwent surgery without complications; follow-up TTE revealed normal function of the mitral mechanical valve and improvement in TR (now mild) and left ventricular ejection fraction (54%).

Discussion

In most chronic RHD elderly patients, no history of acute rheumatic fever is present. Thus, the initial or recurrent insults are subclinical or not detected at a young age.2,5 At a young age, a mild RHD presents no progression after 10 years in 64% of cases, with only 11.4% progressing to severe RHD.5 In elderly patients, rheumatic MVD progresses slowly, gradually, and similarly to degenerative MS, explaining the late onset of symptoms.8 Furthermore, older patients may not recognize symptoms because they have gradually reduced their daily activity levels.5

Screening for early intervention and beginning antibiotic prophylaxis at a young age can reduce the impact of clinical RHD in older patients. Echocardiography has become a valuable tool for detecting RHD, with standardized criteria (Table 3).5,6,9 Active case-finding offers the best chance of detecting mild-moderate RHD early when secondary prophylaxis is most effective. Older patients with rheumatic MVHD face significant challenges, including choice of procedure, preoperative assessment, and postoperative care.1,6 Interventions differ according to the country’s income level, with most countries treating severe VHD with surgery.2,5,6

Elderly patients with MVHD are usually not in critical condition on diagnosis (0.66%).1 However, in assessing risk, the patient’s age is often an important psychological factor, and many physicians refrain from referring them to intervention due to their advanced age.3 Comorbidities, in association with frailty, cause less functional reserve for postoperative recovery, which negatively affects the prognosis and increases the risk of operative intervention.2,3

Management of MVHD should follow the recommendations pertaining to the predominant VHD. Intervention of MVHD in older patients should be guided by the Heart Team’s assessment of the patient’s comorbidities and the risks associated with combining procedures. In rheumatic MVHD, PMC may delay surgery in cases of severe MS with moderate AR.10

Although surgery is associated with an increased risk of morbidity and mortality in this age group, elderly patients with severe MVHD who underwent any valvular intervention had better outcomes than those who were managed conservatively. Furthermore, it improves survival and returns it to age-appropriate norms.1, 2, 3 Patients were transferred to the nearest primary healthcare centers in all 3 cases and continued with long-term anticoagulants without complications. At the follow-up, the patients continued to experience AF but reported improved overall condition, allowing them to return to their habitual routines without any complaints.

Surgeon experience, patient characteristics, and complexity of lesions should be considered when selecting surgery.4 Transcatheter valve technology is preferred in elderly patients with multiple comorbid conditions or high surgical risks.3 Elective interventions coupled with effective risk factor control and treatment for comorbidities could improve outcomes, reduce operative risk, and simplify the procedure for older patients.2

Conclusions

In elderly patients, VHD remains undertreated and underestimated, with RHD being the most common cause of MVHD. By evaluating valvular lesions, patient condition, and perioperative risks, appropriate treatment can be selected for older patients with MVHD. Detecting RHD at an earlier stage in adults can allow us to provide better monitoring and treatment, including secondary prophylaxis, to reduce the disease burden among older patients.
