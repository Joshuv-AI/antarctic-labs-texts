Comprehensive evaluation of triglyceride glucose index–a body shape index (TyG–ABSI) for incident peripheral artery disease: data-driven phenotyping and machine learning-based risk prediction in the UK Biobank
Authors: Wenxin Zhao, Xiaolu Li, Xihao Zhang, Yaming Guo, Bowen Zhang, Yifan Cao, Yongpeng Diao, Zhiyuan Wu, Chengran Lu, Zuoguan Chen, Yong Lan, Yongjun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe prevalence of Peripheral Artery Disease (PAD) is rising globally, yet early risk stratification remains challenging due to the limitations of traditional obesity metrics. TyG–ABSI, an index combining Triglyceride-Glucose (TyG) with A Body Shape Index (ABSI), is a novel marker reflecting both functional insulin resistance and structural visceral adiposity. However, its predictive value for PAD remains unexplored in large prospective cohorts.MethodsWe included 390,274 adults from the UK Biobank. Baseline characteristics were analyzed across TyG–ABSI quartiles and PAD status. Associations between TyG-related indices and incident PAD were assessed using multivariable-adjusted Cox regression, Kaplan–Meier survival curves, and restricted cubic splines. Robustness was evaluated via Fine-Gray competing risk models, propensity score matching, subgroup analyses, and external validation in the NHANES database. Consensus k-means clustering, integrating biochemical and insulin resistance markers, identified metabolic phenotypes and stratified PAD risk. Feature selection (LASSO, Boruta, and Minimum Redundancy Maximum Relevance [mRMR]) guided the development of six machine learning models (logistic regression, GBM, XGBoost, AdaBoost, LightGBM, and neural network) for PAD prediction, with interpretability assessed via SHAP analysis.ResultsHigher TyG–ABSI and related indices were strongly associated with increased PAD incidence (cumulative incidence at 15 years: 4.16% in the top quartile vs. 0.98% in the bottom quartile; fully-adjusted Hazard Ratio [HR] per 1-SD increase for TyG–ABSI: 1.22, 95% Confidence Interval [CI] 1.17–1.27), which were robust in the NHANES external validation cohort. Clustering analysis revealed four distinct metabolic subgroups, with the highest PAD risk in the insulin resistance/glucose dysfunction cluster (HR vs. healthy phenotype: 7.48, 95% CI 6.82–8.21). Feature selection identified 19 key predictors. Logistic regression provided the most stable and generalizable prediction (validation Area Under the Curve [AUC] = 0.788, 95% CI 0.778–0.798), demonstrating superior generalizability compared to complex ensemble methods. SHAP analysis demonstrated TyG–ABSI, age, and neutrophil count as leading predictors for incident PAD and confirmed the interpretability of the model.ConclusionTyG–ABSI is a robust, independent predictor of long-term PAD risk. Data-driven phenotyping and interpretable machine learning facilitate more precise risk stratification. Logistic regression offers optimal performance and interpretability, holding potential clinical utility for individualized PAD risk prediction.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-026-03116-0.Keywords: Insulin resistance, Peripheral artery disease, Triglyceride glucose–a body shape index, Cluster analysis, Machine learning

Introduction

Peripheral artery disease (PAD), characterized by arterial occlusion in the lower extremities, affects over 230 million individuals worldwide [1, 2]. Due to its significant morbidity and mortality, PAD represents a critical public health concern [3, 4]. As the global population ages and the prevalence of metabolic disorders such as diabetes, obesity, and dyslipidemia continue to rise, the burden of PAD is expected to increase substantially [5, 6]. This underscores the urgent need for novel and reliable predictors of vascular risk. While conventional risk factors remain important, emerging metabolic biomarkers, particularly those indicating obesity and insulin resistance (IR), may enhance early risk stratification and enable more targeted preventive strategies for PAD [7].

Metabolic dysfunction, particularly IR, defined as reduced insulin responsiveness in peripheral tissues, is a key pathogenic mechanism driving vascular disease and cardiovascular risk [8, 9]. Unlike complex or invasive assessment methods, the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index has emerged as a simple, non-invasive, and reliable surrogate for IR that is not influenced by exogenous insulin therapy [10, 11]. Substantial epidemiological and clinical evidence supports the association between elevated TyG index and increased risk of coronary artery disease, stroke, and other cardiovascular events [12–14]. However, relying solely on biochemical markers often overlooks the critical contribution of body composition and fat distribution to metabolic risk [15, 16].

To address this, composite indices integrating TyG with anthropometric measurements, such as body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (WC), and waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), were developed, demonstrating enhanced predictive value for cardiovascular and cerebrovascular outcomes [17, 18]. However, their specific utility in PAD requires distinct consideration. Although PAD and coronary artery disease (CAD) share common risk factors, their metabolic landscapes differ significantly. Unlike CAD, where generalized obesity is often a linear risk driver, PAD is frequently complicated by the “obesity paradox” and sarcopenia, rendering traditional weight-based metrics, like BMI, less reliable indicators of risk [19]. In this context, simple lipid profiles or waist circumference measures fail to fully capture the “adiposopathy”, or visceral adipose dysfunction, specific to PAD. The novel composite index TyG–ABSI bridges this gap by integrating the functional state of insulin resistance (TyG) with A Body Shape Index (ABSI), a metric that isolates visceral adiposity independent of BMI [20–22]. By decoupling central fat from general body mass, TyG–ABSI uniquely captures the high-risk phenotype of “visceral lipotoxicity with insulin resistance” that neither TyG alone nor traditional anthropometry can fully identify [23, 24].

Despite the potential of TyG–ABSI, its predictive value for incident PAD remains unexplored in large prospective cohorts. Therefore, our study leveraged the large-scale UK Biobank prospective cohort to investigate the association between TyG–ABSI and incident PAD. Furthermore, we employed machine learning techniques to establish a predictive model, aiming to facilitate early identification of individuals at elevated PAD risk. Our findings may provide new insights and improve risk assessment strategies for PAD.

Methods

Study population

This study utilized data from the UK Biobank, one of the largest prospective epidemiological cohorts globally. Between 2006 and 2010, the UK Biobank recruited over 500,000 adults aged 37 to 73 years from 22 assessment centers across England, Scotland, and Wales. At baseline, participants completed electronic questionnaires and face-to-face interviews, and underwent physical measurements. Comprehensive demographic, lifestyle, and health-related information was systematically collected, and biological samples were obtained. Detailed study design and data collection procedures have been described previously [25, 26]. The UK Biobank received ethical approval from the North West Multi-Center Research Ethics Committee (reference number 21/NW/0157), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

To establish the final analytic cohort, we applied the following exclusion criteria: (1) individuals with prevalent PAD at baseline or with unknown date of PAD diagnosis; (2) individuals with missing data on key variables necessary for the calculation of TyG-related indices, including BMI, WC, body weight, sex, triglycerides, C-reactive protein (CRP), plasma glucose, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), and total cholesterol (TC); and (3) individuals lacking follow-up information. Definition of PAD

Incident PAD after recruitment was identified by the presence of at least one of the following disease codes: self-reported history of operation or illness, Office of Population Censuses and Surveys Classification of Surgical Operations and Procedures version 4 (OPCS-4), or International Classification of Diseases (ICD-9/10) codes. Detailed codes and criteria for PAD identification can be found in Table S1. The follow-up period for each participant commenced at the date of recruitment and concluded at the earliest of the following events: loss to follow-up, death, diagnosis of PAD, or the end of the study (October 25, 2022). Individuals with prevalent PAD at baseline, or with unknown date of diagnosis, were excluded from incident analysis. Loss to follow-up was defined as the last contact date prior to study end in the absence of a PAD diagnosis or death.

Definition of TyG-related indices

The TyG-related indicators (TyG–ABSI [27], TyG–LAP [28], TyG–VAI [29]) are a novel class of biomarkers that integrate the TyG index with various anthropometric parameters, thereby enhancing the accuracy of metabolic dysfunction and visceral adiposity assessment [24, 30]. To comprehensively evaluate their predictive advantages, we additionally incorporated several established indices relevant to insulin resistance, including the Metabolic Score for Insulin Resistance (Mets-IR) [31], Triglyceride-to-HDL cholesterol ratio (THR) [32], C-reactive protein-triglyceride-glucose index (CTI) [33], and the Cholesterol-to-HDL ratio (CHG) [34], for comparative analysis. The calculation formulas for these indicators are provided in the Supplementary Materials. Consistent with previous literature [35–37], these indices were treated as dimensionless continuous variable for analysis.

Measures of covariates

Based on previous studies, we included the following covariates in our analysis: age, sex, Townsend deprivation index, smoking status, alcohol consumption, comorbidities and medication use (ascertained via hospital episode statistics, primary care records, and/or self-report); basic anthropometric measurements (height, weight, BMI, WC, and hip circumference); lifestyle factors (physical activity and a diet quality score constructed according to cardiometabolic health dietary recommendations) [38]; and comprehensive laboratory parameters, including complete blood counts, liver and renal function tests, blood lipids, glycemic indices, and inflammatory markers. A detailed list of all covariates and their definitions is provided in Table S2. The calculation method and components of the dietary score are described in Table S3.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), according to variable distribution. Normality was assessed via the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and evaluation of skewness and kurtosis. Group comparisons employed independent samples t-test or one-way ANOVA for normally distributed data, and Mann–Whitney U test for non-normal data. Categorical data were expressed as counts (percentages) and compared using the chi-square test. Missing data in all covariates were less than 20%; details are presented in Table S2. The pattern of missingness was examined, and random forest-based imputation (MissForest) was applied before analysis.

Primary association analyses

Kaplan–Meier curves visualized cumulative PAD incidence across quartiles of TyG–ABSI and related indices, with the log-rank test for statistical differences. Multivariate Cox regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for associations between TyG-related indices and incident PAD, with adjustment for confounders. Four Cox regression models with progressively increasing covariate adjustment were constructed: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for sociodemographic factors (age, sex, deprivation index, smoking status, and alcohol consumption); Model 3 further adjusted for lifestyle and anthropometric variables (physical activity, dietary score, BMI, hip and waist circumference); and Model 4 additionally adjusted for major comorbidities (including COPD, hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, chronic renal failure, among others).The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using both Schoenfeld residual plots and formal tests. In line with current guidelines [39, 40], HRs were interpreted as time-averaged effects. The discriminatory and predictive performance of TyG–ABSI versus other indices was compared using ROC analysis (AUC), with DeLong’s test applied to assess paired AUC differences. Additional measures of model performance including Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) and Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI), were also calculated.

Propensity score matching (PSM) analyses

To further verify the robustness of our findings, we performed PSM. PSM was performed using 1:2 nearest-neighbor matching without replacement, to balance baseline variables (age, sex, Townsend deprivation index, smoking, drinking, moderate physical activity, vigorous physical activity, diet quality score, diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, stroke, chronic renal failure, COPD, asthma, psoriatic arthropathies, IBD, RA, and medication use) between PAD and non-PAD groups at a caliper width of 0.2. Covariate balance was evaluated using standardized mean differences (SMDs) before and after matching.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate the consistency of the association between TyG-related indices and PAD risk across different clinical strata, including sex, age groups (40–49, 50–59, 60–69, and 70–74 years), comorbidities, and medication use. Interaction terms were tested for potential effect modification among subgroups.

Sensitivity analyses

To further examine the robustness and generalizability of our results, we conducted multiple sensitivity analyses. We first employed Fine and Gray’s competing risk models to account for death as a competing risk for incident PAD. To assess the robustness of our findings, a sensitivity analysis was conducted using a stricter definition of PAD, strictly limited to diagnosis and procedure codes specific to lower-extremity disease. The detailed code list is provided in Table S1. For internal validation, we restricted the analysis to a subgroup of participants who attended the imaging assessment visit (2014 onwards, n = 72,387). Cox proportional hazards models were repeated in this subset to verify the consistency of the associations. To validate the external applicability of our findings, we analyzed data from 7571 participants in the NHANES 1999–2004 cohort. Multivariable logistic regression was used to assess the association between TyG-related indices and prevalent PAD (ABI < 0.90), adjusting for a harmonized set of covariates including age, sex, race, smoking, diabetes, and BMI.

Clustering analysis

To investigate metabolic heterogeneity in relation to PAD risk, we applied a two-stage clustering approach. Continuous variables were standardized prior to analysis. First, consensus clustering with the k-means was performed on 2000 randomly selected subsamples to determine the optimal number of clusters, guided by the consensus matrix heatmap, cumulative distribution function (CDF) plot, and consensus scores [41]. Cluster stability was quantified by consensus scores (> 0.7 indicated high stability). Patients were then assigned to clusters using k-means with the selected cluster number. Clustering robustness was assessed via 100 bootstrap replications, with the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) calculated for each; an ARI > 0.90 indicated stable solutions [42, 43]. Cluster characteristics were summarized using radar plots, where each axis represents a standardized metabolic variable and the distance from the center reflects the mean value of that variable in each cluster. The grid lines correspond to increments in z-score, facilitating intuitive comparison of metabolic profiles across groups. Separation between clusters was further quantified by calculating pairwise standardized mean differences (SMDs) for all primary metabolic variables. To assess differences in PAD risk among clusters, Kaplan–Meier curves for cumulative incidence plots were generated, with statistical differences calculated by the log-rank test and pairwise hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) estimated using Cox regression. Sensitivity analyses were conducted excluding TyG–ABSI from clustering inputs to address potential circularity (see Supplementary Methods).

Predictive feature selection and model development

To further clarify the diagnostic value of TyG–ABSI for PAD, several machine learning models incorporating TyG–ABSI were developed and compared. This allowed evaluation of predictor robustness and importance across different modeling frameworks. We implemented a multi-method feature selection strategy. First, the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was utilized to identify variables with strong predictive value [44]. Next, minimum redundancy maximum relevance (mRMR) analysis was performed to select variables that demonstrated maximum relevance to PAD incidence while minimizing redundancy among themselves [45]. Additionally, the Boruta algorithm, a random forest-based wrapper method, was employed to evaluate and rank the global importance of all candidate features [46]. Only features consistently selected by all three methods were included for model development. A detailed overview of each feature selection algorithm is provided in Table S4. All feature selection procedures (LASSO, mRMR, and Boruta) were performed only within the training set. The independent test set was not involved in any step of feature selection or model training to strictly avoid information leakage and ensure unbiased model evaluation.

Based on the feature selection results, six different TyG–ABSI -based risk prediction models for PAD were constructed using machine learning algorithms: logistic regression [47], gradient boosting machine (GBM) [48, 49], neural network [50, 51], XGBoost [52], AdaBoost [53], and LightGBM [54]. For details, see Table S5. The dataset was randomly divided into a training set (70%) and a validation set (30%). To address the class imbalance in PAD outcomes, the training set was further processed using the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE). The validation set retained the original class distribution to ensure unbiased performance evaluation. Hyperparameter optimization for each algorithm was performed through grid search and five-fold cross-validation, with details provided in the Table S6.

Model evaluation and interpretability

The primary metric for model discrimination was the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), consistent with current recommendations for medical AI model evaluation [55]. The model exhibiting the highest AUC in the validation cohort was selected as the final prioritized model. To provide a comprehensive and clinically meaningful evaluation, model calibration was assessed using the Brier score and calibration slope, and clinical utility was examined via decision curve analysis. Additionally, accuracy, recall, F1 score, G-mean, specificity, and confusion matrices were evaluated in validation cohorts to further assess model performance, particularly under class-imbalance conditions. Model interpretability was assessed using SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanation) analysis. SHAP values were calculated for each feature to quantify its relative contribution to model predictions and to visualize the impact of key variables, notably TyG–ABSI, on PAD risk estimation.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of 390,274 participants stratified by TyG–ABSI quartiles. Higher TyG–ABSI values were associated with older age, male sex, increased smoking and alcohol use, and greater social deprivation (all P < 0.001). Anthropometric indices, including BMI, waist circumference, and hip circumference, progressively increased with higher TyG–ABSI quartiles (all P < 0.001). Conversely, moderate and vigorous physical activity, as well as diet quality scores, declined across quartiles (all P < 0.001). Comorbidities showed stepwise increases across TyG–ABSI quartiles (all P < 0.001 for trend). In particular, hypertension prevalence rose from 15.1% in the lowest to 37.9% in the highest quartile, and diabetes from 0.7% to 6.8%. Laboratory findings indicated that elevated TyG–ABSI correlated with increased counts of red blood cells, hemoglobin, and platelets. Notably, markers of systemic inflammation, including white blood cell counts, neutrophil counts, and C-reactive protein (CRP), were all significantly elevated in the higher quartiles, reflecting a heightened inflammatory profile at baseline. Adverse metabolic trends were observed, with higher levels of plasma glucose, HbA1c, triglycerides, LDL-cholesterol, creatinine, uric acid, and liver enzymes, alongside lower HDL-cholesterol (all P < 0.001). Indices of insulin resistance (TyG, TyG–VAI, TyG–LAP, CTI, METS-IR) also increased steadily with TyG–ABSI. Incidence of PAD and all-cause mortality were higher in the upper quartiles. Additional characteristics by TyG and its derivatives are detailed in Tables S7–S9, and sex-specific distributions are shown in Fig. S1.

Baseline characteristics of participants according to TyG–ABSI index quartiles

Table 2 shows baseline characteristics according to PAD status. Individuals with PAD were older, more often male, reported higher rates of smoking and alcohol consumption and exhibited greater social deprivation, as indicated by a higher Townsend deprivation index (all P < 0.001). They also had higher BMI, waist and hip circumferences, and a greater burden of hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, stroke, and chronic renal failure, with more extensive use of cardiometabolic medications. Regarding lifestyle factors, PAD cases engaged in less vigorous physical activity (1.73 vs. 1.89 days/week; P < 0.001), and had lower diet quality scores (3.03 vs. 3.25; P < 0.001). PAD cases also displayed elevated white blood cells, neutrophils, red blood cells, hemoglobin, and CRP levels as well as slight reductions in albumin and platelet counts, indicative of increased inflammation. Metabolically, PAD was associated with higher creatinine, plasma glucose, HbA1c, and triglyceride levels, reduced HDL-cholesterol, and modestly lower total and LDL-cholesterol, along with mild elevations in liver enzymes. All markers of insulin resistance were significantly higher in PAD cases (all P < 0.001).

Associations of TyG-related indices with incident PAD

During a median follow-up of 13.25 years (IQR: 12.50–13.99), a total of 6212 incident PAD events were recorded. Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated a significant, stepwise increase in cumulative PAD incidence across quartiles of TyG and its related indices (all log-rank P < 0.0001; Fig. 1). Notably, the risk was most pronounced for TyG–ABSI, where participants in the highest quartile had a fourfold higher cumulative incidence at 15 years compared to the lowest quartile (4.16% vs. 0.98%, P < 0.001; Fig. 2B, Table S10).

To further compare the predictive performance of each index for incident PAD, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was conducted. As shown in Fig. 3, TyG–ABSI demonstrated the highest discriminatory ability for PAD diagnosis, with an AUC of 0.664 (95% CI 0.657–0.671). In contrast, traditional TyG index alone yielded a lower AUC of 0.607 (95% CI 0.600–0.614), while TyG–LAP (AUC: 0.616, 95% CI 0.609–0.623) and Mets-IR (AUC: 0.614, 95% CI 0.606–0.621) also showed moderate discrimination. Other composite indices such as TyG–VAI (AUC: 0.599, 95% CI 0.592–0.606), CTI (AUC: 0.642, 95% CI 0.635–0.649), THR (AUC: 0.611, 95% CI 0.604–0.618) and CHG (AUC: 0.583, 95% CI 0.576–0.591) exhibited lower predictive performance compared to TyG–ABSI. Pairwise DeLong’s tests confirmed that the differences in AUCs between TyG–ABSI and each comparator were all statistically significant (all P < 0.001; Fig. 3, Table S11). However, pairwise Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) and Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI) analyses did not reach statistical significance (all P > 0.05; Table S12, Fig. 3). Overall, these results indicate that TyG–ABSI provides superior predictive value for incident PAD among all assessed indices, supporting its use as a robust risk stratification tool in this population.

In the multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses (Table 3), higher values of TyG-related indices demonstrated a consistent and independent association with increased risk of PAD. In the fully adjusted model, individuals in the highest quartile of the TyG index exhibited a 16.4% higher risk of developing PAD compared to those in the lowest quartile (HR = 1.164, 95% CI 1.075–1.261; P < 0.001). Among all evaluated indices, the TyG–ABSI showed the strongest association with incident PAD, with a hazard ratio of 1.343 (95% CI 1.190–1.515; P < 0.001) when comparing the highest versus lowest quartile. Furthermore, when analyzed as a continuous variable, each standard deviation increase in TyG–ABSI was associated with a 21.9% higher risk of PAD (HR = 1.219, 95% CI 1.171–1.270; P < 0.001). Similar patterns were observed for TyG–VAI and TyG–LAP, indicating a consistent relationship across different TyG-related adiposity indices. The proportional hazards assumption for TyG–ABSI and clusters was evaluated using Schoenfeld residual plots (Figs. S2–S3, Table S13); although the global test was significant, the plots did not indicate notable time-dependent violations, supporting the interpretation of HRs as time-averaged effects.

Dose–response relationships of TyG-related indices and PAD risk

The dose–response relationships between TyG-related indicators and the risk of PAD are presented in Fig. 4. Restricted cubic spline analyses demonstrated significant associations for all four indicators (all overall P values < 0.001). In the unadjusted model, TyG–VAI, TyG–LAP, and TyG exhibited clear non-linear dose–response patterns (P for non-linearity < 0.001), while TyG–ABSI showed an approximately linear relationship. With adjustment for demographic and anthropometric variables, all indices remained significantly associated with PAD risk, and the non-linear patterns generally persisted except for TyG–VAI, which became linear (P for non-linearity = 0.89). Further adjustment for lifestyle factorsresulted in most indices exhibiting significant non-linearity, except for TyG–VAI (P for non-linearity = 0.72). In the fully adjusted models, only the TyG index exhibited a significant non-linear association (P for non-linearity < 0.001), while TyG–ABSI (P = 0.08), TyG–VAI (P = 0.37), and TyG–LAP (P = 0.11) demonstrated approximately linear relationships. Overall, higher levels of all TyG-related indices were robustly associated with increased risk of PAD, and substantial threshold effects were primarily observed for the TyG index.

Subgroup analysis

The subgroup analysis demonstrated that the association between TyG-related indices and the risk of adverse outcomes was generally robust and consistent across various clinical subgroups, including sex, age, comorbidities, and medication use (Figs. S4–S6). As shown in Fig. 5, the association between TyG–ABSI (per SD increase) and the risk of adverse outcomes remained robust and consistent across a variety of clinical subgroups, including sex, age, comorbidities, and medication use. Statistically significant interactions were observed for sex (P for interaction = 0.009), hypertension status (P for interaction = 0.013), cardiovascular disease (P for interaction < 0.001), cholesterol-lowering medication use (P for interaction = 0.004), blood pressure medication use (P for interaction = 0.020), and insulin medication use (P for interaction = 0.007).

Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are shown for each subgroup, including sex, age group, diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease (CVD), stroke, chronic renal failure, and medication use (cholesterol-lowering, blood pressure-lowering, and insulin therapy). P values for interaction were calculated to assess effect modification across subgroups. The forest plot summarizes risk estimates for each category

Notably, the association between TyG–ABSI and risk was stronger among females (HR = 1.43, 95% CI 1.37–1.50) than males (HR = 1.33, 95% CI 1.28–1.38), and most pronounced in the middle-aged groups (e.g., HR = 1.52 [1.38–1.68] for age 40–49 and HR = 1.60 [1.52–1.69] for age 50–59). The effect size was attenuated, but remained significant, among participants with hypertension, cardiovascular disease, or those taking cholesterol-lowering, blood pressure, or insulin medications. Despite some differences in the magnitude of association, the overall direction of effect persisted in all assessed subgroups, supporting the robustness and generalizability of TyG–ABSI as a risk predictor in diverse clinical populations.

Senstivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness and generalizability of the association between TyG-related indices and incident PAD across multiple methodological approaches. Results from Fine–Gray competing risk models were highly consistent with the Cox models. In the fully adjusted analysis, individuals in the highest TyG–ABSI quartile had a significantly increased risk of incident PAD (subdistribution HR = 1.383, 95% CI 1.222–1.566; P < 0.001), consistent with primary Cox regression findings. Similar results were observed for other TyG-related indices (Table 5). When a stricter definition of PAD was used, the association between TyG–ABSI and incident PAD became even stronger (Tables S15–S16). The robustness of our findings was further supported by internal validation in the UK Biobank imaging cohort and external validation in the NHANES database, both of which showed consistent effect sizes and statistically significant associations (Tables S17–S18, Fig. S7). Importantly, across all sensitivity analyses and validation cohorts, the risk of developing PAD in individuals in the highest TyG–ABSI quartile (Q4) was consistently greater than that in the lowest-risk group (Q1), with HRs or ORs persistently above 1.0. Although some of these associations did not reach statistical significance after full adjustment, the directionality of the association remained unchanged in every analysis, further supporting the robustness and consistency of our findings across different definitions, adjustment models, and populations.

Cluster analysis

As shown in Fig. 6E–F, the optimal number of clusters (K = 4) was determined by the elbow plot, cumulative distribution function (CDF), and the relative increase in the area under the CDF curve. Clustering stability was supported by a high adjusted Rand index (ARI = 0.93) and average consensus scores above 0.70 for all clusters (Fig. 6G). Further details regarding clustering methodology and robustness are provided in Fig. S8. Four distinct metabolic phenotypes were identified, each with unique profiles (radar charts, Fig. 6A–D): (A) Isolated Dyslipidemia group exhibited elevated lipid parameters but normal glucose and insulin resistance indices; (B) Metabolically Healthy group had all markers within healthy ranges; (C) Dyslipidemia with Insulin Resistance group showed simultaneous increases in lipid and insulin resistance markers; (D) Insulin Resistance/Glucose Dysfunction group displayed pronounced insulin resistance and glucose abnormalities with less marked dyslipidemia. These robust, phenotypically distinct clusters provide a foundation for subsequent analysis of PAD risks among different metabolic subgroups. Pairwise SMDs comparing clusters frequently exceeded 0.5, supporting the strong statistical and clinical distinction between cluster groups (detailed in Tables S19–S22). Notably, PAD incidence varied significantly across these metabolic phenotypes, with the highest risk observed in the group characterized by insulin resistance combined with impaired glucose metabolism, emphasizing the heterogeneity in PAD risk among different metabolic subgroups.

Feature selection

To construct a parsimonious and robust prediction model for incident PAD, based on TyG–ABSI, we employed multiple feature selection methods to systematically assess the importance of candidate variables. As shown in Fig. 8, LASSO regression identified 26 variables with high predictive importance, prominently featuring TyG–ABSI, cardiovascular risk markers, and key biochemical indices. Additional details of the LASSO regression procedure are provided in Fig. S11. Pearson correlation analysis revealed that HbA1c, CVD, cholesterol, and blood pressure were most correlated with new PAD events. Boruta algorithm further ranked feature importance, confirming several metabolic and inflammatory markers. Integration of the three selection methods identified a consensus set of 19 variables (Fig. 8), including glucose metabolism indices, lipid markers, blood pressure, and select hematologic parameters, as the predictive feature set. These selected features were used for subsequent model development.

Model construction and evaluation

The indicators related to insulin resistance demonstrated potential to assist in the early diagnosis and risk stratification of PAD. Therefore, we built PAD risk prediction models based on TyG-related indicators using multiple machine learning methods. Based on the selected features, we constructed and validated multiple predictive models for incident PAD, employing a 70:30 split for training and validation cohorts. As shown in Fig. 9, six machine learning models were compared: logistic regression, gradient boosting machine (GBM), neural network, XGBoost, AdaBoost, and LightGBM. In the training set, LightGBM achieved the highest AUC (0.856, 95% CI 0.852–0.860). However, logistic regression demonstrated consistently robust performance, with an AUC of 0.799 (95% CI 0.795–0.804) and competitive accuracy, recall, F1 score, and specificity values. Furthermore, the relative contribution of individual predictors to model performance is visualized through feature importance rankings for each machine learning algorithm, as shown in Figs. S12–S17. Notably, TyG–ABSI consistently ranked among the top most important features across all models, further supporting its robustness and strong association with incident PAD.

B Bar plot and table summarizing performance metrics (AUC, accuracy, specificity, precision, recall, F1 score, G-mean) for each model in the training set. Logistic regression demonstrated the highest generalizability and overall performance for predicting incident PAD across both cohorts

In the test set, logistic regression outperformed other models with an AUC of 0.788 (95% CI 0.778–0.798) and higher specificity (0.731), as well as superior accuracy (0.707), precision (0.994), F1 score (0.826), and G-mean (0.719) metrics, indicating excellent discrimination in new data. Ensemble models such as GBM, XGBoost, and LightGBM showed a decline in performance on the validation set (AUC = 0.778, 0.770, and 0.758, respectively). Overall, logistic regression provided the more stable and generalizable prediction for incident PAD across both training and validation cohorts. Given the class imbalance, confusion matrices (Figs. S18–S23), were generated for all models, which facilitated a transparent and interpretable assessment of performance across different outcome categories. However, calibration curves revealed suboptimal agreement for most models. Brier scores ranged from 0.085 to 0.117, and net benefit analysis indicated limited clinical utility (Figs. S24–S27). These findings may in part be attributable to the pronounced class imbalance in the dataset, which poses challenges for risk calibration despite good discrimination performance.

Model interpretability with SHAP

Furthermore, we applied SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanation) analysis to evaluate the predictive ability of TyG–ABSI for new-onset PAD. SHAP results consistently identified TyG–ABSI as one of the top contributing features, ranking just after age. The analyses demonstrated a clear positive association between TyG–ABSI levels and predicted PAD risk. SHAP dependence and scatter plots revealed that higher TyG–ABSI values strongly increased model risk estimates. At the individual level, the waterfall plot highlighted TyG–ABSI as a major contributor to predicted outcomes. Overall, SHAP analysis offered transparent and intuitive insights, further confirming the value of TyG–ABSI in predicting new-onset PAD (Fig. S28).

Discussion

This study is the first to systematically evaluate the predictive value of TyG–ABSI for incident PAD in the large-scale, prospective UK Biobank cohort (n = 390,274), directly comparing it with established TyG-based insulin resistance indices. Our findings are fourfold: (1) higher TyG–ABSI levels were robustly associated with elevated PAD risk, independent of demographics, comorbidities, and medications, and each unit increase corresponding to an 21.9% higher risk. This association persisted across dose–response strata, propensity score matched, and multiple subgroup analyses; (2) TyG–ABSI outperformed other insulin resistance indices in PAD prediction according to ROC analysis; (3) unsupervised clustering identified distinct metabolic phenotypes, with the highest PAD risk observed in those with pronounced insulin resistance and glucose dysfunction (HR vs. healthy: 7.48); (4) prediction models indicated that logistic regression and machine learning performed similarly, with TyG–ABSI identified as a key predictor after age. Taken together, these results indicate that novel TyG-related indices, particularly TyG–ABSI, may serve as promising biomarkers for PAD risk identification and prediction in clinical settings.

Despite evidence from previous large-scale studies indicating the predictive utility of insulin resistance markers such as the TyG index for cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease, data on PAD remain scarce, particularly in population-based prospective cohorts [56–58]. This is a critical oversight, as approximately 32% to 54% of PAD patients do not have concomitant CAD or cerebrovascular disease, emphasizing the distinct clinical and pathophysiological features of PAD. Given the strong link between PAD and metabolic disturbances, identifying specific risk indicators for PAD is especially important. Previous studies, such as those by Gao et al., demonstrated that higher TyG index trajectories are independently associated with increased PAD risk [59]. Similarly, a meta-analysis by Samavarchitehrani et al. confirmed a significant relationship between glycated hemoglobin levels and both the incidence and severity of PAD [60]. Building on this, our study, the largest cohort to date incorporating comprehensive metabolic and vascular data, not only confirms but also extends current evidence. We evaluated the utility of TyG in combination with body composition indices and applied unsupervised clustering to delineate the metabolic phenotypes of newly diagnosed PAD patients. These approaches help bridge important gaps in the understanding of PAD risk profiles.

The biological plausibility of our findings is grounded in the synergy between insulin resistance and visceral adiposity [61]. While direct measurement techniques like the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp are the gold standard for IR, they are invasive and infeasible for large-scale population studies [11, 35, 59]. The TyG index serves as a reliable surrogate but fails to account for obesity phenotypes [62]. Elevated BMI, particularly increased fat mass index, is associated with greater risk of cardiovascular disease, likely because obesity exacerbates insulin resistance through mechanisms such as oxidative stress and inflammation [63, 64]. By integrating TyG with ABSI, which specifically targets visceral adiposity, TyG–ABSI captures a more comprehensive picture of “metabolic health” than either metric alone. This combination likely explains its superior predictive value observed in our study, consistent with similar findings in cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease cohorts [65, 66].

The observed dose–response patterns provide critical insights for clinical risk stratification. Mechanistically, the non-linear associations observed in certain indices are biologically plausible, reflecting a threshold effect of glucolipotoxicity. Vascular injury often accelerates disproportionately once insulin resistance and ectopic lipid deposition surpass the body’s compensatory capacity, creating a metabolic “tipping point” [67]. Clinically, the identification of inflection points (TyG–ABSI ≈ 0.7) supports the use of evidence-based cut-offs to identify high-risk populations for intensive screening. Conversely, the linear relationship observed for TyG–ABSI after full adjustment suggests that the deleterious impact of combined visceral adiposity and insulin resistance is continuous. This reinforces a “lower is better” paradigm for prevention, implying that lifestyle interventions to reduce TyG–ABSI may confer vascular benefits even in individuals below traditional high-risk thresholds [68].

Our subgroup analyses revealed that while the association between TyG–ABSI and PAD risk was consistent across a broad range of clinical contexts, the relative risk was highest among younger adults (ages 40–59) and in male participants. These findings suggest that metabolic disturbances may exert a disproportionate effect on PAD risk earlier in life, likely reflecting the cumulative burden of long-term metabolic derangement and a lower prevalence of competing risk factors in younger individuals. This aligns with evidence from the UKPDS 92 study, which demonstrated that patients diagnosed with type 2 diabetes before age 40 exhibit higher rates of obesity and insulin resistance, as well as a substantially greater relative risk of mortality (272% vs 54% increase) compared to those diagnosed at older ages [69, 70]. This underscores the importance of early identification and targeted intervention for metabolic dysfunction in younger populations.

We also observed a “treatment paradox” where the association between TyG–ABSI and incident PAD was stronger in participants without a history of cardiovascular disease (CVD) and those not taking metabolic medications (cholesterol-lowering, antihypertensive, or insulin therapies). This likely reflects pharmacological confounding: in treated populations, biomarkers are therapeutically altered, partially decoupling the calculated index from the underlying pathology [71]. Furthermore, aggressive preventive interventions in these high-risk groups stabilize vascular risk, thereby attenuating the relative predictive magnitude of metabolic indices compared to untreated populations where metabolic toxicity serves as a dominant, unmasked driver of atherosclerosis [19, 71].

To our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically delineate the glycolipid metabolic heterogeneity of PAD patients by integrating unsupervised consensus clustering. We identified four robust metabolic phenotypes, with the “insulin resistance/glucose dysfunction” group exhibited the highest PAD risk (HR: 7.48), far exceeding that of isolated dyslipidemia. These results underscore a critical shift in understanding PAD pathogenesis: while traditional lipid management remains important, metabolic heterogeneity driven by insulin resistance represents a more potent, distinct risk factor that requires targeted identification. Building on this, our risk prediction models integrated TyG–ABSI, age, neutrophil count, and others as critical biological predictors, corroborating the metabolic relevance highlighted by our clustering results. Regarding model performance. While ensemble machine learning methods, such as GBM, XGBoost, and LightGBM, showed high training performance, logistic regression demonstrated superior generalizability and interpretability in the validation set. SHAP analysis visually confirmed the dominant contribution of TyG–ABSI to PAD risk. The framework combines biological nuance with interpretable modeling, laying a solid foundation for precision prevention.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, although we validated our findings using the NHANES database to enhance generalizability, the primary discovery cohort (UK Biobank) is predominantly of European descent and subject to “healthy volunteer” bias. Consequently, while the consistency observed across these cohorts supports the robustness of TyG–ABSI, further verification in prospective studies involving broader ethnic diversity, such as in East Asian or African populations, remains desirable to fully establish global applicability. Second, the lack of survival analysis represents an important limitation. We were unable to assess the impact of TyG–ABSI on long-term prognosis or mortality specifically in PAD patients; future studies should incorporate outcome data to provide a more comprehensive evaluation. Third, although our feature selection strategy integrated multiple robust techniques, residual confounding from unmeasured variables cannot be fully excluded. Methodologically, calibration and decision analyses revealed limited clinical utility across all models, which may be primarily attributed to substantial class imbalance. Therefore, even though TyG–ABSI demonstrated robust importance across models, the predictive results should be interpreted with caution in clinical translation. Furthermore, while machine learning methods are effective at capturing complex interactions among predictors, challenges remain regarding their interpretability and stability during external validation. Future research focusing on explainable artificial intelligence and hybrid modeling approaches may further enhance the clinical applicability of predictive analytics in vascular disease. Fourth, the use of non-fasting blood samples for calculating TyG-related indices deviates from the original definition, potentially introducing measurement error. However, non-fasting biomarkers capture clinically relevant postprandial metabolic stress, and any resulting non-differential misclassification would likely attenuate rather than exaggerate the observed associations, suggesting our findings remain robust. Despite these limitations, the innovative methodology and robustness of our study offer valuable insights for future research in this field.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the innovative value of TyG–ABSI as a predictor for PAD incidence, with superior performance compared to other markers of insulin resistance. Individuals with elevated TyG–ABSI levels were shown to be at higher risk for developing PAD. Cluster analysis further elucidated distinct metabolic profiles associated with PAD risk. Integrating TyG–ABSI into machine learning models notably enhanced risk stratification accuracy, offering a promising approach for the early identification of high-risk individuals and enabling more timely and targeted intervention strategies.
