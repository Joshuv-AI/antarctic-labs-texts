Unveiling the Genomic Landscape of Escherichia coli O1:K1:H7 ST59 in Non-complicated Urinary Infections from Colombia Through Whole-Genome Sequencing
Authors: Andrés Felipe Cuspoca Orduz, Hernán David Roldán Hernandeza, Ravneet Kaur Grewal, Manik Prabhu Narsing Rao, Nury Nathalia Olaya-Galán, Yardani Rafael Méndez Fandiño, Luigi Cavallo, Mohit Chawla
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEscherichia coli (E. coli) is a Gram-negative bacterium known for causing both intestinal and extraintestinal infections in humans. Among extraintestinal infections, urinary tract infections (UTIs) are particularly prevalent and impactful. In Colombia, limited information is available regarding the molecular epidemiology of E. coli. This lack of data hinders the understanding of the local epidemiological landscape and the identification of pathogenic lineages that may contribute to public health concerns. This study aimed to characterize an E. coli strain isolated from a 24-year-old female patient with a community-acquired lower UTI, focusing on genotypic analysis through whole-genome shotgun sequencing (WGSS) and subsequent bioinformatics investigations. The identified strain belongs to phylogroup F, with serotype O1:H7 and sequence type (ST) 59. Several virulence factors, including traT and afimbrial adhesins (afaC), were identified, with afaC being notably uncommon in ST59 phylogroup F. In addition, an antibiotic susceptibility test was performed, and the isolate was found to be sensitive to all the antibiotics tested. This work contributes to the understanding of E. coli phylogroup F in Colombia and provides valuable genomic data, shedding light on the virulence profile of this strain in lower urinary tract infections.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00284-025-04500-x.

Introduction

Escherichia coli (E. coli) is a Gram-negative bacterium of the Enterobacteriaceae family, and it is part of the human microbiota as well as that of other healthy mammals [1]. It is considered an opportunistic pathogen due to its diverse virulence factors (VF) and association with multiple pathotypes [2]. Among the gastrointestinal isolates, the most relevant pathotypes of E. coli include: enteropathogenic E. coli (EPEC), enterotoxigenic E. coli (ETEC), and enteroaggregative E. coli (EAEC). In addition, within the extraintestinal pathogenic E. coli (ExPEC) lineage, the most relevant pathotypes are uropathogenic E. coli (UPEC), neonatal meningitis-associated E. coli (NMEC), and septicemic E. coli (SEPEC) [2]. A further important pathotype is avian pathogenic E. coli (APEC), frequently found in avian colibacillosis and is considered a potential zoonotic infection [3]. The phylogeny of E. coli correlates with its virulence and pathotypes. The Clermont method identifies eight phylogroups (A, B1, B2, C, D, E, F, and G) based on specific genetic markers [4]. Phylogroup G, an intermediate between F and B2, was later added [5]. Phylogroup F has been increasingly detected in feces, extraintestinal sites, companion and wild animals, as well as in poultry meat and environmental water sources [6]. This widespread occurrence underscores its potential involvement in human infections, warranting further emphasis on its clinical significance. In addition to phylogroups, Multilocus Sequence Typing (MLST) has been employed to genotype E. coli. This method analyzes short sequences of housekeeping genes involved in core metabolism, serving as markers of genetic divergence. Unique allelic profiles at each locus define a sequence type (ST), which, when combined with other tools, enhances E. coli classification, facilitates the identification of distinct lineages and aids in detecting potentially pathogenic strains based on their ecological niche [7].

ExPEC strains are of particular concern due to clones like ST131 and serotypes such as O15:K52, which have been identified as (potentially) pandemic lineages with global clonal dissemination and frequent carriage of resistance genes to first-line antibiotics, including beta-lactams and fluoroquinolones [8]. In addition, these strains harbor a higher number of virulence factor genes related to adhesion and invasion, particularly those encoding fimbriae associated with the classical chaperone-usher (CU) pathway [9], such as adhesins fimH (fimbria type 1) and outer membrane protein like iha (adhesin homologous to IrgA). They also carry genes related to iron acquisition genes, including iucD and iutA (aerobactin) and fyuA (yersiniabactin), as well as protectins and serum resistance genes (kpsM II, group II-specific polysaccharide capsule), ompT (outer membrane protease T), traT (complement resistance protein), and toxin-encoding genes, including sat (secreted autotransporter toxin) and usp (uropathogen-specific protein), among others. [2].

Currently, in Colombia, there are a few studies related to the molecular epidemiology of E. coli, and thus, it is unknown which circulating strains of E. coli could be linked to the community-acquired infections related to extraintestinal pathogenicity. This study aimed to isolate and characterize an E. coli strain from a community-acquired urinary infection based on the WGSS and MLST, supported by in silico and bioinformatic tools. The isolate was named “UPTC_GRECO_2” which was clustered in phylogroup F, ST59, and serotype O1:K1:H7. ExPEC biomarkers were also identified, although no VFs associated with complicated UTI were found. Comparisons with similar isolates proposed UPTC_GRECO_2 as a potential strain with significant adaptive and virulence characteristics. Recovered VFs were compared and analyzed with other strains. Alternative antibiotic therapy management was discussed, and the importance of molecular epidemiology studies was highlighted in terms of the recognition of circulating strains of ExPEC with potential pandemic or epidemic behaviors. It is to be remarked that the bacterial strain was isolated from a patient with no prior history of urinary tract infection and no apparent risk factors, who presented with acute lower urinary tract symptoms in the emergency department. In Colombia, empiric antibiotic treatment is commonly used, so we believe that characterizing this strain could offer valuable insights into optimizing antibiotic use and minimizing unnecessary prescriptions. Moreover, further investigation could help identify previously unreported sources of contamination or infection in semi-rural areas, like the one in this study, ultimately informing more effective local public health strategies.

Materials and Methods

The section has been divided into two parts: a wet lab approach and a dry lab approach. These sections are presented in Fig. 1 and are explained in the upcoming sections.

General diagram of the methodology including wet lab and dry lab approaches for the characterization of UPTC_GRECO_2 strain

Clinical Samples and Isolation

Urine samples were collected from patients over 18 years of age with suspected urinary tract infections (UTIs) between June and December 2019 in Duitama, Boyacá, Colombia. Samples were obtained from various departments of the Clínica de Boyacá, with all participants voluntarily providing informed consent. Samples were coded for the research project and were stored at the clinical laboratory of Boyacá Clinic for 24 h at 4 °C. Urine analysis was performed using Multistix® test strips. In addition, conventional microscopy and evaluation of urinary sediment were also performed. This study was carried out under the agreement between Clinica de Boyacá and Universidad Pedagógica y Tecnológica de Colombia (UPTC), located in Tunja, after the approval of the research project by the ethics committee of research and extension of UPTC issued on July 09, 2018.

Microbiological Analyses: Isolation, Identification, and Antibiotic Resistance Profile

About 10 µL of the urine sample was inoculated on MacConkey Agar and incubated at 35 °C for 18 to 24 h. The colonies were re-streaked on the same media until pure colonies were obtained. Gram staining was performed to check the microscopic characteristics. After obtaining pure colonies, antibiograms were performed with the PHOENIX system following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the identification broth (ID) was inoculated with a concentration equivalent to 0.5 McFarland standard, with an approximate concentration of 1.5 × 108 CFU/mL of bacterial suspension. Then, a drop of AST indicator and 25 mL of the previously prepared ID broth were added to the AST broth (antibiogram) for the analysis in the BD PHOENIX UNMIC-407 panel SKU: 448,879 disposed of for Gram-negative urinary infections. They were incubated for 16 to 20 h for the final report.

Preliminary Identification of E. coli

Preliminary identification of E. coli was carried out based on colony morphology, such as medium-sized, circular, convex colonies with smooth, rounded margins, followed by further evaluation on MacConkey agar. Further identification was done by the amplification of uidA gene. The DNA was extracted using UltraClean® Microbial DNA Isolation Kit (QIAGEN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The DNA quality and quantity were estimated using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. The DNA concentration was 152.7 ng/μL, and the 260/280 ratio was 1.92. The primer selection and PCR conditions for the amplification of uidA gene was based on Maheux et al. [10].

DNA Extraction and Whole-Genome Sequencing

E. coli was collected with a sterile swab, placed in the Amies transport medium with activated charcoal and hermetically sealed for shipment. Samples were shipped in disposable coolers at 4 °C. Briefly, after shipment, isolates were grown in Luria–Bertani (LB) broth and incubated at 37 °C for 12 h. After recovery of the culture, total DNA was extracted using UltraClean® Microbial DNA Isolation Kit (QIAGEN). Visualization of the extracted DNA was performed by agarose gel electrophoresis (Figs. S1, S2). The verification of E. coli strains was performed with conventional PCR (Supplementary material, Figs. S3, S4). For genome sequencing, the TruSeq DNA Nano kit (Illumina, Inc.) was used for library construction to obtain paired-end reads of 150 nucleotides per sample on the NovaSeq 6000 system. Quality control for the reads was performed with FastQC v0.11.8 program (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc) and the quality of the reads was validated with a Q20 score.

Assembly, Annotation, and Phylogenomic Analysis

After the raw data were obtained and quality-check was verified, de novo assembly was carried out using SPAdes v3.5.0 [11] using standard predefined parameters. Trimmomatic program [12] was used to remove adapter sequences and cut the low-quality reads. The assembly was evaluated using QUAST [13] and QUAST-LG [14] software. Subsequent annotation of the bacterial genome was performed following the Prokaryotic Genome Annotation Pipeline (PGAP) automatic pipeline available at NCBI [15]. Phylogenomic tree construction was performed using Anvi’o tool (version 7.1) [16]. Anvi-script-reformat-fasta was used to first format the fasta file for the Anvi’o workflow. The scripts anvi-gen-contigs-database and anvi-run-hmms were executed to identify open reading frames and match genes in the contigs to single-copy core genes [17]. The genes were aligned using MUSCLE [18]. The resulting tree was visualized using iTOL [19].

Phylogroups and Serotypes

Phylogroup and serotype identification were performed by bioinformatics analyses. The in silico Clermont Typing algorithm, which simulates the Triple/Quadruplex PCR [20], was used. For serotype identification, ECTyper V0.8.1 was used (available in the GitHub repository; https://github.com/phac-nml/ecoli_serotyping). This stand-alone module uses WGSS to identify bacterial outer membrane lipopolysaccharide antigens (O), as well as flagellar surface antigens (H). Standard conditions of the algorithm were used [21].

Multilocus Sequence Typing (MLST) Identification

For the identification of MLSTs, an in silico approach was performed using the MLST V. 2.19 program (https://github.com/tseemann/mlst), and this approach uses the PubMLST website (https://pubmlst.org/) [22]. From the WGSS reads, allelic profiles for each sequence were identified with the program’s algorithm. The scheme used was––adk, fumC, gyrB, icd, mdh, purA, and recA––available as the default E. coli scheme. According to Wirth et al. [23], standard conditions of the algorithm were used. The ST obtained from this scheme has more information about its pathogenic potential by being part of ST complexes than by grouping into certain phylogenetic clades.

Resistance Factors and Mobile Genetic Elements

For the identification of plasmids and other mobile genetic elements, as well as acquired resistance genes in E. coli, tools from the Center for Genomic Epidemiology by Technical University of Denmark (DTU) portal (http://www.genomicepidemiology.org/services/) were used. WGSS was analyzed using VirulenceFinder 2.0 with parameters set at 90% identity and a minimum length of 60% [24]. PlasmidFinder 2.0 (with default parameters) [25] was applied with 95% identity and a minimum length of 60%, while ResFinder 4.1 (with default parameters) [26] was used with a nucleotide identity threshold of 90% and a minimum length of 60%, selecting E. coli as the target species.

Identification of ExPEC Virulence Factors in UPTC_GRECO2 Strain

To identify relevant VFs in E. coli, the Bacterial Virulence Reference Database (VFDB) (http://www.mgc.ac.cn/VFs/) [27] was used. Sequences exhibiting lower variability were identified as potential virulence factors (VFs) and used as queries in the heuristic Blastp algorithm (https://github.com/ncbi) to search for homologous sequences in reference strains. The analysis employed conservative cutoff values [28] with percent identity ≥ 75 and an E value of e-12, using a BLOSUM62 weight matrix. In the case of requiring further characterization on any VF, UNIPROT (https://www.uniprot.org) [29] was used as an alternate query base. The sequences accession numbers used are presented in Supplementary Table S1. Additionally, a second analysis was performed using the WGSS of UPTC_GRECO_2 as input in VirulenceFinder 2.0 [24] to validate the detection of virulence factors (VFs) identified during the genome annotation. The analysis was conducted with parameters set at 90% identity and a minimum length of 60%.

Comparison of ExPEC VFs of UPTC_GRECO_2 with Proteomes of Clinical Isolates Reference Sequences

For the characterization of the ExPEC VFs present in the strain UPTC_GRECO_2, it was compared with previously reported ExPEC VFs in reference proteomes available in UNIPROT without counting plasmids, associated with specific clinical profiles: acute pyelonephritis, strain 536:UP000009182; acute cystitis, strain UTI89:UP000001952; traveler’s diarrhea, strain E24377A:UP000001122; diarrhea due to enteroaggregative E. coli diarrhea, strain 042: UP000001407; and commensal E. coli, strain HS:UP000001123. In addition, two related isolates were added: strain 28Eco12, which corresponds to NCBI Bioproject 451184 and which indicates a clinical isolate related to urinary tract infection obtained from the Universidad del Bosque in the city of Bogotá, Colombia and strain IAI39:UP000000749, which belongs to phylogroup F, isolated from a patient with pyelonephritis in France. Findings were shown on a matrix with the homology results, constructed with R StudioCloud V 4.0.3 and the Circlize V. 0.4.11 package (available at https://github.com/jokergoo/circlize). The genome was visualized using Proksee [30].

DNA Sequencing Data

The assembled genome is available as ASM1609321v1 in BioProject PRJNA682881 of the GRECO-UPTC group in the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject/PRJNA682881).

Results

Screening of E. coli

Several strains were isolated from the samples. Presumptive E. coli strains were identified based on morphological criteria, including circular, medium-sized, convex colonies with smooth, rounded edges. MacConkey agar, a differential medium, was used, and strains producing pink to red colonies were further selected (due to the lactose fermentation characteristic of E. coli). Further identification was done by the amplification of uidA gene (Figs. S3 and S4). Several isolates were obtained as part of the preliminary analysis, which led to the identification of different isolates of E. coli as shown in Table 1. Notably, a strain from the F phylogroup, designated UPTC_GRECO_2, was linked to a UTI, and consequently, all subsequent analyses focused exclusively on this isolate. The sample was obtained from a 24-year-old female patient from the urban area of Duitama, Boyacá, who consulted the Clínica de Boyacá with a 5-h history of suprapubic pain, dysuria, pollakiuria, dizziness, and nausea, without other symptoms. Microbiological analyses identified a rod-shaped, Gram-negative strain of E. coli, with a count of 75,000 CFU/mL, which was sensitive to antibiotics and identified as the causative agent. Table 2 shows the results of the antibiogram obtained using the PHOENIX UNMIC-407 panel. Paraclinical tests confirmed an uncomplicated lower UTI.

Genome Attributes of UPTC_GRECO_2 Strain

The genome size of UPTC_GRECO_2 was 5207891 base pairs (bp), having 246 contigs (Fig. 2). The genomic G + C content was 50.5%. The genome completeness and contamination were 97.5% and 0.6%, respectively, indicating a good quality genome [31]. Genome annotation using PGAP identified a total of 5356 genes, with 5032 predicted to be protein-coding genes. A total of 16 RNA and 89 tRNA were predicted.

Genome visualization of E. coli UPTC_GRECO_2 showing the length and common characteristics

Strain UPTC_GRECO_2 was classified into phylogroup F, with the serotype O1:K1:H7 and sequence type ST59 (Table 3). The results from WGSS of UPTC_GRECO_2, along with reference clinical isolates, revealed a diverse range of serotypes and sequence types. Notably, the neuC gene (involved in Group 1 capsule formation) in UPTC_GRECO_02 was identical to that found in the UPEC pathotyped strains IAI39 and UTI89 (Fig. 3). In addition, homology with the kpsM and kpsT transporters were identified. Representative UPEC isolates were clustered in phylogroups B2, D and F, while the commensal pathotype and EPEC clustered in phylogroup A, B1, and D. UPTC_GRECO_2 strain, defined as a UPEC strain, was identified as ST59 and serotype O1:K1:H7 in addition to being part of phylogroup F.

Identification of Mobility and Resistance Elements

Analysis using ResFinder 4.1 and PlasmidFinder 2.0 revealed that the UPTC_GRECO_2 strain did not carry any acquired antibiotic resistance genes. However, it was found to harbor the Col replicon (BS512), indicating the presence of a plasmid.

Relevant VFs Identified in UPTC_GRECO_2

Using Blastp, various VFs were identified in the UPTC_GRECO_2 (Table 4). The most significant VFs were related to iron uptake systems and fimbrial factors, particularly those involving the CU pathway and siderophores. Figure 3 highlights the main VFs in UPTC_GRECO_2 compared to reference E. coli sequences. Notably, VFs such as draA, cvaC, and cdtB were absent in UPTC_GRECO_2. In the case of the analysis performed with VirulenceFinder 2.0 (Supplementary Table S2), some VFs were discordant with Blastp. VirulenceFinder 2.0 was able to detect papC but not traT. In addition, it pointed out VFs that may increase bacterial virulence previously reported for other strains of E. coli including AslA, capU, cea (Colicin E1), eilA, hlyE, lpfA, nlpI, terC, usp, and yfcV.

Iron Uptake

Table 4 shows the groups and ExPEC VFs that resulted in homology range in the reference strains of the UPEC, EPEC, and commensal pathotypes. In the UPEC pathotype, on average, a greater number of ExPEC VFs were evident compared to the EPEC and commensal pathotypes, being in order of frequency the adhesin VF group, followed by iron (Fe) binders, protectins and serum resistance-related VFs (Fig. 3). Although redundancy was evident among the VF groups between pathotypes, the iron uptake group was identified as the differential in the UPEC pathotype, grouping the VFs yersiniabactin (ybtS), host heme uptake (chuA), and the dual Fe+2 and manganese siderophore (sitC), present in all strains including UPTC_GRECO_2 as well as in strain 0–42; these VFs were found absent in the other EPEC pathotypes as well as the reference commensal strain used (Fig. 3).

Strain UPTC_GRECO_2 Possesses the Afimbrial/Fimbrial III

In addition, Fig. 3 shows the groups and representative specific VFs in ExPEC that were found to be homologous in the strains analyzed, noting VFs in common, such as curli fibers (cgsG) and outer membrane protein A (ompA). Other frequent VFs among the strains were fimbrial adhesins type 1 (fimE) and antigen 43 (ag43). FimE was found absent in the EPEC pathotype strains, but not in the commensal strain HS or other UPEC, while ag43 was found absent in two UPEC strains, UTI89 and IAI39. The fimbrial gene (afaC) and resistance gene (traT) turned out to be the distinctive VFs in UPTC_GRECO_2 strain, these were linked to adhesion and serum resistance explored in ExPEC infection (Fig. 3). An in-depth analysis seeking to characterize the type of fimbrial (AFA-I, AFA-III, AFA-VII, and AFA-VIII) resulted in the identification of homologous proteins with the afimbrial afa-III operon.

Discussion

E. coli is widely recognized as the primary causative agent of UTIs, being responsible for the majority of cases acquired in the community and a substantial proportion of those occurring in hospital settings [1, 2]. A large proportion of females were likely to experience at least one symptomatic urinary tract infection during their lifetime, and among them, a considerable number develop chronic infections, often leading to repeated antibiotic use and thereby fostering the emergence of antibiotic-resistant uropathogens [32]. E. coli has a large number and diversity of VF [2, 5]. Although the viewpoint has leaned toward empirical management in the elimination of bacterial infection, it is important to improve the prevention and control strategies for these types of infections with an impact on the community. Molecular epidemiology studies are useful for a better understanding of those strains in specific geographical regions and could be useful to improve and establish specific treatments and management of infection. Mild UTI infections caused by E. coli strains that do not ascend to the upper urinary tract and may be managed without antibiotics [33, 34]. However, if there is any concern about the infection spreading, empirical antibiotic treatment is recommended as part of both the treatment and prevention strategy to protect against other urinary tract conditions, as pyelonephritis can be a life-threatening disease [35]. In the present study, an E. coli strain lacking the main VFs referenced as necessary for the establishment of a complicated UTI was found in a young patient with a mild UTI. UPTC_GRECO_2 represents a significant contribution to the epidemiology of E. coli in the management of urinary tract infections. UPTC_GRECO_2 was identified as part of pathotype V of UPEC, a group of strains with both fecal and urinary tropism, low virulence, and typically found in the gastrointestinal tract [36].

Although the present study analysis was limited to VFs, the potential for ST59 to acquire and exchange extrachromosomal VFs remains an important consideration for future studies. UPTC_GRECO_2 did not show any antibacterial resistance profile, aligning with findings by Lau et al. [32], who reported ST59 as a less common clone associated with cefpodoxime-sensitive urinary tract bacteremia. However, ST59 was isolated in a follow-up cohort of patients from 2005 to 2008 at medical centers in the Galicia region of Spain, where it was more frequently associated with urinary infectious foci when harboring the extended-spectrum beta-lactamase CTX-M-14, indicating increased pathogenic potential [37]. Thus, UPTC_GRECO_2 ST59 H1:O7:K1 may possess several epidemiological characteristics worth investigating locally in future studies that integrate both pathogenicity and ecological factors.

To our knowledge, this is the first WGSS isolate of E. coli H1:O7:K1 to be performed in Colombia, which was grouped in phylogroup F and ST59. UPTC_GRECO_2 and the other reference WGSS strains used in this study consistently clustered within specific phylogroups. Some strains grouped closely with strains from South and North America (Supplemental material, Fig. S5). Pathotypes with ExPEC fitness were primarily found in phylogroups B2, D, or F, whereas commensal strains and pathotypes such as EPEC, ETEC, and EAEC clustered mainly in phylogroups A, B1, and D [2].

The UPTC_GRECO_2 phylogroup currently has a sparse characterization due to methodological limitations that may result in erroneous clustering in phylogroup D when using only triple PCR [2, 4]. From the in silico analyses conducted using WGSS in this study, we characterized the isolate UPTC_GRECO_2 as belonging to ST59 and serotype O1:K1:H7. This serotype has been linked to multiple pathotypes, including NMEC, APEC, SEPEC, and UPEC, primarily from human isolates, and has also been associated with asymptomatic bacteriuria [37, 38]. However, ST59 has recently been identified as one of the most frequent sequence types in a large cohort of avian colibacillosis cases, with nearly half of the strains in phylogroup F initially classified as having ExPEC potential [39]. Phylogroup F was notable for its high intra-group diversity and its recent prominence in isolates from poultry raised for human consumption affected by avian colibacillosis. This suggests a potential ecological niche and highlights a zoonotic risk due to the ExPEC characteristics of these strains [39].

According to Johnson et al. [40], UPTC_GRECO_2 was considered an ExPEC strain because it contains the genes coding for the VFs papC, afaC, iucD, and kpsM II. This profile was also consistent with the most frequent VFs found in phylogroup F iha, neuD, and kpsMT II [39]. The UPTC_GRECO_2 strain was found to carry key genes involved in iron uptake, including ybtS, chuA, and sitC, which were also present in other UPEC strains analyzed. These genes were notably absent in enteric pathotypes, including the commensal HS strain. This genetic profile likely enhances the strain’s ability to thrive in the urinary microenvironment, where iron availability (Fe3⁺) was limited, providing a competitive advantage for growth and survival [41]. Some VFs of special interest found in UPTC_GRECO_2 were those that use the periplasmic space and chaperones and an usher to elongate adhesins like fimE and afaC, affecting host cells in a rigid and constant manner, resulting in critical situations in the invasion and colonization phases [9, 42].

Although unusual in ST59, UPTC_GRECO_2 afaC (usher of the afimbrial adhesin) was evidenced, which was reported in up to 6.8% of isolates with ExPEC potential of phylogroup F [39, 43]. In addition, the afa-III operon was identified through the identification of the afaF and afaD subunits. This operon has not been described in ST59 according to the literature reviewed and which was found to be associated with UPEC pathotypes as well as EPEC but also in asymptomatic individuals [44, 45]. This may be explained by the fact that receptors associated with intestinal adhesion of afaE-III, such as CEACAM1, CEA, and CEACAM6 [44], were expressed throughout the digestive tract, which could be associated with intestinal colonization, while the CD55 receptor was more consistently expressed in the urinary tract [46]. From those VFs exclusive of UPTC_GRECO_2, the traT protein was evidenced, which was reported more frequently in ExPEC isolates of the F phylogroup, with up to 82.5% of avian colibacillosis isolates. This proportion was consistent with clinical isolates in humans, which reach up to 90% in ST59 [39]. UPTC_GRECO_2 does not contain the ExPEC fitness-related CU VFs sfaF and focH that facilitate adhesion at the level of the urinary bladder, ureters, and kidney, giving an impression of limited virulence capacity since adhesion and subsequent invasion were considered key events in UPEC pathogenicity [47]. Other adhesins commonly present in ExPEC strains, such as fimH, cgsE, iha, ecpA, and afaC, were notably identified in UPTC_GRECO_2 and play key roles in urinary bladder colonization and recurrent infections. In particular, afaC has been shown in in vitro models to inhibit exfoliation by interacting with CEACAM1 and integrin β1 on CEA-expressing cells of the urinary tract [48], thereby disrupting local protective mechanisms and allowing higher bacterial concentrations to persist in the bladder.

The presence of the afa-III operon in a non-hemolytic strain lacking typical VFs associated with UTI ascent, combined with the clinical presentation, supports the classification of UPTC_GRECO_2 as a fecal reservoir strain capable of causing lower UTIs. Although it lacks VFs linked to renal complications, it retains the ability to colonize and proliferate in urine. Among UPEC virulence traits, adherence factors play a particularly significant role in the clinical management of UTIs [49]. Specifically, the UPTC_GRECO_2 strain may contain other virulence factors not necessarily related to the adhesion profile explored here, as indicated in Supplementary Table S3.

Within the constraints of our methodology, we identified a significant gap: the absence of annotation for the PapC protein in our final assembly, given the critical role of P fimbriae as VF in the development of complicated UTI. To address this, we adopted a thorough approach where we used the missing sequence as a “query sequence” representing the most conserved region in our VF dataset. Although employing VirulenceFinder 2.0 [24] proved effective in locating this sequence at the DNA level, demonstrating the algorithm’s improved efficiency in VF identification, more proteins acting as subunits or mediators of VFs are required. Therefore, we conducted a complementary analysis that included all proteins within the operon, using a homology-based method for enhanced accuracy. Our results revealed the absence of P fimbriae and uncovered various VFs such as afimbrial adhesin, curli fibers, ag43, iron/manganese transport, yersiniabactin siderophore, secreted autotransporter toxin, group 2 capsule, outer membrane protein A, iron‐regulated‐gene‐homologue adhesion, and complement resistance protein. Moreover, the incomplete operon observed suggests potential altered functions in aerobactin siderophore systems and type I fimbriae. Finally, we believe that a comprehensive analysis is essential to identify VFs associated with the development of complicated UTIs in symptomatic patients and to establish specific antibiotic treatments for individual cases. This strategy aims to facilitate rapid and accurate detection in the future using alternatives like next-generation sequencing. Such approaches are likely to reduce antibiotic use and antibiotic resistance pressure in strains with a local pathogenic potential similar to UPTC_GRECO_2.

Conclusion and Future Direction

We present the WGSS of a UPEC strain isolated from a young patient with no major risk factors diagnosed with de novo UTI, one of the most frequent infections in humans. Complicated UTI-VFs were not identified, although similar strains have been described worldwide with a high potential for pathogenicity and antibiotic resistance. The UPTC_GRECO_2 study allows us to propose possible alternative scenarios to first-line empirical antibiotic treatment in order to avoid the emergence of resistance to multiple antibiotics. UPTC_GRECO_2 was found to be associated with a community-acquired infection without evidence of contagious pathogen; however, the ST to which it belongs was described to be associated with contaminated food, especially poultry; this allows reflecting on the clinical attitude of health research, giving rise to possible future research with ecological approach in the study of UTI.

The WGSS obtained from UPTC_GRECO_2 strain contributes to the epidemiology in Colombia and reinforces the need for complementary and similar studies that seek the characterization of bacteria associated with comorbidities of greater prevalence in the regions. This is the first report of isolates in Departamento de Boyacá. Lower urinary tract infections require urgent management due to their sudden onset and impact on quality of life. Empirical antibiotic therapy is often recommended for quick recovery, but the rise of antibiotic-resistant strains with multiple virulence factors complicates treatment. An approach that identifies both resistance and virulence factors would benefit antibiotic stewardship programs locally and in other centers. Given the diverse contamination sources, including poultry, regular surveillance of E. coli strains is essential to identify potential links. Incorporating genomic approach into clinical diagnostics could enhance the precision of empirical treatment decisions, improve patient outcomes, and reinforce antibiotic stewardship efforts. Furthermore, the development of diagnostic tools capable of rapidly detecting key genomic markers in UPEC strains could be instrumental in preventing treatment failure, especially in emergency care or for high-risk patients. Finally, regular surveillance of E. coli from potential environmental and food sources remains crucial for anticipating and mitigating emerging public health threats.
