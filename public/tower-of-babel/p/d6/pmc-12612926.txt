High Permittivity and Supercapacitor Applications of Manganese Substitution in Biosynthesized-NPs Copper Spinel Ferrites
Authors: Chedia Moualhi, Walid Dachraoui, Mohamed Annabi, Abdelhak Othmani, Mouldi Zouaoui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Researchers have paid particular attention to transition metal oxides in recent years due to their higher specific capacitance and conductivity, making them ideal candidates for improved electrode materials. The present study successfully synthesized the spinel ferrites CuFe2–y Mn y O4 (where y = 0.0 and 0.5) using the hydrothermal biosynthesis route at low sintered temperature. Several techniques are used to analyze the synthesized samples: XRD, XPS, S/TEM, and BET surface area analyzer. Complex impedance analysis was employed to examine the electrical and dielectric properties of the CuFe2–y Mn y O4. The substitution of Fe by Mn significantly improved the electrical conductivity by about one hundred factors over a wide range of temperatures. Additionally, the studied compound exhibits a high dielectric constant around εr′ ≈104, with an observed low dielectric loss. Via the obtained electrochemical results, it is found that the substituted sample (CuFe1.5Mn0.5O4) can be considered an effective material for the working electrode in an electrochemical supercapacitor. Thus, the CuFe1.5Mn0.5O4 nanoparticles demonstrate motivating electrochemical performance, such as a high specific capacitance of 396 F/g at 0.1 A/g, energy density of 49.5 Wh/kg, and strong cycling stability, retaining 78% capacity after 1000 cycles at 0.1 A/g.

Introduction

Electrochemical energy storage technologies, electrochemical capacitors/batteries, and fuel cells have garnered significant attention in recent years because of their diverse applications with their particular selection dependent on performance limitations such as energy densities, power densities, and cycle life. − Although supercapacitors (SCs) have a lower energy density than batteries and fuel cells, SCs are considered to be high-power density devices with very short charge/discharge times, which are difficult to achieve with other electrochemical energy storage and conversion devices. Supercapacitors, in particular, have been extensively studied as a promising energy storage device. Several advantages, including low long-term maintenance costs, excellent cycle stability, high power density, and fast charge rates, make them highly attractive for a wide range of applications. Studies have confirmed that manufacturing high-performance capacitors requires many dimensional and electrical characteristics. For spinel ferrites, the type of dielectric material and its geometric properties significantly impact storage capacity, with capacitance directly linked to the material’s relative permittivity. − As we face energy and environmental challenges driven by fossil fuel dependence, efficient energy storage and transformation technologies have become increasingly important. A key focus is the development of low-cost, grid-scale energy harvesting solutions from renewable sources. , Supercapacitors fall into three categories according to their charge storage mechanism: electrical double-layer capacitors (EDLCs), pseudocapacitors, and hybrid capacitors, as shown in Figure . Electric double-layer capacitors (EDLCs) store electrical energy by accumulating charge through the formation of an electric double layer at the electrode/electrolyte interface during polarization. Their capacitance is significantly higher than that of conventional electrostatic capacitors. In EDLCs, cyclic voltammograms exhibit a rectangular shape, characterized by a current that is independent of voltage and linear charge/discharge profiles.

Nanomaterials, known for their remarkable chemical and physical properties, are being explored for various applications, including catalysis, energy storage and conversion, gas storage, and gas detection. − It is widely recognized that transition metal oxides exhibit multiple oxidation states and structures, facilitating rich redox reactions crucial for generating pseudocapacitance. − The spinel structure consists of two types of interstitial sites occupied by metal cations: one with octahedral B oxygen coordination and the other with a tetrahedral A oxygen configuration. In most cases, the bivalent and trivalent ions will occupy both the A and B sites. Therefore, the cations distribution in a spinel structure can be described by the general formula (M1−δ 2+Feδ 3+)­[Mδ 2+Fe2−δ 3+]­O4 where brackets display tetrahedral (A) sites and square brackets indicate octahedral (B) sites. , The variable δ, known as the inversion degree, defines the proportion of divalent (M2+) ion and trivalent (Fe3+) ion in the (A) and (B) sites. They exhibit interesting structural, electrical, and electrochemical properties. That can depend on factors such as the nature and charge of the ions, the method of preparation, and the ability to distribute cations between the available (A) and (B) sites. Spinel exhibits a variety of properties that have long made it a compelling subject of scientific investigation for potential applications. In high-frequency applications, it is important to understand dielectric properties, like permittivity, dissipation factor, and loss tangent, as well as their frequency dependence. These dielectric properties provide information about local charge carriers, which can be utilized for dielectric polarization. Dielectric materials are used in high-energy-density capacitors and have applications in advanced electronics, electrical power systems, hybrid electric vehicles, high-power microwaves, etc. The majority of ferrite spinel exhibits multiferroic behavior due to the iron ions, which have magnetic properties because of their unpaired electrons and electrical characteristics, which result in the relatively easy release of these electrons. These characteristics explain why the scientific community is more focused on ferrite than other spinals, as proved by the vast characterization performed on it. − The conductivity value somewhat impacts the dielectric properties. Researchers often aim to enhance resistivity and minimize losses by doping with various chemical elements, such as Zn, Co, and Ni. Researchers have also reported spinel ferrites, which consist of a combination of bivalent or trivalent metals (ions and cations), known as mixed ferrites. Jagadeesha et al. prepared CuCoFe2O4, NiCuFe2O4, and NiCoFe2O4 by the autocombustion process and investigated the electrochemical behavior of the nanoparticles. Numerous studies have demonstrated that doping spinel ferrites with manganese (Mn) significantly enhances their electrical, dielectric, and electrochemical properties. These improvements highlight the potential of Mn doping for optimizing the performance of spinel ferrite-based materials − because the variable valence cations of the Mn element have been chosen in this work as a dopant to synthesize the CuFe2–y Mn y O4 nanoferrite system, using the hydrothermal biosynthesis route at low sintering temperature. The two ions Mn3+ and Fe3+ have the same ionic radius (r Mn 3+ = r Fe 3+ = 0.64 Å) and r Mn 4+ = 0.53 Å. However, these cations possess an outer-shell electronic configuration similar to that of Mn3+ and Mn4+ ions Fe3+(t 2g↑ 3 e g↑ 2); Mn4+(t 2g↑ 3 e g↑ 0); Mn3+(t 2g↑ 3 e g↑ 1), this facilitates the incorporation of Mn3+/Mn4+ ions into the FeO6 octahedron, allowing them to replace Fe ions without inducing significant lattice distortion. The hydrothermal biosynthesis method yields pure phases at lower temperatures without high annealing temperatures, making it advantageous over other preparation methods. This method is promising because of its simplicity, the environmentally friendly nature of the bioligands, the vast accessibility of precursors, and the low energy requirements.

Experimental Section

Synthesis of the CuFe2–y Mn y O4 Nanoparticles

The initial step in nanoparticle synthesis involves preparing the bioligands. These were extracted from Ficus carica leaves using an eco-friendly process. The leaves were thoroughly washed with distilled water, then dried, and ground into a fine powder. This powder was mixed with distilled water and heated to 80 °C for 6 h. After being cooled to room temperature, the solution was filtered through Whatman paper to remove residual plant material. Using a low-temperature hydrothermal method, this study synthesized spinel ferrites CuFe2O4 (CFO) and CuFe1.5Mn0.5O4 (CFMO). The CFO system was prepared by mixing copper acetate [C4H6CuO4·2H2O] and ferric chloride [FeCl3·6H2O], while the CFMO system was created by mixing copper acetate [C4H6CuO4·2H2O], manganese chloride [MnCl2·4H2O], and ferric chloride [FeCl3·6H2O]. Each mixture was combined with bioligand. Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) was added to adjust the pH to a basic level of 12. The final mixture was stirred for 2 h and transferred into a 50 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave, where it was heated to 180 °C for 24 h, Figure . The resulting precipitate was collected and dried in an oven at 400 °C for 4 h.

Biosynthesis methods for ferrite NPs from CFO and CFMO samples.

Characterization

X-ray diffraction (XRD) with Cu Kα1 radiation using a Bruker AXS D8 Advance system was used for the phase composition of CuF2O4 (CFO) and CuFe1.5M0.5O4 (CFMO) samples. The experimental data were processed and refined by using the Rietveld method using FullProf software. The surface’s oxidation state spectra were obtained using a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha X-ray photoelectron spectrometer with monochromatic Al Kα radiation (hν = 1486.6 eV). For a more precise study of the microstructure, shape, and chemical analysis of biosynthesized nanoparticles, high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM), high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM), selected-area electron diffraction (SAED), and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) were used. The analyses were performed using an FEI Titan Themis 80-300 STEM, equipped with a probe Cs-corrector and operated at 300 kV. A Hewlett-Packard HP 4192A instrument was used to determine the dielectric properties of the prepared Cu–Mn spinel structure. Electrical and dielectric measurements were taken over a wide frequency range (10 Hz–3 MHz) and at a range of temperatures. The energy passes for broad scan and narrow spectrum are 100 and 30 eV, respectively. The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method was used to measure the specific surface area by multipoint analysis of nitrogen adsorption/desorption isotherms. Subsequently, electrochemical measurements, including cyclic voltammetry (CV) and galvanostatic charge–discharge (GCD), were conducted on the nanostructure using an AUTOLAB potentiostat/galvanostatic apparatus (PGSTAT 30).

X-ray Photoelectron Spectrometer Analysis

XPS has been used to analyze the composition of the surface and the chemical state of the different elements. The results obtained were subjected to a calibration based on the carbon peak C 1s = 285 eV. This value is used to adjust the binding energy of C 1s. Survey XPS scans of the CFMO nanoferrite are depicted in Figure a. Strong photoionization signals for the metal ions Cu 2p, Fe 2p, Mn 2p, and oxygen O 1s were observed in the survey scans. Cationic distributions, oxidation states, and relative intensities can be examined through the analysis of narrow scans of Mn 2p, Cu 2p, Fe 2p, and O 1s bands, as illustrated in Figure b–e, which represent long-term measured patterns. The dominant peaks detected at binding energies of 640.8 and 652.1 eV (Figure b), corresponding to the Mn 2p3/2 and Mn 2p1/2 levels, respectively, are characteristic of octahedrally coordinated Mn3+ cations with an energy separation ΔE = 12.1 eV. Moreover, the peaks at 639.4 and 642.6 eV were assigned to tetrahedral Mn2+ cation and octahedral Mn4+ cation, respectively. A noticeable difference was observed in the Fe 2p XPS spectra of CFO and CFMO, as shown in Figure d. Furthermore, the energy level is closely related to the oxidation state, with the excitation edges shifting to higher energy loss values as the oxidation state increases. Specifically, the energy loss for Fe3+ is centered on 710.1 eV, while Fe2+ corresponds to an energy loss of about 709.2 eV. The peaks at (713.5 and 710.1 eV) and (711.4 and 714.7 eV) correspond to tetrahedral and octahedral Fe3+ cations in the CFO and the CFMO samples, respectively. The existence of Fe2+ cations in the CFMO spinel has led to a redistribution of charges, producing an electron transfer environment that appears to be favorable in the conduction process. Furthermore, we observe that the presence of the Mn element implies a reduction of the Fe(Oh) 3+/Fe(Th) 3+ ratio from 0.61 (38% Fe(Th) 3+ and 62% (Oh)) for CuFe2O4 to 0.35 (36.5% Fe(Th) 3+ and 13% (Oh)) for CFMO. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy analysis confirmed the results obtained by XRD. Mn was successfully incorporated into the octahedral sites of the CFO spinel, resulting in a shift of Fe3+ ion occupation from the octahedral to the tetrahedral sites. The decrease of the ratio Fe(Oh) 3+/Fe(Th) 3+ will lead to a relative decrease in I 440 compared to I 220 or I 422. Figure c illustrates the energy levels associated with the two copper states, Cu 2p3/2 and Cu 2p1/2, for the CFO sample, which are observed at binding energies of 933.2 and 953.3 eV, respectively. In the case of the CFMO sample, the Cu 2p3/2 peak is observed at 932.3 eV, accompanied by a corresponding satellite feature at 944.1 eV. Additionally, a weaker peak at 952.1 eV is attributed to the Cu 2p1/2 state. In both CFO and CFMO samples, the binding energy peaks at 933.4 and 953.3 eV and 932.3 and 952.1 eV, respectively, were identified and attributed to Cu 2p3/2 and Cu 2p1/2 for tetrahedral site Cu+ cations. , The difference in the energy level (ΔE = 20 eV) between these two copper peaks is in agreement with the spectrum of the standard copper. The binding energy peaks at (933.9, 953.9 eV) for CFO and (934.1, 954.1 eV) for CFMO resemble Cu 2p3/2 and Cu 2p1/2 for octahedral site Cu2+ cations. In addition, the substitution of the Mn element implies a slight reduction in the Cu2+/Cu+ ratio from 0.16 (14% Cu2+ and 86% Cu+) for CuFe2O4 to 0.12 (11.2% Cu2+ and 88.7% Cu+) for CFMO. The O 1s spectra for CFMO are deconvolved into three contributions in Figure e, at 529.3 and 532.6 eV, corresponding to lattice oxygen (Olatt) and surface adsorbed oxygen (Oads), such as oxygen from hydroxyl groups and adsorbed water species, respectively. The peak at 531.1 eV is closely linked to oxygen vacancies, indicating the dissociative adsorption of molecular oxygen at these surface vacancies. Consequently, the existence of Mn results in charge redistribution for the variable valence cations (Mn2+, Mn3+, and Mn4+) located at different sites. This further creates an environment favorable to electron transfer. Also, the existence of manganese at the octahedral site favors on one hand the conduction mechanism of the Mn2+ + Fe3+ ↔ Mn3+ + Fe2+ type. On the other hand, many studies of the Cu–Fe–Mn structure have shown that Mn4+ ions work as electron holes, and hopping between the Mn3+ and Mn4+ sites gives rise to p-type conductivity in the compound. , The holes formed in octahedral sites are due to the oxidized Mn3+ to Mn4+ with the reduction of Cu2+ ions to Cu+ states, Cu2+ + Mn3+ ↔ Mn4+ + Cu+. The oxidation states of Mn play a crucial role in determining charge carrier density and facilitating hopping mechanisms between Fe2+/Fe3+ and Mn2+/Mn3+/Mn4+. A higher concentration of Mn3+ or Mn4+ can create additional electron hopping pathways, thereby improving the electrical conductivity.

Dielectric Properties of the Material

Impedance spectroscopy provides a powerful characterization technique that can be used to separate the contributions of different processes, in particular, the effects of grains and grain boundaries. − For oxide samples, the study of electrical and dielectric properties provides valuable insights into the movement of localized charge carriers, which are associated with key phenomena, such as electrical conduction mechanisms, dielectric polarization, and dielectric relaxation. Complex impedance spectroscopy enables the distinction between bulk contributions, observed in the high-frequency range, and grain boundary contributions, which dominate in the low-frequency range, in terms of capacitance and resistance. Figure presents the Nyquist plots of the CFMO compound, recorded over a wide frequency range [10 Hz to 1.3 MHz] and at different temperatures (RT–413 K). Within this temperature range, two depressed semicircles are observed, indicating the distinct contributions of grain and grain boundary effects to the electrical properties of the studied spinel ferrite. As the temperature increases, the radius of the depressed semicircular arcs decreases, confirming the enhancement of electrical conductivity in CFMO. In this study, Z-View software analysis confirms the substantial contribution of conductive grains and resistive grain boundaries to the electrical properties of the synthesized material. For the synthesized CFMO compound, the bulk and grain boundary resistances are determined by fitting the Nyquist plot to an equivalent circuit model. As illustrated in the inset of Figure , the most suitable electrical circuit consists of two subcircuits connected in series: one representing the grain contribution (R g//CPEg) and the other representing the grain boundary contribution (R gb//CPEgb). Here, R g and R gb correspond to the resistances of the grain and grain boundaries, respectively, while CPEg and CPEgb denote the capacitance associated with the grain and grain boundaries.

First, space charge polarization and the Maxwell–Wagner–Sillars interfacial effect play a significant role in maintaining high permittivity due to charge accumulation at grain boundaries and structural defects. Additionally, the reduction of high-frequency dipolar relaxation effects contributes to this stability, as dipolar alignment mechanisms remain active across a wide frequency range. The substitution of Mn ions into the copper ferrite structure also affects the charge transport mechanisms, such as the jump conduction of Mn2+/3+/4+, Cu2+, or Fe3+ ions, which helps maintain a high stability of ε′. At each temperature, the broad stability and large value of ε′ in the 10–104 Hz range may be due to polaron formation and carrier hopping between Mn3+–O–Fe3+, Fe3+–O–Mn4+, Mn3+–O–Fe3+, and Mn4+–O–Fe3+ localized networks, along with space charge polarization near grain boundaries. Moreover, the microstructure and grain size directly influence this behavior. Nanostructured materials, with their high surface-to-volume ratio, limit the mobility of charge carriers at grain boundaries, favoring stable permittivity over a wide range of temperatures and frequencies. According to our obtained results, the elaborated spinel ferrites can achieve very high permittivity values, which can lead to increased capacitance, making them advantageous for technological applications.

The variation of the dielectric constant ε′ versus frequency pattern at all temperatures is depicted in Figure c. The polarization process that occurs at the grain boundaries (GBs) of ferrite nanoparticles dominates the dielectric spectrum. The dielectric spectrum is primarily influenced by a polarization phenomenon that originates from the GBs of the ferrite nanoparticles. Figure c shows that the dielectric permittivity is higher (ε′ ∼104) in the low-frequency region and decreases with increasing frequency, which will take the stability of ε′ ∼102. The variation in ε′ versus the frequency can be described by the Maxwell–Wagner polarization model combined with Koop’s theory. In this limit, the prepared ferrites are composed of grains (Gs) separated by resistive GBs. At low frequencies, charges migrate from Gs to GBs, causing local lattice distortion due to the asymmetric charge distribution that aligns with the applied field. For all of the measured temperature values, the decrease in ε′ versus the frequency is due to the slower response of the dipoles, which are unable to keep up with the rapidly changing AC field, thereby reducing their contribution to polarization. As the AC field increases, rotational displacements occur, shifting to higher frequencies with a rising temperature. In correlation with the microstructural results, our data show that the studied system contains an internal barrier layer capacitance (IBLC) configuration. This configuration constitutes the basic factors for controlling the dielectric permittivity stability, order, and losses in the ferrites. According to the IBLC dielectric model (Figure d), the dielectric losses of the investigated nanoparticles are related to the influence of grains and subgrains, the impact of conducting crystallite domains, and the insulating barriers. Consequently, it is noted that barrier-conducting effects will result in leakage loss. Furthermore, the lower dissipation observed as Joule warmth was explained in the literature by the alternating current in the conducting region. In contrast to leak loss, which can be suppressed by increasing the barrier resistance, dissipation loss from conducting zones is expected to decrease as the conductance of the grains and subgrains in the system under study increases. From Figure d, we found that the spinel ferrites Cu–Mn have very important dielectric properties, such as low dielectric loss, which reaches 0.4 at 106 Hz at room temperature. The value of dielectric loss depends on several factors, including the density of the material and the resistance of the grain boundaries. This ceramic is an excellent candidate for various types of capacitors due to its low dielectric loss, high dielectric constant (∼104), and stability over a wide frequency and temperature range. It is important to highlight that the CFMO sample exhibits lower dielectric loss and higher permittivity with good stability across a broader frequency and temperature range compared with other spinel ferrites reported in the literature.

Supercapacitor Application of the Compound

Electrochemical dimensions were performed using a three-electrode setup at room temperature in a 1 M NaCl electrolyte. The working electrode was prepared by creating a suspension of the active material CFMO (70%), carbon black (20%), and poly­(tetrafluoroethylene) (PTFE) binder (10%) in ethanol solvent (99.8%). Carbon black was incorporated to improve electronic conductivity, and the polymer was incorporated to improve the mechanical performance. This well-mixed slurry was then applied onto Ni-foam (1 cm2), and the electrode film was pressed into a piece of 1 cm-diameter stainless steel mesh and finally dried at 100 °C for 5 h to evaporate the organic solvent. The mass of the active electrode material was approximately 1 mg. Ni foam with CFMO nanoparticles was used as the working electrode; a platinum foil was used as the counter electrode; and a Ag/AgCl electrode was used as the reference electrode. So, the electrochemical properties of the biosynthesized nanoparticles of CFMO are investigated by cyclic voltammetry (CV) and galvanostatic charge/discharge (GCD) experiments. Conclusion

In conclusion, Cu–Mn spinel ferrite nanoparticles were successfully synthesized using a simple, eco-friendly, and cost-effective green synthesis method integrated with a hydrothermal process, yielding an average grain size of about 10 nm. The XRD analysis shows that the intensity ratios I 220/I 440 and I 422/I 440 increase for the Mn-substituted sample. It indicates a variation in the proportion of octahedrally oriented to tetrahedrally oriented Fe3+ ions. The X-ray photoelectron spectrum shows the existence of the elements Cu, Mn, Fe, and O in the Cu 2p, Mn 2p, Fe 2p, and O 1s states. The existence of Mn results in a charge redistribution for the flexible valence of the Mn2+/3+/4+ cations located at different sites. This also helps to create a favorable environment for electrons to transfer. Consequently, a significant increase in σdc with the Mn-substitution sample is observed. The variations in the frequency exponent indicate that the AC electrical characteristics of the studied material are explained by the OLPT and NSPT models for medium frequency and by the activation of correlated barrier hopping processes for high frequency. The Cu–Mn sample exhibits a high dielectric constant around εr′ ≈104, which is relatively stable over a frequency range up to 104 Hz by increasing the measurement temperature, with a low dielectric loss observed. As the temperature rises, the disorder of dipoles increases, facilitating the movement of charge carriers. The increased values of the permittivity accompanied by low dielectric loss in the Mn-substituted sample are attributed to the formation of polarons and facilitate the hopping of carriers between the localized networks. The results of this study suggest that Mn-substituted CuFe2O4 ferrite nanoparticles are promising candidates for enhancing electrical and dielectric properties, making them suitable for supercapacitor applications. The electrochemical behavior of the fabricated Bio.NPs-CuMn0.5Fe1.5O4 as a working electrode shows superior pseudocapacitive performance with a specific capacitance of 396 F/g at a charge–discharge current density of 0.1 A/g.
