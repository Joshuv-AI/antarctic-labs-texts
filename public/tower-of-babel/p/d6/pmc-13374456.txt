Advances in cerebrovascular reserve function assessment for moyamoya disease
Authors: Siyu Chen, Tianrui Yu, Yuanyuan Xiang, Liang Chen, Hao Liu, Xuezhi Chen, Xianming Cao, Yanghang Chen, Manqing Zhang, Xiaoping Yin, Zhiying Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMoyamoya disease (MMD) is a rare cerebrovascular disease characterised by gradual progressive stenosis and occlusion of the terminal internal carotid arteries, anterior cerebral arteries and proximal middle cerebral arteries bilaterally. This pathology leads to the formation of a compensatory and fragile collateral vascular network at the brain’s base. With disease progression, MMD may result in ischaemic or haemorrhagic stroke. However, the lack of reliable and effective methods to assess disease progression leaves many patients at high risk of stroke without timely clinical intervention. Therefore, determining the disease course and assessing stroke risk are critical. Cerebrovascular reserve (CVR) reflects the ability of brain tissue to increase blood flow under stress. CVR evaluation assists clinicians in understanding MMD severity, guiding therapeutic decisions and determining prognosis. This narrative review analyses recent literature, highlights the advantages and disadvantages of various CVR assessment techniques, and discusses the interplay between CVR and MMD. In addition, it examines the role of CVR in evaluating disease progression and associated treatments.

Introduction

Moyamoya disease (MMD) is a chronic progressive cerebrovascular disease with unknown aetiology, also known as spontaneous occlusion of arteries in the circle of Willis.1 It is typically characterised by gradual stenosis or occlusion of the terminal internal carotid artery (ICA) and the proximal segments of the anterior cerebral artery (ACA) and middle cerebral artery (MCA). These changes frequently accompany the development of a network of collateral vessels at the skull base.2 This vascular formation resembles ‘smoke’ (termed ‘moyamoya’ in Japanese) on cerebral angiography, thus giving rise to the disease name.3 MMD predisposes patients to ischaemic or haemorrhagic stroke.4 A previous study indicated a mortality rate of 6.8% after the first haemorrhagic event in patients with MMD, dramatically increasing to 28.6% after rebleeding.5 The disease leads to high morbidity, mortality and poor clinical prognosis. Early prediction and assessment of MMD progression could potentially shift the current reactive treatment strategy, which addresses complications only after secondary cerebral haemorrhage or cerebral infarction. Cerebrovascular reserve (CVR) reflects the brain’s capacity to adjust cerebral blood flow (CBF) and serves as a critical compensatory mechanism under hypoperfusion conditions. Impaired CVR independently predicts stroke risk.6 Assessing CVR can predict stroke occurrence, support clinical decision-making,7 monitor therapeutic outcomes8 and determine patient prognosis.9

Pathological features and epidemiology of MMD

Suzuki and Takaku classified MMD into six stages, known as Suzuki staging, based on cerebral angiographic manifestations.3 This staging system is widely used, and lesions may be staged differently between hemispheres. As MMD progresses through these stages, a compensatory collateral network gradually develops along with collateral vessels.10 Pathological changes correspond to Suzuki stages I–VI: carotid branch stenosis, formation of moyamoya vessels (MMVs), increase of MMV, roughening of MMV, reduction of MMV and disappearance of MMV. Some studies suggest that these collateral vessels are actively formed.11 The moyamoya vascular networks at the skull base and collateral circulation develop sequentially, attempting to provide compensatory cerebral perfusion to brain regions distal to stenosis. However, such compensation is often insufficient or unbalanced. The steal phenomenon in MMD can exacerbate ischaemia, while the structural fragility of collateral vessels increases the risk of haemorrhage. Additionally, the compensatory capacity of collateral circulation frequently diminishes as the disease progresses, forming the pathological basis for ischaemic or haemorrhagic stroke. The primary cause of stenosis in MMD is concentric fibroblastic proliferation in the intima,12 characterised by the proliferation of smooth muscle cells and extracellular matrix, gradually thickening the intimal layer.13 Conversely, the medial layer (tunica media) becomes progressively thinner with disease progression.14 One study reported that overexpression of IPO11 and PRMT1 circRNAs might be associated with angiogenesis in MMD, whereas decreased expression of CACNA1F circRNA might contribute to vascular occlusion.15

Common clinical manifestations of MMD include transient ischaemic attack (TIA), haemorrhagic stroke, ischaemic stroke, headache, cognitive impairment and seizures.16 Patients experience intracranial haemorrhagic events due to rupture of vessels within fragile collateral networks or aneurysms.17 The likelihood of haemorrhagic symptoms increases with patient age.18 The prevalence of these symptoms varies according to age and geographical region.19 20 Large-scale epidemiological studies indicate that haemorrhagic presentations in adult patients with MMD are significantly more common in East Asian populations than in Western populations.21 22 Such differences between Caucasian and Asian populations may relate to the timing of arterial occlusive lesions. In Asia, arterial occlusion typically occurs in childhood, coinciding with a peak incidence of ischaemic attacks. The increased risk of intracranial haemorrhage in Asian adults may result from rupture of fragile collateral networks that develop in childhood, occurring decades later.21 22 Paediatric and adult patients with MMD also exhibit distinct clinical differences. Paediatric patients usually present before 10 years of age,23 predominantly with ischaemic symptoms, particularly TIA (81%).24 Adult patients generally present between ages 45 and 54 years,25 mainly experiencing haemorrhagic symptoms such as sudden severe headache, disturbances of consciousness, aphasia and hemiplegia.26

The incidence of MMD is higher in East Asian countries (South Korea, Japan and China) compared with Western countries, particularly in Japan, where prevalence reaches 5.22–10.50 per 100 000.27 28 Genetic factors may contribute to this disparity, as studies have identified the ring finger protein 213 gene (RNF213), located on chromosome 17, as a susceptibility gene for MMD, particularly in East Asians.29 The RNF213 14 429G→A (rs112735431; Arg4810Lys) variant represents the primary founder mutation and genetic risk factor in East Asian populations. Liu et al reported that approximately 16 million Asians residing outside Asia carry this variant.30 Initially, RNF213 was identified as the primary susceptibility gene for MMD. With the advent of high-throughput sequencing, other genes associated with MMD have been gradually discovered.31 The higher prevalence of MMD in East Asia compared with Europe and the USA may be partly attributed to the predominance of this genetic variant.32 Recent data suggest an incidence of MMD in mainland China of 1.14 cases per 100 000 (95% CI 1.12 to 1.16), indicating a gradual annual increase.21 Family members of patients with MMD have a significantly elevated risk (30–40 times higher than the general population), and MMD exhibits an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern.33 Furthermore, approximately 12.1% of patients with MMD have a family history,34 and 50% of offspring of patients with MMD may develop the disease.35 This observation underscores the genetic susceptibility to MMD, suggesting individuals with a family history should consider adopting preventive strategies to reduce the risk of adverse outcomes.

Overview of CVR

Definition and significance of CVR

CVR, also referred to as CBF reserve, cerebral circulation reserve, cerebral perfusion reserve or cerebral haemodynamic reserve,36 describes the ability of cerebral vessels to maintain stable CBF. This occurs through compensatory dilation or constriction of small arteries and capillaries (Bayliss effect) in response to physiological or pathological stimuli. CVR comprises structural reserve (collateral circulation), blood flow reserve (mainly small vessels and capillaries), functional reserve (autoregulation) and metabolic reserve.37 Assessment of CVR significantly aids in stroke risk evaluation, treatment planning, prognosis prediction and evaluation of treatment efficacy. Clinical assessment of CVR depends greatly on the imaging modalities and calculation methods employed, as each technique measures fundamentally distinct physiological signals and uses different quantification standards.38 Positron emission tomography (PET) is an established method to evaluate CBF and oxygen extraction fraction (OEF), where reduced CBF indicates cerebral hypoperfusion and increased OEF suggests impaired reserve. Single photon emission CT (SPECT) assesses reserve by comparing tracer uptake between resting and stress conditions, with insufficient increase in uptake indicating diminished blood flow reserve. Transcranial Doppler (TCD) provides real-time measurement of relative changes in CBF velocity. Both CT perfusion (CTP) and arterial spin labelling (ASL) quantitatively measure changes in CBF, and a reduced increase in CBF following stimulation signifies impaired reserve. Due to differences in physiological significance and quantification units across modalities, CVR results from different imaging techniques are typically not interchangeable. Although technical improvements such as multidelay ASL with arterial transit time (ATT) correction may yield moderate to high correlations between certain modality combinations (eg, ASL and PET), clinical interpretations must explicitly state the modality and stimulation method employed. This approach avoids direct comparison of CVR values derived from different sources.39

Methods and techniques for measuring CVR

Clinicians typically evaluate CVR by employing external agents to induce changes in microvascular resistance. The main methods include the acetazolamide (ACZ) test40 and increased CO2 concentration (CO2 inhalation method41 or breath-holding method42). Following vasodilation stimulation, imaging techniques assess CVR. Each detection method possesses distinct advantages and disadvantages.43

Currently, imaging methods most commonly used to assess CVR in patients with MMD include PET, SPECT, xenon-enhanced CT (Xe-CT), CTP imaging, TCD and magnetic resonance perfusion techniques such as ASL (figure 1, table 1).

Positron emission tomography

PET is mainly applied to chronic clinical conditions and is widely considered the gold standard for evaluating basal CBF and OEF.42 15O-water PET can directly and quantitatively measure CBF,44 thus it is generally regarded as the gold standard for human CBF imaging and functional brain imaging.45 PET simultaneously monitors CBF perfusion, brain function and cerebral metabolism, requiring a relatively low radiation dose (0.5–2.0 mSv).42 In MMD, PET evaluates blood perfusion levels and detects misery perfusion with high sensitivity.46 A study by Kaku et al confirmed that elevated preoperative OEF independently predicts symptomatic hyperperfusion after revascularisation in patients with MMD (p<0.05).47 However, prospective validation studies of OEF as an indicator of stroke risk in MMD yielded negative results. A study by Derdeyn et al found no significant correlation between elevated OEF and stroke risk in MMD,48 which contrasts with findings in carotid artery occlusion, where elevated OEF independently predicts stroke events.49 Therefore, the value of PET in MMD primarily lies in identifying haemodynamic impairment and predicting postoperative complications.

Single photon emission CT

The primary haemodynamic parameter detected by SPECT is CBF. Studies indicate optimal accuracy for CVR imaging when combined with 133Xe and 123I-N-isopropyl-p-iodoamphetamine (123I-IMP).50 51 Compared with PET, SPECT is more widely available and simpler to operate but has inferior spatial resolution and approximately twice the radiation dose.52 Additionally, increased CBF leads to a limited first-pass extraction fraction of 123I-IMP, potentially underestimating blood flow in high-perfusion states.53 Despite these limitations, SPECT exhibits diagnostic value comparable to PET in specific clinical scenarios. A study by Setta et al demonstrated a significant negative correlation (r=0.780) between SPECT-relative CBF and PET-OEF in patients with ischaemic MMD. Using an OEF threshold of >51.3%, relative CBF sensitivity for detecting misery perfusion was 100%, specificity 91%, positive predictive value 67% and negative predictive value 100%. This finding suggests good consistency between SPECT (via rCBF index) and the OEF-based standard in identifying misery perfusion.46 A recent study indicated that if SPECT does not demonstrate misery perfusion, adult patients with ischaemic MMD could be conservatively managed without revascularisation. Although these findings are promising, validation in larger multicentre studies is required before widespread clinical implementation.54 Currently, the primary value of SPECT in MMD lies in preoperative haemodynamic assessment and postoperative efficacy monitoring.

Xenon CT

Xenon CT is relatively inexpensive, widely accessible, non-invasive and offers high resolution. Combined with challenge tests, Xe-CT can quickly and quantitatively assess CVR.55 However, compared with PET, Xe-CT has lower spatial resolution, a higher radiation dose and Xe inhalation may be harmful to patients with severe pulmonary conditions.38 Nariai et al found that in patients with chronic cerebral ischaemia, ΔAT (CBF change after ACZ challenge) measured by Xe-CT correlated negatively with PET-OEF (r=−0.65, p<0.001) and positively with PET-CBV (r=−0.50, p=0.02). Hypoperfusion regions detected by Xe-CT closely matched misery perfusion areas defined by PET.56 However, the small sample size (n=11) and the focus on patients with chronic ischaemia limit the generalisability of these findings to MMD. Prospective validation of stroke prediction in MMD remains insufficient, with existing evidence emphasising technical feasibility and quantitative accuracy of CBF measurement. Bone and motion artefacts can also hinder accurate CVR assessment.38 Thus, the value of Xenon CT in MMD mainly involves serving as an alternative to PET or SPECT when unavailable and providing continuous monitoring of haemodynamic changes preoperatively and postoperatively.

Transcranial Doppler

TCD is a non-invasive technique combining low-frequency sound waves with pulsed Doppler to measure flow velocity, enabling real-time measurement of relative CBF velocity changes.57 CVR measurement by TCD is a recognised marker for stroke risk in asymptomatic carotid artery stenosis.58 However, TCD’s measurement range is limited, providing only a global rather than regional CBF velocity indicator. Additionally, measurement accuracy significantly depends on the operator’s proficiency, necessitating experienced operators.5961 In MMD, TCD assesses haemodynamic changes in major intracranial arteries. A study by Sun et al demonstrated significantly increased MCA flow velocity detected by TCD in unstable paediatric patients with MMD, suggesting utility for paediatric screening.62 Huang et al indicated that colour Doppler ultrasonography could serve as a non-invasive method to monitor preoperative and postoperative superficial temporal artery parameters, predicting surgical collateral formation and evaluating postoperative collateral development in patients with MMD.63 64 However, the complex and variable haemodynamic patterns in MMD, influenced by inconsistent collateral flow directions and poor temporal window penetration, may limit TCD accuracy. Thus, TCD’s value in MMD is primarily for bedside paediatric screening, postoperative collateral circulation monitoring and intraoperative observation of CBF trends.

CT perfusion

CTP is the most frequently used imaging method for acute stroke. Its main haemodynamic parameters include CBF, cerebral blood volume (CBV), mean transit time (MTT) and time-to-maximum of the residue function.65 CTP offers advantages such as high temporal and spatial resolution, simplicity of use and cost-effectiveness.66 However, its limitations include image degradation from patient movement, potential for missed strokes in the posterior fossa and near the vertex and a relatively high radiation dose. A study comparing ACZ-challenged CTP with SPECT in patients with MMD found that while baseline CTP parameters (CBV and MTT) showed a weak correlation with SPECT-derived CVR, the percentage change in CBF demonstrated a strong correlation with quantitative SPECT CVR measurements.67 This finding suggests that ACZ-challenged CTP effectively evaluates cerebrovascular reactivity through CBF changes, demonstrating strong consistency with SPECT. A recent study indicated that quantitative CTP analysis based on preoperative Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score effectively stratifies the risk of cerebral hyperperfusion syndrome (CHS), thus supporting individualised surgical planning and perioperative management.68 Although the results are encouraging, this study was limited by its single-centre retrospective design, small sample size and inclusion of only patients undergoing direct revascularisation. Therefore, the conclusions require validation through multicentre prospective studies before generalising to other surgical types or broader patient populations. Additionally, whole-brain CTP is valuable for assessing combined revascularisation outcomes across different Suzuki stages, using the preoperative mismatch ratio as a marker of collateral circulation status.69 Thus, the primary clinical value of CTP in MMD includes rapid evaluation of acute ischaemic events, identification of preoperative hypoperfusion areas, dynamic monitoring of postoperative revascularisation effects and risk prediction for postoperative complications (such as CHS).

Many methods are unsuitable as universal standards due to limited spatial resolution, restricted availability (eg, PET, Xenon CT) or radiation exposure risks. MRI-based techniques for determining CBF effectively address these limitations and have emerged as the predominant modality.70 With MRI advancements, numerous studies comparing related techniques have emerged. Research indicates that 7.0 T MRI detects and delineates MMV more clearly than 3.0 T MRI, offering higher diagnostic sensitivity and specificity.71 Furthermore, MRI combined with magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) can serve as a screening tool and an effective alternative for patients unable to undergo cerebral angiography.72 ASL is a non-invasive MRI method capable of quantifying CBF and CVR in absolute physiological units (mL/100 g/min).73 ASL demonstrates comparable accuracy to the gold-standard PET for detecting CBF.74 However, ASL’s primary limitation arises from the short lifespan of its endogenous tracer, posing challenges in patients with steno-occlusive arterial disease. If the blood arrival time exceeds tracer lifespan, the resulting loss of signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) makes CBF measurements unreliable unless the postlabelling delay is increased.75 Ease of use and sufficient SNR remain critical factors in implementing robust clinical perfusion imaging using ASL. Studies show that employing a multidelay pseudocontinuous ASL (pCASL) protocol improves the correlation between ASL CBF and CTP in MMD (p<0.05).76 Consequently, single-delay pCASL has become the recommended primary labelling method.77 Research demonstrates that preoperative haemodynamic impairment in patients with MMD detected by pCASL-MRI predicts neovascularisation following combined revascularisation surgery. This finding further supports pCASL’s clinical application in evaluating cerebral perfusion status in MMD.78

Other methods

Other CVR assessment methods include laser Doppler, near-infrared spectroscopy, harmonic perfusion imaging, dual-source CT angiography and magnetic resonance fingerprinting ASL. However, these techniques have limited clinical adoption.

With advancements in science and technology, the range of imaging techniques for evaluating CVR has expanded. Although the following methods remain in the research phase and have not yet been integrated into routine clinical CVR assessment, they represent promising future directions. Deep learning (DL) has advanced rapidly, holds significant potential in medical imaging and is recognised as a powerful tool for automatic information extraction, enabling rapid and accurate diagnoses. Due to the rarity of MMD and the extensive volume of cerebrovascular imaging data, relying solely on radiologists is time-consuming, labour-intensive and prone to missed or misdiagnosis. Therefore, researchers are exploring the development of DL models to assist in MMD diagnosis. Currently, the DL model developed by Lu et al, trained on 600 MRA images, achieved an internal test area under the curve (AUC) of 0.977 and external validation AUC of 0.880, showing diagnostic performance comparable to experienced radiologists. However, the study highlighted challenges in distinguishing MMD from atherosclerosis in early and late Suzuki stages, when MMV are not prominent. Differences in MRI equipment across institutions also suggest the need for further validation to ensure model generalisability. Moreover, the lack of interpretability in neural network decision-making processes limits its trustworthiness and clinical application.79 These challenges indicate that while artificial intelligence (AI) technology shows great promise for MMD diagnosis, critical issues regarding differentiation accuracy, generalisability and interpretability must be resolved prior to widespread clinical adoption.

The interplay between MMD and CVR

The interaction between MMD and CVR is primarily manifested as haemodynamic alterations caused by lesions at the distal ICA and proximal ACA and MCA.4 In cases of ICA occlusion, a significant reduction in CVR may occur even in the absence of obvious neurological deficits. Impaired CVR mainly contributes to ischaemic stroke. When CVR is exhausted and cerebral perfusion pressure decreases beyond the compensatory capacity of OEF, brain tissue fails to obtain sufficient oxygen, resulting in cerebral infarction.80 However, the mechanism of haemorrhagic stroke differs. It is mainly associated with the rupture of fragile collateral vessels (MMV), choroidal collateral formation or microaneurysms.17 81 82 A direct causal relationship between impaired CVR and haemorrhagic stroke has not been clearly established. Chronic hypoperfusion may promote the production of angiogenic factors, facilitating collateral formation and neovascularisation.83 However, the anatomical abundance of collateral vessels does not necessarily indicate adequate compensatory perfusion. According to Martina Sebök et al, patients with ICA occlusion who activate only primary collateral pathways maintain preserved blood oxygen level-dependent cerebrovascular reactivity (BOLD-CVR), whereas activation of secondary collateral pathways is associated with reduced BOLD-CVR.84 Furthermore, Funaki et al demonstrated that choroidal collaterals may serve as a source of high-risk rebleeding and a predictor of haemorrhagic recurrence in MMD.81 In 2019, Funaki et al further reported that the presence of choroidal collateral vessels increases the risk of new haemorrhage in the contralateral hemisphere82 (figure 2).

Haemodynamic patterns differ between paediatric and adult patients with MMD. Kuwabara et al showed that paediatric patients often exhibit markedly increased regional CBV, with some showing elevated regional OEF in areas with reduced regional CBF. This finding suggests that compensation mainly occurs through vasodilation and increased oxygen extraction. In contrast, adult patients demonstrate more heterogeneous haemodynamic features. Although some patients show no significant changes in regional CBV and regional OEF, most studies report prolonged regional transit time, indicating reduced flow velocity and delayed collateral circulation.85 Notably, haemodynamic alterations in adults are generally less severe than in children and are strongly influenced by collateral circulation status. Several studies have confirmed that paediatric patients with MMD exhibit lower CBF than age-matched controls, regardless of the analytical method used.86 These physiological differences provide a basis for selecting appropriate imaging modalities. Paediatric patients primarily rely on vasodilation and increased oxygen extraction and are more sensitive to radiation. Therefore, TCD and ASL are preferred for screening due to their sensitivity and non-invasive nature. However, when using ASL, attention should be given to the potential underestimation of CBF caused by prolonged ATT. For patients requiring accurate haemodynamic evaluation, multidelay ASL can improve measurement accuracy. Adult patients, due to greater haemodynamic heterogeneity, may undergo preoperative evaluation using multidelay ASL, CTP, PET or SPECT, depending on clinical requirements and equipment availability. Therefore, imaging modalities should be selected individually based on patient age, disease stage and haemodynamic characteristics to improve the accuracy of CVR assessment and its clinical utility (table 2).

Application of CVR assessment in MMD

The role of CVR in assessing the progression of MMD

A decrease in CVR is closely associated with an increased risk of ischaemic stroke in patients with MMD.87 Kim et al reported that deterioration in relative CVR grading correlated with poorer overall prognosis, primarily driven by ischaemic rather than haemorrhagic stroke.88 Other studies demonstrated that acute infarction and impaired preoperative CVR independently predict severe ischaemic complications following superficial temporal MCA anastomosis in MMD.89 Therefore, researchers have increasingly focused on modifiable MMD risk factors, proposing scoring models as exploratory tools for clinical risk stratification. Hyun Jin Noh et al divided 104 patients with MMD into surgical and conservative groups to identify predictors of recurrent ischaemic stroke in adults. Their results showed posterior cerebral artery (PCA) stenosis and decreased CVR predicted ischaemic stroke recurrence in the conservative group, while diabetes independently predicted recurrence in both groups.90 In a retrospective cohort study, Zhang et al identified an association between direct anastomosis of the paracortical artery to the anterograde haemodynamic source of the MCA and an increased incidence of postoperative complications.91 Hirano et al reported that anterior choroidal artery anastomosis and microbleeds predicted haemorrhagic MMD onset. They also identified hypertension and dyslipidaemia as cerebrovascular event risk factors in asymptomatic patients with MMD.92 Research has revealed that elevated body mass index and homocysteine levels increased MMD risk, whereas elevated albumin and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol reduced this risk.93 He et al explored genetic factors in MMD, discovering significant overexpression of the WNT4 gene in haemorrhagic MMD. Overexpression reduces microvascular stability, possibly increasing cerebral haemorrhage risk. This discovery provides a theoretical basis for investigating MMD pathogenesis further.94 In a small single-centre study, Hu et al found significant differences (p<0.001, corrected for false discovery rate) in functional connectivity between the right posterior cingulate cortex and paracentral lobule within the hemisphere, as well as interhemispheric connectivity between the superior parietal gyrus and middle frontal gyrus, precuneus and middle cingulate cortex, and cuneus and precuneus. These connectivity indices demonstrated discriminative value in predictive modelling, with incorporation of functional connectivity information improving the accuracy of post-revascularisation outcome prediction to 91.2%.95 Concurrently, researchers have proposed various models for clinical stratification using quantitative scoring methods. One such study developed the Prior Infarcts, Reactivity and Angiography in Moyamoya Disease (PIRAMD) scoring system, combining digital subtraction angiography, structural imaging and haemodynamic MRI; preliminary analysis revealed correlations with MMD haemodynamic severity and clinical symptoms.7 Another study developed a standardised time-to-peak (TTP) mapping and scoring system based on perfusion-weighted MRI, aiming to quantify longitudinal perfusion changes during MMD treatment and to explore the predictive value of preoperative perfusion status.8 Additionally, a retrospective study suggested that vessel wall contrast enhancement correlates with MMD disease activity markers, highlighting its potential as a semiquantitative indicator.96

Currently, most MMD prediction models originate from single-centre retrospective studies with small sample sizes. Many studies enrolled fewer than 100 patients, with some enrolling fewer than 50. For example, the PIRAMD scoring system included only 25 patients7, standardised TTP scoring included 44 patients8 and Kuroda et al’s subgroup with reduced regional CBF and CVR had only 11 cases.97 Although these small studies provide preliminary insights, their statistical robustness is limited, leading to unstable estimates and wide CIs. Small sample sizes constrain multivariate analyses and increase the risk of model overfitting. For instance, Lin et al explicitly included only four predictors due to statistical power limitations, potentially omitting significant variables.7 All studies mentioned lack external validation, thus limiting their generalisability. Some studies also recruited specific patient subgroups; for instance, Kuroda et al included patients who refused surgery,97 potentially introducing selection bias and limiting representativeness. Therefore, existing prediction models require cautious evaluation before clinical application. Future research should prioritise multicentre prospective cohort studies for external validation, and establishing large open-access databases would facilitate iterative model improvements and clinical implementation.

The role of CVR in the treatment of MMD

The treatment of MMD aims to reduce the concurrent risks of ischaemic and haemorrhagic stroke.93 Currently, no research has confirmed that any medication can reverse the pathological progression of MMD. Existing pharmacological treatments primarily target clinical symptoms, such as cerebral infarction and cerebral haemorrhage, while surgical intervention remains the primary therapeutic approach.98 Although the 2017 Chinese Expert Consensus on the Diagnosis and Treatment of MMD and Moyamoya Syndrome recommends active surgical intervention for both ischaemic and haemorrhagic MMD, deepening understanding of MMD pathophysiology has introduced conservative management targeting disease mechanisms to prevent or delay progression.99 For adult patients with ischaemic MMD, observational data suggest that revascularisation may be superior to conservative management, although this is not yet confirmed by randomised controlled trials.100 However, evidence supporting surgical treatment for adult haemorrhagic MMD remains insufficient. Surgical interventions may also induce perioperative complications, including ischaemia and hydrocephalus. Therefore, treatment decisions must consider individual patient circumstances, as surgery is not universally indicated. Guidelines suggest surgical intervention only for patients with significant clinical symptoms or evidence of impaired cerebral perfusion.20 Consensus guidelines emphasise that surgical indications for MMD include evidence of decreased CVR, characterised by reduced regional CBF and impaired CVR98 (figure 3).

Surgical treatments for MMD include direct revascularisation, indirect revascularisation and combined (direct+indirect) approaches.101 The selection of a suitable surgical method should also be individualised. A recent meta-analysis suggested that direct revascularisation may offer advantages in reducing the risk of future ischaemic stroke in adults with symptomatic MMD.102 Another systematic analysis indicated that both combined and direct revascularisation may provide superior clinical outcomes.103 Surgical goals include direct or indirect increases in intracranial blood flow via the external carotid artery system, thus improving CBF and CVR. Therefore, CVR can serve as a critical indicator for monitoring therapeutic effectiveness. Studies reported improved CVR following revascularisation, with cognitive functions remaining stable across most domains.104 For patients with preoperative haemodynamic impairment, direct revascularisation combined with targeted revascularisation of motor areas can enhance haemodynamics in the primary MCA territory, as well as in ACA and PCA regions.31 Researchers generally associate postoperative rebleeding with CHS after direct revascularisation surgery. Impaired cerebral autoregulation represents the principal pathological mechanism. Chronic preoperative hypoperfusion induces compensatory dilation of small arteries. Following surgical restoration of perfusion pressure, persistent vasodilation results in excessive CBF, potentially causing severe complications such as intracerebral haemorrhage or cerebral oedema.105 Ishikawa et al reported that a 30% increase in regional CBF compared with the contralateral hemisphere or a 50% increase from preoperative baseline predicts haemorrhagic hyperperfusion syndrome.106 Therefore, preoperative CBF assessment may help identify patients at high risk for CHS, and guide perioperative blood pressure management strategies. Additionally, recent studies applied resting-state BOLD functional MRI, a non-invasive imaging technique, to compare postoperative CVR between hemispheres with and without revascularisation, thereby assessing the therapeutic effects of surgery.107

In managing MMD, CVR assessment significantly informs treatment strategy selection, guides surgical decision-making and monitors therapeutic outcomes.108 109 Specifically, preoperative CVR measurement identifies haemodynamically compromised regions, aiding surgical planning. Postoperative dynamic CVR monitoring evaluates revascularisation effectiveness, facilitating timely adjustments in treatment strategies.

Prospects

The clinical manifestations of MMD are complex. Integrating CVR assessment into the entire disease management process may help overcome the current passive approach, in which treatment is initiated only after stroke occurs. However, several challenges remain. The key pathological features of MMD include vascular occlusion and proliferation, which are closely associated with impaired CVR. Understanding the factors driving pathological angiogenesis is critical to addressing these challenges. The application of CVR assessment in MMD lacks sufficient pathological controls and pathophysiological analysis due to the difficulty in obtaining arterial specimens from living patients.110 Furthermore, uncertainties regarding MMD pathogenesis have led to ongoing controversies surrounding its diagnosis and therapeutic interventions. Successful experimental models could greatly enhance research into disease mechanisms, with organoids representing a promising direction. However, MMD is likely not a single-gene hereditary disorder but involves multiple genetic contributions. Future MMD research must overcome the limitations of current simplified models. Consequently, current studies often fail to fully elucidate the mechanisms and clinical significance of CVR in MMD. Although multiple staging systems exist, no grading system currently captures the true disease progression based on clinical and imaging features. Future research should focus on developing more accurate and validated models based on existing quantitative frameworks. Such models could assist clinicians in stroke risk assessment, provide evidence for secondary haemorrhage or ischaemia, and prompt earlier clinical interventions. Additionally, standardised analytical guidelines should be established to reduce stroke risk in patients with MMD. In terms of biomarker research, exosomes have emerged as a promising area for investigating both potential biomarkers and the pathogenesis of MMD. He et al reported that altered expression of specific circRNAs in exosomes derived from patients with MMD may be associated with angiogenesis and vascular occlusion.15 However, these biomarkers remain at an early exploratory stage and have not yet undergone clinical validation. Large-scale prospective studies are required to clarify their diagnostic value and their relationship with haemodynamic parameters before clinical translation can be considered. In the field of AI, AI-assisted CVR assessment remains in the exploratory research stage. Future studies may integrate AI with CVR assessment to develop multimodal fusion models capable of comprehensively evaluating haemodynamic status and disease severity in MMD from both structural and functional perspectives. Generalisability could be enhanced through multicentre studies with large sample sizes, while the incorporation of explainable AI techniques may improve clinical interpretability and trust. Such advances could facilitate the gradual expansion of AI from an auxiliary diagnostic tool to a component of the entire MMD management process, thereby providing stronger support for clinical decision-making. In summary, the clinical value of CVR assessment in MMD has been established. However, progress toward precision management will require a stepwise approach. In the near term, efforts should focus on standardising existing assessment techniques and conducting multicentre validation studies. Midterm objectives should emphasise confirming the clinical utility of AI-assisted decision-making through large-scale investigations. Long-term goals may involve integrating biomarkers with multimodal data to establish comprehensive risk prediction frameworks for MMD.
