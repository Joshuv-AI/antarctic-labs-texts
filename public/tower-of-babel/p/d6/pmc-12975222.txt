Implementation of multi-disciplinary morbidity, mortality and improvement conferences in oral and maxillofacial surgeon training
Authors: Jianyao Huang, Wenyi Shen, Jinpeng Jiang, Hantao Zhang, Xianjun Shen, Huiyong Zhu, Dan Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study introduces multi-disciplinary morbidity, mortality and improvement conferences (MD-MM&ICs) to oral and maxillofacial surgery departments and reports the subsequent changes in the incidence of adverse events (AEs). From January 2020 to December 2022, using a reflective learning pathway, MD-MM&ICs were regularly conducted in the form of PPTs of reported cases by young surgeons. The preventive measures, early identification and treatments for AEs were summarized by causally categorizing the key factors for AEs, and postoperative AEs were recorded and classified according to the Clavien-Dindo classification, in order to evaluate the changes in the frequency of AEs and the severity of injuries in the ward after the implementation of MD-MM&ICs. A total of 262 AEs occurred, 68 MD-MM&ICs were conducted, and 122 AEs were reviewed in the meetings. Postoperative hemorrhage/hematoma and wound infection were common complications after oral and maxillofacial surgery, and 60% of surgery-related complications after flap surgery were serious complications (Clavien-Dindo Classification ≥ grade III). Upon the implementation of MD-MM&ICs, the number of AEs decreased year by year, along with serious complications related to the operation from 35.3% to 31.4%. Among them, the incidence of complications after flap surgery showed a trend of decreasing each year. By regularly conducting MD-MM&ICs, the ability of physicians at all levels, especially young physicians, to identify and respond to AEs can be improved. Furthermore, not only can the frequency of AEs be reduced but also the severity of patient injuries, thereby promoting patient medical safety.

1. Introduction

Advancements in oral and maxillofacial surgery as an independent discipline occur significantly later than those in other surgery types. In China, from the undergraduate stage, oral medical students and clinical medical students receive different initial education,[1] which makes junior oral and maxillofacial surgeons lag behind in recognizing systemic diseases or related complications[2] and also be not well equipped to prevent or respond to systemic or surgical complications in hospitalized patients after surgery.[3] On the other hand, the growing problem of population aging has led to the situation that oral and maxillofacial patients tend to be associated with systemic diseases of elderly patients.[4] At the same time, new treatment concepts and the rapid development of medical devices has brought a variety of new medical safety issues.[5] Postoperative complications (POC), the most common cause of surgical failure, may lead to a heavy patient burden, poor short- or long-term quality of life, high risk of sequelae, and poor prognosis.[6] Therefore, it has become vital to quickly improve the identification of adverse events (AEs) by doctors at all levels and give prompt reactions in oral and maxillofacial surgery departments to improve the quality and safety of patient health care.

Morbidity and mortality conferences (M&MCs) as internal medical science meetings are considered to be the “golden tool” of surgical education, with the main goal of improving the quality of medical care by reviewing and discussing AEs that occur during the course of clinical procedures.[7] After years of development, the \original purpose of “assign responsibility for AEs” gradually developed into the goal of “identify errors in healthcare systems and processes and their potential impact on adverse outcomes,” according to a systems-based approach to achieve medical education and quality improvement in healthcare.[8,9] As a result, many medical centers now include “improvement” in the title of conferences, which encompasses engaging multidisciplinary teams in discussing and evaluating key factors in adverse outcomes and identifying improvement opportunities based on the system to improve patient safety. Multi-disciplinary morbidity, mortality and improvement (MM&I) conferences are collaborative efforts to improve patient safety.[10] Currently, MD-MM&ICs are held in pharmacy,[10] otolaryngology,[7] infectious diseases,[11] radiology,[12] and many other clinical departments; however, to date, MD-MM&ICs have not been reported or discussed specifically from the aspect of AEs in oral and maxillofacial surgery departments.

The Clavien-Dindo classification (CDC) is a valid and widely accepted tool for assessing the severity of complications and is currently among the main criteria for assessing perioperative morbidity.[13,14] The system categorizes complications according to their severity into grades I to V. Grade I indicates the presence of a complication that does not require additional drug or surgical treatment; grade II indicates the presence of a complication that requires the use of additional medications for management; grade Ⅲ indicates complications that require surgical, endoscopic, or radiotherapy treatment (where IIIa does not require general anesthesia and IIIb refers to the need for general anesthesia); grade Ⅳ indicates a life-threatening complication (IVa refers to a single organ dysfunction and IVb refers to multiple organ dysfunction); and grade V indicates patient death.

In this study, MD-MM&ICs were introduced into the medical procedure of our oral and maxillofacial surgical department to classify the causality of key factors for the occurrence of AEs and to clear the severity of postoperative complications according to CDC. Then, the methods of prevention, early identification and handling of AEs were summarized, and the frequency of AEs and the severity of injuries in the ward were analyzed after the implementation of MD-MM&ICs.

2. Process and methods

From January 2020 to December 2022, we regularly carried out MD-MM&ICs at the Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery department of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine. The fixed team of MD-MM&ICs comprised medical and nursing staff at all levels of our department, including radiologists and pathologists. The core group, consisting of senior doctors of these departments, was formed to be responsible for the normal implementation of MD-MM&ICs. According to the specific nature of each case, the relevant departments (e.g., radiotherapy, medical oncology, nutrition, vascular surgery, neurosurgery, cardiovascular medicine, infection, etc) were invited to form an individualized multi-disciplinary team.

This single-center study was approved by the ethics committee of The First Affiliated Hospital Zhejiang University School of Medicine (IIT20230291B-R1) and followed the Declaration of Helsinki.

2.1. Pre-MD-MM&IC preparation

Prior to the meeting, the medical group presented to the Medical Record Review Team (led by the head of each medical group) recent AEs or rare cases that had occurred within about 2 months. In line with clinical teaching significance, and for the purposes of exploring and improving value filtering, the review team screened the cases and then finalized the selected ones. Based on the selection, the resident surgeon gathered the relevant information and literature and prepared the case for presentation.

Inclusion criteria: all-cause mortality: all causes of in-hospital deaths or postdischarge death cases; flap surgery-related complications; surgery-related complications (except for flap repair cases); postoperative systemic complications; hospitalization for more than 30 days; hospitalization within 2 weeks of readmission; unplanned secondary surgery cases (except for postoperative complications, such as misdiagnosis/omission of diagnosis or discrepancy between intraoperative and postoperative pathology); cases of medical disputes and complaints.

2.2. MD-MM&IC formal processes

These mainly included 5 parts: meeting introduction, case presentation, discussion and analysis, summary, and post-meeting follow-up feedback (Fig. 1). The content of the meeting was recorded by the medical quality administrators, which included the following items: information related to the participants (name, specialty and title); information related to the case (patient information, diagnostic and therapeutic process, AEs, key factors for the occurrence of the AEs, and the treatment of the AEs and their referral); discussion and recommendations (speakers, and their recommendations); and corrective measures.

The workflow of the MD-MM&ICs. MD-MM&ICs = multi-disciplinary morbidity, mortality and improvement conferences.

2.3. Evaluation methodology

We registered the specialty/discipline of the MD-MM&ICs participants, along with information related to AEs (case patient information, diagnostic and therapeutic procedures, AEs, key factors in the occurrence of AEs, management and fate of AEs, and corrective measures), with an emphasis on AEs in patients undergoing flap repair.

According to the key factors of AEs, they were categorized into medical-related AEs and system-related AEs, and the corrective measures recommended by the MD-MM&IC were summarized. Medical-related AEs were divided into postoperative complications (POCs) and non-postoperative complications (n-POCs).

Every deviation from standard treatment or expected postoperative course is considered as a postoperative complication.[15] According to the treatment methods of postoperative complications, the Clavien-Dindo classification criteria were used to classify the degree of harm from postoperative AEs. Grade III and IV complications were considered serious complications.[5]

2.4. Statistical analysis

The baseline data, such as the amount of MD-MM&ICs, the number of medical staff and departments, and the AEs, were summarized as descriptive data, while the categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

3. Results

3.1. Basic information of participants

From January 2020 to December 2022, a total of 262 AEs occurred on our wards, 68 MD-MM&ICs were conducted, and 122 AEs were reviewed. A total of 122 case preparations and debriefings by resident were completed. A total of 260 disciplines and 588 physicians participated in MD-MM&ICs, including chief/associate chief physicians on 130 occasions, attending physicians on 142 occasions, residents on 316 occasions, and nurses on 151 occasions. The disciplines involved in the discussion were mainly radiology, pathology, radiotherapy, medical oncology, nutrition, anesthesiology, intensive care unit, respiratory medicine, cardiovascular, internal medicine, infection, etc (Table 1).

3.2. Occurrence of adverse events

A total of 122 AEs were discussed in the meetings. After comprehensive analysis, the most common risk factor for AEs was the inadequate recognition of changes in clinical situations by frontline healthcare workers. Other common risk factors included insufficient communication between healthcare team members, insufficient communication between healthcare workers and patients, overloading of healthcare staff leading to errors, and insufficient multi-disciplinary collaboration. 3.2.1. CASR 1: postoperative hemorrhage and hematoma

To conduct MD - MM&ICs on postoperative hemorrhage and hematoma, we organized discussions involving the intensive care unit, operating room, nursing staff, ultrasonic department, imaging department, and other multi-disciplinary fields. This enables young physicians to learn how to prevent and promptly detect postoperative bleeding and how to perform emergency hemostasis. Intermediate physicians can enhance their surgical techniques, such as using a bipolar electro - coagulation device and carefully checking for bleeding points before concluding the operation. Senior physicians can become more proficient in coordinating junior physicians, nursing staff, and the operating room to carry out emergency surgeries.

3.2.2. CASR 2: Postoperative wound infection

To conduct MD-MM&ICs on postoperative wound infection, we organized discussions involving infectious disease, nursing, imaging, laboratory medicine, and other multi-disciplinary fields. This allows young physicians to learn to strictly adhere to aseptic surgical protocols and master dressing changes, intermediate physicians to enhance their skill proficiency, and senior physicians to strengthen intraoperative teamwork.

3.2.3. CASR 3: flap vascular crisis

To conduct MD-MM&ICs on flap vascular crisis, we organized discussions involving multiple disciplines such as vascular surgery, monitoring, operating room, nursing, hand surgery, and imaging. This allows young physicians to learn how to prevent and promptly detect vascular crises, intermediate physicians to discover how to handle vascular anastomosis during operations and salvage flaps postoperation, and senior physicians to become more proficient in making overall arrangements to avoid further patient loss and prevent medical disputes.

Owing to the MD-MM&ICs, the incidence of clinical AEs reduced year-on-year, with the incidence of serious complications (CDC ≥3) being 35.3%, 33.3%, and 31.4%, respectively (see Table 3). The incidence rate of each major complication after free flap repair and reconstruction decreased from 30.3% (99 flap operations/year, 30 complication cases) before the MD-MM&IC was carried out to 2020 (28.7%), 2021 (24.1%), and 2022 (18.7%) (see Table 4). 4. Discussion

Improving the comprehensive clinical competence of frontline clinicians to address the increasingly prominent healthcare safety issues has become an imminent target of medical education in oral and maxillofacial surgery. As M&MCs continue to evolve, MD-MM&ICs, oriented toward identifying and solving systemic problems and involves multi-disciplinary teams, are increasingly used by multiple health systems.[16] It not only emphasizes the importance of learning from failures (complications and deaths) to avoid repeating mistakes in medical education but also reinforces continuous quality improvement oriented to identifying and solving systemic problems and strengthens the continuous quality improvement oriented to identifying and solving systemic problems, thereby improving healthcare safety.[17,18]

The process of debriefing, analyzing and discussing cases means getting to the bottom of AEs, which can be attributed to the following categories of factors: medical, nursing, patient, healthcare systems and processes, medications, environmental equipment, and other factors.[19] In our study, by tracing the occurrence of AEs back to their root cause, we categorized the key triggering factors into 6 main areas: surgeon skills, medical care, teamwork, work environment/stress, non-surgeon skill-related medical problems, and equipment processes, then proposed targeted strategies to address the problems based on these factors (Fig. 2). These are not trivial factors but can all be key contributors to the occurrence of adverse medical events, with 1 or more factors potentially playing an equally important role in the same adverse event. In the critical factor analysis, we found that insufficient identification of changes in the clinical conditions by frontline healthcare workers was often the most common risk factor in the occurrence of AEs, which is consistent with previous research.[19] This means the accurate recognition of postoperative tissue swelling in the operative area as a normal postoperative reaction or local bleeding or infection, early identification of the occurrence of postoperative vascular crises in patients with dermal flaps, and so on. To this end, early management of postoperative infections, hematomas and dermal flap vascular crises is an effective way to reduce CDC grading. Recent studies confirm that standardized reporting tools and structured databases significantly enhance the identification of quality issues and the educational value of case reports.[20,21] In our previous study, we found that participation in MD-MM&ICs significantly improved young resident physicians’ abilities in Patient Reception, Clinical Reasoning, and Auxiliary Result Interpretation.[22] These improvements may be related to the fact that multi-disciplinary discussions and complex cases can help participants expand their clinical knowledge. Through case reviews and summaries, the establishment of correct clinical thinking patterns can be continuously reinforced, which is beneficial for participants’ understanding and recognition of diseases.[22] Therefore the MD-MM&IC can help identify, prevent and address AEs, leading to a yearly reduction in the incidence of severe complications. In Table 2, we also found that the incidence of severe complications such as flap reconstruction complications was reduced yearly, as it is closely related to the clinical skill level of the physicians. In contrast, the incidence of systemic complications has not shown a yearly decrease, which may be related to the complex and diverse factors influencing systemic complications. On the other hand, overloading of medical staff and irrational surgical scheduling are also key factors that need attention.[19] In our study, complex surgeries such as skin flaps that were scheduled on weekdays before holidays often resulted in irreversible necrosis of the flap due to untimely exploration in the event of a vascular crisis.

The ACGME accreditation sets forth that the education of young physicians requires training and education in 6 specific generic competencies, including patient care, medical knowledge, practice-based learning and improvement, interpersonal and communication skills, professionalism, and systems-based practice.[23] For young physicians, the MD-MM&IC is an important clinical learning opportunity that can effectively incorporate the 6 ACGME competencies into a young physician’s clinical training program.[24] Through the MD-MM&IC, young physicians as well as other physicians at all levels can learn how to avoid future recurrences of the discussed situations and the appropriate actions to take when they do recur, resulting in safer healthcare for patients and a foundation of becoming better surgeons.[25,26] In this paper, we summarize the impact of MD-MM&ICs on physicians at all levels of seniority. The greatest impact was seen on young physicians at lower levels of seniority. Before participating in the discussion on the reported cases, these physicians already had a good understanding of the course of AEs, reviewed a lot of literature and were fully immersed in MD-MM&ICs. Our experience is that young physicians should be responsible for the reporting, so as to mobilize their enthusiasm instead of simply participating in lectures, and through multi-disciplinary discussions, broaden their thinking and improve all basic clinical skills. Targeted educational interventions are essential, as residents often lack training in root cause analysis. Our reflective learning pathway addresses this gap by providing necessary practice and feedback.[27] For middle-aged physicians, reducing surgical complications is the main purpose for participating in the conferences. The study of MD-MM&ICs can help them to strengthen their understanding of serious complications, refine their surgical techniques, and avoid blindly carrying out high-level surgeries. Meanwhile, for senior physicians, taking part in MD-MM&ICs mainly aims to improve the success rate of major surgeries and reduce the incidence of medical errors. As for the complication of flap vascular crisis in stomatology, since flap reconstruction can be regarded as one of the more significant surgeries in oral and maxillofacial surgery, when a flap vascular crisis or even flap necrosis occurs, this will be a serious blow to the confidence of physicians at all levels in surgery, causing severe losses to patients and significantly impair the doctor-patient relationship, even causing doctor-patient disputes. After MD-MM&ICs on flap vascular crisis, young physicians can learn how to prevent and timely detect vascular crisis, intermediate physicians can discover how to deal with vascular anastomosis during the operation, how to salvage the flap after the operation, and senior physicians can become more proficient in how to make overall arrangements to avoid further loss of patients and prevent medical disputes. In fact, with the implementation of MD-MM&ICs, the number of AEs in our ward has been decreasing year by year, from 102 cases/year at the beginning to 70 cases/year, and at the same time, serious surgical complications also decreased from 35.3% to 31.4%, which reduced the severity of injuries brought by surgical treatment to patients (see Table 3). These data show that MD-MM&ICs have certain teaching and clinical significance.

The clinical practice of medicine is essentially a joint effort and collaboration between multiple disciplines. High-quality healthcare cannot be achieved without close collaboration between healthcare professionals with a variety of different specialties. Multi-disciplinary MD-MM&ICs provide an ideal environment for communication between these experts, thereby improving patient safety in healthcare.[28,29] Adverse outcomes often involve several medical fields, so multi-disciplinary MD-MM&ICs are critical to providing high-quality healthcare outcomes often involve several medical fields, so multi-disciplinary MD-MM&ICs are critical to providing high-quality healthcare.[24] Unfortunately, often only healthcare professionals from the sponsoring department participate in MD-MM&ICs, while physicians from other specialties are not actively involved.[24] Baker et al[30] suggested that to successfully address system-based errors, “cases must be reviewed by multiple specialties at the same time.” Indeed, multi-disciplinary participation in MD-MM&ICs facilitates the achievement of effective communication between different specialties, the exploration of patient safety issues from different perspectives and levels, the highlighting of systemic issues that may adversely affect patients’ postoperative outcomes, and the presentation of viable solutions to problems. While program-based conferences foster necessary collaboration, diverse backgrounds can threaten psychological safety due to perceived hierarchies. Thus, a “no-blame” culture is vital to ensure effective multi-disciplinary exchange.[31,32]

Post-conference actions should not be overlooked either. To truly realize the improvement of healthcare quality and develop a culture of patient safety, the improvement strategies proposed by need to be effectively implemented by the corresponding departments and personnel. In addition, continuous tracking and monitoring of the implementation of improvement measures and regular summary reports at MD-MM&ICs are necessary. It has been suggested that the low attendance at MD-MM&ICs may be related to the lack of expected quality improvement effects of the meetings.[33] Clinicians’ personal motivation to participate in MD-MM&ICs may be primarily related to systemic and personal improvement.[34] However, if participants do not receive feedback on the changes brought about by the suggestions and ideas presented at the meeting, they may lose confidence in the value of the meeting, which in turn may affect their motivation to attend.[35,36] The following are some examples of how this can be accomplished. Regular feedback on the benign changes brought about by the results of previous conferences is beneficial in demonstrating the effectiveness of MD-MM&ICs in improving healthcare systems, processes and individuals, as well as increasing attendee satisfaction with the conference, which in turn encourages more healthcare professionals to participate in the conference, creating a positive feedback loop. Ensuring the sustainability of the measures through long-term monitoring is an important challenge for quality improvement programs, because in a busy healthcare environment, this extra work cannot be done without a high degree of acceptance and cooperation from hospitals and professionals.[37]

This study has some limitations. First, although our root cause analysis identified 6 main factors, but the surgeon technique and working environment/stress related factors are mainly focused. In the future, we should focus more on the other factors, such as Process/system issues related factors through inviting hospital management and support staffs. Second, our research couldn’t cover all type complications and the deeper influencing factors, such as low BMI preoperation, agedness, operative time and systemic condition, were not analyzed, which we would plan to improve in the future. Furthermore, this is a single-center study conducted in the context of the Chinese oral and maxillofacial surgeon training model, and its findings cannot be generalized to all educational centers.

Undoubtedly, the improvement of clinical thinking level and the clinical competence of physicians at all levels is influenced by many factors, and the reduction in the adverse event rate also benefits from the improvement of a range of clinical factors. To this end, this study cannot unquestionably prove that MD-MM&ICs significantly reduce the incidence of AEs. However, through the implementation of MD-MM&ICs, physicians at all levels clearly learn and summarize the lessons learned, further avoiding the occurrence of clinical AEs.

5. Conclusions

The practice of holding MD-MM&ICs in oral and maxillofacial surgery has proved to have important teaching value and clinical significance, exerting a positive influence on the clinical thinking level and medical service ability of doctors at all levels. Therefore, MD-MM&ICs provide a good teaching platform and consultation opportunity for the training of young physicians in this field. The model implemented in this work is worthy of further promotion in the training of young maxillofacial surgeons.
