Economic Evaluation of Bruton’s Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors for Chronic Lymphocytic Leukaemia in South Africa
Authors: Rochelle Woudberg, Edina Sinanovic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTargeted therapy with Bruton's tyrosine kinase inhibitors has demonstrated promising efficacy and safety outcomes in both untreated and relapsed or refractory chronic lymphocytic leukaemia (CLL); however, evidence regarding their cost effectiveness remains limited. This study evaluated the cost effectiveness of ibrutinib, acalabrutinib, and zanubrutinib for the treatment of CLL from the perspective of South Africa’s public healthcare system.MethodsA partitioned survival model was developed with three health states: progression-free survival (PFS), progression, and death. The model used a 10-year time horizon with a cycle length of 28 days. Clinical inputs were derived from reference trials representing untreated and relapsed or refractory CLL populations. As no head-to-head trials directly compare all three Bruton's tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitors, a naïve (unadjusted) indirect comparison was used, with survival curves sourced independently from pivotal trials for each agent. The distribution of patients in each health state over time was estimated using extrapolated PFS and overall survival (OS) curves for each treatment strategy. Utility values were obtained from published literature, and cost data from national public-sector tariffs. The model estimated total costs, life-years (LYs) gained, and quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) gained. Outcomes were based on calculated incremental cost effectiveness, with costs and effects discounted at 5.0% per year. Deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analyses were conducted to test the robustness of the results.ResultsIn the base-case analysis, acalabrutinib versus ibrutinib resulted in an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio of US$1206 per LY gained and US$2124 per QALY gained in untreated CLL patients and US$1140 per LY gained and US$2104 per QALY gained in relapsed or refractory CLL patients. Zanubrutinib was dominant in both populations compared to ibrutinib, providing greater health benefits at lower total costs. In untreated CLL, zanubrutinib resulted in 0.32 more QALYs and cost savings of US$9086 per patient; in the relapsed or refractory CLL, it yielded 0.35 additional QALYs with US$6052 in savings. Additionally, acalabrutinib was more effective but more costly than zanubrutinib, with incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICERs) of US$24,010 per QALY gained in untreated CLL patients and US$33,009 per QALY gained in relapsed or refractory CLL patients. The model was most sensitive to drug acquisition costs and costs incurred in the progression-free health state. Probabilistic sensitivity analysis confirmed that zanubrutinib had the highest probability of being cost effective, at 61% in untreated and 65% in relapsed or refractory CLL patients at a willingness-to-pay threshold of US$3407 per QALY. Acalabrutinib showed a 23% and 22% probability of being cost effective in the respective groups, while ibrutinib had the lowest cost-effectiveness likelihood.ConclusionZanubrutinib may be a cost-saving and clinically superior treatment option for both untreated and relapsed/refractory CLL compared to ibrutinib in South Africa’s public healthcare system. Its dominance is driven by lower acquisition and adverse event costs, paired with favourable survival and quality-of-life outcomes. Acalabrutinib may also represent a cost-effective alternative compared to ibrutinib, providing meaningful clinical benefit at acceptable additional cost. These results support the prioritisation of zanubrutinib for public sector access and reimbursement, while highlighting acalabrutinib’s value in selected patient scenarios.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40258-026-01031-9.

Introduction

Chronic lymphocytic leukaemia (CLL) is a haematological malignancy characterised by the proliferation and accumulation of monoclonal B-cell lymphocytes in the blood, bone marrow and lymphatic tissues [1]. Globally, an estimated 103,467 new cases were reported in 2019, accounting for 22–30% of all leukaemia cases [2]. In South Africa, CLL is the most prevalent type of leukaemia in adults, accounting for approximately 22% of cases, with an incidence rate of 1.03 cases per 100,000 population [3].

Chronic lymphocytic leukaemia typically presents after the age of 45 years, with the median age at diagnosis being approximately 64–72 years, and occurs more frequently in males [4]. However, in South Africa, CLL patients tend to be diagnosed at a slightly earlier age, with a reported mean age of 62.6 years [3]. This younger age at diagnosis may be influenced by genetic predisposition, environmental exposures, or differences in healthcare access and screening practices [3]. This trend is consistent with findings from other countries, including China, Nigeria, Turkey, Sudan, Bangladesh, Morocco and India [5–10].

The natural history of CLL is highly variable, ranging from indolent, asymptomatic disease to cases characterised by recurrent symptomatic relapses. This variability is influenced by several prognostic factors, including clinical stage as defined by the Rai (from 0 to IV) and Binet (from A to C) systems, cytogenetic abnormalities (e.g., TP53 mutations or 17p deletions), and the mutation status of the immunoglobulin heavy-chain variable region (IGHV) gene [11]. Approximately 70% to 80% of CLL patients are asymptomatic at diagnosis, with a median survival exceeding 10 years [12, 13]. These patients are often managed conservatively with a watch-and-wait approach. In contrast, the remaining 20% to 30% of patients present with or develop an aggressive disease, characterised by rapid progression, requiring therapeutic intervention [12, 13]. Patients with progressive disease have a median survival of less than 6 years, while patients with relapsed or refractory disease face an even poorer prognosis, with survival typically ranging between 3 and 5 years [11].

Recent years have seen a marked improvement in survival outcomes for patients with CLL, largely driven by the introduction of targeted therapies such as Bruton's tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitors (e.g., ibrutinib, acalabrutinib, zanubrutinib) and BCL-2 inhibitors (e.g., venetoclax-based regimens). Notably, the 5-year survival rate has improved considerably, increasing from 78.5% for cases diagnosed in 2000 to 89.3% for those diagnosed in 2015 [14]. These advances have transformed the treatment landscape, allowing for more effective disease control and fewer long-term toxicities compared to traditional chemoimmunotherapy (CIT). Despite these gains, CLL remains an incurable disease, and while many patients can be managed effectively for extended periods, those with relapsed or refractory disease require additional treatment strategies to sustain remission and improve survival [15].

A major shift in the treatment of CLL has occurred with the introduction of BTK inhibitors, marking a move away from traditional chemotherapy and CITs toward targeted therapies such as BTK inhibitors and BCL-2 antagonists (venetoclax), either as monotherapy or in combination with monoclonal antibodies [16]. In 2014, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved ibrutinib, a first-generation BTK inhibitor, for the treatment of relapsed or refractory CLL [17]. This was followed by approval of ibrutinib in 2016 as a first-line treatment, either as a monotherapy or in combination with monoclonal antibody agents, for treatment-naïve CLL patients [18]. Subsequently, second-generation BTK inhibitor acalabrutinib was approved in 2019, and more recently zanubrutinib in 2023, both presenting more favourable safety profiles [19, 20].

Since the approval of BTK inhibitors and the establishment of safety and efficacy, the international clinical guidelines were significantly updated, introducing ibrutinib and acalabrutinib as the treatment of choice for first-line treatment of CLL patients regardless of age, comorbidity, and 17p deletion/TP53 mutation status [13, 21]. Currently in South Africa, multiple effective therapy options are available for CLL patients, particularly BTK inhibitors with ibrutinib being in use since 2017, acalabrutinib being approved in 2022 and zanubrutinib, which became available in late 2024.

The choice of treatment for CLL significantly influences the overall cost of care. Bruton's tyrosine kinase inhibitor treatments are typically administered continuously until disease progression or the onset of intolerable side effects, unlike fixed-duration chemotherapy regimens [22, 23]. While these agents offer substantial clinical advantages, their prolonged use and high acquisition costs pose a considerable financial burden on the public healthcare system as well as on patients through out-of-pocket expenses and potential treatment interruptions [24].

Furthermore, in South Africa the management of CLL is complicated by the country’s unique epidemiological profile, including a younger patient population and the presence of reported comorbidities in CLL patients such as HIV, which was reported to affect 3.75% of patients [25]. Additionally, in South Africa, systematic issues such as limited cytogenetic abnormality testing (available in only 37% of cases), frequent treatment interruptions, poor adherence driven by high out-of-pocket expenses, and the need for patients to travel long distances to reach tertiary care centres contribute to increased risks of disease progression [25]. Although BTK inhibitors are preferred as first-line therapies, cytogenetic testing for TP53 aberrations and IGHV mutational status remain essential for risk stratification and guiding treatment decisions, including in the relapsed/refractory setting where these markers influence prognosis and response to therapies like BTK inhibitors or venetoclax-based regimens [26–30]. These factors emphasise the importance of developing treatment approaches that are both cost effective and accessible, and that are adapted to the realities of the South African healthcare context. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the cost effectiveness of ibrutinib, acalabrutinib, and zanubrutinib for both patients with untreated and relapsed or refractory CLL in South Africa.

Methods

Model Overview

Partitioned survival models were developed from a South African public healthcare provider perspective to assess the cost effectiveness of ibrutinib compared with acalabrutinib and zanubrutinib for the treatment of CLL. A naïve indirect comparison was conducted as no head-to-head trials compare the three BTK inhibitors directly; clinical inputs were derived independently from pivotal trial survival curves for each agent.

Recently, partitioned survival models have become the most common model structures applied to cost-effectiveness analyses of cancer treatments [31]. The partitioned survival model was selected because it allows for the direct use of clinical trial survival curve data (e.g., progression free survival [PFS] and overall survival [OS]) to estimate health state membership over time, avoiding the need for complex transition probability calculations [31]. Additionally, it is well-suited to scenarios where OS data are immature, as it minimises additional uncertainty and ensures that the model closely follows observed trial outcomes.

Two distinct patient populations were modelled separately: one for patients with untreated CLL initiating first-line therapy and another for patients with relapsed or refractory disease. For each patient group, the three treatment strategies were evaluated independently, resulting in a total of six models.

The models included 3 health states: PFS, progressed disease, and death (Fig. 1). This three-state model is consistent with published literature evaluating CLL treatments [32–36], and aligns with ESMO and NCCN guidelines [13, 21, 37]. A cycle length of 1 month (28 days) was used as it aligns with CLL standard dosing and monitoring schedules for BTK inhibitor and monthly cycles of subsequent therapy (venetoclax plus rituximab) in the progressed state. A 10-year time frame was chosen to reflect a lifetime horizon for most CLL patients, capturing the majority of PFS and OS events while minimising uncertainty from long-term extrapolations, particularly in the relapsed or refractory (R/R) setting where median survival is 3–5 years [11, 38, 39]. Costs and effects were discounted at an annual discount rate of 5%, in line with South African Health Technology Assessment (HTA) guidelines [40]. The model outcomes included total costs, life-years (LYs) and quality-adjusted life years (QALYs). The models were developed and analysed using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA).Fig. 1Partitioned survival model structure. In this model, it was assumed that patients enter the model in the PFS state and receive one of the evaluated BTK inhibitors. In each monthly cycle, patients can remain in their current state or transition to a more severe health state. The progressed disease state captures the time from first treatment relapse until death. Transition to the absorbing death state is possible from either the PFS or progressed disease state and includes all-cause mortality. Furthermore, grade 3/4 treatment-induced adverse events (AEs) were assumed to occur only in the first cycle of the PFS state and were captured through one-off costs (no disutilities applied), consistent with oncology published models. It was assumed that all patients would be in only one health state in any 1-month single cycle.

Patient Population and Treatment Interventions

The patient population base-case characteristics for the modelled cohorts, including age and sex distributions, were informed by data from five pivotal clinical trials (Supplementary Table 1). The modelled patient cohorts of untreated CLL patients were based on the RESONATE-2 trial (NCT01722487) for ibrutinib versus chlorambucil, the ELEVATE-TN trial (NCT02475681) for acalabrutinib versus chlorambucil ± obinutuzumab, and the SEQUOIA trial (NCT03336333) for zanubrutinib versus bendamustine plus rituximab in high-risk patients or chlorambucil ± obinutuzumab [41–43]. For the relapsed or refractory CLL patient population, data were obtained from the head-to-head ALPINE trial (NCT03734016) for ibrutinib versus zanubrutinib, and the ASCEND trial (NCT02970318) for acalabrutinib versus investigator's choice (idelalisib + rituximab or bendamustine + rituximab) [44, 45]. The treatment strategies evaluated in the models for both patient populations were ibrutinib (420 mg once daily), acalabrutinib (100 mg twice daily) and zanubrutinib (160 mg twice daily), based on the dosing regimens from the reference clinical trials.

Compared to South African CLL patients (mean diagnosis age 62.6 years) [3], trial populations in the untreated setting were slightly older (median 70–73 years), while R/R populations were more comparable (median 67–68 years). In the R/R trials (ASCEND and ALPINE), patients had received 1–5 prior therapies, primarily alkylating agents, anti-CD20 antibodies, or chemoimmunotherapy; no patients had prior venetoclax exposure, as these trials predated its widespread approval [44, 45].

Clinical Inputs

Survival estimates were sourced from respective clinical trials depending on the treatment strategy. Published Kaplan–Meier (KM) curves for PFS and OS were digitised using Plot Digitizer (Free Software Foundation Inc., Boston, MA) to reconstruct the patient-level data. The patient-level data were extrapolated using parametric distributions over the model time horizon following the extrapolation algorithms of Guyot, Ades [46]. The data points were fitted to the possible parametric distributions, including the exponential, Weibull, lognormal, log-logistic, Gompertz, and generalised gamma distributions. Fitted parametric survival distributions were visually confirmed against observed KM curves (Supplementary Figures S2–S7).

The best fit parameter distribution was chosen using the goodness-to-fit criteria, including Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian information criterion (BIC), with lower values indicating a better fit, and visual judgement for inclusion in the models (Supplementary material Table 2) [33]. The assessment of the best fit for the models was based on up to 42–60 months’ follow-up data for all three strategies. It was determined that the best distribution was Gompertz for PFS of ibrutinib and acalabrutinib, whereas exponential was the best distribution for PFS of zanubrutinib for both models. Additionally, the exponential distribution offered the best fit for extrapolating the OS data of all strategy groups. Due to heterogeneity in trial designs, patient populations (e.g., varying proportions of high-risk features), and comparator arms, a formal indirect treatment comparison was not pursued. Instead, a naïve (unadjusted) indirect comparison of trial-specific survival curves was performed, consistent with approaches in similar cost-effectiveness evaluations of BTK inhibitors in CLL, particularly in resource-limited settings where existing indirect comparisons indicate broadly similar efficacy across agents [47–49].

Costs and Utility Values

The total medical costs associated with drug treatment, drug administration, pre-medication and supportive care, routine follow-ups via consultations, laboratory tests, and management of AEs were included in this study (Table 1). Costs related to terminal or palliative care were not included, as specialised palliative services remain scarce and unevenly distributed in the South African public sector, with limited integration into oncology care pathways [50]. This omission is consistent with standard practice in published CLL cost-effectiveness evaluations, where progressed disease state costs and utilities are considered sufficient to reflect end-of-life resource use and differences across comparators are minimal [48]. Patients were assumed to receive standard doses of ibrutinib, acalabrutinib and zanubrutinib based on the dosing regimens from the reference clinical trials. The standard doses for BTK inhibitors are considered to be indefinite until disease progression or unacceptable toxicity. All patients in the progressed disease state were assumed to receive venetoclax plus rituximab (Ven+R). This regimen was chosen based on NCCN guidelines as the treatment of choice for second-line treatment in progressed disease and for relapsed or refractory disease and because it was not included in the untreated first-line therapies or relapsed or refractory patient strategies [21, 51]. Since venetoclax is known to induce rapid apoptosis of CLL cells and tumour reduction, there is potential risk of patients developing tumour lysis syndrome (TLS) [52]. To account for this in the models, it was assumed that all patients in the progressed disease (PD) stage received a TLS prophylaxis regimen prior to the initial dose of Ven+R. During the treatment phase, patients were assumed to undergo monthly routine follow-up, which included specialist consultations and standard blood tests. Routine laboratory monitoring consisted of full blood count, biochemistry (including renal function tests with urea and creatinine), liver function tests, and lactate dehydrogenase, consistent with recommended monitoring for patients receiving BTK inhibitors and venetoclax-based regimens, as outlined in ESMO and NCCN clinical practice guidelines [13, 21, 37]. These investigations were assumed to be performed monthly during active treatment in both health states, in line with guideline-based follow-up schedules.

For those in the PD state, additional monitoring included an initial CT scan, bone marrow biopsy, and cytogenetic testing, followed by regular monthly consultations with a specialist and laboratory evaluations. Laboratory monitoring in the PD state followed the same monthly schedule, with additional baseline investigations related to venetoclax initiation and TLS risk assessment, as recommended by ESMO and NCCN guidelines [13, 21, 37]. Unit costs for laboratory investigations were applied according to these guideline-recommended testing frequencies within each health state.

For AE costs, the type and incidence rate of AEs were from respective reference clinical trials (Supplementary material Table 3) [41–45]. Only serious AEs with grade ≥ 3 and that occurred with a frequency of at least 5% were considered as one-off costs proportionally to trial-reported incidence rates; no recurring AE costs, disutilities, or survival impacts were assumed, in line with approaches in similar BTK inhibitor evaluations. Furthermore, the models exclude any indirect costs and costs related to intensive supportive care during a terminal phase of the disease.

The drug prices were determined from the database of medicine prices [53]. The consultation and hospitalisation costs were obtained from the SA uniform patient fee schedule (UPFS) [54]. The costs of laboratory tests were extracted from National Health Laboratory Services (NHLS) of South Africa price lists and inflated to 2024 prices [55]. All costs are presented in USD and converted to the average 2024 exchange rate (US$1 = ZAR17.85).

Health state utility values for patients with CLL were used to generate QALYs. Utility values generally range between 0 (death) and 1 (perfect health) [56]. Due to the lack of published South Africa-specific data on CLL health utilities and the absence of robust, treatment-line–specific estimates for BTK inhibitor therapy in the first-line setting, the utility values for the PFS and PD health states were based on those from NICE Technology Appraisal 561, guidance for venetoclax with rituximab for previously treated CLL [57]. These values were assumed to be applicable across treatment lines, as health-related quality of life (HRQoL) is primarily driven by disease state (progression-free vs progressed disease) rather than line of therapy, and were applied consistently across both patient populations for all treatment strategies. No utility decrements were applied for AEs, consistent with the modelling assumption that only serious AEs were included as transient, one-off events with limited long-term impact on HRQoL.

Cost-Effectiveness Analyses

The cost-effectiveness analyses (CEAs) comparing ibrutinib, acalabrutinib, and zanubrutinib were performed by estimating the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER). The effectiveness (LY and QALY) and total costs for each treatment were calculated over the 10-year time horizon. The ICER was calculated as the incremental cost per LYs and QALYs gained between treatment strategies in the base-case analyses.

To determine cost effectiveness, a willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold of $3407 per QALY gained was applied. This value is based on the empirical opportunity cost estimate by Edoka and Stacey [59], which corresponds to 0.53 times South Africa’s 2024 GDP per capita (GDP per capita = $6428). This threshold reflects the marginal productivity of the South African public healthcare system, which is similar to earlier modelling by Woods, Revill [60]. Although derived using disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs), these estimates provide a useful point of comparison for cost-effectiveness interpretation, given the conceptual similarity to QALYs. Therefore, in practice, most countries use the opportunity cost DALY value for QALY as well.

Sensitivity Analysis

Deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analyses were conducted to validate the robustness of the models when the results vary across a reasonable range. Deterministic one-way sensitivity analyses were performed by adjusting individual model inputs within specified ranges to assess their impact on the ICER estimates. Where available, parameters were adjusted within their 95% confidence intervals (CIs); otherwise, a ± 50% variation from the base-case value was applied, consistent with the South African HTA guidelines [40]. The discount rates were varied between 0 and 10% for the analysis as per the HTA methods guide [40].

The probabilistic sensitivity analyses were performed using 1000 Monte Carlo simulations to evaluate how simultaneous alterations in multiple parameters affected the model outcomes based on the assumed statistical distribution form of parameters. Specifically, gamma distributions were applied to costs and beta distributions were applied to utility values. The resulting outputs were used to generate cost-effectiveness scatter plots, illustrating the range and distribution of incremental costs and QALYs. Additionally, cost-effectiveness acceptability curves (CEACs) were plotted to estimate the probability of each treatment being cost effective across a range of WTP ($/QALY) thresholds.

Scenario Analysis

To assess the robustness of the model’s conclusions, a series of scenario analyses were conducted. The first scenario explored the impact of potential reductions in drug acquisition costs, applying hypothetical price reductions of 10%, 20%, and 30% for each treatment strategy. The second set of scenarios evaluated the effect of alternative analytic time horizons: a shorter 5-year period and a longer 15-year period (applied to the untreated CLL population only) against the 10-year base case. The 15-year horizon was not applied to the relapsed/refractory population due to the short median OS in this setting (3–5 years), where the vast majority of patients (> 95%) reach the death state by year 10 [11]. Lastly, the third scenario evaluated the effect of lowering the utility scores from 0.60 to 0.50 in the progressed disease state, as progressed CLL patients may have worse quality of life in real-world public settings. All other model inputs remained unchanged.

Results

Base-Case Analysis

The cost-effectiveness outcomes of ibrutinib, acalabrutinib, and zanubrutinib for both patient populations over the 10-year time horizon are summarised in Table 2. Among patients with untreated CLL, total costs per patient were estimated at $52,622 for ibrutinib, $54,239 for acalabrutinib, and $43,536 for zanubrutinib. Survival outcomes were 10.59 LYs and 7.85 QALYs for ibrutinib; 11.93 LYs and 8.61 QALYs for acalabrutinib; and 10.94 LYs and 8.16 QALYs for zanubrutinib, respectively. Compared with ibrutinib, acalabrutinib was associated with an incremental cost of $1617, yielding an ICER of $1206 per LY gained and $2124 per QALY gained. Scenario Analyses

Zanubrutinib remained the dominant treatment strategy across all scenario analyses, including 10–30% drug price reductions, a shorter 5-year time horizon, a longer 15-year time horizon (untreated population only) and a lower utility value for the progressed disease state. Acalabrutinib consistently remained cost effective under all conditions, although ICERs increased with drug price reductions and remained within the acceptable threshold under both a shorter and extended time horizon. All ICERs remained below the WTP threshold of $3407 per QALY, supporting the robustness of the base-case conclusions. These trends were consistent across both untreated and R/R CLL populations. Discussion

Breakthroughs in targeted cancer therapies, like BTK inhibitors, are offering increased hope for leukaemia patients by significantly improving survival rates and quality of life, particularly for those with previously poor prognoses [61]. Although the introduction of BTK inhibitors has substantial benefits, it is accompanied by increased expenditures, which is in direct competition with other health and social spending. With the recent availability of zanubrutinib, this study represents the first cost-effectiveness evaluation comparing ibrutinib, acalabrutinib, and zanubrutinib for both untreated and R/R CLL patients in South Africa. This comparison relied on naïve (unadjusted) indirect comparisons of survival data from separate pivotal trials, as no direct head-to-head evidence exists across all three agents.

Across both patient populations, zanubrutinib emerged as the dominant strategy, offering superior health outcomes at lower cost compared to ibrutinib. In the untreated population, it resulted in 0.32 more QALYs and cost savings of $9086 per patient; in the R/R group, it yielded 0.35 additional QALYs with $6052 in savings. Acalabrutinib was also cost effective compared with ibrutinib, with ICERs of $2124 and $2104 per QALY in the respective models, both falling well below the South Africa-specific WTP threshold of $3407 per QALY, based on Edoka and Stacey's opportunity cost estimate [59]. When acalabrutinib was compared to zanubrutinib, it produced greater health gains but at higher costs, resulting in ICERs of $24,010 per QALY in untreated patients and $33,009 per QALY in R/R patients, exceeding the South African opportunity cost-based threshold. This indicates that while acalabrutinib may be cost effective relative to ibrutinib, zanubrutinib remains the preferred option when the two newer BTK inhibitors are compared.

The deterministic sensitivity analyses confirmed that drug acquisition costs were the primary drivers of cost effectiveness, followed by costs during the progression-free state. This emphasises the critical role that pricing strategies play in shaping the cost-effectiveness landscape of oncology therapeutics in resource-limited settings. The probabilistic sensitivity analyses showed zanubrutinib had the highest probability of being cost effective, with 61–65% likelihood across models. Scenario analyses further validated its dominance under varying price, time horizons, and utility assumptions.

Thus far, few economic evaluations have been conducted to assess the cost effectiveness of BTK inhibitors for the treatment of CLL [36, 48, 62–65]. Our results are consistent with international studies that demonstrate the clinical and economic value of second-generation BTK inhibitors, particularly acalabrutinib and zanubrutinib, over ibrutinib. In untreated CLL patients, our results are consistent with a US-based study by Alrawashdh et al [48], which identified zanubrutinib as the dominant strategy and acalabrutinib as cost effective due to improved PFS compared to ibrutinib. Similarly, a real-world analysis by Roeker et al [66] found lower discontinuation rates and longer treatment persistence with acalabrutinib, contributing to better clinical and economic outcomes. Further in the R/R setting, evaluations from both the USA and China support the cost effectiveness of zanubrutinib, largely due to its improved safety profile and lower rates of AEs [36, 63]. Brown et al [62] further emphasised the favourable cardiac safety of zanubrutinib, which may reduce downstream healthcare costs.

However, not all evidence is consistent. An early UK analysis by Vreman et al [65] found acalabrutinib not to be cost effective compared to ibrutinib due to uncertainty around survival and utility estimates. Additionally, Liu and Han [64], using a Chinese health system perspective, reported acalabrutinib to be more cost effective than zanubrutinib in the R/R population, driven by survival differences. Differences in cost-effectiveness outcomes compared with international studies arise not only from South Africa-specific public-sector drug acquisition costs (particularly more favourable pricing for zanubrutinib), but also from lower routine care and monitoring costs, the use of a lower WTP threshold reflecting local opportunity cost, and methodological choices such as naïve indirect comparison of trial survival data and the application of consistent utility values across lines of therapy.

These discrepancies underscore the importance of country-specific analyses, including local prices, clinical practice, and cost-effectiveness thresholds. Importantly, our study incorporates South Africa-specific public sector cost data and applies a WTP threshold reflecting the country’s opportunity cost of health spending. In this context, zanubrutinib offers a compelling value proposition. Acalabrutinib also presents a viable alternative, especially in cases where zanubrutinib is unavailable or patients have contraindications.

Although this study presents a robust economic evaluation, several limitations remain. Real-world effectiveness data for BTK inhibitors in the South African context are lacking, and assumptions had to be made based on international trial data. Trial populations were generally older than typical South African CLL patients in the untreated setting (median trial age 70–73 compared to reported mean 62.6 years in South Africa) [3], while R/R ages were more aligned; this may slightly overestimate costs or underestimate tolerance in younger South African patients, although sensitivity analyses confirmed robustness. Additionally, the model relied on a naïve (unadjusted) indirect comparison of survival data from separate clinical trials for each BTK inhibitor, which may introduce bias due to differences in study design and populations. However, this approach is common in cost-effectiveness analyses of BTK inhibitors, and published matching-adjusted indirect comparisons indicate broadly similar efficacy across ibrutinib, acalabrutinib, and zanubrutinib, supporting the validity of our findings in this resource-limited setting [47, 49, 67]. Extensive sensitivity analyses further address this uncertainty by testing the impact of input variability on model outcomes, confirming the robustness of conclusions. Furthermore, the analyses only considered AEs of grade 3/4 events with a frequency of ≥ 5%, potentially underestimating the overall burden of toxicity. Nonetheless, the sensitivity analysis showed that variations in AE probabilities had minimal impact on the overall results. Lastly, the study did not consider the impact of co-morbidities on the treatment pathway for each treatment strategy; the exclusion of this consideration was due to a lack of available data. Future studies should incorporate real-world evidence and broader scenarios to validate and refine these results.

Conclusion

Zanubrutinib may represent a cost-saving and clinically superior option for both untreated and R/R CLL patients compared with ibrutinib in South Africa’s public healthcare setting. Acalabrutinib may also represent a cost-effective alternative compared with ibrutinib, offering improved modelled outcomes at an acceptable additional cost. These findings support the prioritisation of zanubrutinib access, while highlighting the value of acalabrutinib in specific patient scenarios. Future research should incorporate local real-world data to inform national reimbursement and treatment policy decisions.
