Development and characterization of novel magnesium oxide nanoparticle-impregnated chitosan-based guided tissue regeneration membrane – An in vitro study
Authors: Johnisha Harris, Kaarthikeyan Gurumoorthy
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Although a lot of commercially available guided tissue regeneration (GTR) membranes are used, none of them could actually ensure complete bone regeneration so far and they also have certain limitations. This study aims to explore further and develop a membrane that might overcome these limitations and aid in bone regeneration for the treatment of bony defects.Materials and Methods:Magnesium oxide nanoparticles (MgONPs) were prepared from magnesium nitrate prepared by calcination at different temperatures and dried using filter paper under specific temperature. Later, 0.3 ml of 0.2 M 1% acetic acid was added to water and placed in the stirrer for at least 1 h. Chitosan (CS) (2%) of two different concentrations containing 0.588 g and 0.576 g of CS, respectively, was prepared and added to the previous mixture. To these concentrations, the prepared MgONPs were added and stirred using a magnetic stirrer for 1 h. Later, it was cast in the mold and dried. The prepared membrane was immersed in 1 M sodium hydroxide to neutralize acetic acid. After preparation, they were subjected to scanning electron microscope (SEM) analysis, energy-dispersive X-ray (EDAX), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), and contact angle test.Results:In SEM analysis, spherical in size, uniformly dense, and porous agglomeration was noticed. EDAX and FTIR revealed the formation of MgONPs (magnesium oxide) in the membrane. The average contact angles of the CS with MgONPs and control membranes were 85.48° and 80.80°, respectively. Degradation analysis showed that test membrane showed a slower degradation rate than control collagen membrane at the end of the 28th day.Conclusion:On comparing membranes with pure CS, membranes with nanoparticles, and control collagen membranes, the membrane incorporated with nanoparticles showed more favorable positive outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Periodontitis is a severe form of inflammatory disease that affects the tissues surrounding the teeth, which includes the gingiva, cementum, periodontal ligament, as well as alveolar bone. Periodontitis occurs when the bacteria in dental plaque multiply and release toxins that irritate the gingival tissues. The immune system responds by initiating an inflammatory response, causing the gingiva to lose its attachment from the teeth, and forms periodontal pocket. Periodontal bone loss is one of the important characteristic signs of periodontitis. It is the gradual destruction and loss of the alveolar bone, which is the bone that surrounds and supports the teeth. This bone loss occurs as a result of chronic inflammation of periodontal tissues.[1]

When periodontitis progresses, the bacteria in dental plaque and the toxins they produce cause the immune system to mount a response, leading to inflammation. This inflammation triggers the release of substances that break down the connective tissues and bone that anchors the tooth. Over time, the continuous destruction of the alveolar bone can result in tooth mobility and eventual tooth loss.[2]

Periodontal therapy including scaling and root planing, antibiotic therapy, or surgical regenerative procedures is considered the gold standard in the management of periodontal bone loss. Based on the pattern of bone loss, they are classified as horizontal and vertical bone loss. Among these two, vertical/angular bone loss is considered ideal for bone grafting and guided tissue regeneration (GTR).[3]

GTR is a procedure attempting to regenerate lost periodontal tissues through differential tissue responses.[4] Although a lot of commercially available GTR membranes are used, none of them could actually ensure complete periodontal regeneration so far and they also have certain limitations.[5] This study aims to explore further and develop a membrane that might overcome at least some of these limitations and shed light on future research. One of the important polymers that is being explored lately is chitosan (CS). CS is a macromolecular polysaccharide obtained from the exoskeletons of crustaceans. It is studied widely as a vehicle in drug delivery systems and also in GTR due to its increased ability to provide bioavailability of drugs to targeted cells as well as sustained release.[6] The positive groups of the CS molecule interact with the negatively charged groups of the bacterial cells, thereby destroying the pathogens. This contributes to the bacteriostatic property of CS. However, it shows many limitations such as unstable degradation rate and less stability.[7] Our aim is to modify CS with additives like magnesium oxide nanoparticles (MgONPs) which are known for their osteogenic potential,[8] thereby enhancing the properties of the material for its application in periodontal bone regeneration.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A simple solvent casting technique was used for the preparation of the membrane. Initially, MgONPs were prepared from magnesium oxide and nitric acid. Aqueous ammonia was added to the magnesium nitrate solution in an incremental manner. By precipitating the magnesium hydroxide formed and calcinating it at different temperatures, the final precipitate was received. It was then washed with distilled water and dried on filter paper. These prepared MgONPs were later added during the casting of membrane.[9]

0.3 ml of acetic acid was taken in a 10 ml test tube and added to 29.7 ml of water in a 100 ml beaker to form 0.3 N acetic acid. This mixture was placed in the stirrer for at least 1 h. CS (2%) of low molecular weight was dissolved in 0.2 M 1% acetic acid by heating to 50°C for 1 h. Glycerol was also added at a concentration of 0.2 ml/g to this prepared solution to attain homogeneity. To this mixture, MgONPs were dissolved in two different concentrations by stirring for at least 24 h. The first one contained 0.24 g and the second set contained 0.12 g of magnesium oxide (MgO), respectively. The desired volume of this solution was taken and cast in Petri dishes. Later, it was dried at 50°C for about 10 h. Then, the mold which contained the dried membrane was later immersed in 1 M sodium hydroxide solution for about 5 h thoroughly to completely neutralize the acetic acid. The membrane was then rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline thrice for 15 min and later with distilled water to pH = 7 and dried at 50°C [Figure 1].

Steps in fabrication of membrane – (a and b) Measurement of chitosan and magnesium oxide nanoparticles (MgONPs), (c) Mixture of chitosan, MgONPs placed in stirrer for 24 h, (d) Cast membrane immersed in 1 M NaOH, (e and f) Final prepared membranes dried at 50°C

A scanning electron microscope (SEM) was used to observe the surface and cross-section morphology of the test membranes. The cross-section of the sample was prepared by fracturing the sample with liquid nitrogen before morphology observations. The samples were platinum sputter coated using a sputter coater (Jeol JSM-IT800 platinum sputter).

The membranes were characterized by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) by attenuated total reflectance (ATR) mode. The wave number range was 4000–5000/cm2, at a resolution of 1 cm−1 with an average of 64 scans. The equipment used was Bruker Alpha II – compact Fourier transform infrared spectrometer with platinum ATR.

The elemental composition of a sample was detected using a SEM and energy-dispersive X-ray (EDAX). Elements with atomic numbers higher than boron can be found with EDX, and they can be found at concentrations of at least 0.1%. The evaluation and identification of materials, contaminant identification, spot detection analysis of areas up to 10 cm in diameter, quality control screening, and other tasks were all included in the use of EDX. The materials in a standard SEM interact with the electron beam upon collision, releasing distinctive X-rays. The components in the sample must be recognized to be differentiated; the X-ray continuum is not essential for this process. The energy-dispersive spectrometer will record the distinctive X-ray for the purpose of measuring the elemental composition in the specimen.

The tensile tests were done using a dynamic universal testing machine (Instron ElectroPuls E3000) with specimen dimensions 150 mm × 10 mm and a distance of 100 mm was present between the grips initially. A tensile stress was applied at a crosshead speed of 5 mm/min until the specimen broke. The elongation break was recorded in percentage, while the tensile strength and tensile strain were recorded in units of MPa.

The contact angle analysis was done using an Ossila goniometer with an angle range of 5–180°. Wettability and surface tension analysis could be carried out. We have analyzed the wettability of the synthesized GTR membranes.

A hydrolytic degradation test was used to determine the membranes’ rate of deterioration. Through the observation of changes in the membrane’s weight and morphology across various time intervals, this test tracks the behavior of the scaffold and membranes in phosphate-buffered solution (PBS) at 37°C. The test membrane’s initial weight was determined on day 0 by a digital scale with 0.0001 g precision. All samples were then submerged in PBS and allowed to incubate at 37°C. On day 7, the membranes were removed and allowed to dry in a desiccator for 15 min. Afterward, the weight of each sample was determined. Subsequently, these samples were incubated at 37°C and submerged once more in sterile PBS. On days 7, 14, 21, and 28, these steps were repeated to assess the rate of degradation. All the values were tabulated.

The degradation rate was determined by the following formula:

Gravimetric weight loss (%) = (Wi − Wf)/Wi × 100

Wi = Initial weight of the membrane before incubating in PBS

Wf = Final weight of the membrane after each day of assessment (7, 14, 21, and 28 days).

DISCUSSION

The periodontal tissues which are destroyed during the progression of periodontal disease do not possess the innate ability to regenerate on their own. This has led to the search of biomaterials which will aid in the complete regeneration of the periodontal tissues. Over the past few decades, though various biomaterials have been developed, the development of ideal material for periodontal regeneration is still in progress. This search has led to our present study to develop a novel magnesium nanoparticle-impregnated CS-based membrane and to assess its efficacy in bone regeneration.

CS is a partially deacetylated derivative from chitin and composed of glucosamine and N-acetyl-glucosamine which is known for its biocompatible, biodegradable, and osteoconductive properties. They also possess bacteriostatic properties but with a favorable degradation rate and less integration with the surrounding tissues. The low strength and flexibility limit its application in bone regeneration, especially near load-bearing bones. To overcome this shortcoming, additives like nanoparticles were added to them to enhance and achieve the required physical and biological properties. In our study, we have added MgONPs to CS membrane due to its osteogenic properties.[8]

Liu et al. showed that MgO-incorporated membranes help in promoting functional restoration and modulating inflammation. The incorporation of MgO does not influence the configuration of the membrane, but changes in cell behavior were noticed.[10] However, the mechanism behind the maturation of collagen which is boosted by MgO is not clear yet. Similarly, Almontasser et al. showed that MgO exhibited strong antibacterial activity and this could be due to the destructive effects of active oxygen to damage the bacterial cell membrane and eventually bacterial death. The scaffolds are required to degrade after implantation due to its necessity to enhance the new bone formation.[11] Even though CS is widely used as a biomaterial due to its perfect biocompatibility and biological activities, it has demerits like rapid degeneration and poor mechanical properties which affect its ability to be used in long-term success.

In our present study, the mechanical properties such as tensile strength and degradation rate of the synthesized membrane were analyzed in comparison with the conventional collagen membrane. The tensile strength of membrane with MgONPs (high concentration) showed higher tensile strength of 63.82 ± 2.04 MPa than membrane with lower concentration of MgONPs which had a tensile strength of 46.83 ± 5.78 MPa. According to Indrani et al., the tensile strength of CS membrane was 8.97 ± 3.21 MPa which is much lesser than our test groups. This proves that the addition of MgONPs evidently increases the tensile strength when compared to the pure CS membrane.[12] Moreover, the tensile strength of our test group membrane is higher than that of conventional collagen membrane 31.1 ± 1.24 MPa. The test group membrane showing the highest tensile strength was then subjected to analysis of degradation rate and contact angle along with the conventional collagen membrane.

On analysis of contact angles, the average contact angles of the test and control membranes were 85.48° and 80.80°, respectively. Based on the results, the test as well as control membranes have proven to be equally hydrophilic. However, the contact angle of test membrane was higher than control collagen membrane making it more hydrophilic than the latter. This shows the significant impact of nanoparticles and their concentration on incorporation into the membrane. The degradation rates of test and control group membranes were assessed in vitro by incubating the membrane in pH 7.4 of PBS (6 ml). The changes in the weight of the membranes were measured in percentage on the 1st, 7th, 14th, and 28th days. The results revealed that the control group showed faster degradation with an accelerated loss of weight percentage of 36% on the 28th day than the test group where the loss of weight percentage was 29%. Faster degradation implies a faster loss of mechanical strength. The degradation of CS membrane was also around 23%–28% of initial weight after a 90-day in vitro shaking test. This shows that the addition of nanoparticles does not necessarily affect the degradation rates of the membranes.

Evidence have also proved that Mg2+ promotes osseous differentiation of stromal cells present in the human bone marrow by regulating Wnt, MAPK, and other signaling pathways.[13] MgONPs assist in the process of osteogenesis postgraft placement and also possess antibacterial property which plays a crucial role against periodontal pathogens.[14] MgONP formulation proved to be effective in enhancing bone formation, especially during the treatment of intrabony defects over long follow-up both histologically and in micro-CT.[15] The amount of new bone formation was more significant at 6 weeks than at 2 and 4 weeks postoperatively in the calvarial defects implanted with MgONPs as compared to the control.[16] Coaxial MgO-containing nanocellulose membranes show increased alkaline phosphatase activity, comparatively more mineralized nodule formation along with enhanced expression of osteogenic genes.[17]

MgONPs containing an aqueous solution of different concentrations were tested for toxicity in both in vivo and in vitro conditions. There was no statistical difference in the antibacterial activity among various concentrations especially postirrigation at 24, 48, 72, and 168 h in terms of number of colony-forming units.[18] MgO–PCL nanocomposite electro-spun fibers which were implanted in Sprague Dawley rats showed no adverse effects both in biochemical and hematological parameters with deviations not so far from the normal range showing its biocompatible nature. This proves the role of MgONPs in both soft- and hard-tissue augmentation.[19] However, the dosage was also essential, since too much of Mg2+ was proved to inhibit cell differentiation and proliferation.[20] These aspects of the biological activity of Mg nanoparticles in the GTR membrane have to be studied further to know the advantages and disadvantages which will help in the further development of the material according to the needs for periodontal regeneration.

CONCLUSION

The overall results show that the incorporation of MgONPs in CS has a positive influence on the characteristics of the membrane than conventional collagen membranes according to the results of the in vitro study. The membrane which has been attempted so far has only been tested under in vitro conditions. Hence, to validate its application in clinical practice, further animal studies have to be done. Collaboration between material scientists and clinicians can possibly lead to a better breakthrough toward understanding and achieving functional periodontal regeneration.
