Deep multi-instance learning model based on gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI for predicting microvascular invasion of hepatocellular carcinoma: a multicenter, retrospective study
Authors: Yi Luo, Ge Zhang, Shuyuan Zhong, Shaofang Chen, Changzheng Shi, Xianyue Quan, Xinming Li, Genwen Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveMicrovascular invasion (MVI) is of great significance for the individualized treatment of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and preoperative noninvasive prediction of MVI is still an urgent clinical problem. To explore the effects of different regions of interest (ROI) and image input dimensions on the performance of deep learning (DL) models, and to select the best result to develop and validate a DL model for preoperative prediction of MVI.Materials and methodsA total of 206 patients with pathologically confirmed HCC from three hospitals were retrospectively enrolled and divided into training, internal validation and external test set. Based on hepatobiliary phase images (HBP) of gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI, 2D DL, 3D DL and 2.5D deep multi-instance learning (MIL) models were established. The receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) was used to evaluate the predictive efficacy of the above models. Based on the optimal performance model, the T1WI-FS and T2WI-FS images were preprocessed correspondingly, and a multimodal prediction model including three sequences was constructed. The ROC, and decision curve were used to visualize the predictive ability of the model.ResultsCompared with 2D DL and 3D DL models, the 2.5D DL model based on all axial images of ROI had the highest performance, with the AUC values of 0.802 (95% CI, 0.669–0.936) and 0.759 (95% CI, 0.643–0.875) in the validation and test sets. The AUCs of the multimodal MRI model were 0.954 (95% CI, 0.920–0.989) in the training set, 0.857 (95% CI, 0.736–0.978) in the validation set, and 0.788 (95% CI, 0.681–0.895) in the test set.ConclusionThe DL model that selects all axial slices of intratumor and peritumor as input shows robust capability in predicting MVI, which is expected to help clinical decision-making of individualized treatment for HCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-14971-7.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Microvascular invasion, Gadoxetic acid, Magnetic resonance imaging, Deep learning

Introduction

Primary liver cancer is one of the most common clinical malignancies, the sixth most common cancer and the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide [1, 2]. Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) accounts for 75–85% of primary liver cancers and has a poor clinical prognosis [1, 3]. Microvascular invasion (MVI) is the invasion of microvessels by a tumor, defined as the presence of clusters of cancer cells in endothelium-lined vascular lumens that can only be observed microscopically [4]. MVI is an independent risk factor for recurrence and metastasis of HCC, and has a significant impact on the prognosis of patients [5]. In MVI-positive patients, enlarging the margins at the time of hepatectomy can result in a considerable increase in survival rates [6]. Therefore, preoperative prediction of MVI can help doctors more accurately assess the patient’s condition and prognosis, so as to develop a personalized surgical approach and post-operative treatment plan [7, 8].

Nowadays, the diagnosis of MVI relies on postoperative pathological examination, and preoperative prediction of MVI remains challenging. The development of non-invasive methods for predicting factors related to tumor prognosis has always been the focus of clinical research [9, 10]. As a non-invasive examination method that can cover the whole picture of the tumor, imaging examinations have become a key method for the preoperative evaluation of HCC. Among them, gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI may improve the diagnostic ability of lesions due to its specific imaging performance in hepatobiliary phase (HBP) [11, 12]. Moreover, quantitative radiomics analysis based on contrast-enhanced CT or MRI can improve the predictive accuracy of MVI [13–15]. In recent years, the development of deep learning (DL) in the medical field has provided new opportunities for accurate prediction of MVI. Compared to traditional radiomics methods, DL excels in automatically capturing complex patterns and structures within medical images, facilitating a comprehensive understanding of the high-dimensional relationships between imaging features and clinical outcomes [16]. Current studies have confirmed the important value of DL in the preoperative prediction of MVI in HCC [17, 18].

Two-dimensional (2D) or three-dimensional (3D) images can be selected for the training of DL models. The 2D DL technique selects the maximum cross slice of the ROI as this sample representative, while the 3D DL technique selects the entire ROI as the image input. The selection of input images varies across studies, with approaches including the largest tumor cross-slice [19] the largest tumor area along with adjacent upper and lower slices [20], or whole 3D images [21]. 2D DL primarily processes single-slice image data, making it easier to train and less data-intensive. However, it lacks spatial and inter-slice correlations, which may result in the loss of critical lesion information. 3D DL is computationally demanding, typically requiring larger datasets, greater computational resources, and longer processing times [22], posing challenges for medical research. Recently, the concept of 2.5D DL has emerged [23], utilizing multi-slice 2D images from different orientations as input. This approach preserves richer lesion information while allowing the use of simpler 2D convolutional neural networks (CNNs), thereby reducing computational complexity and mitigating model overfitting. Most previous studies have focused on evaluating the clinical utility of either 2D or 3D DL techniques for classification tasks, while the impact of different input image dimension selection strategies on model performance remains largely unexplored.

This study retrospectively collected HCC patient data from three hospitals and evaluated the predictive performance of 2D, 2.5D, and 3D DL models for MVI based on preoperative HBP imaging of gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI, aiming to determine the optimal image input dimension. Furthermore, a multimodal fusion model incorporating fat-suppressed T1WI (T1WI-FS), fat-suppressed T2WI (T2WI-FS), and HBP was developed, and its performance was validated using an external dataset.

Methods

Study cohort

A total of 206 patients with HCC from three hospitals were retrospectively collected, including 119 patients treated in Zhujiang Hospital of Southern Medical University (set1) and 70 patients treated in Shenzhen People’s Hospital (set2) from April 2016 to May 2023. And 17 patients treated in The First Affiliated Hospital of Jinan University from April 2023 to February 2024 (set3). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with histologically confirmed HCC after surgical resection; (2) gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI examination was performed within 1 month before surgery. Exclusion criteria were as follows: The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients who underwent radiotherapy, chemotherapy, radiofrequency ablation, or other anti-tumor treatments before MRI; (2) poor MR image quality impairing region of interest delineation. The 136 patients in set1 and set3 were randomly divided into training set and validation set according to 7:3, and set2 was used as an independent test set (Fig. 1).

Clinical features and construction of clinical models

The baseline clinical information of each patient was collected, including age, gender, platelet count (PLT), albumin (ALB), total bilirubin (TB), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT), prothrombin time (PT), alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) and whether they were infected with hepatitis B virus (HBV) and hepatitis C virus (HCV).

Histopathological examination of surgical specimens was performed blinded by pathologists, including tumor type and MVI grade. MVI was classified into three grades: M0 (no MVI), M1 (≤ 5 MVI foci within 1 cm of the tumor or adjacent tissue), and M2 (< 5 MVI foci or MVI in non-tumor tissue) [24]. MVI-negative was defined as M0, and MVI-positive was defined as M1 and M2.

Based on the clinical data of patients in the training set, the independent risk factors for positive MVI were screened by univariate and multivariate analysis. Logistic Regression (LR) was used to establish a clinical prediction model of MVI. The ROC was used to evaluate the diagnostic performance of the comprehensive model in the validation and test sets.

MRI data collection and image preprocessing

MRI examinations were performed at 1.5 T (Philips Multiva/GE Voyager/Siemens Avanto/United Imaging uMR 660) or 3.0 T (Philips Ingenia/Philips Elition X/Siemens Skyra) using various scanners. The specific scanning parameters are shown in Table 1. The gadoxetic acid (Primovist, Bayer Schering Pharma, Germany) was injected through an artificial venous high-pressure syringe at a dose of 0.025mmol/kg and a flow rate of 1 ml/s. HBP images were acquired 15–20 min after contrast agent injection.

A radiologist with more than 3 years of experience in abdominal imaging diagnosis independently delineated the tumor boundaries using the United Imaging uAI Research Portal (version: 20240430), and a senior radiologist with more than 20 years of experience in abdominal imaging diagnosis checked and corrected the ROIs. Two types of ROI were defined on T1WI-FS, T2WI-FS and HBP sequences. ROI1 was defined as the tumor area, while ROI2 was defined as the tumor and the 10 mm peritumoral area (not beyond the liver boundary) (Fig. 2). For images with multiple lesions, ROI delineation was performed on the largest lesion. To minimize the differences caused by different scanning devices and parameters, all images were resampled to 1 mm×1 mm×1 mm and were normalized by Z-score.

Development of DL models based on HBP

2D, 2.5D, and 3D DL models were constructed based on HBP images. In 2D DL, the input image was the maximum cross slice of the ROI (hereafter, 2D). The 2.5D DL models were constructed using four different input configurations: (1) the maximum cross-sectional image of the ROI along with one layer above and below it (referred to as 3 Slices); (2) the maximum cross-sectional image of the ROI with two layers above and below it (referred to as 5 Slices); (3) all axial slices through the tumor (referred to as 2.5D-X); and (4) all axial, coronal, and sagittal slices of the tumor (referred to as 2.5D-XYZ). In 3D DL, the input image is the Bounding cube of the ROI. The size of the cropped subregion image was resized to 224× 224 pixels using nearest neighbor interpolation, and ResNet50 was used for 2D and 2.5D models training. The 3D images and their corresponding labels were then used as input to the 3D CNN and the 3D model was trained using ResNet18. Real-time data augmentation techniques were used during the training phase, including horizontal and vertical flips and random cropping. The study utilized SGD (Stochastic Gradient Descent) as the optimizer, and softmax cross entropy as the loss function. The ResNet architectures used in the study had been pre-trained on the ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge 2012 (ILSVRC-2012) dataset.

Additionally, ROI2 was cropped to generate the above different dimensional image inputs, and separate 2D, 2.5D, and 3D DL models were again constructed for each set. In total, 12 DL models were developed based on HBP images. The overall workflow is illustrated in Fig. 3.

Construction of 2.5D DL models based on Multi-instance learning

The 2D and 3D models predict the probability of MVI for each patient, while the 2.5D models predict the probability of MVI for each MRI slice. Two feature extraction methods, Predict Likelihood Histogram (PLH) and Bag of Words (BoW), were used within the framework of Multi-instance Learning (MIL) to analyze all MRI slices from the same patient, ultimately determining the patient-level MVI probability. PLH that map out the predictive probabilities and labels for each slice provide a probabilistic summary of the prediction landscape. The BoW method was used to segment each image into slices and extract data to compile seven predictive results per sample, using the Term Frequency-nverse Document Frequency (TF-lDF) method for analysis. The features from PLH and BoW were combined to train conventional machine learning classifiers to predict MVI status of each patient.

Principal component analysis (PCA), t-test, Pearson’s correlation coefficient and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression were used to reduce the dimension of the extracted multi-instance learning features. MIL models were constructed using seven distinct machine learning algorithms: Logistic Regression (LR), LightGBM classifier, Random Forest (RF), ExtraTrees classifier, XGBoost classifier, Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), and Support Vector Machine (SVM). To ensure the robustness of the models, 5-fold cross-validation was performed on the training set and hyperparameters were optimized via Grid-Search. The model that provided the highest AUC was selected as the optimal patient-level MVI prediction model for each image dimension. The efficacy of each model was evaluated using ROC analysis, with the model exhibiting the highest AUC chosen as the final predictor.

Construction of the multimodal fusion model

The T1WI-FS and T2WI-FS images were preprocessed using the best-performing ROI and image input dimensions, which were identified based on the AUC values of the individual models described above. These three sequences (T1WI-FS, T2WI-FS, and HBP) were then fused at the feature level, followed by feature selection using t-tests, Pearson’s correlation coefficient, and LASSO regression. Machine learning models were then used to construct the final multimodal fusion model for predicting MVI status.

The efficacy of the multimodal fusion models was assessed using ROC curves on both the validation and test sets. Delong’s test was employed to compare the predictive performance of each model. To evaluate the clinical utility of the multimodal fusion models, decision curve analysis (DCA) was conducted by calculating the net benefit at various threshold probabilities across the combined validation and test sets. This analysis provided insights into the potential clinical applicability of the fusion models.

Statistical analysis

The baseline data of patients were evaluated using SPSS (version 21.0). Comparisons of categorical variables between the three sets were performed using Chi-square or Fisher’s tests, and comparisons between continuous variables were performed using one-way analysis of variance. The performance of the predictive models was assessed using the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) curves and AUC, accuracy, sensitivity and specificity. Deep learning data processing was all performed using Python version 3.7.12.

Results

Baseline characteristics of patients

A total of 206 patients (171 males, 35 females; mean age 56.53 ± 11.92 years) were enrolled, including 100 MVI-positive and 106 MVI-negative cases. There were 95 cases in the training set, 41 cases in the validation set, and 70 cases in the test set. The proportions of MVI-positive cases across these groups were comparable, with 50.53% (48/95) in the training set, 48.78% (20/41) in the validation set, and 45.71% (32/70) in the test set (p > 0.05). While PT showed statistically significant variation among the groups (p < 0.05), other demographic characteristics and laboratory parameters remained consistent across all datasets. Construction and efficacy evaluation of clinical models

In the training set, univariate and multivariate analyses of clinical and laboratory parameters were performed to identify significant predictors of MVI status. The results showed that ALB, ALP and GGT were independent risk factors for MVI. These variables were subsequently incorporated into a LR-based clinical predictive model. The results of ROC showed that the AUC values of the clinical model in the training set, validation set and test set were 0.783 (95%CI, 0.691–0.876), 0.757 (95%CI, 0.588–0.926) and 0.609 (95%CI, 0.474–0.743), respectively.

Comparison of 2D, 2.5D and 3D DL models based on HBP

In the DL models constructed based on ROI1, the AUC values for the 2D model are 0.700, 0.723, 0.704 in the training, validation and test sets, while the corresponding AUC values for the 3D model are 0.710, 0.612 and 0.548. In the DL models constructed based on ROI2, the AUC values for the 2D model are 0.731, 0.693, 0.672 in the training, validation and test sets, while the corresponding AUC values for the 3D model are 0.733, 0.756 and 0.729.

PLH and BoW were used to extract 100 features from the 2.5D model, respectively. Seven machine learning models were used for patient-level modeling, of which the NaiveBayes classifier demonstrated superior performance. Among the models established based on ROI1, the 2.5D-X model showed the highest performance, with AUC values of 0.777 in the validation set and 0.711 in the test set. The overall performance of the models based on ROI2 is improved compared to the model based on ROI1. The AUC values of the 2.5D-X model in the validation and test sets were 0.802 and 0.759, showing the best prediction efficiency, surpassing the 3 Slices (AUCval: 0.658, AUCtest: 0.570), 5 Slices (AUCval: 0.636, AUCtest: 0.663), and 2.5D-XYZ (AUCval: 0.719, AUCtest: 0.671) models (Table 3; Fig. 4). Construction and evaluation of multimodal model

The multimodal model exhibited superior discriminative performance, achieving the AUCs of 0.954 (95% CI, 0.920–0.989) in the training set, 0.857 (95% CI, 0.736–0.978) in the validation set, and 0.788 (95% CI, 0.681–0.895) in the test set (Fig. 6). These results significantly outperformed the clinical model’s predictive efficiency (p < 0.05 via Delong’s test). The calibration curve showed that the DL model confirmed good calibration in the validation and test sets (Figure S1). Further validation through DCA confirmed the clinical applicability of this preoperative MVI prediction model, yielding a net clinical benefit across a wide threshold probability range (Fig. 7).

Discussion

Accurate preoperative diagnosis of MVI holds strategic importance in optimizing risk stratification and guiding personalized therapeutic strategies for HCC patients. In this multicenter study, we developed a novel 2.5D DL model based on multi-instance learning, leveraging intratumoral and peritumoral images from gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI. The model demonstrated robust generalizability, maintaining diagnostic accuracy in an independent external validation cohort. Notably, this study found that all axial slices within ROI was identified as the optimal input strategy, achieving maximum predictive performance compared to selective slice sampling approaches.

Preoperative prediction of MVI in liver cancer has attracted much attention. Recent studies have confirmed the potential of DL and radiomics for MVI prediction [25–27]. Results of a previous prospective study suggest that the DL model of gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI has better sensitivity for predicting MVI than CT [17]. The basis for this may be that MVI can block microvessels around tumors, leading to hemodynamic alterations and impaired function of peritumoral hepatocytes, which in turn leads to reduced uptake of contrast by peritumoral organic anion-transporting peptide transporters [11, 12]. In this study, the AUC of the multimodal model in validation set and test set were 0.857 and 0.788, respectively, confirming the predictive efficacy and generalization ability. Meanwhile, our results showed that selecting ROI from both intratumoral and peritumoral regions had higher predictive value than tumor region only (AUCtest 0.759 VS. 0.711), which was consistent with the results of previous meta-analysis [28]. This can be explained by the fact that more than 85% of MVI occur in the peri-tumor area within 1 cm from the tumor border [29].

Despite considerable efforts to leverage DL for non-invasive MVI prediction, prior studies have primarily focused on evaluating the predictive value of either 2D or 3D DL techniques in isolation. Zhou et al. conducted a meta-analysis of 11 studies to assess the utility of CT-based radiomics for MVI prediction in HCC, demonstrating that Both 3D radiomics models and DL models outperformed their 2D counterparts [30]. However, despite their superior performance, 3D models demand approximately 20 times more computational memory than 2D models [22], and require larger datasets to effectively capture complex spatial features. In the absence of sufficient data, this can result in suboptimal performance or overfitting [31]. 2.5D DL techniques offer a balance between these two approaches, utilizing continuous slices to preserve spatial correlations within lesions while mitigating the computational burden associated with 3D modeling. Kim et al. constructed a 2.5D Desnet model based on lung CT, which outperformed the 3D DenseNet model in lung cancer prediction [32]. Similarly, our findings indicate that 2.5D DL models achieve comparable or even superior predictive performance relative to 3D DL models. Given these advantages, 2.5D DL techniques may serve as viable alternatives to 3D DL, particularly in settings with limited data and computational resources.

This study attempts to explore which image dimension gives better performance gain. Previous research suggests that incorporating multiple adjacent slices as input yields better results than a single-slice 2D input [33]. However, beyond a certain number of slices, additional input does not necessarily improve performance [34]. Our findings align with this observation, as models using continuous neighboring slices from the axial plane outperformed those using only 3 or 5 slices. Although models constructed using images from the X, Y, and Z axes achieved high AUC values (0.992-1.000) in the training set, their performance dropped significantly in the validation and test sets, indicating potential overfitting due to excessive input images. Another possible explanation is the inherent variability in MRI spatial resolution based on imaging protocols, where the X-axis often has a higher resolution than the Y- and Z-axes [34]. At the same time, for DL models with high-dimensional inputs, large samples are the key to ensure the robustness of the model and prevent overfitting. In the future, we will further expand the sample size to explore the application of the 2.5D-XYZ model. However, when data is limited, the DL model based on the continuous slice of X-axis could be the optimal choice.

A key challenge in 2.5D DL techniques is mapping the predicted probabilities of multiple slices to a single training sample, as the number of slices varies depending on tumor volume. In MIL, each training sample is treated as a bag containing multiple instances, with labels assigned at the bag level. A tumor is classified as MVI-positive if at least one slice within the tumor predicts MVI. This weakly supervised learning approach reduces the subjectivity and inaccuracies associated with manual tumor segmentation and effectively mitigates false negatives caused by intratumoral heterogeneity. While DL combined with MIL has been applied in pathology image analysis [35, 36], its use in radiological imaging remains limited. A recent study used MIL-based deep learning models to predict osteoporosis in cervical cancer patients undergoing external beam radiotherapy, which confirmed the effectiveness of multi-instance learning in fusing deep learning features [37]. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to employ this approach for preoperative MVI prediction in HCC.

There are still some limitations to this study. First, this study was a retrospective study, and the sample size of the third medical center dataset and the external test set is small. Prospective, large sample and multi-center data sets are still needed to further validate the effectiveness of the model. Second, the ROIs in this study were manually delineated by radiologists, which may cause a certain bias. The application of automatic segmentation tools will be explored in future studies. Third, similar to other deep learning models, our prediction model may also lack clinical interpretability, and we will further explore the value of interpretable machine learning models in predicting MVI in the following study. Finally, although the performance of the model has been validated in an independent external test set, there are still some obstacles to its clinical application, such as the challenges of generalization caused by different MRI scanners and the integration with clinical workflow.

In conclusion, the 2.5D deep learning model established by selecting all axial slices of the ROI (intratumoral and peritumoral regions) as input shows strong diagnostic efficacy in MVI prediction. MIL effectively addressed slice number variability arising from heterogeneous tumor volumes, enabling robust feature aggregation across imbalanced datasets. The 2.5D-MIL multimodal model demonstrated reliable diagnostic accuracy in an independent external validation, providing a potential non-invasive method for the preoperative evaluation of HCC. Prospective studies with external validation could provide strong clinical evidence for this prediction model, potentially enabling personalized surgical planning and adjuvant therapy stratification.
