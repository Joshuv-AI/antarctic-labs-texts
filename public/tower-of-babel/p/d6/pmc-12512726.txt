Sex-specific associations between household drinking water sources and depressive symptoms in middle-aged and older Chinese populations: evidence from one 8-year nationwide cohort
Authors: Yu Wu, Lei Zhang, Yihao Zhao, Panliang Zhong, Shuai Guo, Xiaoying Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUnstable access to safe drinking water may harm mental health, yet its impact by sex remains unclear. We aimed to explore sex-specific associations between household water sources and depressive symptoms (DS) in Chinese middle-aged and older adults.Methods 5,868 participants aged ≥ 40 years were selected from China Family Panel Studies (2012–2020). We used time-varying Cox models to assess the association between household water sources and DS. Group-based trajectory models were employed to identify water-use trajectories, and logistic regression was used to examine the relationship between trajectory groups and post-follow-up DS. Meanwhile, we quantified the increase in DS burden attributable to the use of unstable water.ResultsThere was a significant association between household water sources and DS, with notable differences between sexes. For females, relative to stable water, using unstable water was significantly associated with higher follow-up DS risk [hazard ratio (HR) = 1.260, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.116, 1.423], and this risk increased with greater water instability (cellar-stored water: HR = 1.240, 95% CI: 1.091, 1.409; surface-exposed water: HR = 1.403, 95% CI: 1.062, 1.853). However, these associations were not observed in males. Meanwhile, we identified three water-use trajectories by sex: compared to the constant stable water group, constant unstable water was significantly linked to post-follow-up DS, whereas switching from unstable to stable water reduced this risk in females but not in males. In addition, 5.42% of DS burden in females was attributed to unstable water use.ConclusionsUnstable household water sources were associated with an increased DS risk in females but not in males. Sustainable policies securing reliable water supply are needed to protect females’ mental health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24665-1.

Introduction

Depression, a major global health problem, is one of the most common psychiatric disorders [1, 2]. According to GBD 2021, depression resulted in 56.3 million years lived with disability (YLDs) globally, ranking second among the 25 leading level 3 causes of YLDs [2]. Currently, approximately 332 million individuals grapple with depression worldwide [2], with middle-aged and older adults identified as particularly high-risk populations [3].

Drinking water is a crucial determinant of population health and development [4]. Despite significant progress, unsafe drinking water remains a major global health issue, contributing to serious diseases and mortality [5–7]. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that billions of people lack access to safe drinking water and sanitation services, leading to 1.4 million fatalities and 74 million disability-adjusted life-years [4, 5]. These findings underscore the importance of a stable supply of safe drinking water [8].

Safe drinking water is typically defined by two key criteria: the safety of water quality, which ensures the absence of contaminants and suitability for consumption, and the supply stability for daily use, independent of seasonal or climatic variations [9, 10]. Numerous studies have investigated the relationship between water quality and mental health [8, 9, 11, 12]. Poor water sources are more susceptible to contamination by a range of pollutants, including bacteria, viruses, parasites, pesticides, antibiotics, excessive heavy metals and minerals, and emerging contaminants like microplastics, bisphenol A (BPA), and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) [5, 7, 13–21]. Previous studies have shown an association between long-term exposure to heavy metals, residual pesticides, chemical fertilizers, microbial infections, polystyrene microplastics, BPA, and PAHs and an increased risk of depression, potentially through mechanisms such as inflammation, disruptions in the gut microbiome, and neurotoxicity [22–27]. Specifically, pollutants like heavy metals and organophosphorus pesticides may trigger an overactive immune response when they enter the body, leading to chronic inflammation, which can damage cellular function and increase the risk of depression [22, 27, 28]. For instance, arsenic has been shown to activate immune cells, like macrophages, and promote the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, causing brain inflammation that impairs emotional regulation and may contribute to depression [23, 28]. Furthermore, these toxic substances can alter the composition and diversity of the gut microbiota, leading to microbial imbalances that disrupt gut immune function and even affect brain activity through the “gut-brain axis,” resulting in mood swings and depression [29]. In addition, these pollutants may directly affect the nervous system by interfering with neurotransmitter transmission, leading to nervous system dysfunction and potentially triggering depression [22]. For example, organophosphorus pesticides inhibit acetylcholinesterase activity, causing an accumulation of acetylcholine at synapses, which overactivates the nervous system and subsequently induces depression [22].

Most studies focus on specific substances in drinking water and their effects on mental health [9]. However, the stability of water supply and its association with mental health have been largely overlooked [9, 30]. As a daily necessity, unstable household water sources could cause disruptions and inconveniences, forcing residents to spend more time and effort securing safe water, which in turn may heighten concerns about future uncertainties and daily challenges [30]. Research has shown a strong link between household water sources and depression in middle-aged and older adults, with those using unstable water sources being more prone to depression compared to those with stable tap water [31]. Transitioning to a stable water source significantly reduces the risk of depression [31]. This underscores the crucial role of water supply stability in supporting mental health [31].

Notably, water insecurity may be associated with greater emotional distress in females [30]. One recent survey in rural Uganda found that residing in a water insecurity hotspot was associated with a higher risk of probable depression among females, but not males [32]. This suggests that unstable household water supplies may have sex-specific impacts on mental health, with females potentially being at higher risk. Males and females take on different roles in the household, with females usually spending more time at home and assuming more responsibilities such as cooking, cleaning, and childcare. We speculate that this situation makes females more vulnerable in terms of socio-economic status and more susceptible to external environmental influences like unstable water supply. However, to date, no studies have specifically examined sex-specific associations between unstable household water and depression among middle-aged and older populations. Given the unique roles of females in Chinese households, it is crucial to investigate the association between water instability with their mental health.

Therefore, this study aims to investigate sex-specific associations between household drinking water sources and depressive symptoms (DS) in middle-aged and older Chinese populations using a large, nationally representative cohort dataset. The findings would help increase attention to females’ mental health and provide insights for improving water supply policies.

Methods

Data sources and sample selection

The data utilized in this study were obtained from the China Family Panel Studies (CFPS), an annual longitudinal Survey with national representation administered by Peking University. The CFPS, initiated in 2010, has released six sets of public data collected in the years 2010, 2012, 2014, 2016, 2018, and 2020. The CFPS employed a multi-stage, probability proportional-to-size sampling approach to Survey approximately 15,000 households nationwide, including all members of the selected households [33]. The samples encompassed 25 provinces, representing 95% of China’s entire population. Peking University’s Biomedical Ethics Review Committee gave formal approval for data collection (approval number: IRB00001052-14010).

We utilized four-wave CFPS data (2012, 2016, 2018, and 2020) due to the consistency of DS measurement. The sample selection was governed by the following criteria: (1) a baseline age of 40 or older in 2012; (2) a follow-up duration of eight years; (3) completed measurements for household water and DS at all four follow-up waves; (4) absence of DS at baseline. In 2012, a total of 35,715 respondents were recruited. Of these, 15,603 participants aged 40 and above, who were free of DS, completed the baseline Survey. From this group, 6,980 participants completed the household water use survey during the follow-up period. We then excluded 1,112 individuals lacking follow-up DS assessments, yielding a final study cohort of 5,868 participants. Additionally, given that 236 respondents had missing baseline covariate data, we applied the missForest method for imputation [34]. MissForest is a non-parametric imputation method based on random forests that handles both continuous and categorical variables, making it ideal for mixed-type data [34]. It uses an iterative process and out-of-bag error estimates to ensure accurate and efficient imputation, especially in datasets with complex interactions and non-linear relationships [34]. Household drinking water sources assessment

Culinary use is the most important use of household drinking water; thus, cooking water can reflect the overall drinking water type of the household to some extent. This study used the question “what has been your household’s primary water source for cooking in the past three months” to identify participants’ household drinking water type. Based on accessibility and storage convenience, we divided water sources into two groups: stable water sources (tap, mineral, purified, and filtered water) and unstable water sources (rivers, lakes, streams, rainfall, ponds, springs, mountain springs, and wells or cisterns).

Building on this, we further classified the water sources based on the instability of storage and usage into the following categories: collectively stable water (tap, mineral, purified, and filtered water); cellar-stored natural water (wells or cisterns); and surface-exposed natural water (rivers, lakes, streams, rainfall, ponds, springs, mountain springs) [7]. The term “cistern” refers to a tank or a similar container, typically constructed underground, used for rainwater collection. Cistern water signifies the water stored in cisterns in regions facing water scarcity. Respondents who selected the “other” option were excluded due to ambiguity about their preferred water type.

DS assessment

DS were assessed using an 8-item version of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D8). The query set comprised the following items: “I felt depressed,” “I felt everything I did was an effort,” “my sleep was restless,” “I was happy,” “I felt lonely,” “I enjoyed life,” “I felt sad”, and “I could not get going” [35]. Respondents were instructed to rate the frequency of experiencing these listed emotional states or behaviors during the past week on a scale from 0 to 3 (0 = rarely or never, 1 = not too often, 2 = sometimes or half the time, and 3 = most of the time). The items “I was happy” and “I enjoyed life” were reverse-coded. The total CES-D8 score ranged from 0 to 24, and a score of ≥ 9 indicated clinically significant DS [35].

The Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale is a widely adopted self-report tool for assessing depressive symptomatology in both clinical and non-clinical settings [36]. The original scale consisted of twenty items, and several adaptations have been implemented in large longitudinal surveys for practical or theoretical purposes [37]. CES-D8 has been shown to be a reliable and valid measure of DS among middle-aged and older Chinese populations [3, 38]. In our study, the Cronbach’s alpha values for CES-D8 among participants aged 40 and above in the 2012, 2016, 2018, and 2020 waves were 0.754, 0.796, 0.780, and 0.778, respectively, confirming good internal consistency.

Covariates

Based on established literature, we identified four categories of potential confounders associated with DS [38–40]. The selection and measurement of these covariates were as follows: (1) Socio-demographic: age was included as a continuous variable; gender was categorized as male or female; residence was classified according to the National Bureau of Statistics’ criteria for “urban-rural individual classification”; marital status was categorized as married or non-married (including single, divorced, and widowed individuals); educational level was divided into three categories: below primary school, primary school, and middle school or higher; income level was evaluated through the question “how do you rate your income relative to your local area”, with responses categorized as low, medium, or high; and family size was defined as the number of people in a household. (2) Health-related: body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared, and categorized as underweight (BMI < 18.5), normal (18.5 ≤ BMI < 24.0), and overweight and obese (BMI ≥ 24.0); chronic disease was assessed by asking respondents whether they had been diagnosed with a chronic disease in the past six months; instrumental activities of daily living (IADL) disability was assessed using the 7-item Lawton IADL scale [38]. Respondents were asked whether they could independently perform seven activities: going outdoors, dining, kitchen activities, using public transportation, shopping, cleaning, and doing laundry. Inability to complete any of these activities independently was defined as IADL disability. (3) Behavioral: smoking was measured by asking respondents whether they had smoked in the past month; drinking was assessed by asking whether they had consumed alcohol more than three times per week in the past month. (4) Mental: given that baseline DS scores may influence follow-up DS, these scores were also included as a continuous covariate (CES-D8 score).

Statistical analysis

First, the distribution of participant characteristics was described as frequencies with proportions or means with standard deviations (SD). Group differences were assessed using t-tests or analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed continuous variables, the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed continuous variables, and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Follow-up changes in household water types and DS within the cohort sample were illustrated by Sankey diagrams.

Second, we implemented time-varying Cox regression to explore the association between household water sources and DS in sex-specific populations. In these models, follow-up time was treated as the time scale, and hazard ratios (HR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) were computed [41]. We constructed four models with increasing levels of adjustment: Model 0 was unadjusted; Model 1 adjusted for socio-demographic covariates (age, gender, residence, education level, marital status, income level, and family size); Model 2 additionally adjusted for health-related and behavioral covariates (BMI, chronic disease, IADL disability, smoking, and drinking); Model 3 additionally included baseline CES-D score. To examine potential effect modification, we conducted stratified and interaction analyses. Time-varying Cox regressions were performed within subgroups defined by key baseline characteristics. Interaction effects were tested by including cross-product terms between household water sources and each selected characteristics in the model.

Third, to better understand how transitions in household water types affect DS in sex-specific populations, we used group-based trajectory models, implemented via the traj-command in Stata, to identify water-use trajectories over time [41–43]. Subsequently, we employed logistic regression to estimate odds ratios (OR) and corresponding 95% CIs for the association between identified trajectory groups and DS at the final follow-up.

Fourth, we estimated the population attributable fractions (PAF) to quantify the DS burden attributable to the use of household unstable water in sex-specific middle-aged and older populations. This can be conceptualized as the potential reduction in incidence proportion if all subjects used stable water during the follow-up. Pe denotes the cumulative exposure rate within the population, signifying the prevalence of household unstable water usage in the cohort sample. For Pe, we incorporated the time-weighted exposure rate of household unstable water. This rate accounted for variable exposure status at different time intervals, reflecting the average duration of unstable water use during the study period. HR is indicative of the hazard ratio involved in the association between unstable water and DS.

Finally, in the sensitivity analysis, we repeated our main analyses with the following adjustments to test the robustness of our findings: (1) excluding participants with missing baseline covariate values to assess the potential impact of imputation on the results; (2) using different cut-off values (≥ 8 or ≥ 10 scores) to define clinically significant DS, in order to evaluate whether results are sensitive to the threshold used; (3) additionally including participants who did not complete all CES-D8 items in the original sample by defining DS based on whether their partial scores reached the threshold of 9, to explore the impact of partial data inclusion; (4) selecting samples with at least one follow-up DS assessment during the follow-up period, rather than only those who completed all assessments, to evaluate the impact of participant attrition on the results; (5) given the potential impact of environmental pollutants on household drinking water quality [44–46], we further adjusted the main model by including: residential chemical plant exposure (evaluated based on the question “is there a chemical plant within 5 kilometers of your residential community”), the risk of toilet contamination to household drinking water sources (assessed based on the question “distance between household toilet and drinking water source”. According to drinking water standards, low risk is defined as using a flush toilet or a distance > 10 m; high risk is defined as using a non-flush toilet with a distance ≤ 10 m), and household waste contamination risk to drinking water sources (based on the question “how is household waste disposed of”. High risk is defined as disposal in nearby ditches or around the house, while low risk is defined as disposal in public trash bins or collection by dedicated personnel); and (6) to assess whether baseline covariates confound the association between unstable water use and DS, we performed univariate Cox models followed by stepwise multivariables adjustments, focusing on changes in the effect estimates, especially by sex.

All statistical analyses were performed using STATA 17.0, except for the missForest imputation in R 4.2.1. A two-tailed P-value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of participants

The study included 5,868 participants: 3,114 males (53.1%) and 2,754 females (46.9%). Among them, 4,503 were aged 40–59 (52.2% males, 47.8% females) and 1,365 were 60 and older (55.8% males, 44.2% females); 3,250 participants were from rural areas (54.6% males, 45.4% females) and 2,618 were from urban areas (51.2% males, 48.8% females). At baseline, 3,695 used stable water sources (52.4% males, 47.6% females), and 2,173 used unstable water sources (54.3% males, 45.7% females). Significant differences were observed between household water types (stable vs. unstable) across various characteristics. Among males, these differences were evident in residence, education level, family size, drinking status, BMI, and baseline CES-D scores. In contrast, among females, significant differences were found in age, residence, marital status, education level, family size, smoking status, chronic disease, IADL disability, and baseline CES-D scores. The basic characteristics of the cohort are shown in Table 1. Besides, Sankey diagrams indicated that the proportion of participants using stable household water sources increased from 62.9 to 81.3% over the 8-year follow-up period. Meanwhile, the DS prevalence rose to 18.1%, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Sex-specific associations between household drinking water sources and DS

The association between unstable household water sources and the follow-up DS was significant, with notable differences observed between sexes. The time-varying Cox model revealed that in the unadjusted model, the association between unstable household water sources and DS was stronger in females [HR = 1.541, 95% CI: 1.370, 1.732] than in males [HR = 1.327, 95% CI: 1.154, 1.525]. After adjusting for socio-demographic covariates in Model 1, the association remained higher for females [HR = 1.338, 95% CI: 1.184, 1.512] than males [HR = 1.155, 95% CI: 1.001, 1.333]. However, after further adjustments in Model 2 (health-related and behavioral factors) and Model 3 (health-related and behavioral factors, along with baseline CES-D score), the relationship was no longer significant in males [Model 2: HR = 1.143, 95% CI: 0.990, 1.319; Model 3: HR = 1.107, 95% CI: 0.961, 1.275], but persisted in females [Model 2: HR = 1.356, 95% CI: 1.200, 1.533; Model 3: HR = 1.260, 95% CI: 1.116, 1.423]. A significant positive association between unstable water sources and DS was also consistent across all models for the entire population. The detailed results are provided in Table 2.

Stratified and interaction analyses

The stratified analyses revealed sex-specific associations between household water sources and DS across various subgroups, as presented in Table 4. In the fully adjusted Cox model, substantial differences emerged between males and females. Among males, no significant associations were found across most subgroups. However, in females, a significant positive association was observed between unstable water sources and DS in subgroups characterized by younger age, rural residence, married status, lower educational level, lower income, non-smoking status, normal or higher BMI, and the absence of chronic diseases and IADL disability. This association was absent in other comparable subgroups. Significant positive associations were consistently observed across all subgroups defined by family size and drinking status. However, no significant interaction was detected between the use of unstable household water sources and the aforementioned factors in either males or females. The subgroup associations in the overall population closely mirrored those in the female population.

Sex-specific associations between household water trajectories and post-follow-up DS

We applied group-based trajectory modeling to evaluate the sex-specific association between transitions in household water sources and post-follow-up DS. The optimum trajectory categories were identified based on both model fit indicators and their theoretical relevance. The three-category trajectory models showed the best fit for both male and female groups [males: Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) = −5263.75, Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) = −5296.99, Log-Likelihood = −5252.75, Entropy = 0.876, smallest group percentage = 16.8%; females: AIC = −4482.86, BIC = −4521.35, Log-Likelihood = −4469.86, Entropy = 0.778, smallest group percentage = 15.3%], as illustrated in Supplementary Tables 1–2. The household water use trajectory patterns were relatively consistent between males and females, as shown in Fig. 2. Based on this, we unified these trajectory categories into three distinct water use groups: constant stable water, switching from unstable to stable water, and constant unstable water.

Four logistic regression models, aligned with the covariates mentioned above, demonstrated that the association between water use trajectories and post-follow-up DS varied significantly between males and females, as shown in Table 5. In the male population, both switching from unstable to stable water and constant unstable water were significantly positively associated with post-follow-up DS risk compared to constant stable water. However, the association for switching group did not show a weaker risk compared to constant unstable water, suggesting that changes in water source types may not reduce post-follow-up DS risk in males. In females, both switching from unstable to stable water and persistent use of unstable water were positively associated with post-follow-up DS risk compared to constant stable water. Notably, switching group exhibited a lower OR value compared to constant unstable water. Although this association was not statistically significant, it may suggest that changes in water source types could potentially reduce post-follow-up DS risk in females.

Population attributable fraction

The PAF demonstrated the proportion of DS burden that could be attributed to the use of unstable household water. The time-weighted exposure rates to unstable water (Pe) were 22.19% in entire population, 23.08% in males, and 22.03% in females, respectively. Drawing on HR values derived from fully-adjusted Cox models above, we found that 3.99% (95% CI: 3.63%, 4.35%) of DS burden in the entire population and 5.42% (95% CI: 4.79%, 6.04%) of DS burden in females could be attributed to the use of unstable household water. Sensitivity analyses

The sensitivity analyses further confirmed the robustness of the primary findings in this study. First, similar results were obtained in the sample after the exclusion of all participants with missing baseline covariate values, indicating that our main results were not biased by how missing values were handled (see Supplementary Tables 3–7). Second, when applying different DS cutoff values, the results were consistent with the primary analysis, suggesting that slight variations in the cutoff did not significantly impact the main findings (see Supplementary Tables 8–15). Third, after including participants who completed only a subset of the CES-D8 items in the original sample, the results remained nearly identical, suggesting that missing data from incomplete responses had minimal impact on the conclusions (see Supplementary Tables 16–18); Fourth, when allowing for sample attrition during the follow-up period (with at least one assessment), the main results showed no marked changes, indicating that participant attrition did not bias the study’s conclusions (see Supplementary Tables 19–21). Fifth, after further adjusting for potential residential chemical plant exposure, toilet contamination, and household waste contamination, the main results showed no marked changes, indicating that the impact of these pollutants on the results is limited (see Supplementary Tables 22–23). Sixth, with the exception of smoking, drinking, and chronic disease status, the individual associations between most covariates and DS remain consistent across sexes. Throughout the process of sequentially including covariates, although the association between unstable water use and DS shows slight fluctuations, it remains generally stable, particularly the sex-specific association, indicating that the conclusions of this study are minimally affected by covariates (see Supplementary Tables 24–25).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the sex-specific association between household drinking water sources and DS in Chinese middle-aged and older adults. We found that, first, there was a significant association between household water sources and DS, with notable differences between sexes. Using unstable household water significantly increased the future DS risk in females, with this risk increasing with greater water instability. However, no consistent effect was observed in males. Second, compared to those using stable water over the long term, constant use of unstable water was significantly linked to post-follow-up DS. Switching from unstable to stable water could reduce this risk in females but not in males. Third, 3.99% of the DS burden in the entire population and 5.42% in females could be attributed to unstable water use. These findings would provide certain reference for developing targeted household water-supply policies to protect female mental health.

While several studies have demonstrated the relationship between water quality and physical health, the association between the supply stability of household water sources and mental health has long been an overlooked public health issue [7–9, 11, 47]. We confirmed the existence of a significant positive association between using unstable drinking water sources and DS in middle-aged and older adults in China, which is consistent with previous research findings [31]. According to the vulnerability-stress model, the interaction between an individual’s inherent vulnerabilities and external stressors may increase the risk of mental disorders [11, 48]. As an external stressor, using unstable water sources typically means that individuals struggle to access sufficient, reliable, and safe water to maintain a healthy life [49, 50]. Those relying on unstable water sources face numerous challenges, such as seasonal water shortages, water pollution, and the time costs associated with water collection [30]. These challenges may impose greater psychological, social, and economic burdens compared to households with access to safe water [9]. This external pressure not only exacerbates poverty, illness, anxiety, and uncertainty but may also increase the risk of depression [9]. Additionally, perceptions of water insecurity, unfairness in water services, and anxiety over inequitable access are potential stressors that may further increase the likelihood of depression [51].

Notably, we found for the first time that using unstable water sources was significantly linked to increased DS risk in females but not in males. This sex-specific association highlights that middle-aged and older females in China are more vulnerable to the adverse impacts of water instability, and their mental health deserves more attention. We speculate that this difference may be due to several reasons. First, in the traditional Chinese family structure, females, especially middle-aged and older females, often bear more responsibility and decision-making related to household water, such as fetching water, cooking, and caring for the family. If the water supply is unstable, this additional burden may impose greater stress on females, increasing their psychological load and leading to depression. Second, there are neurobiological differences between females and males, and they handle emotions and stress differently [52, 53]. Females may be more emotionally sensitive and reactive to stress, making them more likely to experience intense emotional reactions. These include more frequent worry about the future or feelings of helplessness and frustration in the face of external pressures like unstable water sources. Several studies have shown that the depression prevalence in middle-aged and older females is higher than in males, making them a potential high-risk group for depression [38, 53]. Third, in the Chinese social context, males tend to have more resources and support in their social support networks compared to females, which could help males buffer the stress caused by unstable water sources. Lastly, individual differences in psychological resilience may lead to different impacts of the same stressor on different genders [54]. Females may have lower psychological resilience when facing persistent stress (such as unstable water sources), thus leading to DS. Additionally, we found that the sex-specific associations between unstable water sources and DS remained unchanged, regardless of which covariates were included. However, the inclusion of residence, education level, and income significantly altered the effect. Urban-rural differences, education, and income not only directly influence DS, but also indirectly affect mental health by shaping water source choices and coping resources. This highlights the important role of socio-economic factors in the impact of unstable water sources on mental health.

Based on the previous findings, we further subdivided household water sources and found that, in females, using surface-exposed and cellar-stored natural water sources was significantly associated with an increased risk of depression, with surface-exposed water presenting a higher risk. In contrast, no such associations was not observed in males. We speculate that surface-exposed natural water is more vulnerable to seasonal changes, natural disasters, and various forms of pollution, which may heighten anxiety, depression, and insecurity in females. Previous research has confirmed that surface-exposed natural water carries greater health risks than cellar-stored water, especially in relation to chronic diseases [7]. These findings suggest that greater instability in water supply may elevate the risk of depression in females, and interventions should prioritize individuals using highly unstable sources. However, due to the limited sample size for certain water source categories, further validation is needed.

Additionally, this study identified three common water usage trajectories among residents. Compared to those consistently using stable water sources, both consistently using unstable water and switching from unstable to stable water were associated with increased post-follow-up DS risk. In females, the transition group had a lower risk compared to those consistently using unstable water, while no similar pattern was observed in males. This suggests that prolonged exposure to unstable water may have a cumulative effect on DS risk in females, and timely switching to a stable water source may help reduce this risk. It underscores the importance of China’s efforts to improve stable water access. The limited impact on males may be due to their lesser involvement in household water-related tasks, resulting in a less substantial benefit from switching to a stable source. Also, we estimated that 3.99% of the DS burden in the entire population and 5.42% in females could be attributed to unstable water use. Despite China’s implementation of various policies [55], such as the Village-to-Village Tap Water Project and dynamic water quality monitoring, which have increased the availability of safe and stable water sources, more targeted water supply policies are still needed to address the potential depression burden associated with unstable water use.

In addition, we conducted several subgroup analyses to further explore the associations between using unstable household water sources and DS in specific subgroups. The associations showed minimal variation across male subgroups but more pronounced differences among females. Middle-aged, rural, low-income, low-educated, non-smoking females without chronic diseases or disabilities using unstable water showed higher DS risks. We speculate that middle-aged and rural females usually carry heavier household and caregiving responsibilities, and using unstable water may increase their burdens and anxiety, thus leading to DS [56]. Females with lower income and education often lack the resources to improve water sources or living conditions, leaving them chronically exposed to unstable water conditions, thereby increasing their risk of depression. Financial stress may further heighten their anxiety about water safety, compounding the likelihood of DS. This is consistent with previous similar studies [38]. Furthermore, females in marriage need to manage more family care beyond themselves [57]. Additional stress related to family health due to the uncertainty and cost of using unstable water sources may raise higher DS risks.

Interestingly, we observed that high-income males showed a higher DS risk when using unstable water sources, while females exhibited the opposite trend. In the cultural context of China, the roles of males and females in the household differ [58]. Females, especially those with lower incomes, often bear more household and caregiving responsibilities [57, 58]. They face greater constraints on resources and living conditions, and economic instability makes them more reliant on stable basic living conditions [57]. The instability of household water sources, combined with a lack of financial resources to find alternatives, may amplify their daily stress [57], leading to increased DS risk. In contrast, high-income females typically have more economic resources, social support, and better psychological coping abilities, and they are less likely to bear extensive caregiving responsibilities [58]. This enables them to address unstable water sources and reduce their DS risk. On the other hand, males, particularly high-income males, traditionally carry greater economic and familial support responsibilities while being less involved in domestic tasks [57, 58]. When faced with unstable water sources, they may experience heightened societal expectations and pressures, leading to increased anxiety. High-income males may perceive this instability as a challenge to their abilities, thereby increasing their DS risk.

Compared to females with chronic diseases and disabilities, healthy women often carry more household responsibilities [57]. While they don’t face the physiological burdens of illness or disability, societal expectations may make them more sensitive to environmental stress [59]. Unstable water sources can disrupt their basic needs, leading to increased anxiety and depression. In contrast, those with chronic conditions or disabilities have faced more challenges, developing coping mechanisms for stress and relying on family or social support [60]. As a result, the impact of water instability may be less for them, though they are not immune to its effects. Non-smokers, who have fewer stress-coping mechanisms and are more health-conscious than smokers, may be more vulnerable to the negative effects. Notably, we did not identify any interactions between subgroups, which may be partly attributable to the limited size. Future research with larger samples is needed to validate these findings.

Additionally, an unstable supply of household drinking water is often linked to water quality problems [61], so we should interpret our conclusions cautiously without water quality monitoring data. Unstable water sources are more prone to contamination by bacteria, viruses, parasites, pesticides, antibiotics, heavy metals, and emerging pollutants like microplastics, BPA, and PAHs [5, 7, 13–21], which may increase the risk of depression [22–27]. The association between using unstable water and DS may also be driven by physiological changes such as inflammation, gut microbiome disruption, and neurotoxicity caused by these contaminants [22–27]. Therefore, to minimize the potential confounding effect of water quality in the absence of direct monitoring data, we further adjusted for three variables potentially influencing water quality in the sensitivity analysis [44–46]: residential chemical plant exposure, the risk of toilet contamination to household drinking water sources, and the risk of household waste contamination to drinking water sources. The results after adjustment still showed consistent findings. While this method partially adjusts for the influence of water quality, we cannot fully exclude its potential impact on the association between the use of unstable drinking water sources and DS due to lacking precise water quality monitoring data. Future research should further validate these findings in larger samples with water quality monitoring data.

Finally, we also need to consider the potential impact of the classification of water source stability on the conclusions. In this study, water sources are classified based on daily accessibility and storage convenience into stable supply sources (tap water and purified water) and unstable supply sources (rivers, lakes, streams, rainwater, ponds, springs, mountain springs, and wells, etc.). Compared to tap water and purified water, the latter are more susceptible to seasonal changes, climate fluctuations, and pollution, resulting in poorer long-term stability [30, 61]. However, in remote rural areas of China, due to insufficient pipeline coverage and an underdeveloped water management system, tap water supply is often unstable, and river or lake water sources may actually be more accessible, especially when tap water is in short supply [61]. Considering that only 0.51% of individuals in this study used river or lake water as their primary drinking water source in the baseline year, we believe the impact of this on the conclusions is minimal. However, it is necessary to explore the association between unstable water supply and depression under different classification standards in larger samples in future research. Meanwhile, households with access to multiple water sources may have a stronger sense of water security [62]. Compared to households with a single water source, those with multiple sources can more easily switch between alternatives in case of water shortages, thus increasing their sense of security. However, since the database used in this study only recorded the primary drinking water source and did not capture the total number of available water sources, the relationship between the number of water sources and depression was not explored. Future research should address this gap.

Strengths and limitations

This study has three strengths. First, it is the first study to explore the impact of unstable water sources on the mental health of Chinese females, raising public awareness and providing important evidence for improving the water supply policies in China. Second, we adopted a comprehensive, large-scale, longitudinal research design involving nationally representative cohorts, which guarantees the robustness and quality of our findings. Third, we utilized time-varying Cox regression, accounting for changes in water source type over time, enabling a more accurate assessment of the association between the use of unstable water usage and DS.

This study also has several limitations. First, poor water quality due to exposure to water pollutants may contribute to depression [22, 23]. Due to database limitations, this study could not directly measure household drinking water quality. Although we controlled for environmental factors that may influence water quality in the sensitivity analysis, showing the robustness of the results, we still cannot fully exclude the potential impact of water quality on our conclusions. Future research should quantify water pollutant exposure and examine its effects on mental health, considering both water supply stability and quality. Second, the number of accessible drinking water sources owned by a household can partially reflect the stability of water supply [62], which may influence depression, especially in remote rural areas. However, due to the limitations of the database used in this study, which only investigated the primary drinking water source of residents and did not include the number of available drinking water sources, the latter was not incorporated into the classification of water supply stability. This may have some impact on the conclusions of the study. Future research should further explore the relationship between the number of household water sources and depression. Third, older adults with lower educational levels or severe physical or mental health conditions may not be able to complete the full DS assessment. Although our sensitivity analysis shows that the main results remain consistent across different sample inclusion criteria, we still need to consider the potential impact of sample attrition or incomplete responses on the conclusions. Fourth, while most households use the same water for cooking and drinking, a minority may use different sources, which could potentially affect the conclusions. Lastly, we could not assess prior exposure to cooking water or transitional changes before the study baseline, which may influence the results.

Conclusion

The stability of household drinking water supply has largely been overlooked in terms of its impact on residents’ mental health, especially for females. In middle-aged and older adults in China, there was a significant association between the type of household water source and DS, with notable differences between sexes. Using unstable household water sources is associated with an increased DS risk in females but not in males. In the future, authorities should enact more targeted water supply policies to promote the availability of stable and safe household water sources, thereby protecting the mental health of females.
