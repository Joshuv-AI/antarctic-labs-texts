Beyond genital phenotype – Commentary on "Long-term outcomes in 45,X/46,XY mosaicism: a 30-year retrospective study in Hong Kong"
Authors: So Yoon Jung
Published: 2026

45,X/46,XY mosaicism remains one of the most clinically heterogeneous sex chromosome disorders in pediatric endocrinology. Even with the same chromosomal constitution, affected individuals may present with widely different phenotypes, ranging from a typical male appearance to genital ambiguity or Turner syndrome-associated features. This variability complicates decisions about sex assignment, gonadal management, growth, and long-term follow-up. Poon et al. [1] reported long-term outcomes in a large cohort of individuals with 45,X/46,XY mosaicism followed into adulthood, adding valuable data on gonadal tumors, pubertal development, growth, and Turner syndrome-associated comorbidities.

A key finding is the association between external genital phenotype and gonadal tumor risk. Patients with lower external genitalia scores were more likely to develop gonadal neoplasia, whereas no gonadal tumors were identified in those with a typical male phenotype. This supports careful phenotypic assessment when counseling families and considering surveillance strategies [2].

At the same time, this study shows the limits of phenotype-based prediction. Although genital phenotype was associated with tumor risk, it did not predict long-term gonadal function. All males in the cohort spontaneously entered puberty, including those with marked genital ambiguity. Conversely, several individuals with apparently typical male genitalia later developed gonadal insufficiency and required testosterone replacement therapy. Prior longitudinal studies have reported similar findings [3-6]. Together, these findings suggest that genital phenotype helps estimate gonadal tumor risk, but offers limited guidance regarding future gonadal function.

Growth remains a major clinical concern in this population. Short stature is common and is thought to reflect, at least in part, the contribution of the 45,X cell line and SHOX haploinsufficiency [3,4]. Although growth hormone therapy is often considered, evidence for its effect on adult height has remained limited and inconsistent [3,4,7]. In the present study, final height did not differ significantly between treated and untreated individuals. This finding should be interpreted cautiously because of the small treated subgroup, late treatment initiation, variable treatment duration, and retrospective design. A recent Korean study in Turner syndrome also found that 3-year growth response to growth hormone treatment did not differ significantly according to karyotype, supporting the broader view that chromosomal category alone may offer limited guidance for predicting growth response [8].

Beyond growth and gonadal function, this study reminds us that 45,X/46,XY mosaicism should not be viewed solely through the lens of disorders of sex development. Cardiovascular, renal, thyroid, metabolic, and other Turner syndrome-associated comorbidities were observed across the cohort, while screening for these conditions was not consistently performed. This is clinically important because individuals with 45,X/ 46,XY mosaicism share features with Turner syndrome and may benefit from similarly broad surveillance strategies [7,9,10]. Current Turner syndrome guidelines emphasize structured lifelong monitoring of cardiovascular, endocrine, reproductive, and metabolic health, and recommend that surveillance guidelines also apply to individuals with 45,X/46,XY mosaicism with ambiguous or male external genitalia [11].

In summary, this study illustrates both the value and the limitations of genital phenotype in 45,X/46,XY mosaicism. The degree of virilization remains informative for estimating gonadal tumor risk, but offers limited guidance regarding long-term gonadal function, growth outcomes, and Turner syndrome-associated comorbidities. This discordance between genital phenotype, peripheral blood karyotype, and long-term clinical outcomes remains one of the defining challenges in the management of 45,X/46,XY mosaicism [1,3-6]. Even when the external phenotype is close to that of a typical male, both this cohort and previous longitudinal studies support the need for continued assessment of gonadal function and broader health outcomes over time. In this context, the structured lifelong surveillance outlined in Turner syndrome guidelines may provide a practical framework for the long-term care of individuals with 45,X/46,XY mosaicism. These observations highlight the need to look beyond genital phenotype in 45,X/46,XY mosaicism.
