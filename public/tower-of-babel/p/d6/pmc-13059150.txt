Injectable dual-drug hydrogel containing curcumin and glycyrrhizic acid for biofilm inhibition and immunomodulatory therapy in periodontitis
Authors: Xiaoyu Chen, Yubo Hu, Chenyu Xu, Tengye Lian, Haocong Li, Lu Sun, Shiduo Sun, Meilin Hu, Yinsong Wang, Dayong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeriodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease characterized by excessive oxidative stress, persistent bacterial biofilms, and progressive destruction of periodontal tissues. Current clinical treatments primarily focus on controlling bacterial infection but often show limited long-term efficacy due to unresolved immune dysregulation. Therefore, therapeutic strategies that simultaneously target microbial biofilms and the pathological immune microenvironment are urgently needed. In this study, we developed an injectable dual-drug hydrogel incorporating curcumin (CUR) and glycyrrhizic acid (GL) for the treatment of periodontitis.MethodsCUR was dissolved in melted polyethylene glycol distearate and then dispersed in an aqueous medium to form micelles (CURM). Compared to CUR, CURM exhibited improved solubility and stability, thereby displaying greatly enhanced antioxidative, anti-inflammatory, and antibacterial activities. CURM were subsequently embedded within a hydrogel self-assembled from glycyrrhizic acid and polyvinyl alcohol (GLH) to form a dual-drug hydrogel system (CURM@GLH). Experimental periodontitis was established in mice to test their in vivo effects.ResultsOwing to the intrinsic anti-inflammatory and antioxidative properties of glycyrrhizic acid, the hydrogel exhibited combined effects in regulating immune dysregulation. The CURM@GLH effectively protected cells from oxidative damage, reduced intracellular reactive oxygen species levels, promoted macrophage polarization from the proinflammatory M1 phenotype toward the pro-regenerative M2 phenotype, and downregulated proinflammatory cytokine expression. In a ligature-induced rat model of periodontitis, local administration of the hydrogel significantly alleviated periodontal oxidative stress and inflammation and markedly reduced alveolar bone resorption.ConclusionsThis study presents an injectable dual-drug hydrogel, CURM@GLH, that integrates biofilm inhibition with immunomodulatory regulation, offering a promising host-directed therapeutic strategy for periodontitis. Introduction

Increasing evidence has shown that reactive oxygen species (ROS) play a vital role in bacterial elimination in periodontitis. However, excessive ROS can exacerbate periodontal tissue deterioration by inducing oxidative damage and disrupting the homeostasis of the immune microenvironment in periodontal tissues [1–3]. Current clinical periodontal therapeutic modalities, including local mechanical removal of biofilms and assisted antibiotic delivery (such as minocycline), focus primarily on controlling bacterial infections but insufficiently improve periodontal tissue repair due to impaired regenerative ability caused by immune dysregulation [4, 5]. Moreover, prolonged antibiotic use may disrupt the oral microbiome and contribute to antimicrobial resistance [6]. Several promising strategies have been developed, including stem cell transplantation, the use of small molecule inhibitors to achieve inflammation resolution and periodontal regeneration [7–12]. However, owing to cell source limitations and uncontrolled drug release, developing a combined therapeutic strategy to enhance tissue regeneration and improve clinical outcomes remains challenging.

Numerous natural compounds have been widely used to treat a variety of diseases, particularly periodontitis, because of their well-established antibacterial, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory qualities [13]. Among them, curcumin (CUR), a natural antioxidant extracted from the rhizome of turmeric, is a polyphenolic compound that has antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, and antibacterial properties [14–18]. The dual role of turmeric as both a spice and medicinal plant provides CUR with food safety and therapeutic benefits, avoiding the resistance concerns associated with prolonged antibiotic usage [19]. Consequently, CUR represents a promising candidate for periodontal therapy. However, its limited water solubility restricts both its efficacy and potential applications [20–22]. Hence, developing methods to increase CUR solubility and bioavailability holds substantial scientific and therapeutic importance. Owing to their distinctive core‒shell architecture, polymeric micelles have emerged as a key focus in drug delivery system research, particularly for improving poorly soluble drug solubility [23]. Surfactants, which are amphiphilic molecular structures, form micelles in aqueous environments [24]. Non-ionic surfactants can modify bacterial cell surface physicochemical properties, including surface hydrophobicity and surface energy, thereby reducing the ability of bacteria to adhere to material surfaces [25]. Polyethylene glycol distearate (PEG-DS), a high-molecular-weight nonionic surfactant commonly used as a thickening and emulsifying agent, effectively encapsulates hydrophobic CUR as curcumin micelles (CURM) to improve its solubility. Additionally, as a nonionic surfactant, it inhibits dental plaque biofilm formation.

Furthermore, to enhance the anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects of CUR for periodontitis treatment [26], glycyrrhizic acid (GL) is formulated as an injectable hydrogel, GLH, to facilitate local administration and achieve sustained drug release. As a traditional natural Chinese medicinal compound, GL is present in liquorice roots and has pharmacological benefits, including anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties [27, 28]. GL exhibits surface-active properties that increase the water solubility of CUR [29, 30]. Additionally, the amphiphilic nature of GL provides unique self-assembly capabilities, enabling it to form low-molecular-weight gels with other molecules in water [31, 32]. Consequently, an injectable hydrogel network containing natural GL and polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) was fabricated to deliver CURM. Recently, injectable hydrogels have gained significant attention as adjuvant treatments for periodontitis [33, 34]. This approach addresses the limitations of mechanical debridement, particularly in filling irregular periodontal defects and adapting to difficult-to-reach or complex alveolar defect sites [35, 36]. It also maintains local drug concentrations within the therapeutic range for extended periods [37].

In this study, we present an injectable dual-drug hydrogel for the treatment of periodontitis. CURM was encapsulated in PEG-DS to form micelles. Compared with CUR, CURM demonstrated improved solubility and stability and exhibited enhanced antioxidative, anti-inflammatory, and antibacterial properties. PEG-DS increased the solubility of CUR while inhibiting bacterial adhesion, thus preventing bacterial biofilm formation. CURM was subsequently incorporated into an injectable GLH network formed by GL and PVA, successfully developing CURM@GLH for periodontitis treatment. Owing to the anti-inflammatory and antioxidative properties of both GL and CUR, CURM@GLH has enhanced effects on immune dysregulation. CURM@GLH effectively protected cells from oxidative damage, decreased intracellular ROS levels, repolarized macrophages from the M1 phenotype to the M2 phenotype, and reduced the expression levels of proinflammatory cytokines. In a rat model of ligature-induced periodontitis, CURM@GLH effectively mitigated periodontal oxidative stress and inflammation, thereby alleviating alveolar bone loss. By inhibiting biofilm formation and regulating the immune microenvironment, the CURM@GLH system has significant potential for treating periodontitis, providing a novel therapeutic strategy for clinical applications (Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

Materials

Curcumin (CUR) with 98% purity was obtained from Meilun Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Dalian, China). Glycyrrhizic acid (GL) was sourced from Heowns Biochem Technologies, LLC. Poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) was purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. Polyethylene glycol distearate (PEG6000DS, PEG-DS) was acquired from Shandong Yousuo Chemical Technology Co., Ltd. Acetic acid, methanol, and anhydrous ethanol were obtained from Tianjin Kemiou Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China).

Cell lines and animals

The bacterial strain Pg (code BNCC337441) was sourced from BeNa Culture Collection (Beijing, China) and cultured on sheep blood agar plates or brain heart infusion (BHI) broth supplemented with 0.5% chlorhematin and 0.5% vitamin K1 in an anaerobic chamber with an atmosphere of 80% N2, 10% H2, and 10% CO2 at 37 °C. The mouse macrophage RAW264.7 cell line (RRID: CVCL-0462) and the mouse fibroblast line L929 (RRID: CVCL-0493) were sourced from BioVector NTCC (Beijing, China) and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Meilun Biotech, Dalian, China). The above cell culture media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Biological Industries, Israel) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Solarbio, Beijing, China) and cultured in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C. RAW264.7 cells and L929 cells tested negative for mycoplasma contamination and were authenticated via a mycoplasma detection kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China).

Male Sprague‒Dawley (SD) rats, aged six to seven weeks, were obtained from SPF Biotechnology (Beijing, China). After one week of acclimatization, the rats were randomly divided into six groups (n = 5): (i) Control group: healthy rats without periodontitis; (ii) Periodontitis group (negative control): rats with ligature-induced periodontitis treated with H₂O; (iii) GLH group: rats with ligature-induced periodontitis treated with GLH; (iv) CUR group: rats with ligature-induced periodontitis treated with free CUR solution; (v) CURM@GLH group: rats with ligature-induced periodontitis treated with CURM@GLH; (vi) Minocycline group (positive control): rats with ligature-induced periodontitis treated with minocycline (Periocline, Sunstar Inc., Osaka, Japan). All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of the Experimental Animal Center at the Institute of Radiation Medicine, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences.

Preparation and characterization of CURM

To prepare CURM, 0.36 g of PEG-DS was melted in an oil bath maintained. After 0.03 g of CUR was added, the mixture was stirred magnetically at 800 rpm under a nitrogen atmosphere until CUR was fully dissolved, resulting in the formation of CURM. The resulting micelles were then dispersed in deionized water to form a sample solution. The morphology and dispersibility of the CURM were examined via transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Hitachi HT7700, Japan). The hydrodynamic size and polydispersity index (PDI) of CURM were measured using a Nano-ZS90 detector (Malvern Instruments, U.K.) at 37 °C to evaluate micelle stability in aqueous media. The synthesis of CURM and GLH was affirmed by the Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrum, which was recorded on a FTIR spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS50, USA) in the range of 4000–400 cm− 1.

Preparation of CURM@GLH

GLH was synthesized by dissolving 0.75 g of PVA and 0.06 g of GL in 6 mL of deionized water. The mixture was heated at 85 °C for 45 min while being stirred at 800 rpm to ensure a homogeneous solution. CURM was then added to this mixture, which was subsequently mixed mechanically. After cooling to room temperature, CURM@GLH formed. The amount of CUR incorporated into the hydrogel was determined by weighing.

Characterization of CURM@GLH

Morphology

The morphology of the freeze-dried hydrogel was analysed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, ZEISS Gemini 300, Germany). The hydrogel was lyophilized for 48 h after being rapidly immersed in liquid nitrogen. The cross-sections were then attached to a metal pedestal with double-sided adhesive tape and gold-coated via vacuum sputtering.

Injectability and rheological properties

The injectability of the hydrogel was evaluated by a combination of rheological analysis and syringe-based extrusion tests. Rheological measurements were performed using a rotational rheometer (Anton Paar, China) equipped with a temperature-controlled plate set at 37°C. The storage modulus (G’) and loss modulus (G”) of GLH, PEG-DS@GLH, and CURM@GLH were determined through time‒frequency sweeps at a frequency of 20 Hz and a strain amplitude of 0.1% to assess their stiffness. Subsequently, shear-thinning behavior, which is critical for injectability, was evaluated by recording viscosity as a function of shear rate ranging from 0.1 to 1000 s⁻¹.

In vitro degradation

The in vitro degradation behavior of CURM@GLH was evaluated by measuring mass loss over time. Briefly, hydrogel samples with identical initial mass were prepared. In vitro release of CUR and GL

The release medium employed in the drug release study was the same as that used for the hydrogel degradation experiment. At predetermined intervals, 1 mL samples were removed for absorbance measurement, and 1 mL of fresh release medium was added to the tube, which was then returned to the incubator at 37 °C for 10 days. The drug content in the supernatant was quantified via UV‒vis spectrophotometry (U-3310, Hitachi, Japan) at 260 nm for GL and 427 nm for CUR. The amounts of CUR and GL in the supernatant were used to calculate the drug release rate from CURM@GLH.

Bacterial biofilm inhibition property of CURM@GLH

The CCK-8 assay was used to evaluate the antibacterial activity of CURM@GLH against P. gingivalis. P. gingivalis was diluted to 1.0 × 106 CFU/mL, seeded into 96-well plates, and incubated with GLH, free CUR, CURM, PEG-DS, or CURM@GLH. The final concentrations of CUR ranged from 20 to 200 µg/mL. After 12 h of incubation, the P. gingivalis were treated with the CCK-8 agent (Meilun, Dalian, China).

To simulate the complex microenvironment of periodontitis, we established a multispecies biofilm model using microorganisms derived from rat gingival crevicular fluid (GCF). Briefly, GCF samples collected from donor rats with induced periodontitis were pooled and anaerobically cultivated on CDC (Hepobio, China) anaerobic blood agar. We subsequently standardized the microbial suspension to 1 × 108 CFU/mL in BHI (Solarbio, Beijing, China) broth. The biofilms then underwent a 3–4 day maturation period at 37 °C on sterile substrates under anaerobic conditions.

The inhibitory effect of CURM@GLH on bacterial biofilms was evaluated via crystal violet staining (Meilun, Dalian, China), SEM, and confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM; Axio-Imager LSM-800, Zeiss, Germany). Bacterial suspensions treated with free CUR, PEG-DS, or CURM were incubated in 12-well plates for 48 h to allow biofilm formation. After fixation with 10% methanol and washing with PBS, the biofilms were stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Quantification was performed by measuring the OD₅₉₅ after the stain was dissolved in 33% acetic acid. For SEM imaging, biofilms grown on coverslips in 24-well plates were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde (Aladdin, China), rinsed with PBS, and dehydrated through a graded ethanol series. The samples were air-dried, sputter-coated with gold, and observed via SEM. For CLSM analysis, biofilms on coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Meilun, Dalian, China), stained with FITC (50 µg/mL; Meilun, Dalian, China) in the dark, and washed with PBS. Polysaccharides and bacterial cells within the biofilm were visualized via green fluorescence via Z-stack imaging.

In vitro antioxidative assay of CURM@GLH

Bacterial LPS and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) were used to induce endogenous and exogenous oxidative stress in RAW264.7 and L929 cells, respectively [36].

Cell viability in L929 cells, following exogenous oxidative damage treatment, was assessed via live/dead cell staining (Sigma‒Aldrich, USA) and a CCK-8 kit (NCM Biotech, China). After 30 min of exposure to 1 mM H₂O₂, L929 cells were cultured with free CUR, CURM, CURM@GLH, GLH, or without any treatment (H₂O₂ group). The negative control group consisted of cells that were neither treated nor exposed to H₂O₂. Live/dead staining was performed via an AM/PI double-staining kit. The stained cells were examined via a fluorescence microscope (ZEISS, Germany).

RAW264.7 cells were plated in 12-well plates at a density of 3 × 10⁵ cells per well and cultured for 24 h. LPS (1 µg/mL, from P. gingivalis, Solarbio, Beijing, China) was then applied to activate the cells for 1 h. Subsequently, the cells were treated with free CUR, CURM, CURM@GLH, GLH, or no treatment (LPS group) and incubated for an additional 12 h. DCFH-DA (Sigma‒Aldrich, USA) was then added to the culture medium and incubated with the cells for 30 min. Fluorescence was analysed via CLSM, and flow cytometry (FACSVerse, BD, USA) was used to quantify ROS levels by measuring fluorescence intensity (Ex: 488 nm, Em: 525 nm).

In vitro anti-inflammatory activity of CURM@GLH

The anti-inflammatory effects were evaluated by measuring cytokine expression and assessing macrophage polarization.

RAW 264.7 cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 10⁵ cells per well in 12-well plates and exposed to 1 µg/mL LPS for 1 h, followed by a 12 h incubation with GLH extract, CURM@GLH extract, free CUR solution, or CURM solution, while the control group was not exposed to LPS or subjected to any treatment. The cells were subsequently stained with CD80 (Invitrogen, USA) and CD206 (BioLegend, USA) antibodies and analysed via flow cytometry to assess macrophage polarization. In parallel, cells seeded at the same density in 6-well plates were treated with 1 µg/mL LPS for 1 h and then incubated with the same samples. The cytokine expression levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α were subsequently quantified via quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) and ELISA (Abcam, UK). β-actin was used as the reference gene to normalize the expression of the target genes. For quantification of gene expression, the ΔΔCt method was used. The primer sequences are listed in Supporting Table S1.

In vivo establishment of a periodontitis rat model

Experimental periodontitis was induced via a ligature model. The rats were anaesthetized with isoflurane (2% in 100% oxygen). Orthodontic ligature wires (0.2 mm in diameter) were placed subgingivally around the cervical region of the maxillary bilateral second molars (M2) for a duration of four weeks. After four weeks, the ligature wires were removed, and local injections were administered to the ligation sites weekly. The severity of periodontal inflammation was evaluated to ensure consistency across all groups. After four weeks of treatment, the rats were euthanized with excess isoflurane, and the maxillae were surgically excised for further analysis. All surgical procedures were performed by the same operators under anaesthesia.

Micro-CT analysis

To assess bone parameters, including the bone volume-to-tissue volume ratio (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb. Th), trabecular number (Tb. N), and trabecular separation (Tb. Sp), the collected maxillae were scanned via micro-CT (Bruker, Germany), followed by three-dimensional (3D) models and two-dimensional (2D) image reconstruction. The distance between the alveolar bone crest (ABC) and the cementoenamel junction (CEJ) on the buccal side of each M2 root was measured via ImageJ software.

Histology and immunohistochemistry

For histological analysis, the samples were sectioned, paraffin-embedded, decalcified, and dried. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, along with Masson’s trichrome staining, was employed to assess histological changes in the periodontal tissue. Additionally, dihydroethidium (DHE) staining was used to detect ROS in the periodontal tissue surrounding the maxillary second molar. Immunohistochemical staining for TNF-α and IL-1β was performed to evaluate vascularization and inflammatory responses in the periodontitis model.

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as the means ± standard deviations (SD), with a sample size of n ≥ 3. Statistical analyses were conducted via GraphPad Prism version 10.1.2. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test was employed to assess group differences. Statistical significance is indicated by asterisks: * P < 0.05, ** P < 0.01, and *** P < 0.001, while ns denotes P > 0.05, indicating no significant difference.

Results and discussion

Fabrication and characterization of CURM

The synthesis of CURM was accomplished through the precipitation method. Figure 2A presents a comprehensive outline of the micelle preparation process. FTIR analysis revealed characteristic peak shifts after CURM formation, indicating the successful encapsulation of CUR into PEG-DS (Fig. S1A). When viewed above, the CURM dispersion exhibited a ginger-yellow appearance (Fig. 2B). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis revealed that CURM exhibited a spherical micellar morphology with a characteristic core–shell structure (Fig. 2C). The micelles showed a relatively uniform size distribution, with an average diameter of approximately 500 nm. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis further indicated that CURM maintained a consistent hydrodynamic size distribution with no evident aggregation over 7 days in aqueous media, supporting its colloidal stability (Fig. S2). These results suggest the successful formation of micelles through the self-assembly of the amphiphilic PEG-DS copolymer in aqueous environments. In this system, curcumin is encapsulated within the hydrophobic domains of PEG-DS, while the hydrophilic segments extend outward to form a stabilizing shell, consistent with previously reported PEG-based micellar structures [38]. Such a core–shell architecture is known to improve the aqueous dispersibility of hydrophobic drugs and reduce their susceptibility to degradation [39].

Characterization of CURM@GLH

GL and PVA demonstrate the ability to self-assemble into stable physical networks through hydrogen bonding [40] (Fig S1 B). The CURM was encapsulated to establish a dual-drug delivery hydrogel system, CURM@GLH. In an inverted vial test, both GLH and CURM@GLH exhibited stable hydrogel states and maintained their form without flowing. (Fig. 2E). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis, as shown in Fig. 2D, revealed that all the composite hydrogels possessed three-dimensional porous networks. These porous structures enable nutrient and oxygen movement, supporting cellular penetration and proliferation, which promotes tissue regeneration. Oscillatory rheology testing was used to evaluate the variations in the loss modulus (G’’) and storage modulus (G’) over time (Fig. 2F). The results indicated that the storage modulus consistently exceeded the loss modulus throughout the testing period, demonstrating the mechanical stability of the hydrogel. This stability is essential for maintaining the structure within the periodontal pocket against external forces. As the shear rate increased from 10⁻¹ to 10³/s, all the composite hydrogels exhibited significant viscosity reductions due to dynamic crosslinking within the hydrogel network (Fig. 2G).

The CURM@GLH demonstrated superior injectability in both air and underwater environments, as illustrated in Fig. 3A and B. This property confirms its suitability for syringe-based injection into irregular periodontal pockets. The tensile properties and remodelling potential of the hydrogels were evaluated through strain testing. The results showed that the hydrogel remained stable across various tensile strains (Fig. 3D) and demonstrated moldability into different shapes (Fig. 3C). These characteristics suggest that the hydrogel can adapt to irregular periodontal defects, ensure complete filling, and maintain structural integrity under pressure from surrounding soft tissues. The swelling behaviour in aqueous environments represents a crucial characteristic of hydrogels [41]. Upon immersion in PBS, CURM@GLH exhibited progressive swelling over time (Fig. 3E). This swelling capacity facilitates drug diffusion and release, as well as nutrient and metabolite transport.

The hydrogel exhibited a gradual mass loss over time under physiological conditions, indicating a controllable degradation profile (Fig. S3). Consistent with this behavior, UV–visible absorption spectra of the drug-releasing supernatants displayed two characteristic peaks at 260 nm and 426 nm, corresponding to GL and CUR, respectively (Fig. 3F and G). The standard curves for GL and CUR enable assessment of the release profiles for both drugs (Fig. 3H), with GL demonstrating more frequent release compared to CUR. The sustained release of GL and CUR, which target inflammatory sites in periodontitis, ensures the prolonged pharmacological activity of the hydrogel, supporting effective treatment.

ROS emerge as natural byproducts of cellular metabolism, with bodily defense mechanisms maintaining a dynamic balance against these free radicals [42]. When this balance becomes disrupted, excess ROS can generate cytotoxic effects and induce oxidative stress in cells and tissues, potentially triggering or intensifying inflammatory responses. Antioxidants such as GL and CUR are incorporated into periodontitis treatments to neutralize excess ROS and mitigate oxidative stress. The ROS-scavenging activity of CURM@GLH was evaluated by assessing its antioxidant effects on the 2,2′-azobis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) radical (ABTS+•) and the 1,1-diphenyl-2-trinitrophenylhydrazine radical (DPPH•). The nitrogen-centered radical ABTS+• serves as a widely utilized indirect antioxidant detection method (Fig. 3I). At equivalent CUR concentrations, CURM@GLH exhibited enhanced performance compared with CURM and free CUR. These results suggest that PEG-DS encapsulated hydrophobic CUR to form CURM, increasing its water solubility and subsequent antioxidant activity. Additionally, the combined antioxidant effects of CUR and GL had significant cooperative effects (Fig. 3J and K). The DPPH• radical, a nitrogen-centered free radical commonly used in antioxidant capacity assessment, was employed (Fig. 3L). Antioxidants interacting with the DPPH• radical can lead to decolorization of its purple alcoholic solution. The findings in Fig. 3M and N indicated that CURM@GLH demonstrated remarkable nitrogen-centered free radical scavenging abilities.

Bacterial biofilm inhibition property of CURM@GLH

Dental plaque biofilms have been established as the primary etiological factor in the initiation and progression of periodontal disease [43]. Bacterial biofilm formation can hinder antibiotic penetration, subsequently leading to bacterial resistance. The majority of antibacterial treatment failures are attributed to biofilm formation [44]. Research has demonstrated that CUR has antimicrobial properties, whereas PEG-DS has strong antiadhesion characteristics against bacteria. Therefore, our approach aims to inhibit bacterial biofilm formation through simultaneous bacterial elimination and adhesion reduction (Fig. 4A). P. gingivalis, the principal pathogenic bacterium involved in periodontal diseases, was selected for coculture in different groups [45]. As shown in Fig. 4B and C, CURM demonstrated superior antimicrobial efficacy against P. gingivalis compared with free CUR, which was attributed to the increased solubility of CUR rather than the antimicrobial properties of PEG-DS. The antimicrobial activity of CURM plateaued at CUR concentrations above 50 µg/mL; hence, this concentration was selected for subsequent experiments. Additionally, CURM@GLH and CURM exhibited comparable antimicrobial capacities, indicating that GL has minimal antimicrobial activity, which was further corroborated by the results shown in Fig. S5.

To evaluate the effectiveness of antibacterial treatment on biofilms, a mixed biofilm of periodontal pathogenic bacteria was developed and subjected to various conditions. Crystal violet staining demonstrated that CURM@GLH effectively suppressed bacterial biofilm formation (Fig. 4D and E). Analysis of residual bacterial biofilms after treatment confirmed that CURM@GLH reduced the rate of biofilm formation to less than 30% (Fig. 4F). SEM was employed to observe bacterial biofilms treated with various interventions after 24 h (Fig. 4G). The biofilm displayed an interconnected structure, with visible surface collapse and varying degrees of bacterial membrane rupture following free CUR treatment. PEG-DS treatment resulted in a loosened film structure, with the characteristic film-like morphology becoming indistinct. The CURM group exhibited a similar loose biofilm structure, membrane disruption, and bacterial surface collapse. Furthermore, FITC-labelled fluorescent dyes were utilized to identify fluorescently tagged proteins and extracellular DNA (eDNA) in biofilms (Fig. 4H). Compared with those in the other groups, the bacterial biofilms in the CURM group were significantly smaller. Notably, the CURM@GLH group demonstrated performance comparable to that of the CURM group. These findings confirm the superior biofilm-eradication ability of CURM@GLH.

Cytocompatibility and cellular antioxidant properties of CURM@GLH

The cytocompatibility of materials is crucial for the successful implementation of biomaterials. To evaluate this property, a cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay was utilized to assess the in vitro cytocompatibility of free CUR, CURM, CURM@GLH, and GLH with mouse fibroblasts (L929) and murine macrophages (RAW 264.7). After 24 h of coculture with the various materials, both L929 and Raw 264.7 cells demonstrated viability exceeding 90%, indicating excellent biosafety (Figs. 5A and S4 A-C).

Elevated ROS levels intensify the inflammatory environment of periodontitis, resulting in cellular and tissue damage while contributing to alveolar bone resorption and degradation of periodontal support tissues [3, 46]. Given the ability of CUR and GL to scavenge ROS, their intracellular ROS-scavenging activity and protective effects against oxidative damage were investigated in vitro [47]. Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) induced oxidative damage in L929 cells, substantially reducing cell viability. Treatment with varying concentrations of CURM@GLH increased the viability of L929 cells. The cell viability tended to increase, followed by stabilization, indicating that CURM@GLH was able to alleviate the oxidative damage caused by H₂O₂ in the cells (Fig. 5B). The protective effect tended to stabilize at CUR concentrations greater than 7.5 µg/mL, which was selected for further study. After L929 cells were treated with H₂O₂, the protective effects of GLH, free CUR, CURM, and CURM@GLH were compared. As shown in Fig. 5C and D, compared with GLH, free CUR, and CURM, CURM@GLH significantly increased the survival of L929 cells. These findings revealed that CURM@GLH provided superior cytoprotection against H₂O₂-induced oxidative damage.

The RAW264.7 macrophage line was selected because of the essential role of macrophages in ROS production and their involvement in macrophage activation [48]. This cell line serves as a standard model for studying immune responses, inflammatory cytokine release, and macrophage polarization. The ROS probe 2,7-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) was utilized to evaluate the ability of the probe to scavenge intracellular ROS. As shown in Fig. 5E-G, the LPS group presented increased ROS production compared with the control group, as evidenced by intense fluorescence. Conversely, GLH, free CUR, CURM, and CURM@GLH all decreased the intracellular ROS levels to varying degrees. The increased water solubility of CUR in CURM resulted in a greater ROS-scavenging capacity than that of free CUR. The combined effect of CUR and GL further enhances the reduction capability, with CURM@GLH showing the most substantial decrease. These results indicate that CURM@GLH possesses superior antioxidant properties and effectively reduces oxidative damage to cells.

Anti-inflammatory property of CURM@GLH

Macrophages undergo polarization under various conditions, developing distinct functional phenotypes. The M1 and M2 phenotypes exhibit proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory characteristics, respectively. Periodontitis pathogenesis involves both destructive and reparative phases associated with different macrophage populations [49]. Research indicates that transitioning macrophages from the proinflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype promotes tissue repair and accelerates inflammation resolution [50]. To examine the effect of CURM@GLH on immunoregulation through macrophage polarization, the periodontitis microenvironment was replicated in vitro using LPS induction. As shown in Fig. 6A-D, the proportion of M1-type CD80 + macrophages was greater in the LPS group than in the control group, confirming successful M1 polarization. Following treatment, all the treatment groups presented an increase in M2 macrophages and a decrease in M1 macrophages. Compared with GLH, free CUR, and CURM, CURM@GLH had more pronounced effects.

During the innate immune response, macrophages undergo phenotypic alterations in response to inflammation and secrete various cytokines to counter stimuli [51, 52]. As shown in Fig. 6E-J, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analyses revealed that LPS significantly elevated the secretion of M1-associated proinflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β) across all groups. These results indicate that the immune microenvironment in periodontitis predominantly directs macrophage polarization toward the M1 phenotype. Among the three treatment groups, free CUR showed the weakest inhibitory effect on IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β gene expression compared with the LPS group. Additionally, the CURM formulation, which enhances CUR solubility, exhibited stronger inhibition of M1 markers than did free CUR. The synergistic effect of CUR and GL on CURM@GLH resulted in the most substantial reductions in IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β at both the gene and protein levels. These findings collectively demonstrate that CURM@GLH exerts cellular anti-inflammatory effects by facilitating macrophage polarization from the proinflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype.

Therapeutic evaluation in a periodontitis rat model

A rat model of chronic periodontitis was established by placing a ligature around the maxillary second molar (M2) for four weeks to evaluate the in vivo effects of CURM@GLH treatment. Following ligature removal, the rats received treatments at seven-day intervals for a total of four applications, corresponding to the hydrogel’s in vitro drug release profile (Fig. 7A). Minocycline, a clinically established antibiotic, served as the positive control for comparing therapeutic efficacy. Micro-CT imaging was utilized to assess alveolar bone loss (Fig. 7B). The distance between the cementoenamel junction and the alveolar ridge apex (CEJ-ABC), marked in red, indicates the alveolar bone height around the tooth. The increased CEJ-ABC distance in the ligature group validated the chronic periodontitis model, as illustrated in Fig. 6C. Compared with the CURM@GLH treatment groups, the GLH and free CUR groups presented greater CEJ-ABC distances, indicating the limited therapeutic efficacy of single drug-loaded hydrogels. The comparable CEJ‒ABC distances between the minocycline- and CURM@GLH-treated groups suggested equivalent bone mass restoration. Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 7D-F, the trend of other bone parameters in the CURM@GLH treatment group was similar to that of the CEJ-ABC distance, including an increase in the bone volume fraction (BV/TV, %), an increase in the trabecular thickness (Tb. Th, mm), and a decrease in the trabecular separation (Tb. Sp, mm), which improved bone catabolism and had a positive therapeutic effect on periodontitis.

Dihydroethidium (DHE) staining was employed to measure the level of ROS, which can penetrate cell membranes and undergo specific ROS-mediated oxidation, resulting in red fluorescence. As demonstrated in Fig. 7G, compared with the ligature group, both the CURM@GLH and minocycline groups presented significantly reduced red fluorescence signals, demonstrating their in vivo ability to scavenge ROS.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome staining were performed to histologically evaluate inflammatory status, fibrous tissue distribution, and collagen fiber organization in periodontal tissues (Fig. 8A and B). In the control group, healthy periodontal tissues exhibited well-organized and densely packed periodontal ligament fibers. In contrast, the ligature group showed pronounced tissue destruction, characterized by disrupted elastic and collagen fibers, sparse and disorganized fiber arrangement, and extensive inflammatory cell infiltration. Compared with the ligature and other treatment groups, CURM@GLH treatment markedly reduced inflammatory cell infiltration, as further supported by semi-quantitative histological scoring (Fig. S6). In addition, Masson’s trichrome staining revealed denser collagen deposition and a more orderly fiber architecture in the CURM@GLH group, which was quantitatively confirmed by a significantly increased collagen volume fraction (Fig. S6). Local inflammatory response assessment was performed on the rats. Immunohistochemical analysis of inflammatory markers (Fig. 8C and D) revealed significantly elevated IL-1β and TNF-α levels in periodontitis-affected periodontal tissues than in control periodontal tissues. CURM@GLH treatment effectively inhibited the inflammatory response and decreased the IL-1β and TNF-α levels. Collectively, these results indicated that CURM@GLH effectively alleviated periodontal inflammation and promoted the restoration of periodontal tissue structure, highlighting its therapeutic potential in periodontal tissue regeneration.

Histological examination of tissue sections from vital organs confirmed the biosafety of CURM@GLH, with no significant intergroup variation, supporting its potential clinical application (Fig. S7). To further evaluate the local biosafety of the hydrogel system, gingival tissues were collected after completion of the treatment period and subjected to histological examination. High-magnification H&E staining revealed intact tissue architecture without evidence of foamy macrophage accumulation, foreign body giant cell formation, or abnormal chronic inflammatory infiltration in the CURM@GLH-treated group (Fig. S8 A-F).

These results demonstrate the effectiveness of CURM@GLH in reducing alveolar bone loss and ameliorating periodontal symptoms in ligated rats, indicating its therapeutic potential for the treatment of periodontitis.

Discussion

Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease driven by persistent oxidative stress and immune dysregulation, which severely impairs the therapeutic efficacy of conventional antimicrobial therapies. In this work, we developed an injectable dual-drug hydrogel (CURM@GLH) for the local co-delivery of CUR and GL, aiming to modulate the periodontal immune microenvironment rather than merely suppressing bacterial burden.

Encapsulation of CUR into PEG-DS to form CURM significantly improved its stability and bioavailability, addressing a major bottleneck limiting the therapeutic application of free CUR. The stable micellar structure of CURM, in combination with the sustained release behavior provided by the hydrogel matrix, likely contributed to the enhanced antioxidative and anti-inflammatory effects observed in cellular assays. Meanwhile, GLH acted not only as a localized drug delivery reservoir but also as an active immunomodulatory matrix, as GL itself possesses inherent anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties. The improved therapeutic outcomes of CURM@GLH are therefore best explained by the complementary actions of CUR and GL in immunoregulation, rather than a strictly defined synergistic effect [53].

CURM@GLH effectively reduced intracellular reactive oxygen species accumulation, suppressed proinflammatory cytokine expression, and promoted macrophage polarization toward an M2-like phenotype. Given the central role of macrophage-driven inflammatory dysregulation in periodontal tissue destruction, such targeted immune modulation is particularly relevant for restoring tissue homeostasis. In vivo, local administration of CURM@GLH markedly alleviated periodontal inflammatory infiltration and alveolar bone loss in a ligature-induced rat periodontitis model, exhibiting therapeutic efficacy comparable to that of minocycline—the clinical gold standard for adjunctive periodontal therapy. Importantly, this effect was achieved through host-modulatory mechanisms and biofilm inhibition, rather than antibiotic-dependent bacterial killing alone, which may offer advantages for long-term clinical safety and the avoidance of antimicrobial resistance.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Although sustained drug release and hydrogel degradation were systematically evaluated in vitro, the in vivo degradation behavior and drug release kinetics may be influenced by the complex and heterogeneous periodontal pocket microenvironment. In addition, formal quantitative synergy analysis was not conducted, and the observed enhancement should therefore be interpreted as a functional improvement arising from complementary mechanisms. Future studies incorporating quantitative drug combination analyses and long-term biosafety assessments, and clinical translation studies in larger animal models will further clarify the translational potential of this CURM@GLH system for periodontitis treatment.

Conclusion

This study reports the development of an injectable dual-drug hydrogel (CURM@GLH) composed of natural bioactive compounds (CUR and GL) with integrated antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antibacterial properties for periodontitis treatment. In contrast to conventional clinical treatments focused primarily on antibacterial effects, this injectable dual-drug hydrogel achieves therapeutic efficacy by simultaneously modulating the local pathological immune microenvironment and inhibiting dental plaque biofilm formation. The hydrogel system enables sustained release of CUR and GL, which exert complementary antioxidative and immunomodulatory activities, thereby collectively protecting cells from oxidative damage, reducing intracellular ROS levels, regulating macrophage polarization, and suppressing the expression and secretion of proinflammatory cytokines. Functionally, the system effectively reduced M1 macrophage levels while increasing M2 macrophage proportions in vitro. In a periodontal inflammation animal model, CURM@GLH demonstrated substantial anti-inflammatory and antioxidative effects, effectively preventing alveolar bone loss. PEG-DS increased CUR solubility while inhibiting bacterial adhesion, significantly contributing to the anti-biofilm activity of the hydrogel. Compared with free CUR, CURM demonstrated superior antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and biofilm inhibition activities because of enhanced CUR solubility. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the CURM@GLH is a safe, robust, and versatile platform for chronic periodontitis treatment.
