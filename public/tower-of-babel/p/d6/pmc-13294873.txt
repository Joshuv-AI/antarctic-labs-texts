Characterization of the Anti-Alzheimer Drug Lecanemab by Mass Spectrometry
Authors: Jiaojiao Sha, Zijin Geng, Shanyu Qi, Liangjia Du, Qianqian Hu, Minqi Cai, Dezhu Chen, Yiqiang Chen, Siyuan Liu, Hongyan Song, Tianze Ling, Cheng Chang, Bing Bai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lecanemab is a humanized mouse monoclonal antibody that is clinically approved for treating Alzheimer’s disease by binding to amyloid oligomers, protofibrils, and insoluble fibrils to clear Aβ aggregates in the brain. Although therapeutically effective, lecanemab is also associated with side effects, and its quality might vary among different batches due to impurities and modifications. Herein, we provide proteomics, intact protein, and N-glycoproteomics analyses of lecanemab by liquid chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry. We found that the lecanemab drug is highly pure but displays two major peaks in the chromatography and contains many species with close molecular weights around 150 kDa under mass spectrometric scanning. There were 40 N-glycans found at the N304 site of lecanemab of the heavy chain. Further analysis by MSFragger identified other modifications and additional glycosites. These results provide data as resource for the identification of critical quality attributes of lecanemab in studying its therapeutic effectiveness, side effects, and possible batch-to-batch qualities.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is an aging-related irreversible neurodegenerative disorder and is hallmarked by amyloid species and neurofibrillary tangles in brain tissues. − The major component of the amyloid aggregates is the Aβ peptides from the cleavage of the protein APP. These peptides are aggregation-prone, and some are neurotoxic. Deregulation of this APP cleavage and accumulation of Aβ peptides are considered the essential initiating event in AD pathogenesis, − as currently, three familial AD genes (APP, PSEN1, and PSEN2) are all directly involved in the cleavage of APP to generate Aβ. − Therefore, timely clearance of excessive Aβ from the brain is probably the most efficient way to control this devastating disease, especially at the early stages before massive death of neurons and the occurrence of dementia. −

Lecanemab (BAN2401) is a humanized IgG1 monoclonal antibody that is currently used in clinics for treating Alzheimer’s disease by binding amyloid oligomers, protofibrils, and insoluble fibrils to clear Aβ aggregates in the brain. , This monoclonal antibody was initially developed in mouse as mAb158 (IgG2a) in 2006 with Aβ42Arc protofibrils as the immunogen. Its humanized version called lecanemab gained accelerated approval by the FDA as Leqembi in January 2023 and was converted to traditional approval six months later. −

In contrast to the first generation of anti-Aβ mAbs (bapineuzumab, solanezumab, and crenezumab) that target monomeric species of Aβ42 or senile plaques and show either evidence of Aβ clearance or modest clinical effectiveness, the second generation of anti-Aβ mAbs (lecanemab, aducanumab, and gantenerumab) demonstrates increased selectivity for soluble Aβ oligomers. Lecanemab prefers soluble Aβ protofibrils (large oligomers) to reduce amyloid in the early stage of AD and moderately slow down cognitive decline with non-negligible adverse events. −

The physicochemical properties determine the therapeutic effectiveness and other biological activities of mAbs such as purity and residual process impurities, amino acid sequence, size, charge, higher-order structure, glycosylation, and other post-translational modifications. − Heterogeneity in various forms of sizes, charges, oxidation, and other chemical reactions can also be developed during manufacturing and storage, and this can also result in batch-to-batch product inconsistencies.

Here, we use mass-spectrometry-based approaches to analyze lecanemab regarding its purity, molecular size, and glycosylation, as well as other possible protein modifications. Data of this study might be useful not only for characterization of its critical quality attributes but also for future studies on its side effects and novel therapeutic mechanisms to treat this globally challenging disease.

Materials and Methods

Source of Lecanemab

Lecanemab (Laqembi, Eisai Inc., 200 mg/2 mL) was purchased from a drug store in our city (Nanjing, China) with the National Drug Code 8445015 and the serial number 0000634985174. It was manufactured by Biogen US Corporation with the lot number 8080900. The production and expiration dates were March 31, 2024, and March 30, 2026, respectively.

SDS-PAGE and Western blot

10 μL lecanemab was taken and diluted in 1× LDS (lithium dodecyl sulfate) sample loading buffer (NP0008, Thermo Fisher Scientific) to make 20 μg/μL and then further to 2 μg/μL concentrations. DTT (1 M), or deionized water as the negative control of non-reductive condition, was added to the 2 μg/μL sample at the final concentration of 10 mM, which was then heated at 75 °C for 10 min for reduction. The 2 μg/μL sample and its 2-fold serial dilutions in 1× LDS buffer were then received by SDS-PAGE (4–12% gradient Bis–Tris gel, M00654, SurePAGE, Genscript), followed by Coomassie blue staining.

Proteomic Analysis of Lecanemab by LC–MS/MS

For the proteomic analysis of lecanemab by LC–MS/MS, three samples of 50 μg lecanemab each together with the three deionized water as the negative controls in the same volume as that of lecanemab were taken and precipitated with 80% acetonitrile. After washing with 80% acetonitrile, the precipitates (not visible in the blank samples) were resuspended in 8 M urea (in 25 mM TEAB, pH 8.5) with the addition of 0.5 μg LysC for 4 h or longer at room temperature until fully dissolved or became clear. After dilution of the urea to 2 M by 3 volumes of 25 mM TEAB (pH 8.5), 0.5 μg trypsin was added for further in-solution digestion. After 4–8 h or overnight at room temperature, DTT and IAA were added sequentially for reduction and alkylation, respectively, followed by DTT quenching. The samples were then acidified with 1% formic acid for peptide desalting by a C18 column. The desalted peptides were dried and resuspended from which 1 μg was loaded through the column (50 μm ID and 30 cm long, packed with C18, 1.9 μm), separated mainly by a 5–25% gradient of acetonitrile generated by solvent B (80% acetonitrile, 0.2% formic acid, and 3% DMSO) and solvent A (0.2% formic acid and 3% DMSO) for 1 h at the flow rate of 0.3 μL/min, and scanned by the mass spectrometer (Q Exactive HF-X, Thermo) under the DDA mode.

The raw MS data files were processed by the Proteome Discoverer 2.4 software (Thermo Fisher) and searched by the Sequest HT engine against the human proteome database (UP000005640) from the Uniprot, with incorporation of the amino acid sequences of the lecanemab heavy and light chains. Specific parameters were basically as the default, including full trypsin, maximum cleavages of 2, peptide length ranging from 6 to 144, 10 ppm in precursor mass tolerance, and 0.02 Da in fragment mass tolerance, b and y ions in spectrum matching, maximum modifications of 3 and 15.995 Da (M) with N-terminal acetylation, methionine loss or both in the dynamic modifications, and carbamidomethyl (+57.021) in the static modifications. The false discovery rates (FDRs) for the protein and peptide identification were set at 0.01 and 0.05, respectively. The sequence of lecanemab was downloaded from the KEGG database with the entry number D11678 (https://www.kegg.jp/entry/D11678) and constructed into the database for mapping. As lecanemab is a humanized mouse IgG, both human and mouse proteomes from Uniprot were used as the protein database for peptide matching and protein derivation.

Intact Protein Analysis of Lecanemab by LC–MS

For the intact protein analysis by LC–MS, 20 μg of lecanemab protein sample was desalted by a 10-kDa centrifugal filter with 0.1% formic acid. The reconstituted sample was loaded onto the reverse-phase HPLC system (Vanquish, Thermo) through a column (MAbPac RP 4 μm, 2.1 × 50 mm2, Thermo) to run for 15 min under the main gradient of acetonitrile (10–70%) in 0.1% formic acid, and scanned by the mass analyzer Q Exactive HF-X (Thermo).

The molecular weight was deconvoluted by the software Biopharma Finder 5.1 (Thermo). Chromatogram parameters included an m/z range (400–6000), chromatogram trace type (TIC), and sensitivity (high), and the source spectra were generated by average over the selected retention time by using the large-molecular chromatographic peak detection. Deconvolution parameters included ReSpect (isotopically unresolved), an output mass range (140,000–160,000), and deconvoluted spectra display mode [(isotopic profile (new)], as well as a deconvolution mass tolerance (20.00 ppm) for charge-state distribution and the choice of peak model (Intract Protein).

N-Glycoproteomics Analysis of Lecanemab by LC–MS/MS

For N-glycoproteomics analysis, the dried peptide pellets were resuspended in 200 μL of 80% acetonitrile/5% TFA solution and loaded onto a zic-HILIC column (Merck Millipore, 5 μm, 200 Å) pre-equilibrated with 80% acetonitrile/5% TFA. After washing with more than five column volumes of 80% acetonitrile/5% TFA, the N-glycopeptides were sequentially eluted with 100 μL 0.1% TFA and 50 mM ABC. The eluants were combined, dried, and resuspended in ultrapure water.

The peptides were loaded onto a trap column (200 μm/inner diameter, 5 cm long) packed with C18 beads (Phenomenex Jupiter C18, 5 μm, 300 Å) and then separated through an analytical column (75 μm/inner diameter, 75 cm long) packed with the same beads by the gradient of acetonitrile between 9 and 40% in 0.1% formic acid for about 200 min. The parameters for MS1 included: 700–2000 (m/z range), 60k (mass resolution, m/z of 200), 3 × 106 (automatic gain control target), and 20 ms (maximum injection time). MS2 setups included: 30k (mass resolution, m/z of 200), Top 20 (data-dependent acquisition), 5 × 105 (automatic gain control target), 250 ms (maximum injection time), 1.4 m/z (precursor ion isolation window), 30.0 s (dynamic exclusion), and 20, 30, and 31% of HCD-stepped normalized collision energies.

The raw MS data were searched and processed by the software GPseeker3. The isotope peak abundance cutoff, isotope peak mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) deviation, and isotope peak abundance deviation for both the precursor and fragment ions were set to 40%, 20 ppm, and 50%, respectively. The parameters for intact N-glycopeptide spectrum matches (GPSMs) included: Y1 ion Top4, the minimum percentage of matched fragment ions of the peptide backbone ≥10%, the minimum matched fragment ion of the N-glycan moiety ≥1, Top2 hits with Top1 hit(s) having the lowest P score, and G-bracket ≥1. The G-bracket for a given N-glycosite was calculated according to the number of peptide backbone b/y fragment ion pairs, each of which can independently and uniquely be localized to the N-glycosite. The GF score of a given N-glycan sequence structure was derived based on the number of structure-diagnostic fragment ions, each of which can independently distinguish the structure from all the other candidate structures with the same molecular formula (including the same monosaccharide composition).

Analysis of Modifications of Lecanemab by MSFragger

MS raw files generated by LC–MS/MS were searched by the software FragPipe (version 22.0) , against a combined human reference protein database containing 37,652 sequences, including the UniProt human proteome database, the UniProt mouse proteome database, and the amino acid sequences of lecanemab heavy and light chains.

The analysis employed the software’s built-in “Open” workflow, utilizing the MSFragger localization-aware open search (LOS) algorithm with deisotoping, mass calibration, parameter optimization, and monoisotope correction. Other parameter selections were: mass range (150–500 Da), up to 2 missed cleavages, PeptideProphet with extended mass model, Crystal-C for artifact removal, Precursor ion quantification by FreeQuant, and PTM-Shepherd for mass shift summarization. The cutoff of FDR against the target–decoy database was 1% for both proteins and peptides.

The raw MSFragger search results were further filtered manually according to these criteria: (1) it has to be naturally occurring; modifications like “13C(2) 15N(2)”, “13C3 15N1 label for SILAC”, “13C4Methionine label”, “Water quenched monolink of EGS cross-linker”, etc. were excluded; (2) modifications from common artifacts, chemical derivatives, or other uncontrollable experimental processing such as “Replacement of 2 protons by magnesium”, “Michael addition with methylamine”, “methylglyoxal-derived argpyrimidine”, etc.; (3) the amino acid to be modified in the annotated modification had to be truly present in the peptide; e.g., a peptide identified to have “tryptophan oxidation to kynurenin” but actually lacking tryptophan was excluded; (4) terminal modifications had to be on the terminal amino acids from the protein terminal peptides; and (5) modifications annotated as first, second, and third isotopic peaks were filtered out. After all, the peptides bearing the same modifications potentially on the same amino acid site with two or more PSMs were finally summarized into the list (Table ).

Results

Protein Content Analysis of Lecanemab

We first verified the effectiveness of lecanemab in binding to Aβ peptides by the dot immunoblot (Figure S1), and then used SDS-PAGE to visualize the protein purity of lecanemab, as size variants are commonly generated during the production and storage of recombinant monoclonal antibodies, in which high-molecular-weight species (such as modifications and aggregates) and low-molecular-weight species (such as fragments and degradants) also referred to as product-related impurities, can potentially elicit immune response and affect drug effectiveness and pharmacokinetics.

Under the nonreducing condition, lecanemab at 0.25 μg loading exhibited a major band at the size above 170 kDa and a minor band below. More bands started to appear and five additional bands around 100, 70, 50, and 25 kDa were seen at the sample loading of 2 μg (Figure A). These bands other than the antibody itself were probably due to loss of a light chain, heavy chain dimers, half mAbs, Fc-Fab mAbs and Fabs, mispaired light chains, or other formats commonly resulting from the processes of antibody production and storage. In contrast, in the reducing condition, there were only two bands around 50 and 25 kDa that can be observed at both low and high sample loadings (Figure A), indicating that all bands previously seen in the original sample without reduction were all from the heavy chain and light chain of lecanemab. This also demonstrated that proteins other than lecanemab in the sample were very few, and this drug was highly pure.

We also used antihuman IgG heavy chain to blot these samples and found the bands around 50 kDa and above in the gel contained the heavy chain, as expected (Figure B).

To further analyze the protein contents of lecanemab, we performed proteomic analysis by LC–MS/MS in triplicate with deionized water as the negative control (Figure C). The main protein profiled from the drug sample was lecanemab with other proteins, including immunoglobulins, albumin, SERPINA1, etc. (Figure D and Table S1). To examine whether those proteins other than lecanemab were from the drug sample itself or from the liquid chromatography and mass spectrometry system, we compared the proteomic results of the lecanemab sample with those of the blank controls using peptides unique to lecanemab. The result showed that lecanemab was the only protein highly enriched in the lecanemab sample (Figure E). Peptides unique to lecanemab were only seen in the lecanemab sample, and peptides shared with other immunoglobulins were present in both the lecanemab samples and the blank controls at similar levels (Figures S2A,B and S3). These indicate that the lecanemab drug is highly pure in the protein content.

In this proteomic analysis, both the heavy chain and the light chain of lecanemab were nearly fully sequenced except for the amino acid sequence between 155 and 225, in which the tryptic peptides were either too long or too short (Figure F), unsuitable for the LC–MS/MS analysis. This verified the accuracy of the amino sequence of lecanemab.

Intact Protein Analysis of Lecanemab by LC–MS

To examine more closely the heterogeneity of lecanemab, we performed an intact protein analysis by LC–MS. There were two well-separated major peaks appearing in the liquid chromatography (Figure A), indicating two major protein formats in this drug, which were probably the two strong bands seen in the gel (Figure A). Combined spectra scanned from these two peaks, respectively, showed that the distributions of their m/z values were very close (Figure B). By deconvolution, the molecular masses were highly complexed for the first peak, ranging from 140 to 160 kDa, while concentrated at 150 kDa for the second peak (Figure C). In both spectra, the neighboring peaks showed a few thousand in the difference of their molecular masses. Proteins shown in the gel bands around 100, 70, 50, and 25 kDa bands (Figure A) were not seen in the deconvoluted results, probably due to inefficient liquid chromatography and overlay of their spectra.

N-Glycoproteomics Analysis of Lecanemab by LC–MS/MS

Glycosylation is one of the common posttranslational modifications (PTMs) that plays an important role in antibody effector functions, binding of Fc- domain with Fc receptors and complement C1q, antibody stability, conformation, and pharmacokinetics. , The glycans on the antibody at the same amino acid site could be highly diverse and exert different and even opposite effects on the half-life, antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, and complement-dependent cytotoxicity functions of mAbs; and some particular glycans may elicit autoantibodies.

In the N-glycoproteomics analysis, there were 40 glycans identified on the amino acid site N304, which corresponds to the N299 (Figures A and S4A), a well-known glycosylation site on human IgG1. Among the 40 glycans, the most abundant glycan according to the mass spectral signal of peak area is N4H3F0S0, followed by N4H4F0S0 and N2H5F0S0, consistent with the report. ,, A recent report that has studied 209 FDA-approved therapeutic antibodies expressed by CHO, NS0, and Sp2/0 cell lines on their glycan profiles and found ten predominant Fc N-glycans with six glycans common to all systems. For example, in this report, among the Fc glycan profiles of 123 IgG antibodies produced in CHO cells, the most abundant glycans include G0F, G1F, G2F, G0, M5, etc. In contrast, the most abundant glycan on lecanemab identified in this study was G0. This is probably due to a different expression system used for lecanemab production, as glycan profiles were strongly dependent on the expression system according to this report. These three glycans occupy about 57% among the total 40 glycans identified (Figures B and S5A). Analysis of Modifications of Lecanemab by MSFragger

Posttranslational modifications are common in mAbs. These modifications include to glycosylation, C-terminal lysine clipping, N-terminal pyro-Glu formation, deamidation, oxidation, glycation, cysteinylation, trisulfide bonding, hydroxylation, nonenzymatic fragmentation (clips), isomerization, etc. Among these, isoaspartate (deamidation of asparagines in the CDR of IgG molecules), glycosylation, and oxidation (principally methionine and tryptophan) are particularly important in quality attributes of mAbs that affect their biological activities and biopharmaceuticals.

Using the prevalent software MSFragger, 1191 spectra were identified to have modifications among all 4567 spectra identified by this software (Table S3). After screening, potentially true modifications with 2 or more PSMs were listed (Table ), including N-terminal cyclization (also called pyroglutamate formation, pyro-Glu, one of the major N-terminal modifications of mAbs), tryptophan oxidation to kynurenin, hydroxylation, dehydration, quinone, and others. It is notable that besides the glycans profiled in N304 of the heavy chain in the previous N-glycoproteomics analysis, MSFragger identified a hexose on a peptide of the heavy chain in the constant domain and four peptides of the light chain in both the light and heavy chains. MSFragger also found an N-acetylhexosamine on one of these four peptides of the light chain (Figure S5) in the variable domain, which might possibly affect the binding of lecanemab to Aβ peptides. These are probably O-glycosylations or glycosylated on amino acids other than asparagine, since they are not detected in the previous N-glycoproteomic analysis (Figure S5).

In addition to glycosylation, oxidation was also extensively detected, and it was located to methionine, tryptophan, and other possible unassigned amino acids (Table S3). These oxidation events especially in the variable domains of heavy or light chain, such as the potential oxidation of methionine within the peptide “LSCSASGFTFSSFGMHWVR” from amino acid 20–38 in the heavy chain, might also affect the binding of lecanemab to Aβ species.

Besides, deamidation is also commonly seen in mAb, and it involves conversion of asparagine to a mix of isoaspartate and aspartate at the ratio of about 3:1. Deamidation was also extensively identified in the raw MSFragger result (Table S3), but few had passed the criteria to be present in the final list (Table ). Nevertheless, it might be truly present, as it is usually generated slowly.

Discussion

In this study, some protein bands demonstrated on the polyacrylamide gel were not detected in the LC–MS intact protein analysis. Besides the possible reason that those missed smaller protein species did not perform well or were not within the scanning range, overlay of their m/z(s) with the two peaks of protein species was more likely the case, as the deconvolution algorithm considers the full antibody around 150 kDa as the target of first choice. It is notable that many post-translational modifications can contribute to the charges of proteins. For example, deamidation, glycation, sialylation, and oxidation increase the negative charges of the protein, while C-terminal lysine and cyclization of glutamic acid/N-terminal glutamine to pyroglutamate introduces positive charges. These modifications significantly increase the complexity of mass spectrometric analysis. The large number of PTMs identified by MSFragger in this study (Tables and S3) almost covered all these modifications and contributed to the complexity of the charges on lecanemab. It is also worth mentioning that as a traditional method, SDS-PAGE is simple and straightforward to demonstrate those multiple protein species, demonstrating it as an efficient complementary approach in studying mAb purity.

These different protein species might be different combinations of heavy and light chains, as after reduction, only two of these chains were shown. To solve this, the disulfide bonds and free thiols could be determined by mass spectrometry, in which the 13C-labeled IAA (iodoacetamide) could be added to alkylate free thiols of the protein, followed by the reduction with dithiothreitol and alkylation again by native IAA. The disulfide bonds and free thiols can be quantitatively differentiated by the 13C and 12C IAA adducts by MS.

The analysis of PTMs by MSFragger is a uniqueness in this study and will become an advantageous tool in studying mAb modifications. , The mAbs can have many PTMs as reported, including N-glycosylation, glutamine pyroglutamination, asparagine deamidation, methionine and tryptophan oxidation, lysine glycation and loss, etc. MSFragger can not only identify these common modifications but also find other ones, providing a comprehensive view of PTMs of lecanemab with mapped sites. It is noteworthy that in this study, among the total 4567 identified PSMs, 1191 displayed modifications on the peptides, indicating a significant portion of protein copies bearing certain types of modification. This is probably more than what we expect usually and increases the protein diversity remarkably, imposing a great challenge in mAb quality assurance and molecular attribute characterization. In addition, the MSFragger analysis revealed that additional glycans were found on both the heavy and the light chains that might affect the antigen-binding affinity, IgG effector functions, and the stability of the lecanemab. ,, Nevertheless, MSFragger is not sophisticated and needs more examinations for its results to be valid.

Conclusions

In this study, we characterized lecanemab by using mass spectrometry as the main approach. Lecanemab showed several protein species that were expected to all from the heavy and light chains as visualized on the polyacrylamide gel, possibly resulting from loss of the light chain or other combination of subunits. It demonstrated two major peaks on the liquid chromatogram under UV detection, which were two clusters of protein species that vary in size by a few thousand daltons, similar to the molecular weights of glycans. N-Glycoproteomics identified 40 composite glycans with N4H3F0S0, followed by N4H4F0S0 and N2H5F0S0 as the major ones. MSFragger found a comprehensive view of modifications with additional glycans on both heavy and light chains of lecanemab. The protein species with different sizes and modifications are highly diverse and complex in the lecanemab. These data could be used in future studies for further investigation of this anti-Alzheimer’s drug.
