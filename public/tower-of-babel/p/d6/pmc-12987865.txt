Molecular characterization and functional effect on canie peripheral blood mononuclear cells of phosphoglycerate kinase from Echinococcus granulosus
Authors: Zhao Jiaxin, Chen Xuke, Huang Juncheng, Wei Linying, Zhang Yuxia, Zhang Yanyan, Sun Yan, Bo Xinwen, Wang Zhengrong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCystic echinococcosis (CE), a major zoonotic disease caused by Echinococcus granulosus, poses significant global health challenges. Definitive hosts are crucial for its transmission and control, yet the immune interactions between E. granulosus and these hosts are not well understood. Research indicates that the parasite’s phosphoglycerate kinase might play a role in immune regulation, though the specifics are unclear. To explore this, the gene’s open reading frame was cloned and analyzed with bioinformatics, and the PGK protein was produced using prokaryotic expression technology. The protein’s antigenicity was evaluated using Western blot, and its localization was studied during E. granulosus development. Recombinant Eg-PGK’s effect on PBMCs was analyzed via qPCR. The ORF sequence of the Eg-PGK gene was successfully cloned. Bioinformatics showed the protein lacks transmembrane domains and signal peptides but has multiple B-cell epitopes. Its secondary structure is mainly α-helices (45.06%) and random coils (39.04%). SDS-PAGE confirmed the recombinant protein’s molecular weight as 62 kDa. Western blot showed it elicited a strong immune response in positive canine serum. Eg-PGK expression was much higher in adults than in protoscoleces (P < 0.0001). Western blot showed the recombinant protein was well-recognized by canine serum, indicating a strong immune response. It also boosted canine PBMC proliferation at 10 µg/mL (P < 0.001) and increased NO secretion at 5 µg/mL (P < 0.001). qPCR showed it affected IFN-γ secretion and expression in canine PBMCs. qPCR results demonstrated that higher concentrations of rEg-PGK led to decreased expression of Bcl-2 and GASP1. Combined with the flow cytometric data, these findings confirm that the antigen exerts an anti-apoptotic effect. Meanwhile, the increased expression of IL-18 suggests a pro-pyroptotic role of rEg-PGK. Thus, Eg-PGK inhibits cell apoptosis, promotes pyroptosis, and enhances the secretion of Th1-type cytokines, thereby boosting the host immune response and facilitating parasite clearance. These results provide valuable insights and experimental basis for the development of vaccines targeting the definitive host against echinococcosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00436-026-08625-1.Keywords: Echinococcus granulosus, Phosphoglycerate kinase, Peripheral blood mononuclear cells, Cytokines, Apoptosis, Pyroptosis

Introduction

Echinococcosis, also referred to as Hydatidosis, is a zoonotic disease caused by the larvae of the Echinococcus genus. E. granulosus is currently identified as one of the four species within this genus capable of infecting humans (Borhani M, et al. 2024). The distribution of E. granulosus is global, with the disease being highly endemic in regions such as Western China, Central Asia, South America, Mediterranean countries, and Eastern Africa (Eckert et al. 2001). On a global scale, echinococcosis presents a significant disease burden, particularly in low-income and resource-constrained settings. Epidemiological forecasts indicate that both the incidence and prevalence of echinococcosis are expected to increase over the next 25 years (Lai et al. 2025), highlighting its significance as an urgent public health issue requiring immediate attention. The parasite utilizes cloven-hoofed animals, such as sheep, goats, and cattle, as intermediate hosts, while canines, including dogs, foxes, and wolves, serve as definitive hosts (Aziz et al. 2025). Humans act as accidental intermediate hosts in the lifecycle of echinococcosis. The parasite is expected to significantly impact public health and impose a substantial economic burden. Statistical data indicate that the annual economic loss attributable to animal echinococcosis is approximately USD 1.2 billion (Hong-Yu S, et al. 2023).

Over the past decade, an integrated strategy that combines praziquantel treatment in canines (Shao et al. 2023a, b)with EG95 vaccination in ovine hosts has been proposed for the management of echinococcosis. While this approach has demonstrated efficacy in disease control, it falls short of achieving complete eradication (Borhani et al. 2024a, b). Vaccination serves as a supplementary measure to enhance the control of echinococcosis. Currently, vaccines are commercially available only for intermediate hosts, offering a protection rate of 96%–98% (Pan et al. 2017a, b.). However, no commercial vaccine has been developed for definitive hosts. Research on E. granulosus has predominantly concentrated on immune interactions with intermediate hosts, encompassing areas such as parasite-induced hepatic fibrosis(Wang et al. 2025), immune evasion mechanisms༈Gottstein et al. 2017༉, gut microbiota dynamics (Akhlaghi et al. 2024.), and comprehensive omics analyses—including total transcriptomics, metabolomics, spatial transcriptomics, and single-cell transcriptomics of infected organs (Wang Z, et al. 2023). In contrast, research on immune interactions with definitive hosts remains limited, primarily focusing on post-infection antibody monitoring (Allan JC, et al. 1992), analyses of cytokine expression changes (Riganò R, Profumo E and Siracusano A.1997), and investigations into intestinal microbiota following infection.To address this research gap, we have previously conducted a systematic study to characterize the transcriptional response profiles of canine intestinal epithelial cells (CIECs) to protoscolex infection (Wang et al. 2024.). As the definitive host of Echinococcus granulosus, the intestinal mucosa of dogs serves as the primary site for parasite colonization and host-parasite interactions. Canine intestinal epithelial cells, together with lamina propria immune cells, form a critical first line of defense: upon stimulation by E. granulosus protoscoleces, CIECs secrete chemokines (e.g., CXCL10, CCL2) to recruit macrophages and dendritic cells (DCs) to the infection site (Wang et al. 2024). However, our current understanding of the immune crosstalk between the parasite and its definitive host remains superficial. This knowledge gap not only hinders in-depth insights into the mechanisms underlying host-parasite immune interactions but also impedes the development of vaccines targeting definitive hosts. Thus, there is an urgent need for further in-depth investigation into this regulatory mechanism.

In our preliminary screening, the laboratory identified parasite-derived antigens, including phosphoglycerate kinase (PGK), that are capable of activating key intestinal immune cells in canines (unpublished data). PGK, a glycolytic enzyme found in various parasites, has been reported as a candidate molecule for the development of numerous drugs and vaccines (Kumar et al. 2017.). Existing studies have identified PGK as a potential drug target for the treatment of parasitic diseases such as fascioliasis, clonorchiasis, and trypanosomiasis (Qiu et al. 2024.). However, there is a paucity of research on PGK in relation to tapeworms. This experiment aims to elucidate the connection between PGK and E. granulosus. In this study, we analyzed the biological function of PGK and conducted its prokaryotic expression. Subsequently, cell experiments were performed to assess its impact on canine peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). The findings of this study demonstrate that PGK plays a critical role in the growth, development, and immunomodulation of E. granulosus. This antigen stimulates the secretion of Th1-type cytokines and induces host immune responses, thereby facilitating parasite clearance. Our initial experiments indicate that EgPGK demonstrates significant potential as a candidate for vaccine antigen development. Nonetheless, additional studies involving animal models are necessary to substantiate this hypothesis.

Protoscoleces of parasites and experimental animals Protoscoleces come from cysts in the liver and lungs of sheep suffering from hydatid disease. Protoscoleces and adults are provided by the State Key Laboratory of Genetic Improvement and Healthy Breeding of Sheep co-built by the Provincial Department of the Institute of Animal Husbandry and Veterinary Medicine, Xinjiang Academy of Agricultural Reclamation Sciences. Six-week-old BALB \ c mice were purchased from the Experimental Center of Xinjiang Medical University, and eight-month-old female beagle dogs were purchased from the Beagle Dog Breeding Center of Sichuan Musk Research Institute.

The Eg PGK protein sequence was downloaded from NCBI online website (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi)). The Clustal W program in MEGA11.0 software was used to compare multiple protein sequences. The phylogenetic tree was constructed by the neighbor-joining tree method (NJ), and the Bootstarp setting was selected to repeat the calculation 1000 times.

Cloning and expression of Eg PGK

RNA was extracted from protoscoleces utilizing RNA extraction kits (Tiangen, Beijing, China) and subsequently reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using reverse transcription system kits (Takara, Dalian, China). The EgPGK sequence was then amplified and cloned into the pMD19-T Vector (Takara, Dalian, China). A single bacterial colony was selected for polymerase chain reaction (PCR) identification, and the positive plasmid was sequenced to confirm accuracy. The correctly sequenced EgPGK plasmid was digested with restriction endonucleases and integrated into the pET32a(+) plasmid (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), followed by transformation into Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) (Tiangen, Beijing, China). The expression of recombinant EgPGK (rEgPGK) was induced using isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG). Solubility analysis of the recombinant protein indicated that rEgPGK was present in the form of inclusion bodies. The bacterial cells were treated with phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) (Suolaibao, Beijing, China), subjected to four freeze-thaw cycles using liquid nitrogen to facilitate lysis, sonicated on ice, and centrifuged at 4 °C for 30 min. The precipitate was collected and the inclusion bodies were solubilized using an 8 M urea solution. The supernatant and inclusion bodies were subjected to analysis using 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). The recombinant protein was subsequently purified utilizing a nickel ion (Ni²⁺) affinity chromatography column (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

Preparation of anti-rEgPGK polyclonal antibody serum

A cohort of six BALB/c mice, each aged six weeks, was allocated into two groups: an experimental group and a control group. In the experimental group, a mixture of 50 µg of recombinant protein and QA adjuvant in a 1:1 ratio was prepared, and each mouse received an immunization dose of 100 µL. The control group mice were administered phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) in an equivalent volume to the vaccine. Both groups were immunized concurrently. Each mouse underwent four immunization sessions, and 1 mL of blood was collected from the orbital sinus of each mouse. One week following the fourth immunization, serum containing polyclonal antibodies was extracted.

Western blotting analysis of rEgPGK

The recombinant protein underwent antigenic analysis via Western blotting. The purified recombinant EgPGK was transferred onto a PVDF membrane. The primary antibody used for rEgPGK was a custom-produced canine positive serum, diluted at 1:200, obtained from a dog infected with hydatid disease. The secondary antibody, diluted at 1:2000, was horseradish peroxidase-labeled rabbit anti-dog IgG (Solebol, Beijing, China). The immune response protein signal was visualized using an enhanced HRP-DAB chromogenic substrate kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China).

Immunofluorescence localization and RT-qPCR analysis of EgPGK

The protoscoleces and adult specimens were subjected to washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing dual antibiotics, followed by fixation, permeabilization, and blocking. Subsequently, a primary antibody was applied. This primary antibody was a custom-produced polyclonal antibody, utilized at a 1:100 dilution. FITC-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG antibody was used as the secondary antibody, at a 1:2000 dilution. Post-staining with propidium iodide (PI), fluorescence signals were visualized and captured using a laser confocal microscope. The expression levels of EgPGK in protoscoleces and 28-day-old worms were quantified via reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), with β-actin serving as the internal reference for normalization. The qPCR primer sequences are detailed in Table 1. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three independent experiments and were analyzed using the 2-ΔΔCT method as described by Livak and Schmittgen (2001). GraphPad Prism software was utilized for data visualization, and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Isolation of canine PBMCs

Blood samples were obtained from the forelimb vein of female Beagle dogs aged over eight months. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated using the canine PBMC isolation kit (Solaibo, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The isolated cells were then treated with 1 ml of red blood cell lysate and incubated on ice for 10 min. Cell viability was assessed using trypan blue staining and was found to be greater than 95%. The cells were subsequently resuspended in RPMI 1640 complete medium (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 2% penicillin-streptomycin (HyClone, Logan, UT, USA) and cultured in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C.

Proliferation and nitric oxide (NOs) analysis of rEgPGK co-cultured with canine PBMC cells

PBMCs at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells per 100 µl per well were cultured in 96-well plates and exposed to recombinant EgPGK at concentrations of 5, 10, 20, and 40 µg, as well as an equivalent volume of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Following a 24-hour incubation period at 37 °C, cell proliferation was assessed using the CCK-8 kit (Soleibao, Beijing, China), in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. Additionally, PBMCs at the same concentration were incubated with either recombinant EgPGK (5, 10, 20, and 40 µg) or PBS at 37 °C for 48 h. Nitric oxide (NO) production was subsequently measured using the Total NO Assay Kit (Addison, Beijing, China).

rEg PGK on four cytokines in canine peripheral blood mononuclear cells

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was used to detect the effects of recombinant protein at different concentrations on the transcription levels of canine cytokines, including interferon-γ (IFN-γ), interleukin-10 (IL-10), transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1), and interleukin-17 A (IL-17 A), in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). The specific procedures were as follows: Canine PBMCs were seeded into 24-well cell culture plates at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per well. The experimental groups were treated with the recombinant protein at final concentrations of 5, 10, 20, and 40 µg/mL, while the control group was treated with an equal volume of PBS (blank control). All groups were set with triplicate wells. The culture plate was incubated at 37℃ in a 5% CO₂ incubator for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells in each group were gently pipetted and collected into sterile EP tubes separately, centrifuged at 1800 rpm for 3 min, and the supernatant was discarded. Total RNA was extracted according to the instructions of the RNA extraction kit and reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA).

Primer details are provided in Table 1. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) assays were performed as previously described (Xiaolu et al. 2012), with an annealing temperature of 60 °C. The canine β-actin gene was used as an internal reference, and cytokine expression levels were quantified relative to this reference gene.

Effect of rEgPGK on apoptosis of canine PBMCs

Apoptosis was detected using three approaches as follows:

Flow Cytometry (Quantitative Detection) Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were adjusted to a concentration of 1 × 10⁶ cells/ml and seeded into wells. The cells were cultured with recombinant Echinococcus granulosus phosphoglycerate kinase (rEg PGK) at final concentrations of 5, 10, 20, and 40 µg/ml, or with 10 µl PBS (blank control). After incubation at 37℃ in a 5% CO₂ incubator for 24 h, the cells were collected, resuspended in PBS, and washed twice. Subsequently, 195 µl of Annexin V-FITC binding buffer was added(Beyotime, shanghai, China)), followed by gentle pipetting to mix thoroughly. Then, 5 µl of Annexin V-FITC and 10 µl of propidium iodide (PI) staining solution were added sequentially. Apoptosis was detected using a Cyto-FLEX flow cytometer (Beckman, Brea, CA, USA). The total apoptosis rate (%) was calculated as: Total apoptosis rate (%) = Early apoptosis rate (%) + Late apoptosis rate (%) (He et al. 2023).

Flow Cytometry (Quantitative Detection) Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were adjusted to a concentration of 1 × 10⁶ cells/ml and seeded into wells. The cells were cultured with recombinant Echinococcus granulosus phosphoglycerate kinase (rEg PGK) at final concentrations of 5, 10, 20, and 40 µg/ml, or with 10 µl PBS (blank control). After incubation at 37℃ in a 5% CO₂ incubator for 24 h, the cells were collected, resuspended in PBS, and washed twice. Subsequently, 195 µl of Annexin V-FITC binding buffer was added(Beyotime, shanghai, China)), followed by gentle pipetting to mix thoroughly. Then, 5 µl of Annexin V-FITC and 10 µl of propidium iodide (PI) staining solution were added sequentially. Apoptosis was detected using a Cyto-FLEX flow cytometer (Beckman, Brea, CA, USA). The total apoptosis rate (%) was calculated as: Total apoptosis rate (%) = Early apoptosis rate (%) + Late apoptosis rate (%) (He et al. 2023).

Confocal Laser Scanning Microscopy (Qualitative Observation) The cultured cells were centrifuged at 1000×g for 5 min, and the sediment was collected. After resuspending in 100 µl of Annexin V-FITC binding buffer and mixing well, the cells were stained with Annexin V-FITC and PI. Smears were prepared, and the cells were observed and photographed under a confocal laser scanning microscope.

Confocal Laser Scanning Microscopy (Qualitative Observation) The cultured cells were centrifuged at 1000×g for 5 min, and the sediment was collected. After resuspending in 100 µl of Annexin V-FITC binding buffer and mixing well, the cells were stained with Annexin V-FITC and PI. Smears were prepared, and the cells were observed and photographed under a confocal laser scanning microscope.

Quantitative Real-Time Polymerase Chain Reaction (qPCR) (Quantitative Analysis of Apoptosis-Related Factors) After co-culturing canine PBMCs with the recombinant protein for 24 h, the cells were collected to extract total RNA, which was then reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) as the template. qPCR was performed to quantitatively analyze the expression levels of three apoptosis-related factors, aiming to evaluate the effect of rEg PGK on the apoptosis of canine PBMCs.

Effect of Recombinant protein on pyroptosis of canine PBMCs

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed to detect the effect of the recombinant protein (rEgPGK) on three pyroptosis-related factors in canine peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). Briefly, canine PBMCs were co-cultured with the recombinant protein at different concentrations (5, 10, 20, and 40 µg/mL) for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were collected, washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and total RNA was extracted. The RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA), which served as the template for qPCR analysis to quantify the expression levels of the three pyroptosis-related factors. This assay was designed to evaluate the effect of rEgPGK on pyroptosis in canine PBMCs.

Statistical analysis

For qPCR data analysis, the results were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three independent replicate experiments. The relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCT method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001). For ELISA data, the results were also presented as the mean ± SD.Statistical analyses were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test in IBM SPSS Statistics 20 software to compare differences between groups. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical methods were explicitly indicated at the corresponding positions in the original manuscript(Huang J et al. 2025). Statistical significance of differences was marked with asterisks in the figures and tables, following the notation: ns (not significant, P > 0.05), * (P < 0.05), ** (P < 0.01), *** (P < 0.001), and **** (P < 0.0001).

Result

Bioinformatics analysis of EgPGK

The ORF of EgPGK gene comprises a sequence length of 1248 bp, and the protein’s chemical formula is C1958H3188N526O591S21, with a theoretical molecular weight of approximately 64 kDa. Predictions indicate that EgPGK lacks both a signal peptide and transmembrane regions, and it contains multiple B cell epitopes. In terms of secondary structure, α-helices constitute 45.06% of the protein, extended strands make up 15.90%, and random coils account for 39.04%, with a predominance of α-helices and random coils (Fig. 1).

B-cell epitopes (A), secondary structure prediction (B), and tertiary structure prediction (C) of Eg-PGK Note: In the secondary structure prediction (B): h : α-helix; e : extended strand; c : random coil; t : β-turn

The evolutionary history of the EgPGK protein was inferred using the neighbor-joining method implemented in MEGA 11 software. The analysis revealed that the gene clusters with E. multilocularis, indicating a close evolutionary relationship, while it is evolutionarily distant from canines. Amino acid sequence alignment across different species demonstrated low similarity, suggesting a minimal likelihood of cross-reactivity of the antigenic protein (Fig. 2).

Expression and immunological characteristics of EgPGK

The Eg-PGK sequence was successfully cloned, yielding a 1248 bp fragment. Following double enzyme digestion, the recombinant plasmid PET 32a-PGK was digested with Sac I and Hind III. Agarose gel electrophoresis confirmed that the size of the target fragment post-digestion was accurate (Fig. 3A). The EgPGK protein was successfully expressed, with the recombinant EgPGK exhibiting a molecular weight of approximately 64 kDa. Notably, rEgPGK was found to be present in inclusion bodies, indicating it is not a soluble protein (Fig. 3C). Western blot analysis demonstrated that rEgPGK was recognized by sera from dogs infected with echinococcosis, indicating a strong immune response (Fig. 3B). Immunofluorescence localization revealed that the EgPGK protein is present on the surface of protoscoleces, predominantly around the epidermis and the apical groove. In the 28-day-old adult stage, the protein is primarily localized in the epidermis of the organism, with some fluorescence aggregation observed at the initial region of the adult sucker. These findings are consistent with previous studies (Wu Maodi.2019) (Fig. 4).

The effects of rEgPGK on the proliferation of PBMC and nitric oxide (NOs) in dogs

Cell proliferation was evaluated by measuring optical density (OD) at 450 nm using a microplate reader in the CCK-8 assay, as depicted in Fig. 6(A). The recombinant protein rEgPGK significantly enhanced cell proliferation at a concentration of 10 µg/mL (P < 0.001). Conversely, at concentrations of 5, 20, and 40 µg/mL, an inhibitory trend on the proliferation of canine peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) was observed, although these differences did not reach statistical significance (P > 0.05).

To further explore the impact of rEgPGK on nitric oxide (NO) production in PBMCs, NO levels were quantified in cell culture supernatants. The findings indicated that the recombinant protein stimulated NO production at a low concentration of 5 µg/mL (p < 0.001). In contrast, higher concentrations (10 µg/mL, P < 0.001; 20 µg/mL, P < 0.01; and 40 µg/mL, P < 0.0001) significantly inhibited NO production, as illustrated in Fig. 6(B).

The effects of rEg PGK on four cytokines in canine PBMC were detected by q PCR

qPCR analysis revealed that recombinant EgPGK (rEgPGK) elicited a dose-dependent upregulation of IL-17 A and IFN-γ expression, with higher concentrations correlating with increased expression levels. In contrast, IL-10 expression initially rose at lower concentrations but subsequently declined as the dose increased. TGF-β1 expression demonstrated a biphasic pattern, characterized by downregulation at low concentrations (5–10 µg/mL) and upregulation at higher concentrations (20, 40 µg/mL), as illustrated in Fig. 7.

Effect of recombinant protein on apoptosis of canine PBMCs

The effect of rEgPGK on the apoptosis of canine peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) was detected by flow cytometry. Results showed that compared with the control group, rEgPGK significantly inhibited the apoptosis of canine PBMCs in a concentration-dependent manner (P < 0.001) (Fig. 8A). Subsequently, fluorescent staining was performed on cells cultured for 24 h, and images captured under a confocal laser scanning microscope confirmed consistent findings (Fig. 8B). Finally, quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis demonstrated that rEgPGK exerted a concentration-dependent inhibitory effect on the expression of Bcl-2 and GASP1 compared to the control group. Additionally, Bax expression exhibited a decreasing trend at higher concentrations (20 and 40 µg/mL). Collectively, these results suggest that rEgPGK possesses anti-apoptotic activity (Fig. 8C).

In summary, three independent experimental approaches were employed to verify the effect of rEgPGK on apoptosis, and all yielded consistent results: rEgPGK inhibits the apoptosis of canine PBMCs.

Effect of recombinant protein on pyroptosis of canine PBMCs

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis revealed that IL-18 expression was upregulated across all tested concentrations of recombinant Echinococcus granulosus phosphoglycerate kinase (rEgPGK), indicating that rEgPGK promotes IL-18 production. Similarly, rEgPGK enhanced IL-1β expression at concentrations of 5, 10, and 40 µg/mL, showing an overall increasing trend. Furthermore, gasdermin D (GSDMD) levels were significantly elevated at both low (5 µg/mL) and high (40 µg/mL) concentrations of rEgPGK compared to the control group. Collectively, these data demonstrate that rEgPGK induces pyroptosis in canine peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) (Fig. 9).

Discuss

The tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle is a ubiquitous metabolic pathway in aerobic organisms, serving as the central pathway of cellular metabolism and playing a crucial role in energy production. Phosphoglycerate kinase (PGK) is a key enzyme within the TCA cycle, essential for energy acquisition in numerous organisms. Inhibition of PGK function can result in metabolic dysfunction (PirelaM.L.R, et al. 2020). Existing research indicates that PGK is also a promising candidate for drug and vaccine development. It is considered a potential drug target for the treatment of schistosomiasis japonica (Cao et al. 2015), Plasmodium (Tehlan A, et al. 2022), filariasis (Kumar R, Ahmad F and Rathaur S 2019), and trypanosomiasis (Villafraz O, et al. 2018). However, there is a paucity of reports regarding its application in the treatment of echinococcosis.

Bioinformatics analysis has demonstrated that the target protein is devoid of transmembrane domains and signal peptides. Predictive algorithms have identified multiple B-cell antigenic epitopes, indicating a significant potential for vaccine development. The secondary structure analysis of phosphoglycerate kinase (PGK) revealed a composition comprising 45.06% α-helices, 15.90% extended strands, and 39.04% random coils. The predominance of α-helices, which contribute to structural stability by forming the protein backbone, in conjunction with the substantial proportion of random coils—typically situated on the protein surface with flexible, loosely packed conformations—supports its potential to harbor immunodominant epitopes (Pu Na et al. 2024). These bioinformatics findings preliminarily suggest that this antigen represents a promising vaccine candidate. Subsequent Western blot analysis confirmed that the recombinant protein was recognized by sera from Echinococcus-infected dogs, thereby demonstrating its strong immunoreactivity.

Quantitative PCR analysis demonstrated that EgPGK is expressed in both protoscoleces and 28-day-old adult worms, with expression levels significantly elevated in the adult stage. This suggests a pivotal role for EgPGK in the growth and development of E. granulosus. This expression pattern is consistent with immunofluorescence localization findings, which identified EgPGK fluorescence predominantly in the tegument of both protoscoleces and adult worms (Dalton, Skelly &,Halton. 2004). Given that the tegument serves as the primary interface for host-parasite interactions (Ayan et al. 2019), surface-localized molecules such as EgPGK are likely involved in essential physiological processes, including nutrient uptake, waste excretion, and immune evasion (Smyth & McManus, 1989). We propose that EgPGK may play a role in energy metabolism, thereby supporting the parasite’s survival and development within the host.

Nitric oxide (NO) plays a central role in the immunomodulation of parasitic infections (Alleva DG, Burger CJ and Elgert KD. 1994). Studies have demonstrated that certain parasites can stimulate host cells to produce NO, which in turn inhibits T cell proliferation as an immune evasion strategy. During inflammatory responses, NO levels are positively correlated with the severity of inflammation (Chen et al. 2024). In the present study, low concentrations of the recombinant protein (rEgPGK, 5 µg/mL) significantly promoted NO production (P < 0.001), suggesting its potential to enhance host immune responses at low doses. These findings support the application prospect of low-concentration rEgPGK as a candidate antigen for subsequent vaccine development. Furthermore, we observed that NO levels decreased conversely with increasing rEgPGK concentrations. We hypothesize that under high-concentration stimulation, the NO initially produced may have triggered a self-limiting mechanism or impaired the function of some NO-producing cells. The combined effects of upregulated anti-inflammatory factors (e.g., TGF-β1), differential responses of distinct immune cell subsets (Jin et al. 2025), NO-mediated feedback inhibition, and induced pyroptosis collectively overrode and reversed the pro-NO production effect of the single IFN-γ signal, thereby leading to the phenomenon of inhibition at high concentrations. This further reflects the complex, multilayered regulation of the host immune system in response to parasitic antigens, whose ultimate goal is to balance pathogen clearance and prevention of excessive inflammatory damage.

Cytokine-mediated apoptosis is a pivotal component of host-parasite interactions in veterinary contexts. (González-Sanz et al. 2023) highlighted that apoptosis is closely intertwined with the growth, development, and infectious processes of parasites. Parasites can modulate their burden within the host by regulating apoptosis, thereby enhancing their immune evasion capabilities (Baysal et al. 2024). In the present study, compared with the control group, the expression levels of apoptosis-related factors (Bcl-2 and GASP1) exhibited a concentration-dependent decrease with increasing concentrations of the recombinant protein (rEgPGK). Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) analysis further revealed that the pro-apoptotic factor Bax showed a downward trend in the high-concentration rEgPGK groups (20 µg/mL and 40 µg/mL) relative to the control. These findings suggest that rEgPGK exerts an anti-apoptotic effect at high concentrations. Consistent with the qPCR results, flow cytometric data confirmed the apoptotic phenotype, and typical apoptotic cells were visualized under confocal microscopy. Based on these observations, we hypothesize that EgPGK may facilitate the parasite’s evasion of the host’s immune response by inhibiting host cell apoptosis, thereby reducing the parasitic burden in canines.

In contrast to apoptosis, pyroptosis is characterized by cellular swelling, plasma membrane rupture, and release of cellular contents, which subsequently triggers robust inflammatory responses mediated by cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-18 (Shi et al. 2016)—a key immunological process in veterinary parasitic infections. qPCR results demonstrated that the expression level of the pyroptosis-related factor IL-18 increased with escalating rEgPGK concentrations. Additionally, compared with the control group, the expression of gasdermin D (GSDMD) was significantly upregulated in both low- (5 µg/mL) and high-concentration (40 µg/mL) rEgPGK-treated canine peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). These results indicate that EgPGK induces pyroptosis in canine PBMCs, with effective concentrations of 5 µg/mL, 10 µg/mL, and 40 µg/mL for IL-1β, and 5 µg/mL and 40 µg/mL for GSDMD. Pyroptosis is accompanied by the upregulation of inflammatory cytokines, leading to enhanced inflammatory responses. This intensified inflammatory state is detrimental to the parasite’s immune evasion mechanisms and may therefore facilitate the host’s clearance of the parasitic burden in veterinary settings.Furthermore, (Song et al. 2025) reported that the Echinococcus granulosus EgG1Y162 protein inhibits pyroptosis in canine macrophages by cleaving GSDMD, thereby reducing IL-1β release and attenuating inflammatory responses. In contrast, our findings demonstrate that rEgPGK upregulates the expression of GSDMD and IL-1β, indicating its pyroptosis-inducing activity. This phenomenon may reflect that rEgPGK disrupts the parasite’s pyroptosis-inhibitory evasion strategy, thereby potentiating anti-parasitic inflammatory responses—an important immunomodulatory mechanism in canine echinococcosis control.

Cytokines are crucial in initiating and regulating immune responses against parasitic infections. Contemporary research generally suggests that Th1-type cytokines are detrimental to parasite survival within the host, whereas Th2-type cytokines have the opposite effect (Zhang, et al. 2014). The Th1-type response, characterized by interferon-γ (IFN-γ), is critical for the clearance of Echinococcus granulosus in dogs.(Milhau N, et al. 2020) demonstrated that IFN-γ can activate canine macrophages to induce the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and nitric oxide (NO), which directly damage the parasite’s tegument.In our study, quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis revealed a significant increase in interferon-γ (IFN-γ) levels in the experimental group compared to the PBS control group (P < 0.0001), where IFN-γ serves as a representative Th1-type cytokine. The elevated IFN-γ levels indicate enhanced secretion of inflammatory molecules, thereby promoting inflammatory responses. These results demonstrate that the recombinant PGK protein (rEgPGK) stimulates IFN-γ production, which in turn induces a Th1-type immune response(Sharifi Y, et al. 2021). This may further enhance the secretion of CXCL10 by canine intestinal epithelial cells (CIECs), strengthening the recruitment of mucosal immune cells.Secretory immunoglobulin A (sIgA) in canine intestinal mucus plays a critical role in inhibiting parasitic adhesion to intestinal epithelial cells. Studies by (Petavy AF, et al. 2008) have demonstrated that recombinant Echinococcus granulosus antigens can induce sIgA production in dogs. Consistently, our Western blot data further confirm that rEgPGK exhibits strong immunogenicity, suggesting that it may also trigger sIgA-mediated mucosal immunity. This highlights the potential of rEgPGK as a candidate antigen for mucosal vaccines in veterinary applications.In conjunction with our findings on apoptosis and pyroptosis, these data suggest that PGK promotes inflammatory responses that may facilitate parasite clearance by the host. Furthermore, Th17-mediated cellular immunity has been confirmed to be closely associated with the progression of echinococcosis. As a key cytokine secreted by Th17 cells, IL-17 A primarily mediates chronic inflammatory responses (Ji Y Z, Mei X L and Liu J S R. 2021). Accumulating evidence indicates that the Th17/IL-17 A axis serves as a critical regulatory factor in the immune response during human and experimental echinococcosis. Clinical studies in humans have shown that, compared with healthy individuals, patients with cystic echinococcosis (CE) exhibit a significantly increased frequency of Th17 cells in peripheral blood and elevated serum IL-17 levels; notably, the expression level of IL-17 is positively correlated with cyst size, the degree of hepatic parenchymal damage, and disease severity (Tuxun et al., 2012). In experimental mouse models infected with Echinococcus granulosus, infection can induce robust Th17 cell polarization and enhanced secretion of IL-17 A/F (Lechner et al., 2014). This process facilitates the recruitment of neutrophils and monocytes, thereby promoting local inflammatory responses and granuloma formation around hydatid cysts (Gazi, et al. 2024). Additionally, existing studies have confirmed that knockdown of IL-17 or inhibition of Th17 cell differentiation can reduce cyst burden and alleviate liver fibrosis in infected mice, highlighting the pathogenic role of the Th17/IL-17 axis in the progression of echinococcosis (Pan, et al., 2017). Consistent with the above findings, the data from this study demonstrated that the protein concentration of IL-17 A was significantly increased at all four tested concentrations compared with the PBS control group (Fig. 7). These results confirm that Echinococcus granulosus infection can effectively induce IL-17 A production in the host, which is consistent with the established paradigm that the Th17/IL-17 axis is activated during the occurrence of echinococcosis. Furthermore, the consistent elevation of IL-17 A observed in this study suggests that the intensity of the Th17 response may be associated with the exposure level to Echinococcus granulosus, a finding of great significance for clinical practice. Collectively, these results further substantiate that the PGK gene may be involved in enhancing the host’s defense mechanisms against parasitic infections.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study successfully achieved the cloning and expression of EgPGK and conducted preliminary functional analyses, thereby providing essential data for the development of vaccines targeting the definitive host of E. granulosus. The findings indicate that PGK is integral to the growth and development of E. granulosus. EgPGK expression was observed in both 28-day-old worms and protoscoleces, suggesting its presence throughout all life cycle stages and highlighting its biological significance. Moreover, EgPGK was recognized by canine positive serum, confirming its strong immunoreactivity. In cellular experiments, the gene demonstrated favorable immunogenic properties. Functionally, PGK is involved in the inhibition of apoptosis while promoting pyroptosis and enhances Th1-type immune responses, potentially aiding in parasite clearance by the host. These results propose that EgPGK is a promising candidate for a vaccine target antigen, although further validation through animal experiments is necessary to confirm this potential.
