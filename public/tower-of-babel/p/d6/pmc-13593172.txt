A comprehensive endoscopic and clinical evaluation of Cameron ulcers: Insights from a single-center cross-sectional experience
Authors: Furkan Ali Uygur, Gökhan Aydin, Enes Ağirman, Selahattin Vural, Ahmet Cumhur Dülger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aims to provide a comprehensive analysis of the endoscopic and clinical characteristics of Cameron ulcers, focusing on their morphological presentation, associated comorbidities, and therapeutic outcomes in a single-center cohort. This retrospective study evaluated 43 patients diagnosed with Cameron ulcers at our center between January 2020 and October 2024. Data were collected from medical records, endoscopy reports, and imaging. Patients’ demographic, clinical, laboratory, and histopathological parameters were analyzed. Statistical methods were applied to identify significant differences across subgroups. Cameron ulcers were predominantly associated with chronic gastritis (74.4%), followed by chronic gastritis with intestinal metaplasia (9.3%). The mean hiatal hernia size was 4.6 ± 1.8 cm, with larger sizes observed in chronic Helicobacter pylori-related gastritis (5.3 ± 2.1 cm, P = .003). Markers of systemic inflammation (C-reactive protein [CRP]) and iron metabolism (ferritin) demonstrated significant variability among pathology groups, with the chronic inflammation group exhibiting the lowest ferritin levels and highest CRP concentrations (P < .001 and P = .025, respectively). Gender differences revealed higher ferritin and CRP levels in females, while males presented with elevated aspartate aminotransferase (AST) values. Cameron ulcers represent a multifactorial condition influenced by anatomical variations, chronic inflammation, microbial factors, and systemic iron metabolism. The findings underscore the importance of considering these ulcers in patients with large hiatal hernias presenting with anemia or gastrointestinal bleeding. Further studies are needed to establish standardized diagnostic and treatment protocols.

1. Introduction

Cameron lesions are mucosal erosions, frequently linear and superficial, that occur within large hiatal hernias, typically at the diaphragmatic constriction point of the herniated stomach.[1] Although first described several decades ago, the pathophysiology of these lesions remains incompletely understood.[2] Various etiological factors have been proposed, ranging from mechanical trauma caused by the repetitive sliding motion of the stomach across the hiatus to local ischemia, gastric acid reflux, Helicobacter pylori infection, and vascular stasis.[3-5] Despite these hypotheses, a definitive causative mechanism has not been established.

The clinical significance of Cameron lesions lies in their potential to cause chronic blood loss and even life-threatening gastrointestinal bleeding.[6] Patients may present with iron-deficiency anemia, melena, or overt bleeding, thereby impacting their quality of life and increasing healthcare costs.[7] Yet, due to limited data and the largely nonspecific endoscopic appearance of these lesions, their diagnosis and management remain challenging.

Existing literature is constrained by the scarcity of large-scale, systematically collected data. Most publications are limited to isolated case reports or small case series, providing an incomplete picture of the prevalence, endoscopic characterization, risk factors, and clinical outcomes associated with Cameron lesions.[8,9] Moreover, the absence of standardized diagnostic criteria and treatment protocols complicates clinical decision-making and underscores the need for more robust evidence to guide practice.

Against this backdrop, the aim of the present study is to provide a comprehensive overview of the endoscopic and clinical features of Cameron lesions, with a particular focus on their morphological presentation, associated comorbidities, and therapeutic interventions. By aggregating data from a single-center cohort and conducting a detailed analysis, we hope to contribute to the existing literature, improve diagnostic accuracy, and ultimately inform the development of more effective management strategies.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Patients and study design

A total of 43 patients who presented to our center between January 2020 and October 2024 and were diagnosed with Cameron ulcers were retrospectively analyzed for this study. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Giresun University Faculty of Medicine on November 27, 2024 (Decision number: 11.11.2024/06). Patient consent was waived, given the retrospective nature of this study. The diagnosis of Cameron ulcers was confirmed by reviewing the medical records, endoscopy reports, images, and/or videos of all cases included in the study.

2.2. Endoscopic evaluation and diagnostic criteria

The endoscopic examinations of the patients were routinely performed using upper gastrointestinal endoscopy. Cameron ulcers were defined as benign, linear gastric ulcers observed along the diaphragmatic constriction line in patients with large hiatal hernias. In our study, the diagnosis of Cameron ulcers was established in accordance with the definitions in the relevant literature, based on the presence of benign linear ulcers detected along the diaphragmatic constriction line within the hiatal hernia sac. The size of the hiatal hernia was determined by withdrawing the endoscope proximally from the diaphragmatic constriction region, measuring the distance from the teeth. A large hiatal hernia was generally defined as the presence of a hernia larger than 50 mm. H pylori infection was diagnosed by histopathological examination.

To exclude other potential sources of bleeding (peptic ulcers, esophagitis, or ulcerations secondary to gastroesophageal reflux disease), focal ulcers within the hernia sac above the diaphragm level and ulcers at the gastroesophageal junction were not classified as Cameron ulcers. This approach was adopted to distinguish Cameron ulcers from other upper gastrointestinal bleeding sources with different pathophysiological mechanisms.

2.3. Data collection and evaluation

The demographic and clinical data of the patients were retrospectively obtained from patient files, the hospital information management system, and endoscopy records. The collected data included gender, age, hemoglobin, hematocrit, platelet count, white blood cell count (WBC), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), creatinine, albumin, potassium, sodium, chloride, lactate dehyrogenase (LDH) levels, the presence of H pylori, pathological evaluation results, hiatal hernia size (cm), ferritin (mg/nL), and C-reactive protein (CRP, mg/L) values. Endoscopic reports, photographs, and/or videos were rereviewed to confirm the diagnosis of Cameron ulcers. The symptoms and findings at the time of presentation (melena, hematemesis, hematochezia, iron-deficiency anemia, occult blood in stool), medication history (nonsteroidal anti-inflammatorydrugs, aspirin, anticoagulants), prior bleeding and transfusion history, accompanying comorbidities, endoscopic findings (number and size of ulcers, accompanying lesions, and endoscopic interventions performed) were evaluated. All endoscopic examinations were performed by a single experienced surgeon specialized in upper gastrointestinal system endoscopy.

2.4. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk). All data were initially evaluated with descriptive statistics. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Additionally, the normality assumption was evaluated through histograms, Q–Q plots, and skewness–kurtosis values. Continuous variables following a normal distribution were reported as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed variables were reported as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages. Depending on the distribution of the data, parametric or nonparametric tests were applied to evaluate differences between groups. For comparisons of continuous variables between 2 independent groups, the independent samples t test was used for normally distributed data, and the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for non-normally distributed data. To assess the effect of 4 differentpathological groups on continuous variables, a one-way ANOVA test was applied when normality and homogeneity of variance assumptions were met, and the Kruskal–Wallis test was used otherwise. Relationships between categorical variables were evaluated using the chi-square test, or Fisher exact test when the expected cell frequency was low. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

A total of 43 patients diagnosed with Cameron ulcers were retrospectively evaluated. The following sections detail the baseline demographic and clinical features of the study population, as well as their endoscopic findings, laboratory results, and histopathological assessments. Gastroscopy was performed in 4 patients due to signs of melena (gastrointestinal bleeding), in 18 patients due to reflux symptoms (gastric contents coming up into the mouth, burning sensation in the chest, chronic cough), in 10 patients due to dyspeptic complaints (bloating, abdominal pain, nausea). Eleven patients underwent gastroscopy for screening purposes.

The study population (n = 43) showed a nearly balanced gender distribution (51.2% female, 48.8% male). H pylori was detected in 30.2% of participants. The predominant histopathological finding was chronic gastritis (74.4%), with a smaller proportion presenting chronic gastritis with intestinal metaplasia (9.3%) and chronic H pylori-related gastritis (7.0%; Table 1). In chronic gastritis, ulcer sizes were observed to be smaller and no signs of active bleeding were observed. According to the Forrest Classification, 4 patients were diagnosed as Forrest Ib, 1 patient was diagnosed as Forrest IIb, and 38 patients were diagnosed as Forrest III.

Among the parameters assessed, hiatal hernia size, ferritin, and CRP levels showed significant differences across the gastric pathology groups. Patients with chronic H pylori gastritis had the largest mean hiatal hernia size (5.3 ± 2.1 cm, P = .003), while those in the chronic inflammation group presented with the smallest mean hiatal hernia size (3.0 ± 1.7 cm). Ferritin levels were markedly lower in the chronic inflammation group (21.0 ± 11.7 mg/L) compared to the other groups (P < .001), suggesting reduced iron stores. Additionally, CRP levels differed significantly (P = .025), with the chronic inflammation group showing the highest mean CRP (27.3 ± 15.3 mg/L), indicating a potential elevation in systemic inflammatory activity within this subgroup (Table 3).

Significant differences emerged between female and male patients in terms of several key parameters. Female patients presented with a larger mean hiatal hernia size (5.1 ± 1.9 cm vs 4.0 ± 1.6 cm; P = .003), higher ferritin levels (76.2 ± 123.7 mg/L vs 48.8 ± 53.5 mg/L; P < .001), and elevated CRP concentrations (22.9 ± 29.5 mg/L vs 11.8 ± 17.1 mg/L; P < .001), indicating greater iron stores and potentially heightened systemic inflammation. Male patients exhibited higher mean AST values (35.9 ± 49.8 U/L vs 19.3 ± 9.9 U/L; P = .007), suggesting possible variations in liver-associated parameters. Other evaluated factors, including age, hemoglobin, and WBC counts, did not differ significantly between the 2 groups (Table 6).

4. Discussion

The findings of our study reveal significant differences in both anatomical and histopathological aspects in patients with Cameron ulcers. Particularly, the marked variations in the size of hiatal hernias among certain pathological subgroups suggest that structural changes in the esophagogastric junction region may play a key role in the development of these ulcers. In addition to these structural characteristics, the significant alterations in markers related to inflammation and iron metabolism, such as ferritin and CRP, across different pathology groups point to the involvement of chronic inflammatory processes, microbial factors, and metabolic imbalances in the etiopathogenesis of Cameron ulcers. Furthermore, the prominence of certain enzyme levels and anatomical variability based on gender highlights the multidimensional interaction of individual susceptibilities. These findings underscore the need to evaluate Cameron ulcer development not solely through mechanical trauma or anatomical abnormalities but also by considering inflammation, microbial interactions, iron metabolism changes, and gender-based biological differences collectively.

It has been reported that Cameron ulcers can be treated using a minimally invasive, single-incision, transgastric closure method.[10] Depending on the clinical condition of the patient, hernia repair is often performed during the same surgery to prevent recurrence of Cameron ulcers. In patients undergoing endoscopic treatment, particular care should be taken during thermal therapy to avoid perforation, especially in cases of deep ulcers, due to the lack of fibrous tissue around the gastric wall at the gastroesophageal junction.[11] Cases of deep ulcers adhering to the pericardium have been reported, where thermal therapy is considered extremely high-risk and possibly contraindicated.[12] Lin et al reported the treatment of visible vessels in Cameron ulcers using band ligation.[13] Another study documented the use of endoscopically placed hemostatic clips to control bleeding.[14] A case report presented by Le et al in 2024 highlights Cameron ulcers as a significant etiology that can sometimes be overlooked, but which can lead to severe iron-deficiency anemia and recurrent upper gastrointestinal bleeding.[7] Additionally, a case report published by Suha Jabak and Jana G. Hashash in 2020 emphasizes a life-threatening case of upper gastrointestinal bleeding caused by a large and deep Cameron ulcer.[15] Although this case was based on a single patient, it demonstrates that Cameron ulcers, developing in the context of large hiatal hernias, can occasionally lead to severe bleeding and that standard endoscopic interventions may not always suffice. This case highlights that mechanical trauma, inflammatory processes, and potential vascular complications (such as pseudoaneurysms) can significantly impact the clinical course of Cameron ulcers. While this underscores certain pathophysiological mechanisms, the findings of our study primarily focus on changes in chronic inflammation markers (e.g., CRP and ferritin), differences in hiatal hernia size, and the potential roles of microanatomical and microbial factors (e.g., H pylori). Our results suggest that the pathogenesis of Cameron ulcers is not solely limited to mechanical trauma but also involves chronic inflammation, microbial factors, and disturbances in iron metabolism.

A case report published in 2020 by Mehershahi et al highlights that Cameron ulcers can sometimes pose diagnostic challenges by being overshadowed by commonly known risk factors (e.g., alcohol use disorder, low-dose aspirin use).[16] In this case, the patient was initially evaluated for bleeding suspected to originate from varices or peptic ulcers due to heavy alcohol consumption, but a rare cause, Cameron ulcers, was eventually identified. This case emphasizes that Cameron ulcers can present not only with chronic, subtle iron-deficiency anemia but also occasionally with acute and prominent upper gastrointestinal bleeding. The findings of our study suggest that the pathogenesis of Cameron ulcers may involve multiple factors, including variations in hiatal hernia size, changes in chronic inflammation markers (CRP and ferritin), and microbial agents (e.g., H pylori). In this regard, the case presented by Mehershahi et al confirms that Cameron ulcers do not follow a uniform clinical presentation but rather arise from the complex interplay of different risk factors. It also underlines the importance of considering less obvious diagnostic possibilities in certain clinical scenarios.

The study by Gray et al in 2015 demonstrated that large hiatal hernias increase the risk of Cameron lesions and associated significant gastrointestinal bleeding. The study also reported substantially lower hemoglobin levels and a frequent need for surgical intervention in these patients.[3] However, in our dataset, although hiatal hernia sizes showed significant variability across different gastric pathology groups, no similarly dramatic differences were observed in anemia-related parameters, such as hemoglobin levels. For example, while Gray et al’s findings revealed notably reduced hemoglobin levels and predominant bleeding symptoms in patients with Cameron lesions, our study did not find statistically significant differences in hemoglobin levels between different pathological groups. This suggests that the presence of large hiatal hernias may not yield the same clinical outcomes in all patient populations and that specific endoscopic lesions, such as Cameron lesions, might play a more critical role in determining hemoglobin levels and bleeding risk. Thus, while Gray et al.’s findings highlighted severe clinical outcomes in patients with Cameron lesions associated with large hiatal hernias, our series indicates that although hiatal hernia size is a variable, it does not prominently feature a similar severity of bleeding or anemia profiles.

When interpreting the present findings, the regional epidemiology of H pylori infection should be taken into account. Turkey is considered a high-prevalence country for H pylori, although the distribution of infection shows significant regional and demographic variability. In the nationwide TURHEP study, the prevalence of H pylori in the adult population was reported to be approximately 82.5%, with higher rates in individuals with lower socioeconomic status and in certain geographic regions.[17] Similar epidemiological studies conducted in Turkey have confirmed that H pylori infection remains common, but the prevalence has gradually declined in recent decades, particularly in younger populations, likely due to improved sanitation, widespread antibiotic use, and increasing eradication therapy.[18]

Despite the high overall prevalence, recent reports indicate that the proportion of H pylori-negative chronic gastritis has increased. This phenomenon may be explained by previous eradication treatment, long-term proton pump inhibitor use, and changes in environmental and socioeconomic conditions.[19]

Another important factor is the variability in diagnostic methods, biopsy sampling sites, and histopathological evaluation, which may influence the detection rate of H pylori. Regional differences in clinical practice and endoscopic biopsy protocols may therefore contribute to heterogeneous reporting of H pylori-negative gastritis across different centers. Considering these regional epidemiological characteristics, the relatively low or variable detection of H pylori in our cohort should be interpreted with caution. The presence of chronic gastritis without detectable H pylori in our study population may reflect prior eradication, regional prevalence differences, or methodological factors rather than a truly low infection rate.

This study has several limitations. First, it was designed as a single-center, retrospective study, which may reduce the generalizability of the findings and lead to potential issues such as missing records or reporting errors. Additionally, the lack of long-term follow-up data limits the comprehensive evaluation of the natural course of Cameron ulcers, recurrence rates, and treatment responses. However, many publications in the literature are primarily based on case reports or small series with a limited number of cases. In this context, our study significantly expands the available data due to the relatively high number of patients diagnosed with Cameron ulcers, allowing for stronger conclusions on the subject. Furthermore, the fact that all endoscopic evaluations were performed by a single experienced endoscopist ensured more consistent and standardized application of diagnostic criteria, which can be considered one of the strengths of our study.
