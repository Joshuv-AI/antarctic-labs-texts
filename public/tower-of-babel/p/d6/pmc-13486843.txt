MicroRNA-125a-5p promotes NLRP3/caspase-1/GSDMD pathway-mediated pyroptosis in endothelial cells during Kawasaki disease
Authors: Ying Li, Zhixiang Wu, Min Kong, Zhijuan Kang, Lu Yi, Yanan Hu, Qin He, Yuanbojiao Zuo, Zhuoying Li, Zuocheng Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Kawasaki disease (KD) is an acute systemic vasculitis that the primary cause of secondary heart disease in children. Dysregulation of microRNA (miRNA) expression drives gene expression patterns across multiple biological processes, thereby mediating the pathogenesis and progression of KD. This study investigated how miR-125a-5p regulates pyroptosis in KD-associated endothelial cells. This study used Lactobacillus casei cell-wall extract (LCWE) to stimulate male C57BL/6 mice and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) to establish KD-like models. Experimental data demonstrated marked upregulation of miR-125a-5p expression in both KD-associated models. In vitro analysis revealed that targeted inhibition of miR-125a-5p increased HUVEC survival while reducing NLRP3/caspase-1/GSDMD-driven pyroptosis. In vivo, targeted inhibition of miR-125a-5p inactivated the NLRP3/caspase-1/GSDMD signaling pathway and diminished pyroptosis and inflammation in coronary artery tissues. This study found that miR-125a-5p exacerbates LCWE-induced pyroptosis in HUVECs and vascular injury in mice, suggesting a potential therapeutic strategy targeting miR-125a-5p for KD-related endothelial injury and inflammation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56467-9.

Introduction

Kawasaki disease (KD) is an acute, self-limited systemic vasculitis that predominantly affecting children under five years of age and represents the leading cause of acquired heart disease1. This immune-mediated condition triggers panvasculitis in medium-sized arteries, with a particular predilection for the coronary arteries (CA), resulting in endothelial dysfunction and impaired myocardial perfusion2. Approximately 25% of untreated patients develop coronary artery aneurysms (CAAs), which may progress to thrombotic complications or myocardial ischemia if not promptly intervened3. Due to the absence of definitive diagnostic biomarkers and incomplete understanding of its pathogenesis, there is an urgent need to elucidate the precise pathophysiological mechanisms underlying coronary vulnerability in KD. This knowledge is critical to inform targeted therapeutic strategies aimed at mitigating long-term cardiovascular sequelae4.

Endothelial death and inflammatory responses are key drivers of vascular damage in KD5. Pyroptosis is a pro-inflammatory programmed cell death process. Sustained endothelial pyroptosis not only triggers the release of pro-inflammatory mediators but also directly contributes to coronary arteritis, making it a critical therapeutic target for mitigating cardiovascular complications6,7. Mechanistically, the NLRP3 inflammasome, which contains the NACHT, LRR and PYD domains, mediates caspase-1-dependent gasdermin D (GSDMD) cleavage, thereby driving the formation of plasma membrane pore and ultimately promoting interleukin (IL)-1β/IL-18 release and endothelial barrier disruption8–10. Targeted inhibition of NLRP3 can effectively block pathological endothelial pyroptosis11. However, the regulatory mechanisms of this signaling axis in KD have not yet been fully elucidated.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are a class of small non-coding RNAs that regulate gene expression through transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulation12. Their regulation of the inflammatory gene networks has been demonstrated to be a key factor in controlling dysregulation of the host inflammatory responses13. In KD, differentially expressed miRNAs drive pathogenic transformations by altering the transcriptional profile controlling cell proliferation, migration, apoptosis, and immune activation14. A previous study by our team found that plasma miR-125a-5p levels were significantly elevated in acute KD patients compared to healthy controls15, suggesting its potential as a biomarker indicating disease occurrence. However, the mechanism of miR-125a-5p in KD pathogenesis remains unclear.

Due to the unclear etiology of KD, no single model system can fully replicate the complexity of the human disease. The Lactobacillus casei cell-wall extract (LCWE)-induced vasculitis model demonstrates high histopathological fidelity and closely recapitulates the characteristic clinical manifestations of KD, such as aortic root inflammation, coronary arteritis, and abdominal aortic aneurysm formation16. This model has been widely used to identify therapeutic targets that suppress coronary inflammation in animal systems, providing possible insights into underlying mechanisms that may translate to therapeutic strategies for human KD17–19. Clinical evidence from biomarker analysis and vascular ultrasound confirms that endothelial dysfunction is a critical pathological feature in KD patients, where divergent endothelial responses determine disease outcomes20,21. In vitro, human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) can recapitulate endothelial cell dysfunction and inflammatory cascades during KD in response to KD-related stimuli such as LCWE, inflammatory cytokines, and others22–24. Furthermore, HUVECs can partially model the impact of the KD inflammatory microenvironment on endothelium through co-culture with macrophages or supplementation with patient serum/inflammatory factors25–27. Previous studies utilizing this model have identified molecules capable of alleviating endothelial inflammation, apoptosis, and mitochondrial dysfunction, which show promise as potential therapeutic candidates for KD25,28,29. Therefore, we employed the LCWE-induced mouse model and the LCWE-induced HUVEC model to investigate the potential regulatory role of miR-125a-5p in endothelial pyroptosis and inflammation.

Therefore, this study identifies miR-125a-5p as a pathogenic factor in KD-associated endothelial pyroptosis. We demonstrate that LCWE induces the upregulation of miR-125a-5p, and that targeted inhibition of miR-125a-5p effectively alleviates LCWE-induced pyroptosis in HUVECs and coronary artery injury. Further investigation revealed that the combination of LPS and ATP triggers pyroptosis, counteracting counteract the protective effects of miR-125a-5p inhibitor, suggesting the contribution of the NLRP3 signaling pathway. These findings indicate that miR-125a-5p serves as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target, with its inhibitors holding therapeutic potential for alleviating vascular inflammation in KD.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and treatment

HUVECs were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (PCS-100-010; ATCC) and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 for 7 days. The medium was replaced every 72 h, and cells were passaged when they reached approximately 70% confluence. For experimental purposes, HUVECs from the second or third passage were used. HUVECs were exposed to LCWE from Lactobacillus casei (11578, ATCC) at concentrations of 1, 5, and 10 µg/mL for 12 h to induce KD-related endothelial damage and inflammation30–32. HUVECs were stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (1 µg/mL, 6 h) and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) (5 mM, 30 min) to induce pyroptosis33.

LCWE preparation

According to the reported methods30–32, Lactobacillus casei was cultured in MRS medium for 48 h, and the bacterial cells were collected by centrifugation at 4 °C and washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The bacteria were incubated in twice the volume of 4% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) overnight to disrupt the cell wall. The cell wall fragments were washed with PBS and then sonicated in a dry ice and ethanol bath for 2 h, with the temperature maintained at 4 °C. Subsequently, the cell wall fragments were centrifuged at 4 °C (20,000 ×g, 1 h) to obtain the supernatant. The LCWE content in the supernatant was expressed as milligrams of total rhamnose per milliliter of PBS, which was measured by colorimetric phenol-sulfuric assay.

Cell transfection

The miR-125a-5p mimic, miR-125a-5p inhibitor, and their respective negative control (NC mimic and NC inhibitor) were transfected into HUVECs using Lipofectamine 2000 transfection reagent (2028090, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The transfection efficiency was assessed using fluorescently labeled miR-125a-5p NC, mimic, and inhibitor with Alexa Fluor 488 via flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter). The miR-125a-5p mimic (HG-MI029693), miR-125a-5p inhibitor (HG-In029693), and corresponding controls were commercially ordered from HonorGene (Changsha, China). Their sequences are as follows: miR-125a-5p mimic (5’- UCCCUGAGACCCUUUAACCUGUGAUU − 3’), NC mimic (5’- UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT − 3’), miR-125a-5p inhibitor (5’-AAUCACAGGUUAAAGGGUCUCAGGGA − 3’), and NC inhibitor (5’- CAGUACUUUUGUGUAGUACAA − 3’).

Animal model

All experimental protocols and animal procedures were conducted in compliance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and adhered to the ARRIVE guidelines. The study was approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Third Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (No. 2021xydw0105).

Twenty-four male C57BL/6 mice (5 weeks old) were purchased from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Changsha, China) and randomly divided into four experimental groups: (1) Sham group, (2) LCWE group, (3) LCWE + negative control (NC) inhibitor group, and (4) LCWE + miR-125a-5p inhibitor group. Each group was randomly assigned six mice, with three used for histological analysis and the other three for marker detection.

Eighteen mice received a single intraperitoneal injection of 500 µg LCWE to induce KD-related vasculitis34,35. Six mice in the Sham group received a single intraperitoneal injection of the same dose of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). On the first day of LCWE injection, NC inhibitor or miR-125a-5p inhibitor at 5 mg/kg was administered via the tail vein. This procedure was repeated every 72 h. No mice died during the experimental period. After 14 days of LCWE injection, mice were euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (100 mg/kg). When animals exhibited cessation of spontaneous breathing for 2–3 min and disappearance of the blink reflex, blood samples were collected from the orbit and heart tissues were harvested for subsequent analysis. The blood was centrifuged at 1000 ⋅g for 15 min at 4℃ to obtain serum samples. Throughout the study, efforts were made to minimize animal suffering and discomfort.

Flow cytometry

Cell pyroptosis was assessed using using flow cytometry with propidium iodide (PI) and caspase-1 co-staining. HUVECs were treated with LPS and ATP to induce pyroptosis, with Z-YVAD-FMK treatment as an inhibitor control group, and an untreated group was established. HUVECs were washed with PBS, then green fluorescently labeled FAM-YVAD-FMK was added for caspase-1 labeling. Subsequently, PI dye was added for 1 h, followed by analysis using a flow cytometer (A00-1-1102, Beckman Coulter, Fullerton, CA, USA) and CytExpert v2.5. Unstained cells and single-stained controls (caspase-1 single stain, PI single stain) were used to automatically calculate the compensation matrix, ensuring no fluorescence spillover. Cell debris was excluded using forward scatter (FSC) and side scatter (SSC) plots, and single cells were selected using forward scatter height (FSC-H) and forward scatter area (FSC-A) gating. caspase-1+PI+ double-positive cell populations were determined as pyroptotic cells, with caspase-1 detection channel at 488 nm and PI detection channel at 594 nm.

CCK-8 assay

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay was used to assess cell viability. HUVECs were treated with 1, 5, and 10 µg/mL LCWE for 24–48 h. A 300 µL cell suspension containing 1 × 10⁴ HUVECs was seeded into 96-well plates, and 30 µL of CCK-8 solution (NU679, DOJINDO, Tokyo, Japan) was added. After incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 4 h, absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (MB-530, HEALES, Shenzhen, China).

EdU (5-ethynyl-2-deoxyuridine) staining

Cell proliferation was assessed using the EdU detection kit (C10310, RiboBio, Guangzhou, China). HUVECs were seeded in 96-well plates, and 50 µM EdU was added and incubated overnight. After washing with PBS, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with Triton X-100, and then quenched with glycine. Apollo staining solution was applied and incubated for 30 min, followed by staining DNA with Hoechst 33,342 dye. After washing with PBS, cell staining was analyzed by a fluorescence microscope (DSZ2000X, Beijing Cnmicro Instrument Co., Ltd., Beijing, China).

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Coronary artery tissue samples were fixed in formalin, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and embedded in paraffin. Sections (5 μm thick) were deparaffinized in xylene for 15 min and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (100% to 75%). Tissue staining was performed with hematoxylin and eosin (AWI0020, Abiowell, Changsha, China). Sections were then dehydrated through an ascending ethanol gradient (75% to 100%) and cleared in xylene. Tissue sections were mounted with neutral gum for bright-field microscopic analysis of histopathological lesions. Vascular inflammation scoring referenced the previous protocol22, where 0 points indicate no inflammation, 1 point indicates rare inflammatory cells, 2 points indicate scattered inflammatory cells visible, 3 points indicate diffuse inflammatory cell infiltration visible, and 4 points indicate dense inflammatory cell clusters visible.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Coronary artery tissue samples were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution (AWI0097, Abiowell), then post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide (18456, TED PELLA, Redding, CA, USA). After thorough washing with PBS, samples were dehydrated using an ethanol series and infiltrated with propylene oxide. Ultrathin sections were collected on copper grids and double-stained with 4% uranyl acetate and 0.5% lead citrate. Cellular ultrastructure and pyroptotic vesicles were analyzed using a JEM-1400 TEM (JEOL, Beijing, China).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

IL-1β (CSB-E08053h/CSB-E08054m) and IL-18 (CSB-E07450h/CSB-E04609m) ELISA kits (CUSABio, Wuhan, China) were used to analyze HUVEC supernatant and serum samples. Samples were centrifuged at 1000 ⋅g for 15 min at 4℃ to remove cell debris, then incubated with biotinylated detection antibodies at 37℃. After washing, horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated streptavidin was added and incubated at 37℃ for 1 h. Chromogenic reaction was initiated using 3,3’,5,5’-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate under dark conditions, followed by termination with stop solution. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) assay

Serum LDH activity was determined using a colorimetric assay kit (A020-2, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China). For the analysis group, four parallel wells were prepared, including blank control, standard, test sample, and negative control. After incubation at room temperature, absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (15596062, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). To detect the expression of the genes encoding NLRP3, caspase-1, PYCARD/ASC, and GSDMD, 1 µg of RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the HiScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (CW2569, CWBIO, Taizhou, China). For miR-125a-5p, reverse transcription was performed using the Taqman MicroRNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA). qRT-PCR amplification was performed using an UltraSYBR Mixture (CW2601, CWBIO) on a QuantStudio1 real-time quantitative PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). β-actin (for mRNA) and U6 (for miRNA) were used as internal controls, and relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−∆∆Ct method. Primer sequences are shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Western blot analysis

Total proteins were extracted from cell lysates and tissue homogenates in RIPA buffer (R0010, Solarbio, Beijing, China), then quantified using bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (AWB0104, Abiowell). Proteins were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), then transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (1620112, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Membranes were blocked in TBST (Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20) containing 5% non-fat milk at room temperature for 1 h. Primary antibodies diluted in TBST were incubated overnight at 4℃, including NLRP3 (19771-1-AP, 1:1000, Proteintech, Chicago, IL, USA), caspase-1 (22915-1-AP, 1:1000, Proteintech), ASC (10500-1-AP, 1:1000, Proteintech), GSDMD (20770-1-AP, 1:5000, Proteintech), GSDMD-N (ab215203, 1:1000, Abcam), and β-actin (60008-1-Ig, 1:5000, Proteintech). Membranes were washed using PBST and then incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature. Chemiluminescent signals were developed using the WesternBright ECL Substrate (K-12045-D50, Advansta, Menlo Park, CA, USA) and quantified using a ChemiScope 6100 imaging system (CLINX, Shanghai, China). Band intensities were normalized to β-actin using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

Following deparaffinization and rehydration of coronary artery tissues, microwave-mediated antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer. After cooling to room temperature, sections were incubated with 1% periodic acid solution to inhibit endogenous peroxidase activity. Primary antibodies against IL-1β (1:200, 16806-1-AP, Proteintech) and IL-18 (1:200, 10663-1-AP, Proteintech) were incubated overnight at 4°C. After three washes with PBS, sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated rabbit secondary antibody (AWI0629, Abiowell) for 1 h. Chromogenic reaction was developed using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate, followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin. Sections were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (75%-100%), cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral resin. Quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 software (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA). Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates and three technical replicate measurements. For comparisons between two groups, unpaired Student’s t-test was used. Multiple group comparisons were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

LCWE activates the NLRP3 pathway and induces pyroptosis in HUVECs

To establish an in vitro model of KD, HUVECs were exposed to three concentrations of LCWE (1, 5, and 10 µg/mL). Morphological analysis revealed a concentration-dependent reduction in viable cell density and progressive deterioration of cell morphology with increasing LCWE concentrations (Fig. 1A). Quantitative assessment using CCK-8 demonstrated that LCWE significantly suppressed HUVEC viability compared to untreated controls, with maximal inhibition observed at 10 µg/mL (Fig. 1B). EdU proliferation assays further confirmed the anti-proliferative effect of LCWE on HUVECs in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1C). Flow cytometry analysis showed that LCWE increased the dual-positive proportion of caspase-1 and PI in a concentration-dependent fashion (Fig. 1D-E), indicating LCWE-induced pyroptosis in HUVECs. This finding was supported by upregulated expression of pyroptosis-related markers including cleaved caspase-1 (p20), NLRP3, ASC, and GSDMD-N (Fig. 1G-I), along with increased release of IL-18 and IL-1β (Fig. 1J). Additionally, we observed dose-dependent elevation of miR-125a-5p levels following LCWE exposure (Fig. 1F). These data demonstrate that LCWE promotes NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis and upregulates miR-125a-5p in HUVECs.

MiR-125a-5p promotes LCWE-induced pyroptosis in HUVECs

To elucidate the regulatory role of miR-125a-5p in HUVEC pyroptosis, HUVECs were transfected with miR-125a-5p mimic or inhibitor followed by LCWE intervention. qRT-PCR confirmed that miR-125a-5p mimic increased miR-125a-5p levels in HUVECs, whereas miR-125a-5p inhibitor suppressed its expression (Supplementary Fig. 1 A-B). The efficacy of miR-125a-5p mimic and inhibitor was consistent in both LCWE-induced HUVECs and HCAECs (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Fig. 2). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that miR-125a-5p mimic amplified the dual-positive proportion of caspase-1 and PI in LCWE-treated HUVECs, while miR-125a-5p inhibitor reduced this proportion (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, miR-125a-5p mimic enhanced the expression of cleaved caspase-1 (p20), NLRP3, ASC, and GSDMD-N, along with increased IL-18 and IL-1β release in LCWE-stimulated HUVECs. Conversely, miR-125a-5p inhibitor exerted opposite effects (Fig. 2C-E). These findings indicate that miR-125a-5p acts as an inducer of HUVEC pyroptosis in the context of LCWE exposure.

The combination of LPS and ATP abrogates the anti-pyroptotic effect of miR-125a-5p inhibitor

Next, LPS and ATP were administered to miR-125a-5p-downregulated HUVECs to induce pyroptosis. qRT-PCR analysis revealed that miR-125a-5p inhibitor downregulated LCWE-induced miR-125a-5p levels, whereas LPS-ATP treatment did not affect miR-125a-5p expression (Fig. 3A). Further investigations demonstrated that LPS-ATP significantly promoted HUVEC pyroptosis, which reversed the effect of miR-125a-5p inhibitor. This reversal was evidenced by increased dual-positive proportion of caspase-1 and PI staining (Fig. 3B), upregulation of cleaved caspase-1 (p20), NLRP3, ASC, and GSDMD-N (Fig. 3C-D), and enhanced release of IL-18 and IL-1β (Fig. 3E). These data confirm that miR-125a-5p promotes NLRP3/caspase-1/GSDMD pathway-mediated pyroptosis in HUVECs in response to LCWE-induced KD-like injury.

MiR-125a-5p inhibitor alleviates coronary artery pyroptosis and vascular inflammation in vivo

To validate the pathophysiological role of miR-125a-5p in vivo, coronary artery lesions were induced in male C57BL/6 mice using LCWE. Subsequently, miR-125a-5p was knocked down via tail vein injection of miR-125a-5p inhibitor. We observed that LCWE-treated mice exhibited significant inflammatory infiltration and intimal hyperplasia in the coronary artery tissue, with increased vascular inflammation scores. Conversely, miR-125a-5p inhibition alleviated these pathological features, restoring the vascular structure in the coronary artery tissue to near normal levels and reducing the vascular inflammation score (Fig. 4A-B). Elastin, a crucial extracellular matrix protein, whose deficiency leads to vascular wall stiffening36. LCWE-induced coronary artery inflammation in mice was associated with elastin degradation37. Consistent with this observation, LCWE triggered elastin degradation in mouse coronary artery tissues, whereas miR-125a-5p inhibitor significantly alleviated this phenomenon (Fig. 4C). LDH release serves as a biochemical marker of pyroptosis, and miR-125a-5p inhibitor markedly reduced LCWE-induced serum LDH levels (Fig. 4D). TEM revealed that miR-125a-5p inhibitor mitigated LCWE-induced pyroptosis in coronary artery tissues, exhibiting intact cellular membranes and minimal cytoplasmic swelling (Fig. 4E). We further observed that LCWE upregulated miR-125a-5p, cleaved caspase-1 (p20), NLRP3, ASC, and GSDMD-N in coronary artery tissues, while miR-125a-5p inhibitor reversed these expressions (Fig. 5A-C). IHC staining of coronary artery tissues demonstrated that LCWE significantly promoted IL-1β and IL-18 expression, whereas miR-125a-5p inhibitor substantially suppressed their expression (Fig. 5D). The downregulation of IL-1β and IL-18 was further confirmed in serum by ELISA (Fig. 5E). Collectively, these data demonstrate that miR-125a-5p inhibitor effectively suppresses NLRP3/caspase-1/GSDMD pathway-mediated pyroptosis and inflammation in coronary artery tissues.

Discussion

KD is an acute systemic vasculitis primarily affecting coronary artery tissues38. Growing research focuses on the therapeutic potential of miRNAs in mitigating vascular inflammatory complications39. In our study, we observed miR-125a-5p expression was upregulated in the LCWE-induced KD model. This upregulation exacerbates NLRP3/caspase-1/GSDMD pathway-mediated pyroptosis in endothelial cells and coronary artery inflammation. Targeted inhibition of miR-125a-5p effectively suppressed LCWE-induced pyroptosis and coronary inflammation. These findings reveal the molecular basis for pyroptosis-targeted therapy against KD-associated vasculitis and suggest miR-125a-5p as a potential predictive biomarker and therapeutic target for KD.

MiRNAs regulate the expression of numerous genes and play pivotal roles in diverse biological processes40. As an important regulatory miRNA, miR-125a-5p exerts a dual regulatory role in vascular inflammation through transcriptional regulation of specific genes. Studies report that miR-125a-5p directly inhibits the expression of Ninjurin1, significantly reducing macrophage cell-cell and cell-matrix adhesion capabilities while decreasing pro-inflammatory factor release, ultimately alleviating vascular leakage in diabetic retinopathy41. Upregulated miR-125a-5p induces macrophage M2 polarization and angiogenesis to promote tendon regeneration and functional recovery42. Additionally, miR-125a-5p targets epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) in vascular smooth muscle cells, inhibiting cell growth, migration, and invasion43. However, its role in KD-associated coronary artery injury remained unclear. Our study demonstrates that miR-125a-5p inhibitor suppresses LCWE-induced pyroptosis in HUVECs and ameliorates coronary inflammation, underscoring the significance of miR-125a-5p in vascular inflammation regulation.

Endothelial pyroptosis leads to impaired vascular structural and functional through the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines44. Reverting pyroptosis in coronary endothelial cells is considered a key therapeutic strategy for KD-induced vasculitis7,45. Pyroptosis is characterized by activation of inflammatory caspases, resulting in GSDMD cleavage, plasma membrane perforation, and sustained cytokine release. In the caspase-1-dependent pathway, the NLRP3 sensor activates caspase-1 through ASC46. KD patients exhibit elevated serum levels of caspase-1, ASC, GSDMD-N, IL-1β, IL-18, and LDH26, and upregulated serum NLRP3 serves as a potential biomarker for predicting coronary aneurysm formation47. Consistent with these findings, our study confirms pyroptosis activation in LCWE-induced KD models. Further research demonstrates that targeted inhibition of miR-125a-5p suppresses NLRP3/caspase-1/GSDMD pathway-mediated pyroptosis in endothelial cells. Previous studies report contradictory regulatory roles of miR-125a-5p in NLRP3 regulation. Studies shows that miR-125a-5p positively regulates NLRP3 to promote oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL)-induced endothelial pyroptosis48, whereas it targets CCL4 to inhibit NLRP3 in ox-LDL-treated vascular smooth muscle cells49. These discrepancies may originate from cell type-specific signaling networks, highlighting the context-dependent roles of miR-125a-5p in NLRP3 regulation.

This study has several limitations. HUVECs, to some extent, simulate the endothelial cell subtypes and microenvironment associated with KD. HUVECs may not fully replicate coronary artery-specific factors such as hemodynamic stresses. The miR-125a-5p signaling axis requires validation in primary coronary cells under disease-specific conditions. We will utilize a patient cohort to validate the association between pyroptosis markers and miR-125a-5p and provide support for clinical translation. Although our data strongly support the notion of pyroptosis, future research could start from the perspective of spatial localization (such as immunofluorescence for GSDMD and ASC localization) to improve specificity. Furthermore, miR-125a-5p may multiple target genes and cellular processes, which are modulated by upstream mechanisms including epigenetic modifications and transcription factors. We also will employ RNA sequencing combined with CRISPR knockout models to focus on screening the main targets of miR-125a-5p and perform functional validation.

Conclusion

This study reveals that miR-125a-5p promotes NLRP3/caspase-1/GSDMD pathway-mediated pyroptosis in HUVECs and vascular injury in mice. Application of miR-125a-5p inhibitor effectively alleviates these pathological phenotypes. These findings suggest miR-125a-5p as a potential therapeutic target for KD and expand the theoretical foundation for treating KD by inhibiting endothelial pyroptosis.
