Modified retrograde K-wire intramedullary fixation for treating pediatric distal radius diaphyseal–metaphyseal junction fractures: a novel method and a retrospective study
Authors: Wenzhi Bi, Wenqiang Xu, Yongfei Fan, Yao Yao, Xiulin Ma, Jianqiang Zhang, Wei Wang, Chaoyu Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to introduce a novel method, the modified retrograde K-wire intramedullary fixation (KWIF-R), for the treatment of pediatric distal radius diaphyseal–metaphyseal junction (DRDMJ) fractures, and to verify its clinical efficacy by comparing it with the retrograde elastic stable intramedullary nailing (ESIN-R).MethodsThe clinical data of 26 children with DRDMJ fractures admitted to our hospital from April 2023 to November 2024 were retrospectively analyzed. Among them, 15 cases were treated with KWIF-R (KWIF-R group), and 11 patients were treated with the ESIN-R(ESIN-R group). By reviewing the medical records and follow-up results,surgical time, intraoperative bleeding, fluoroscopy times, alignment rate, and angulation on radiography were compared between the two groups. Forearm rotation, wrist function, and complications were compared at the last follow-up.ResultsBoth groups had successful operations, and the fractures healed completely. In the comparison of mean age、causes, and from injury to surgical time between the two groups, the difference was not statistically significant (P > 0.05). The surgical time and fluoroscopy times of KWIF-R were lower than ESIN-R, and the difference was statistically significant (P < 0.05). In addition, the KWIF-R with no incision. The postoperative alignment rate of KWIF-R and ESIN-R was all satisfied (P > 0.05). In addition, no complications such as tendon rupture, skin irritation, or premature epiphyseal closure occurred in both groups.ConclusionKWIF-R provides a novel method for the treatment of pediatric DRDMJ fractures with satisfactory recovery of upper extremity motor function, and, with shorter surgical time, no incision, and less radiation exposure than the classical ESIN-R. It is a noteworthy surgical method for the treatment of pediatric DRDMJ fractures.Keywords: DRDMJ fractures, Pediatric, Novel method, K-wire, Intramedullary fixation

Introduction

Distal radius fracture is a common injury in pediatrics [1]. DRDMJ fractures in juveniles denote a unique variant of distal radius fracture, and the anatomical localization of this site is distinctive. It leads to this disparity, rendering stabilization and fixation notably arduous and predisposing the patient to complications such as fracture displacement and epiphyseal injury [2]. Currently, the successful management of DRDMJ fractures, ensuring economic feasibility, safety, and minimal complications, remains a hot topic and an unavoidable challenge in clinical practice.

Closed reduction with internal fixation or minimally invasive fixation techniques emerges as the predominant treatment modality following unsuccessful conservative management of DRDMJ fractures in clinical settings [3]. ESIN-R and retrograde K-wire fixation are the mainstay procedures for the treatment of DRDMJ fractures in children [4, 5]. ESIN-R is a well-established treatment modality that has achieved satisfactory clinical outcomes; however, its drawbacks, such as the need for an incision, high cost, and difficulty in removal, should not be overlooked [6]. Surgeons are hesitant to choose K-wires for percutaneous fixation because they need multiple K-wires to enhance fixation and place K-wires [7]. However, it is indisputable that percutaneous fixation with K-wires has the advantage of being inexpensive, minimally invasive, and easily removable, which seems to compensate for the shortcomings of ESIN-R.

Therefore, we attempted to employ the modified technology, KWIF-R, to treat DRDMJ fractures in children and compared it with ESIN-R. It was verified to be a safe and reliable novel treatment method.

Patients and methods

A retrospective analysis from April 2023 and November 2024, and data of 26 patients with DRDMJ fractures who received surgical treatment involving the KWIF-R or ESIN-R technique were collected and analyzed. Our study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fuyang People's Hospital Affiliated to Anhui Medical University. Informed consent was obtained from all included Children's legal guardian(s).

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1)with DRDMJ fractures; (2) closed fractures; (3)with manual repositioning fails to achieve or maintain a stable reduction after repositioning; (4) surgery with the KWIF-R or ESIN-R technique (5) is aged 7–14 years; and The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) pathologic fracture; (2) DRDMJ fractures concomitant with other fractures, excluding distal ulna fracture; (3) the time from injury to operation was > 2 weeks; and (4) inadequate follow-up data, with < 4 months of follow-up.

To diminish variations in X-ray imaging and the actual size of the distal radius influenced by a child's age and height [8–11], we adopted Li et al.'s [12] definition of DMJ (Fig. 1).

Surgical procedures

The child was positioned supine, anesthetized with general anesthesia, protected by a lead jacket to shield the glands, and covered with sterilized towels. For pediatric distal ulna fractures, depending on the degree of fracture displacement, we selected either manipulative reduction, ESIN, or the K-wire for fixation. DRDMJ fractures reduction and fixation employ the KWIF-R or ESIN-R. ESIN-R was performed using classic, traditionally reported techniques. Fix the stable bone first; if both are unstable, fix the ulna first.

KWIF-R was a novel technique performed by two attending surgeons as follows (Fig. 2): The operator palpated and adjusted to determine the position of the fracture's distal fragment, as shown in Fig. 2A. Then, percutaneously insert a 1.5 or 2.0 mm K-wire into the distal fragment's appropriate site directly into the medullary cavity of the distal fragment. The K-wire is oriented towards the Lister tubercle of the radius in the anterior–posterior position and slightly dorsal to the lateral extension of the medullary cavity (Fig. 2A). Under fluoroscopy, the position of the K-wire is confirmed, and the K-wire is then passed intramedullary through the distal fragment (Fig. 2B), emerging from the skin at a predetermined distal position (Fig. 2C). DRDMJ fracture is manually reduced, with fluoroscopic verification; if the reduction is inadequate, a New "K" wire is used for manipulation and reduction of the fracture. Upon achieving satisfactory reduction, the ESIN T holder rotates the K-wire back into the proximal fragment medullary cavity, securing the fracture proximally while minimizing thermal damage compared to an electric drill (Fig. 2D). Fluoroscopic anterior and lateral views are employed to confirm fracture alignment and internal fixation positioning (Fig. 2E). Post-fixation, the K-wire's end is bent at a 90° angle, a sterile dressing is applied, and a plaster splint is applied.

Surgical procedure: A In: percutaneous insertion of a smooth K-wire into the medullary cavity of the distal fragment of radius from the fracture site; B Out: drawing the K-wire out through the skin at the distal fragment end; C Manipulation: adjusting the fracture ends with the K-wire; D Back: once alignment is satisfactory, the K-wire is advanced into the proximal fragment of the medullary cavity to maintain fracture alignment and secure fixation; E Verification: radiographs confirm the fracture fixation by the K-wire

Evaluation management

Surgical time, fluoroscopy times, bleeding loss, and fracture alignment rate were recorded. The anteroposterior (AP) and the lateral plane (LP) fracture alignment were calculated as follows: (actual contact surface length of two fragments/length of fracture line) *100% on X-ray films. It is worth noting that to increase confidence, the above data relate only to DRDMJ fractures. The plaster was removed 4–6 weeks postoperatively, and active functional exercise was advised in the two groups. The plaster, K-wires, and ESIN were removed according to fracture healing after surgery. At the last follow-up, wrist function was evaluated using the Gartland–Werley criteria, and forearm rotation, infection, and radial nerve injury were evaluated.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Chi-square test or the Fisher exact test was applied to analyze categorical variables. The measurement data were tested for normality, and the data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). If they normally distributed, the comparison between the two groups was performed by an unpaired t test. If non-normally distributed, the data were represented as M(Q1, Q3), and the Mann–Whitney U test of two independent samples was used for comparison between the two groups. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, with the significance threshold α = 0.05 (two-tailed).

Results

Comparison of general postoperative characteristics between the two groups

Both groups had successful operations, and the fractures healed completely. In the comparison of mean age、causes (sports injury), and from injury to surgical time of KWIF-R(9.0(8.0–13.0) years, 13/15, 3.87 ± 3.31 days) and ESIN-R(8.0(8.0–13.0) years, 8/11, 5.45 ± 3.56 days), the difference was not statistically significant (P > 0.05). The surgical time and fluoroscopies of KWIF-R(18.07 ± 2.94 min, 8.00 ± 1.41 times) were lower than ESIN-R(23.18 ± 5.13 min, 10.82 ± 2.71times), and the difference was statistically significant (P < 0.05). The KWIF-R with no incision and the incision length of ESIN-R were 1.45 ± 0.42 cm (Table 1).

Discussion

Characteristics of DRDMJ fractures

The concept of the distal metaphyseal migration zone of the radius in children was initially proposed by Lieber et al. [9] and has been redefined as the diaphysis–metaphyseal junction (DMJ). This anatomically distinctive region features a distally broad and proximally narrow radius, a trumpet-like transformation in the medullary cavity [13], a widened proximal–distal medullary gap, and a propensity for fractures under transmitted stress. The fracture end’s diameter and contact area are comparatively smaller than those of a DMJ, rendering it unstable post-repositioning. This site, less pliable than the metaphysis, presents greater treatment challenges, particularly in older children [6, 14].

Current mainstream treatment of DRDMJ fractures

Children's bones exhibit significant plasticity, and their muscle strength is inferior to that of adults. Therefore, combining manipulative reduction with plaster fixation can yield satisfactory outcomes [15]. However, for DRDMJ fractures, the success rate of manipulative reduction with plaster fixation is low, and the displacement rate of the fracture end post-reduction is high, warranting cautious consideration. Particularly in older children, the bone's remodeling ability diminishes, and an excessive emphasis on conservative treatment may impair forearm rotational function [16, 17]. Surgical intervention is often necessary when reductions fail to maintain stability after manual repositioning [18]. Surgical techniques vary, and no universally accepted standard procedure exists as a first choice [19].

Traditional ESIN-R tends to exacerbate fracture end displacement during insertion and fails to achieve effective "three-point support," impeding the maintenance of fracture stability, length, and rotational alignment [6]. Some scholars have highlighted the "L"-type de-elasticization [20] and "S"-type precise contouring techniques [21] as superior methods for DRDMJ fracture treatment. Although these techniques yield better reduction outcomes, they may prolong operation time and necessitate a second hospitalization for nail removal, thereby increasing patients' medical expenses [7, 22]. The anterograde ESIN technique carries a risk of injuring the deep branch of the radial nerve, resulting in a loss of finger and hand extension compared to the contralateral side. Conversely, closed reduction with small incision-assisted external fixation can circumvent the fracture site for nail placement and allow early functional exercise. However, this approach is more prone to nail tract infections, requires meticulous nail site care, is costlier, and is often preferred for open fractures [23, 24]. The classic technique of percutaneous fixation with crossed K-wires is favored by many clinicians because it requires no incision, is cost-effective, relatively safe, and easy to remove [25]. However, in cases of DRDMJ fractures, where the fracture line of the DRDMJ is too high, the K-wire should be inserted too obliquely into the proximal fragment, where the cortex is thicker, which is demanding on the operator [19].

To address these issues, we employed the KWIF-R technique, which effectively reduces the shortcomings of the traditional method and achieves satisfactory clinical efficacy.

Analysis of the reasons for the satisfactory efficacy of KWIF-R for DRDMJ fractures

For KWIF-R, by pre-setting the path of the K-wire, the insertion can achieve fracture reduction and maintain a good alignment. In contrast, ESIN-R requires relatively precise shaping in advance. Comparatively speaking, this technique is more convenient to operate and has a high degree of reproducibility. This has a decisive impact on the operation time and the number of fluoroscopic examinations. This technique involves inserting into the medullary cavity from the fracture end first, which ensures optimal placement of the Kirschner wire at the fracture site. It can also be applied to the ulna, clavicle, and other regions.

This novel method is applicable to both stable and unstable fractures and penetrates the epiphysis only once. Single K-wire fixation and minimal epiphyseal plate disturbance significantly mitigate the risk of epiphyseal plate damage inherent in traditional K-wire placement, thereby substantially reducing the likelihood of iatrogenically induced premature epiphyseal closure. Moreover, the application of a single K-wire minimized the cost of treatment. To minimize complications such as neurovascular tendon injuries, we hold the K-wire directed towards the Lister tubercle of the radius in both the anteroposterior and lateral views, which define the “safety zone” [26]. Clinical results showed no associated complications, indicating that this is effective.

KWIF-R does not require an incision, while ESIN-R requires an incision, which increases the operation time and trauma. In addition, it is necessary to keep the incision clean after the operation (it needs to remove and fix the plaster), which may reduce the alignment rate of the fracture fragments. At the same time, with this technique, the K-wire can be easily removed without the need for anesthesia.

Moreover, it is worth noting that surgeons may be concerned about its stability. We have applied plaster splint fixation as an adjuvant treatment in combination, which conforms to the treatment concept of "sparse internal fixation and firm external fixation" for pediatric fractures, and the follow-up results show that the fracture stability is reliable.

In addition, this study possesses shortcomings, including its confinement to a solitary center, the follow-up time is short, and the sample size included in this study is small. At the same time, for DMJ, bone marrow cavities are too large, and the stability of this method may be a concern. It would be of interest to compare the novel method with more surgical techniques or to perform biomechanical analysis.

Conclusion

This modified method, KWIF-R with single K-wire, for the treatment of DRDMJ fractures in children is highly maneuverable and results in satisfactory fracture reduction and functional recovery, while offering the advantages of an accelerated surgical procedure, minimal trauma, and reduced radiation erosion. It is therefore a viable surgical approach for surgeons.
