Safety and efficacy assessment of fecal microbiota transplantation as an adjunctive treatment for IgA nephropathy: an exploratory clinical trial
Authors: Wenqiang Zhi, Aizhong Li, Qian Wang, Xiaoli Yuan, Jianbo Qing, Caixiang Zhang, Yuxin Wang, Yafeng Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
To assess the safety and efficacy of fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) as an adjunctive therapeutic intervention for IgA nephropathy (IgAN). Fifteen patients with IgA nephropathy were recruited based on inclusion and exclusion criteria and underwent FMT using enteric microbial capsules. Clinical indicators, intestinal microbiota and metabolomic profiles, as well as changes in serum immune cells and cytokines, were monitored before and after FMT. No severe adverse reactions were observed in the subjects. After FMT, there was a reduction in the 24-h urinary protein quantification in subjects. The relative abundances of Phocaeicola_vulgatus, Bacteroides_uniformis, Prevotella_copri, Phocaeicola_dorei, Bacteroides_ovatus, Bacteroides_xylanisolvens, Parabacteroides _distasonis, Bifidobacterium_pseudocatenulatum, Bacteroides_sp._HF-162, and Bifidobacterium_longum changed after FMT. In terms of intestinal metabolites, the levels of acylcarnitine18:0 (ACar.18:0), cotinine, N-arachidonoyl-L-serine, phosphatidylcholine (PC. (18:3e/22:6)), serotonin, and fumagillin showed significant changes. Flow cytometry analysis showed the absolute count of plasma B cells decreased in subjects, and this change correlated with alterations in the intestinal microbiota and metabolites. This study preliminarily evaluates the safety and efficacy of FMT in patients with IgAN. No significant adverse reactions were observed, and the administration of FMT alongside ACEI/ARB therapy was effective in reducing urinary protein levels in patients with IgAN, a process that may be associated with B-cell immunity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74171-4.

Introduction

IgA nephropathy (IgAN), characterized by the deposition of immunoglobulin A (IgA) in the glomerular mesangium, is the most common form of primary glomerulonephritis worldwide1. Research indicates that individuals of Asian descent with IgAN tend to exhibit more severe clinical manifestations and face a higher risk of disease progression compared to populations in other regions2–5. Despite its prevalence, the pathogenesis of IgAN remains incompletely understood, and therapeutic options are limited. Current treatment modalities, including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs) or angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), primarily target blood pressure control and proteinuria reduction but do not address the underlying immune dysfunction. Commonly used immunosuppressive agents such as cyclophosphamide, mycophenolate mofetil, and azathioprine are associated with a high incidence of adverse events6. Regarding steroid therapy, the KDIGO guidelines recommend considering steroid therapy when persistent proteinuria persists despite maximal supportive treatment. However, due to the numerous adverse effects associated with steroids, there remains controversy over their use in primary IgAN patients at high risk of progression6–10. The efficacy of tonsillectomy in the management of IgAN still requires further validation.

The widely accepted pathogenesis of IgAN is the ‘multiple-hit hypothesis’. Dysbiosis of the gut microbiota can compromise the integrity of the intestinal mucosal barrier, leading to the release of galactose-deficient IgA1 (Gd-IgA1) into the bloodstream. Upon binding with specific antibodies, these IgA1 deposits in renal tissues, contributing to the development of IgAN. In this process, dysbiosis of the gut microbiota plays a pivotal role as a “trigger point”11–13. Altered gut microbial composition has been associated with increased gut permeability and aberrant immune responses, which may exacerbate kidney damage. Budesonide in the form of enteric-targeted release (Nefecon) is a novel therapeutic agent for IgAN that modulates immune function by targeting the gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT). Clinical studies have demonstrated that this approach significantly reduces urinary protein levels in patients with IgAN14.

FMT is primarily employed in the management of chronic diseases associated with intestinal dysbiosis. It represents a shift in the treatment of intestinal microbiota from previous approaches of eliminating individual microorganisms or specific pathogens and increasing beneficial gut microbiota to the reconstruction of the overall intestinal microbiota profile. Initially used to treat Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI), studies have shown that FMT currently achieves a cure rate of 90% for recurrent CDI. Its application in other diseases such as inflammatory bowel disease, irritable bowel syndrome, obesity, diabetes, systemic lupus erythematosus, and others, is currently a research focus15,16. The modulation of gut microbiota through FMT as a therapeutic approach for IgAN represents a novel endeavor that has not yet been subjected to rigorously designed large-scale clinical studies. One study conducted in a humanized IgAN mouse model (α1KI-CD89Tg) demonstrated promising results. The study revealed that IgAN mice receiving fecal microbiota transplantation from healthy controls exhibited a more significant decrease in the urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR), downregulation of inflammatory cytokines expression, and improvement in IgAN phenotype17. Clinical research in this area is currently limited to case reports18,19. Further well-designed clinical trials are warranted to validate the efficacy and safety of FMT in treating IgAN. In view of the established link between gut microbiota and the aetiology of IgAN, the present study is designed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of FMT as a potential treatment for IgAN. The findings of this research are anticipated to furnish evidence that will inform the development of future microbiome-targeted therapeutic strategies for IgAN.

Methods

Patients

This study enrolled fifteen patients with IgAN from January 1, 2022 to December 31, 2023 at Shanxi Provincial People’s Hospital. All participants provided written informed consent. Inclusion criteria (all must be met for participation): (1) IgAN diagnosed through renal biopsy; (2) 24-h urine protein quantification > 500 mg/d after 3–6 months of ACEI/ARB therapy; (3) intolerance or unwillingness to endure the side effects of corticosteroids and immunosuppressants; (4) negative urinary pregnancy test and no plans for pregnancy in the next 18 months with reliable contraceptive measures; (5) ages 18–70; (6) signed informed consent forms for clinical research and FMT. Exclusion criteria (presence of any disqualifies participation): (1) Secondary IgAN, e.g., systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), cirrhosis, IgA vasculitis; (2) antibiotic use within the last 14 days; (3) malignant hypertension or other uncontrolled severe hypertension (systolic > 180 mmHg or diastolic > 110 mmHg); (4) active systemic infection or serious infection within one month before enrollment; (5) leukopenia < 3.0 × 109/L, anemia (hemoglobin < 80 g/L), thrombocytopenia < 80 × 109/L, or other hematological disorders; (6) malignancy or other diseases with a life expectancy of < 3 months; (7) severe cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease and gastrointestinal dysfunction; (8) other immunological diseases; (9) inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI), or gastrointestinal tumors; (10) active gastrointestinal bleeding or acute/chronic gastrointestinal inflammation; (11) has previously undergone or is currently undergoing FMT; (12) psychiatric or cognitive disorders; (13) history of alcohol or drug abuse. Disqualification criteria: (1) non-compliance with inclusion criteria; (2) withdrawal from the study for reasons other than adverse reactions or poor efficacy. Criteria for mid-study withdrawal include: (1) medical necessity as determined by the researcher; (2) participant’s request to stop the trial.

Enteric microbial capsules preparation

Participants underwent FMT via enteric microbiota capsules, prepared and screened by Oriental Yeekang (Beijing) Medicine Technology Co., LTD. Donors were initially assessed through questionnaires covering medical history, behavioral risks, and current health status. Potential candidates underwent stool and serological testing to rule out infectious diseases and potential gut microbiome dysbiosis. After screening, 2–3% qualified as donors. Fresh feces collected from donors were processed in class 10,000 cleanroom through dilution, filtration, and centrifugation to produce the enteric microbiota capsules.

Drug administration and follow-up

The enteric microbiota capsules were transported under cold chain conditions to Shanxi Provincial People’s Hospital and stored at -80℃. Each bottle contained 20 capsules, with three bottles constituting one treatment course. Participants ingested 20 capsules on Day 1, Day 8, and Day 15 of the study, with each bottle uniquely coded for traceability. Researchers collected baseline data and clinical information from participants before enrollment and collected urine, stool, and blood samples before and after the FMT, one and three months post the last FMT. Urine samples were analyzed for 24-h protein quantification, urinalysis, and specific proteins. Stool samples were analyzed through metagenomic sequencing for gut microbiota and mass spectrometry for metabolomics. Blood samples were analyzed for complete blood count, liver enzymes, electrolytes, and creatinine, and flow cytometry was used to measure serum immune cells and cytokines.

Detection of immune cells and cytokines

T-cell subsets were detected using Myriad CD3/CD8/CD45/CD4 test reagents and CD3/CD16 + 56/CD45/CD19 test reagents, regulatory T-cells were detected using Beckman CD4 test reagents, CD25 test reagents, CD3 test reagents, CD127 test reagents, 12 cytokines including (IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12p70, IL-17, IFN-α, IFN-γ, TNF-α) were detected using the United Medicine 12 cytokines detection kit. Results were analyzed using Flow Jo software and LEGEND plex v8.0.

Detection of gut microbiota and metabolites

Metagenomic sequencing and gut metabolite testing were conducted by Novo gene Co., LTD. The process of metagenomic sequencing includes nucleic acid extraction, library construction, sequencing, and data quality control and analysis. Detection of non-targeted gut metabolomics using LC-MS/MS technology.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using R software (version 4.2.0). The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to test for normality. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x ± s) and analyzed using t-tests or ANOVA; non-normally distributed data were expressed as median (25th percentile, 75th percentile) and analyzed using variance analysis or the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical data were expressed as number (percentage) and analyzed using chi-square tests. LefSe (Linear discriminant analysis Effect Size) was used to explore differences in metabolites in IgAN patients before and after medication. Additionally, Pearson or Spearman correlation analyses were conducted to investigate the pairwise relationships among the top 10 abundance expressions of gut microbiota, differential metabolites, and clinical indices in IgAN patients before and after medication. All tests were two-sided with a significance level set at α = 0.05.

Clinical trial registration

This study has been registered at the China Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2100053206, 14/11/2021) and clinicaltrials.gov (NCT05182775, 10/01/2022) .

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

This study included a total of fifteen participants, comprising five females and ten males. Following treatment, six patients exhibited a decrease in urinary protein quantification of > 50% compared to baseline, while nine patients exhibited a decrease of < 50%. The mean 24-h urinary protein quantification for all participants before enrollment was 1.15 ± 0.94 g/day. Among those with a > 50% decrease in urinary protein after treatment, the mean 24-h urinary protein quantification before enrollment was 1.37 ± 1.09 g/day, whereas for those with a < 50% decrease, it was 1.00 ± 0.87 g/day. Before enrollment, six patients experienced gastrointestinal symptoms, with three experiencing diarrhea and three experiencing constipation. Among those with a > 50% decrease in urinary protein after treatment, two patients had diarrhea and one had constipation. Among those with a < 50% decrease, one patient had diarrhea and one had constipation. All patients showed improvement in gastrointestinal symptoms after treatment (Table 1).

Changes in clinical indicators

Changes in 24-h urinary protein quantification

The 24-h urinary protein quantification values of the fifteen participants exhibited a decreasing trend after FMT and during follow-up at 1 month and 3 months post-FMT compared to baseline (see Fig. 1A). During the follow-up period, two participants showed an acute increase in 24-h urinary protein quantification due to acute upper respiratory tract infections. After excluding these two participants, the remaining thirteen participants demonstrated a significant decrease in 24-h urinary protein quantification at 1 month and 3 months post-FMT compared to baseline (see Fig. 1B). These results indicate that the adjunctive use of enteric microbiota capsules with ACEI/ARB therapy has a proteinuria-lowering effect in IgAN patients.

Changes in specific urinary proteins

Subsequently, an analysis of variance or Kruskal–Wallis test was conducted to investigate the changes in specific urinary proteins of the participants before FMT, and at 1 month and 3 months post-FMT. By comparing the numerical ranges or averages at different time points, the study examined the variations of these indicators in participants before and after medication.

Specifically, indicators such as ACR, MCR, urinary immunoglobulins, urinary α2-macroglobulin, urinary α1-microglobulin, urinary NAG enzyme/urinary creatinine ratio, urinary κ light chain, and urinary λ light chain did not exhibit significant differences between different time points (p > 0.05). Overall, while there were slight fluctuations at different time points, the overall changes in these indicators before and after treatment were not remarkable (Table S1).

Changes in immune cells and cytokines

The production of inflammatory factors plays a crucial role in the development of IgAN, with B cells playing a central role in pathogen and mucosal inflammatory diseases. This study focused on the changes in immune cells and cytokines before and after FMT in the participants. To do so, the analysis included immune cells at various time points, including lymphocyte counts, T cell percentages, T cell counts, helper/inducer T cell percentages, helper/inducer T cell counts, suppressor/cytotoxic T cell percentages, suppressor/cytotoxic T cell counts, B cell percentages, B cell counts, NK cell percentages, NK cell counts, as well as cytokines such as IL-5, IL-4, IL-2, IL-10, IFN-α, IL-1B, IL-12P70, IL-8, IL-17 A, IL-6, IFN-γ, and TNF-α. After FMT, there was a significant decrease in B cell absolute counts compared to before (p < 0.05) (Table 2), while the changes in other indicators at different time points were not statistically significant (p > 0.05) (Table S2).

Changes in safety indicators

Most of the blood parameters and liver and kidney function indicators showed no significant changes between different time points before and after FMT (p > 0.05) (Table S3). However, there were statistically significant differences in serum sodium and serum calcium between different time points (p < 0.05) (Table 2), although they were not clinically significant. Nonetheless, this highlights the importance of paying special attention to changes in serum sodium and serum calcium when evaluating the safety of FMT for patients with IgAN.

Adverse events

None of the participants involved in this study experienced any severe adverse events. Throughout the study period, a total of five adverse events occurred in two patients. During the follow-up period, two participants experienced acute upper respiratory tract infections, accompanied by an elevation in urinary protein. Among them, one participant reported mild abdominal discomfort, which resolved spontaneously after discontinuation of the medication (Table 3).

Changes in gut microbiota composition and correlation analysis with differential clinical indicators

To assess whether there were changes in the composition of the gut microbiota before and after FMT in the participants, fecal samples were collected at different time points before and after treatment, and subjected to metagenomic sequencing. Firstly, the top 30 abundant bacterial species before and after microbiota transplantation were identified. Analysis of the alpha diversity of the flora demonstrated that the diversity of the intestinal flora of the subjects increased after one month of FMT (Fig. S1). β-diversity demonstrates that there are variations in the composition of the intestinal flora of subjects before and after FMT (Fig. S2). Subsequently, ten bacterial species were selected based on their relatively high abundance and significant changes before and after FMT. These species included Phocaeicola_vulgatus, Bacteroides_uniformis, Prevotella_copri, Phocaeicola_dorei, Bacteroides_ovatus, Bacteroides_xylanisolvens, Parabacteroides_distasonis, Bifidobacterium_pseudocatenulatum, Bacteroides_sp._HF-162, and Bifidobacterium_longum (Fig. 2).

Further, Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to explore the correlation between the relative abundance changes of the selected bacterial species and the differential clinical indicators. As shown in the analysis, the relative abundance changes of Bacteroides_uniformis and Bacteroides_ovatus were significantly positively correlated with changes in B cells (p < 0.05), while Prevotella_copri showed a significant negative correlation with changes in B cells (p < 0.05). Apart from these three bacteria, the relative abundance changes of other species were not significantly correlated with changes in B cells (p > 0.05). Moreover, there was no significant correlation between the relative abundance changes of all bacterial species and changes in serum calcium and serum sodium (p > 0.05) (Table 4).

Intestinal metabolite composition changes and correlation analysis with differential clinical indicators

For comparing the changes in the composition of intestinal metabolites before and after FMT in the participants, fecal samples were collected at different time points before and after treatment and subjected to untargeted metabolomic analysis using mass spectrometry. The LEFse analysis was employed to investigate changes in intestinal metabolites before and after treatment, revealing the following metabolites: acylcarnitine18:0 (ACar.18:0), cotinine, N-arachidonoyl-L-serine, phosphatidylcholine (PC.(18:3e/22:6)), serotonin, and fumagillin. Discussion

This is the inaugural clinical study to investigate the potential of FMT for the treatment of IgAN. Throughout the FMT procedure and the subsequent 3-month follow-up period, no severe adverse reactions were observed in any of the patients. During the study period, a total of five adverse events were recorded. Both instances of upper respiratory tract infection occurred during the prevalence of infectious diseases, with one patient diagnosed with novel coronavirus infection via nucleic acid testing, and another diagnosed with influenza via nucleic acid testing. In both cases, acute elevation of proteinuria was observed during the upper respiratory tract infection period, which returned to pre-infection levels within 24 h of infection control. Based on this evidence, the researchers concluded that the occurrence of these adverse reactions was not directly associated with the ingestion of fecal microbiota capsules. One case of abdominal discomfort was characterized by mild bloating during capsule ingestion, which may be related to the intake of fecal microbiota capsules, yet the symptoms resolved spontaneously within 1–2 days post-capsule consumption. No significant changes were observed in the blood routine, liver enzymes, or serum creatinine levels before and after FMT in all subjects. This study demonstrated a statistically significant increase in serum calcium and sodium levels post-FMT, albeit within the clinically normal range, suggesting no practical clinical significance. However, given the relatively short duration of the FMT regimen in this study, this result highlights the importance of monitoring changes in serum electrolyte levels in future long-term FMT interventions for IgAN patients.

The effectiveness was investigated by monitoring changes in 24-h urinary protein quantification before and after FMT, as well as during follow-up. The results revealed that despite two patients experiencing acute elevation in 24-h urinary protein quantification due to acute infections, the values still decreased after FMT. Excluding these two patients, the remaining thirteen participants exhibited a significant reduction in 24-h urinary protein quantification. It preliminarily demonstrates the potential of FMT via fecal microbiota capsules to reduce urinary protein excretion in IgAN patients. The reduction in 24-h urinary protein quantification observed in this study can serve as supportive data for future research and aid in determining sample sizes for future randomized controlled trials.

This study utilized flow cytometry to monitor changes in serum immune cells and cytokines in IgAN patients, alongside examining alterations in various urinary protein profiles. The results indicated no significant changes in urinary protein profiles before and after treatment. However, post- FMT, a decrease in serum B cell absolute count was observed, correlating with alterations in gut microbiota and certain differential metabolites. As the source of galactose-deficient IgA1 (Gd-IgA1), B cells play a central role in the pathogenesis of IgAN20. “Multiple-hit” theory suggests that dysbiosis of mucosal microbiota may lead to activation of the mucosal immune system. Experimental evidence indicates that pathogens or resident microbiota may activate mucosal immune cells via Toll-like receptors, upregulating the expression of B cell activating factor and A proliferation-inducing ligand. Subsequently, the activation of mucosal B cells is further facilitated through interactions between B cell activating factor receptors, B cell maturation antigens, and T cell-activating factors, as well as calcium-regulating ligands21–23. The findings of this study suggest that FMT may improve the condition of IgAN by modulating intestinal microbiota, ameliorating abnormal mucosal immune responses, and regulating B cell expression. However, the specific mechanisms underlying this process require further investigation in subsequent research.

Through comparative analysis, we found that the relative abundance changes of Bacteroides_uniformis and Bacteroides_ovatus after FMT were significantly positively correlated with changes in serum B cell counts, while the abundance change of Prevotella_copri was significantly negatively correlated with serum B cell counts. Bacteroides_uniformis has been previously associated with chronic kidney disease and Behçet’s disease, with higher abundance observed in the guts of patients with these conditions compared to healthy controls24,25. Bacteroides_ovatus can stimulate abnormal activation of mucosal-associated invariant T cells (MAIT cells), leading to the secretion of interleukin 17 (IL-17) and triggering mild inflammatory responses in the body26. Prevotella_copri are typically associated with a plant-based diet and a reduction in their abundance has been linked to certain diseases such as allergies and multiple sclerosis27.The specific mechanisms by which the changes in these bacterial species modulate intestinal mucosal immunity and consequently influence the progression of IgAN remain open for further exploration in future research. Furthermore, through non-targeted metabolomic analysis of intestinal metabolites, this study identified six significantly differentially abundant intestinal metabolites before and after FMT. Among these, ACar.18:0 is a fatty acid metabolite, and phosphatidylcholine (PC.(18:3e/22:6)) is a product of phospholipid metabolism, both belonging to the lipid metabolism pathway. The changes in ACar.18:0 were found to be correlated with the relative abundance of Prevotella_copri and Bacteroides_uniformis, as well as changes in serum calcium levels, suggesting that lipid metabolism may play a role in improving intestinal microbiota in IgAN. Serotonin levels in the gut increased after FMT, and were correlated with changes in Parabacteroides_distasonis and Bacteroides_uniformis. Over 90% of serotonin is produced in the digestive tract, and studies have found that it plays a crucial role in controlling intestinal motility. Elevated serotonin levels can enhance cognitive ability and autonomic nervous system function28. Fumagillin is an antibiotic with various biological activities. Previous studies have found its anti-angiogenic properties, and it has been investigated in the treatment of diseases related to tumors, cardiovascular conditions, the central nervous system, and the respiratory system. Cotinine is the primary metabolite of nicotine in the body, and its changes are correlated with alterations in the relative abundance of Parabacteroides_distasonis29.

The limitation of this study is the small sample size, and the conclusions drawn from it require further substantiation through a larger-scale randomised controlled trial. Nevertheless, the results obtained in this study can serve as a basis for future randomised controlled trials. This study only investigated the safety and efficacy of oral capsule-based FMT in the treatment of IgA nephropathy. While this method is simple and minimally invasive, the results cannot be generalized to other FMT modalities, such as those administered via endoscopy. This study did not include an analysis of serum metabolites in patients with IgA nephropathy. Future research should consider incorporating serum metabolite profiling to further elucidate the potential mechanisms and therapeutic effects of FMT in the treatment of IgA nephropathy.

Conclusion

This study preliminarily verifies the safety and efficacy of FMT in patients with IgAN. No significant adverse reactions were observed, and the administration of FMT alongside ACEI/ARB therapy was effective in reducing urinary protein levels in patients with IgAN, a process that may be associated with B-cell immunity.
