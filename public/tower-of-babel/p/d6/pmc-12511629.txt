Social-ecological filters influence plant and invertebrate diversity in educational gardens in a South American metropolis
Authors: María Sofía Herrera-Oesterheld, Sonia Reyes Paecke, Alejandra E. Muñoz, Paul Amouroux, Catalina Trujillo, José Tomás Ibarra
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Educational gardens are green areas in schools that are cultivated with vegetables and are at least partially under the care of students. Although they are usually small in size, they can be biodiversity refuges and act as stepping stones for the flow of species within cities. Biodiversity in educational gardens may be “filtered” by social-ecological processes occurring within the garden, around the garden, and by management practices. This study analyzed species richness and the social-ecological filters that may influence plants and invertebrates in school gardens in the Metropolitan Area of Santiago (MAS), Chile. We sampled 33 vegetable gardens in municipal schools, identifying vascular plants (cultivated and spontaneously growing) and invertebrate morphospecies richness in gardens. We further measured the area of the garden, classified the garden’s ground cover and the land cover around the garden’s buffer zone, and conducted an interview with the main gardener. A total of 362 plant species and 127 invertebrate morphospecies were recorded. The most influential social-ecological filters of plant richness were garden area (m2), percentage of cultivated ground cover, and irrigation during the summer. The most influential factors influencing invertebrate richness were plant species richness, bare ground cover (with a negative effect) and, to a lesser extent, tree cover in the garden’s buffer zone and irrigation during the summer. We conclude that the biodiversity of school gardens in the MAS is more related to social-ecological filters within the garden and management practices than to filters within the garden’s buffer zone.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-19375-y.

Introduction

South America is the most urbanized continent in the world, alongside North America1, with nearly 84% of its total population residing in urban settlements2. In Chile, about 75% of the population already lives in urban areas3. Santiago, as the capital, hosts 6.6 million inhabitants, representing 40% of the country’s population4, and it is expected to reach 7.2 million by the year 20305. Between 1997 and 2013, the built-up surface of Santiago increased by 124%, primarily along the peri urban areas of the city6. Urban expansion in Santiago has serious implications for biodiversity conservation given that the city is located in the Chilean Mediterranean climate region, one of the 35 global biodiversity hotspots7. These ecosystems are considered to be among the most vulnerable to biodiversity loss8.

Due to the declining of terrestrial biodiversity9, global biodiversity within urban ecosystems needs to be enhanced. Urban gardens can play a vital social-ecological role promoting biodiversity in cities10–15, and form an interconnected matrix, producing a cumulative effect on the urban ecosystem12,13. However, the biodiversity present in urban gardens will be influenced by social-ecological filters—human-nature factors that influence the taxonomic diversity of small animals, removing species according to their functional traits16–18. These processes can be grouped into filters occurring within the garden (e.g., garden size and structure), surrounding the garden (e.g., human infrastructure and land-cover), and that are the result of garden management practices18. The latter, for example, includes watering regimes and the application of insecticides or synthetic fertilizers13,15,19. Our understanding of various attributes of urban garden biodiversity in developing regions of the world is however lacking, requiring more extensive research (see Supplementary Note S1, Supplementary Table S2, Supplementary Figure S3, and Supplementary Figure S4 for more on this)10,11,19,20.

Educational gardens are green areas within schools which are cultivated with vegetables and are at least partially under the care of students21. Schools’ greenspaces can act as small springboards for the flow of species22, and like home gardens, they can be considered social-ecological systems that act as complex microenvironments in which useful plants are cultivated17,23. Although they are usually small in size, school gardens can be biodiversity refuges and have the potential to become stepping stones for the flow of species within cities22,23. In addition to the ecological benefits of these green spaces, by exposing and connecting children to nature, educational gardens have the potential to result in greater happiness, mental and physical wellbeing, social wellbeing (fewer peer problems), improved attention in class, and sustainable behaviors in the children who attend schools with gardens24–26. Meanwhile, the main gardeners of school gardens may experience improved mental health and emotional wellbeing, as well as increased physical activity26.

In Chile, most research on garden biodiversity and the role of social-ecological filters has been conducted in rural and peri-urban home gardens17,18,27. The cadaster of community gardens in the city of Santiago reported a total of 227 urban gardens, of which 19% were school gardens28. Moraga29 compiled 84 urban agriculture experiences (the ways in which urban agriculture is practiced, relating to commercial, educational, medicinal, productive, social, and therapeutic uses), of which 25% were related to school gardens. Although the COVID-19 pandemic resulted in an increase in alternative food system practices, including school gardens30, there is still a lack of studies on the biodiversity of educational gardens in highly populated cities.

We sought to determine how social-ecological filters within and external to gardens, along with management variables, relate to plant and invertebrate biodiversity in municipal school gardens (hereafter referred to as “gardens”) in the Metropolitan Area of Santiago (MAS), Chile. Specifically, we determined plant and invertebrate richness in gardens and evaluated the influence of social-ecological filters occurring within the garden, around the garden, and through management practices on the biodiversity present in gardens. The ultimate goal of this research was to better understand biodiversity patterns and processes in gardens in an attempt to grasp which social-ecological processes have the potential to enhance urban biodiversity in a Metropolis embedded in a global biodiversity hotspot.

Within the school garden

Plant richness

Almost half of the gardens’ ground cover surface lacked vegetation (46.6%), about a third had natural ground cover (32.8%), and only about a fifth of the surface had cultivated ground cover (20.6%). Regarding vegetative coverage by vertical strata, the highest was the tree stratum followed by the grass/herb stratum, the shrub stratum 1, and lastly, the shrub stratum 2 (29.2%, 26.5%, 13.5%, and 6.2% species respectively).

We recorded a total of 362 plant species belonging to 94 families (Supplementary Table S5). Nearly 89% of plant species were non-native (n = 323), of which 13% (n = 43) are classified as invasive. Only 31 were native and only 8 were endemic species. The mean plant richness was 55.7 ± 21.4 species per garden. Asteraceae, Lamiaceae and Poaceae were the families with the highest species richness (44, 19, and 17 species, respectively). Thirty-nine families were represented by only one species. The total number of cultivated species (265) was almost triple that of the number of spontaneously growing species (97).

Invertebrate richness

We registered a total of 127 morphospecies, belonging to a total of 16 invertebrate orders. Hymenoptera was the order with the most morphospecies (n = 32), followed by Araneae (n = 21), Hemiptera (n = 20), Diptera (n = 19), and Coleoptera (n = 14). On average, we recorded 23.8 morphospecies per garden, though the range between gardens was considerable (Table 2; see Supplementary Table S6 for richness by garden). The orders present in the most gardens were Diptera and Hemiptera, followed by Araneae, Isopoda, and Hymenoptera (with presence in 31, 31, 30, 29, and 29 gardens respectively). The orders with the least presence were Odonata, followed by Blattodea, and Zygentoma (with presence in 1, 2, and 2 gardens respectively) (Table 2). In the Hymenoptera order, Apis mellifera (honey bee) was registered in 20 gardens. We also observed 10 morphospecies of wild bees throughout the study and were able to identify five native bees to the species level (Supplementary Table S7). In the genus Bombus, only two non-native species of Bombus were found (Table 2).

School garden surroundings (buffer zone)

The land-cover classification analyzed within a one-kilometer buffer zone surrounding the garden showed that a very high percentage (78.3) had no, or very insignificant, vegetation (built-up and bare ground land-cover classes) and only 21.9% had vegetative cover (grass/herbs and tree cover). Nevertheless, these percentages varied greatly among the different gardens, revealing that the garden’s matrix can differ significantly.

Management practices

The average age of the main gardeners was 46 years, with an average gardening experience of 15 years (Table 1). Eleven gardens had only one person in charge, 19 gardens had two, and three gardens had three. The average number of hours the gardens were worked on by the gardeners, students, and teachers, in a normal school week, was 6.6 h.

Regarding irrigation, 15 gardens were not watered during the summer, another seven were only watered occasionally or only in certain areas, and the other 11 were watered during the summer break. A total of 18 gardens used compost as fertilizer and 15 gardens made their own compost. Twenty-one gardens used other kinds of natural fertilizers (vermicompost, dry leaves, manure, and others) and only one garden used chemical fertilizers (NPK). Thirty-two gardens practiced manual weed removal, only one garden did not remove weeds, and none used chemicals for this practice.

One third of the gardeners stated that the gardens did not suffer from any pests and over half of the gardens did not use any kind of pest control (20). Only one garden used chemicals to control pests — the same garden that used chemical fertilizers.

Garden area was strongly correlated with plant richness (ρ = 0.70). Meanwhile, cultivated ground cover had a strong positive influence over plant richness, as did watering during the summer. The strong relationship between cultivated ground cover and plant richness was to be expected, as most of the plant richness was composed of cultivated plants (61.9%) versus only 37.7% spontaneously growing plants. Finally, even though gardener’s age appeared in the final model, it was not possible to establish a significant relation with plant richness.

The garden surroundings variables showed very little influence in these models and only the bare ground land-cover class emerged, presenting a slight negative impact within the best-supported model (Table 3; to see all GLM results, refer to Supplementary Table S8).

Invertebrate richness

We found a high correlation between invertebrate richness and plant richness (ρ = 0.66), especially when considering only spontaneous plant richness (ρ = 0.72).

The best model predicting invertebrate richness included two variables within the garden (plant richness and impervious ground cover), one garden surroundings variable (tree land-cover), and one management variable (watering in summer; Table 3 below; to see all GLM results, refer to Supplementary Table S9).

It was not possible to establish a significant relation between tree land-cover and invertebrate richness. Meanwhile, watering during the summer was included in the best supported model, but it did not show a significant relation with invertebrate richness.

Discussion

This research provides an important contribution to the understanding of the factors influencing urban garden biodiversity in a highly populated Latin American city, showing the complexities of promoting biodiversity in these urban environments. Our results indicate a strong association between plant and invertebrate richness in urban educational gardens, with management practices within the gardens playing an influential role on both plant and invertebrate richness, and ecological phenomena influencing invertebrate richness as well. These influences relate to how complex and different the mechanisms that promote invertebrate morphospecies in urban environments can be. We were able to verify that educational gardens in the MAS provide, in their vast majority, beneficial, heterogenous, and chemical-free micro-habitats that promote plant and invertebrate biodiversity within the city.

The richness of plant species and invertebrate orders found in this study are in line with previous research stating that urban gardens, although small, can hold and favor a high biodiversity level, even if surrounded by highly built areas with few urban green spaces11,31,32. Although our sample size was relatively small, the results for plant and invertebrate richness were higher than has been reported in several other studies, such as in India (in Bangalore, 142 plant species and 10 invertebrate orders were identified after surveying 50 gardens), Portugal (in Coimbra, 252 plant species and 10 invertebrate orders were found), and the United Kingdom (in Sheffield, surveys in 61 domestic gardens identified 12 invertebrate orders)11,33,34. However, there have been studies with larger sample sizes that have reported higher richness, such as the analysis of 1045 gardens in Bujumbura (Burundi), which identified 567 plant species35.

In addition to cultivating edible and medicinal plants, gardeners grew many ornamental plants, likely influencing the relatively high plant richness we found. Indeed, many gardeners reported cultivating cacti and succulents only for ornamental purposes. Students, parents, and the school community in general were also often encouraged to bring plants from their homes. Consequently, a variety of actors could have contributed to creating a relatively highly rich plant ecosystem in several schools.

The percentage of non-native plants (89%) detected in school gardens in the MAS was similar, though higher than the results reported in many urban garden studies around the world, which have ranged from about 70% to as high as 88%35–39. Although research shows that gardens that emphasize native plants may have greater invertebrate abundance, non-native vegetation can also support a variety of invertebrates, particularly when there is greater canopy cover40. However, it should be considered that urban gardens are known to be potential sources of invasive plant species35 and our study indeed found that 13% of the non-native plant species identified in the gardens are classified as invasive. As a result, there is a need for scientists, educators, and authorities to focus on strengthening the knowledge and use of native species41. To do this, arrangements of plant species with ecological, educational, health and economic potential should be promoted for these small-scale social-ecological systems27,42.

The relation between plant and invertebrate richness observed in this study can be explained by the fact that a larger number of plant species produces higher heterogeneity, offering more resources and habitats for invertebrates15,32. Furthermore, research shows that increasing plant structural complexity and diversity alters microclimates (e.g., temperature, humidity) in such a way that improves heat mitigation in invertebrates, allowing for greater activity43,44. The opposite effect is caused by the impervious ground cover, which we found had a negative influence over invertebrate richness. Therefore, we agree with Smith et al.15 who suggest that if we had to choose one strategy to increment invertebrate richness in urban gardens, it would be to increment its plant diversity. Specifically, it would be important to increase the number of native and endemic plant species as a method of in situ conservation, to promote native invertebrates, adapt to climate change, and to educate school children about the local flora and fauna.

The variables with the largest influence over plant richness were all directly related to human actions that can be enhanced to increase biodiversity in urban gardens. This is in accordance with existing literature that states that gardens are highly managed systems whose biodiversity is vastly dependent on how they are built and cared for, meaning that the way in which these gardens are managed determines how sustainable they are and if they represent a contribution or a hazard to urban biodiversity13,15,19. The almost null use of chemicals overall in the gardens is thus very positive, given that such practices can be the most harmful for biodiversity11.

The strong correlation between school garden area and plant richness is consistent with existing literature results10,15,17,35,36, though it was not possible to establish a significant beta estimate in our models, possibly due to our small sample size. Furthermore, our finding that garden size is an important predictor of plant richness within gardens corroborates the results from previous studies that recommend keeping the largest area possible of a garden with multiple vegetation strata covering the ground, ideally with a large diversity of plant species to promote greater invertebrate richness11,17,32,45. These results also show that it is favorable for invertebrate richness to keep an area of the garden wilder, with spontaneously growing plants. This was apparent in our field observations, where we observed spontaneously growing plants being more resistant to drought and providing numerous floral resources which attracted a variety of flying insects46.

The positive association between plant richness and watering during the summer can be attributed to this management practice’s importance in preventing gardens from completely drying out during the MAS’ long and dry summers. This was exemplified by what many gardeners explained during the interviews — if they did not have a watering system in place during the summer break, all the plants died, meaning they had to start all over again upon their return to school. Given the association between plant and invertebrate richness, it was also to be expected that watering during the summer would have a positive effect on invertebrates by providing resources and habitat. This concurs with other studies that indicate that a garden’s management practices have an influence over invertebrate richness, which can be positive or negative depending on each practice11,15.

Although the main gardener’s age was also part of the final model for predicting plant richness, further studies about gardeners’ profiles are necessary, as we were unable to determine a significant relation. This is particularly important given that the existing literature demonstrates that the main gardener plays a fundamental role in favoring biodiversity in gardens17,32,47.

While it was somewhat surprising that variables surrounding the gardens had little influence over plant richness, the almost lack of influence of external factors in the garden reinforces the notion that the autonomy of these spaces and general environmental conditions may co-determine, but do not entirely define, garden species composition48. Our analysis of gardens’ surroundings found that there can be significant variation in the type of land cover surrounding these gardens. As a result, further studies regarding the influence of landscape configuration on urban garden biodiversity are needed, especially in areas subject to high rates of transformation and edification such as Metropolitan cities of South America [49]. Tree cover could be a particularly important variable to analyze in future studies given the social inequity that is associated with it, as municipalities with higher poverty rates tend to have much lower tree coverage50. Other studies state that green areas and garden surroundings variables influence different invertebrate taxa, such as poor mobility species like Coleoptera versus high mobility taxa like bees, in dissimilar ways15,51. Therefore, studies that analyze different taxa separately might be necessary to understand this variable´s full effect, such as proximity to natural areas within urban settings, which may influence more mobile species.

Final considerations

This research concludes that plant and invertebrate biodiversity in educational gardens is more related to social-ecological filters working inside the garden, such as the garden area, cultivated ground cover, impervious ground cover, and watering in summer, than to factors surrounding the garden’s buffer zone.

This study covers several social-ecological dimensions within and around school gardens, offering a first impression of their surroundings, their richness, and the way in which they are built and cared for. There are still many questions open for exploration and we hope that future studies seek to deepen the knowledge about the singularity of school gardens and their potential impact on biodiversity conservation in an increasingly urban world.

School gardens represent an opportunity and a powerful tool for schools which can, in a small space, exert a significant influence over urban biodiversity, even if their surroundings have a low percentage of green spaces and vegetation. Furthermore, our findings could influence or be integrated with practice and policy by working with different public institutions and proposing simple programs and initiatives based on sustainable management practices for public and private gardens and parks that promote biodiversity. This could include certification programs for private gardens52–55, training programs for municipal workers and parks maintenance businesses on biodiversity practices, and supporting the Ministry of the Environment’s municipality certification program56. Other initiatives could also target the creation and support of more school gardens across the country as a more wide-based initiative rather than only at a municipal level, as some municipalities do not have the interest or the funding needed to sustain it in the long run. Another option could be to strengthen school certification programs, also run by the Ministry of the Environment, adding additional financial and technical support for the school garden sector57. An effective option is for municipalities to allocate public employees to oversee school educational gardens, as seen in the Municipality of Recoleta58. As a result, with the help of city-wide initiatives to increment the number of school gardens and plant and invertebrate richness, educational gardens could maximize their role as trampolines for urban biodiversity and inspiration for future gardeners and biodiversity ambassadors.

Methods

Study area and educational garden characteristics

The study was conducted in Santiago, Chile, which harbors an estimated population of 6.6 million inhabitants4. The MAS (Metropolitan Area of Santiago, Chile) is located at 33°27’ S, 70°42’ W at the foothills of the Andes Mountain range, with an elevation range from 450 to 1000 m above sea level. It has a Mediterranean semi-arid climate, with rainy winters averaging 199 mm of annual precipitations59,60 and a long, dry summer season61,62.

We studied 33 school gardens, roughly equally distributed throughout the study area, located in 13 municipalities of the MAS (Fig. 2). Gardens were selected by mapping all public municipal schools in the MAS that had maintained a functioning garden for at least one year prior to our study in 2019. We included preschools, primary schools, secondary schools, and special education schools, but excluded nurseries and adult-only education centers. All eligible schools were contacted by phone and email, and the final sample of gardens corresponded to those that granted permission to participate in the study. More details about the schools and how they are distributed throughout the region can be found in Supplementary Figure S10 and Supplementary Table S11.

Two researchers surveyed plant and invertebrate biodiversity of each garden, twice: (i) during early spring 2019, between September 3rd and October 12th ; and (ii) during late spring 2019, between November 12th and December 13th. The first visit, conducted in early spring when most plants would have germinated, focused on vegetation and garden attributes. We also interviewed the main gardener to determine management practices implemented in the garden. The second visit, conducted in late spring when temperatures are higher and most insect species were expected to have emerged, focused on sampling invertebrate richness.

This study was approved by the Scientific Ethical Committee of Social Sciences, Arts, and Humanities at Pontificia Universidad Católica de Chile, within the framework of a larger study (protocol ID no. 170714019), and all methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Permission to conduct this study at each of the school gardens in the MAS was granted by school administrators. No permissions were required to capture invertebrates in the MAS as no invertebrates were killed in the study (see Invertebrate surveys section below for details).

Vegetation surveys

We recorded the vascular plant species in each garden, determining plant richness per site, following a guided walk throughout the entire garden with the main gardener to identify and classify each cultivated plant according to its principal function: nutritional, medicinal, or ornamental uses63. If a plant had multiple uses, we considered its most frequent use within the school garden. During a second walk, we identified previously unmentioned plant species growing spontaneously in the garden. The nomenclature, taxonomic family, and origin followed the “Catalog of vascular plants of Chile”64 or the online catalog ThePlantlist.org65. We classified plants by vertical strata: grass/herb stratum (< 0.3 m), shrub stratum 1 (0.3–1 m), shrub stratum 2 (1–2 m), and tree stratum (> 2 m)17,66. We then visually estimated from the center of the garden, the percentage of vegetation cover by vertical strata and by ground cover divided into three classes: cultivated ground cover, natural ground cover (with spontaneously growing plants), and impervious ground cover (bare ground, built-up, or compacted soil). These three classifications added up to 100%. Following Ibarra et al.17, the latter classification was used to build a structural complexity index, obtained from the sum of the coverage of each vegetative stratum (%) divided by 100 (Table 4).

Invertebrate surveys

We sampled invertebrates between 9:00 am and 4:00 pm on sunny days with no or slight wind (Beaufort scale ≤ 3), and temperatures ranging between 14 °C and 30 °C. Sampling was carried out by the same two observers. Invertebrates larger than 1 mm were visually identified using the “Non-Lethal Rapid Biodiversity Assessment” method (NL-RBA)67. This protocol allows to rapidly identify invertebrates directly in the field without having to collect them68,69. We took photographs of some individuals, which were sent to entomologists for identification66. Individuals were identified to morphospecies at the order level, with some individuals identified to species level. It should be noted that this invertebrate identification method made it possible to determine how the variables analyzed in this study influence biodiversity, but it also meant that patterns within taxonomic groups may have been missed.

The following protocol (i.e., the duration of the observations or the number of repetitions) was adapted in function of the garden’s area (Supplementary Table S12). We conducted the following steps successively in each garden (if the stratum was present): (i) Soil stratum: we actively searched for invertebrates in a dug-out soil sample. A hole was dug for each representative ground cover by completely inserting a hand trowel in the soil, resulting in an approximately 14 cm by 7 cm hole, and then searching the extracted substrate and resulting hole for invertebrates (for gardens < 50 m2, we dug three holes; four holes for gardens 50–100 m2; five holes for gardens 100–200 m2; six holes for gardens > 200 m2) ; (ii) Surface stratum: we actively searched for invertebrates on the soil surface and under rocks, branches, and other elements (for gardens < 50 m2, the search lasted 6 min; 8 min for gardens 50–100 m2; 10 min for gardens 100–200 m2; 12 min for gardens > 200 m2).; (iii) Grass and shrubbery stratum (plants < 1.5 m): we actively searched the whole garden for flying invertebrates or any invertebrates (for gardens < 50 m2, the search lasted 6 min; 8 min for gardens 50–100 m2; 10 min for gardens 100–200 m2; 12 min for gardens > 200 m2); (iv) Tree stratum (plants > 1.5 m): we actively searched the whole garden for flying invertebrates or invertebrates on trees or tall shrubbery (for gardens < 50 m2, the search lasted 6 min; 8 min for gardens 50–100 m2; 10 min for gardens 100–200 m2; 12 min for gardens > 200 m2); (v) Aerial stratum: we swept with an insect net over the grass and shrubbery, over natural or cultivated ground, or wherever there was potential of catching flying and/or small invertebrates (for gardens < 50 m2, we swept with the net once; twice for gardens 50–100 m2; three times for gardens 100–200 m2; four times for gardens > 200 m2. Additionally, each sweeping of the net consisted of 10 quick sweeps).

School garden surroundings

We used Sentinel 2 satellite images from November 2019 (< 30% cloud cover) to obtain the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) using QGIS 3.8 software70. We calculated the NDVI average within a one-kilometer buffer zone surrounding the garden’s center (Fig. 1). NDVI was used as an index to quantify the vegetation level around the garden. We also used land-cover classification data, which classifies the MAS into five categories: built-up, bare ground (i.e., compacted soil), grass/herbs, evergreen trees, and deciduous trees50. We also merged the evergreen trees and deciduous trees into a single tree class because our fieldwork took place during late spring, during which time all trees had leaves.

Management practices

To determine garden management practices, we conducted semi-structured interviews with the main gardeners17,71. We obtained verbal participant informed consent to participate in the study and to be audio recorded, prior to conducting the interviews. Participation in the interviews was thus informed, voluntary, and confidential71. Participant verbal consent was audio-recorded.

The interview included some personal questions, such as the gardener’s age and their experience gardening, as well as general questions about the garden. The main gardener was asked to identify the main objectives of the garden. The last part of the interview included questions about the management practices used in the garden, such as how many hours were dedicated to working in the garden each week, if they used compost or fertilizer, types of irrigation systems, and strategies used to control weeds and pests.

Data analysis

Data analysis was done in RStudio 1.2.1335 using the Stats package, version 3.6.372. We began by calculating Spearman’s correlation between plant and invertebrate richness, as well as between invertebrate richness and cultivated, native, and spontaneously-growing plant richness. The correlation between plant richness and garden area was also determined.

We ran a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test to determine if the numeric variables (Table 4) followed a normal distribution73 and calculated Spearman and Pearson correlations35,66 to test for multicollinearity (Spearman or Pearson r > 0.4) among variables. We eliminated variables that displayed multicollinearity and kept only those that showed the strongest correlation with the dependent variables or the ones that had the best ecological support in the literature (the most biologically influential variables or those that were most likely to be managed by gardeners, Table 4)17. We then used Generalized Linear Models (GLM) to explore which independent variables were the most influential on the two dependent variables — plant richness on the one hand, and invertebrate richness on the other. Subsequently, our models examined the effect of the following independent variables on the dependent variables: (i) within the garden: garden area, ground cover class, structural complexity index; (ii) surrounding the garden: NDVI, land-cover classification (only bare ground cover and tree cover classes); and (iii) management practices: school garden’s age, gardener’s age, time spent working in the school garden, watering, and compost (Table 4). Finally, a GLM selection process was carried out based on model weights (wi) and the precision of the estimated coefficients, using Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC). The models with the best support were those that obtained equal to or less than two units of ΔAICc (ΔAICc: the difference in the AICc value between each model and the model with the lowest AICc)17.

As the methods used for invertebrate sampling varied in function of garden area, we conducted Hill Numbers analysis74 to compare the diversity of the gardens. This analysis was done in RStudio 4.1.3 using the iNEXT package, version 3.0.075.
