Alginate and Chitosan‐Based Hydrogels for the Treatment of Radiation Dermatitis
Authors: Zintle Mbese, Omobola Oluranti Okoh, Blessing Atim Aderibigbe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRadiation dermatitis (RD) remains a prevalent and challenging adverse effect of radiotherapy in cancer patients, significantly impairing patient quality of life and potentially interrupting treatment regimens. In recent years, the development of biopolymer‐based hydrogels has emerged as a promising strategy for preventing and managing RD. Among these, alginate and chitosan‐based hydrogels have attracted considerable attention due to their excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, and wound‐healing properties. This comprehensive review highlights the therapeutic efficacy of alginate/chitosan composite hydrogels in RD management. The unique physicochemical characteristics of these hydrogels, including moisture retention, oxygen permeability, and controlled drug release capabilities, make them ideal candidates for treating radiation‐induced skin injuries. Mechanistically, these hydrogels exert anti‐inflammatory, antioxidant, and antimicrobial effects while promoting re‐epithelialization and collagen, which are critical in skin repair. Preclinical studies revealed significantly reduced RD severity scores and histopathological improvements following hydrogel application. Although clinical translation remains limited, initial trials showed promising outcomes in human subjects. Overall, alginate/chitosan hydrogels represent an effective system with immense potential to revolutionize RD management in oncological care.Keywords: alginate, chitosan, hydrogel, natural polymers, radiation dermatitis, wound healing

Introduction

Radiation dermatitis (RD), also known as radiodermatitis, is a typical side effect of radiotherapy, characterised by alterations in the skin caused by exposure to ionising radiation [1]. It is generally divided into acute and chronic types according to symptoms and presentation mode. Acute RD (ARD) is a process that occurs within 90 days of radiation and manifests as a range of skin reactions, from erythema to dry desquamation, to moist desquamation and, finally, to ulceration and necrosis in radiation necrosis (RN), in severe cases [2, 3]. The severity is frequently assigned a grade using established scales, such as the common terminology criteria for adverse events (CTCAE) of the National Cancer Institute and the Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (RTOG) criteria [4]. In clinical practice, ARD is associated with various symptoms, such as pain, itching, burning, and discomfort, which can severely affect a patient's quality of life by interfering with daily activities and self‐care activities [5]. Chronic radiation dermatitis typically develops from months to years after radiotherapy. It is characterised by skin thinning (atrophy), visible blood vessels (telangiectasia), fibrosis, changes in skin pigmentation, and an increased risk of developing secondary skin carcinomas [6]. The skin changes may not only be distressing for patients but can significantly affect their quality of life with discomfort, pain, and psychological distress [7]. RD occurs in up to 95% of patients treated with radiotherapy [8]. The occurrence and intensity of RD are influenced by multiple factors, such as the radiation dose, the specific area being treated, and individual patient characteristics [9]. For example, patients undergoing treatment for breast, head, neck, and pelvic cancers are particularly susceptible due to the proximity of the radiation field to the skin surface [10].

Management of RD primarily focuses on reducing symptoms and preventing complications. Conventional care involves treatment with topical corticosteroids to reduce inflammation and relieve symptoms, such as itch and discomfort, particularly in ARD, although their effectiveness remains debated across clinical studies. Some studies suggest a reduction in RD severity, whereas other trials report little effect compared to the placebo. Long‐term use can lead to side effects, including skin atrophy and enhanced infection risk [11, 12]. Emollients, especially (non‐perfumed) water‐based and non‐wool‐related creams containing shea butter or glycerin, can assist in keeping the skin hydrated and maintaining the skin barrier [13, 14]. For moist desquamation, modern dressing agents such as hydrogel, hydrocolloid, and foam help promote the healing process by creating a moist environment and relieving pain [15]. These moist wound dressings promote re‐epithelialization and pain relief [16]. Silver‐based dressings also have antimicrobial properties and reduce the spread of infection in damaged skin [17]. There are many types of intervention, but the effectiveness of standard treatments for RD is limited. They are of variable efficacy, and not all patients with atopic eczema respond to corticosteroids and emollients in the same way [12]. Long‐term use of corticosteroids can lead to skin atrophy, delayed wound healing, and infection, and certain dressings can induce irritation or allergies [18]. However, patient compliance is influenced by the frequency of application, pain, cost, discomfort, and restricted availability of suitable dressings [19].

Hydrogels are three‐dimensional (3D) hydrophilic polymer networks that can absorb large amounts of water and are similar in structure to human tissues' natural extracellular matrix (ECM). This feature also renders them ideal for wound dressings and treating RD [20, 21]. Hydrogel is essential for wound healing because it creates a moist environment vital for optimal healing processes, including cell migration, proliferation, and differentiation [22]. The moisture balance of hydrogels increases healing ability and has a cooling effect that immediately relieves burning and itching feelings frequently related to skin conditions [23]. Moreover, they can serve as antibiotic/anti‐inflammatory drug carriers, providing a controlled and sustained drug release to the wound [22]. Hydrogels increase therapeutic effectiveness and reduce systemic side effects, enhancing patient comfort and potential success. Among these, hydrogels composed of natural polymers like alginate and chitosan have gained significant attention. The anionic polysaccharide alginate, derived from brown seaweed, has good gelling properties and high absorbency of wound exudate and can be used for exuding wounds [24]. Chitosan, derived from the deacetylation of chitin extracted from the shells of crustaceans, possesses good antimicrobial activity, promotes haemostasis, and enhances tissue healing [25]. The alginate and chitosan in the hydrogel dressing are used in such a way that they act synergistically; the reason is that the dressing not only effectively manages wound exudate but also functions as an antimicrobial barrier and promotes tissue regeneration [26, 27, 28]. These composite hydrogels displayed preclinical potential for improving wound healing, particularly in the case of RD [29].

This review aims to comprehensively analyse alginate and chitosan‐based hydrogels' therapeutic efficacy and underlying mechanisms in preventing and treating RD. Based on the most recent preclinical and clinical evidence, the physicochemical characteristics, mechanisms of action, and therapeutic potential will be reviewed. Furthermore, the formulation approaches for the preparation of such biopolymer‐based hydrogels and associated challenges for translation of these hydrogels in RD clinical practice will also be addressed in this review.

Alginate and Chitosan: Properties and Biomedical Potential

Alginate Properties

Alginate is a natural anionic polysaccharide that is extracted from brown seaweed, such as Laminaria, Ascophyllum, and Macrocystis. It is a linear copolymer made of β‐D‐mannuronic acid (M) and α‐L‐guluronic acid (G) organised in homo‐polymeric (MM, GG) and hetero‐polymeric (MG) blocks. The ratio and order of these monomers play an important role in the physicochemical properties of alginate [30]. Its exceptional gel‐forming ability (gelation is induced by divalent cations such as Ca2+ which deposit on the alginic acid part of alginate by ionic crosslinking forming hydrogels through the “egg‐box” unit model) [31], biocompatibility, non‐toxicity, and well‐tolerated by tissues and hence suitable for drug delivery and wound healing applications [32], ability to retain moisture and porosity due to the hydrophilic nature of alginate, so alginate‐based hydrogels support a moist environment which is important for wound healing [33], hemostatic activity, which initiates clot formation by platelet activation and concentrating clotting factors, and a mild preparation process [34] are well documented in the works of literature. These properties make it an ideal biomaterial for wound dressings and drug delivery systems. Cao et al. proved that hydrogels based on alginate are effective in providing moist healing of wounds and autolytic debridement and promoting healing of wounds, including when these are heavily infected in patients with severe RD [35]. Moreover, alginate's exudate absorbent and conformability also contribute to its application in treating more complex radiation‐induced wounds [36].

Chitosan Properties

Chitosan is a cationic polysaccharide derived from the deacetylation of chitin, which is present in crustacean exoskeletons and fungal cell walls. It primarily comprises β‐(1 → 4)‐linked D‐glucosamine and N‐acetyl‐D‐glucosamine units [37]. Its solubility, viscosity, and bioactivity depend on the degree of deacetylation and molecular weight [38]. Owing to its amino groups, causing chitosan to be a positively charged molecule, it can associate (in various ways) with negatively charged molecules like alginate and form polyelectrolyte complexes [39]. Its main features are its antimicrobial activity that disrupts the microbial cell membrane, resulting in the inhibition of bacteria, fungi, and some viruses [40], biodegradability, which can be dismantled by lysozymes into non‐toxic oligosaccharides [41], muco‐adhesiveness, owing to its positive charge that can bind to negatively charged mucosal surfaces and cell membranes [42], and hemostatic and wound healing effects, which induce platelet adhesion and aggregation, stimulate fibroblast proliferation and accelerate tissue regeneration [43]. Chitosan hydrogels have been largely investigated for the non‐invasive administration of drugs to promote the healing of wounds and to limit the pain to the patients during the treatment [44]. Furthermore, their antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory activities make them suitable for alleviating the oxidative stress and inflammation linked to RD [45].

Synergistic Properties of Alginate‐Chitosan Composites

The combined use of alginate and chitosan has synergistic effects, overcoming certain drawbacks of each material and extending their biomedical applications. Alginate‐chitosan hydrogels have been found to have increased strength, imposed control over drug release, and improved wound healing effects in preclinical studies [46]. These composite materials have also been explored for their potential to deliver bioactive compounds and therapeutic agents directly to the radiation‐damaged skin, thereby accelerating the healing process and reducing the severity of RD [47]. The recent progress in the development of multifunctional alginate and chitosan‐based hydrogels has allowed new strategies in the therapy of RD. For example, including bioactive agents in hydrogels like hesperidin or zinc has indicated improvement in angiogenesis, antimicrobial, and tissue regeneration in radiation‐induced wounds [36]. Furthermore, these hydrogels have enhanced their therapeutic actions with other advanced wound healing treatments, including nano‐emulsions and egg‐yolk oil [44]. In addition, they also form polyelectrolyte complexes via ionic interactions between the carboxyl groups of alginates and the amino groups of chitosan. This interaction also improves hydrogels' mechanical strength and stability for sustained applications such as wound dressings and drug delivery systems [44]. The degradation rate of alginate chitosan composites can be modulated by adjusting the ratio of the two polymers. This property is especially useful in biomedicine, where controlled degradation is desired, such as tissue scaffolding or drug carriers [48]. The combination of hydrogels represents a novel approach for treating RD, offering enhanced biocompatibility, therapeutic potential, and flexibility. Incorporating these cell sources could be highly promising for future advanced hydrogel systems and may eventually contribute to improved treatment strategies and answer to the demands in RD management. Sadeghianmaryan et al. studied the combination of alginate and chitosan in 3D bio‐printing of scaffolds to mimic the extracellular matrix. The scaffolds enabled cells to grow and differentiate, and are suitable for tissue regeneration in RD [49]. Liu et al. demonstrated that alginate‐chitosan hydrogel can be accurately printed, and these complex structures allow skin dressing construction [50]. The immunomodulating properties of chitosan, including macrophage activation and cytokine release modulation, are improved when combined with alginate. This feature is especially beneficial for tissue engineering, in which a modulated immune response is important for tissue remodelling and regeneration [51]. Novel crosslinking techniques such as photo‐crosslinking and enzyme crosslinking have been used to improve alginate‐chitosan hydrogels' mechanical properties and functionalities. These methods can fine‐tune gelation kinetics and bioactive molecules that will form the basis of a new generation of biomaterials [52]. RD can be attributed to oxidative stress resulting from the overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Xu et al. showed strong scavenging activities against ROS when alginate and chitosan were co‐immobilised, which is important for alleviating oxidative damage in RD [47]. The aminic groups of chitosan react with the free radicals, which are then neutralised, while alginate represents a holding matrix that can help to improve the bioavailability of the antioxidant agents. Sable et al. have reported the anti‐oxidative stress properties of alginate‐chitosan hydrogels by loading natural antioxidants, curcumin [53]. For example, incorporating curcumin NPs in alginate‐chitosan hydrogels provided impressive ROS scavenging and radioprotective effects in vivo preclinical models [53]. In addition, the multi‐functionalized nanocomposites also promote targeted delivery of therapeutic agents so that systemic exposure can be reduced and therapeutic effect can be improved [46]. Introducing nanoparticles into alginate‐chitosan hydrogels has revolutionised new options for targeted forms of RD. For example, silver NPs incorporated into alginate‐chitosan scaffolds exhibit good antimicrobial activity, minimising the risk of infections in radiation wounds [46].

The fabrication of thermosensitive alginate‐chitosan hydrogels has improved the treatment of RD by achieving sustained drug release. These hydrogels are temperature‐responsive and locally release therapeutic drugs at the injury site. Su et al. showed that the chitosan hydrogel containing transforming growth factor‐β (TGF‐β) significantly increased the rate of wound healing by enhancing angiogenesis and epithelialization [54]. Moreover, the thermosensitive nature of such hydrogels ensures that the drug is released only when the hydrogel is applied to the skin, resulting in minimal systemic toxicity [54]. In addition, alginate and chitosan combined with vascular endothelial growth factors (VEGF) or platelet‐derived growth factors (PDGF) have been found to have a promising capacity for promoting RD repair. These growth factors stimulate angiogenesis, collagen synthesis, and epithelialization, which are required for wound healing and the suppression of scar formation in preclinical models [54]. Inflammation is one of the features that defines RD, and alginate‐chitosan composites have the potential to regulate inflammation. The microporous structure of alginate can serve as a biocompatible carrier for chitosan. Chitosan promotes cytokine release and activates the macrophages when combined with alginate; the proinflammatory properties of chitosan can be modulated for long‐term anti‐inflammatory effects [55]. Throughout the past years, alginate‐chitosan hydrogels loaded with anti‐inflammatory drugs (dexamethasone, curcumin) were explored in research. Preclinical studies demonstrated that these hydrogels could downregulate pro‐inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF‐α and IL‐6 [55]. The stability of alginate/chitosan hydrogels is an essential requirement for their use in treating RD. Novel crosslinking methods, such as ionic crosslinking with the addition of calcium ions or covalent crosslinking using genipin have been established to yield improved mechanical properties, including stability of the hydrogels [56]. Reza et al. reported the excellent structural integrity and drug release of genipin‐crosslinked chitosan‐alginate hydrogels during radiation to wounds. The cross‐linking also increased the hydrogels' enzymatic resistance, making them applicable for chronic wound treatment [57].

The above review reflects the characteristics and applicability of alginate and chitosan in biomedicine, especially their potential for treating radiation skin injury. Biomedical Applications of Alginate and Chitosan in Radiation Dermatitis

Alginate and chitosan hydrogels are commonly used in wound dressing as they have biocompatibility, moisture retention, and antimicrobial properties. Polyelectrolyte complexes are formed upon the combination of alginate and chitosan, and they result in the improvement of the mechanical properties and the promotion of cell proliferation. By incorporating antibacterial activity and self‐healing and injectability properties into hydrogel dressings, multifunctional hydrogel dressings are currently being increasingly developed, which aids the enhancement of wound healing outcomes [58]. Chitosan‐based hydrogels have demonstrated strong potential in skin wound dressings because of their high‐water content, oxygen permeability, and bioactivity [58]. Both alginate and chitosan can become pH‐sensitive, which is appropriate for a drug delivery system targeted to the gastrointestinal tract. For targeting the colon, pH‐sensitive chitosan‐alginate hydrogels were developed to respond to the pH changes in the GI tract for site‐specific drug delivery in the colon [59]. Furthermore, microcapsule technologies using alginate and chitosan have been designed for sustained drug release, resulting in improved drug stability and bioavailability [60]. Alginate‐chitosan nanoparticles have been investigated for controlled‐release antibacterial agents to enhance their efficacy and decrease complications. By enhancing the stability and target delivery of these agents, alginate‐chitosan nanoparticles are promising for treating infection [61]. Alginate and chitosan‐based hydrogels are being used successfully as scaffolds for tissue engineering, where they also form ideal matrices to encourage cell proliferation and tissue regeneration. In the area of bone and cartilage repair, the composite scaffolds of chitosan/hydroxyapatite/alginate showed promising effects in promoting cell adhesion, proliferation, and the synthesis of bone matrix and thus have been a suitable candidate for cartilage tissue engineering/repair [62]. Alginate‐chitosan hydrogels are of recent interest for cancer immunotherapy and diagnostics applications, as such systems are biocompatible, biodegradable, and enable targeted therapeutic delivery. In the context of breast cancer, these hydrogels have been promising for delivering immuno‐therapeutic agents and diagnostic tools, exemplifying their application flexibility and advanced biomedical significance [63]. Commercially Available Wound Dressings Based on Chitosan and Alginate for Treating Radiation Dermatitis

The management of RD increasingly benefits from the clinical use of advanced biopolymer‐based dressings that maintain an optimal moist wound environment, promote re‐epithelialization, and reduce infection risks. Among these, chitosan and alginate‐based dressings have attracted particular attention due to their excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, and wound‐healing properties. A notable example is the Sufu chitosan hydrogel dressing, which has been specifically tested in cervical cancer patients receiving radiotherapy. Clinical results indicated that Sufu effectively delayed the onset and reduced the severity of ARD while accelerating epithelial recovery and minimising patient discomfort [74]. Additionally, the Axiostat hemostatic dressing, although primarily designed for traumatic bleeding and surgical wounds, highlights the proven antimicrobial, bioadhesive, and hemostatic properties of chitosan, which are highly relevant for fragile, radiation‐damaged skin [75]. Alginate‐based dressings such as Kaltostat, Sorbsan, Algisite M, and Melgisorb are well established for managing moderate‐to‐heavy exudate wounds, burns, and donor sites. Their capacity to form a hydrophilic gel on contact with wound exudate helps maintain moisture balance, supports autolytic debridement, and ensures atraumatic removal, all critical factors for reducing patient discomfort and facilitating faster wound healing in RD [76, 77]. While a combined commercial alginate‐chitosan hydrogel dressing is not yet widely marketed, the complementary properties of these biopolymers support their combined or sequential use in clinical practice to optimise wound healing outcomes in patients undergoing radiotherapy [78]. Formulation Strategies for Alginate‐Chitosan Hydrogels

Hydrogels, especially those fabricated from natural polymers (such as alginate and chitosan), have been suggested for wound healing and skin repair applications because of their biocompatibility, biodegradability, and moist wound healing effect [83, 84]. Alginate, extracted from seaweed, is well known for easily forming a gel, which provides a relatively mild environment to encapsulate cells and thus is often used for drug delivery and tissue engineering [85]. Another important board in hydrogel design involves the addition of chitosan, an antimicrobial polysaccharide with wound healing capability based on its property of promoting angiogenesis, obtained from chitin [86]. The composition of alginate and chitosan in hydrogels provided synergistic benefits, such as improved mechanical stability, bioactivity, and drug‐controlled release [87]. These characteristics appear particularly useful in treating RD due to inflammation, infection, and dermal regeneration failure [87]. The recent progress in nanotechnology has also improved the function of the alginate‐chitosan hydrogel coupled with the nanoparticles, such as nano‐silica, to promote their physicochemical and biological properties [86]. New drug formulations, such as thermosensitive and composite hydrogels, have been designed to provide a sustained and localised drug release to lesional skin, improving patient comfort. For example, thermosensitive chitosan hydrogels have shown promising value in applications such as angiogenesis and wound healing in preclinical trials [54]. In addition, incorporating biologically active agents, including curcumin and plant polyphenols, into alginate‐chitosan hydrogels has demonstrated potential for regulating inflammation and promoting tissue healing [85]. These functional hydrogels can treat RD and serve as a carrier for local drug delivery, growth factor, and stem cell therapy [27]. Cross‐linking is essential for preparing alginate‐chitosan hydrogels, which determines the mechanical strength, drug‐release kinetics, and biocompatibility. The polyelectrolyte complex formed by alginate with chitosan increases the hydrogel's mechanical strength and structural integrity, supported by ionic crosslinking. This property is critical for treating RD, where the hydrogel must stick to the skin and maintain its structure under physiological conditions [88]. In this regard, recent development has been directed towards a dual crosslinking approach to enhance the mechanical properties of hydrogels in physiological conditions. A study by Saberian et al. showed the application of calcium ions and genipin as dual crosslinkers to produce a hydrogel with improved mechanical properties and long‐term stability in moist environments, which is crucial for treating RD [27]. In addition, microwave heating to form physically crosslinked hydrogels has appeared as an advanced concept. This approach allows for faster gelation times and homogeneous crosslinking throughout the hydrogel matrix. In a recent report prepared by Albarqi et al., the capability of microwave irradiation to enhance the mechanical properties of alginate‐chitosan hydrogels, making them more compatible for wound healing, was demonstrated [89]. A study conducted by Abdelbasset et al. showed that the alginate‐chitosan hydrogel could retain stability in simulated body fluids over 14 days, indicating that it was suitable as a sustained drug‐releasing application [90]. The prospect of biogenic silver nanoparticle‐encapsulated alginate‐chitosan hydrogel in treating RD with better wound healing and a low rate of infection [90]. Recent improvements have been centred on the hydrogel scaffold to improve drug release kinetics. For example, Gwak et al. prepared carboxymethyl chitosan/sodium alginate hydrogel with polydopamine coatings for an improved and extended release of therapeutic moieties over 10 days [91]. Similarly, Sheir et al. reported that alginate‐chitosan nanoparticles could effectively deliver bioactive compounds to diabetic and non‐diabetic wounds, indicating their potential for drug delivery [92]. Furthermore, the hydrogels may be modified to respond to external stimuli, such as pH or temperature, for on‐demand drug release. This feature is especially desirable when the inflamed or wounded tissue environment is generally characterised by adjusted pH [93]. pH‐sensitive alginate–chitosan hydrogels have been reported to release the encapsulated drugs more efficiently in an acidic environment, and they are used for radiation‐induced skin injury treatment [93]. Injectable hydrogels have been adopted for minimally invasive purposes. Saadinam et al. fabricated an injectable alginate‐chitosan hydrogel incorporated with graphene oxide to improve mechanical strength, promoting tissue regeneration in preclinical animal studies using radiation‐induced dermatitis [94]. Sánchez‐Cid et al. prepared an injectable self‐healing hydrogel based on N‐succinyl chitosan and oxidised pectin with better mechanical properties and self‐healing ability. These developments demonstrate the promise of uniting the chemical and ionic crosslinking strategy for enhancing the stability of alginate‐chitosan hydrogels [95].

The wound‐healing potential of alginate‐chitosan hydrogels has been ascribed to their effects of inducing angiogenesis, reducing inflammation, and improving collagen deposition. These characteristics are critical to conditions such as RD, where delayed wound healing and chronic inflammation are common. A study by Hung et al. assessed the PVP‐gelatin‐chitosan composite hydrogel containing egg‐yolk oil for RD treatment. The hydrogel remarkably facilitated the process of wound healing while increasing angiogenesis and reducing oxidative stress in the irradiated tissue [96]. Similarly, Gordienko et al. developed alginate‐chitosan sponges loaded with biogenic silver nanoparticles that showed potent antibacterial effects and promoted wound healing in preclinical models [97]. The moist wound environment benefits wound healing, which is considered one of the advantages of hydrogels. Moist wound healing is essential to minimise scarring and optimise tissue regeneration. Alginate‐chitosan hydrogels may allow exudate absorption while maintaining a hydrated environment and thus are suitable for moist wounds from radiation therapy. RD frequently results in secondary infections due to skin barrier disruption. The antimicrobial activity of the alginate‐chitosan hydrogels is attributed to the chitosan addition. Chitosan also breaks down bacterial cell membranes, which results in cell death, and alginate forms a protective layer against microbial infection [98]. In addition, the antimicrobial activity of these hydrogels has been improved with the support of additional substances. For instance, Hong et al. reported a multi‐functional chitosan hydrogel platform, such as antimicrobial activity improvement by incorporating peroxynitrite‐generating agents [99]. Similarly, Do et al. reported the potential of alginate‐chitosan hydrogels incorporating natural bioactive agents in infection and wound healing with powerful antioxidants and antimicrobial activities [100]. The antioxidative activity of alginate‐chitosan hydrogels also has an important role in suppressing oxidative stress, a characteristic of radiation‐exposure‐induced skin damage. These hydrogels can clear ROS and minimise tissue injury to contribute to the healing process. Antioxidants like polyphenols or vitamin C can also be added to the hydrogel matrix, which has been shown to augment such effects [101]. Recent studies in alginate‐chitosan hydrogel functionalization have seen advancements in drug/drug delivery systems, antioxidant incorporation, and nanoparticle combinations. The introduction of pH‐sensitive drug release, multi‐drug loading, and bioactive nanoparticles has increased the hydrogel's possibility for the drug delivery system to target inflamed tissue and to avoid secondary infection [90].

Mechanisms of Action in Radiation Dermatitis Management

The mechanisms underlying the efficacy of chitosan‐alginate hydrogels in RD management are multifaceted. These hydrogels serve as semi‐occlusive dressings and protect the wound from external contaminants while ensuring gas transfer. The high moisture content and ability to form a gel when in contact with the exuding wound support the moist environment protection and insulation of the wound site from external contamination. They also transport bioactive materials, such as antioxidants and anti‐inflammatory factors, to the wound area, reducing radiation‐induced oxidative stress and inflammation [54]. Also, their modulation of the wound microenvironment through the regulation of moisture content and the promotion of fibroblasts leads to facilitating tissue regeneration [102]. Chitosan‐alginate hydrogels can regulate the inflammatory response by decreasing the expression of proinflammatory cytokines and increasing the M2 macrophage polarisation (towards the healing phase) [103]. Su et al. have demonstrated that chitosan suppresses TNF‐α and IL‐6 release and inhibits the inflammatory cascade of RD [54]. Moreover, hydrogels promote angiogenesis and epithelialization, which are important for skin healing. Chitosan stimulates the proliferation of keratinocytes and fibroblasts, while alginate serves as a matrix for migrating cells and tissue structures. This combined effect leads to faster healing of wounds and the recovery of skin structures [54]. High or low doses of radiation can produce ROS, inducing oxidative stress and tissue damage. The addition of antioxidants to the hydrogel, for example, curcumin or vitamin E, scavenges ROS and reduces oxidative damage, promoting healing [104].

Infection becomes a common complication in RD, further delaying wound healing. The cationic characteristic of chitosan facilitates interaction between the positively charged chitosan and the negatively charged cell membrane of bacteria, resulting in increased cell membrane permeability and cell death. This effect is further promoted by alginate, which keeps the wound moist and prevents bacterial growth. This antimicrobial action is important in preventing secondary infection in radio‐damaged skin [105]. Besides the reported antibacterial activity, chitosan‐alginate hydrogels also possess antifungal activity, which would be very useful in avoiding any secondary infection in RD wounds. Chitosan disrupts fungal cell membranes by electrostatic interactions, while alginate facilitates the effect by keeping a moist environment that prevents fungal growth [65]. The recent report of Taokaew et al. proved the activity of chitosan‐alginate hydrogels in the reduction of the fungal colony counts and increasing of the inhibition zones against fungi, Candida albicans and Aspergillus niger, among others. This dual antimicrobial action gives hydrogel potential capabilities for the management of RD [106]. Hydrogels can be employed to release growth factors, such as VEGF or basic fibroblast growth factors (bFGF), and stimulate angiogenesis and tissue formation. VEGF‐loaded chitosan‐alginate hydrogels have been shown to significantly enhance wound healing in radiation‐induced skin damage [107]. Alginate‐chitosan hydrogels can be engineered to deliver therapeutic agents in a controlled manner, ensuring sustained release at the wound site. This localised administration increases treatment effectiveness and reduces adverse events at a systemic level [107]. These mechanisms collectively contribute to the efficacy of alginate‐chitosan hydrogels in managing RD, offering a multifaceted approach to treatment by addressing moisture balance, inflammation, oxidative stress, microbial invasion, tissue regeneration, and targeted drug delivery.

Preclinical and Clinical Evaluation of Alginate‐Chitosan Hydrogels

A chitosan‐alginate hydrogel enriched with albumin and Aloe vera demonstrates promising physicochemical and biological properties for managing radiation‐induced skin injury [108]. The enhanced swelling capacity and hydrophilicity of the hydrogel can address the excessive dryness and trans‐epidermal water loss commonly observed in RD, maintaining a moist wound environment conducive to re‐epithelialization [108]. Aloe vera's anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant effects, which inhibit the chronic inflammatory response and oxidative stress induced by radiation‐damaged skin, and chitosan's antimicrobial activity can beneficially prevent secondary infections, a common complication of RD lesions [108, 109]. L929 fibroblast biocompatibility testing results indicate that cytocompatibility is excellent, with > 95% cell viability after 24 h exposure (Aloe vera enhances fibroblast proliferation, indicating potential benefit for dermal regeneration) [109]. While these hydrogels have mainly been evaluated in non‐irradiated wound settings, their multifunctional activity makes them particularly appealing candidates for translation to RD therapy, specifically for supporting granulation tissue formation, collagen deposition, and epithelial barrier integrity, major processes compromised in radiation skin injury [109]. Enhanced mechanical properties, swelling behaviour, and moisture‐holding capacity of the hydrogel can meet the special demands in radiation‐damaged skin, such as the fragility of the skin barrier, exudate management, and microbial invasiveness [108, 109]. These attributes indicate a promising translational potential of the treatment for RD, which should be further evaluated in dedicated radiotherapy‐induced skin damage models. Toumaj et al. prepared 3D porous alginate‐chitosan hydrogels containing obestatin peptide at 1, 10, 100, and 150 μM concentrations, and tested them for wound healing. In vitro examination with MTT reported increased fibroblast proliferation with no cytotoxicity at concentrations of 100 μM and below, while in hemolysis assays, good hemolytic compatibility was observed at doses ≤ 100 μM but slightly increased at 150 μM, potentially indicating dose‐dependent toxicity at higher doses [110]. Toumaj et al. also yielded an obestatin‐loaded alginate‐chitosan hydrogel that exhibited rapid biodegradation, sustained peptide release, low hemocompatibility in a rat full‐thickness skin wound model as well as enhanced fibroblast proliferation, gene expression of VEGF, TGF‐β1, collagen I and III, and histological indicators of re‐epithelialization and ECM deposition; although, at higher peptide incorporated doses (150 μM), mild cytotoxicity occurred [110]. Its potential antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, and pro‐regenerative roles have promising implications in radiation‐induced skin injury models, and further investigations are required even in radiation‐specific preclinical settings [110]. Guo et al. constructed a multi‐functional glycopeptide‐based hydrogel (oCP@As) resembling ECM for radioprotection and radiation injury healing in a murine model. The hydrogel was formed via Schiff base reaction and formed an ECM‐like nanofibrous structure with oCP glycopeptide for structural mimicry and oxidised chondroitin sulfate (As) for immunomodulatory support [111].

Hydrogel significantly reduced visible radiation‐induced skin injury symptoms such as erythema and ulceration, enhanced epidermal regeneration, and promoted angiogenesis. Levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines were lower in treated tissues compared to the controls, suggesting a successful modulation of the inflammatory reaction [111]. Overall, the oCP@As hydrogel plays a protective and reparative role in the treatment of radiation‐induced skin injury through oxidative stress, DNA damage, and chronic inflammation, and its ECM‐mimicking effect contributes to the tissue regeneration and vascularization synergistically, indicating it as a potential entity for clinical translation in the therapy of RD. Cao et al. assessed the effectiveness of alginate‐chitosan hydrogel dressing in patients with Grade 4 ARD. Remarkable improvements in wound condition, such as decreased erythema and reduction of exudate levels, were observed after 2 weeks of treatment according to this study [35]. Similarly, preclinical studies demonstrated that such hydrogels might be loaded with bioactive substances like aloe vera and sucralfate, and such formulations improved the therapeutic efficacy [112]. Additionally, the capability of alginate‐chitosan hydrogels to resemble the extracellular matrix and promote cell growth makes them suitable for tissue regeneration in severe cases of RD.

Preclinical studies have provided robust evidence of the effectiveness of alginate‐chitosan hydrogel in treating RD. In a study conducted by Saberian et al., a rat model of Grade 3 RD was treated with hydrogels loaded with aloe vera extract. After 3 weeks, the wound area in the treatment group was reduced by approximately 70%, while that of the control group was reduced by 30% [27]. This study indicates that natural compounds can be incorporated into hydrogel formulations to potentiate their therapeutic effects. In another preclinical study by Zhang and Zhao, they employed the pig model and studied the effectiveness of alginate‐chitosan hydrogel in treating full‐thickness radiation burns. It was also reported that bacterial colonisation was reduced significantly, and wound closure was improved by 50% in comparison with traditional dressings [113]. Sauce‐Guevara et al. have additionally confirmed the effectiveness of alginate‐chitosan hydrogel on RD treatment by carrying out a randomised controlled trial in 100 patients with Grade 2–3 RD, comparing the hydrogel against standard care. Hydrogel‐treated patients showed a 45% more rapid decrease in erythema and a 30% change in pain scores within 2 weeks [114]. Preclinical and clinical evidence further validates the efficacy of alginate‐chitosan hydrogels in patients with RD. Studies demonstrate their ability to accelerate wound closure, reduce erythema, and improve tissue regeneration through mechanisms such as angiogenesis and modulation of inflammatory responses.

Comparative Analysis of Alginate‐Chitosan Hydrogels to Standard Treatments and Biomaterials in Radiation‐Induced Dermatitis

Alginate‐chitosan‐based hydrogels are an attractive type of multifunctional wound dressing and have advantages compared to conventional standards of care and other modern biomaterials for managing RD. Conventional therapies for RD, including corticosteroids and barrier creams, primarily focus on symptomatic relief without treating the underlying wound healing pathway. In contrast, alginate‐chitosan hydrogels create a moist wound‐healing environment and encourage autolytic debridement as well as the process of epithelialization. One randomised trial comparing hydrogel dressings with conventional treatments reported that hydrogels shortened healing time and increased patient comfort [35]. Unlike traditional dressings that may cause irritation or allergic reactions, alginate‐chitosan hydrogels are derived from natural biopolymers, ensuring excellent biocompatibility. Their non‐toxic and non‐immunogenic properties make them suitable for long‐term use in sensitive patients, including those undergoing radiation therapy [115]. Also, alginate/chitosan hydrogels have an intrinsic anti‐inflammatory and antimicrobial effect, which is another advantage compared to a standard dressing that needs continuous systemic antibiotics administration or risks corticosteroid‐induced dermal atrophy in the long‐term application [27]. In terms of cost and availability, although some of these advanced hydrogels may have higher initial costs than conventional dressings, their potential to decrease healing time and the complication rate can make a cost‐effective cost savings [114]. In contrast to other advanced biomaterials and hydrogel systems, including synthetic PEG‐based hydrogels and glycopeptide‐based ECM‐mimetic templates, alginate‐chitosan hydrogels exhibit similar structural biocompatibility, cell‐supportive architecture, and simplicity of modification. Nevertheless, glycopeptide systems can offer better ROS scavenging and DNA repair stimulation, acting directly on oxidative damage that represents an important path mechanism in RD [111]. Challenges and Future Perspectives of Alginate‐Chitosan Hydrogels for Radiation Dermatitis Management

Despite promising preclinical outcomes, many barriers remain to be overcome before the alginate‐chitosan hydrogels can effectively treat RD in a clinic. A fundamental constraint is the absence of extensive human clinical trials proving their effectiveness and safety. Most existing information is based on in vitro and animal experiments and may not entirely reflect what happens in the irradiated human skin's more complex in vivo setting. Variations of radiation dose, comorbidity, and skin microenvironment in oncologic patients are additional obstacles affecting the clinical transition. Furthermore, scavenging of ROS, a critical factor in radiation‐induced skin injury, is limited unless antioxidants are incorporated into the hydrogel matrix. Although Aloe vera or peptides have been incorporated into alginate‐chitosan systems for ROS suppression, the development of multi‐functional alginate‐chitosan systems possessing the in situ ability to neutralise ROS upon generation is still in its early stage compared to ROS‐scavenging ECM‐mimetic hydrogels.

The future development of alginate‐chitosan hydrogels for managing RD holds significant promise, driven by advancements in material science, biotechnology, and personalised medicine. While current preclinical studies demonstrate encouraging results regarding biocompatibility, antimicrobial activity, and wound healing acceleration, several strategic innovations could transform these hydrogels into clinically viable, next‐generation therapeutics. One key perspective involves the biofunctionalization of hydrogels with growth factors such as VEGF, TGF‐β1, or peptide sequences that enhance cell signalling for faster tissue repair and angiogenesis. Integration of antioxidant agents, such as polyphenols or plant extracts, could address the intrinsic limitation of ROS scavenging, a critical factor in mitigating radiation‐induced oxidative stress. Integrating innovative technologies such as stimuli‐responsive drug release, real‐time wound monitoring, and personalised therapy platforms could significantly enhance the clinical exploration of alginate‐chitosan hydrogels. Furthermore, 3D bioprinting approaches offer the potential to create patient‐specific hydrogel dressings with optimised porosity, mechanical properties, and drug‐loading profiles essential for addressing anatomical and functional variability in patients receiving radiotherapy.

Conclusion

Radiation dermatitis remains a significant clinical challenge, especially among cancer patients undergoing radiotherapy, where current standard therapies primarily offer symptomatic relief without actively promoting tissue regeneration. Alginate‐chitosan hydrogels have emerged as promising biomaterials due to their excellent biocompatibility, inherent antimicrobial properties, moisture retention capability, and the potential to stimulate angiogenesis, fibroblast proliferation, and collagen deposition. Recent preclinical evidence underscores their ability to accelerate wound closure, modulate inflammation, and enhance extracellular matrix remodelling in radiation‐like skin injury models. Functional modifications, such as incorporating plant‐derived antioxidants, further expand their therapeutic potential. Compared with conventional therapies and other advanced biomaterials, alginate‐chitosan hydrogels offer a balanced safety, efficacy, and affordability profile. However, critical challenges, including limited clinical translation, mechanical optimization, and ROS‐scavenging enhancement, must be addressed to facilitate their adoption into routine clinical practice. Future efforts should focus on large‐scale clinical trials, refining hydrogel formulations for human skin application, and integrating innovative technologies for controlled drug release and real‐time wound assessment. With sustained interdisciplinary research and regulatory pathway development, alginate‐chitosan hydrogels could redefine the standard of care for radiation dermatitis, offering therapeutic efficacy and improved patient quality of life.
