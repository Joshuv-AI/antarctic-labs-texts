Systemic MyoAAV-saRNA delivery activates endogenous utrophin and rescues dystrophic pathology in mdx mice
Authors: Zhikai Xiahou, Tianyuan Wang, Zilian Zhang, Na Li, Hong Xiao, Zhuo Li, Yanming Gan, Xinwei Hou, Yafeng Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTherapeutic activation of endogenous utrophin, a homolog of dystrophin, represents a mutation-agnostic therapeutic strategy with considerable potential for treating Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD). However, the large size of current dCas9-based activation systems hinders efficient adeno-associated virus (AAV) delivery, and the long-term feasibility and therapeutic durability of such treatments remain uncertain. To overcome these limitations, we developed a muscle-targeted utrophin activation platform based on small activating RNA (saRNA), termed MyoAAV-saRNA.MethodsScreening of a panel of saRNAs targeting the Utrn promoter identified Utrophin-257 as the most effective variant, capable of inducing more than a 3.7-fold increase in dystrophin mRNA expression in vitro. This candidate was subsequently packaged into AAV vectors for systemic administration.ResultsFollowing intravenous delivery of MyoAAV 2A-saRNA-257 in mdx mouse models, significant upregulation of utrophin mRNA was observed. This activation led to functional improvements, including partial restoration of the sarcolemma, histopathological amelioration (reduced centronucleation, inflammation, and fibrosis), and enhanced muscle strength.ConclusionCollectively, these preclinical findings establish MyoAAV-saRNA as a promising and translatable platform for utrophin induction, highlighting its therapeutic potential for DMD.

Introduction

Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is a severe X-linked recessive neuromuscular disorder, affecting approximately 1 in 3,500 to 5,000 male births worldwide [1]. The underlying pathogenic mechanism is attributed to mutations in the dystrophin-encoding gene, which result in the absence or deficiency of functional dystrophin protein, ultimately leading to a progressive imbalance between muscle fiber necrosis and regeneration [2]. Clinically, DMD is characterized by progressive motor dysfunction, frequently accompanied by cardiomyopathy and restrictive respiratory impairment, with patients typically succumbing to cardiorespiratory failure between the ages of 20 and 30 [3]. To date, no definitive cure has been established for this condition [4].

Although traditional therapies such as glucocorticoids can modestly delay disease progression, they are associated with significant adverse effects and do not offer a definitive cure [5,6]. Within the field of gene therapy, several strategies, including antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), premature termination codon therapy, and micro/mini-dystrophin gene replacement therapy, have progressively transitioned from basic research to clinical application [7]. Nevertheless, these approaches continue to face notable limitations, such as immunogenicity risks linked to adeno-associated virus (AAV) delivery, dose-limiting toxicities, and potential carcinogenic risks stemming from off-target effects [[8], [9], [10]].

Utrophin is a homolog of dystrophin, exhibiting a high degree of structural and functional similarity [11]. It is highly expressed in muscle tissues during fetal development and at mature neuromuscular junctions, gradually being replaced by dystrophin on the sarcolemma after birth [12]. Notably, in the muscle tissues of patients with DMD, utrophin is re-expressed at the sarcolemma, resulting in improved early-stage muscle function [[13], [14], [15]]. Given its structural resemblance and endogenous origin, utrophin is considered the most promising candidate for dystrophin replacement, with the added advantage of lacking immunogenic potential.

Small activating RNA (saRNA) is a 21-nucleotide double-stranded RNA molecule that belongs to a class of non-coding RNA molecules with regulatory functions [16]. Compared to other RNA-based activation approaches, saRNA operates through a more direct molecular binding mechanism, providing higher targeting specificity, stronger transcriptional activation, and a longer-lasting duration [17,18]. These features enable rapid and efficient upregulation of target genes. saRNA has been extensively studied in the field of tumor therapy. For instance, p21-saRNA has been applied in mouse models of bladder cancer, p53-saRNA demonstrates the ability to suppress tumor growth and migration, and the saRNA-based therapeutic agent MTL-CEBPA has been shown to enhance the anti-tumor efficacy of cancer treatments by targeting dysregulated myeloid cells [[19], [20], [21]].

In this study, we developed a highly efficient, targeted Utrn gene activator based on saRNA and achieved its single-component delivery using the AAV vector, termed MyoAAV-saRNA. Subsequently, we systematically evaluated its therapeutic efficacy. The results demonstrated that MyoAAV-saRNA effectively activated the expression of the endogenous full-length utrophin in the mdx mouse model. This upregulation significantly ameliorated the disease phenotype of mdx mice, indicating that MyoAAV-saRNA represents a promising therapeutic strategy for DMD.

Materials and methods

Ethics statements

The experimental procedures involving mice strictly adhered to the animal welfare and ethical guidelines established by the Laboratory Animal Center of Beijing Sport University. All animal experiments conducted in this study were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the School of Sport Science, Beijing Sport University (Approval No.: 2023028A), in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the National Institutes of Health (NIH).

Mice

Experimental animals were purchased from Jackson Laboratory, including two strains: C57BL/10ScSn (Stock No. 000476, referred to as C57 mice) and C57BL/10ScSn-Dmdmdx/J (Stock No. 001801, referred to as mdx mice). The animals were bred by Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd., and housed in the Laboratory Animal Center of Beijing Sport University. The housing environment strictly followed established laboratory animal welfare standards: ambient temperature was maintained at 22–25 °C, relative humidity was kept between 45 % and 55 %, and a 12-h light/dark cycle was implemented (light phase: 07:00–19:00). The animals had free access to food and water, and were housed at a density of 3–5 mice per cage. DMD is an X-linked recessive genetic disorder; therefore, only male mdx/Dmd mice and WT mice were selected for the experiment. All selected mice received the corresponding treatment intervention at one week of age. Serum creatine kinase (CK) level measurements, in vitro functional assessments, and Western blot analyses were performed at the time points specified in the manuscript. Each experimental group, including the WT group, treatment group, and control group, comprised five mice, serving as biological replicates for subsequent statistical analysis.

Cell culture and transfection

The C2C12 cell line was obtained from the National Certified Cell Culture Collection Center and cultured in DMEM medium containing 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin (PS) at 37 °C in a cell culture incubator with 5 % carbon dioxide. For transfection, cells seeded at a density of 1.2–1.5 × 105 per well in 6-well plates were transfected with saRNA using Lipofectamine™ 3000 (Invitrogen, #L3000150) in Opti-MEM medium. After 4–6 hours, the medium was replaced with complete DMEM.

Plasmid and viral vector construction

The saRNA sequence was designed to target the promoter region of the mouse Utrn gene (NCBI RefSeq), in accordance with established saRNA design principles for GC-rich regulatory elements [16]. The sequences (Table 1) were synthesized and cloned into the pAAV-U6 vector. The saRNA sequence of Utro-257 (showing the highest efficacy in vitro) was cloned into the MyoAAV 2A plasmid (Addgene, #224440) under the control of the U6 promoter (Fig. S1). The recombinant virus (MyoAAV 2A-saRNA-257) was packaged in HEK293T cells using a three-plasmid system consisting of the core plasmid, capsid plasmid and pHelper plasmid. The virus was purified by iodixanol gradient ultracentrifugation (48,000 rpm, 18 °C, 2 hours), dialyzed against PBS, and concentrated through an Amicon® Ultra filter. Silver staining

The silver staining experiment was performed in accordance with the instructions of the silver staining kit (Beyotime, #P0017S). After removal of the silver staining termination solution, ddH2O was added, and the samples were washed in a shaker for 2–5 minutes. Subsequently, the protein bands were stored at room temperature, and images were acquired. Silver staining confirmed the presence of VP1, VP2, and VP3 capsid subunits migrating at their expected molecular weights, with a subunit ratio consistent with 1:1:10 (Fig. S2).

AAV injection in Dmd mice

One-week-old male mdx mice and control mice (one-week-old male C57BL/6 mice) were intravenously injected via the tail vein with virus or an equal volume of PBS, respectively. At least five mice were used for each experimental condition.

qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from C2C12 cells or muscle tissues using Trizol (InvivoGen, #15596018). cDNA was synthesized with MML-V Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen, #28025013). qPCR utilized SYBR Green Fast Mix (Abclonal, #RK21203) on a CFX96 Real-Time Quantitative Fluorescence PCR System. GAPDH was used as an internal control for normalization, and the relative expression levels of the target genes were analyzed by the ΔΔCt method. Each sample was measured in triplicate. The primer sequences are listed below: mUtrophin: F-5′-GAGGAGGAGTTCGAGATCCAGGA-3′, R-5′-TTCTTCTGCAGCTCCATCAG-3′; mGAPDH: F-5′-CAGAACATCATCCCTGCATC-3′, R-5′-TACTTGGCAGGTTTCTCCAG-3′.

Western blotting

Weigh 20 mg of skeletal muscle tissue and add 200 μL of lysis buffer that has been pre-supplemented with protease inhibitors (Roche, #05892791001) and PMSF (Solarbio, #P0100). Subsequently, add zirconia grinding beads. After thorough homogenization, incubate the sample at 4 °C for 30 minutes to ensure complete lysis of the proteins. Centrifuge the sample at 4 °C and 12,000 rpm for 10 minutes, and then carefully aspirate the supernatant for subsequent use. Proteins from muscle lysates were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, and probed with the following antibodies: anti-utrophin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, #SC33699), anti-α-sarcoglycan (Abcam, #189254), anti-vinculin antibody (Sigma, #V9131), and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology, #7074S/#7076S). Signals were detected using ECL.

Histological analysis

Muscle tissues were embedded in OCT compound and sectioned into 8 μm cryosections using a Leica CM3050 S cryostat. The sections were stained with hematoxylin (Solarbio, #G1080) and eosin (Solarbio, #G1100). The percentage of centrally nucleated myofibers was quantified across at least three randomly selected fields per sample using ImageJ software. Morphological assessment of fibrosis and inflammatory cell infiltration was performed on the stained sections.

ELISA determination of mice serum CK

CK in mouse serum was measured using the CK Activity Assay Kit (Colorimetric) (Abcam, #ab155901). The measurement was carried out strictly following the manufacturer's instructions to ensure accurate quantification.

Behavioral analysis

The mouse grip strength meter (Jinan Yiyan Co., Ltd.) was used to measure the forelimb grasping force in mice. The tester placed the mouse's forelimbs flat on the horizontal sensor rod of the device and gently pulled the middle portion of the tail in a parallel direction until the mouse released its grip. This measurement was repeated three times, and the average was recorded as the absolute grip strength of the mouse's upper limbs (unit: g). The body weight of each mouse was measured, and the ratio of grip strength to body weight was calculated as the relative grip strength. To evaluate exercise capacity, mice were subjected to a treadmill running test. The treadmill was set at a 0 % incline. Mice ran at an initial speed of 10 m/min for 5 minutes, followed by an incremental phase with a speed increase of 1 m/min every minute until exhaustion. Exhaustion was defined as the point at which the mouse was unable to continue running for 10 consecutive seconds despite mechanical stimulation.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted using n = 5 biological replicates. One-way ANOVA was performed for comparison among the three groups, followed by Tukey's post hoc test for multiple pairwise comparisons. Statistical analyses were visualized using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0) software. Sample size and p-values are indicated in the figure legends and source data files. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To evaluate the regulatory effects of the synthetic saRNAs on Utrn gene expression, complexes of the eight saRNAs with nanoliposomes were transfected into C2C12 cells. Total RNA was extracted 24 hours post-transfection, and utrophin mRNA expression levels were analyzed using quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). Compared to the control group without saRNA treatment, significant upregulation of utrophin mRNA was observed for Utrophin-29 (1.8-fold, p < 0.05), Utrophin-257 (3.7-fold, p < 0.001), Utrophin-351 (1.7-fold, p < 0.05), Utrophin-536 (3.4-fold, p < 0.001) and Utrophin-605 (2.2-fold, p < 0.01). In contrast, Utrophin-825, Utrophin-916, and Utrophin-1040 did not exhibit similar effects; instead, a reduction in utrophin expression was observed (Fig. 1B). To further evaluated the duration of the saRNA effect, Utrophin-257, Utrophin-351 and Utrophin-536 were selected for transfection into C2C12 cells. Utrophin mRNA levels were assessed by qPCR at 24, 48, and 96 hours post-transfection. As shown in Fig. 1C, utrophin mRNA expression peaked at 48 hours and declined at 96 hours.

MyoAAV 2A-saRNA-257 effectively activates utrophin in mdx mice

To achieve specific and robust activation of endogenous utrophin in muscle tissues, we utilized the MyoAAV 2A vector, which is known for its high skeletal muscle specificity [22]. The efficacy of utrophin activation by MyoAAV-saRNA was systematically evaluated through tail vein injection of either PBS or MyoAAV 2A-saRNA-257 (1 × 1013 vg/kg) into mdx mice one week after birth (Fig. 2A). Muscle tissue samples were collected four weeks post-treatment for subsequent analysis. At the mRNA level, endogenous utrophin expression in saRNA-treated mdx mice was significantly upregulated compared to both WT and untreated mdx controls (p < 0.05, Fig. 2B). Although utrophin protein was detected in all groups, the levels in the mdx + saRNA group were the highest; however, they did not reach statistical significance compared to WT or mdx groups (Fig. 2C and D). Notably, compared with the mdx group, the expression of α-sarcoglycan, a component of the dystrophin-associated glycoprotein complex (DGC), was significantly upregulated by 1.5-fold in the mdx + saRNA group (p < 0.05). Activation of endogenous utrophin alleviates dystrophic phenotypes in mdx mice

We next evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of MyoAAV 2A-saRNA-257 in mitigating dystrophic phenotypes. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was used to assess inflammatory infiltration and central nucleation in the quadriceps femoris (QF) and triceps brachii (TB) muscles. As is shown in Fig. 3A, WT mice exhibited minimal central nucleation and no detectable inflammatory infiltration. In contrast, mdx mice displayed severe pathological features, including approximately 80 % centrally nucleated fibers and prominent inflammatory infiltration (Fig. 3B). Serum CK levels (Fig. 3C), a marker of muscle membrane damage, were significantly decreased in the saRNA-treated group compared to the mdx group (p < 0.05), although they remained elevated compared to the WT group (p < 0.05). Muscle strength and exercise tolerance were assessed through behavioral tests at four weeks post-injection. Mice treated with MyoAAV 2A-saRNA-257 exhibited significant improvements in grip strength (p < 0.05, Fig. 3D). Moreover, a trend toward increased motor ability was observed; however, this did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.05, Fig. 3E).

Taken together, these findings indicate that MyoAAV 2A-saRNA-257 partially restores sarcolemmal integrity, alleviates muscle damage and inflammation, and exerts protective and stabilizing effects on muscle tissue.

Discussion

Previous therapeutic strategies for DMD, such as glucocorticoid administration, exon skipping, and micro-dystrophin gene replacement, have encountered significant limitations, including systemic toxicity, mutation-specific applicability, and immunogenicity risks [9,22,23]. Although CRISPR-Cas9-based activation strategies hold promise for mutation-agnostic therapy, the large size of the CRISPR-Cas9 system exceeds the packaging capacity of AAV, and split versions often result in reduced efficacy [[24], [25], [26]].

To overcome the limitations of conventional therapies, we developed MyoAAV-saRNA-257, a novel therapeutic platform that utilizes a U6 promoter-driven saRNA to efficiently activate endogenous utrophin within a single AAV vector. Through systematic screening of saRNAs targeting the Utrn promoter, we identified Utrophin-257 as a highly potent activator, capable of inducing more than 3.7-fold mRNA upregulation in vitro. Although AAV doses of 1 × 1014 vg/kg are commonly used in murine models, clinical evidence indicates that such high dosages are associated with severe hepatorenal toxicity and life-threatening adverse events [27]. Compared to previously reported activation systems [[28], [29], [30], [31]], MyoAAV-saRNA-257 achieves effective systemic muscle delivery and utrophin activation at a reduced dose of 1 × 1013 vg/kg, thereby mitigating AAV-associated immunogenicity and dose-limiting toxicity. This integrated platform demonstrates substantial functional improvement in mdx mice, including sarcolemmal restoration, histopathological amelioration, and enhanced muscle strength, underscoring its strong translational potential for mutation-agnostic therapy in DMD.

In mdx mice treated with MyoAAV 2A-saRNA-257, significant upregulation of utrophin mRNA did not result in statistically significant increases in protein levels at four weeks post-treatment. This temporal discrepancy suggests that the duration may be insufficient for mRNA upregulation to fully translate into detectable and stable protein accumulation. Nevertheless, the concurrent increase in α-sarcoglycan protein levels confirms functional translation of utrophin and partial stabilization of the DGC, which accounts for the observed histopathological improvements, including reduced central nucleation, inflammation, and fibrosis. The significant improvement in forelimb grip strength, in contrast to the non-significant change in treadmill endurance, indicates differential therapeutic effects across muscle types and functional metrics. Grip strength improvements likely reflect sarcolemmal stabilization in proximal muscles, whereas endurance improvements may depend on sustained metabolic adaptations. The four-week treatment period post-injection in mice may be insufficient to fully reverse the chronic metabolic impairments, mitochondrial dysfunction, or cumulative muscle damage that affect sustained performance. Furthermore, greater levels or longer duration of utrophin expression may be required to achieve more comprehensive functional benefits.

Despite the high specificity and potency of saRNA therapy, its clinical translation faces substantial delivery challenges, including susceptibility to degradation by plasma and tissue RNases, rapid hepatic and renal clearance, immunogenicity, and endosomal entrapment that limits functional cytosolic release. Our study is constrained by the short-term evaluation period (4 weeks), lack of assessment of cardiorespiratory function, and incomplete immunogenicity profiling, despite MyoAAV's inherently low hepatotropism. Future research should prioritize longitudinal efficacy studies (≥6 months) with dose optimization, validation in large dystrophic animal models incorporating cardiorespiratory endpoints, and comprehensive immune response analyses of both saRNA and capsid components.

Overall, MyoAAV 2A-saRNA-257 effectively activates utrophin in mice. The upregulation of utrophin significantly alleviates dystrophic symptoms in mdx mice, suggesting that MyoAAV 2A-saRNA-257 is a promising therapeutic strategy for DMD.

Conclusion

In this study, we demonstrated that systemic delivery of MyoAAV-saRNA-257 activated endogenous utrophin expression, improved sarcolemmal integrity and muscle strength, reduced histopathological damage, and attenuated inflammation and fibrosis in mdx mice. These findings support MyoAAV-saRNA as a promising mutation-independent therapeutic strategy for DMD.
