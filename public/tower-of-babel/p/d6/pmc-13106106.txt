Interrater Reliability of the Ankylosing Spondylitis Performance Index in Patients With Axial Spondyloarthritis
Authors: Christopher Alexander Rasmussen, Sebastian Bülow Mink, Jette Primdahl, Kirsten Lykke Knak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionIn recent years, the Ankylosing Spondylitis Performance Index (ASPI) has emerged as a new objective measure of physical function in patients with axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA). So far, only the relative interrater reliability of the ASPI test has been reported, but the absolute interrater reliability remains to be investigated.ObjectiveThis study investigated the interrater absolute and relative reliability of the ASPI test in patients with axSpA from a [Nordic] country.MethodsPatients with axSpA were recruited from [a Danish] rheumatology outpatient clinic between October and November 2023. The participants performed the ASPI twice with two different raters, with a 15‐min pause between tests. Absolute and relative reliability were assessed.ResultsFifty‐two participants were included. The standard error measurement for the overall ASPI test was 3.4%, and the minimal detectable difference was 9.5%. The participants performed the ASPI test faster with the second rater (mean 38.15 s (standard deviation, SD 12.16 s) compared to the first rater (40.92 s (SD 12.07 s)) with a statistically significant reduction in time of −2.77 s (SD 4.53 s) (p < 0.001). The intraclass correlation coefficient of the ASPI test was 0.91 (95% CI 0.76–0.96).ConclusionThe study showed high absolute interrater reliability with low interrater measurement error for single individuals and groups in patients with axSpA. The relative reliability was also high. However, ASPI demonstrated a learning effect at group level. This study offers clinically relevant guidance for interpreting differences in the ASPI scores when tests are repeated by different raters, relevant for real‐world clinical practice.

Introduction

For axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA), the worldwide prevalence rates range between 130 and 1400 per 100,000 persons, and mainly prevalence rates between 100 and 200 per 100,000 persons are reported for ankylosing spondylitis (Bohn et al. 2018). The disease most often appears at 20–30 years of age (Sieper et al. 2006). AxSpA is characterised at an early stage with no radiographic proof of changes in the spine and sacroiliac joints (non‐radiographic axSpA) and a later stage with radiographic changes visible on x‐rays (radiographic axSpA or ankylosing spondylitis) (Sieper and Poddubnyy 2017). AxSpA is a chronic and progressive inflammatory disease that primarily affects the axial skeleton, which can lead to spinal stiffness and reduced spinal mobility (Sieper and Poddubnyy 2017). Patients with axSpA may experience pain (Michelsen et al. 2015), fatigue (Michelsen et al. 2015), sleep problems (Li et al. 2019), physical disability (Ariza‐Ariza et al. 2003), impaired working capacity (Rohekar and Pope 2010), restricted social life (Ozgül et al. 2006), and depression (Vestergaard et al. 2024), leading to socioeconomic consequences (Strömbeck et al. 2010).

Decreased spinal mobility makes everyday physical activities such as putting on socks, getting out of bed, and reaching for things from the floor difficult. The Assessment of SpondyloArthritis international Society—Outcomes Measures in Rheumatology (ASAS‐OMERACT) recommends Physical function as one of the mandatory instrument domains for all axSpA trials (Navarro‐Compán et al. 2023). The Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Functional Index (Calin et al. 1994) is considered the gold standard for health professionals' assessment of physical function in patients with axSpA/ankylosing spondylitis (Navarro‐Compán et al. 2023). The Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Functional Index is a patient‐reported outcome measure targeting the subjective perceived level of physical functioning in daily life. However, it is argued that the Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Functional Index is more associated with pain and exertion than mobility performance (van Weely et al. 2012). Therefore, the Ankylosing Spondylitis Performance Index (ASPI) was developed in the Netherlands in 2009 based on the Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Functional Index questionnaire (van Weely et al. 2009). The ASPI test is an objective battery of tests to measure spinal mobility performance in everyday activities and captures changes in the physical function of patients with axSpA in a more responsive manner than the Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Functional Index (Fongen et al. 2020). Studies indicate that the ASPI test is reliable when repeated tests are measured by the same clinician (intrarater reliability) (van Bentum et al. 2020; van Weely et al. 2009, 2015). Ideally, when patients are measured repeatedly over time, a patient should be tested by the same rater to minimise measurement errors caused by differences between raters. However, in clinical trials and clinical practice, different raters for a measurement may be necessary due to real‐world pragmatic circumstances. Therefore, reporting the degree of error between raters (interrater reliability) of the ASPI test in patients with axSpA is highly relevant. The literature has reported a single South American study on the relative interrater reliability with 62 patients with axSpA (van Bentum et al. 2020), but absolute interrater reliability remains to be investigated. Absolute reliability establishes measurement in seconds to perform the ASPI test, which is easily interpretable for clinical practice—in contrast to relative reliability (intraclass correlation coefficient), which provides only an index score. It would be useful to provide novel data on interrater absolute reliability and confirm the relative reliability of the ASPI test in a [Nordic] country.

Thus, this study aimed to investigate the absolute and relative interrater reliability of the ASPI test in patients with both radiographic and non‐radiographic axSpA in a [Nordic] country.

Materials and Methods

The study was a pragmatic prospective cross‐sectional study based on the COSMIN (Consensus‐based Standards for the selection of health status Measurement Instruments) checklist for reliability and measurement error of outcome measurement instruments (Mokkink et al. 2020).

Recruitment

We aimed to recruit ≥ 50 participants, which was considered adequate for measurement error analyses (Mokkink et al. 2019). Participants were recruited consecutively from October to November 2023 from the outpatient clinic at the Danish Hospital for Rheumatic Diseases. Inclusion criteria were adults (age ≥ 18 years) and a rheumatologist‐confirmed axSpA diagnosis based on the modified New York criteria for ankylosing spondylitis (van der Linden et al. 1984) and/or the Assessment of SpondyloArthritis international Society (ASAS) criteria for axSpA (radiographic and non‐radiographic axSpA) (Rudwaleit et al. 2009). Patients were excluded from enrolment if they could not understand instructions or could not physically perform the ASPI test (e.g., if a patient reported being unable to get up from the floor even with support from a chair).

The ASPI Test

The ASPI test consists of three standardised objective assessments of the functional performance of daily activities (van Weely et al. 2009). First, bending to pick up six pencils from the floor (one trial) (straight knees were preferred; however, bending the knees was permitted if necessary). Second, putting on socks (mean score from three trials) (if necessary, a chair was allowed for support). Third, standing up from a supine position on the floor (mean score from three trials) (if necessary, a chair was allowed for support). For each participant, measurement tape was placed on the floor to ensure that everything was in the same place. An overall ASPI score was calculated as the sum of the three subtests (National Advisory Unit on Rehabilitation in Rheumatology (NKKR) 2017). The ASPI test was conducted according to existing guidelines (van Weely et al. 2009). The standardised instructions were available online in Spanish (van Bentum et al. 2021). The Spanish instructions were pragmatically translated into Danish using Google Translate, followed by proofreading by two people who spoke fluent Danish and Spanish (one was a Spanish native speaker and the other a Danish native speaker). There was no forward‐backward translation of the instructions to the ASPI test, but a consensus was reached.

Data Collection

The two raters were instructed and trained simultaneously by an experienced physiotherapist (KLK) on how to use the ASPI guidelines and score pain and exertion. The two raters (CAR and SBM) were physiotherapist students in the final stage of their physiotherapy degree.

The rater verbally provided the translated standardised instructions to the participant with no added encouragement during the test. Similar to van Bentum et al. (2020), we measured perceived exertion with each subtest by asking the participants about exertion according to the Borg scale (Borg Category‐Ratio 10 Scale 0–10) (Borg 1982) and assessed pain by a numerical rating scale (NRS 0–10). The participants were asked about exertion and pain right after they had performed each trial. The mean exertion and pain were used across the three trials for subtests two and three. For all assessments, the participants were encouraged to perform the tasks as independently as possible, but support was provided if needed to ensure safety according to the guidelines (National Advisory Unit on Rehabilitation in Rheumatology (NKKR) 2017; van Bentum et al. 2021).

The ASPI tests were performed in an undisturbed physiotherapy consultation room at the hospital. Participants were randomly allocated to start with either rater CAR or SBM to avoid systematic bias. Each participant was instructed to perform the ASPI test twice with a 15‐min rest between the first and second raters. The second rater was blinded to the first rater's test results, and participants were blinded to all test results. The stopwatches' calibration was validated every day before participant testing.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive demographic data and ASPI data (performance for rater one and rater two, pain, and exertion) are reported as mean (standard deviation, SD) for normally distributed data, median and range for non‐normally distributed data, and numbers and percentages for dichotomous and categorical data. The distribution of data was checked with qq‐plots. We analysed the interrater reliability of the ASPI test for both the overall ASPI test and the three ASPI subtests. Systematic error on a group level was investigated by a two‐sided paired t‐test with the results considered significant if p ≤ 0.05. Absolute reliability describes the agreement between repeated measurements and expresses measurement error in absolute values (e.g., seconds) (Atkinson and Nevill 1998). Absolute reliability was visualised by Bland Altman plots with limits of agreement (LoA) calculated by the method described by Altman et al. (1983) (Supporting Information S1: Appendix 1).

Absolute reliability was assessed by calculating the smallest change beyond measurement error; Standard Error of Measurement (SEM) on a group level, and Minimal Detectable Difference (MDD) on an individual level with 95% confidence. These metrics were computed using equations from previous studies (Schwenk et al. 2012; Weir 2005) (Supporting Information S1: Appendix 1).

The minimal clinical important difference (MCID) is the smallest difference considered important for a patient (Jaeschke et al. 1989). MCID was measured both as an absolute number and as a percentage using equations outlined by Norman et al. (2003) (Supporting Information S1: Appendix 1).

Relative reliability is the percentage of reliability about the group and was analysed using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) with a two‐way random‐effects model based on a single measurement by each rater and employing the criterion of absolute agreement to account for both random and systematic error (Koo and Li 2016). The ICC levels used to interpret the results were: < 0.50 was interpreted as poor, 0.50–0.75 as moderate, 0.75–0.90 as good, and > 0.90 as excellent (Koo and Li 2016).

The above analyses were conducted according to the mean time (seconds) of the performances from subtests two and three according to the ASPI guideline (van Weely et al. 2009). Supplementary reliability analyses were performed on the fastest time in subtests two and three instead of using the mean time. Scores for the mean performance were compared to scores for the best performance for subtest two (putting on socks) and subtest three (standing up from the floor).

Ethical Considerations

According to [Danish] regulations, the study did not require formal approval from the Scientific Ethics Committee in [Denmark] ([S‐20232000‐127 reference number]). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before participation in the tests. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (World Medical Association (WMA) 2024). Data were managed according to the European General Data Protection Regulations and [Danish] law for data safety.

Results

Fifty‐four patients gave their written consent to participate. Two participants withdrew before participation due to illness, resulting in 52 participants for the study. Each enroled participant was able to perform all ASPI subtests. A chair for assistance was applied by 52% of the participants in subtest 2 and by 23% in subtest 3. The Overall ASPI Test

All participants completed the ASPI test twice with both raters. The absolute measurement error for the overall ASPI test between the two raters was 1.36 seconds (s) on a group level (SEM) and 3.77 s on an individual level (MDD), which was lower than the MCID of 6.04 s (Table 2). The MDD of 3.77 s suggests that a change of ≥ 3.77 s in repeated tests with different raters likely represents a true change in performance‐based physical function. The ASPI test was performed faster with the second rater (mean 38.15 s (SD 12.16 s)) compared to the first (40.92 s (12.07 s)) with a statistically significant difference of −2.77 s (4.53) s (p < 0.001) (Table 2). The Bland–Altman plot for the overall ASPI test showed no relationship of disagreement between the raters (measurement error) and the level of physical function by the participants (mean performance time) (Figure 1). The overall ASPI test demonstrated good to excellent relative interrater reliability (ICC 0.91, 95% Confidence Interval (CI) 0.76–0.96) (Table 2).

The Three ASPI Subtests

For absolute reliability, subtests one and two demonstrated SEM < 10% and MDD < 20%, and subtest three showed SEM and MDD ≤ 5% with the lowest value for the SEM (Table 2). The MDD was similar to the MCID for subtest one and lower than the MCID for subtests two and three (Table 2). For all subtests, the overall performance time for the second rater was faster than the performance time for the first rater (p < 0.001) (Table 2). The Bland Altman plots for the three subtests showed no trend or relationship of disagreement between the raters (measurement error) and the level of physical function of the participants (mean performance time) (Figures 2, 3, 4). The relative reliability of the ASPI subtests was good to excellent for subtest one (picking up pencils), moderate to excellent for subtest two (putting on socks), and excellent for subtest three (standing up from the floor) (Table 2). Discussion

Our study aimed to assess the absolute interrater reliability of the ASPI test, which is an important addition to existing research, and to confirm the relative interrater reliability in a [Nordic] population. The present ASPI test presents measurement errors in seconds, making the findings practical and clinically applicable. While a previous study (van Bentum et al. 2020) reported measurement error in seconds for tests conducted by the same rater, our study extends this by evaluating measurement error across different raters. This reflects more realistic clinical settings. The main findings of this interrater reliability study were that the ASPI test demonstrated a high level of absolute interrater reliability with low measurement error at the group and individual levels.

The absolute reliability for both the overall ASPI test and its subtests was high, with low measurement error on a group level (SEM < 10%) and on the individual level (MDD < 20%). The overall ASPI test and subtest three (standing up from the floor) demonstrated the lowest measurement error (SEM < 5% and MDD < 10%). The MDD for the overall ASPI test was lower than the minimal clinically important difference, confirming that clinically important changes are detectable and not concealed by measurement error. The interrater reliability findings (SEM ≤ 1.27 s and MDD ≤ 3.52 s) closely resemble the intrarater reliability results reported by van Bentum et al. (van Bentum et al. 2020) (SEM ≤ 1.40 s and MDD ≤ 2.56 s). Notably, subtest three consistently demonstrated the lowest measurement error, indicating its higher reliability compared to the other subtests.

A statistically significant learning effect was observed for the overall ASPI test and its subtests, with improved performance on subsequent testing. This is comparable to previous findings for ASPI subtests two and three (van Bentum et al. 2020). Familiarisation and practice trials may mitigate systematic group‐level errors although its effect on systematic error remains untested. Comparing mean with best scores revealed a tendency for better reliability with best scores for subtest two, albeit insufficient to warrant guideline changes. It is important to adhere to the existing guidelines for the comparison of repeated test findings and findings across studies.

For relative reliability, our finding of good to excellent interrater reliability of the overall ASPI test (ICC 95% CI 0.76–0.96) is in alignment with van Bentum et al.’s previously published work (0.88–0.96) (2020), even though caution is advised when comparing ICC values across studies, as these values are strongly influenced by the heterogeneity of the sample (Portney and Watkins 2009).

Despite a 15‐min break between the two ratings to eliminate day‐to‐day variance, similar to a previous study (van Bentum et al. 2020), this may have contributed to the learning effect observed, enhancing performance through perceived warming up and strategy optimisation. This finding aligns with the overall report of reduced exertion during subsequent performances assessed by the second rater in both the present and previous studies (van Bentum et al. 2020). However, an increase in pain was noted during the second subtest of standing up from the floor in this study, likely due to the challenging nature of the activity and its tendency to become more painful with repetition.

Compared to prior study (van Bentum et al. 2020), our sample was older and had a longer disease duration, factors that may influence test outcomes. In contrast to the Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Functional Index questionnaire with self‐reported physical function, the ASPI test excludes disabled and elderly patients with axSpA who are unable to stand up from the floor with support from a chair. This limits the usability of the ASPI test in this patient group. Building on prior reliability studies in axSpA (van Bentum et al. 2020; van Weely et al. 2009, 2015), this paper adds to the existing methodological literature. The study may inform future literature‐based research on axSpA by highlighting methodological aspects beyond what is currently represented in the literature (Bagcier et al. 2021).

Strengths and Limitations

The pragmatically non‐professional translation of the ASPI instruction can be considered a limitation of the present study. This may have resulted in some information loss, but the instructions were provided verbally to the participants, and there were no indications that they were not understood. A strength of this study was that the two raters were instructed on how to perform the ASPI test at the same time by the same experienced physiotherapist. With only a few exclusion criteria, the study has high external validity. It is generalisable to the background population, as the study population resembles the overall population with axSpA, apart from the inherent limitations in patients who cannot stand up from the floor. Often, raters from clinical or research studies are experienced physiotherapists. In the present study, the raters were physiotherapist students at the end of their physiotherapy degree, making the findings even more generalisable as our study shows that it is possible to involve less experienced physiotherapists or potentially other healthcare professionals to perform the ASPI test if good training is given. Another strength was the consistency of the testing environment, as all tests were conducted in the same rooms throughout the study. The present study is the first study to present data on measurement error in seconds across different raters, which is easily interpretable and applicable in clinical practice ‐ in contrast to relative reliability (ICC).

In conclusion, the study demonstrated a high level of absolute and relative interrater reliability for groups and individuals for the overall ASPI test in patients with radiographic axSpA (ankylosing spondylitis) and non‐radiographic axSpA. However, the overall ASPI test was associated with a learning effect on a group level, and, therefore, a practice trial of the ASPI test is recommended to ensure familiarity with the test, minimising learning effects during the main assessment. The findings on measurement error in seconds offer clear guidance for clinicians on interpreting difference scores of the ASPI test across repeated tests by different raters, common in clinical practice.
