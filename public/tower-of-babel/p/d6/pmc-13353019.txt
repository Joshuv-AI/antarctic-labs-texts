The association between social support and pregnancy stress among Turkish citizens and immigrants of diverse legal statuses: a comparative study
Authors: Büşra Erensayın, Gülnihal Artuğ Cansızlar, Çağla Çılğın, Ümran Oskay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis cross-sectional study investigated the association between social support and stress among Turkish citizens and immigrant pregnant women from Syria, Iraq, and Afghanistan.MethodsThe study was conducted with 600 pregnant women at a tertiary training and research hospital in Istanbul, categorized into three cohorts: Turkish citizens (n = 200), short-term immigrants (n = 200), and long-term immigrants (n = 200). Participants were evaluated for perceived social support and prenatal stress levels using validated scales.ResultsAlthough sociodemographic characteristics were largely comparable across groups, short-term immigrants exhibited significantly lower educational attainment, higher unemployment rates, and greater income–expenditure imbalance. Turkish participants reported significantly higher levels of perceived social support, particularly from familial networks. Conversely, both immigrant cohorts demonstrated elevated pregnancy stress scores relative to the Turkish group. A statistically significant negative association between social support and pregnancy stress was identified across all cohorts. Notably, long-term immigrant women appeared to derive the most substantial benefits from social support in mitigating prenatal stress.ConclusionsPerceived social support was highest among Turkish pregnant women, followed by long-term and short-term immigrants, respectively. Pregnancy-related stress was significantly more pronounced in both immigrant groups compared to the native population. These findings underscore the critical role of social support systems in alleviating prenatal distress among vulnerable immigrant populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27791-6.Keywords: Immigrant, Migration, Pregnancy, Refugee, Social support, Stress

Introduction

Pregnancy is a physiological process characterized by extensive biological changes. While inherently natural, these physiological transitions may occasionally lead to undesirable outcomes [1]. The main causes of pregnancy-related stress include hormonal changes, social factors, and anxieties experienced during the preparation process for pregnancy. High levels of such stress are associated with adverse outcomes, including preterm birth and low birth weight. Research indicates that pregnant women experience significantly higher stress levels compared to their non-pregnant counterparts [2, 3].

Conversely, migration represents a critical determinant of an individual’s health and social status, potentially predisposing pregnant immigrants to higher levels of stress [4]. In recent years, immigration has emerged as a significant global phenomenon with profound implications for countries such as Türkiye. By 2021, the global population of forcibly displaced individuals, reaching 89.3 million, was primarily driven by conflict, violence, persecution, and human rights violations. Within this demographic, women accounted for approximately 29% of the total [5]. Türkiye hosts over 4 million individuals under various protection statuses, primarily from Syria, Iraq, and Afghanistan. Throughout this study, the term ‘immigrant’ is employed as a standardized academic descriptor to encompass these various nationalities and legal statuses within the sample, ensuring conceptual consistency while acknowledging their shared experience of international relocation [5, 6].

The migration process frequently exposes pregnant women to physical and psychological trauma, leading to significant challenges related to education, housing, and economic stability. Such circumstances may adversely impact both maternal and neonatal health [7]. Furthermore, maternal and fetal well-being are influenced by a multitude of determinants, including prior experiences, migration conditions, cultural beliefs, educational attainment, socioeconomic status in the host country, social networks, and access to healthcare services [8]. Existing literature concerning immigrant women indicates that this population faces an elevated risk of obstetric complications, potential exposure to abuse or violence, and various psychological disorders [9]. Throughout these processes, language barriers frequently impede immigrant women from adequately utilizing health promotion services and accessing reproductive healthcare [10]. During periods when this population was most adversely affected by the migration process, Türkiye implemented various initiatives to facilitate comprehensive healthcare access. These interventions included establishing specialized migrant health centers, providing interpretation services within existing facilities, creating dedicated call centers, and employing foreign healthcare professionals. Additionally, maternal and child health screenings and follow-up services are integrated into these migrant health centers [11]. Nevertheless, despite the availability of such services, the migration experience is associated with significant stress and trauma for pregnant women. These stressors stem from multifaceted factors, including language barriers, the erosion of established social support networks, cultural adaptation challenges, and perceived discrimination. Furthermore, navigating an unfamiliar healthcare system and enduring economic instability may exacerbate psychological distress during the prenatal period [8].

Social support encompasses practical and emotional assistance and resources that individuals receive through social interactions in their daily lives [12]. This support can be provided by family, friends, colleagues, community organizations, or other social relationships and generally includes emotional, informational, and instrumental support [13]. Previous research has consistently shown that social support is a protective factor for mental health and reduces stress [14]. Additionally, it has been noted that receiving high levels of social support is an independent protective factor against mental health issues such as depression, anxiety, and burnout [15]. Nevertheless, despite established research linking social support to mental health outcomes, empirical evidence examining the association between perceived social support and pregnancy-related stress, specifically in comparisons between immigrant and native pregnant women, remains limited. Consequently, this study aims to evaluate the relationship between social support and stress levels among both Turkish and immigrant pregnant populations.

In the migration literature, five years is widely recognized as a significant timeframe for immigrants to achieve social integration, language proficiency, and cultural adaptation. Furthermore, this period aligns with the Turkish Civil Code, which establishes five years of continuous residence as a primary prerequisite for citizenship applications [16]. Consequently, for this study, immigrant participants were categorized into two distinct groups based on their length of residency: those residing in the country for less than five years and those with a residency of five years or more [16]. Within five years, immigrants generally assume rights and responsibilities such as securing employment, utilizing social services, and paying taxes, thereby becoming active members of society [16]. For this reason, in our study, we categorized pregnant immigrants into long-term (five years or more) and short-term (less than five years). Unlike studies focusing on a single refugee group, this research encompasses an inclusive immigrant population with diverse and undisclosed legal statuses, reflecting the complex socio-political reality of metropolitan migration hubs like Istanbul.

Materials and methods

Design

In this study, a comparative cross-sectional research design was adopted to examine the association between social support and stress levels among Turkish and immigrant pregnant women.

Study sample

The study population consisted of pregnant women who presented to the obstetrics outpatient clinic of a training and research hospital in Istanbul between February and September 2024 and provided informed consent to participate in the study. The study used convenience sampling; all pregnant women who presented to the obstetrics clinic during the study period and met the inclusion criteria were included in the sample. A power analysis was conducted to determine the required sample size for the study. Statistical power was calculated using G*Power 3.1. Based on a similar study by Vaillancourt M. et al. [17], which reported an effect size of 1.012 for the Prenatal Distress Questionnaire-Revised, it was determined that to achieve a power exceeding 95% at a 5% significance level, a total of 588 participants across three groups (196 per group) was necessary (df = 12; t = 1.7822). To ensure high statistical power and account for potential data loss, the study aimed to include 200 Turkish and 400 immigrant pregnant women.

The immigrant group was further categorized into two subgroups based on whether they had resided continuously in Türkiye for less than five years or for five years or more. Participants were categorized into two groups based on their duration of residence in Türkiye: less than five years and five years or more. This threshold was determined in accordance with the Turkish Civil Code’s residency requirements for citizenship applications, which is also recognized in migration literature as a relevant period for assessing long-term cultural and social adaptation. To uphold ethical standards and ensure participants’ safety, specific legal residency statuses (e.g., Temporary Protection, International Protection, or irregular status) were intentionally not recorded. This approach allowed for a more representative and trust-based data collection process among vulnerable pregnant women who might otherwise fear legal repercussions [18].

A total of 200 Turkish, 200 short-term (less than five years), and 200 long-term (five years or more) immigrant pregnant women were included in the study. The immigrant participants had migrated from Syria, Iraq, and Afghanistan. Inclusion criteria consisted of pregnant women aged 18 years and older, who were beyond the 20th week of gestation, had no prior psychiatric diagnosis, and volunteered to participate in the study. Conversely, pregnant women who could not speak or understand Turkish were excluded from the sample. Participants who were unable to communicate in Turkish were excluded to ensure data accuracy and to prevent potential misinterpretations during the administration of the perceived social support and stress scales. This criterion was established to maintain response reliability, as professional translation services were unavailable during data collection.

Data collection

The researchers collected the data through face-to-face interviews with pregnant women in a private, quiet room at the obstetrics clinic. The data collection process for each participant lasted an average of 20 min.

Measures

During the interviews, a 19-item Participant Introduction Form developed by the researchers based on the relevant literature, the 36-item Pregnancy Stress Rating Scale, and the 12-item Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support were administered.

Participant introduction form

The form developed by the researchers, based on the literature, consists of 19 questions. It includes items related to sociodemographic, obstetric, and social support characteristics [12, 13].

Pregnancy Stress Assessment Scale (PSAS)

Developed by Chung-Hey Chen in 1983, the Turkish validity and reliability study was conducted by Aksoy et al. in 2019. The scale consists of five subdimensions and has a Likert-type structure with 36 items. All items are positively worded, and the scoring is based on the following scale: definitely no (0), mild (1), moderate (2), severe (3), and very severe (4). The total score is obtained by summing all item scores, ranging from 0 to 144. Higher scores indicate greater perceived prenatal stress. In the original study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was reported as 0.94 [19]. In this study, Cronbach’s alphas were 0.94 for total pregnancy stress, 0.90 for Safe Health Care, 0.88 for Social Support, 0.93 for Baby Health, 0.87 for Baby Identity and Care, 0.85 for Body Image, 0.91 for Socio-Economic Life, and 0.87 for Psychological Condition.

Multidimensional Perceived Social Support Scale (MPSSS)

The scale developed by Zimet et al. (1988) measures perceived social support from three sources: family, friends, and a significant other. The MSPSS consists of 12 items grouped into three subdimensions, each containing four items. These include family (items 3, 4, 8, and 11), friends (items 6, 7, 9, and 12), and a significant other (items 1, 2, 5, and 10). Each item is rated on a seven-point Likert-type scale. Subscale scores range from 4 to 28, and the total score ranges from 12 to 84. Higher scores indicate higher levels of perceived social support [20]. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha value was 0.95 for the total scale, 0.92 for the family subscale, 0.91 for the significant other subscale, and 0.89 for the friends subscale.

Data analysis

The data obtained in the study were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 22.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). In determining the descriptive characteristics of the pregnant women participating in the study, frequency and percentage analyses were used, and means and standard deviations were calculated for scale variables. To determine whether the research variables followed a normal distribution, kurtosis and skewness values were examined. In the relevant literature, kurtosis and skewness values falling between + 1.5 and − 1.5 [21], and between + 2.0 and − 2.0 [22], are considered indicative of a normal distribution. The variables were found to be normally distributed. Therefore, parametric methods were used in the analysis. Differences in the proportions of categorical variables between independent groups were analyzed using chi-square tests. The relationships among the dimensions that determine the scale levels of pregnant women were examined using Pearson correlation and linear regression analyses.

In short-term immigrant pregnant women, total perceived social support was also a strong negative predictor of pregnancy stress (B = −0.558, β = −0.662, p = 0.000). Perceived social support from family showed a significant association (B = −0.830, β = −0.372, p = 0.050), whereas support from a significant other and friends did not significantly predict stress levels in this group.

In long-term immigrant pregnant women, total perceived social support showed the strongest negative association with pregnancy stress (B = −0.715, β = −0.708, p = 0.000). Perceived social support from family (B = −0.803, β = −0.310, p = 0.014) and from a significant other (B = −0.781, β = −0.249, p = 0.028) were also significant predictors of reduced pregnancy stress. However, perceived support from friends was not significantly associated with stress in this group (p = 0.269).

Discussion

In this study, the association between social support and stress in Turkish and immigrant pregnant women was examined. While the inverse relationship between social support and stress is well-documented, this study provides unique insights by comparing these dynamics across three distinct groups: Turkish citizens, short-term immigrants, and long-term immigrants. Most existing literature focuses on single refugee populations (e.g., only Syrians); however, our findings demonstrate how the duration of residency and diverse legal statuses within a metropolitan context (Istanbul) differentially influence these psychological markers. This nuanced comparison offers a specific clinical roadmap for healthcare providers in transit and host countries, highlighting that long-term residency does not necessarily eliminate the heightened need for specialized social support interventions.

In this study, although no significant differences were observed between groups for most sociodemographic variables, short-term immigrant pregnant women had lower educational levels, lower employment rates, and greater income–expenditure imbalance than the other groups. Studies conducted with immigrant pregnant women living in Türkiye have reported that a large proportion of these women do not work, have low levels of education, and limited income [2, 23–26]. Similarly, research conducted in Germany found that Turkish-origin pregnant women had lower education levels compared to native pregnant women [27]. Studies from the United States and Taiwan have also reported low educational attainment and high unemployment rates among immigrant pregnant women [28, 29]. These findings are consistent with the present study. As stated in the World Health Organization guidelines (2018), immigrant pregnant women generally have lower education levels than the local population, placing them at a disadvantage in multiple domains [30]. This lower educational attainment often translates into reduced health literacy, limited access to stable employment, and increased difficulty navigating legal and healthcare systems, thereby adversely affecting psychosocial well-being and prenatal care [27].

The study also found that long-term immigrant pregnant women had a higher number of pregnancies and living children compared to Turkish and short-term immigrant women. Findings from previous studies are inconsistent. For example, research conducted in New York found no differences in parity or gravida between immigrant and native women [31], whereas studies from Canada reported lower parity among immigrant women [32]. Studies conducted in Türkiye have reported mixed results, with some indicating similar outcomes and others reporting higher parity among immigrant women [26, 33, 34]. These discrepancies may be attributed to differences in sample characteristics, cultural norms, and national family policies [32]. Such findings highlight the need for targeted interventions to ensure equitable access to maternal healthcare. Addressing structural barriers, including geographical access, childcare responsibilities, spousal support, language assistance, and prenatal education, is essential for improving maternal outcomes in these populations [34].

In this study, perceived social support levels were highest among Turkish pregnant women, followed by long-term and short-term immigrant women. Family-based social support was higher among Turkish and long-term immigrant women compared to short-term immigrants. Similarly, perceived support from friends was higher among Turkish women and, to a lesser extent, among long-term immigrants. These findings are consistent with previous studies indicating that immigrant women experience higher levels of stress and anxiety due to social isolation, limited support networks, and changes in living conditions [35–37]. Research conducted in Thailand and the United States has also shown that immigrant pregnant women, particularly recent immigrants, report significantly lower levels of social support [28, 38].

Pregnancy is a period during which stress is frequently heightened [39]. Among immigrant women, pregnancy-related stress is shaped by a complex interplay of economic conditions, obstetric factors, migration-related challenges, healthcare access, and psychosocial stressors [40]. In the present study, pregnancy stress levels were highest among short-term immigrant women, followed by long-term immigrants, and lowest among Turkish women. These findings are consistent with previous studies indicating that recent immigrants experience significantly higher stress levels than both long-term immigrants and native populations [39, 41, 42].

Physiological changes and evolving social roles during pregnancy increase the need for social support [43]. Immigration further compounds this need by introducing additional stressors such as economic hardship, language barriers, and social isolation [44, 45]. In this study, a significant negative association between perceived social support and pregnancy stress was identified across all groups. The strength of this relationship was particularly pronounced among long-term immigrant women, suggesting that the development of stable social networks over time may enhance the protective effects of social support. A similarly strong association was observed among Turkish women.

Previous studies support these findings. Research conducted in the United States and Korea has demonstrated that higher levels of social support are associated with lower pregnancy stress among immigrant women [28, 46]. Studies from Germany and the United States have further highlighted the role of partner relationships and social networks in mitigating stress and depressive symptoms during pregnancy [47, 48]. A systematic review also reported a consistent association between low social support and elevated stress among immigrant pregnant women [49].

Immigrant pregnant women represent a particularly vulnerable population due to the cumulative impact of migration-related stressors and limited social support. The findings of this study indicate that short-term immigrant women experience lower perceived social support and higher stress levels compared to both long-term immigrants and Turkish women. Additionally, among short-term immigrants, family-based social support appears to play a particularly significant role in mitigating pregnancy stress.

Nurses and midwives are in a unique and strategic position to support immigrant women, as they are often the primary point of contact during the prenatal period. Their ability to provide continuous, holistic care and establish a trust-based relationship is essential for the early identification of psychosocial risks [38]. The results of this study suggest that nursing and midwifery interventions should prioritize the systematic assessment of social support systems in managing pregnancy-related stress. The literature emphasizes that culturally sensitive care and targeted social support interventions are essential for improving maternal health outcomes among immigrant populations [48]. Providing care through healthcare professionals who share the same cultural and linguistic background, rather than relying solely on interpreters, may further enhance the quality of prenatal care [28]. Furthermore, facilitating immigrant women’s integration into maternal and child health programs and ensuring awareness of available healthcare services are critical for achieving equitable healthcare delivery [38].

Strengths and limitations

The study has certain limitations. First, since the research was conducted in a single tertiary training and research hospital in Istanbul, the results cannot be generalized to the entire population. Another limitation is the inability to categorize participants by their specific legal statuses. While the Presidency of Immigration Management of the Republic of Türkiye classifies individuals from Syria under ‘Temporary Protection’ and those from Iraq and Afghanistan typically under ‘International Protection,’ we opted to use the inclusive term ‘immigrant’ to ensure conceptual consistency across these diverse groups. We acknowledge that the lack of legal status verification limits the interpretation of the socio-political context of the findings.

Participants were not asked about their immigration status, as this could have posed potential risks or disadvantages. Therefore, the participants’ immigration statuses in this study may vary, and the findings may not provide status-specific insights for research focused on distinct immigrant categories. However, a strength of the study is that it contributes to the limited literature comparing the association between perceived social support and stress among immigrant pregnant women living in Türkiye and Turkish pregnant women.

The exclusion of non-Turkish-speaking individuals is another limitation that may have introduced selection bias. This approach potentially excluded the most vulnerable immigrant groups who face greater language barriers and may experience different levels of social support and stress. Future research incorporating multilingual instruments and professional interpretation services is needed to enhance the generalizability of these findings to the broader immigrant population.

Finally, the focus on a metropolitan population may be interpreted as both a strength and a limitation, as it reflects the realities of urban migration contexts while potentially limiting applicability to rural settings. Additionally, sociodemographic variables such as age, education, and parity were not included as covariates in the regression models, which should be considered when interpreting the strength of the observed associations.

Conclusion

This study examined the association between perceived social support and pregnancy stress among Turkish and immigrant pregnant women. The results indicate a significant negative association between social support and pregnancy stress across all groups. Specifically, perceived social support was found to be a significant predictor of stress levels, particularly among long-term immigrant pregnant women. While Turkish pregnant women reported the highest levels of perceived social support, both immigrant groups experienced significantly higher levels of pregnancy stress compared to Turkish women.

These findings highlight the need for healthcare providers to routinely screen for social support networks and stress levels in both local and immigrant populations. Strengthening multidimensional social support systems is recommended to improve prenatal well-being in these diverse groups.
