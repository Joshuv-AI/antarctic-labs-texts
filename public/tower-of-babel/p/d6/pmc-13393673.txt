Tracer Gas Physicochemical Properties Influence DLCO Measurements in Patients With Ventilation Heterogeneity
Authors: Sarah B Heili-Frades, Alba Naya Prieto, María del Pilar Carballosa de Miguel, Karen Lozano Policroniades, Marcel José Rodríguez-Guzmán, María Jesús Rodríguez Nieto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Lung diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide, Gas diffusivity, Ventilation heterogeneity

Measurement of the lung diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO) requires an insoluble tracer gas to estimate alveolar volume (VA).1, 2 Helium (He) is traditionally used because of its low molecular weight and high gaseous diffusivity,2, 3 although alternative gases such as methane (CH4), argon (Ar), or sulfur hexafluoride (SF6) are employed in some pulmonary function systems.4 Tracer gases with different molecular weights exhibit distinct diffusivity profiles and distribute differently within the lung, particularly in the presence of ventilation heterogeneity.5 In homogeneous lungs, this effect is negligible; however, in diseased lungs, differences in gas diffusivity and convective-diffusive transport may influence VA estimation and consequently DLCO values. Standardization statements recommend demonstrating equivalence when alternative tracer gases are used,2, 3 yet variability between equipment and laboratories persists. We hypothesized that tracer gas physicochemical properties may influence DLCO measurements

We conducted and observational physiological pilot study to evaluate the influence of the tracer gas selection on DLCO test performance and its relationship with ventilation distribution. The study was approved by our local ethics committee. Twenty-seven subjects were studied in a tertiary hospital pulmonary function laboratory and classified into four groups: Group A (n = 7): healthy controls with normal spirometric and DLCO parameters, in accordance with ATS/ERS standards. Group B (n = 8): patients with a history of airflow obstruction FEV1/FVC < 70 and DLCO > 80% (e.g., asthma or COPD diagnosed based on GINA and GOLD criteria). Group C (n = 7): emphysema patients defined by the presence of compatible computed tomography findings, previous preserved spirometry, and DLCO < 80% predicted). Group D (n = 5): patients exhibiting a combined obstructive–restrictive ventilatory pattern, consistent with combined pulmonary fibrosis and emphysema (CPFE) or interstitial lung disease (ILD) with concomitant airflow limitation. All of them demonstrated DLCO < 80% and were diagnosed according to ATS/ERS/JRS/ALAT criteria. Baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1.

DLCO was measured using a gas mixture containing 0.3% CO, 0.3% CH4, 2.5% He, 3% Ar, 2.5% SF6, 21% O2, and nitrogen balance. The Cosmed Quark PFT 4 infrared CO/CH4 analyser was calibrated prior to each session using a two-point procedure (zero and span) with certified reference gas mixtures, while volume calibration was performed with a 3-L syringe in accordance with ATS/ERS recommendations.3 A quadrupole mass spectrometer (Marquette Electronics, model 1100) was calibrated daily using a certified multigas reference mixture.

Gas concentration signals were temporally aligned with flow measurements from the pneumotachograph by applying empirically derived delay corrections (Cosmed ≈200 ms; mass spectrometer ≈100 ms). Each subject performed three acceptable single-breath maneuvers (inspiratory volume ≥90% of vital capacity; breath-hold time 8–10 s, calculated using the Jones and Meade method3).

DLCO was calculated at 25%, 50%, and 75% of the expiratory curve following dead space washout, and values were corrected for hemoglobin concentration.3 Carboxyhaemoglobin (COHb) was assumed to be within normal range, as no participants were active smokers at the time of testing. Infrared absorption spectra confirming the absence of relevant spectral interference are shown in Supplementary Fig. S2.

Within each group, differences in DLCO across the four tracer gases were assessed using the Friedman test for repeated non-parametric measures. When significant, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Bonferroni adjustment for multiple comparisons within each group. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05 after correction.

No differences in DLCO measurements were observed among tracer gases in healthy subjects. In contrast, significant differences emerged in the other groups. Patients classified as obstructive non-DLCO alterations exhibited statistically significant variations depending on the tracer gas used, particularly in the early portion of expiration. On the other hand, the largest discrepancies were seen in non obstructive but DLCO-reduced patients (emphysema) reaching up to +26.7% (Group C, SF6 vs He at 25% sampling point) a magnitude that may be clinically relevant. Differences decreased toward 75%, suggesting improved gas mixing. Phase III slope analysis confirmed increased ventilation inhomogeneity in diseased lungs, and the magnitude of DLCO variability correlated with indices of uneven ventilation distribution. These findings indicate that differences in tracer gas physical properties influence DLCO measurements in patients with ventilation heterogeneity, being probably diffusivity and convective-diffusive transport the primarily mechanisms explaining our findings. Gases with lower density and higher diffusivity, as He, penetrate further into the lung periphery, distributing across a larger effective VA. This reduces estimated initial alveolar CO concentration and consequently lowers calculated DLCO relative to less diffusible gases such as SF6. The net effect is that DLCO is paradoxically lower with He than with SF6 in diseased lungs, with Ar and CH4 showing intermediate values. Whether gas-specific effects on the diffusion process itself contribute independently cannot be excluded with the current study design.

Ventilation heterogeneity and reduced DLCO are related but distinct physiological phenomena. Ventilation heterogeneity, defined as non-uniform regional gas distribution, may be present despite preserved DLCO, as observed in Group B. In contrast, reduced DLCO reflects impairment of alveolar–capillary gas transfer and may occur independently of, or in combination with, ventilation heterogeneity. The graded inter-gas differences in DLCO across Groups A–D likely reflect the interaction between these two mechanisms.

Sampling at different expiratory fractions revealed that the greatest variability occurs early in expiration, reflecting incomplete gas equilibration in poorly ventilated lung units.6, 7 Later sampling reduces variability through improved gas mixing. This is a physiological observation providing mechanistic insight; prospective validation is required before any modification to clinical practice could be recommended.

Non-uniform ventilation plays a central role in all diseased groups. These differences primarily reflect gas distribution behavior (diffusivity-dependent VA estimation differences) rather than true changes in alveolar-capillary diffusion, though an independent contribution cannot be fully excluded.8, 9, 10, 11

Several limitations should be acknowledged: the sample size was modest and subgroup numbers were small; however, differences were physiologically coherent. Multiple comparisons made may increase false-positive risk. Also, age imbalance between Group A (mean 33.4 years) and diseased groups (>50 years) may have minimized age-related heterogeneity in controls; Group D patients had combined obstructive-restrictive physiology potentially amplifying intergas discrepancies through dual mechanisms.

In this pilot physiological study, differences in tracer gas diffusivity were associated with discrepancies in DLCO measurements in diseased lungs, whereas no effect was observed in healthy subjects. Standardization of tracer gas composition would enhance inter-laboratory comparability of DLCO. When standardization is not feasible, later expiratory sampling may mitigate variability, although this approach requires prospective validation. Larger studies are needed to confirm these findings.
