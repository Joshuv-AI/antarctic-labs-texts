Multifunctional Aliphatic Diamine-Based Polynaphthoxazines: From Synthesis and Polymerization to Bioactivity and Photoluminescence
Authors: Abdulsalam Mahdy, Jalal A. Zahra, Randa N. Haddadin, Violet Kasabri, Ahmed A. K. Mohammed, Mohamed Gamal Mohamed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A series of aliphatic diamine-based naphthoxazine monomersdesignated as NZ-EDA, NZ-PDA, and NZ-HDAwere successfully synthesized via a one-pot Mannich condensation reaction involving 2-naphthol, paraformaldehyde, and three different aliphatic diamines: 1,2-diaminoethane, 1,3-diaminopropane, and 1,6-diaminohexane, respectively. Structural confirmation was achieved using FTIR and advanced NMR techniques (1H, 13C, and DEPT-135). Unlike conventional naphthoxazine systems, the synthesized monomers exhibited markedly lower polymerization temperatures and a broader processing window, thereby enhancing their practical utility. Among the resulting polymers, poly­(NZ-PDA) exhibited the highest thermal stability, with a 10% weight-loss temperature (T d10) of 367 °C. Photoluminescence analysis revealed intense blue emission across all monomers and their polymers, thanks to the molecular packing effects within the crystal structures, consistent with previous research findings. Morphological investigations via SEM showed that the NZ-EDA, NZ-PDA, and NZ-HDA monomers formed distinct spherical flower-like nanocrystals. The XRD results indicated that the average crystallite sizes of these monomers were 26, 15, and 20 nm, respectively. Their polymers exhibited a characteristic broad peak at 2θ ≈ 22.5°, indicating the formation of an amorphous structure that aligns with expectations. Biological assessments revealed that NZ-EDA and NZ-HDA exhibited potent anticandidal activities, with minimum inhibitory concentration values of 2 μg/mL against Candida albicans and 7.8 μg/mL against Candida parapsilosis, respectively, but with low antibacterial activity. In comparison to substantially antioxidative vitamin C, NZ-PDA> NZ-EDA > NZ-HDA had the appreciable descending order of antioxidative DPPH radical scavenging capacities (with IC50 values 4.9 < 9.4 < 57.8 μM, respectively), but conversely, a considerable ascending order of antimicrobial properties. Any inferences of dual antioxidative-antimicrobial efficacies could not be established.

Introduction

Benzoxazine (BZ) derivatives constitute a prominent class of heterocyclic compounds, typically synthesized via a Mannich condensation involving a phenolic precursor, formaldehyde, and a primary amine. − These compounds have attracted considerable interest owing to their diverse and potent biological activities across human, plant, and animal systems. The structural simplicity and modularity of the benzoxazine framework facilitate extensive chemical modification, enabling the generation of a wide spectrum of bioactive molecules. Numerous benzoxazine-based derivatives have demonstrated significant antifungal, antibacterial, anticancer, anticonvulsant, and anti-inflammatory properties. − In many cases, the addition of heterocyclic motifs to Mannich bases further enhances their pharmacological potential, providing a platform for designing new therapeutic agents with improved efficacy and selectivity. , The Mannich reaction remains a fundamental process in medicinal chemistry, facilitating the synthesis of a wide range of structurally diverse and biologically significant benzoxazine derivatives. Moreover, polybenzoxazines (PBZs) are a class of high-performance thermosetting polymers that are synthesized through catalyst-free ring-opening polymerization (ROP) of oxazine rings. Compared to traditional phenolic resins, PBZs have emerged as promising alternatives in advanced material applications. These polymers exhibit a unique combination of advantageous properties, including excellent chemical resistance, low moisture uptake, minimal shrinkage, high glass transition temperatures (T g), superior thermal and mechanical stability, and high flexibility. Notably, their polymerization proceeds without the formation of volatile byproducts, making the process clean and efficient. − In recent years, significant research efforts have focused on further enhancing the performance characteristics of PBZs through molecular design and hybridization strategies. Structural modifications incorporating benzophenone, biphenyl, cyclohexanone, cyclopropanone, naphthalene moieties, and the development of clay-based nanocomposites have been explored to enhance thermal, mechanical, and functional properties of PBZs for advanced technological applications. − Recent studies have demonstrated that poly­(oxazines) featuring intramolecular and intermolecular hydrogen bondingspecifically of the types–OH···O,–OH···N, and–OH···πexhibit enhanced thermal stability and elevated glass transition temperatures (T g). Among these systems, naphthoxazine (NZ) derivativescharacterized by the fusion of an oxazine ring with a naphthalene coreare synthesized via the condensation of primary amines, formaldehyde, and β-naphthol. More recently, NZs have been prepared through one-pot synthetic approaches and evaluated for various biological applications, such as fungicidal and bactericidal agents, and notably for potential use in treating Parkinson’s disease. Despite these promising bioactivities, the application of NZ in materials science remains relatively underexplored. Only a limited number of studies have investigated the polymerization of NZ to form high-performance thermosetting materials. One of the primary challenges hindering their broader use is the volatility of certain NZ monomers during polymerization, which can result in premature loss and reduced yield. Nevertheless, polymers incorporating naphthalene moieties are known to display excellent thermal and mechanical properties, due to their rigid, aromatic-rich backbone structures. Monofunctional benzoxazine (BZ) and naphthoxazine (NZ) monomers typically undergo polymerization to yield linear or branched oligomeric structures, with molecular weights generally ranging between 500 and 2000 g/mol. Previous works also investigated polybenzoxazines derived from linear aliphatic diamines, emphasizing the effects of chain length, network formation, and polymerization kinetics. − Further developments have included the synthesis of benzoxazine resins using aliphatic diamines, ranging from linear and branched to cyclic and triamine structures, via reactions with eugenol and paraformaldehyde. In a novel approach, Gungor and Bati reported a one-pot, two-step synthesis enabling the integration of two distinct oxazine moieties, BZ and NZ, within a single molecular architecture. The resulting asymmetric oxazines (NZ-BZ) were synthesized with similar ease as traditional benzoxazines, offering new avenues for structural diversification. Moreover, the formation of flower-like polymeric particles has gained increasing interest due to their high specific surface area and complex morphologies, which are advantageous for applications in energy storage, sensing technologies, and catalysis. In this study, three novel NZ monomers were synthesized via the condensation reaction of 2-naphthol, paraformaldehyde, and three distinct aliphatic diamines1,2-diaminoethane, 1,3-diaminopropane, and 1,6-diaminohexanein 1,4-dioxane. The chemical structures of the resulting monomers were confirmed by FTIR and NMR spectroscopy (1H, 13C, and DEPT-135) analyses. Their thermal polymerization behavior was investigated using DSC and in situ FTIR, while TGA was employed to assess their thermal stability and flame resistance. Crystallinity and surface morphology were examined via X-ray diffraction (XRD) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM), respectively. Finally, to explore their potential biomedical applications, the antimicrobial and antioxidant activities of the NZ monomers were also evaluated.

Materials and Methods

Materials

2-Naphthol (99%), 1,2-diaminoethane (EDA, >99%), 1,3-diaminopropane (PDA, 99%), 1,6-diaminohexane (HDA, 99.5%), ethyl acetate (99.8%), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), chloroform (CHCl3), and 1,4-dioxane were procured from Thermo Fisher Scientific, Germany, and used as received. Also, all microbiological media used were from Liofilchem-Italy. Ciprofloxacin HCl, fluconazole, and voriconazole were procured from Thermo Fisher Scientific.

General Procedure for the Synthesis of NZ-EDA, NZ-PDA, and NZ-HDA

A mixture of the distinct aliphatic diamines (6.7 mmol, 0.4 g), paraformaldehyde (26.7 mmol, 0.8 g), and 30 mL of 1,4-dioxane was placed in a round-bottom flask and stirred at 70 °C for 30 min. The quantity of 2-naphthol (13.9 mmol, 2 g) was then added, and the temperature was raised to 105 °C for a duration of 10 h. The progress of the reaction was monitored using thin-layer chromatography (TLC) with a solvent mixture consisting of 2 parts chloroform to 5 parts hexane. After the reaction mixture was cooled to room temperature, the solvent was evaporated using a rotary evaporator. The resulting product was dissolved in chloroform, washed with 1 M sodium hydroxide, and then dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate for 10 h. The targeted difunctional NZ monomers were recrystallized from ethanol, followed by drying the product under a vacuum at 50 °C for 48 h. This procedure yielded approximately 75, 83, and 95% for NZ-EDA, NZ-PDA, and NZ-HDA, respectively.

Optical Properties of NZ-EDA, NZ-PDA, and NZ-HDA Monomers and Their Polymers

The absorption and photoluminescent (PL) spectra of the samples were obtained in DMSO at room temperature on a CARY50 UV–vis spectrophotometer and an FLS920 fluorescence spectrophotometer.

Polymerization of NZ-EDA, NZ-PDA, and NZ-HDA Monomers

The polymerization of NZ-EDA, NZ-PDA, and NZ-HDA was achieved by heating at temperatures of 120 °C, 150 °C, 180 °C, 210 °C, and 240 °C for 2 h at each stage, resulting in cross-linked PNZ-EDA, PNZ–PDA, and PNZ-HDA, respectively.

Density Functional Theory (DFT) Calculations

We performed DFT calculations on all molecules to reveal their structure and gain insights into their properties. The ground state geometry of all structures was optimized using DFT with the B3LYP functional with the D3 correction and the basis set 6–31G­(d). This dispersion correction is necessary to account for long-range and noncovalent interactions. Several conformers were considered for the ground state geometry, and the one with the lowest energy was selected. The lack of imaginary frequencies proved that these geometries are true minima. All geometry optimizations were performed using the Gaussian 16 software.

Minimum Inhibitory Concentration Determination (MIC)

The compounds were tested against a Gram-positive bacterium (Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 6538), a Gram-negative bacterium (Escherichia coli ATCC 8739), and two yeasts (Candida albicans ATCC 10231 and Candida parapsilosis ATCC 22019). Due to poor water solubility, the compounds were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to achieve a concentration of 1000 μg/mL (except fluconazole and ciprofloxacin, which were dissolved in water). For bacterial strains, the EUCAST (2025) method was adapted. A few colonies of each bacterial culture, previously grown on Mueller Hinton agar for 24–48 h, were suspended in normal saline and adjusted to 0.5 McFarland (ca 1.5 × 108 CFU/mL). A 1/10 dilution was prepared in Mueller-Hinton broth (MHB) for inoculation. In the wells of a 96-well flat-bottom microtiter plate, double serial dilutions of each compound were prepared in MHB. Each well was inoculated with the bacteria to achieve approximately 5 × 105 CFU/mL, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C overnight. Positive control (bacteria in culture medium only) and negative control (culture medium only) were prepared. Each compound was tested in duplicate. After incubation, the plates were inspected visually for growth inhibition. MIC is the lowest concentration of an antimicrobial agent that completely prevents visible growth of the tested microorganism. To test the antimicrobial activity of DMSO, double serial dilutions of DMSO in broth medium were prepared, processed, and inoculated as the samples, but without the addition of the compounds. The broad-spectrum antibacterial drug, ciprofloxacin, was used as a reference for comparison. For C. albicans, the same procedure applied to bacteria was used, except that after inoculation, the plates were incubated at 35 °C for 24 h. Following incubation, the absorbance was measured at 530 nm using a plate reader (EUCAST, 2023). If the positive control wells showed absorbance ≤0.2, the plates were further incubated for another 24 h, and the absorbance was measured again for all the wells. The absorbance of the wells was corrected by subtracting the absorbance of the blank. The MIC was considered the minimum concentration, inhibiting growth of ≥50% of that of the positive control (EUCAST, 2023). The antifungal drugs fluconazole and voriconazole were used as references for comparison. Since DMSO resulted in growth inhibition at concentrations of 12.5% and above for the bacteria and yeast tested, the MIC was considered the minimum compound concentration that results in growth inhibition, where the DMSO concentration is 6.25% or below. Therefore, under the experimental conditions, the highest concentration of the drugs that can be achieved without interference in activity from DMSO is 62.5 μg/mL. ,

DPPH Free Radical Scavenger Assay

The reduction of the radicals, which causes the color to change from oxidized purple to reduced yellow, is the basis for this technique. In methanol (MeOH) solution, diphenyl-2-picryl-hydrazyl (DPPH) mostly undergoes reduction due to the production of the nonradical form DPPH-H in the presence of a hydrogen-donating molecule. Vitamin C was the robust and classical reference agent for antioxidative radical scavenging. A DPPH radical is stable and can yield repeatable spectroscopic values, in contrast to other radical scavenging assays. In a 96-well plate, treatment components or ascorbic acid were combined with DPPH solution (0.2 mM) to be diluted with MeOH in a 1:1 concentration ratio (resulting in 1.563–200 μM as final concentration range). Following 1 h incubation, a change in absorbance at 517 nm wavelength was assessed (for quantification of color shift using a spectrophotometer microplate reader, Bio-Tek Instrument, USA). As A represents photometric absorbance, the % antioxidation was evaluated = (A control – A sample)/A control x 100%, and mean IC50 values of tested compounds ± SD (standard deviation; n= 4 independent experiments) were calculated using GraphPad Prism software [version 5.0 for Windows; GraphPad software, San Diego, CA, USA]. −

Results and Discussion

Synthesis of NZ-EDA, NZ-PDA, and NZ-HDA

Aliphatic diamine-based NZ monomers with high purity were successfully synthesized via a one-pot Mannich condensation reaction. The one-pot synthetic strategy allows for multiple sequential chemical transformations to occur within a single reaction vessel, thereby streamlining the synthetic process. This approach offers significant advantages, including high atom economy, shortened reaction times, simplified purification procedures, and minimized intermediate isolation, making it an efficient and sustainable method for the preparation of complex organic molecules. This reaction involved distinct aliphatic diamines, 2-naphthol, and paraformaldehyde in a molar ratio of 1:2:4, as shown in Scheme . The resulting difunctional NZ monomers were in high yields, with the NZ-HDA monomer, which has the longest aliphatic diamine chain, demonstrating the highest product yield. The structures of the synthesized monomers were confirmed using FT-IR and NMR spectroscopy (1H, 13C, and DEPT-135).

Synthesis of NZ-EDA, NZ-PDA, and NZ-HDA Monomers

Figure S1 shows the 1H, 13C, and DEPT-135 NMR spectra of NZ-EDA. In the 1H NMR spectrum, the characteristic resonances at 4.29 and ∼4.91 ppm correspond to the NZ framework, specifically the N–CH2–Ar and O–CH2–N groups, respectively. A singlet at ∼2.90 ppm is attributed to the methylene group of the aliphatic diamine, and the aromatic protons are found at 6.9–8.0 ppm. In the 13C NMR spectrum, the characteristic carbon resonances at 50.68 and 82.69 ppm are confirmatory of the NZ structure, N–CH2–Ar and O–CH2–N carbons. A singlet at 47.78 ppm corresponds to the N–CH2–CH2 linkage of the diamine, in agreement with literature data. The DEPT-135 spectrum further validates the NZ structure, showing inverted characteristic resonance signals in the aliphatic region between 50 to 82 ppm corresponding to CH2 groups from both the oxazine ring and the aliphatic diamine moiety. Figure S2 displays the NMR spectra of NZ-PDA. This 1H NMR spectrum shows the characteristic resonance singlets at 4.34 and 4.95 ppm for N–CH2–Ar and O–CH2–N groups in the NZ structure, respectively. Due to its asymmetric structure, an additional singlet at 4.55 ppm is observed, also attributed to N–CH2–Ar from the oxazine ring. Methylene groups of the aliphatic diamine appear as doublet proton resonances around 2.91 and 1.90 ppm. Its 13C NMR spectrum shows doublets carbon resonances at 52.60 and 50.60 ppm (N–CH2–Ar), and a singlet at 82.62 ppm (O–CH2–N). The characteristic carbon resonances at 49.60 and 27.02 ppm confirm the presence of the diamine methylene carbons. In addition, the DEPT-135 spectrum displays seven inverted characteristic resonances in the aliphatic region between 46 and 82 ppm, consistent with the CH2 environments of both the oxazine ring and diamine segments. Figure S3 presents the NMR spectra of NZ-HDA. The 1H NMR spectrum shows peaks at 4.19 and 4.83 ppm for N–CH2–Ar and O–CH2–N groups, respectively. Methylene signals from the aliphatic diamine are observed at 2.63, 1.47, and 1.26 ppm. The 13C NMR spectrum features characteristic resonances at 51.94 and 82.32 ppm, attributed to the NZ structure, while additional peaks at 47.95, 27.80, and 26.80 ppm correspond to methylene carbons in the diamine chain. The aromatic carbons are found between 110 and 132 ppm. The DEPT-135 spectrum reveals five inverted characteristic resonances due to the CH2 oxazine ring and aliphatic cores, further confirming the expected molecular structure. The structures of NZ-EDA, NZ-PDA, and NZ-HDA were verified by FTIR spectra, as shown in Figure . All spectra exhibit characteristic absorption bands at approximately 1030 and 1232 cm–1, corresponding to the symmetrical and asymmetrical stretching vibrations of the C–O–C bonds from the aromatic oxazine rings. Additionally, distinct bands observed at 918 cm–1 (NZ-EDA), 939 cm–1 (NZ-PDA), and 914 cm–1 (NZ-HDA) are assigned to the oxazine ring. ,

The UV–Vis absorption spectra of NZ-EDA, NZ-PDA, and NZ-HDA monomers exhibit a strong absorption peak at approximately 335 nm (Figure a), which corresponds to π–π* electronic transitions associated with the conjugated naphthalene ring system. The emission spectrum of the same monomers (Figure b) displays maximum emission wavelengths at 396 nm (NZ-EDA), 426 nm (NZ-PDA), and 438 nm (NZ-HDA), respectively, under an excitation wavelength of 350 nm. These emissions fall within the blue-light region, indicating notable photoluminescent behavior.

Thermal Polymerization Behavior and Optical Properties of NZ-EDA, NZ-PDA, and NZ-HAD after Ring-Opening Polymerization (ROP)

The polymerization behavior of NZ-EDA, NZ-PDA, and NZ-HDA was investigated using DSC and FTIR (Figures a–d and S4a,b). Key thermal parameters, including melting temperature (T m), and maximum polymerization temperature (T p), are summarized in Table S1. All three monomers exhibited sharp melting peaks at 155.4 °C (NZ-EDA), 128.2 °C (NZ-PDA), and 121.8 °C (NZ-HDA), respectively, indicating high purity and a significant degree of crystallinity. As the aliphatic chain length of the diamine increased, the T m values decreased. This trend is attributed to the increased flexibility of the NZ monomers. Among the three, NZ-EDA displayed the lowest T p value (189.1 °C), which is likely due to the presence of intramolecular hydrogen bonding that facilitates the ring-opening polymerization process. The DSC and FTIR results for NZ-EDA cured at various temperatures [120–240 °C] are shown in Figure a,c. From this Figure, it was observed that, after curing the NZ-EDA sample at a temperature of 150 °C, no exothermic peak was detected (Figure a), confirming complete ring-opening polymerization for oxazine units. Complementary FTIR spectra (Figure c) showed the disappearance of characteristic oxazine absorption bands at 918, 1110, and 1231 cm1, further confirming full polymerization at this temperature. The exothermic curing peak (T p) of the NZ-PDA and NZ-HDA appeared at 204 and 197.4 °C, respectively (Figures b and S4a). After curing at 180 °C, the disappearance of exothermic peaks indicates that the ring-opening polymerization of the oxazine units was completed. FTIR spectra of the NZ-PDA and NZ-HDA (Figures d and S4b) also showed that the characteristic oxazine absorption bands for both NZ-PDA and NZ-HDA disappear after curing at this temperature, supporting the DSC findings. ,

(a, b) DSC and (c, d) FTIR analyses illustrate the polymerization behavior of NZ-EDA and NZ-PDA at different temperatures [25–250 °C].

A wider processing window is desirable for thermal processing, indicating these monomers possess suitable processability for material applications. The T g values of the fully cured polymers at 240 °C, as determined by DSC (Figure S5), were 215 °C for PNZ-EDA, 249 °C for PNZ–PDA, and 224 °C for PNZ-HAD. These results indicate that the synthesized polymers display properties of thermally stable, cross-linkable, and processable thermoplastics, suggesting potential applications in advanced materials and high-performance thermosets. Potential Chemical Structures of PNZ-EDA, PNZ–PDA, and PNZ-HDA after Thermal ROP

The UV–Vis absorption spectra of the corresponding polymersPNZ-EDA, PNZ–PDA, and PNZ-HDAshow a strong absorption band centered at 261 nm, attributed to σ–σ* transitions of the aliphatic chains in the polymer backbone. The absorption intensity increases with longer alkyl chain lengths. In addition, shoulder peaks around 334 nm are observed, arising from π–π* transitions of the conjugated naphthalene structures within the polymer framework (Figure a). Notably, no significant absorption is observed beyond 500 nm, suggesting that these polymers are transparent in the visible region. This transparency supports their potential application as matrix materials or in optoelectronic devices The emission spectra of the PNZ-EDA, PNZ–PDA, and PNZ-HDA (Figure b,c) reveal broad fluorescence bands ranging from 371 to 422 nm at an excitation wavelength of 330 nm, and from 402 to 444 nm under 350 nm excitation. These emission maxima are red-shifted compared to their monomeric counterparts, and this behavior is attributed to the presence of multiple conformations within the polymer matrix and intermolecular interactions between the fluorophores and the polymer backbone. A slight red shift in the polymer emissions relative to the monomers further supports this interaction. Moreover, in the solid state, PNZ-EDA exhibits visible blue-light emission under UV irradiation, as shown in Figure d. We carried out DFT calculations to optimize the molecular geometries and investigate the electronic properties of all compounds. The HOMO and LUMO orbitals, as well as their corresponding energy levels, were computed using the B3LYP-D3/6-31G­(d) method. Figure S6 presents the isosurface maps of the HOMO and LUMO for NZ-EDA, NZ-PDA, and NZ-HDA. All three molecules exhibit similar electron distribution patterns. The HOMO is primarily delocalized over the naphthalene core and the lone pairs of the adjacent oxygen atom, while the LUMO is mainly distributed over the naphthalene ring, with minimal contribution from either the aromatic substituent or the alkyl chain. Figure S7 displays the HOMO and LUMO isosurface maps for PNZ-EDA, PNZ–PDA, and PNZ-HDA. In contrast to the previously discussed molecules, the alkyl chain in PNZ-EDA and PNZ–PDA contributes noticeably to the HOMO distribution. Specifically, the HOMO in these two compounds is delocalized across the β-naphthol ring, the nitrogen atom’s lone pair, and the alkyl chain. In the case of PNZ-HDA, however, the HOMO is confined to the β-naphthol ring, likely due to the greater spatial separationsix carbon atomsbetween the two aromatic systems. For all three molecules, the LUMO is consistently delocalized over the naphthalene ring, with no significant contribution from the alkyl chain or other substituents.

Thermal Stability of NZ-EDA, NZ-PDA, and NZ-HAD before and after the Thermal ROP Process

The thermal stability of the synthesized NZ-EDA, NZ-PDA, and NZ-HAD before and after curing at various temperatures was evaluated using TGA. The TGA curves, which show weight loss (%) as a function of temperature under a nitrogen atmosphere, are presented in Figure a–c, and the corresponding thermal data are summarized in Table S2. The temperature at which 10% weight loss occurred (T d10) for PNZ-EDA, PNZ–PDA, and PNZ-HAD cured at 240 °C was found to be 351 °C, 367 °C, and 302 °C, respectively. Among the three, PNZ–PDA exhibited the highest thermal stability (T d10 = 367 °C) and the greatest char yield (43%). The TGA results also revealed that increasing the curing temperature enhanced the thermal stability of PNZ-EDA, PNZ–PDA, and PNZ-HAD. This improvement is attributed to the increased cross-linking density within the polymer network at higher curing temperatures. PNZ-EDA, PNZ–PDA, and PNZ-HAD samples exhibited two major degradation stages, likely corresponding to the decomposition of aliphatic amine segments and the degradation of the NZ ring system. Notably, the thermal stability of the synthesized PNZ-EDA, PNZ–PDA, and PNZ-HAD samples exceeds that of previously reported aromatic diamine-based PNZs, such as poly­(M-B). Flame resistance was assessed using the limiting oxygen index (LOI), a widely accepted metric for determining polymer flammability. Materials with LOI values below 26 are considered flammable, while those with higher values are classified as self-extinguishing. The LOI values were calculated based on char yield (CY) using the van Krevelen equation. The calculated LOI values for PNZ-EDA, PNZ–PDA, and PNZ-HAD samples cured at 210 and 240 °C were all above 28, indicating excellent flame resistance. These results are particularly significant considering that the polymers are derived from halogen-, silane-, and phosphorus-free biobased monomers, which enhances their appeal for environmentally friendly flame-retardant applications.

Morphological Studies

Insightful morphological features of the difunctional naphthoxazine (NZ) monomers, which include both flexible aliphatic and rigid aromatic segments, were revealed through SEM analysis. The SEM images of NZ-EDA and NZ-HDA at various magnifications (Figure a–f) showed a distinct spherical, flower-like nanocrystalline morphology with smooth surface textures. These spherical assemblies, varying in size, appear to form through the self-assembly of NZ molecules, likely driven by intermolecular hydrogen bonding and π–π aromatic stacking interactions. The SEM observations were further supported by X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis (Figure S8). The XRD patterns of NZ-EDA, NZ-PDA, and NZ-HDA displayed sharp diffraction peaks within the 2θ range of 5–30°, indicating nanocrystalline structures. Complementary structural analysis using X-ray diffraction (XRD) (Figure ) shows that PNZ-EDA, PNZ–PDA, and PNZ-HDA each display broad diffraction peaks centered around 2θ ≈ 21.5°, indicating their mainly amorphous nature. The lack of sharp crystalline peaks suggests a disordered polymer network, which generally provides better mechanical flexibility and ease of processing.

Biological Studies

The antimicrobial activities of NZ-EDA, NZ-PDA, and NZ-HDA were evaluated against a range of microorganisms, with results summarized in Table . Among the tested compounds, NZ-HDA demonstrated the most potent antimicrobial activity, showing minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of 7.8 μg/mL against Staphylococcus aureus and 15.6 μg/mL against Escherichia coli. While these values are lower than those of the standard antibacterial agent ciprofloxacin, they still indicate noteworthy antibacterial potential. NZ-EDA and NZ-HDA exhibited extreme antifungal activity against Candida albicans, a major cause of hospital-acquired infections. Both compounds had MIC values of 2 μg/mL, which are comparable to the clinically used antifungal drugs fluconazole and voriconazole (MIC ∼ 3.9 μg/mL). Additionally, they showed moderate activity against Candida parapsilosis (MIC = 7.8 μg/mL), although less effective than the commercial antifungals. Overall, NZ-HDA displayed the broadest and most effective antimicrobial profile among the three, with activity against S. aureus (7.8 μg/mL) similar to that of previously reported biobased benzoxazine monomers (e.g., TF-BOZs, MIC = 5 μg/mL against S. aureus). These findings suggest that these compounds, especially NZ-HDA, hold potential for use in disinfection-related applications, such as in water purification systems, medical device coatings, and antimicrobial surface treatments. In addition to antimicrobial assessments, the antioxidant properties of the compounds were evaluated using the DPPH radical scavenging assay in vitro, also presented in Table . Compared to the benchmark antioxidant vitamin C, the three compounds showed variable radical scavenging efficiencies. While NZ-HDA exhibited the strongest antimicrobial activity, its antioxidant performance was nonetheless relatively low. In contrast, NZ-EDA and NZ-PDA demonstrated significant DPPH scavenging qualities, with NZ-PDA emerging as the most active antioxidant, with an IC50 value of approximately 5 μM vs the substantial antioxidative capacities of vitamin C. Taken together in comparison to respective robust and classical reference agents, the tested compounds had the appreciable descending order of antioxidative DPPH radical scavenging capacities but conversely considerable ascending order of antimicrobial properties. Any inferences of dual antioxidative-antimicrobial efficacies could not be established.

Conclusions

In this study, difunctional NZ monomers incorporating aliphatic diamines were successfully synthesized via a one-pot Mannich reaction, offering high yields and short reaction times. The chemical structures of the NZ monomers were confirmed by FTIR and NMR (1H, 13C, DEPT-135) spectroscopy. Moreover, according to the DSC results, these monomers exhibited low polymerization temperatures, with NZ-EDA showing the lowest (189 °C), likely due to intramolecular hydrogen bonding facilitating the ring-opening process. Among the synthesized polymers, PNZ–PDA demonstrated the highest thermal stability (T d10 = 367 °C) and the highest char yield (43%), based on the TGA results. In addition, SEM results revealed that the monomers formed flower-like nanocrystalline structures, while their corresponding cured polymers displayed an amorphous structure and fracture-free surfaces. Biologically, the monomers showed distinct activity profiles: NZ-HDA demonstrated the strongest antimicrobial effects (as in duality of antibacterial and anticandidal efficacies); while NZ-PDA had noticeably the highest antioxidant capacity, as indicated by the DPPH scavenging assay. Nonetheless, no compound exhibited strong dual activity of both antioxidant and antimicrobial properties.
