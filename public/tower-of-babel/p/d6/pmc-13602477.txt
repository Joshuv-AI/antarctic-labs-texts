cAMP-response element binding protein (CREB)-mediated glycolysis enhancement promotes apoptosis of RAW264.7 macrophages infected with BCG under high glucose conditions
Authors: Yiming Wu, FuYang Song, Gang Zhao, Yujiong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMacrophages serve as the primary host defense against Mycobacterium tuberculosis (M.tb). Cellular glycolysis plays a critical role in macrophage-mediated resistance to mycobacterial infection. A hyperglycemic microenvironment impairs host defense and exacerbates mycobacterial infection. However, the role of cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB), a key transcription factor regulating metabolism and apoptosis, in macrophages’ anti-mycobacterial response under high glucose (HG) conditions remains unclear. Here, we used the attenuated M.tb strain Bacillus Calmette‑Guérin (BCG) to investigate the role of CREB in HG‑modulated glycolysis and apoptosis in infected macrophages.ResultsIn the present study, we found that BCG infection significantly induced apoptosis and upregulated the expression of key glycolytic enzymes in RAW264.7 macrophages in vitro, and these effects were further amplified under HG conditions. In vivo, C57BL/6J mice fed a high-fat high-sugar (HFHS) diet and infected with BCG showed more pronounced lung tissue inflammatory cell infiltration and bacterial burden, along with upregulated p-CREB, GLUT4 and Cleaved-Caspase 9, compared with infected mice fed normal chow. siRNA-mediated CREB knockdown significantly reduced apoptosis-related proteins and glycolytic enzymes, decreased intracellular ROS accumulation, and improved cell survival in BCG-infected RAW264.7 cells under HG conditions. The glycolysis inhibitor 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG) suppressed glycolytic enzyme expression and apoptosis, with further enhanced protection when combined with CREB siRNA.ConclusionBCG infection upregulated CREB expression in a time-dependent manner, and this effect was further potentiated under HG conditions. CREB upregulation was accompanied by enhanced glycolysis, increased ROS accumulation, and elevated macrophage apoptosis, and these changes were associated with exacerbated BCG infection under HG conditions. These findings suggest a mechanistic link between hyperglycemia and impaired antimycobacterial defense, and point to CREB as a candidate for further investigation under HG metabolic stress.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12866-026-05430-9.

Background

Tuberculosis (TB), caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (M.tb) [1], remains a major public health burden in many developing countries [2]. The comorbidity of diabetes mellitus (DM) and TB synergistically accelerates disease severity and leads to poor treatment outcomes [3, 4]. It has been reported that patients with DM have a 3-fold higher risk of developing TB and an approximately 1.5-fold elevated mortality rate compared with non-DM individuals [5]. Clinical and experimental studies have confirmed that a hyperglycemic microenvironment impairs host innate immunity and increases susceptibility to TB [6–12], but the molecular mechanisms underlying this enhanced susceptibility remain poorly defined.

Macrophages are the primary host cells for M.tb [13], and their anti-infective functions are tightly regulated by glucose metabolism [14–16]. Hyperglycemia induces robust glycolytic reprogramming in macrophages, leading to abnormal accumulation of glycolytic metabolites and excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production [17–19]. Critically, this metabolic dysfunction serves as a core upstream signal that triggers aberrant macrophage apoptosis via excessive lactate and ROS accumulation [20, 21]. The dysregulated apoptosis further impairs bacterial clearance and allows persistent intracellular mycobacterial survival, forming a vicious cycle of metabolic disorder, immune cell dysfunction, and uncontrolled infection [22–24]. However, the key molecule that modulates glycolysis, macrophage apoptosis, and mycobacterial persistence under high glucose (HG) conditions to determine M.tb infection outcomes remains unidentified.

cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB) is unique in its ability to be activated by a wide range of extracellular stimuli and to coordinate the expression of genes governing diverse biological processes, including metabolism, apoptosis, and inflammation [25]. Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that CREB plays a crucial regulatory role in metabolic diseases and infectious diseases [26]. For instance, previous studies reported that M.tb activates CREB to promote its intracellular survival [27], and CREB regulates hepatic gluconeogenesis to maintain glucose homeostasis [28]. Consistent with these findings, our previous proteomics screening further identified CREB as a significantly upregulated protein during Bacillus Calmette‑Guérin (BCG) infection, implying its potential involvement in mycobacterial immune regulation. However, it remains unclear whether CREB serves as the key molecule that modulates the outcome of M.tb infection by regulating glycolysis in macrophages under HG conditions.

Here, we used RAW264.7 macrophages and C57BL/6J mice to investigate the role of CREB in glycolysis and macrophage apoptosis induced by attenuated M.tb strain Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) under HG conditions. This study aimed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which CREB contributes to impaired antimycobacterial host defense under HG conditions.

Materials and methods

Bacterial culture

BCG (Mycobacterium tuberculosis attenuated strain) was purchased from Chengdu Institute of Biological Products Co., Ltd. (Chengdu, China). The lyophilized bacterial preparation was dissolved in DMEM medium (Gibco; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.), followed by gentle sonication to disrupt bacterial clumps and prepare a single-cell suspension. Glycerol was supplemented to a final concentration of 20%, and the bacterial suspension was aliquoted and stored at -20 °C. All aliquots were used within 10 days. Bacterial viability was confirmed by CFU titration prior to each infection to ensure stable MOI.

Cell culture and infection

The murine monocyte/macrophage-like cell line RAW264.7 was purchased from the Cell Bank of Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Cells were cultured in complete DMEM containing 25 mM D-glucose supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) at 37 °C under 5% CO₂. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 1 × 10⁶ cells/well and incubated for 12 h before treatment. Cells were pre-incubated in standard (25 mM) or high glucose (HG, 50 mM) DMEM for 24 h before BCG infection. The HG medium was prepared by supplementing basal DMEM with additional D-glucose. All groups were infected with BCG at an MOI of 10 for 24 h. For osmotic control, 25 mM mannitol was added to the standard basal DMEM to match the total osmolarity of the HG medium, followed by BCG infection under identical conditions. For glycolysis inhibition, cells were pretreated with 5 mM 2‑deoxy‑D‑glucose (2‑DG; MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China) for 2 h, and subsequently infected with BCG (MOI = 10) for 24 h in HG medium. After 24 h of infection, cells were further incubated with DMEM containing 100 µg/mL amikacin for 1 h to eliminate residual extracellular bacteria, then washed twice with PBS and harvested for protein extraction or other assays.

Bacterial colony-forming unit count

Intracellular infection was confirmed in preliminary experiments by Ziehl‑Neelsen staining of infected cells at 2 h and 24 h post‑infection, which showed acid‑fast bacilli inside the cytoplasm (data not shown). The ability of macrophages to internalize and clear BCG was evaluated by CFU assay. Briefly, RAW264.7 cells were infected with BCG under standard basal DMEM (25 mM) or high glucose (HG, 50 mM) conditions. After 2 h, the cells were washed three times with pre‑warmed PBS to remove non‑adherent bacteria. To eliminate remaining extracellular bacteria, cells were incubated with fresh complete DMEM containing 100 µg/mL amikacin for 1 h, then washed twice with PBS. Cells were then lysed by adding 1% Triton X-100, and serial dilutions of the lysate were plated onto 7H10 agar plates. The plates were incubated for three weeks at 37 °C, and the number of colonies was counted and expressed as colony‑forming units (CFU).

siRNA transfection

Three small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting CREB (Shanghai GenePharma, China) were used and the sequences are listed in Table 1. RAW264.7 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at 1 × 106 cells per well and cultured for 12 h. Then the cells were transfected with siRNA for 24 h using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After transfection, cells were subjected to BCG infection at an MOI of 10 for 24 h under HG conditions.

Western blotting

Total cellular protein was extracted using the M-PER Mammalian Protein Extraction Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.). A BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) was used to measure the total protein concentration. Subsequently, 10 µg protein was mixed with 6X loading buffer, resolved by 10% SDS-PAGE, and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking with SuperBlock (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) at 37˚C for 1 h, the membrane was incubated with the primary antibodies overnight at 4˚C, including GAPDH (1:1000), CREB (1:1000), p-CREB (1:1000), PKM2 (1:1000), Hexokinase II (1:1000), GLUT4 (1:1000), LDHA (1:1000), G6PD (1:1000), Cleaved-Caspase 9 (1:1000), Bax (1:1000), Cyt-C (1:1000), and Bcl-2 (1:1000). The samples were then incubated with the secondary antibodies HRP-conjugated Affinipure goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (1:10,000; cat. no. SA00001-1; ProteinTech Group, Inc.) or HRP-conjugated Affinipure goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (1:10,000; cat. no. SA00001-2; ProteinTech Group, Inc.) at room temperature for 1 h. A ECL chemiluminescence kit (Analytik Jena US LLC) was used to visualize protein bands. The intensity of each protein band was normalized to the respective GAPDH band and analyzed using ImageJ (version 1.46; National Institutes of Health).

Reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNAs were isolated using the MiniBEST Universal RNA extraction kit (Takara Bio, Inc.), and RNA was reverse-transcribed to cDNA using a PrimeScript™ RT reagent kit (Takara Bio, Inc.). The reaction condition was: 95˚C for 30 s; followed by 40 cycles of 95˚C for 5 s and 65˚C for 30 s. Relative expression levels were evaluated using the 2−∆∆Cq method and normalized to GAPDH as the internal control. The primers used for qRT-PCR were listed in Table 1.

EdU cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was detected using an EdU Cell Proliferation Kit (Cat# C0075S; Beyotime, China). After the indicated treatments, RAW264.7 cells were incubated with EdU (10 µmol/L) for 2 h at 37 °C, washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 15 min at room temperature. After incubation with 0.3% Triton X-100, nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33,342 (10 µg/mL) for 10 min at room temperature. Cells were washed three times with PBS, and images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope.

Measurement of apoptosis

After the indicated treatments, RAW264.7 cells were washed three times with cold PBS and pelleted by centrifugation (1000 rpm, 5 min). The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was resuspended in 500 µL of binding buffer. Then, 5 µL of Annexin V‑FITC and 5 µL of propidium iodide (Annexin V/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit, KeyGen Biotech, Nanjing, China) were added consecutively, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 5 min in the dark. After incubation, cells were analyzed using a FACSCanto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) with BD FACSDiva software to determine the percentage of apoptotic cells.

MTT assay

The viability of the cells was assessed using the MTT kit assay according to the manufacturer’s instructions. 20 µL of MTT solution was added to each well. After incubation for 4 h at 37 °C, DMSO (100 µL) was added to each well, and the absorbance (560 nm) was measured using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA).

Intracellular ROS detection

Intracellular ROS levels were detected using the CellROX™ Deep Red Kit (#C10422, ThermoFisher) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Fluorescent signals were visualized via confocal laser scanning microscopy and quantified by flow cytometry.

Mitochondrial fluorescent staining

Cellular mitochondria were stained using CellLight™ Mitochondria-GFP BacMam 2.0 (#C10600, ThermoFisher) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Finally, a confocal laser scanning microscope was used to detect the fluorescence of cell mitochondria.

Detection of glucose, lactate and pyruvate levels

The levels of glucose, pyruvate, and Lactate were determined using the Glucose Content Assay Kit (Solarbio, Cat. No. BC2505, China), Pyruvate Content Assay Kit (Solarbio, Cat. No. BC2200), and Lactic Acid Content Assay Kit (Solarbio, Cat. No. BC2235) in accordance with the manufacturers’ protocols.

Transmission electron microscopy

Cells pellet was incubated with 3% glutaraldehyde for 10 min at 4 °C, then with 1% osmium tetroxide. Next, the cells were serially dehydrated with acetone and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin Sect. (50 nm) were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate for 15 min. Cell morphology was observed using a transmission electron microscope.

Animals

A total of 24 male C57BL/6J mice (4 weeks old, weight 15 ± 5 g) were purchased from Gempharmatech Co. Ltd (Jiangsu, China). Mice were randomly divided into 4 groups (n = 6/group). For group 1 (NG + BCG), mice were fed with normal chow and then infected with BCG. For group 2 (NG + BCG+666 − 15), mice were fed with normal chow, and then infected with BCG and treated with CREB inhibitor 666 − 15. For group 3 (HG + BCG), mice were fed with high-fat high-sugar (HFHS) chow for 12 weeks, and then infected with BCG. For group 4 (HG + BCG+666 − 15), mice were fed with high-fat high-sugar (HFHS) chow for 12 weeks, and then infected with BCG and treated with CREB inhibitor 666 − 15. Intervention details: BCG (2 × 106 CFU/mouse in 100 µL saline) was administered via tail vein injection. CREB inhibitor 666 − 15 (MCE, Shanghai, China) was intraperitoneally injected at a dose of 10 mg/kg every 3 days, starting 1 day before BCG infection and continuing until sacrifice. Before all experiments, the mice were acclimated for 7 days. At 49 days post-infection, mice were deeply anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (100 mg/kg body weight, intraperitoneal injection). Animals were confirmed to be fully unconscious and unresponsive to toe-pinch stimulation before lung tissue collection. Following tissue dissection, mice were humanely euthanized by cervical dislocation. This method is consistent with internationally recognized guidelines for the humane euthanasia of experimental mice, ensuring rapid unconsciousness and minimal pain or distress. All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Ningxia University (approval No. 2023-047). The study was conducted in accordance with local legislation and institutional requirements.

Histological evaluation and immunohistochemistry

Fresh lung tissues were harvested and fixed overnight at 4˚C in 4% paraformaldehyde (Solarbio, Cat. No. P1110). Subsequently, the samples were dehydrated in ethanol and xylene, followed by paraffin embedding and sectioning into 4-µm thick slices. For histological evaluation, hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining was performed at 37˚C for 10 min, and the sections were observed under a light microscope at a magnification of ×100. For immunohistochemical staining, the sections were washed three times with water, and antigen retrieval was performed by microwave treatment in 0.01% sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 15 min. To eliminate non-specific antibody binding, the sections were blocked with 5% BSA in PBS at room temperature for 15 min. The sections were then incubated with the primary antibodies including anti-p-CREB (1:200), anti-CREB (1:200), anti-Cleaved-Caspase 9 (1:200), and anti-GLUT4 (1:200) overnight at 4˚C. After three times washing with PBS, the sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Zhongshan Company) at room temperature for 20 min, and then washed with PBS. The antibody-antigen complexes were visualized using DAB reagent (Zhongshan, China) with a 10 min color development step. Finally, the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin solution (Zhongshan, China) at room temperature for 20 s, washed once with distilled water. The representative images were captured using a light microscope (magnification, ×100; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Statistical analysis

All in vitro cellular data were obtained from at least three independent biological replicates, and in vivo mouse data were collected from six mice per group. Such sample sizes were determined based on our preliminary trials and published analogous reports. All results are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 6.0 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA). Prior to any parametric statistical analysis, we implemented Shapiro‑Wilk test for normality assessment and Levene’s test to examine homogeneity of variance. For statistical comparisons, two‑tailed Student’s t-test was used for pairwise two-group comparisons, and one‑way ANOVA coupled with Tukey’s post‑hoc test was adopted to control the family-wise error rate during multiple-group comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05; *, **, and *** correspond to p < 0.05, p < 0.01 and p < 0.001, respectively.

Results

BCG induces apoptosis and expression of glycolysis in RAW264.7 cells

Growing evidence indicates that glucose metabolism plays a critical role in macrophage-mediated resistance to M.tb infection. Here, we used the attenuated M.tb strain BCG to investigate whether it induces apoptotic responses in macrophages and regulates host cell glycolysis. To exclude non-specific effects of prolonged culture, we included time-matched uninfected controls. As shown in Fig. 1A, the protein levels of PKM2 and G6PD remained stable in the uninfected group over the 24 h culture period, with no significant changes compared to the 0 h time point. Moreover, apoptosis was evaluated using flow cytometry (Fig. 1B). The results demonstrated that the total apoptosis rate in the uninfected group was approximately 6.0% at 0 h and 6.0% at 24 h, showing no significant difference between the two time points. Next, RAW264.7 cells were infected with BCG at an MOI = 10, and samples were collected at different time points (3 h, 6 h, 12 h, 18 h, 24 h). In contrast, western blot analysis showed that BCG significantly upregulated the expression of apoptotic effector molecules (Cleaved-Caspase 9 and Cyt-C) and key rate-limiting enzymes in glycolysis (PKM2, Hexokinase II, and G6PD) in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 1C-H). MTT assay results revealed a gradual decrease in cell viability with prolonged BCG infection (Fig. 1I). Additionally, EdU assays demonstrated a significant decrease in the number of positive cells, indicating that cell proliferation was decreased by BCG infection (Fig. 1J). These findings suggest that BCG infection enhances glycolysis by upregulating related metabolic enzymes and triggers apoptosis in RAW264.7 macrophages.

High glucose conditions promote BCG-induced glycolysis and apoptosis in RAW264.7 cells

To mimic the hyperglycemic microenvironment in diabetic patients, RAW264.7 cells were infected with BCG under either standard glucose (25 mM) or high glucose (HG, 50 mM) conditions. We first investigated the effect of HG on the intracellular bacterial burden of macrophages, as well as the impact of BCG infection on glycolysis and apoptosis-related proteins under HG conditions. CFU assays at 2 h post-infection revealed that higher glucose levels resulted in reduced intracellular BCG burden (Fig. 2A). Western blot analysis showed that compared with the BCG group, the HG + BCG group exhibited significantly increased expression of key glycolytic enzymes (Fig. 2B-C). Consistently, we detected changes in intermediates of glycolysis using commercial kits. The results showed that under HG conditions, BCG infection significantly increased glucose consumption, lactate and pyruvate levels (Fig. 2D-F). Next, we examined apoptosis-related proteins. Under HG conditions, BCG infection further increased apoptotic proteins (Cleaved-Caspase 9 and Cyt-C), inhibited the expression of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2, thereby promoting cell apoptosis (Fig. 2G). Consistent with these findings, cell viability was significantly reduced in the HG + BCG group (Additional file 1 A). To explore the underlying mechanism, we measured intracellular ROS levels. Immunofluorescence staining (Additional file 1B) and flow cytometry (Fig. 2H) both showed that HG conditions enhanced BCG-induced ROS accumulation compared to standard culture glucose controls. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) further revealed that BCG infection caused mitochondrial swelling and cristae disruption, and these morphological abnormalities were more severe under HG conditions (Fig. 2I). We next explored the role of ROS in this process using the ROS scavenger NAC (5 mM, 1 h prior to infection). The results indicated that NAC significantly reduced HG-induced ROS accumulation in BCG-infected cells (Fig. 2J). Importantly, flow cytometry analysis confirmed that NAC treatment also markedly reduced the apoptosis rate (Fig. 2K). To exclude the possibility that the observed effects under high glucose were due to hyperosmotic stress rather than glycolysis itself, we performed additional experiments using 25 mM mannitol as an osmotic control. RAW264.7 cells were infected with BCG under standard glucose (25 mM glucose), HG (50 mM glucose), and standard glucose+mannitol (25 mM glucose + 25 mM mannitol). Key indicators, including PKM2 expression, intracellular ROS production, and apoptosis, were examined. As shown in Additional file 2A-C, the mannitol + BCG control group did not show significant differences in apoptosis, ROS production, or PKM2 expression compared with the BCG group. In contrast, all these parameters were markedly elevated in the HG + BCG group. Collectively, these results demonstrate that high glucose enhances BCG-induced glycolysis and subsequent ROS production, which acts as a critical mediator to aggravate apoptosis in BCG-infected RAW264.7 macrophages.

BCG infection promotes CREB expression and activation in RAW264.7 macrophages

Our previous proteomics data identified CREB as a significantly upregulated protein following BCG infection. To further confirm this observation, RAW264.7 cells were infected with BCG at an MOI of 10 for different time points (3 h, 6 h, 12 h, 18 h, 24 h). Western blot and qRT-PCR assays demonstrated that total CREB expression was upregulated at both the protein and mRNA levels in a time-dependent manner. Notably, BCG infection also significantly increased Ser133 phosphorylation of CREB (Fig. 3A). Consistent with these results, the combined treatment with HG and BCG further enhanced both total CREB expression and the p-CREB/CREB ratio compared with BCG infection alone (Fig. 3B). These results indicate that BCG infection promotes CREB expression and activation in RAW264.7 cells, and high glucose further potentiates this effect.

CREB inhibition attenuates BCG infection under high glucose conditions in vivo

To investigate the contribution of CREB to the exacerbation of BCG infection under HG conditions, we further evaluated glycolysis, pulmonary bacterial colonization, and pathological damage in vivo. After 12 weeks of HFHS feeding, mice in the HG groups developed significant hyperglycemia, as indicated by elevated fasting blood glucose and GSP levels, confirming successful establishment of the HG model (Fig. 4A). Subsequently, all mice were infected with the M.tb attenuated strain BCG via tail vein injection. Mice in the NG + BCG+666 − 15 and HG + BCG+666 − 15 groups received intraperitoneal injections of the CREB inhibitor 666 − 15 every 3 days. Gross observation showed that the HG + BCG group had more severe focal to multifocal white swelling in lung lobes than the NG + BCG group, while lung swelling was significantly reduced in the HG + BCG+666 − 15 group (Fig. 4B). HE staining further confirmed that the HG + BCG group exhibited more marked pathological damage-including interstitial edema, congestion, and massive inflammatory cell infiltration-than the NG + BCG group. In contrast, CREB inhibition significantly alleviated lung injury in both glucose environments (Fig. 4C). CFU assays demonstrated that the HG + BCG group had a significantly higher lung bacterial load than the NG + BCG group. Pharmacological inhibition of CREB significantly decreased the lung bacterial load in the HG + BCG+666 − 15 group and NG + BCG+666 − 15 group compared with the HG + BCG group and NG + BCG group, respectively (Fig. 4D). IHC analysis revealed that the expression levels of p-CREB, CREB, GLUT4, and Cleaved-Caspase 9 proteins in the HG + BCG group were significantly higher than those in the NG + BCG group. Furthermore, CREB inhibition significantly reduced the expression levels of the four proteins in the HG + BCG+666 − 15 group compared with the HG + BCG group; a similar reduction was observed in the NG + BCG+666 − 15 group compared with the NG + BCG group (Fig. 4E). Mouse body weight was monitored weekly for 7 weeks post‑infection across all groups, and no severe or sustained weight loss was observed (Additional file 3), confirming that neither the HFHS diet nor 666‑15 treatment caused overt systemic toxicity. Collectively, these in vivo data indicated that CREB inhibition alleviates glycolysis-related pathological changes and reduces BCG infection under HG conditions, indicating that CREB activity contributes to these processes, though the underlying molecular mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated.

si-CREB decreases BCG-induced glycolysis and apoptosis in RAW264.7 cells under HG conditions

To investigate the role of CREB in BCG-induced apoptosis and glycolysis in RAW264.7 cells, three siRNA sequences were designed and synthesized for targeting CREB. si-CREB#3-transfected RAW264.7 cells exhibited the highest efficiency of CREB knockdown (Fig. 5A). Furthermore, western blot and qRT-PCR results demonstrated that CREB protein and mRNA levels were significantly downregulated in the si-CREB + BCG group compared with the si-NC + BCG group under HG conditions (Fig. 5B). We then examined the impact of CREB on BCG-mediated glycolysis and apoptosis under HG conditions. RAW264.7 cells were transfected with si-CREB or si-NC for 24 h, followed by infection with BCG at an MOI = 10 for 24 h under HG conditions. Western blot analysis showed that si-CREB resulted in markedly decreased expression of GLUT4 and key glycolysis enzymes, including Hexokinase II, LDHA, PKM2 and G6PD in RAW264.7 cells subjected to HG treatment and BCG infection (Fig. 5C-D). qPCR analysis further confirmed that GLUT4 mRNA was selectively upregulated under combined HG + BCG stimulation, and this induction was significantly reversed by CREB silencing. In contrast, GLUT1 mRNA levels remained largely unaltered across all experimental groups (Additional file 4). These findings suggest that GLUT4 represents a CREB-associated and condition-responsive gene in the HG + BCG co-stimulation model. Importantly, we did not investigate whether CREB directly interacts with the proximal promoters of these glycolytic genes; thus, the observed transcriptional changes may arise from direct CREB-DNA binding, indirect downstream regulatory mechanisms, or a combination of both. Consistent with the decrease in the expression of enzymes involved in glycolysis, glucose consumption, lactate production, and pyruvate production in the si-CREB + BCG group were significantly decreased compared with those in the si-NC + BCG group (Fig. 5E-G). Next, we examined the expression of apoptosis-related molecules at protein and mRNA levels. Western blot and qRT-PCR results revealed that compared with the si-NC + BCG group, the si-CREB + BCG group presented lower protein expression and mRNA levels of Cleaved-Caspase 9, Cyt-C, and Bax (Fig. 5H-J). Meanwhile, MTT assay results showed that si-CREB promoted the survival of BCG-infected RAW264.7 macrophages (Fig. 5K). As expected, immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that si-CREB significantly reduced intracellular ROS accumulation in the cells infected with BCG (Fig. 5L). Additionally, morphological observation revealed that si-CREB significantly alleviated mitochondrial swelling and cristae disruption in BCG-infected RAW264.7 cells under HG conditions (Fig. 5M). Collectively, these data demonstrate that CREB knockdown suppresses BCG-induced glycolysis and apoptosis under HG conditions, although the precise mechanism-particularly whether CREB acts directly on the promoters of glycolytic genes or through upstream mediators-remains to be elucidated.

The glycolysis inhibitor 2-DG attenuates BCG-induced macrophage apoptosis

The above findings indicate that a HG environment promotes BCG-induced glycolysis and apoptosis in RAW264.7 cells. Therefore, we used 2-DG, a glycolysis inhibitor, to inhibit glycolysis by acting on hexokinase before BCG infection. To test the effect of 2-DG on BCG-induced apoptosis, cells were treated with different concentrations of 2-DG (0, 5, 8, 10, 15 mM) for 2 h, and then infected with BCG (MOI = 10) for 24 h under HG conditions. Western blotting showed that a concentration of 5 mM significantly inhibited BCG-induced Hexokinase II expression (Fig. 6A). For the combined intervention experiment, cells were first transfected with si-CREB for 24 h, then pretreated with 5 mM 2-DG for 2 h, and finally infected with BCG (MOI = 10) for 24 h under HG conditions. The results showed that 2-DG downregulated the expression of PKM2 and Hexokinase II in infected cells. Compared with the HG + BCG group, the expression levels of PKM2 and Hexokinase II were significantly decreased in the HG + BCG+2-DG group. Furthermore, compared with the HG + si-CREB + BCG group, the combination of si-CREB and 2-DG further suppressed their expression, suggesting that CREB knockdown is associated with decreased expression of these glycolytic enzymes (Fig. 6B-D). Similarly, Western blotting results showed that compared with the HG + BCG group, the expression levels of Bax and Cyt-C proteins were significantly decreased in the HG + BCG+2-DG group, and the combination of si-CREB and 2-DG further inhibited their expression in BCG-infected macrophages compared with the HG + si-CREB + BCG group (Fig. 6E-G). TEM observation revealed that the HG + si-CREB + BCG+2-DG group largely attenuated mitochondrial swelling and cristae rupture compared with the HG + si-CREB + BCG group (Fig. 6H). Moreover, apoptosis was evaluated using flow cytometry (Fig. 6I). The results demonstrated that the HG + si‑CREB + BCG group had a lower apoptosis rate compared with the HG + BCG group. Additional treatment with the glycolysis inhibitor 2‑DG further reduced apoptosis in the HG + si‑CREB + BCG+2‑DG group. We also examined whether 2‑DG or si‑CREB pretreatment could reduce apoptosis indirectly through potential effects on intracellular bacterial burden. Intracellular BCG CFU measured at 2 h post‑infection showed no significant intergroup differences among HG + BCG, HG + BCG+2‑DG, HG + si‑CREB + BCG, and HG + si-CREB + BCG+2-DG groups (Additional file 5). Thus, the observed reduction in apoptosis is not attributable to decreased initial bacterial entry. Collectively, these results indicate that glycolysis plays a critical role in BCG-induced apoptosis, and that CREB contributes to this process.

Discussion

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a leading global cause of morbidity and mortality [1]. Clinical studies have confirmed that TB-diabetes mellitus (DM) comorbidity presents more severe inflammation and organ damage [29]. The prevalence of DM is rising, especially in low‑income countries [30, 31], and prediabetic status favors M.tb survival by impairing host immunity [12, 32–35]. Consistent with these clinical observations, our animal experiments revealed that a high glucose (HG) environment favors the persistence of BCG, an attenuated strain of Mycobacterium tuberculosis (M.tb), in diabetic mice. Exploring the crosstalk between host immune dysfunction and metabolic disorders under TB-DM comorbidity may provide clues for host-directed therapeutic strategies. However, BCG lacks critical virulence factors present in virulent M.tb, and its interaction with host macrophages differs from that of pathogenic M.tb. Therefore, the translational relevance of these findings to clinical tuberculosis requires future validation with virulent M.tb.

Macrophages act as a first line of defense against intracellular mycobacterial infection [36]. Cellular glucose metabolism and metabolic reprogramming are tightly coupled with macrophage immune functions [37, 38]. Previous studies have shown that pathogenic microbes commonly manipulate macrophage glycolysis to disrupt antibacterial immunity. For example, M.tb shifts macrophage metabolism toward aerobic glycolysis via regulating HIF-1α, which impairs phagosome maturation and bactericidal activity, and exacerbates local tissue inflammation [39–41]. Similarly, other pathogens also reprogram macrophage glycolysis to evade host immune clearance [42]. Consistent with these reports, our data showed that BCG infection enhanced glycolysis under HG conditions. The 50 mM glucose concentration used to establish HG conditions was selected based on previous studies investigating hyperglycemia-induced metabolic changes in macrophages. Although this concentration is higher than the glucose levels typically observed in diabetic patients, it provides a robust and reproducible model for studying glucose-induced cellular responses. Osmotic controls confirmed that the observed effects were glucose-dependent rather than caused by hyperosmolarity (Additional file 2). Previous studies have revealed that TB-DM comorbidity exacerbates infection through a dual mechanism, including providing sufficient carbon sources for M.tb, thereby facilitating its colonization and dissemination, while impairing immune cell function and weakening the host anti-tuberculosis defense capacity [43, 44]. These findings prompted us to explore the relationship between glycolytic reprogramming and immune response during BCG infection.

In the present study, we found that BCG infection induced mitochondrial activation and ROS accumulation in macrophages under HG conditions. Previous studies have suggested that increased glycolysis may promote ROS overproduction and subsequent cell apoptosis [45, 46]. Our results suggest that enhanced glycolysis triggered by BCG infection under HG conditions is closely associated with increased ROS accumulation and macrophage apoptosis via the mitochondrial pathway. We further verified this association using 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG), a glycolysis inhibitor, and N-acetylcysteine (NAC), a ROS scavenger. 2-DG treatment suppressed glycolysis, reduced mitochondrial damage and alleviated BCG-induced apoptosis. Similarly, NAC-mediated ROS elimination also markedly relieved macrophage apoptosis. Notably, combined inhibition of glycolysis and CREB exerted a stronger protective effect against apoptosis. These findings indicate that elevated glycolysis is closely associated with BCG-triggered mitochondrial apoptotic signaling in macrophages under HG conditions.

CREB is a key intracellular transcription factor that regulates cellular metabolism, apoptosis, and inflammation, and has been implicated in infectious diseases [47–49]. For instance, the lethal toxin of Bacillus anthracis can directly inhibit CREB-dependent anti-apoptotic signals in macrophages, thereby inducing macrophage apoptosis [50]. Adenylate cyclase gene (Rv0386) of M.tb regulates TNF-α production by mediating CREB phosphorylation, which enhances the survival rate of intracellular bacteria [51]. In addition, high glucose activates the cAMP/PKA/CREB pathway in Müller cells [52]. On the other hand, as a positive regulator of hepatic gluconeogenesis, CREB exerts a key regulatory function in maintaining glucose metabolic homeostasis. For instance, CREB cooperates with other proteins to regulate hepatic glucose production, serving as a core molecule in sustaining gluconeogenesis and systemic glucose homeostasis [53, 54]. Consistent with prior reports, we confirmed that BCG infection markedly increased CREB expression and p-CREB levels in macrophages under HG conditions. In vitro knockdown of CREB reduced the levels of glycolytic proteins, ROS and mitochondrial apoptosis-related molecules, and improved macrophage survival. Our in vivo results using C57BL/6J mice further showed that 666 − 15 treatment significantly reduced p-CREB (Ser133) levels, downregulated glycolytic and apoptotic markers, relieved lung pathological damage and reduced mycobacterial load. Collectively, our data suggest that CREB and its phosphorylation are associated with glycolytic reprogramming, ROS accumulation and mitochondrial apoptosis during BCG infection under HG conditions. These observations are consistent with previous studies that have proposed a role for CREB in oxidative stress and apoptosis under hyperglycemic conditions [55–57]. Our data suggest that CREB is associated with BCG-induced mitochondrial apoptosis in vivo and in vitro under HG conditions. Although our data indicate that CREB is associated with increased expression of key glycolytic enzymes and glycolysis-related metabolic changes during BCG infection under HG conditions, whether this occurs through direct transcriptional activation of glycolytic genes or indirect mechanisms remains to be further clarified. Several limitations of this study should be noted. First, as noted above, BCG lacks critical virulence factors of pathogenic M.tb and its interaction with host macrophages differs. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted within the framework of a BCG infection model, and their relevance to clinical tuberculosis requires further validation using virulent M.tb infection models. Second, the glycolytic changes in this study were inferred from enzyme expression and metabolite levels, which are surrogate markers rather than direct measurements of glycolytic flux. Without isotope tracing, the actual metabolic flux cannot be conclusively determined, and the findings should be interpreted as indicative of glycolytic reprogramming rather than quantitative flux changes. Third, RAW264.7 cells, as an immortalized macrophage cell line, may not fully recapitulate the phenotype and functional responses of primary macrophages. Fourth, uninfected control groups were not included in the in vivo experiments; however, uninfected baseline conditions were extensively characterized in vitro. Fifth, the executioner caspase cascade (cleaved Caspase-3 and PARP) was not directly assessed. Collectively, future studies will combine virulent M.tb, primary macrophages, isotopic flux analysis, macrophage-specific CREB-knockout mice, uninfected in vivo controls, and comprehensive assessment of apoptotic executioner caspases to address these limitations.

Conclusion

In summary, our results show that BCG infection is correlated with increased macrophage apoptosis alongside elevated glycolysis under HG conditions. Furthermore, CREB activation is associated with glycolysis-related metabolic changes, ROS accumulation and mitochondrial apoptosis. Taken together, using a BCG infection model, this study demonstrates that high glucose enhances BCG-induced glycolysis, which is accompanied by increased ROS accumulation and macrophage apoptosis, while CREB activation may contribute to these responses, though the precise regulatory mechanisms await further investigation and validation in virulent M.tb infection models.
