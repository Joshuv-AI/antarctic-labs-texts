Profound hyponatraemia in early-onset preeclampsia: a case report of a rare and complex presentation
Authors: Eleanor Horsburgh, Amanda Lazzaro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhysiological hyponatraemia in pregnancy reflects adaptive changes in fluid homeostasis, with serum sodium levels typically in the range 130–135 mmol/L. Hypertensive disorders affect 3–8% of pregnancies worldwide, and early-onset preeclampsia (<34 weeks of gestation) occurs in approximately 6.3 per 1000 births and is associated with significant maternal and perinatal morbidity. Hyponatraemia has been reported in approximately 9% of pregnancies complicated by preeclampsia and is increasingly recognised as a marker of severe disease; however, its pathophysiology and optimal management remain incompletely defined. This report describes the case of a 32-year-old woman, with type 1 diabetes mellitus, chronic hypertension, and chronic kidney disease, who presented at 24 + 4 weeks of gestation with severe early-onset preeclampsia and profound hyponatraemia (Na 118 mmol/L). Laboratory evaluation indicated hypervolaemic hyponatraemia. Despite fluid restriction, her serum sodium continued to decline, and antihypertensive therapy was maximised. She received antenatal corticosteroids and underwent emergency caesarean section. Perioperative hypertonic saline was administered, and her sodium normalised by day 4 postpartum. The neonate had moderate hyponatraemia that rapidly normalised as well as prematurity-related complications that required management. Severe hyponatraemia in preeclampsia is rare and requires multidisciplinary management, including careful fluid and electrolyte strategy and timely delivery. Serum sodium monitoring should form part of surveillance for patients with severe preeclampsia.Keywords: Preeclampsia, Hyponatraemia, Early-onset preeclampsia, Hypervolaemic hyponatraemia, Hypertonic saline, High-risk pregnancy

Introduction

Physiological hyponatraemia in pregnancy is well recognised and reflects adaptive changes in osmoregulation and plasma volume expansion, resulting in serum sodium levels typically between 130 and 135 mmol/L [1]. Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, including preeclampsia, complicate 3–8% of pregnancies worldwide and remain a leading cause of maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality [1], [2]. Early-onset disease (<34 weeks of gestation) is less common, with an incidence of approximately 6.3 per 1000 births, but is associated with significant adverse outcomes [3].

Hyponatraemia, when defined as serum sodium <130 mmol/L, has been reported in approximately 9% of pregnancies complicated by preeclampsia [4], [5]. Most cases occur in presentations with severe features; however, the pathogenesis remains poorly understood. Retrospective series and literature reviews suggest that abnormal antidiuretic hormone (ADH) regulation, intravascular volume depletion with secondary water retention, endothelial dysfunction, and impaired free water excretion may contribute [4], [5]. Moderate to severe hyponatraemia has been associated with acute kidney injury, fetal growth restriction, and increased maternal admission to intensive care; it may act as a biochemical marker of disease severity, prompting expedited delivery [4].

This report presents a case of severe hypervolaemic hyponatraemia in the setting of severe early-onset preeclampsia, outlines the diagnostic challenges and highlights the management considerations.

Case Presentation

A 32-year-old woman presented for antenatal care in her first ongoing pregnancy. Her obstetric history included a previous first-trimester surgical termination of pregnancy. Her medical history was significant for type 1 diabetes with multiple microvascular complications secondary to suboptimal control during her adolescence, with a glycosylated haemoglobin of 15% at that time. She had biopsy-proven diabetic glomerulonephritis with preexisting nephrotic range proteinuria and a baseline albumin-creatinine ratio of 141 mg/mmol, proliferative retinopathy that had required bilateral vitrectomies and anti-VEGF therapy, peripheral neuropathy, and gastroparesis. She also had chronic hypertension treated with an ACE inhibitor prior to pregnancy.

Prior to conception, her diabetes was well controlled with an insulin pump with 80% time in range and a glycosylated haemoglobin of 6.1%.

Antenatal serology was unremarkable, and cell free fetal DNA showed a low chance of aneuploidy. Low-dose aspirin and calcium were commenced for preeclampsia prophylaxis. Early structural scan and fetal morphology ultrasound were both normal.

The patient presented for a planned antenatal visit and fetal ultrasound at 24 weeks of gestation. She was noted to have worsening hypertension, headaches, and peripheral oedema. Investigations revealed doubling of her proteinuria, a serum sodium of 126 mmol/L and an sFLT-1/PlGF ratio of 206. The remainder of her biochemical investigations were within normal range. Fetal ultrasound showed a fetus that was small for gestational age. She was diagnosed with early-onset preeclampsia and admitted for inpatient management.

Further investigation of her hyponatraemia showed a low serum osmolality with inappropriately concentrated urine and low urine sodium in the setting of clinical hypervolaemia, consistent with dilutional hypervolaemic hyponatraemia [6]. Despite fluid restriction, serum sodium continued to decline and reached a nadir of 118 mmol/L (Fig. 1). Concurrently, there was rapid escalation of her hypertension, requiring five antihypertensive agents. Antenatal corticosteroids were administered in preparation for preterm delivery. A decision for delivery at 24 weeks and 4 days of gestation was made on maternal grounds for refractory hypertension and severe hyponatraemia. Caesarean delivery was performed using a high transverse uterine incision and was otherwise uncomplicated.

Postoperatively, the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit for treatment with hypertonic 3% saline, which was ceased after six hours. Serum sodium normalised on day 4 after delivery.

The patient's blood pressure was controlled by day 10 postpartum on oral antihypertensive therapy. She received six weeks of enoxaparin for venous thromboembolism prophylaxis and underwent outpatient blood pressure monitoring before discharge to her general practitioner on diltiazem.

Placental histopathology demonstrated features of maternal vascular malperfusion consistent with preeclampsia. Antiphospholipid screening was negative, and renal function remained stable at follow-up. She was counselled regarding recurrence risk and recommended early specialist care and low-dose aspirin in future pregnancies. A levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine device was inserted four months postpartum.

The neonate weighed 546 g at delivery and had mild hyponatraemia (127 mmol/L) that normalised within 24 h. The neonatal course was complicated by respiratory distress syndrome, hyaline membrane disease, and intraventricular haemorrhage. Mild mixed respiratory and metabolic acidosis was consistent with extreme prematurity. There were no seizures or clinical features of cerebral oedema during the admission. They were discharged home after 136 days.

Discussion

This patient had multiple established risk factors for the development of preeclampsia, including long-standing type 1 diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, chronic hypertension, and nulliparity. Women with type 1 diabetes mellitus are significantly more likely to develop preeclampsia than the general population, and the presence of diabetic nephropathy further increases this risk [7], [8].

In this context, the diagnosis of superimposed preeclampsia is clinically challenging due to baseline proteinuria and pre-existing hypertension. A greater than 100% increase in proteinuria has been shown to suggest superimposed disease [9]. Angiogenic biomarkers provide useful adjunctive assessment [10]. The sFlt-1/PlGF ratio has been validated for short-term risk stratification, with a threshold ≤38 demonstrating excellent rule-out performance for development of preeclampsia within one week (negative predictive value ∼99%). For rule-in assessment, gestation-specific cut-offs are applied: a ratio ≥ 85 before 34 weeks of gestation is associated with increased likelihood of disease progression within four weeks. Markedly elevated ratios (>200–300), as observed in this case, correlate with early-onset disease, need for delivery within one week, and increased risk of adverse maternal outcomes. In suspected superimposed preeclampsia, a substantially elevated ratio therefore supports true placental-mediated pathology and heightened risk of deterioration despite confounding baseline clinical features [10].

Hyponatraemia in preeclampsia is increasingly recognised as a marker of severe disease, although its pathogenesis remains incompletely understood [11], [12], [13]. Recent observational data demonstrate associations between hyponatraemia and increased emergency antihypertensive therapy, antenatal corticosteroid administration, caesarean delivery, and preterm birth, supporting its role as a marker of disease progression rather than an isolated electrolyte disturbance [11], [12]. Proposed mechanisms include non-osmotic ADH release secondary to reduced effective circulating volume, impaired renal free water excretion, endothelial dysfunction with capillary leak and third-space fluid shifts, and altered placental vasopressinase activity, resulting in dilutional, hypervolaemic hyponatraemia [11], [12], [13].

In this patient, biochemical evaluation demonstrated low serum osmolality with inappropriately concentrated urine and low urine sodium, consistent with a hypervolaemic dilutional state rather than renal salt wasting [11].The patient's daily use of water enemas for management of constipation secondary to gastroparesis may have contributed to excess free water intake; however, given the recognised association between severe preeclampsia and dilutional hyponatraemia in contemporary cohorts, this is the more likely explanation.

Management of hyponatraemia in preeclampsia is not standardised and is guided by severity and symptomatology. Observational data support initial cautious fluid restriction, with hypertonic saline reserved for severe or symptomatic cases, particularly where neurological features are present [11], [12], [13]. Diuretics are generally avoided due to concerns regarding further reduction in effective circulating volume and potential compromise of uteroplacental perfusion [11]. Delivery remains the definitive treatment for preeclampsia and associated biochemical derangements [11], [12], [13]. Most cases demonstrate resolution of hyponatraemia within 24–72 h postpartum, consistent with reversal of placental-mediated pathophysiology [11], [12], [13]. In this case, normalisation required four days, likely reflecting the severity of placental dysfunction and the degree of maternal comorbidity.

This case represents an unusually early presentation of profound hypervolaemic hyponatraemia associated with severe preeclampsia, occurring at 24 + 4 weeks of gestation and reflecting an extreme of both biochemical severity and gestational prematurity [11], [12], [13]. Contemporary cohorts describe hyponatraemia most commonly in preeclampsia with severe features later in gestation, with profound reductions in serum sodium remaining uncommon [11], [13]. It is further distinguished by detailed biochemical characterisation of volume status, complex maternal comorbidity, demonstration of maternal–neonatal sodium correlation, and the use of the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio to objectively support the diagnosis of superimposed preeclampsia.

This case highlights several important clinical learning points. First, serum sodium should be included in biochemical surveillance for women with severe or early-onset preeclampsia, particularly those with renal disease or significant comorbidity [11]. Second, objective placental biomarkers assist in diagnostic clarification when pre-existing hypertension and proteinuria obscure traditional criteria [10]. Third, worsening or refractory hyponatraemia may represent an additional marker of disease severity and should prompt multidisciplinary discussion regarding timing of delivery [11], [12], [13].

Conclusion

Severe hyponatraemia in early-onset preeclampsia is rare. This case highlights the importance of vigilant biochemical monitoring and multidisciplinary management, including appropriate fluid and electrolyte care and timely delivery to optimise outcomes.
