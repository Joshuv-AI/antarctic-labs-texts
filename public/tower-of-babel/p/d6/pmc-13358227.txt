Nursing managers’ experiences of delegation strategies and practices: a qualitative content analysis in Tehran, Iran
Authors: Tahereh Gilvari, Masoud Fallahi-Khoshknab, Hamidreza Khankeh, Abbas Ebadi, Mohammadali Hosseini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveDelegation is a key nursing management skill that enhances staff empowerment, team performance and quality of care. In complex healthcare environments, effective delegation requires structured managerial strategies that balance responsibility with staff empowerment. This study aimed to explore nursing managers’ experiences of delegation strategies and practices in clinical settings.DesignA qualitative study using a conventional content analysis approach. Data were collected between 2024 and 2025 through semistructured, in-depth interviews and analysed concurrently using an inductive content analysis process, allowing iterative refinement of emerging categories.SettingThe study was conducted in selected hospitals in Tehran, Iran.ParticipantsEleven nursing managers were recruited through purposive sampling and sampling continued until data saturation was achieved.ResultsAfter analysing the data, three main categories and seven subcategories emerged. The first category, formalisation and executive framework, included (a) formal communication and introductions in meetings, (b) clarification of task descriptions and delegation objectives and (c) delineation of boundaries and non-delegable authorities. The second category, continuous mentoring and support, encompassed (a) gradual training to consolidate learning and (b) active guidance and advocacy. The third category, supervision and evaluation of the delegation process, comprised (a) performance monitoring to ensure accountability and (b) benchmarking outcomes against established standards and indicators.ConclusionsDelegation allows nursing managers to focus on strategic duties while empowering nurses to participate actively and improve care quality. In challenging clinical settings with workforce shortages, strengthened managerial training and supportive organisational policies are essential. Future research should explore individual, cultural and organisational factors that influence delegation.

Introduction

Delegation is a fundamental managerial skill in nursing and is widely recognised not only as an individual competency but also as a strategic organisational mechanism that empowers staff, enhances team effectiveness and improves the quality of patient care.1 2 In high-demand clinical environments, nursing managers encounter complex responsibilities, heavy workloads and persistent staff shortages.3 Therefore, effective delegation is not merely an operational necessity; it is a strategic decision aimed at achieving organisational goals and enhancing performance.4 Implementing appropriate managerial strategies in delegation allows nursing managers to concentrate on macro-level responsibilities and policy-making while promoting professional growth, active participation and patient safety within the nursing team.1 5

The process of delegation is a complex phenomenon influenced by multiple factors, including organisational structure, leadership styles, staff competence, workplace conditions and the effectiveness of supervisory systems.2 6–8 Within this context, selecting appropriate delegation strategies that align with nurses’ individual and professional characteristics is particularly crucial.9 Additionally, nurses’ personal motivation, professional experience and communication skills significantly impact the effectiveness of delegation within the team.1 10 Previous studies emphasise that effective delegation extends beyond the simple assignment of tasks; it requires clear communication, structured accountability mechanisms and continuous monitoring to ensure the achievement of desired outcomes.4 11

In practice, nursing managers use a range of strategies and approaches for task delegation, which can be either formal and structured or informal and flexible, depending on clinical conditions, staff experience levels and individual management styles.4 9 These strategies aim not only to achieve clinical outcomes but also to enhance nurses’ self-confidence, foster critical thinking and promote professional empowerment.4 A study by Kurt et al in Turkey found that nurses with well-developed delegation skills reported higher job satisfaction, lower stress levels and improved patient outcomes.12 However, a review of previous studies indicates that most research has primarily focused on individual competencies and the barriers or facilitators to delegation, with less attention given to the in-depth exploration of nursing managers’ strategic experiences and the diverse approaches to delegation in clinical settings.13 14

Comprehensive investigations—particularly those conducted in diverse cultural and organisational contexts such as Iran—have been largely overlooked. This oversight is significant because cultural values, organisational hierarchies and social traditions directly influence the selection and implementation of delegation strategies.2 15

Given the critical role of nursing managers in designing and directing delegation strategies, as well as the significance of this process for organisational efficiency and the quality of nursing care, it is essential to investigate their lived experiences from both strategic and operational perspectives. Therefore, this qualitative content analysis study aims to explore the strategies and approaches to delegation in clinical nursing management.

Design

This qualitative study was conducted from 2024 to 2025 to explore an in-depth understanding of nursing managers’ experiences related to strategies and approaches for delegation in clinical settings. Due to the nature of the phenomenon under investigation, a conventional content analysis approach was employed. This method is widely used in qualitative research, particularly within nursing science. It facilitates the extraction of key themes from raw data and offers a structured representation of participants’ lived experiences.16 17

Setting and participants

This study was conducted in three hospitals located in Tehran, selected as the research settings. Two of the hospitals were public and university-affiliated, while one was a private, non-university hospital. This selection aimed to ensure diversity in the organisational and structural contexts of the clinical settings.

The target population consisted of clinical nursing managers working in these hospitals. A total of 11 nurse managers were recruited through purposive sampling. Inclusion criteria were: holding at least a bachelor’s degree in nursing, having a minimum of 5 years of clinical nursing management experience, being actively involved in delegation practices and currently holding a managerial position. Participants were also expected to have the ability to articulate and reflect on their managerial experiences.

The first participant was selected based on their managerial experience and willingness to participate in the study. Subsequently, maximum variation sampling was used to capture a diverse range of experiences regarding delegation strategies and approaches among nursing managers. Participants’ characteristics

Data collection

Data were collected through in-depth, semistructured interviews. All interviews were conducted individually and face-to-face at mutually agreed on times and locations. Before each session, the study objectives were clearly explained, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Based on an interview guide developed by the research team, each interview commenced with broad, open-ended questions, such as: Please describe your experience with delegation in clinical settings, and What strategies or approaches do you use when delegating responsibilities to nurses? During the interviews, probing questions like Could you explain more about that? were employed to elicit deeper and richer data. At the conclusion of each interview, participants were asked, Is there anything else you would like to add or discuss regarding this topic? to ensure that all relevant experiences were captured. The duration of the interviews varied based on participants’ willingness, averaging around 60 min (see the interview guide in online supplemental file 1).

All interviews were conducted by the primary researcher, who had prior professional experience in clinical nursing management and academic involvement in nursing research. While this insider position provided contextual familiarity and facilitated communication with participants, it also carried the potential risk of interpretive assumptions and influence on participant responses. To address these concerns, several reflexive strategies were systematically employed throughout the study. During data collection, efforts were made to minimise potential power imbalances by adopting a neutral, non-directive and participant-centred interviewing approach. Participants were repeatedly reassured that their responses would remain confidential, participation was entirely voluntary, and there were no right or wrong answers. Leading or judgmental questions were intentionally avoided, and participants were encouraged to openly describe both positive and challenging experiences related to delegation practices. To further minimise potential researcher bias during data interpretation, reflexive journaling was maintained throughout the research process to critically examine emerging assumptions, preconceptions and analytical decisions. In addition, peer debriefing and regular collaborative discussions within the research team were used to challenge interpretations, enhance analytical transparency and strengthen confirmability of the findings.

Data analysis

In this study, data were analysed using the conventional content analysis approach based on the five-step process proposed by Graneheim and Lundman.18 First, the audio-recorded interviews were transcribed verbatim. To gain an in-depth understanding of the content, the transcripts were read multiple times to obtain a comprehensive sense of the data. In the next step, meaning units were identified and labelled with appropriate codes, considering the overall context of the text. A list of codes was developed, and through constant comparison and review of similarities and differences, similar codes were grouped into subcategories.16 18 Related subcategories were then merged into main categories. Throughout the analysis process, the research team continuously discussed, reviewed and refined the coding and categorisation to ensure the rigour and trustworthiness of the findings. Codes were generated inductively from meaning units and continuously compared and refined to form subcategories and categories. For example, codes such as ‘providing clear task instructions’ and ‘explaining expected outcomes’ were grouped under the subcategory ‘clarification of task descriptions,’ which contributed to the main category ‘formalisation and executive framework’. The coding tree and details of the analysis process are provided in online supplemental file 2.

Although an inductive conventional content analysis approach was used, theoretical frameworks (Kanter, Edmondson, and French and Raven) were not applied a priori for coding. Instead, they were used as interpretive lenses during the discussion phase to contextualise and deepen the understanding of the emergent categories.

Data collection continued until saturation was achieved. Saturation was assessed through ongoing concurrent analysis, whereby each interview was coded and compared with previous interviews. Data collection ceased when no new codes or concepts emerged, existing categories became repetitive, and sufficient depth had been achieved to fully understand the properties and dimensions of the categories. This level of saturation is consistent with empirical evidence suggesting that studies with relatively homogeneous samples and focused research objectives commonly achieve saturation within a similar range of interviews.19 All interviews were conducted in Persian and transcribed verbatim. Translations into English were performed during transcription and subsequently reviewed by two bilingual researchers to ensure semantic and conceptual equivalence.

Trustworthiness of data

To ensure the trustworthiness of the data, this study applied Lincoln and Guba’s criteria, which include credibility, dependability, transferability and confirmability. To enhance credibility, the researcher engaged in prolonged involvement in the interview process, allowing for the collection of in-depth and reliable data. Maximum variation sampling was also employed. Dependability was ensured through the immediate transcription of interviews, simultaneous data collection and analysis, and member checking, where the initial coding of some interviews was shared with participants to confirm the alignment of findings with their actual experiences. Confirmability was established through careful documentation of the research process and evaluation of findings by two faculty members. Finally, transferability was strengthened by providing direct quotes from participants.18 20

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

A total of 11 nursing managers participated in this study, including 2 matrons, 6 supervisors and 3 head nurses. All participants, except for two who held a bachelor’s degree, possessed a master’s degree in nursing. Their ages ranged from 39 to 56 years, with an average work experience of 22 years and approximately 10 years of managerial experience. Strategies and practices of delegation by nursing managers

Formalisation and executive framework

One of the first and fundamental strategies highlighted in the experiences of nursing managers was formalisation and the development of clear executive frameworks for the delegation process. This category reflects the way nursing managers implement delegation in a structured and formalised context to prevent ambiguity or role conflicts. Such an approach not only enhances clarity in the distribution of responsibilities but also fosters trust and greater acceptance of the delegation process among staff, ensuring that all organisational members are aware of and adhere to these frameworks. A systematic structure clearly defines tasks, authorities and limitations, providing a foundation for their consistent and coordinated implementation.

This category comprises the following subcategories: Formal communication and introductions in meetings, Clarification of task descriptions and delegation objectives and Delineation of boundaries and non-delegable authorities.

Formal communication and introduction in meetings

Participants emphasised that issuing written directives and formally introducing the responsible individual in meetings enhances the legitimacy of the delegation process. This approach signals to staff that the delegated responsibility is taken seriously and helps prevent role conflict while strengthening collaboration within the nursing team.

Most of the time, we have an official substitute staff member with a written directive. This ensures that others know they have authority and responsibility. If the delegation is only verbal, many problems can arise; especially when disagreements occur, only the official directive can serve as the reference point for action. (P2)

If the delegation is only verbal, many problems can arise; especially when disagreements occur, only the official directive can serve as the reference point for action. (P2)

When I am going to delegate a responsibility to someone, I always place an official directive in their file and announce it in front of the team. Everyone should know who is responsible for what, and with an official directive, I find it easier to follow up on tasks. (P7)

Clarification of task descriptions and delegation objectives

Participants observed that clearly and unambiguously defined delegated tasks enhance staff performance by providing a precise understanding of their authority and expectations. This clarification not only mitigates misinterpretations but also encourages active participation in decision-making processes.

In the first session, I clarified several matters for them. For example, I explained their detailed responsibilities, which included preparing shift schedules, coordinating leaves, liaising with the on-call physician, and addressing potential staff complaints. (P4)

Clarifying tasks is the most important aspect of delegation. I typically discuss various scenarios and situations with my staff, providing examples and encouraging them to ask questions. Together, we agree on the specific tasks to be completed and the corresponding timeframe. (P5)

When I want to assign a task to a staff member, I go over all my duties and expectations with them line by line. Simply telling them ‘You are responsible for this task’ is not enough; they must know exactly what needs to be done, what outcomes are expected, and by when. (P10)

Delineation of boundaries and non-delegable authorities

Nursing managers highlighted the importance of delegating certain responsibilities; however, they underscored that specific tasks must be managed directly by them. These tasks commonly relate to legal matters, safety concerns, media relations and the professional credibility and standing of the manager.

There are some responsibilities that I prefer to keep myself, such as the final evaluation of educational instructors. Even if others help with parts of the process, I feel responsible for the final decision and its consequences. (P3)

Continuous mentoring and support

The findings suggest that mentoring and ongoing support provided by nursing managers are critical strategies in the delegation process. Within this framework, managers work closely with nurses by delivering continuous training, responding to questions and providing practical guidance in complex situations.

This category includes two subcategories: Gradual training to consolidate learning and Active guidance and advocacy.

Gradual training to consolidate learning

In this approach, responsibility is gradually transferred, accompanied by continuous training, allowing the individual to acquire the necessary skills and knowledge in a deep and lasting way. This method enables learners not only to gradually absorb new information but also to consolidate and apply it alongside their existing skills. Gradual training provides ample opportunities to ask questions, clarify doubts and gain practical experience, thereby preventing forgetfulness or momentary errors. After responsibility is delegated, training continues until nurses achieve full mastery.

Whenever I delegate a responsibility, I explain everything step by step, from the beginning to the end. I try to make sure they understand why each part of the work is important, so they feel more confident and can gradually handle the responsibility on their own. (P6)

Whenever I delegate a responsibility, I explain everything step by step, from the beginning to the end. I try to make sure they understand why each part of the work is important, so they feel more confident and can gradually handle the responsibility on their own. (P6)

After I delegate a responsibility, I stay beside them during the first few shifts and show them how things should be done. Every couple of weeks we sit together and talk about any problems they have faced. I continue this support until I feel confident they can manage the work on their own. (P9)

After I delegate a responsibility, I stay beside them during the first few shifts and show them how things should be done. Every couple of weeks we sit together and talk about any problems they have faced. I continue this support until I feel confident they can manage the work on their own. (P9)

In my experience, people should not be given full responsibility all at once. I first let them do the work step by step and stay involved until I see they are comfortable and capable. As they gain experience, I gradually step back and give them more independence. (P11)

Active guidance and advocacy

This subcategory encompasses the experiences of managers who perceived their roles not solely as delegators of responsibility but as active supporters and advisors working collaboratively with nurses. Support was demonstrated through responding to questions, providing immediate feedback and offering practical solutions to challenges.

During the first days after delegation, I stay close to the person and keep an eye on how things are going. If they are unsure about something or run into a problem, I try to help immediately instead of waiting until the work is finished. (P6)

Support is not just telling staff that everything will be fine. I try to give them opportunities to do the work themselves while I am nearby. If they make a mistake or feel uncertain, I can step in, guide them, and help them learn from the experience. (P8)

It is important to me that staff know they are not alone. When they take on a new responsibility, I want them to feel that they can come to me if they face a problem. This gives them confidence to accept responsibility while still knowing that they are accountable for the work. (P11)

Supervision and evaluation of the delegation process

Participants emphasised that delegation requires systematic monitoring and evaluation rather than task abandonment. Managers used performance tracking and feedback to ensure accountability and improve outcomes. Although performance monitoring often occurred alongside mentoring and supportive interactions, participants described it as a distinct aspect of delegation focused on accountability, adherence to standards and evaluation of outcomes. In contrast, mentoring and support were primarily directed toward guidance, confidence-building and problem-solving.

Performance monitoring to ensure accountability

A common strategy used by nursing managers to maintain delegation quality is the regular monitoring of nurses’ performance. Managers systematically and continuously observe and evaluate staff performance and delegated processes. This approach reinforces that accountability is a fundamental component of the delegation process.

Accountability is the golden principle of delegation. Therefore, every time I delegate a task, I set specific times to review progress and provide feedback. Regular monitoring keeps staff committed to their responsibilities and motivates them to take their tasks more seriously. (P5)

Accountability is the golden principle of delegation. Therefore, every time I delegate a task, I set specific times to review progress and provide feedback. Regular monitoring keeps staff committed to their responsibilities and motivates them to take their tasks more seriously. (P5)

We cannot just delegate responsibility and then leave it to chance. We need to monitor step by step how the individual is progressing and ensure they are accountable for their work. Just as I must report to my superior, they must also be accountable to me and deliver quality work. (P10)

Benchmarking outcomes against established standards and indicators

A primary approach to monitoring and evaluation involves comparing delegation outcomes with established standards and indicators. The application of formal tools, checklists and professional indicators enables nursing managers to assess staff performance in relation to organisational standards.

To evaluate the outcomes of delegation, I always rely on objective data and professional indicators. For example, I review the achievement of accreditation indicators, results of quality assessments, and the number of in-service trainings conducted. Comparing these data with departmental standards helps me accurately identify the strengths and weaknesses of the process and propose improvement strategies. (P4)

Discussion

This study explored nursing managers’ experiences of delegation strategies and practices in clinical settings. The findings suggest that delegation is not merely an administrative task but a complex socioorganisational process shaped by managerial authority, relational trust and contextual constraints.1 2 21 Rather than occurring as isolated actions, delegation functions as an integrated practice involving role clarification, capacity building and supervisory oversight through which managers negotiate accountability while promoting staff autonomy.4 5 22 23 Delegation therefore emerges as both a structural and relational practice influenced by organisational hierarchies, professional autonomy and trust.23–26 This interpretation aligns with structural empowerment theory and relational leadership perspectives, which emphasise access to support, information, trust and ongoing interaction.27–29 These theoretical perspectives were used as interpretive lenses to contextualise and deepen understanding of the findings and were not employed as a priori analytical frameworks during coding or category development.

A key finding of this study highlights the importance of role clarity and structured delegation frameworks.1 2 11 The findings suggest that unclear boundaries are not simply procedural gaps but sources of organisational uncertainty that can undermine accountability and disrupt clinical coordination.2 5 14 From a theoretical perspective, this reflects the function of structural power in shaping delegation practices, whereby managers actively define and enforce boundaries to maintain control while enabling task distribution.30 Consistent with Moradi et al, ambiguity in expectations contributes to informal delegation patterns1; however, the present study extends this understanding by demonstrating that managers deliberately use clarification strategies as a form of preventive governance to reduce role conflict and strengthen accountability within clinical teams. In contrast to healthcare systems where delegation is regulated through formal institutional frameworks,11 the findings indicate that, in the Iranian context, role clarity is largely dependent on individual managerial agency rather than system-level standardisation.31 32 Recent evidence also suggests that nurses frequently retain tasks that could be delegated because of uncertainty regarding role boundaries, accountability and trust in delegated performance, reinforcing the importance of clear delegation structures and expectations.33

Effective delegation was also shaped by relational processes, with mentoring and continuous support proving essential for fostering trust and staff competence.2 22 34 Delegation was not experienced as a simple transfer of tasks but as a dynamic, trust-based interaction in which responsibility is progressively negotiated.24–26 Through feedback, guidance and ongoing support, managers fostered both competence and professional trust among staff.22 35 36 This aligns with the concept of relational delegation, where authority is co-constructed through interaction and mutual confidence.28 While previous studies, such as Abd et al, have emphasised mentoring as a strategy for learning and performance improvement,22 the present findings further demonstrate that mentoring functions as a trust-building infrastructure, enabling managers to extend responsibility while maintaining confidence in outcomes.23 24 26 In this way, delegation supports managerial efficiency while simultaneously fostering professional development, confidence and organisational learning.22 23 37

Building on the relational processes described above, gradual and structured training further facilitated staff development by enabling a staged transition towards professional autonomy.37 Managers reported using incremental responsibility models to prepare staff for independent practice, reflecting a scaffolded delegation process in which competence development precedes full responsibility transfer.37 Although previous research has highlighted the importance of continuous training in managerial preparedness,35 the present study adds a more interpretive perspective by identifying staged delegation as a risk-management strategy. By progressively increasing autonomy, managers reduce uncertainty while supporting safe skill acquisition and sustainable staff development.37 These findings suggest that delegation is not simply an endpoint of competence but a developmental process through which competence itself is cultivated.22 This interpretation is supported by recent qualitative evidence demonstrating that delegation competence develops progressively through experience, guided practice, mentoring and ongoing support rather than through formal instruction alone.38

Systematic monitoring and evaluation emerged as essential components of effective delegation, highlighting the dual role of supervision in supporting accountability and organisational learning.37 Managers used performance monitoring to ensure standards while simultaneously providing constructive feedback to improve practice.36 This reflects a control–learning dynamic in which supervision simultaneously constrains and enables professional growth.37 While existing literature, including Nkomazana et al, emphasises the role of supervision in maintaining care quality,36 the present findings extend this understanding by conceptualising supervision as a feedback-driven learning system that reinforces continuous improvement within clinical teams.

The effectiveness of these delegation strategies, however, was strongly influenced by organisational culture. The findings indicate that trust, open communication and psychological safety are fundamental prerequisites for successful delegation.24 26 In environments where staff perceive delegation as fair, transparent and supportive, they are more likely to accept responsibility willingly and perform autonomously.23 26 This observation aligns with Edmondson’s concept of psychological safety, which emphasises the importance of safe interpersonal environments for learning, participation and performance.27 The present findings therefore suggest that delegation effectiveness depends not only on managerial competence but also on the broader socioemotional climate of the organisation.

Cultural and systemic factors further shaped delegation practices. Within the Iranian healthcare context, hierarchical organisational structures and high-power distance significantly influenced how delegation was enacted.31 39 Decision-making tended to remain centralised, and professional interactions were shaped by norms of authority and obedience, potentially limiting open dialogue and negotiation of responsibilities.26 39 These findings suggest that delegation is shaped by broader cultural norms regarding hierarchy and authority. From a theoretical perspective, this reflects established models of power and influence in organisations, particularly the framework proposed by French and Raven, in which delegation involves both structural authority and relational negotiation.30 In such contexts, delegation may remain task-oriented rather than empowerment-oriented, potentially limiting its developmental potential.22 23

The current findings provide a basis for interpretation through established theoretical frameworks in leadership and organisational behaviour. Delegation in nursing management may be understood as a dynamic balance between control and autonomy, reflecting principles of empowerment theory (Kanter), in which access to support, information and organisational resources enhances employees’ capacity to perform effectively.22 23 29 In this context, mentoring and gradual responsibility transfer function as mechanisms of structural empowerment.22 29 Moreover, the findings support relational leadership perspectives by highlighting the importance of trust and psychological safety in facilitating effective delegation and professional autonomy.24 26 Delegation therefore operates not only as a managerial technique but also as a sociorelational process shaped by trust, power and organisational context.2 21 24

Integrating these findings, delegation in nursing management can be conceptualised as a multidimensional process operating through three interrelated mechanisms: (1) structural formalisation through role clarity and defined boundaries, (2) relational development through mentoring and trust-building and (3) regulatory supervision through monitoring and feedback systems. In the studied context, these mechanisms were largely driven by individual managerial initiative rather than formal institutional policies. This contrasts with healthcare systems where delegation is guided by standardised frameworks and national regulatory standards, such as those proposed by the American Nurses Association, highlighting a gap between practice and policy.11 Consistent with this view, recent evidence has shown that effective delegation is closely linked to managerial effectiveness and time-management capacity, highlighting delegation as a core leadership competency rather than merely a task-distribution strategy.40

From an international perspective, reliance on individual managerial discretion rather than formalised delegation frameworks has important implications.6 11 In many high-income healthcare systems, delegation is supported by formal guidelines, competency frameworks and regulatory standards that enhance consistency, accountability and patient safety.8 11 37 In contrast, delegation in the present study relied more heavily on managerial discretion and experiential decision-making. Integrating context-sensitive adaptations of international best practices may therefore strengthen delegation processes by improving standardisation, supporting workforce development and enhancing patient safety, particularly in resource-constrained or hierarchical healthcare settings.11 37

Despite its contributions, this study should be interpreted with consideration of several methodological and contextual limitations. The findings are based on self-reported experiences, which may be influenced by recall bias and social desirability effects, potentially leading participants to emphasise idealised rather than actual delegation practices. Nevertheless, in-depth probing during interviews helped to capture more nuanced accounts of real-world practice. The study included a relatively small sample (n=11), consistent with qualitative research focused on depth rather than breadth, which may limit the range of experiences across different managerial contexts; however, sampling continued until data saturation was achieved, ensuring comprehensive coverage of emerging categories and subcategories. Conducted within a single metropolitan area (Tehran), the study’s findings may reflect contextual specificities and thus may not be fully transferable to rural or less-resourced healthcare settings. Variations in organisational structures, resource availability and workforce composition across regions should be considered when interpreting these results. Some findings may be particularly shaped by the Iranian healthcare context and its hierarchical organisational culture. For example, the strong emphasis on role formalisation, explicit boundary setting, managerial accountability and reliance on individual managerial discretion may reflect organisational environments characterised by centralised decision-making and higher power distance. In contrast, findings related to mentoring, gradual competence development, supportive guidance, supervision and feedback mechanisms may have broader relevance across diverse healthcare systems. Therefore, rather than assuming direct transferability, readers are encouraged to evaluate the fit and resonance of these findings within their own organisational and cultural contexts.

Furthermore, by focusing exclusively on nursing managers, the study provides rich insights into managerial perspectives but does not capture the experiences of staff nurses who receive delegated responsibilities. This should be considered a structural limitation of the present conceptual framework, as the findings primarily reflect managerial interpretations of delegation. Given the inherently relational nature of delegation, inclusion of delegatees’ perspectives might have revealed additional experiences, expectations and challenges associated with delegated responsibilities. Consequently, the conceptual framework presented in this study may have evolved differently if both delegators’ and delegatees’ perspectives had been incorporated. Finally, hierarchical organisational structures may have affected the openness of participants’ responses, particularly regarding sensitive managerial practices, a contextual factor inherent to many clinical environments that should be taken into account when interpreting the findings.

Conclusions

This study provides one of the first in-depth qualitative insights into nursing managers’ experiences of delegation within the Iranian healthcare context. The findings demonstrate that delegation is not only a managerial skill but also a strategic and relational process that contributes to staff empowerment, accountability and the quality and safety of patient care. The results highlight the importance of structured training, clear role definition and supportive organisational policies to enhance effective delegation practices. Future research should adopt more rigorous and diverse designs to build on these findings. For example, mixed-methods studies integrating qualitative insights with quantitative measures, such as staff empowerment, job satisfaction and patient outcomes, could provide a more comprehensive evaluation of delegation effectiveness. In addition, interventional studies, such as the development and evaluation of structured delegation training programmes or mentorship-based delegation models, are recommended to assess their impact on managerial performance and clinical outcomes. Cross-cultural comparative studies are also needed to examine how organisational structures and cultural factors influence delegation practices across different healthcare systems.
