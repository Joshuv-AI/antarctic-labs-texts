A micro-CT–integrated 3D simulation framework reveals fluid transport mechanisms and void dynamics in root canal fillings
Authors: Amir Raoof, Maryam Raoof, Hossein Fathi, Behdad Pouran, Reza Derakhshani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA micro-CT–integrated three-dimensional simulation-assisted leakage analysis methodology (3D-SALAM) was developed to address the long-standing limitations of destructive, poorly reproducible leakage tests traditionally used to evaluate the sealing performance of root canal fillings. Despite decades of research, the long-term stability of root canal fillings remains uncertain because existing in vitro models fail to capture the complex, three-dimensional transport of fluids through micro-void networks. To bridge this methodological gap, the present study introduces and demonstrates 3D-SALAM—an innovative, non-destructive platform that integrates high-resolution micro-computed tomography with computational fluid dynamics to mechanistically characterise void geometry, connectivity, and fluid transport behaviour within obturated teeth. Micro-CT datasets (10 μm voxel size) from human single-rooted teeth were converted into numerical meshes for finite-volume simulations, systematically varying surface wettability (contact angle 25°–150°), injection velocity, and applied pressure. These simulations are presented as a proof-of-concept application of the workflow, illustrating how hydrophilic surfaces achieved up to 92% void saturation with less than 10% trapped air, while hydrophobic domains retained over 25%. Medium injection velocities produced optimal filling efficiency by balancing capillary and viscous forces, and applied pressure accelerated transport but induced pronounced local concentration gradients. This proof-of-concept study demonstrates that 3D-SALAM enables reproducible, quantitative, and mechanistic mapping of fluid dynamics in complex root canal filling architectures. Beyond characterising initial obturation quality, this approach provides a methodological foundation for longitudinal evaluation of material degradation, interfacial stability, and fluid-mediated transport phenomena. The framework’s adaptability also extends its relevance to a wide range of porous and composite fillings in regenerative medicine and biomedical engineering, where void connectivity and capillary behaviour critically influence long-term performance.Keywords: Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT), Computational fluid dynamics (CFD), Void connectivity, Capillary transportSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Each year, more than 24 million endodontic procedures are performed worldwide1. The long-term success of these treatments depends on achieving a durable, hermetic seal of the root canal system to prevent bacterial ingress and egress between the oral cavity and periapical tissues2. Inadequate sealing is implicated in up to 60% of endodontic failures3. Although gutta-percha (GP) combined with a sealer remains the most widely used obturation system, its lack of natural adhesion to dentin and the technical complexity of canal preparation make void-free fillings difficult to achieve4–6.

Numerous in vitro techniques have been employed to evaluate the effectiveness of root canal fillings, including dye penetration, bacterial culture, glucose transport, and fluid filtration7. Each of these methods has well-documented shortcomings. Glucose may react with some sealers8; bacterial models are prone to confounding through alternative leakage routes or broth contamination9; and antimicrobial activity of filling materials may obscure results10. Dye-based methods, particularly those using methylene blue, have been widely adopted for their simplicity and low cost11. However, they are destructive, prone to artefacts such as trapped air, and challenging to standardise12. Additional issues include dye decolorisation13–15 and variability in outcomes depending on whether vacuum or passive conditions are applied16–18. Considerable variation in pressure application protocols across studies16,19,20, together with the frequent use of artificial substrates such as glass or resin rather than natural dentin21, further complicates interpretation.

These limitations have raised concerns about the reliability and reproducibility of leakage-focused studies11,22. Alternative approaches such as fluid transport and capillary flow porometry have been explored to quantify through-and-through voids22–28. Yet their clinical relevance remains limited, in part due to the influence of water’s high surface tension24 and the high pressures required to displace entrapped air24.

Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) has advanced the field by enabling non-destructive, three-dimensional quantification of filling and void volumes29. While extensively applied to evaluate obturation quality1, void percentage alone shows weak correlation with outcomes obtained from conventional leakage tests30. Editorials in leading journals have highlighted that existing methods often lack reproducibility, standardisation, and demonstrated clinical significance, and have called for new approaches capable of generating data with stronger links to long-term performance31,32.

Attempts to relate ex vivo measurements of sealing quality to clinical outcomes, such as periapical health or treatment success, have yielded inconclusive results33,34. These inconsistencies may reflect methodological insensitivity rather than an absence of correlation. Meanwhile, persistent problems such as interfacial instability, leakage, and limited biocompatibility of filling materials remain unresolved11. There is therefore a critical need for reproducible, non-destructive tools that can characterise void connectivity within complex canal anatomies and monitor changes in material behaviour over time. The proposed framework is not intended to replace conventional leakage, dye penetration, or fluid transport tests, which remain valuable for routine, low-complexity screening and comparative assessments. Instead, 3D-SALAM is positioned as a complementary, research-oriented methodology designed to address questions that simpler techniques cannot resolve, such as three-dimensional void connectivity, spatially heterogeneous transport pathways, and the mechanistic influence of wettability and flow conditions. Owing to its imaging and computational requirements, the framework is expected to be applied primarily in specialised research settings, where detailed, non-destructive, and mechanistic analysis is required to support material development, validation, or hypothesis-driven investigation.

This study introduces a three-dimensional simulation-assisted analysis methodology (3D-SALAM) that integrates high-resolution micro-CT imaging with computational fluid dynamics to model fluid transport behaviour within the void spaces of obturated human teeth. While the combination of micro-computed tomography and image-based numerical simulation has long been established in fields such as the geosciences and porous media research35, its application to endodontic fillings presents distinct challenges related to anatomical complexity, material heterogeneity, wettability, and clinically relevant transport conditions. The 3D-SALAM framework adapts these established techniques into a unified, non-destructive workflow specifically designed to analyse void geometry, connectivity, and fluid transport within obturated root canals. In this context, the workflow’s contribution lies in its domain-specific integration and clinical translation, rather than in the introduction of a fundamentally new imaging or modelling methodology. By systematically varying parameters such as void geometry, surface wettability, and injection conditions, the method seeks to overcome limitations of traditional tests and provide longitudinally relevant, non-destructive insights into filling material performance. We hypothesised that void space geometry, surface contact angle, and injection parameters significantly influence fluid dynamics within voids, and that 3D-SALAM can reproducibly quantify these effects with potential translation to clinically relevant leakage scenarios.

Methods

This study followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Laboratory studies in Endodontology (PRILE) 2021 guidelines (Fig. 1). Ethical approval was not required, as the teeth had been previously extracted for periodontal reasons and voluntarily donated for educational or research purposes.

The study design according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Laboratory studies in Endodontology (PRILE), 2021 guidelines.

Sample selection and size justification

Four recently extracted, intact, human single-rooted teeth were included. This sample size was chosen for this proof-of-concept study to allow detailed micro-CT scanning, segmentation, and computational simulation within available computational resources, while enabling assessment of method reproducibility across different anatomical variations. Inclusion criteria were: single canal confirmed radiographically, mature apical foramen (Vertucci Type I), curvature < 20° (Schneider’s method), and absence of fractures, root caries, external resorption, or prior endodontic treatment. Teeth not meeting these criteria were excluded.

Specimen preparation

Prior to instrumentation, all teeth were scaled ultrasonically to remove calculus and soft tissue, then stored in isotonic saline. Each specimen was inspected under ×20 magnification (dental microscope, Zumax, China) to exclude defects. Decoronation was performed at the cementoenamel junction (CEJ) using a high-speed diamond bur under water cooling.

Working length (WL) was established by introducing a size #15 K-file (Maillefer, Dentsply, Switzerland) until visible at the apical foramen and subtracting 1 mm. A crown-down preparation was completed with RaCe rotary instruments (FKG Dentaire, Switzerland) in the following sequence: 40/0.10, 35/0.08, 25/0.04, 25/0.06, and 30/0.06. Between instruments, canals were irrigated with 2 mL of 5% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) delivered via a 30-gauge NaviTip™ needle (Ultradent Products, USA), positioned 3 mm short of WL. A final rinse of 2 mL 17% EDTA for 5 min, followed by 2 mL distilled water, completed the cleaning protocol.

Obturation was performed using cold lateral compaction with a size 30/0.04 GP master cone and AH26 sealer applied with a lentulo spiral. Accessory cones (size 20/0.02) were placed until the spreader penetrated only to the coronal third. To deliberately preserve detectable voids for analysis, final vertical condensation was omitted. Radiographs (bucco-lingual and mesio-distal) confirmed obturation quality. Cavit™ (3 M ESPE, USA) was placed as a temporary coronal seal. Specimens were stored at 37 °C and 100% humidity for 72 h.

Micro-CT image acquisition and reconstruction

Micro-CT scanning was performed using a U-CTUHR system (MILabs B.V., The Netherlands) operated at 65 kV and 180 µA in Ultra Focus magnification mode. An aluminium beam-hardening filter was applied during acquisition to attenuate low-energy X-ray photons and reduce beam-hardening artefacts. Each specimen was scanned over a full 360° rotation around the vertical axis with a rotation step of 0.2°, ensuring isotropic angular sampling.

Raw projection data were reconstructed using the Feldkamp–Davis–Kress (FDK) algorithm, with manufacturer-recommended beam-hardening correction settings and a Hann pre-filter applied to suppress high-frequency noise. The resulting datasets had an isotropic voxel size of 10 μm and were used consistently for all subsequent segmentation and computational analyses. An isotropic voxel size of 10 μm was selected as a balance between spatial resolution, field of view, and image quality required to scan entire human roots while maintaining adequate signal-to-noise ratio. This resolution does not represent the technical limit of the scanner, but was chosen to reliably resolve micrometre-scale voids and interconnected pathways within the obturation mass across the full specimen volume. Quantitative analysis showed that the majority of resolved voids had characteristic dimensions substantially larger than the voxel size, ensuring adequate representation of void geometry and connectivity for subsequent segmentation and transport simulations.

Segmentation and void geometry analysis

Micro-CT datasets were imported into Fiji (ImageJ) and segmented using a semi-automated thresholding protocol optimised for GP and sealer differentiation. Segmentation reproducibility was assessed by re-segmenting two randomly selected specimens one week apart. 3D visualisation and void volume quantification were performed using the 3D Viewer plugin. Void geometry parameters (volume, surface area, connectivity) were extracted, and voids were classified as: (a) isolated, (b) interconnected coronal, or (c) interconnected apical.

Computational simulation of fluid transport

Segmented void geometries were converted into stereolithography (STL) meshes and refined in MeshLab. The computational meshes were generated directly from the segmented void geometries and consisted predominantly of unstructured polyhedral cells derived from the voxel-based surfaces, rather than regular hexahedral elements. Local mesh refinement ensured that cell sizes were comparable to or smaller than the smallest resolved void dimensions in the micro-CT datasets. This approach allowed accurate representation of void boundaries, interconnections, and curvature, while maintaining numerical stability and sufficient resolution to capture velocity gradients, interfacial dynamics, and capillary–viscous effects within the resolved void network. Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations were performed using OpenFOAM (v8) with the finite volume method. The incompressible Navier–Stokes equations were solved, coupled with advection–diffusion equations for tracer transport.

Across all simulations, flow conditions remained within the laminar regime. Based on local characteristic void diameters (tens to several hundreds of micrometres) and simulated injection velocities, the Reynolds number ranged from < 10⁻³ in diffusion- and capillary-dominated conditions to approximately 10⁻¹ in the highest-velocity cases. These values are well below the threshold for inertial flow, indicating that viscous and capillary forces dominated transport behaviour. Corresponding capillary numbers likewise remained low, consistent with fluid movement observed during endodontic irrigation, leakage testing, and coronal fluid ingress under clinical conditions. The selected velocity and pressure ranges therefore reflect physiologically and experimentally relevant transport regimes rather than extreme or non-physical flow conditions. Flux boundary conditions were applied at the coronal entry, with the apical outlet set to atmospheric pressure. Simulations comprised more than 100,000 cells to ensure numerical stability, with a Courant number ≤ 0.2, and were run in parallel for computational efficiency. Convective flow was generated by applying a constant pressure difference of 10 kPa at the coronal inlet, while simulations without applied pressure represented diffusion-dominated conditions. This pressure magnitude was selected to induce measurable convective transport while remaining within the range commonly used in fluid transport and leakage studies.

Validation and reproducibility

To assess reproducibility, one tooth was re-analysed from segmentation through simulation. Simulation parameters (viscosity, surface tension) were benchmarked against experimental data from the literature for similar geometries16,18,24. For this proof-of-concept study, micro-CT reconstruction parameters were kept constant across all specimens to avoid introducing variability unrelated to void geometry. Reproducibility was therefore assessed at the segmentation and simulation stages, which represent the primary sources of uncertainty influencing void connectivity and transport behaviour. While systematic variation of reconstruction settings may further strengthen reproducibility assessment, such analyses were beyond the scope of the present study and are planned for future validation work.

Discussion

This proof-of-concept study examined how void space geometry, surface wettability, and injection parameters influence fluid transport within obturated root canals, using a three-dimensional simulation-assisted methodology (3D-SALAM) integrated with high-resolution micro-CT imaging. By combining realistic anatomical modelling with computational fluid dynamics, the approach provided controlled, reproducible, and non-destructive insights into transport phenomena that may underlie clinical leakage and long-term material degradation. It is important to emphasise that the primary contribution of this study is the development and demonstration of the 3D-SALAM framework itself, with the reported transport results intended as proof-of-concept examples illustrating the workflow’s analytical capabilities rather than as definitive material-performance comparisons. Because the present study is designed as a proof-of-concept demonstration, figures comparing outcomes across specimens are presented to illustrate methodological sensitivity rather than to support population-level inference. Establishing statistically validated trends will require application of the framework to larger datasets, which lies beyond the scope of the current work but represents a clear direction for future studies. Accordingly, the value of the present results lies not in identifying new transport mechanisms, but in demonstrating how established fluid-mechanical principles can be interrogated quantitatively and non-destructively within anatomically realistic endodontic systems using the 3D-SALAM framework.

Consistent with established porous-media theory, the simulations illustrate how fluid distribution within root-filled canals reflects the combined influence of capillary forces, viscous flow, and void network microstructure, as resolved within the proposed framework. Although the present work was simulation-based, the observed behaviours aligned with prior laboratory reports and provide mechanistic understanding of how fluids, bacteria, or their by-products might traverse residual spaces within filled canals.

In water-filled specimens, molecular diffusion resulted in relatively uniform tracer spread across interconnected and dead-end voids in the absence of pressure, whereas applied pressure produced rapid convective flow through large, well-connected voids, leaving stagnant zones to be filled later by diffusion. These findings are consistent with reports that pressures used in fluid filtration studies (10–20 psi) may yield widely variable results36 and that high pressures can exaggerate defects in some filling materials24. This suggests that interpretation of transport behaviour should consider fluid dynamics rather than rely solely on penetration distance or volume.

The wettability-dependent transport behaviour observed in the simulations is consistent with well-known capillary phenomena and is presented here to demonstrate the framework’s ability to capture surface-chemistry effects within complex endodontic void geometries.

Under initially dry conditions, transport behaviour shifted from capillary-dominated at low velocity to viscous-dominated at high velocity. Hydrophilic systems reached maximum filling at intermediate velocities, where both small and large pores were accessed efficiently. Hydrophobic systems showed their highest filling at the capillary-viscous transition, though values remained lower than hydrophilic counterparts. Clinically, these findings suggest that both the driving force for fluid movement and surface properties of the canal walls influence how irrigants, sealers, or contaminants traverse void spaces, relevant to irrigant penetration during retreatment or to coronal leakage challenges.

Specimen variability also played a role. Smaller voids achieved higher saturation at lower velocities due to stronger capillary forces, whereas larger voids benefitted more from higher flow rates. This observation is in line with Bruyne et al., who demonstrated significant differences in maximum pore diameters among root-end filling materials37. Such variability underscores the importance of analysing void size distribution, not only void volume, when evaluating filling performance.

It is acknowledged that a smear layer may persist on portions of the root canal wall following chemomechanical preparation, particularly in anatomically untouched areas. This layer exhibits a densely packed, low-porosity structure at the micro- and nano-scale, with hydraulic properties distinct from the larger voids detectable by micro-CT. Such features fall below the spatial resolution of the present imaging protocol and were therefore not directly modelled.

The current analysis was intentionally confined to resolvable void spaces within the obturation mass and at material interfaces, which represent the primary conduits for fluid transport under clinically relevant pressure and capillary conditions. While nano-porous pathways associated with the smear layer may influence molecular diffusion locally, their contribution to bulk fluid transport is expected to be secondary compared with that of interconnected micro-void systems. Future studies integrating higher-resolution imaging or multi-scale modelling approaches may further elucidate the role of smear-layer–mediated transport mechanisms.

The effectiveness of image-based transport simulations is inherently scale dependent and requires consistency between imaging resolution, mesh discretisation, and the characteristic physical features governing transport. In this study, a voxel size of 10 μm was selected to resolve micrometre-scale voids and interconnected pathways within the obturation mass that are known to dominate bulk fluid transport and leakage-related phenomena. Features at smaller length scales, such as nano-porosity within the smear layer or material microstructure, fall below the imaging resolution and were therefore outside the scope of the present analysis.

To ensure numerical adequacy, the segmented void geometries were discretised using meshes with local cell sizes comparable to or smaller than the resolved void dimensions, resulting in more than 100,000 cells per simulation. This resolution was sufficient to capture velocity gradients, interfacial dynamics, and capillary–viscous interactions within the resolved void network. Accordingly, the framework is intended to model transport processes governed by micro-void architecture, while multi-scale extensions incorporating higher-resolution imaging or sub-grid modelling would be required to address nano-scale transport mechanisms.

It is recognised that radiographic artefact formation is material dependent. Bioceramic sealers typically generate fewer beam-hardening artefacts than gutta-percha, while epoxy-resin sealers such as AH26 exhibit high radiopacity due to their silver content. In this proof-of-concept study, AH26 was deliberately chosen as a well-established reference material to evaluate the performance of the 3D-SALAM framework under conservative and imaging-challenging conditions. Importantly, void analysis and subsequent transport simulations were based on segmented binary void geometry rather than grey-value–based material properties, reducing sensitivity to material-specific artefacts. Future studies will extend this workflow to bioceramic sealers to directly compare artefact behaviour and transport characteristics across material classes. A limitation of the present methodology relates to the reconstruction strategy used for the micro-CT datasets. In this study, projection data were reconstructed using a conventional filtered back-projection approach with standard beam-hardening correction provided by the manufacturer. While this approach was sufficient for reliable segmentation of void geometry and connectivity, iterative reconstruction algorithms have been shown to further reduce beam-hardening artefacts and may provide a more realistic representation of void spaces. Because the 3D-SALAM simulations rely primarily on the extracted binary void architecture rather than grey-value–based material properties, the main conclusions are unlikely to be affected. Nevertheless, the adoption of iterative reconstruction techniques could further improve image fidelity and represents a potential methodological refinement for future implementations of the framework.

Although 3D-SALAM addresses several limitations of conventional tests, such as destructive sectioning and two-dimensional artefacts, it has constraints that temper direct clinical translation. While micro-CT data were derived from real obturated teeth, transport was modelled computationally without parallel in vitro validation, limiting immediate extrapolation to clinical outcomes. The “dry tooth” scenarios were included to isolate capillarity effects but do not reflect in vivo conditions, where canals remain moist and sealer–dentin interactions differ10. This pilot study also tested only one sealer–core combination (AH26 with gutta-percha) and included four teeth, meaning larger datasets and multiple materials are required for statistical and comparative validation. Future research should therefore integrate experimental validation, assess different obturation systems, and examine clinically dynamic variables such as temperature fluctuations, functional loading, and bacterial challenge.

Despite these limitations, 3D-SALAM offers several advantages. The framework enables reproducible, non-destructive three-dimensional mapping of void networks, quantitative assessment of connectivity, and time-resolved modelling of transport behaviour—capabilities that are difficult to achieve with conventional leakage methods. As highlighted by De-Deus et al.11, there is a clear need for standardised and clinically relevant approaches that move beyond simple leakage endpoints and instead provide mechanistic and predictive insight; 3D-SALAM has the potential to contribute to such a workflow.

Beyond endodontics, the methodology is readily adaptable to other dental and biomedical applications. Comparable micro-CT–driven simulations could be employed to investigate interfacial transport at restorative margins, post–core systems, or implant–abutment junctions, where microgaps and fluid dynamics similarly influence long-term performance. While the 3D-SALAM framework supports longitudinal, non-destructive evaluation of void geometry and fluid transport, the present study does not explicitly incorporate functional occlusal loading. In vivo, repetitive mastication may generate transient hydrostatic pressure spikes that exceed those applied under controlled experimental conditions and could affect interfacial stability over time. Accordingly, the current approach captures time-dependent transport phenomena under defined hydraulic conditions rather than mechanical fatigue effects. Incorporation of cyclic mechanical loading or pressure transients represents a logical extension of the framework and may further enhance its clinical relevance in future studies.

In summary, transport behaviour within root-filled teeth is shaped by the combined effects of void architecture, surface chemistry, and flow dynamics. The 3D-SALAM approach provides detailed mechanistic insight into these factors and represents a promising workflow for future investigations, provided that subsequent studies include experimental validation and clinically relevant conditions.

Conclusion

This study introduced the micro-CT–integrated three-dimensional simulation framework (3D-SALAM) as a non-destructive and reproducible method to investigate how void geometry, surface wettability, and flow dynamics determine fluid transport within root canal fillings. The framework enables systematic investigation of how capillary forces, viscous flow, and void architecture—known determinants of transport in porous systems—may influence sealing effectiveness when analysed within root canal geometries. By directly addressing the limitations of conventional leakage assays, 3D-SALAM introduces an analytical paradigm that links realistic micro-architecture to dynamic transport behaviour in a quantifiable, three-dimensional context. This approach advances the scientific understanding of how technical and material properties and micro-void networks may affect clinical durability, providing a possible bridge between laboratory modelling and in vivo performance prediction. Beyond endodontics, the framework offers applicability to other porous and composite dental materials—including restorative interfaces, bone scaffolds, and implant junctions—where fluid dynamics and interfacial stability may be critical to longevity. In essence, 3D-SALAM may transform static imaging into a predictive, mechanistic tool for studying fluid–material interactions, laying the foundation for more reliable evaluation and design of dental materials.
