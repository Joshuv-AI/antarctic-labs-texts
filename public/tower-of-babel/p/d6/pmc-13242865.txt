Analysis and prediction of sexual well-being variables in men with and without lifelong premature ejaculation in a non-western context
Authors: Azamjon Makhmudov, Azamjon Tulaboev, David L Rowland, Sarvar Aliev, Jamshid Rikhsiboyev
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPremature ejaculation (PE) is often viewed as couples’ problem, with research increasingly emphasizing the importance of evaluating the man’s PE in the context of partner variables as a meaningful outcome in PE management.AimTo describe and compare differences in self and perceived partner sexual well-being in men with lifelong PE (LPE) and men without PE, and further, to understand the role of various PE symptoms (ejaculatory control, ejaculatory latency [EL], consistency of symptomology) on sexual well-being specifically within the LPE group.MethodsOne thousand two hundred sixty-five men visiting a urology clinic in Uzbekistan responded to validated items asking about the intensity of PE symptoms, their sex-related distress/bother, and the perceived dissatisfaction of their partner. Regression modeling was used to identify salient predictors of sexual well-being related to both the man himself and his partner.OutcomesPredictors of sexual well-being in both the overall sample (LPE and non-PE men) and in just the LPE group.ResultsAlthough both LPE (n = 187) and non-PE (n = 990) men from this clinical sample showed negative effects of urologic symptomology on their well-being, PE men’s well-being was diminished even further (P < .001, all measures). The strongest predictors of diminished well-being in the overall clinical sample were, in order of effect size, consistency of the PE problem, lack of ejaculatory control, and short ELs (R2 = .416-.197). The strongest predictor of diminished well-being within the LPE group was consistency of the PE problem, with EL playing a lesser or non-significant role (R2 = .167-.006).Clinical TranslationIn addition to managing PE symptoms, clinicians should help patients and their partners explore ways to address the effects of PE symptomology on their sexual well-being.Strengths and LimitationsThe well-validated items, multifactorial definition of groups, multiple sexual-well-being outcome variables, and well-powered analyses represented study strengths. The lack of the partner’s own assessment of sexual dissatisfaction and the lack of contextual factors such as relationship satisfaction, sexual interest, and partner sexual functioning prevented a more comprehensive view regarding patient and partner well-being.ConclusionMen with PE report greater negative effects on sexual well-being than men with other urological problems, with consistency of the PE problem standing out as the strongest factor associated with diminished sexual well-being and poor ejaculatory control and short ELs playing a lesser role. Findings highlight the importance of treatment that addresses the psychosexual well-being of both the man and his partner.

Introduction

Premature ejaculation (PE) occurs in about 5%-15% of men1-5 and is characterized by ejaculating before desired during partnered sex. Current professional definitions of PE share three common elements: (1) ejaculation upon minimal stimulation, typically operationalized by a short ejaculation latency (EL); (2) a lack of ability to control or postpone ejaculation; and (3) dysfunction-related negative consequences such as bother and anxiety.6-8 Premature ejaculation has been further characterized as occurring on “all or almost all” sexual occasions.6,8 Depending on the developmental pathway, it may be categorized into either “lifelong” (LPE) or “acquired” (APE) subtypes.7,8

Despite the focus on the man’s symptoms in various definitions, PE is, in fact, often viewed as a couples’ problem, as the disorder has negative consequences not only for the man but also for his partner.9-11 Research has increasingly emphasized the importance of evaluating the man’s PE in the context of partner variables as a meaningful outcome in PE management. Accordingly, several studies have attempted to delineate women partners’ perceived and/or actual sexual and relationship satisfaction and to characterize the “burden” of PE by assessing a composite representing both the patient’s anxiety/distress and the perceived (or actual) distress of the partner.11-16

The psychological burden of PE: The perpetuating cycle of anxiety and distress on sexual response

Studies have consistently demonstrated that PE engenders bother/distress in the patient and, indeed, this condition is a critical condition for all PE diagnoses.6-8,14-16 Furthermore, dysfunction-related distress is not only a consequence of PE,17 but it may also cause the problem to worsen.18,19 Specifically, the anticipation of partnered sex is likely to increase performance anxiety which, in turn, increases the likelihood of response failure, with recurring failures having significant cognitive and emotional fallout. Cognitively, men come to obsess about their problem,20-22 thus decreasing self-efficacy and potentially creating a self-fulfilling prophecy. Emotionally, they may be so overwhelmed by anxiety that sex is viewed as an occasion for shame, embarrassment, and distress rather than an opportunity for pleasure, positive feelings, and intimacy.9,10,23,24

The psychological burden of PE: Sexual and relationship satisfaction in partners of men with PE

Studies have indicated that the man’s PE response may affect their partner, including their sexual and/or overall relationship satisfaction.25,26 A review of 11 studies9 on men with PE and their partners verified that 3 of them reported elevated distress in their partners as well. However, such variability in findings suggests that women partners of men with PE may well respond differently to the PE condition than the men themselves. In further support of this idea, Rosen et al14 reported that while men with PE consistently experienced diminished feelings of intimacy with their partner, the man’s PE status was not a reliable predictor of lower sexual satisfaction and emotional closeness for women partners. This disparity in satisfaction between PE men and their partners has also been reported in other studies.27,28 But in contrast are studies that tell a different story, suggesting that partners of PE men not only report decreased sexual satisfaction but, in many cases, lower relationship quality as well.12,13,29-31

What might account for the disparity in the effects of PE on women partners? One explanation based on Finnish partners13 noted that while the association between PE and diminished relationship satisfaction in the partner was statistically significant, the actual effect size was very small, indicating associations that were variable and not particularly robust. A second approach has suggested that the specific consequences of PE to the partner—likely varying in both type and intensity—may explain why some partners are so negatively affected and others only minimally so. For example, some women partners of PE men experience diminished arousal, vaginal lubrication, and orgasmic response comparable to a female sexual dysfunction.12,13,15 Other PE-related factors that predict (or mediate) the partners’ diminished sexual and relationship satisfaction include the partner’s loss of emotional connection,29-31 the man’s own distress30 and lack of ejaculatory control,16,30-33 the stigma associated with the dysfunction,29 and, though less consistently,29,30 the man’s very short EL. In fact, the effects of the PE and short ELs were reportedly so serious that some partners considered ending their relationship.30-32 In summary, partners of men with PE show considerable variability in how they respond to the problem, with variability likely related to the type and magnitude of the consequences.

The overall psychological/emotional burden for men with PE: Self distress + partner dissatisfaction

Men with PE not only worry about their own inadequate sexual response; they often worry about—and assume responsibility for—the distress borne by their partner.11,33 Few studies, however, have explored predictors of PE men’s compounded (self + partner) distress, yet this “overall burden” intensifies the man’s guilt and shame, thus further jeopardizing sexual intimacy and relationship satisfaction.34 In 1 study on the topic,11 men’s level of “overall burden” was tied to their own PE-related distress/anxiety, to lower relationship satisfaction, and to greater importance of having partnered sex. However, that study failed to examine the role of critically important variables related to PE symptomology on men’s overall burden, such as levels of ejaculatory control, consistency of PE symptomology during partnered sex, and ELs during intercourse.

Rationale and goals of the current study

Although studies on PE have explored well-being issues in PE men and their partners, significant gaps remain. In this study, we were interested in how specific components of PE symptomology (eg, EL, ejaculatory control, consistency of PE events) were related to specific well-being outcomes, as defined by PE men’s own distress, their perceived partner dissatisfaction, and their perceived overall burden. Such information would be helpful in devising treatment protocols that go beyond the mere management of PE symptomology by directly addressing the dyadic burden of the PE on both the man and his partner. To this end, in a urological clinical sample of men with and without PE, we addressed the following questions:

What differences in PE symptomology (the predictor covariates) occur between men with LPE and men without PE in a urological clinic sample (aim 1)?

What differences in self/partner sexual well-being outcome variables occur in men with LPE and men without PE in a urological clinic sample (aim 2)?

What differences in self/partner sexual well-being outcome variables occur in men with LPE and men without PE in a urological clinic sample (aim 2)?

What are the predictors of self/partner sexual well-being outcomes in the overall sample consisting of men with LPE and men without PE in a urologic clinic sample (aim 3)?

Method

Research design and participants

This prospective (cross-sectional) investigation was carried out at the treatment and diagnostic department of the State Institution “Republican Specialized Scientific and Practical Medical Center of Urology” under the Ministry of Health of the Republic of Uzbekistan (SI RSSPMCU) in Tashkent. Data collection occurred between 2023 and 2024. Approximately 2000 questionnaires were distributed to male patients at the Center. Of these, about 1600 were returned.

The study included men who had been sexually active for the past 6 months, had regular (at least twice a week) sexual intercourse, and were able to complete the questionnaires in Uzbek language. Men in the PE group were required to meet diagnostic criteria for LPE according to established clinical definitions as described below, whereas men in the control group had no history of PE and presented mainly to the clinic for urolithiasis. Participants were excluded from the study if they reported significant medical or psychiatric conditions that could impact sexual function (including erectile dysfunction [ED]), were taking medications that affected ejaculation or sexual activity, or if questionnaire data included incomplete, invalid, and/or out-of-range responses. The final sample included 1265 fully completed questionnaires for analysis. As a preliminary step, respondents self-identified into two groupings, with or without PE, with further subclassification into LPE and APE groupings.

Strategy for refined classification into No-PE and PE groups

Of the 1265 men recruited for the study, 814 (64.3%) self-reported having PE during intercourse, and 451 (35.7%) reported no PE during intercourse. Given that self-reported diagnoses are generally unreliable,34 to verify the PE classification, further refinement of groupings was undertaken on the basis of two diagnostic criteria: ejaculatory control (EJAC-Control) and ejaculatory latency (EL) during intercourse.35 Specifically, participants qualified for the PE group only if they (1) scored 3 or higher on a 5-point scale assessing the lack of ejaculatory control (1 = no lack; 5 = severe lack), and (2) scored 4 or 5 on a 5-point scale assessing EL during intercourse, with these categories representing “1-3 minutes” and “≤1 minute.”

This procedure resulted in 275 men (21.7%) classified as having PE (age 32.9 ± 6.5), and 990 men (78.3%) classified as not having PE (age 34.1 ± 10.0; P < .001). Of the men classified as having PE, 187 (68.0%) indicated having LPE, and 11 (4.0%) having APE in response to an item asking whether their condition occurred since their first sexual experiences (LPE) or emerged only later, after a period of normal ejaculatory response. The remaining 77 (28.0%), because they did not initially self-identify as having PE, were not presented with the question about LPE or APE subtypes. Accordingly, this study focused only on the 187 men who initially self-identified as having PE and were then subsequently confirmed as having LPE based on the refined criteria above.35

Sexual well-being outcome variables and predictor variables

Outcome variables

Three variables related to sexual psychological well-being served as outcomes: (1) the PE man’s level of dysfunction/problem-related distress/anxiety (Distress/Anxiety); (2) his perception of his partner’s level of sexual dissatisfaction (Partner Dissatisfaction); and (3) the overall burden (Overall Burden) for PE men, represented by the combination of Distress/Anxiety + Partner Dissatisfaction (Table 1 & Suppl Table 1).

Dysfunction-Related Distress/Anxiety was derived from 6 equally-weighted preliminary items designed to assess this construct. These questions tapped into the same general construct, but used variations in wording. Perceived Partner Dissatisfaction was derived from 6 equally weighted preliminary items designed to assess the level of the PE man’s perceived sexual dissatisfaction of his (primary) sexual partner using a procedure identical to that described above. Predictor covariates

Predictor covariates for the regression models included three parameters related to PE symptomology, namely, ejaculatory control (EJAC-Control), ejaculatory latency/duration (EL), and consistency of PE symptomology during intercourse (PE Consistency). Each of these variables was constructed in a manner identical to the outcome variables above as part of the validating process for a PE diagnostic instrument,36,37 that is, they were derived from a set of multiple items designed to assess the construct, which were then evaluated for face and content validity, with EFA used to identify items for inclusion of the final composite variable. The number of finalized items and Cronbach alphas for each construct were: EJAC-Control (3 items α = 0.69); EL (3 items, α = 0.74); and PE Consistency (3 items, α = 0.89) (Table 2). Patient ages were also included as a control covariate in the regression analyses.

Procedure and ethical considerations

Linguistic examination for accuracy, translation (original forms in Russian and Uzbek languages, further translation to English for publication), and implementation of the questionnaire was carried out at the Institute of Oriental Studies. Approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Uzbekistan Ministry of Health (Certificate No. 9/2-1827, dated December 25, 2023). Participants provided written informed consent after being informed of the study objectives, procedures, and the right to withdraw at any time without consequences. To ensure anonymity, no personal identifiers were used and data were stored on password-protected devices accessible only to the research team. Participation was voluntary and involved no incentives.

Data analysis methods

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 25.0). Descriptive statistics, including frequency distributions, means, standard deviations were calculated to summarize the demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants. Comparisons across LPE and non-PE groups were carried out with t-tests. Linear regression models for each outcome variable; (Distress/Anxiety, Partner Dissatisfaction, Overall Burden) used the forced entry method, with sets of selected predictor variables described in the “Results.”

Results

Comparison of LPE and non-PE groups on predictor covariates and outcome variables (aim 1)

Independent t-tests compared the mean scores of final LPE and non-PE classifications (Table 2). For all measures, items were scaled on a 5-pt scale, with higher scores indicating greater severity of PE symptomology or a worse outcome. As expected, for predictor covariates assessing PE symptomology (Tables 1-3), the LPE group showed greater severity, with large effect sizes (Cohen’s d ranging from 1.22 to 2.02). For outcome variables assessing self/partner sexual well-being variables (Tables 2, 4, 5), LPE men reported significantly greater Distress/Anxiety (Cohen’s d = 0.67), greater Partner Dissatisfaction (Cohen’s d = 1.27), and greater Overall Burden (Cohens’ d = 1.23). That is, both PE symptomology (predictor) variables and self/partner well-being (outcome) variables differed between LPE and non-PE groups.

PE symptoms that predict well-being variables in the overall urological/clinical sample (aim 3)

Regression analysis was used to determine which PE symptomology (predictor) variables (EJAC-Control, EL, PE Consistency, and when appropriate Distress/Anxiety) contributed unique variance to the well-being outcome variables of Distress/Anxiety, Partner Dissatisfaction, and Overall Burden (Table 4). Age was included as a control covariate. For this set of analyses, we used the entire sample, consisting of men with LPE and men with no PE. Our goals were (1) to assess how increasing PE symptomology, ranging from none to severe, impacted sexual well-being variables, and (2) to determine which specific PE symptoms were most strongly associated with each well-being variable.

For Distress/Anxiety, adjusted R2 = .197 (P < .001). In order of effect size, PE Consistency (β = .283, P < .001) and EJAC-Control (β = .170; P < .001) emerged as the two significant predictors. EL (β = .065, P = .104) was not significant, and increasing age was marginally associated with increasing Distress/Anxiety (β = .050, P = .064).

For Partner Dissatisfaction, adjusted R2 = .416 (P < .001). All predictor covariates were significant, with effect sizes in the following order: PE Consistency (β = .359, P < .001), then Anxiety/Distress (β = .252, P < .001). EJAC-Control (β = .093, P = .001) and EL (β = .096, P = .005) showed similar effect sizes. Increasing age was associated with greater perceived Partner Dissatisfaction (β = .091, P < .001).

For Overall Burden, adjusted R2 = .396, (P < .001). For this analysis, Distress/Anxiety was omitted as a predictor variable, as it was used to construct the outcome variable (Overall Burden). All predictor covariates were significant, with effect sizes in the following order: PE Consistency (β = .435, P < .001), then EJAC-Control (β = .170, P < .001), then EL (β = .110, P = .002). Increasing age was associated with greater Overall Burden (β = .097, P < .001).

The above analyses demonstrate that within a clinical urological population of men both with and without PE, all three symptoms of PE contribute unique variance to the prediction of the well-being outcome variables; about 40% of the variance in both Partner Dissatisfaction and Overall Burden was predicted but only about 20% for the patient’s own Anxiety/Distress. Results further show that PE Consistency emerged as the strongest and most consistent predictor of negative well-being outcomes, followed by EJAC-Control and then EL. Higher age was consistently associated with diminished well-being.

Focus on the LPE group: Correlations among variables for LPE men (aim 4)

In the final two sets of analyses, our goal was to understand relationships among PE symptomology variables and sexual well-being variables but only within the LPE group. In the first set of analyses, correlations were determined to identify non-collinear variables for inclusion in the regression models (Supplementary Table 2).

Items dealing with PE symptomology (1-4) showed correlations between 0.43 and 0.51, values fairly consistent with other studies.32,38 Items related to sexual well-being were also intercorrelated. Distress/Anxiety and Partner Dissatisfaction correlated most strongly with Overall Burden (0.43 and 0.71, respectively), as each variable was a component of that composite variable. Accordingly, neither variable was included as a predictor covariate in the subsequent regressions (next section) on Overall Burden. Anxiety/distress and Partner Dissatisfaction correlated moderately, r = −0.28.

Focus on LPE group: Predictors of distress/anxiety, partner dissatisfaction, and overall burden (aim 5)

In the final set of analyses, also restricted to the LPE group (Table 5), regression models on each of the well-being outcome variables used three predictor variables: EL, PE Consistency, and Age. EJAC-Control was initially considered for inclusion but was subsequently dropped for both conceptual and statistical reasons. Conceptually, EJAC-Control represents a construct and, as such, is not physically/tangibly manifested within the dyadic interaction during partnered sex. Statistically, EJAC-Control was sufficiently correlated with both EL and PE Consistency to exclude it based on collinearity.

Discussion

The findings from this study provide novel insights into well-being variables in a sample of men visiting a urology clinic and, more specifically, a sample of men having lifelong premature ejaculation. We first demonstrated that men with LPE differed from men without PE on all study variables of interest, those related to PE symptomology and those related to sexual well-being, the latter characterized by the patient’s distress/anxiety, his perceived partner dissatisfaction, and the overall burden of his urological problem.

We further characterized the overlapping yet distinct distributions for each of the well-being variables for these two groups. The overlap was not surprising, as any group of men visiting a urology clinic likely has a condition or problem that requires amelioration, with circumstances and/or concerns acting as powerful motivators for seeking medical help.10,39,40 However, the much higher bother/distress and overall burden in the LPE group attests to the even stronger negative consequences of having a sexual dysfunction, particularly one having significant potential to affect the partner’s sexual satisfaction.11-16

The strong negative feelings and diminished well-being in men with LPE are understandable, given their intense feelings of guilt41-43 and low sexual self-efficacy.24 Specifically, men with PE engage in a high level of self-blaming for their problematic response, more so, for example, than men with ED who can often attribute their problem to various pathophysiological developments (eg, chronic disease).10,41-45 That is, men with LPE internalize the source of their problem, attributing it to something “wrong” about themselves as opposed to something external, for example, a disease, their relationship, the situation, or their partner.41-45 This self-blaming often extends beyond the dysfunctional response itself to include other negative issues occurring within the dyadic relationship,42,43 not only heightening the sense of shame, anxiety, and distress but also reinforcing the expectation of failure in future sexual situations and often creating a self-fulfilling prophecy.20,24 Given this scenario, it is not surprising the PE engenders such overwhelming negative effects on well-being, compared with men who are visiting the urology clinic for other issues. At the same time, such strong self-blaming can sometimes have a positive effect, providing the needed motivation for seeking help.

Predictors of sexual well-being variables in the larger urological sample

One goal of our study was to assess how increasing PE symptomology—extending from no or minor symptomology to strong/intense symptomology—affects psychological/sexual well-being in the overall clinical sample. All 4 PE symptom variables were associated with diminished sexual well-being. High PE Consistency was the strongest and most consistent predictor of lower well-being, with lack of EJAC-Control playing a secondary yet moderately strong role. In the one analysis in which Anxiety/Distress was included (Partner Dissatisfaction), it too played a strong role, second only to PE Consistency. Despite short ELs being a concern to the PE patient and, presumably, his partner,31,32 it was the least potent factor in predicting negative effects on sexual well-being.

These findings partly confirm previous studies, but also add a new dimension to understanding negative sexual well-being related to PE symptomology. Previous research had identified a central role for ejaculatory control in defining PE,30,46,47 and our analysis confirmed a prominent role for this factor in predicting all sexual well-being variables. In contrast, the man’s very short EL played a more limited role: While it was not associated with the man’s own anxiety/distress, it did predict his perception of his partner’s dissatisfaction.

Standing out, however, was the preeminent role of PE Consistency in predicting diminished sexual well-being on all outcome measures. Previous research exploring the role of PE symptomology on men and their partners has seldom included a measure of the consistency of symptoms during partnered sex, despite its inclusion as a diagnostic criterion in the ISSM, DSM, ICSM, and AUA definitions.6,8,48,49 The current findings, however, indicate that the most deleterious effect on well-being is the relentless and persistent nature of the PE problem. That is, the various symptoms associated with PE (eg, short EL) may be “bothersome,” but the high frequency, constant repetition, and persistence of the problem seem to make it most exasperating.

Predictors of well-being in the LPE sample

A further goal of our study was to determine which PE symptoms were most associated with negative effects on well-being within just the PE group. Once again, PE Consistency emerged as the preeminent variable, strongly predicting the patient’s own Anxiety/Distress and moderately predicting his Overall Burden. EL, on the other hand, played a lesser role for these two well-being outcomes, and interestingly, neither variable predicted perceived Partner Dissatisfaction. Age, included as a control covariate, was negatively associated with the patient’s Distress/Anxiety and Overall Burden, consistent with the idea that as men age, they are typically less bothered by their sexual problems.2,4,50,51

The overall findings from this study underscore the importance of symptom consistency in explaining diminished well-being in the patient and his partner. In fact, PE Consistency as a diagnostic criterion variable has received little attention in the study of men with PE and thus warrants further exploration regarding its contribution to the diagnosis of PE and relationship to sexual well-being. Several recent studies, for example, indicate that some men experience recurring, episodic, or subclinical PE symptomology that, when it does resurface, is comparable in severity to men with either LPE or APE.52-54

Although we did not have sufficient numbers of men with APE to compare them with our sample of LPE men, previous studies have demonstrated that men in these 2 PE subtypes report comparable levels of bother/distress.55,56 What remains to be tested, however, is whether men with APE share similar levels of perceived partner dissatisfaction and overall burden related to their symptomology as men with LPE—to our knowledge, this hypothesis is yet untested, as Medline and Psych Info searches revealed no studies on the topic.

Treatment considerations

When treating a man with PE, it is necessary to conduct a comprehensive assessment of PE, its developmental pathway (which influences treatment choices), possible underlying risk/cause factors, the patient’s bother/distress, and self- and partner- relationship and sexual satisfaction.7 Unfortunately, none of the existing screening or diagnostic questionnaires consider all these factors simultaneously. Symptom management without considering the patient’s (and when feasible and appropriate, the partner’s) emotional state, anxiety level, self-esteem, and sexual expectations may lead to an unstable clinical effect and a higher relapse rate. In this context, integrating psychosexual assessment into the diagnostic and treatment algorithm for PE is particularly important. Furthermore, most specialists emphasize the need to consider the partner’s context, as PE may have a significant impact also on the partner’s sexual satisfaction and psychological well-being. Involving the partner in treatment can reduce interpersonal tension, develop realistic treatment expectations, and improve compliance.

Strengths and limitations

Three strengths of this study stand out: First, the confidence level regarding both the PE symptomology variables and well-being variables was high, as each variable represented a statistically-derived composite using EFA based on a set of 5 or more individual items. Second, the PE and non-PE groupings were carefully determined using empirically derived thresholds for ejaculatory control and ejaculatory latency. Third, the sample sizes of the LPE and non-PE groups resulted in well-powered analyses, low statistical error rates (alpha and beta), and confidence in the ensuing conclusions. We further note that while there was substantial alignment between our findings and those using Western-based samples, cultural differences in the way PE men perceive both their problem and its impact on their relationship could benefit from further study.57

Regarding limitations, we assessed only the patient’s perceived sexual dissatisfaction of his partner. This variable is valuable in its own right, as it allows assessment of patients’ overall sense of burden and responsibility for the sexual problem. But optimally, assessment of the partner’s self-reported (actual) level of dissatisfaction would have enabled the opportunity to evaluate alignment within the couple. Furthermore, well-being variables might have been better contextualized by assessing covariates related to relationship quality and satisfaction, ED status, level of sexual interest/desire, non-sexual urological and non-urological comorbidities, and partner dysfunctions. Complicating the issue, however, due to cultural expectations, spouses seldom accompany men to clinic visits in this part of the world, and some men may have multiple sexual partnerships. Along these same lines, given the cultural context of this study, it was not possible to include a sexually diverse sample, thereby preventing generalization to relationships involving same sex partners. Finally, we note that our results for the LPE analyses had to rely on a truncated variable for EL, as this variable was used as a threshold for defining the LPE group. The limited response range for this variable may have increased the error rate in detecting stronger effects on sexual well-being variables within that group.

Conclusion

This study has demonstrated the greater deleterious effects or PE on self and partner-related well-being, compared with men seeking help for other urological problems. It further identified the consistency of PE symptomology as the variable most associated with diminished sexual well-being. Poor ejaculatory control and short ELs played significant but lesser roles in predicting well-being. These findings highlight the importance of treatment that extends beyond the management of PE symptomology and that addresses the psychosexual well-being of both the man and his partner.
