Development and validation of a sensitive HPLC-MS/MS method for the analysis of semaglutide in human plasma
Authors: Christopher Tudan, Joel Stickling, Emily Northwick Darden, Lauren Devine, Shane Needham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundSemaglutide is a human glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) peptide analog. To date, the literature does not report a validated HPLC-MS/MS assay using a triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer for the quantitation of semaglutide in human plasma in support of pharmacokinetics. A robust, sensitive HPLC-­MS/MS assay supporting this class of compounds, is provided and full validation demonstrated.Methods and resultsA triple-quadrupole HPLC-MS/MS method has been developed and fully validated for the quantitation of semaglutide in human plasma, using a stable isotope labeled internal standard and an effective, efficient protein precipitation sample extraction. The method has a sensitive analytical range of 5 ng/mL to 200 ng/mL (and to 1000 ng/mL with a dilution QC) and exhibits excellent accuracy (92.7% to 109.4%) and precision of <10.6%. This highly selective method provided high recovery of the analyte, with minimal matrix effects.ConclusionA robust and simple HPLC-MS/MS method to quantify semaglutide in human plasma has been fully validated in accordance with the 2022 FDA M10 guidance. This method is suitable for implementation in clinical studies within the validated analytical range and stability conditions and can be extended to the bioanalysis of other GLP-1 peptide-based therapeutics with similar structural and physiochemical properties.KEYWORDS: GLP-1, semaglutide, peptides, ICH M10, HPLC-MS/MS, method validation

Introduction

Therapeutic peptides including glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists are emerging as a new effective generation of therapeutics because of the advancements in modification chemistry and analytical technologies and rapid progression in proteomics [1–3]. Moreover, peptide drug development has expanded with recent advances in structural biology, recombinant biologics, and new synthetic technologies [3,4]. Peptide-based therapeutics have also attracted attention because of their exceptional specificity, minimal immunogenicity, low toxicity, and therapeutic efficacy [2,5]. Since 2000, more than 30 non-insulin peptide drugs have been approved worldwide [2,3]. Nonetheless, quantitative peptide bioanalysis remains a challenge due to their high molecular weight, complex structures, significant endogenous interference, low in vivo concentrations, and susceptibility to nonspecific binding [6]. These aspects of peptides can lead to substantial loss during preparation and increase the risk of matrix interference.

Recently, a new class of GLP-1 receptor agonists has provided excellent therapies for the treatment of diabetes (type 2 diabetes mellitus) and obesity [7–9] and has been demonstrated as potential treatment options for depression [10], major cardiovascular events [7], glaucoma [11], diabetic retinopathy [12,13], and Parkinson’s disease [14]. Examples of this class of peptides include semaglutide, dulaglutide, liraglutide, and tirzepatide, to name a few [2,15,16]. Given the multiple applications of the GLP-1 receptor agonists, there remains a need for continued research on incretin-based therapeutics, such as semaglutide. These GLP-1 peptide analogs were designed to have reduced susceptibility to enzyme degradation and increased in vivo half-life while providing effective therapeutic efficacy [17–20]. Semaglutide is a long-acting analog of human GLP-1 with 94% amino acid identity to human GLP-1. The long-acting effect of semaglutide is a result of a covalently linked fatty acid moiety that facilitates binding to albumin resulting in an extended half-life with favorable pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic characteristics [21].

GLP-1 receptor agonist peptides exhibit a long half‑life with circulatory concentrations in the low nmol/L range [20,22]. Therefore, to accurately describe the pharmacokinetics of these compounds, it is critical to develop a sensitive and robust bioanalytical method to quantify these proteins and their metabolites at very low concentrations in blood. Peptide and protein bioanalytical techniques have traditionally been immunoassay-based assays, such as enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA), due to their high sensitivity and high throughput capability [23,24]. However, associated method development for ELISA can be challenging because of the need to identify a specific antibody to the analyte of interest, and moreover, the ELISA has a narrow linear range and potential cross-reactivity when the antibodies cannot structurally discriminate related compounds or analogs [6,15].

The obstacles associated with ELISA protein quantitation have been overcome with HPLC-MS/MS. Mass spectrometry has, in recent years, become the standard in peptide analysis, taking advantage of the fact that peptides can become hydrolyzed into multiple peptides with smaller molecular weights and then the shorter peptide can be selected based on full scan intensity for quantitation [9,20]. Moreover, today’s tandem mass spectrometers are now sufficiently sensitive for peptide quantitation due to their analytical advantages of having a wider dynamic range, greater accuracy, and superior specificity due to a lack of cross‑reactivity [15,25]. HPLC-MS/MS has become the primary method for GLP-1 peptide bioanalysis [15–18,25–28], including non-clinical (non-human) assays for semaglutide [5,17,18,29].

There currently exist many ELISA-based assays on the market and used in drug development targeting semaglutide, but there does not exist a published, fully validated HPLC-MS/MS assay for the quantitation of semaglutide in human matrix in the literature. This report presents a novel and robust HPLC-MS/MS assay for quantifying semaglutide in human plasma as well as the associated full validation performed in accordance with the 2022 FDA M10 guidance [30]. This method can be readily adapted for use with other GLP-1 agonist peptide-based therapeutics with similar structural and physiochemical properties.

Materials and methods

Materials

Semaglutide was provided by Caymen Chemical (Ann Arbor, MI, USA), and the internal standard, heavy semaglutide (97% purity) that represents semaglutide containing stable isotope labeling with 13C and 15N so that the mass increase is 40 daltons compared to the parent peptide semaglutide, and having the formula 13C35 12C152 H291 15N5 14N40 O59, was produced and provided by Biosynth (Gardner, MA, USA), ThermoFisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA) supplied chemicals including acetonitrile, methanol, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), formic acid, and HPLC grade water. Human plasma (K2EDTA), lipemic human plasma (K2EDTA), and human whole blood (K2EDTA) were supplied by BioIVT (Woodbury, NY, USA). The SiliGuardTM low binding deep well plates (17P687GC) that contain a unique SiliGuardTM treatment that reduces nonspecific binding were acquired from Analytical-Sales (Flanders, NJ, USA). The Protein Low-Bind tubes (022431102) which are free from surface coating and optimal for potential peptide nonspecific binding were obtained directly from Eppendorf (Mississauga, ON, Canada).

Preparation of solutions

Individual calibration and quality control (QC) 1.0 mg/mL stock solutions of semaglutide were prepared from powder in 4% formic acid in methanol/water (90:10). A 0.1 mg/mL stock solution of heavy semaglutide internal standard was prepared from powder in DMSO. All stock solutions were stored under refrigeration at 6°C when not in use. The calibration standards were prepared by spiking the 1.0 mg/mL stock solution into human plasma and serially diluting in the Eppendorf Protein LowBind tubes to form an eight-point standard curve consisting of semaglutide concentrations of 5, 10, 20, 40, 120, 180, and 200 ng/mL. QC solutions were prepared similarly by serially diluting the 1.0 mg/mL stock solution into human plasma, with concentrations of 5 ng/mL (lower limit of quantitation quality control, LLOQ-QC), 15 ng/mL (low-quality control, LQC), 60 ng/mL (mid-quality control, MQC), and 150 ng/mL (high-quality control, HQC). The standard and QC solutions were prepared and aliquoted into LoBind polypropylene tubes and stored at ­-20°C.

Processing of samples

Control plasma, calibration standards and QCs were thawed at room temperature. Each sample was subsequently vortexed prior to being aliquoted at 50 μL into a 96-well plate. To the sample wells was added 150 μL of 70:30 acetonitrile\methanol containing 50 ng/mL internal standard (IS; heavy semaglutide), except for the matrix blanks (control plasma), to which 70:30 acetonitrile\methanol was added without IS. The plate was sealed and mixed on a plate shaker at 850 rpm for five minutes. The plate was subsequently centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for five minutes at ambient temperature. To each well was added 150 μL of 0.5% formic acid in water\acetonitrile\methanol (50:25:25) to a clean SiliGuardTM low binding deep well plate. Fifty microliters of the supernatant was transferred to the plate and sealed, with subsequent mixing at 850 rpm for five minutes. The plate was then transferred to the HPLC autosampler (set to 6°C) for analysis.

HPLC-MS/MS conditions

A Sciex QTRAP SCIEX 6500+ mass spectrometer with Analyst (version 1.7.2) software coupled with a Shimadzu Nexera X2 UPLC on a Dell PC workstation was used for HPLC-MS/MS data collection and processing. A sample injection volume of 5.0 μL was injected onto a Phenomenex Kinetex C8, 30 × 2.1 mm, 100Ǻ, 2.6 μm column, maintained at 40°C. Mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water and mobile phase B consisted of 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile; the flow rate of 0.7 mL/min. The acquisition time was 3.6 minutes. The mass spectrometer parameters are provided in Table 1. The expected retention time for both semaglutide and the IS was 2.3 minutes (±0.4 mins).

Assay validation

A detailed method validation was performed in accordance with the current FDA 2022 M10 guidance on method validation and sample analysis [30]. This validation assessed linearity and range, accuracy and precision, selectivity, recovery, hemolysis effect, lipemic effect, matrix effect, carryover, dilution integrity, whole blood (sample collection) stability, freeze/thaw stability, long­term stability, benchtop stability, and reinjection reproducibility. Acceptance criteria are described below.

Calibration curve and range

The calibration curve range is defined by the LLOQ (lower limit of quantitation) and the ULOQ (upper limit of quantitation). Eight concentrations of semaglutide were prepared in human plasma (between 5 ng/mL and 200 ng/mL). One set of eight calibration standards was included in each quantitative batch. Two matrix blanks and two Zero blanks (matrix blank with IS) were analyzed following the highest stands to assess carryover and interference. For the batch to be accepted, at least 75% of the calibration standards were required to have a back-calculated concentration of 100.0% ± 15.0% of nominal concentration (100.0% ± 20.0% for the LLOQ). Linearity of the calibration curve was assessed in each quantitative batch. The correlation coefficient (r-value) of the calibration curve needed to be ≥0.99 to demonstrate acceptable linearity. A linear regression of 1/x2 was applied to all validation batches to produce the best fit.

Accuracy and precision

Intra-batch accuracy and precision were assessed by spiking semaglutide into blank matrix at four concentrations: LLOQ-QC (5 ng/mL), LQC (15 ng/mL), MQC (60 ng/mL) and HQC (150 ng/mL). Each accuracy and precision batch contained six replicates (n = 6) at each of the QC concentration levels. Freshly prepared calibration standards were used in at least one accuracy and precision batch. The intra-assay accuracy and precision were considered acceptable if the six replicates of QC samples at the LLOQ, low, medium, and high concentrations had a precision (%CV) of ≤15.0% (≤20.0% for the LLOQ) and a mean accuracy of 100.0% ± 15.0% (100.0% ± 20.0% for the LLOQ). Three individual accuracy and precision batches were prepared and analyzed to assess inter-assay accuracy and precision of the method.

Selectivity

At least six individual sources/lots of control blank matrix were analyzed without the addition of analyte or internal standard. Any response present in the blank matrix at the corresponding retention time needed to be ≤20.0% of the analyte response of the LLOQ and not more than 5.0% of the internal standard response of the LLOQ.

Recovery

The recovery was evaluated by comparing peak areas of extracted samples spiked prior to extraction at the low, medium, and high QC levels (n = 3) with the peak areas of extracted blank matrix samples spiked post-extraction (n = 3). The ratio of the mean response of the extracted QC samples to the mean response of recovery samples was expressed as percent recovery. The results of the recovery evaluation were acceptable if the precision (%CV) was ≤15.0% for extracted and unextracted replicates at all QC levels and the %CV of the recovery percentages across all concentration levels was ≤20.0%.

Hemolysis effect

The effect of hemolysis on the method’s accuracy and precision was evaluated by analyzing three replicates of LQC and HQCs, each prepared containing 2% hemolyzed blood and analyzed in a quantitative batch. The results of the hemolysis effect evaluation were acceptable if the precision (%CV) was ≤15.0% and the mean accuracy was 100.0% ± 15.0% of the nominal concentrations of the LQC and HQC.

Lipemic effect

The effect of lipemic plasma on the method’s accuracy and precision was evaluated by analyzing three replicates of LQC and HQCs, each prepared in lipemic plasma and analyzed in a quantitative batch. The results of the lipemic effect evaluation were acceptable if the precision (%CV) was ≤15.0% and the mean accuracy was 100.0% ± 15.0% of the nominal concentrations of the LQC and HQC.

Matrix effect

The matrix effect was evaluated by analyzing three replicates of LQC and HQCs, each prepared using matrix from six different sources/lots. The results of the matrix effect evaluation were acceptable if the accuracy was 100.0% ± 15.0% of the nominal concentration and the precision (%CV) was ≤ 15.0% in all individual sources/lots.

Carryover

Carryover was assessed by injecting matrix blank samples immediately following the ULOQ calibration standard in each batch. Carryover was acceptable if the blanks demonstrate no significant peak area at the retention time for the analytes (≤20.0% LLOQ) and internal standard (≤5.0% LLOQ).

Dilution integrity

Dilution integrity was evaluated by preparing dilution QC samples (DQC) with a semaglutide concentration (1,000 ng/mL) that exceeded the ULOQ and subsequently diluting 10-fold into the analytical range using control plasma. Six replicates were tested in a minimum of one batch. The dilution integrity was acceptable if the precision (%CV) was ≤15.0% and the mean accuracy of the dilution QC samples for the dilution factor was 100.0% ± 15.0% of the nominal concentration (100 ng/mL).

Whole blood (sample collection) stability

Blank human whole blood (K2EDTA treated) was pre-incubated for 10 minutes in an incubator set to 37°C. Semaglutide was then spiked into the whole blood at the LQC and HQC concentrations and equilibrated for 10 minutes at 37°C. The spiked whole blood samples were then split into three aliquots. The first whole blood aliquot was placed on wet ice (two-hour wet ice) and the second whole blood aliquot was placed at room temperature (two-hour RT). The third aliquot was processed to plasma immediately (zero-hour) and then placed on wet ice. After two hours, the remaining whole blood aliquots were processed to plasma. All samples were extracted in three replicates per concentration level and analyzed according to the bioanalytical method. The sample collection stability was acceptable if the difference between the mean responses (peak area ratio of analyte to internal standard) of the two-hour (wet ice or RT) and zero-hour samples was ≤15.0% at both.

Freeze/Thaw stability

The freeze/thaw stability (F/T) of semaglutide in human plasma was evaluated at −20°C using three aliquots of LQC and HQC samples during five repeated freeze/thaw cycles. Stability QCs were frozen for at least 12 hours between the thawing cycles and thawed for a minimum of one hour at room temperature. Stability QCs for freeze-thaw stability were assessed using freshly prepared calibration standards and QCs for which stability had been proven. The F/T stability was acceptable if the precision (%CV) was ≤15.0% and mean accuracy of the stability QC samples at each concentration was 100.0% ± 15.0% of the nominal concentration.

Long-term stability

Long-term stability (LTS) of semaglutide in human plasma was evaluated after storing QC samples at −20°C for 97 days. LTS was assessed by assaying three aliquots of LQC and HQC stability samples along with a freshly prepared calibration curve and batch acceptance QCs. The storage stability interval was defined as the difference between the time the QCs were stored and the time the QCs were removed from storage. Note that the determination of stability at −20°C would cover stability at colder temperatures [30]. The long-term stability was acceptable if the precision (%CV) was ≤15.0% and the mean accuracy of the stability QC samples at each concentration was 100.0% ± 15.0% of the nominal concentration.

Benchtop stability

The benchtop stability of semaglutide in human plasma was assessed by processing three aliquots of QC samples at LQC and HQC concentrations after storage at room temperature for 24 hours. The benchtop stability was acceptable if the precision (%CV) was ≤15.0% and the mean accuracy of the stability QC samples at each concentration was 100.0% ± 15.0% of the nominal concentration.

Reinjection reproducibility

Extracted matrix blank, control zero (blank matrix with IS), calibration standards, and six replicates (n = 6) of LQC, MQC, and HQC samples from a batch that had met quantitative run acceptance criteria were stored at 6°C (e.g., autosampler or refrigerator) for 94 hours after the end of the original analysis. After this storage period, the batch was reinjected. The reinjection reproducibility was acceptable if the results met the acceptance criteria listed in sections 2.51 and 2.52.

Specificity

The specificity of the method for semaglutide bioanalysis was assessed by performing a mass-based exercise to determine the difference in molecular weight between semaglutide and possible concomitant medications (as described in the drug label information sheet). These medications included Metformin, Warfarin, Digoxin, Atorvastatin, Ethinylestradiol, Levonorgestrel, as well as Acetaminophen, and vitamins B12 and D. None of the possible comedications were within 20 daltons from semaglutide and therefore it was determined that there would be no effect on the analysis of semaglutide in the presence of these comedications.

Stock solution stability

The stability of semaglutide in a 4% formic acid in methanol/water (90:10) stock solution was evaluated at room temperature for at least 6 hours and at 6◦C for 7 days. One aliquot of a stock solution was kept at room temperature for 6 hours and another at 6◦C for 7 days and then diluted to a concentration for appropriate instrument response. One aliquot of a freshly prepared stock solution was diluted in the same manner. The stability of the stock solutions was considered acceptable if the absolute difference between the mean instrument response (peak area ratio) of the test solution and the freshly prepared solution was ≤10.0%.

Results

Method validation

Mass spectrometry results

The full Q1 mass scan of semaglutide in the QTRAP 6500+ mass spectrometer is shown in Figure 1(A). As a large molecular weight peptide (m.w. = 4114), multiple charged precursor ions were observed in the full scan of the protonated semaglutide. The most abundant precursor ion in the Q1 mass scan spectrum was [M + 4H]4 + at 1029.4 m/z. In the product ion mass spectra of the protonated semaglutide, several product ion fragments were observed (Figure 1(B)). Following analysis, the MRM transitions selected were m/z 1029.4 → 1238.2 for semaglutide and m/z 1039.1 → 1246.2 for the stable labeled internal standard, as these exhibited the greatest signal intensity with minimum interference for the quantification of semaglutide. The charge state of product ion illustrated in Figure 1(B) was [M + 3H]3+. Figure 2 shows the representative MRM chromatograms of semaglutide and the internal standard. The retention time of both semaglutide and the internal standard was 2.3 minutes.

No significant interference from endogenous components in six of the six plasma lots screened was observed at the retention time and mass transition of semaglutide and heavy semaglutide (IS). The percent peak area of the human plasma lots compared to the LLOQ standard ranged from 1.9% to 3.7%.

Hemolysis effect

The mean accuracy and precision of the hemolysis LQC samples were 88.7% and 5.0%, respectively, and the mean accuracy and precision of the hemolysis HQC samples were 96.0% and 7.2%, respectively. The results demonstrated that there was no effect from hemolysis with this method.

Lipemic effect

The mean accuracy and precision of the lipemic effect LQC samples were 93.3% and 12.5%, respectively, and the mean accuracy and precision of the lipemic effect HQC samples were 104.0% and 2.4%, respectively. The results demonstrated that there was no effect from lipemia with this method.

Matrix effect

The mean accuracy ranged between 91.3% to 101.3%, and the mean precision ranged from 1.5% to 8.2% for the LQC samples across all six matrix lots. The mean accuracy ranged between 92.7% to 103.3%, and the mean precision ranged from 2.5% to 8.1% for the HQC samples across all six matrix lots. The matrix effects data are presented in Table 5.

Carryover

The mean response of semaglutide in blank plasma injected after the ULOQ was 4.3%, ranging from 0.0% to 9.6%. The mean response for the IS in blank plasma was 1.5%, ranging from 0.1% to 3.9%. Both results met the acceptance criteria for carryover.

Dilution integrity

A DQC of semaglutide was prepared with blank human plasma at 1,000 ng/mL and diluted by a factor of 10 using control human plasma. Diluted samples were then analyzed (n = 6) to establish the dilution factor that can be applied to dilute samples into quantifiable range. The accuracy and precision across the six diluted samples were 88.3% and 1.8%, respectively, confirming integrity of dilutions up to 10-fold.

Whole blood (sample collection) stability

The differences between the mean responses (peak area ratio of analyte to internal standard) of the whole blood stability LQC samples stored for zero-hours and two-hours at room temperature and on wet ice were 7.2% and 10.6%, respectively. The differences between the mean responses of the whole blood stability HQC samples stored for zero-hours and two-hours at room temperature and on wet ice were 1.8% and 10.2%, respectively. Therefore, whole blood stability was established for two hours at room temperature and on wet ice.

Freeze/Thaw stability

LQC and HQC samples were cycled between room temperature and −20°C for five cycles. The accuracy and precision across the three LQC samples were 89.3% and 1.3%, respectively, and for the HQC samples, the accuracy and precision were 94.7% and 4.2%, respectively, demonstrating freeze/thaw stability across five cycles.

Long-term stability

LQC and HQC samples were maintained at −20°C for 97 days before thawing and analyzing. The accuracy and precision across the three LQC samples were 104.7% and 8.5%, respectively, and for the HQC samples, the accuracy and precision were 104.7% and 4.1%, respectively, demonstrating long-term stability for 97 days.

Benchtop stability

Benchtop stability at room temperature was established for 24 hours. The accuracy and precision across the three LQC samples were 91.3% and 7.1%, respectively, and for the HQC samples, the accuracy and precision were 94.7% and 3.6%, respectively.

Reinjection reproducibility

Reinjection reproducibility (autosampler stability) was assessed in extracted samples, which were maintained in an autosampler (set to 6°C) for 94 hours before being reinjected. The resulting accuracy and precision across the LQC samples were 100.0% and 1.8%, respectively. The resulting accuracy and precision across the MQC samples were 96.7% and 5.9%, respectively; and the resulting accuracy and precision across the HQC samples were 92.7% and 3.1%, respectively. The accuracy range of the standards across the calibration curve was 97.8% − 101.7%. Therefore, reinjection reproducibility was established at 6°C for 94 hours.

Stock solution stability

The stability of semaglutide in a stock solution composed of 4% formic acid in methanol/water (90:10) was evaluated at room temperature for 6 hours and at 6◦C for 7 days. One aliquot of a stock standard solution was kept at room temperature for 6 hours, then diluted to a concentration for appropriate instrument response. Another stock solution was kept at 6◦C for 7 days and tested against a fresh stock solution in the same manner. An aliquot of a freshly prepared stock solution was diluted in the same manner. The difference between the mean instrument response (peak area ratio) of the test solutions and the freshly prepared solutions was 2.8% for the 6-hour stock solution and 8.5% for the 7-day stock solution. The results demonstrate the stock solution was stable on the benchtop (room temperature) for 6 hours and at 6◦C for 7 days.

Discussion and conclusion

The method validation described within resulted in a robust, highly sensitive method capable of quantifying semaglutide in human plasma using a stable labeled isotope internal standard, and capable of analyzing clinical samples across a wide concentration range (5 ng/mL to 1000 ng/mL). The method represents a relatively simple, protein-precipitation-based triple-quadrupole HPLC-MS/MS approach for this purpose. The validation of this method was in accordance with the current 2022 FDA M10 guideline [30] for method validation and sample analysis and met all the requirements for subsequent use in supporting clinical studies of semaglutide. This method demonstrates accuracy and precision, calibration curve linearity, selectivity, recovery, hemolysis effect, lipemic effect, matrix effect, carryover, dilution integrity, whole blood stability, reinjection reproducibility, and stability including long-term, benchtop, and freeze/thaw stability. Investigations of possible interference of concomitant medications (specificity) with the quantitation of semaglutide were not conducted as part of this validation, but a rationale provided, namely, of all of the possible concomitant drugs that was surmised to be administered, none had a Q1 mass that was within 20 daltons of semaglutide. Since clinical samples were not analyzed as part of this validation, incurred sample reanalysis could not be assessed, which is usually conducted following method validation and during sample analysis.

The development and validation of this method demonstrated, importantly, that protein precipitation was sufficient to extract semaglutide and its internal standard. Previously, the extraction of other GLP-1 peptide analogs [6,15,16,18,25,26,31] often used solid phase extraction (SPE) or supportive liquid extraction (SLE) sorbent plates, which are more time consuming, costly, and prone to potential variations in recovery [6,18,23,31–34]. Furthermore, the novel method established in this paper can be extended to the bioanalysis of other GLP-1 agonist peptide-based therapeutics with similar structural and physiochemical properties. Currently, our laboratory is exploring the capabilities of the high‑resolution 8600 ZenoTOF (Sciex) mass spectrometer for the evaluation of GLP-1 peptides, including semaglutide, and comparing the associated sensitivity to that of the 6500+ QTRAP (Sciex) used for this and other GLP-1 peptide analyses.
