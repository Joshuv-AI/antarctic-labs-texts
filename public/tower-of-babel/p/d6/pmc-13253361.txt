High‐fat diet is associated with accelerated gray matter atrophy in cognitively unimpaired older adults but slower atrophy in individuals with existing mild cognitive impairment
Authors: Lei Fan, Yunyi Sun, Dandan Liu, W Hudson Robb, Kimberly R Pechman, Niranjana Shashikumar, Yukti Vyas, Bennett A Landman, Timothy J Hohman, Angela L Jefferson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONPrior studies showed inconsistent links between dietary fat and Alzheimer's disease (AD) risk. We examined whether dietary fat affected brain atrophy markers differentially based on risk factors like female sex, apolipoprotein ε4 (APOE ε4) status, and cognitive status.METHODSParticipants from the Vanderbilt Memory and Aging Project, classified as cognitively unimpaired (CU) or with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), were included (n = 758). Linear mixed‐effects regression models examined associations between total fat intake (Tfat, times/day) and percentage of energy from fat (Pfat, %) and longitudinal gray matter volumes, as well as interactions.RESULTSOver 4.6 ± 3.1 years, Pfat interacted with cognitive status on longitudinal temporal lobe (p = 0.009) and inferior lateral ventricle volume (p = 0.002). Higher Pfat was associated with faster reduction in temporal lobe volume in CU participants (β = 47.2, p = 0.007) but slower enlargement of the inferior lateral ventricle among participants with MCI (β = −22.5, p = 0.006).DISCUSSIONDifferent mechanisms may underlie the fat–neurodegeneration relationship across cognitive statuses.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, cognitive status, gray matter atrophy, high‐fat diet, quick food scan, Vanderbilt Memory and Aging Project

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) and related dementias are caused by gene–environment interactions, with up to 40% of the risk attributable to modifiable lifestyle factors, including diet. 1 High‐fat diet consumption has been linked to increased incidence of metabolic conditions like obesity, diabetes, and insulin resistance, which are established risk factors for sporadic AD. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 Yet epidemiological studies investigating the link between dietary fat intake and AD or dementia risk have yielded mixed results. For instance, while a Mendelian randomization analysis found that total fat and saturated fat intakes were associated with a higher risk of all forms of AD, 6 systematic reviews and meta‐analyses have reported null associations between intake of total fat, saturated fat, and unsaturated fat with dementia risk. 7 , 8

A chronic high‐fat diet leads to major metabolic disruptions in a mouse model, promoting neuropathological changes, including increased tau hyperphosphorylation and formation of neurofibrillary tangles. 9 , 10 The hallmark tau deposition leads to neurodegeneration and, in turn, glucose hypometabolism, both of which synergistically contribute to cognitive deficits and progressive loss of neurons. 11 , 12 Cortical thinning and brain atrophy assessed by structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) have been demonstrated to be sensitive markers in the early presymptomatic stages of AD. 13 , 14 AD‐specific regional atrophy, like in the hippocampus and temporal lobes, is a proxy measure of AD‐related neurodegeneration and pathology. 15 , 16 , 17

Past evidence suggested that obesity and visceral fat accumulation correlated with accelerated brain aging, 18 diminished volume, and cortical thickness in the prefrontal cortex and temporal areas. 19 , 20 , 21 While obesity and adiposity have been extensively investigated, longitudinal associations between high‐fat diet as a modifiable lifestyle factor and early imaging markers of AD indicative of neurodegeneration and brain atrophy have been less studied in non‐dementia older adults at the presymptomatic and prodromal stages. Furthermore, several AD risk factors, including female sex, apolipoprotein ε4 (APOE‐ε4) status, and cognitive status, may contribute to the discrepancies in previous human studies investigating fat in relation to AD. 22 , 23 , 24 For example, subtypes of fatty acids have varying effects on the risk of developing mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and AD, depending on an individual's cognitive status at baseline. 22 , 23 APOE ε4 carriers in the prodromal AD phase undergo significantly faster gray matter atrophy in the hippocampus and temporal neocortex than APOE ε4 non‐carriers. 25 Understanding the role of dietary fat in AD‐relevant brain atrophy, particularly in AD high‐risk subgroups, will facilitate the development of precision nutrition interventions in presymptomatic stages of AD. The current study aimed to examine whether dietary fat intake at study entry was associated with longitudinal changes in gray matter atrophy detected by structural MRI over a follow‐up period of up to 9 years, as well as their interactions with cognitive status, sex, and APOE ε4 status among older adults free of clinical dementia and stroke at baseline.

METHODS

Study cohort

The Vanderbilt Memory and Aging Project (VMAP) is a longitudinal observational study investigating vascular and neurological health and aging and includes community‐dwelling participants free of clinical dementia. 26 The current study included 709 participants from the VMAP Legacy Cohort and the Expansion Cohort. Recruitment of the Legacy Cohort began in September 2012 and required participants to be age ≥60 years, speak English, have adequate auditory and visual acuity, and have a reliable study partner. Recruitment of the Expansion Cohort started in August 2021 and is ongoing, enrolling participants aged 50 and older, with lumbar puncture being optional only for participants from underrepresented groups. Participants were excluded for a cognitive diagnosis of dementia, MRI contraindication, history of neurological disease (e.g., multiple sclerosis, stroke), heart failure, major psychiatric illness, head injury with loss of consciousness > 5 min, or a systemic or terminal illness affecting follow‐up participation. As previously described, participants were cognitively classified as cognitively unimpaired (CU), early MCI, and MCI. 27 CU was defined as (1) Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) = 0 (no dementia), (2) no deficits in activities of daily living directly attributable to cognitive impairment, and (3) no evidence of neuropsychological impairment defined as standard scores falling within 1.5 standard deviations (SD) of the age‐adjusted normative mean. Early MCI was defined as (1) CDR = 0.5 (reflecting mild severity of impairment), (2) no deficits in activities of daily living attributable to cognitive issues, and (3) no neuropsychological impairment defined as standard scores falling within 1.5 SD of the age‐adjusted normative mean. 28 MCI was defined as (1) CDR = 0 or 0.5 (reflecting mild severity of impairment); (2) relatively spared activities of daily living; (3) neuropsychological impairment within at least one cognitive domain (i.e., performances falling > 1.5 SD outside the age‐adjusted normative mean or pre‐morbid level of functioning); (4) concern of a cognitive change by the participant, informant, or clinician; and (5) absence of a dementing syndrome. 29 At enrollment, participants completed a comprehensive examination, including (but not limited to) a fasting blood draw, physical examination, clinical interview, echocardiogram, brain MRI, and an optional lumbar puncture. 27 Data collection was performed at baseline and at 18‐month, 3‐year, 5‐year, 7‐year, and 9‐year follow‐up visits. 30 Participants included in this current study had at least one epoch of follow‐up MRI data and were excluded for missing baseline structural MRI data or baseline covariate data. The protocol, guided by the Belmont Report, was approved by the Vanderbilt University Medical Center Institutional Review Board, and written informed consent was obtained prior to data collection. Data, analytic methods, and study materials can be obtained by contacting the corresponding author.

Dietary fat intake assessment

The Quick Food Scan (QFS) fat screener is a brief National Cancer Institute (NCI)‐developed tool to capture an individual's usual intake of percentage energy from fat. 31 In NCI validation studies, the QFS fat assessment captures total dietary fat intake using a data‐driven, pragmatic approach based on analyses of a large‐scale, nationally representative dietary data source, the US Department of Agriculture's 1994–1996 Continuing Survey of Food Intakes by Individuals (CSFII). Fifteen food items were selected as the strongest predictors of fat intake variability, incorporating portion‐size estimates and gender‐specific regression coefficients in the regression equation. 31 , 32 For example, high‐fat food items included eggs, sausage or bacon, beef or pork hot dogs, cheese, French fries, mayonnaise, salad dressings, added margarine, butter, or oil on bread, veggies, rice, or pasta. In contrast, low‐fat food items included skim milk, orange juice or grapefruit juice, fruit, rice, and cold cereal. 31 By including both high‐fat contributors and low‐fat markers, the QFS fat screener captures the full spectrum of fat intake variation more accurately with just 15 food items. Validation against 24‐h recalls and a complete 120‐item food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) showed that the QFS fat screener provided estimates and correlations comparable to true intake, confirming good predictive performance with minimal respondent burden. 33 , 34 NCI‐recommended scoring procedures were used to derive variables, including total fat intake (Tfat, times/day) and percentage of energy from fat (Pfat, %). Tfat and Pfat reflect similar yet mildly different aspects of dietary fat intake. While a higher Tfat indicates a higher amount of fat consumption based on frequency, the Pfat further adjusted the impact of inter‐person differences in total calorie intake and reflects the relative contribution of energy from dietary fat. The age‐ and sex‐specific portion sizes for each food were from two nonconsecutive 24‐h dietary recalls in the US Department of Agriculture's 1994–1996 CSFII. 35 Tfat was quantified by the sum of the margarine, butter, or oil added on bread, rolls, pancakes; margarine or butter on vegetables, including potatoes; and margarine, butter, or oil on rice or pasta 35 :

Structural brain MRI

Between 2012 and 2017, participants were scanned at the Vanderbilt University Institute of Imaging Science on a 3T Philips Achieva system (Best, the Netherlands) with an eight‐channel SENSE receiver head coil. In 2017, the system was upgraded to a 32‐channel dStream head coil. Detailed imaging parameters have been previously described. 36 In brief, in the VMAP Legacy Cohort, T1‐weighted MPRAGE images were acquired using the following parameters: TR = 8.9 ms, TE = 4.6 ms, and spatial resolution = 1 × 1 × 1 mm3. In the Expansion Cohort and VMAP Legacy Cohort from 9‐year follow‐up and onward, T1‐weighted MPRAGE images were acquired using the following parameters: TR = 6.5 ms, TE = 2.9 ms, and spatial resolution = 1 × 1 × 1 mm3.

T1 images were post‐processed with an established Multi‐Atlas Segmentation pipeline, 37 , 38 including total gray matter volume and lobar volumes (frontal, parietal, temporal, occipital), to determine general patterns of atrophy. Additionally, hippocampal volume and inferior lateral ventricle volume were measured to determine AD‐specific patterns of atrophy. 39 Because T1‐weighted image sequence parameters differed between the VMAP Legacy and Expansion Cohorts, we used longitudinal ComBat to harmonize regional brain volumes across both cohorts, as previously described. 40 Visual checks were completed for all images to look for artifacts or errors in acquisition. Multi‐Atlas segmentations were reviewed for registration and segmentation errors. For non‐harmonized and harmonized values, we generated spaghetti plots and checked that there are no values outside of expected ranges.

Analytical plan

Linear regression models with ordinary least squares estimates cross‐sectionally related baseline Tfat and Pfat to gray matter volumes (total, frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital lobe, hippocampus, and inferior lateral ventricle), adjusting for covariates including age (continuous), sex (male, female), race/ethnicity (non‐Hispanic White, other), education years (continuous), APOE ε4 status (yes/no carrier), Framingham Stroke Risk Profile (FSRP, minus points for age, continuous), cognitive status (CU, early MCI, MCI), and intracranial volume (ICV). The aforementioned covariates were determined a priori based on potential AD‐related confounding factors. 30 , 41 For longitudinal analyses, linear mixed‐effects regression models with follow‐up time interaction terms were used to examine associations between baseline Tfat and Pfat and longitudinal gray matter volumes, adjusting for covariates as above. Interactions with cognitive status (CU vs MCI), sex (male vs female), and APOE ε4 status (carrier vs non‐carrier) were tested, followed by stratified analyses in each subgroup. The threshold for statistical significance was set a priori at p < 0.05 using a two‐sided test. A false discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple comparisons (for each set of analyses per hypothesis) was performed using the Benjamini‐Hochberg procedure. 42 Statistical analyses were completed using R 4.1.2 (www.r‐project.org)

Interactions with cognitive status, sex, and APOE ε4 status

Pfat and Tfat interactions with sex and APOE ε4 status on longitudinal gray matter volume

Although the interaction test of Pfat and Tfat with sex on longitudinal gray matter volumes was not significant, higher Pfat was associated with a slower rate of enlargement in inferior lateral ventricle volume (β = −8.02, p = 0.003, pFDR = 0.02) among female individuals. While higher Tfat was associated with a faster rate of enlargement in inferior lateral ventricle volume (β = 38.8, p = 0.03) among male individuals, the association was not significant after multiple‐comparisons correction (pFDR = 0.26) (Table S2). We did not observe significant interactions between Pfat and Tfat with APOE ε4 status on longitudinal total and region‐specific gray matter volumes (Table S3).

Secondary analyses

We further conducted subgroup analyses to assess the association between Pfat and inferior lateral ventricle volume in individuals with existing MCI. Although the interaction tests failed to achieve significance, higher Pfat was significantly associated with a slower rate of enlargement of the inferior lateral ventricle in APOE ε4 carriers (β = −36.0, p = 0.003) and/or female individuals (β = −28.4, p = 0.005) (Table S4).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we captured total and region‐specific gray matter atrophy over a mean follow‐up period of 4.6 years and found that higher baseline dietary fat intake was associated with faster reduction in temporal lobe volume among CU older adults but not in participants with existing MCI at baseline. In contrast, higher dietary fat intake was associated with slower brain atrophy among individuals with MCI, which is mainly driven by APOE ε4 carriers and female participants.

The current findings advance our understanding of the dynamic role of dietary fat in neurodegeneration and AD progression. We observed that dietary fat intake was associated with a faster reduction in temporal lobe volume among CU adults, suggesting that accelerated neurodegeneration may occur in the presymptomatic stages of AD, which warrants early intervention. Previous animal studies found that early‐life exposure to a high‐fat diet increased vulnerability to its detrimental effects on structural and cognitive changes, with greater hippocampal and memory impairment observed compared to consumption of the same diet in later life. 43 , 44 Notably, the impaired hippocampal function induced by a chronic high‐fat diet during early ages can be restored by switching to a control diet. This indicates a critical period in which healthy lifestyle changes can partially reverse damage and protect against AD onset. 45 , 46 Epidemiological studies found distinct impacts of fat on AD risk across the life course, varying by disease stage. 47 , 48 , 49 , 50 , 51 Consistent with past work, the current findings suggest that dietary fat may play a critical role in driving the faster neurodegeneration in the early presymptomatic stage of AD.

To the best of our knowledge, we are among the first to report that higher dietary fat intake was associated with slower rate of enlargement in inferior lateral ventricle volume, reflecting medial temporal lobe atrophy among individuals with existing MCI. While some longitudinal studies focusing on overall brain measures or the hippocampus alone reported progressively accelerated brain atrophy throughout the disease process, 52 , 53 other studies found that AD‐specific cortical thinning and atrophy exhibited a sigmoidal pattern, with initial acceleration followed by deceleration in later stages of AD. 54 , 55 The rate of AD‐specific cortical thinning increased with deteriorating cognitive function, peaking at approximately the Mini‐Mental State Examination score of 21, which indicates MCI or mild dementia, beyond which the rate of thinning started to decline. 54 We observed halted gray matter atrophy, manifested as slower rate of enlargement in inferior lateral ventricle volume among individuals with existing MCI, suggesting that different mechanisms may be involved in the fat–neurodegeneration association during the MCI phase compared to the CU phase.

A high‐fat diet may serve as part of compensatory mechanisms and alternative energy sources for brain hypometabolism and, in turn, may halt brain atrophy in the prodromal stage of AD. Microstructural changes, such as decreased neurite density in the gray matter and hippocampus, occur as the Braak stage increases, posing significant challenges to metabolic and functional needs. 56 Animal studies found that a high‐fat diet in rats induced compromised glucose utilization, exacerbated oxidative metabolism in neurons and astrocytes, and triggered metabolic shifts toward glycolysis for lactate production and other available substrates, such as fats. 57 , 58 Past quantitative kinetic positron emission tomography and MRI imaging studies found glucose‐specific deterioration in brain energy metabolism and mild ketone hypermetabolism in MCI. 59 Furthermore, a 45% decline in glucose metabolism and an 18% decline in the metabolic rate of oxygen have been observed during the transition from CU to dementia, 60 driving the need for energy substrates, which may contribute to the varying influence of dietary fat consumption across the cognitive aging spectrum. Additionally, being overweight or obese in midlife increases the risk of late‐onset dementia. In contrast, weight loss and malnourishment are highly prevalent in patients with MCI or dementia, indicating a shift in energy metabolism from glucose to fats. 61 Halted gray matter atrophy, observed exclusively in individuals with existing MCI, may reflect a potential compensatory mechanism in response to metabolic challenges such as glucose hypometabolism, striving to meet the metabolic needs required for neuronal activities.

While we proposed mechanistic underpinnings such as compensatory mechanisms and alternative energy sources in MCI subgroups, the interpretation of these results warrants caution. One alternative explanation for these discrepant findings is reverse causation, wherein early cognitive symptoms prompt dietary modifications. Although we observed a balanced percentage of energy from fat between CU and MCI participants at baseline, we cannot fully rule out the possibility of an alternative explanation that intentional adoption of a high‐fat diet inflates apparent protective associations in the MCI group. Additionally, survival bias may contribute to the observed patterns. Higher dietary fat intake may confer survival advantages, thereby amplifying protective associations among MCI participants who remain in the study. In contrast, CU participants may experience less selective pressure, suggesting no obvious effects of high‐fat intake. Future cohort designs with minimal attrition and linkage to mortality registries would strengthen causal inferences.

Our drill‐down subgroup analyses confirmed that the high‐fat diet‐associated halted atrophy in individuals with existing MCI was mainly driven by AD high‐risk subgroups, such as APOE ε4 carriers and/or female individuals who are most vulnerable to significant AD pathology and glucose hypometabolism. Impaired lipid transport promotes the formation of neurotoxic lipid droplets in APOE ε4 carriers, who are more susceptible to exacerbated metabolic dysfunctions due to desensitized hypothalamic neurons and reduced glucose metabolism. 62 Previous evidence also showed that increased mitochondrial fatty acid metabolism and the catabolism of myelin lipids to generate ketone bodies for adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production underlie white matter degeneration in the aging female brain, serving as an adaptive response to meet the brain's fuel and energy demand. 63 Fat may serve as an alternative energy source and mitigate neurodegeneration in APOE ε4 carriers and/or female individuals with existing cognitive deficits, who face substantial challenges in maintaining cognitive function and energy metabolism. Nevertheless, these post hoc analyses remain exploratory, were performed in small subsets of participants, and should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Subregional brain measures, such as hippocampal volume, often exhibit smaller effect sizes and greater variability compared to global measures in aging studies. This heterogeneity may contribute to findings that did not survive multiple‐comparisons corrections, as observed in a subset of associations in the current study. While the two main interactions remained significant after this correction, future studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to confirm these findings.

We used the QFS fat screener, a validated instrument specifically designed to capture the usual (habitual) percentage of energy from fat over the past 12 months, rather than short‐term or single‐day consumption. Despite this design intent, a single dietary assessment at baseline may introduce non‐differential measurement error due to within‐person variation in intake. In most prospective contexts, non‐differential error tends to dilute the association and bias toward the null, meaning the true associations are expected to be stronger than reported. Beyond non‐differential error, differential misclassification is also possible as emerging cognitive changes could influence subsequent dietary intake or reporting, potentially contributing to reverse causation and inflating observed associations. To mitigate these concerns, we excluded participants with dementia from enrollment, as their diets may undergo substantial changes due to increased caregiver involvement. Our analyses were restricted to participants who were CU or had MCI at baseline and who were more likely to maintain their habitual diets independently. Furthermore, we used dietary data collected exclusively at baseline, ensuring that exposure measurement preceded all imaging outcomes, and they were not the result of deteriorating cognition after controlling for multiple covariates. These methodological strategies strengthen causal inference by establishing a clearer temporal sequence between baseline dietary intake and subsequent changes in imaging outcomes and minimize the risk of reverse causation.

The current study has several strengths, including a clinically well‐characterized longitudinal aging cohort with scientifically rigorous methods, careful consideration and statistical adjustment of potential confounding factors, use of core laboratories using quality control procedures to analyze brain MRI measurements with technicians blinded to participant information, and the well‐validated NCI QFS questionnaire for measuring dietary fat intake in comparison to multiple non‐consecutive 24‐h dietary recalls and FFQ as reference instruments. Limitations include restricted generalizability due to our cohort being predominantly White, well educated, and relatively healthy. Additional limitations include a lack of information regarding subtypes of fats and a single time measurement of self‐reported dietary fat intake, which may underestimate the true associations and may not reflect long‐term variation in dietary intake. Finally, using multiple comparisons may increase the risk of Type I error, which was mitigated by using a Benjamini‐Hochberg procedure to correct the FDR.

CONCLUSION

High‐fat diet is associated with accelerated gray matter atrophy, particularly in AD‐relevant regions such as the temporal lobe, in CU adults, but is associated with slower atrophy in individuals with existing MCI, which is mainly driven by APOE ε4 carriers and/or female individuals. Different mechanisms may be involved in the fat–neurodegeneration relationship across cognitive statuses. Halted atrophy associated with a high‐fat diet, observed exclusively in individuals with existing MCI, may reflect the role of fat as part of a compensatory mechanism in response to progressive AD pathology and metabolic challenges.
