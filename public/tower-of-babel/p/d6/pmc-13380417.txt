When “Candida ciferrii” Isn't Candida: Sequencing reveals multidrug-resistant Blastobotrys adeninivorans PD peritonitis
Authors: Uraiwan Parinyasiri, Korawee Sukmee, Banpot Suwatronnakorn, Dhammika Leshan Wannigama, Tanittha Chatsuwan, Talerngsak Kanjanabuch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fungal peritonitis is a severe complication of peritoneal dialysis (PD), increasingly involving rare yeasts that challenge routine diagnostics. We report PD-associated peritonitis caused by Blastobotrys adeninivorans, initially misidentified as Candida ciferrii by automated platforms (VITEK 2 and MALDI-TOF MS). Persistent neutrophilic inflammation and elevated effluent β-D-glucan prompted multilocus sequencing, which confirmed B. adeninivorans. Susceptibility testing showed markedly elevated MICs to amphotericin B, azoles, and echinocandins, interpreted cautiously because validated breakpoints are unavailable. This case illustrates how persistent culture-positive PD peritonitis with discordant or unusual yeast identification should prompt molecular confirmation. Clinical resolution followed PD catheter removal and transition to haemodialysis, underscoring the importance of early source control in refractory PD-associated fungal peritonitis.

Introduction

Fungal peritonitis is among the most serious complications of peritoneal dialysis (PD), often resulting in catheter removal, transfer to haemodialysis (HD), and substantial mortality [1,2]. Although Candida species remain the predominant pathogens, molecular diagnostics have broadened the recognised spectrum of PD-associated fungal infections to include rare yeasts that may be misidentified by conventional phenotypic or automated systems. These organisms can also show variable and poorly characterised antifungal susceptibility profiles, making accurate species-level identification important for both clinical management and epidemiologic surveillance [3,4].

The genus Blastobotrys comprises environmental, dimorphic ascomycetous yeasts within the order Dipodascales. Blastobotrys adeninivorans (syn. Arxula adeninivorans; basionym Trichosporon adeninivorans) was originally described as a soil-associated yeast [5]. Human infection is rare, with reported presentations including catheter-related fungaemia and pulmonary disease with bloodstream involvement, mainly in vulnerable hosts [6,7]. Its role in PD-associated peritonitis, however, remains poorly defined. Under-recognition is likely, as routine diagnostic platforms may misclassify Blastobotrys species because of limited reference databases, phenotypic overlap with common yeasts, and taxonomic proximity to sibling species. In particular, overlap with members of the Candida ciferrii complex may lead to erroneous identification unless molecular confirmation is performed [8,9].

Here, we report a case of PD-associated fungal peritonitis initially identified as Candida ciferrii by automated methods and subsequently reclassified as Blastobotrys adeninivorans by multilocus sequencing. The isolate showed markedly elevated MICs across all tested antifungal classes. This case underscores the diagnostic value of sequencing for unusual yeasts and the central role of early source control in refractory PD-associated fungal peritonitis.

Case presentation

A 61-year-old man with kidney failure secondary to long-standing type 2 diabetes mellitus and hypertension was receiving continuous ambulatory PD (CAPD; 2 L × 4 exchanges/day). His PD catheter had been inserted on 31 July 2019, and PD was initiated on 13 August 2019. He performed exchanges independently. Baseline comorbid features included anaemia and hypoalbuminaemia.

On Day 0, 27 January 2020, he developed abdominal pain and cloudy peritoneal dialysis effluent (PDE) without fever. He was haemodynamically stable, with blood pressure 173/81 mmHg, pulse 86 beats/min, and respiratory rate 20 breaths/min. Initial laboratory testing showed haemoglobin 8.5–9.9 g/dL, leukocyte count approximately 13.1 × 103/μL, serum albumin 2.4 g/dL, blood urea nitrogen 38 mg/dL, and serum creatinine 8.3 mg/dL. On Day +1, PDE contained 6560 cells/μL with 99% neutrophils; Gram stain was negative. Empirical intraperitoneal cefazolin and ceftazidime were started at 250 mg per bag, 4 exchanges daily, together with oral fluconazole prophylaxis at 200 mg/day. PDE culture subsequently yielded Escherichia coli; cefazolin was discontinued and ceftazidime was continued.

Despite initial antibacterial therapy, the inflammatory course was not fully controlled. The PDE leukocyte count remained elevated at 3200 cells/μL on Day +7, decreased to 142 cells/μL on Day +12, and then rose again to 330 cells/μL on Day +17. Fungal culture was performed by inoculating PDE onto Sabouraud dextrose agar, potato dextrose agar, and liquid medium, followed by incubation at both 25–30 °C and 35–37 °C. On Day +15, repeat cultures from PDE and the PD transfer set yielded a yeast initially identified by routine phenotypic methods as Candida ciferrii. Subsequent fungal cultures obtained after antifungal initiation remained positive for the same yeast. Colonies on Sabouraud dextrose agar and potato dextrose agar were dry, cerebriform, and wrinkled (Fig. 2A and B). Lactophenol cotton blue preparation showed temperature-dependent morphology, with predominantly yeast forms at 25 °C and filamentous elements at higher temperatures (Fig. 2C–E). Antifungal therapy was escalated to oral voriconazole, with a loading dose of 6 mg/kg twice daily on Day +18, followed by 4 mg/kg twice daily from Day +19 to Day +44. Persistent neutrophilic inflammation in PDE, however, suggested ongoing infection. Repeat cultures obtained after initiation of antifungal therapy remained positive for the same yeast. The discrepancy between the refractory clinical course and the automated identification as C. ciferrii raised suspicion of an uncommon or misidentified yeast. PDE β-D-glucan was markedly elevated (>523 pg/mL), whereas PDE and serum galactomannan remained negative. Prior PDE studies support β-D-glucan and galactomannan as adjunctive biomarkers for fungal peritonitis, with provisional cut-offs of ≥240 pg/mL and ≥0.5, respectively; the negative galactomannan result was interpreted cautiously given its limited sensitivity for some yeast-associated peritonitis [10,11].

Multilocus sequencing of colonies recovered from PDE established the organism as Blastobotrys adeninivorans. Small subunit ribosomal RNA (SSU rRNA) sequencing with NS1-NS4 primers showed 99.9% identity and 100% coverage to AB000659.1. Large subunit ribosomal RNA (LSU rRNA) sequencing with NL1–NL4 primers showed 99.2% identity and 100% coverage to KY106199.1. Internal transcribed spacer (ITS) sequencing with ITS1–ITS4 primers confirmed the identification, with 100% identity and 100% coverage to MK737546.1. Species assignment was based on concordant high-identity matches across SSU rRNA, LSU rRNA, and ITS loci, requiring ≥99% identity and ≥98% query coverage; ITS was used as the primary species-level discriminator. Sequencing targets, primers, database matches, query coverage, and assignment criteria are detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

Sequencing of the RNA polymerase II second-largest subunit gene using fRPB2-5F and fRPB2-7cR primers showed low identity. This result was attributed to limited curated Blastobotrys RPB2 reference data and greater variability of this protein-coding locus, and was not considered to undermine the ribosomal-locus identification. Calmodulin and β-tubulin sequencing using cmd5–cmd6 and Bt2a–Bt2b primers, respectively, was not interpretable.

Repeat identification by VITEK 2 and MALDI-TOF MS, including VITEK MS with SARAMIS/PRIME V3.3 databases, continued to classify the isolate as Candida ciferrii. The absence of B. adeninivorans from the VITEK MS PRIME V3.3 knowledge base likely accounted for this persistent misidentification. Its inclusion in the research-use SARAMIS V4.17 database suggests that updated or expanded spectral libraries may improve recognition of this species.

Antifungal susceptibility testing was performed by broth microdilution according to the CLSI M27 reference method, with MIC endpoints read using EUCAST criteria. Quality control was performed with Candida albicans ATCC 90028, and testing was performed in duplicate. MICs were markedly elevated for amphotericin B (>16 mg/L), fluconazole (>64 mg/L), voriconazole, itraconazole, and posaconazole (>16 mg/L each), and caspofungin (>8 mg/L). Because validated clinical breakpoints are unavailable for Blastobotrys, these values were reported descriptively and were not interpreted as categorical resistance. The susceptibility method and MIC values are summarised in Supplementary Table S1.

In the setting of refractory fungal peritonitis, persistent peritoneal inflammation, and markedly elevated MICs across the tested antifungal classes, the PD catheter was removed on Day +23 and the patient was transitioned to haemodialysis, with subsequent clinical resolution. He was alive at last follow-up in May 2026.

Discussion

This case illustrates how diagnostic uncertainty, taxonomic complexity, and markedly reduced antifungal susceptibility can converge in PD-associated fungal peritonitis. The isolate was repeatedly identified as Candida ciferrii by routine platforms, including VITEK 2 and MALDI-TOF MS, but ribosomal multilocus sequencing established Blastobotrys adeninivorans as the causative organism. This distinction was clinically important: species-level identification determined how the MIC profile should be interpreted and strengthened the rationale for early source control.

The initial misidentification reflects a recognised limitation of phenotypic and database-dependent systems when rare yeasts are recovered from sterile sites. Blastobotrys species belong to a taxonomically challenging group of uncommon ascomycetous yeasts, with phenotypic overlap and limited representation in commercial identification libraries [12]. International recommendations from ISHAM, ECMM, and ASM advise confirmatory sequencing or verification against curated MALDI-TOF MS databases when sterile-site yeast isolates yield ambiguous, unexpected, or clinically discordant identification results [9]. In this case, persistent recovery of yeast from PDE and the PD transfer set, together with ongoing neutrophilic inflammation despite antifungal prophylaxis, prompted molecular work-up. Concordant high-identity matches across SSU rRNA, LSU rRNA, and ITS resolved the discrepancy; RPB2 was less informative, most likely because of limited curated Blastobotrys reference data and greater variability of this protein-coding locus.

The biomarker profile provided supportive, but not standalone, evidence of fungal infection. PDE β-D-glucan was markedly elevated, exceeding provisional PDE thresholds proposed in prior studies, whereas galactomannan remained negative. This pattern is compatible with yeast-associated peritonitis, as galactomannan has limited sensitivity for several yeasts compared with mould-associated disease [10,13]. In this patient, β-D-glucan was therefore interpreted in context: persistent neutrophilic inflammation, repeat yeast isolation, device involvement, and molecular confirmation together supported a superimposed fungal process.

The susceptibility profile was notable. Broth microdilution showed markedly elevated MICs to amphotericin B, azoles, and caspofungin. Because validated clinical breakpoints are unavailable for Blastobotrys, these results should not be labelled as categorical resistance. They are best reported descriptively as reduced susceptibility across the tested antifungal classes. Current rare-yeast guidance emphasises that MIC interpretation should integrate organism identity, pharmacology, infection site, and clinical trajectory rather than extrapolating directly from Candida breakpoints [9]. In this setting, the combination of persistent peritoneal inflammation, refractory clinical course, and multiclass MIC elevation supported prioritisation of catheter removal.

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of B. adeninivorans PD-associated peritonitis. A focused PubMed and Scopus search using combinations of “Blastobotrys,” “Arxula,” “adeninivorans,” “peritonitis,” “peritoneal dialysis,” “fungaemia,” and “antifungal susceptibility,” without date restriction, identified no prior B. adeninivorans peritonitis. The only previously reported Blastobotrys PD-associated peritonitis was caused by B. proliferans [11]. Other invasive Blastobotrys infections have been described mainly as catheter-related fungaemia and pulmonary disease in vulnerable hosts [6,7]. Consistent with the present case, prior reports suggest reduced fluconazole susceptibility, variable activity of other agents, and favourable outcomes often linked to device removal or other source-control measures [6,14].

This report has limitations. Whole-genome sequencing and formal phylogenetic analysis were not performed, and the molecular basis of the observed reduced susceptibility could not be determined. Susceptibility testing is also difficult to interpret for rare yeasts without species-specific clinical breakpoints. Finally, although β-D-glucan and galactomannan have been evaluated in PDE, the proposed PDE thresholds are based on limited pilot and proof-of-concept data and should be interpreted as adjunctive rather than definitive diagnostic markers.

Management of PD-associated fungal peritonitis hinges on source control. The ISPD 2022 Peritonitis Guidelines recommend early catheter removal once fungal peritonitis is identified or strongly suspected, as delayed removal is associated with poor outcomes [2,15]. In this patient, persistent inflammation despite antifungal therapy and markedly elevated MICs led to PD catheter removal on Day +23 and transition to haemodialysis, followed by clinical resolution. The clinical course reinforces that, in refractory fungal peritonitis, eradication of the infected catheter is often more decisive than escalation of antifungal therapy alone.

In summary, this case highlights the diagnostic limitations of routine identification systems for rare yeasts, the value of sequencing for sterile-site isolates with discordant clinical behaviour, and the need for cautious MIC interpretation when breakpoints are unavailable. It also suggests that PDE β-D-glucan may be a useful adjunctive marker and reinforces early catheter removal as the key intervention in PD-associated fungal peritonitis.
