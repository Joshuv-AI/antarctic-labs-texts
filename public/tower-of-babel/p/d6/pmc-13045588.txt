American-Style Football Players
Authors: Rachel Grashow, Campbell Rogers, Jonathan Kim, Aaron Baggish
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Visual Summary

American-style football (ASF) is one of America's most popular sports. There are 1 million, 80,000, and 2,000 active participants at the high school level, collegiate, and professional levels, respectively, and the number of living former ASF athletes is an order of magnitude greater. Although sports participation is typically regarded as a health-promoting activity, prior data suggest that participation in ASF may place athletes at risk for early-life subclinical cardiovascular disease. Specifically, a growing body of literature has documented a maladaptive cardiovascular phenotype among young competitive collegiate players characterized by vascular stiffening, incident hypertension, and concentric left ventricular hypertrophy with relative functional impairment.1 The implications of these findings, with respect to later life health outcomes, remain poorly understood and underscore the need for a dedicated study of post-career ASF players.Take-Home Messages•Further research is required to understand why American-style football players demonstrate increased cardiovascular mortality and morbidity despite their elite fitness status.•Novel risk factors have emerged as significant contributors to this maladaptive phenotype (eg, head injury, deliberate weight gain, and anterior cruciate ligament injury), underscoring the need for well-designed clinical studies that better protect athletic and general populations from preventable cardiac diseases.

Novel risk factors have emerged as significant contributors to this maladaptive phenotype (eg, head injury, deliberate weight gain, and anterior cruciate ligament injury), underscoring the need for well-designed clinical studies that better protect athletic and general populations from preventable cardiac diseases.

Mortality studies have consistently shown increased longevity among ASF players compared with the general population. However, studies that compare elite athletes with nonathletic populations are subject to healthy worker effects (ie, intrinsic differences in lifelong physical activity habits, access to medical care, financial resources, and performance-enhancing genetic differences), which can lead to biased effect estimates and misguided conclusions. Not surprisingly, comparison of professional ASF players with athletes from other professional sports leagues (eg, Major League Baseball) have found significantly elevated rates of cardiovascular deaths among National Football League players, even after adjustment for race and age.2 To better understand reasons for increased cardiovascular mortality among National Football League players, additional studies of active and post-career players are warranted.

Ongoing work in active and former player cohorts suggests that both traditional risk factors and novel football-specific exposures contribute synergistically to overall cardiovascular risk. Among a prospective cohort of collegiate ASF players, concussion was independently associated with short-term increases in blood pressure and pulse wave velocity and relative impairment of left ventricular diastolic function.3 In addition, cumulative head injury exposure during ASF has been associated with increased odds of post-career hypertension and hypercholesterolemia,4 and anterior cruciate ligament tears during active play are associated with higher risk of myocardial infarction in later life.5 Finally, weight gain during the transition from collegiate to professional ASF participation is an independent predictor of later life incident cardiovascular and cardiometabolic disease. In aggregate, these findings have relevance beyond mortality risk and begin to explain the recent observation that former professional ASF players experience premature cardiovascular and metabolic aging, leading to a 10-year reduction in healthspan compared with the general population.6 Future work delineating the relationships between traumatic head and joint injury and cardiovascular health should be conducted to examine these phenomena in other sport types and at-risk populations.

Atherosclerotic coronary artery disease is the most important final common pathway linking subclinical cardiovascular risk to adverse outcomes. Studies documenting the burden of atherosclerotic coronary disease in former ASF athletes are limited. One prior study documented coronary artery calcium (CAC) levels in former ASF players that were higher than expected based on traditional coronary disease risk factors.7 Another suggested that men who played at an ASF lineman field position are more likely to demonstrate a high CAC burden than men who played at other field positions.8 Mechanisms underlying these observations remain speculative. Prior work has not adequately addressed the independent or perhaps synergistic relationships between traditional drivers of cardiovascular disease (eg, alcohol consumption, sleep disorders, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia) and football-specific determinants of risk. In addition, the existing data rely solely on the use of CAC scoring, which is an imperfect surrogate for clinically relevant atherosclerosis in athletes as it does not reflect noncalcified plaque burden. To further untangle the complex interplay between ASF participation, traditional and football-specific risk factors, and long-term outcomes, imaging studies are needed. Specifically, imaging techniques that permit comprehensive coronary artery phenotyping with the ability to quantify high-risk plaque burden and physiologic impairment coupled with long-term outcomes data will be key.

Future study of coronary biology can be justified simply by the large number of former youth, collegiate, and professional ASF players who may be uniquely at risk. Beyond this discrete population, former ASF players serve as a valuable working model for others who experience repeated head injury including military personnel, other contact and combat sport athletes, victims of domestic violence, and patients with traumatic brain injury. Recent evidence from clinical populations with a single episode of mild, moderate, and severe head injury demonstrated increased risk of adverse cardiometabolic outcomes within 5 years of injury. The value of studying former ASF athletes extends beyond the goals of changing the trajectory of sport and improving the long-term health outcomes of former ASF athletes. This line of inquiry holds the potential to inform the clinical care of similar at-risk populations, including but not limited to other sporting disciplines, and therefore represents a critical area study worthy of further scientific investment.
