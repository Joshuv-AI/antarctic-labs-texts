Natural History of Fetal Non‐Nuchal Lymphatic Malformations: A Single Center Retrospective Study
Authors: Yada Kunpalin, Dilkash Kajal, Lennart Van der Veeken, David Chitayat, Manuel Carcao, Paolo Campisi, Greg Ryan, Shiri Shinar, Tim Van Mieghem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo document the natural history and complications of second‐ and third‐trimester non‐nuchal lymphatic malformations (LM) and identify predictors of these complications.MethodsWe conducted a single‐centre retrospective review of fetuses diagnosed with LMs between January 2015 and January 2024. Lesion size was quantified using the lymphatic malformation volume ratio (LVR): LVR = 0.523 x width x length x height (cm3) / head circumference (cm). Outcomes and LVR growth trends were analyzed using Student's t‐test and a linear mixed‐effects model.ResultsEighteen cases were included (10 cervical, 8 axillary). Uncomplicated cervical LMs grew at 0.3 cm2/week (p < 0.001). Intralesional bleeding (n = 3) was associated with LVR > 11 cm2 and significantly higher growth rates (2.6 vs. 0.3 cm2/week; p = 0.005). All cases with LVR > 5 cm2 required subsequent tracheostomy after birth. Axillary LMs grew at 0.21 cm2/week. One case with bleeding had LVR 4.4 cm2. Median LVR was significantly lower in vaginal deliveries compared with complicated births (2.0 vs. 7.3 cm2; p = 0.03). All LVR > 4 cm2 led to delivery complications.ConclusionHigher LVR predicts haemorrhage and airway intervention in cervical LMs, and delivery complications in axillary LMs. These exploratory observations warrant confirmation in larger prospective cohorts.

Introduction

Lymphatic malformations (LM) are congenital, low‐flow vascular anomalies caused by abnormal lymphatic channel development, leading to lymphatic fluid accumulation and formation of cystic structures. These abnormalities can develop as early as 4–10 weeks of gestation and most commonly affect the neck (65%–75%), followed by the axillary, retroperitoneal, limb, and mediastinal regions [1, 2, 3]. With an incidence of approximately 1 in 4000 live births [4], lymphatic malformations present a wide range of symptoms depending on lesion size, location and infiltration, potentially causing disfigurement, functional impairment, infection, or intralesional hemorrhage [1, 5].

Non‐nuchal LM differs from nuchal LM or cystic hygromas, which are typically detected in the first trimester of pregnancy. Cystic hygromas are located at the back of the neck and are frequently associated with chromosomal abnormalities, cardiac defects, and high perinatal mortality [4, 6]. In contrast, non‐nuchal lymphatic malformations are usually identified in the second or third trimester, lack chromosomal associations, and commonly appear in submandibular, supraclavicular, anterior, or lateral neck regions rather than the posterior cervical area [4].

Recently, there has been a growing interest in fetal treatment of large macrocystic LM, with sirolimus [7, 8] and in utero sclerotherapy with OK‐432 [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. However, the indications and optimal timing for clinical application remain unclear. This study aims to document the natural history and associated complications of LM at various body locations diagnosed during the second and third trimesters of pregnancy as a first step toward developing an evidence‐based treatment path for LM.

Material and Methods

Study Setting

We conducted a retrospective cohort study that included all cases of prenatally diagnosed non‐nuchal LM identified through either ultrasound and/or MRI at the Ontario Fetal Center, a collaboration between the Maternal‐Fetal medicine unit at Mount Sinai Hospital and The Hospital for Sick Children, Toronto, Canada between January 2015 and Jan 2024. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee for clinical studies (REB# 23‐022‐C) at Mount Sinai Hospital.

Study Population

We identified all cases of non‐nuchal LM seen at our center between January 2015 and January 2024 from our maternal‐fetal medicine database (Astraia software GmbH, Nexus, Munich, Germany) at Mount Sinai Hospital. Sonographic and MR images of all fetuses were reviewed. Non‐nuchal LM were diagnosed when a multiseptated cystic mass was identified on the neck, axilla, chest wall, abdominal wall, and extremities. Cases of nuchal LM (cystic hygroma), where the lesion was limited to the posterior aspect of the neck and the upper shoulder region detected during the first trimester of pregnancy, were not included in this study. The diagnosis was confirmed through external examination or fetal autopsy in cases where termination of pregnancy (TOP) was chosen or based on postnatal examination when expectant management was selected. In the latter cases, tumor characteristics and growth were monitored from diagnosis to delivery. The natural history of the lesion was only described in cases managed expectantly with available postnatal outcomes.

The following maternal and fetal characteristics were collected: maternal age, parity, gestational age (GA) at the initial presentation and at birth, fetal sex, fetal biometry including head circumference (HC), and amniotic fluid volume (deep vertical pocket (DVP) or amniotic fluid index (AFI). We also documented any additional anomalies, genetic testing [non‐invasive prenatal testing (NIPT) and chromosomal microarray (CMA) where available] and the prenatal management strategy chosen by the patient (expectant management, fetal therapy or termination of pregnancy). Information on delivery details, including mode of delivery, need for ex‐utero intrapartum treatment (EXIT) procedure, and any delivery complications, was collected. Postnatal outcomes included requirement for surgical or pharmacological interventions, need for subsequent tracheostomy following intubation during the EXIT procedure for ongoing airway compromise, and survival at 28 days of life (neonatal survival).

Ultrasound Acquisition

All ultrasound assessments were performed and/or supervised by experienced fetal medicine specialists using Philips IU22 (Philips, Amsterdam, The Netherlands) or GE Voluson E10 (GE Healthcare, Zipf, Austria) ultrasound machines. All digitally stored images were reassessed by the first author (Y.K.). Polyhydramnios was defined as an amniotic fluid index (AFI) > 25 cm or a single deepest amniotic fluid pocket (DVP) > 8 cm by ultrasound. LM dimensions (width, length, and height) were measured in centimetres (cm). Tumor volume was determined using the formula: 4/3π x width/2 x length/2 x height/2 or 0.523 x width x length x height (cm3). Subsequently, LM volume ratio (LVR) was retrospectively computed with the following formula: LM volume (cm3)/head circumference (cm), similar to the CVR (CPAM volume ratio) for chest masses [15, 16]. LVR was used to monitor the growth of the lesion prenatally.

MRI Acquisition

Fetal MRI examinations were performed using a 1.5‐T imaging system (GE 1.5 T Twinspeed, Milwaukee, WI, USA) utilizing single‐shot fast spin‐echo T1‐and T2‐weighted sequences in three orthogonal planes without the administration of contrast agents. To detect intralesional bleeding, blood sensitive sequences such as echo‐planar imaging (EPI) and/or susceptibility‐weighted imaging (SWI) were obtained in addition to routine T1 weighted sequences. Section thickness was 3–4 mm. The US and MR studies were within a week from each other.

Intralesional Bleeding

Intralesional bleeding was suspected when the mass displayed cloudy or lace‐like reticular echogenicity on ultrasound (Figure 1A–E) and a hyperintense T1 signal appeared within the lesion on MRI. Confirmation of this diagnosis was achieved when there was an elevated peak systolic velocity (PSV) of the middle cerebral artery (MCA‐PSV > 1.5 MoM), or when there was progression in the lesion's echogenicity from a cloudy or lace‐like reticular pattern to fluid‐fluid levels or solid clot with absence of flow on US imaging (Figure 1.) or when a blooming artifact was observed on EPI or SWI protocol on MRI (Figure 2A–D).

Airway Assessment

For cervical lesions, the fetal airway was assessed on ultrasound and T2 weighted MR sequences and airway compression was defined as subjective obliteration or narrowing of the nasopharynx or trachea as well as airway deviation or proximal and/or distal airway dilatation. Additionally, tracheoesophageal displacement index (TEDI) was also quantified. TEDI was measured on axial fetal MRI and defined as the sum of the lateral and anterior displacement of the trachea from the ventral aspect of the cervical spine [17]. Esophageal compression was suspected from a small or absent stomach, often associated with polyhydramnios (Figure 3A–C).

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics including mean ± standard deviation (SD), median and interquartile range (IQR) were used for numerical variables and percentages were used for categorical variables. Student's t‐test and Mann Whitney U test were used to compare normally distributed and non‐normally distributed continuous data, respectively. Predictive strength of LVR on intralesional bleeding was determined by Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis (LVR measurement closest to the time of bleeding detection was used for the analysis) using GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA).

A linear mixed‐effects model of repeated measures was performed to assess LVR measurement at different timepoints. The agreement between LVR measurements obtained via US and MRI (within a 1‐week interval) was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) in the R Statistical Software (R studio V.4.4.2).

Results

Study Population

In total, 26 fetuses were identified with LM through ultrasound examination, with 15, 9 and 2 cases presenting with cervical, axillary, and lower extremity LM, respectively. Five cases were lost to follow‐up and therefore excluded from the study. Additionally, three cases in our cohort, two with extensive lower limb LM and one with cervical LM, chose to terminate the pregnancy between 21‐ and 23‐weeks’ gestation. For cervical LM cases, the gestational age at presentation was 22 weeks, whereas lower limb LM cases were diagnosed at 20 and 22 weeks' gestation. The limb LM extended from the gluteal region to the calf in both cases; one case involved the left side, while the other was located on the right side. Additionally, the mass extended into the retroperitoneal space, reaching subdiaphragmatic levels. Both pregnancies were terminated, and autopsy confirmed the diagnosis of LM (Figure 3.).

All remaining cases were confirmed to have LM either through autopsy or postnatal examination. The mean maternal age in this cohort was 31.9 ± 6.1 years. The mean GA at presentation was 26.3 ± 5.5 weeks. No additional anomalies were identified in these fetuses. CMA results were available for six cases, including four with cervical lesions and two with axillary lesions, all of which were normal. In addition, another two cases with cervical lesions had a normal NIPT.

Cervical Lymphatic Malformation

We included 10 cases of cervical lymphatic malformations in this analysis. The median gestational age at presentation was 26.1 weeks (IQR 21.6–28.8 weeks). Ultrasound images were available for all cases, while MRI scans were performed in eight cases. The lesions were located on the anterior neck in 80% (8/10) of cases and 20% (2/10) were on the lateral side of the neck. Forty percent (4/10) had the lesion on the left side of the neck. The median tumor volume and LVR at presentation were 43.2 cm3 (IQR 16.9–218.7 cm3) and 2.2 cm2 (IQR 0.7–17.3 cm2), respectively. Half of the cases showed mediastinal extension on MRI. Uncomplicated cervical LM (without intralesional bleeding) demonstrated an LVR growth rate of 0.3 cm2 per week. Two patients in our cohort were treated with sirolimus antenatally for a rapidly enlarging mass with MRI confirming airway obstruction. The treatment was at the discretion of the primary physician overseeing the case given no accepted criteria currently exist to guide prenatal treatment [7]. Prenatal treatment did not significantly impact lesion growth compared with non‐treated fetuses (p = 0.35).

There were five cases of suspected intralesional bleeding and three of five cases with confirmed intralesional bleeding. Among those with confirmed bleeding, the bleeding was diagnosed at 28.7, 34.1, and 36.3 weeks, respectively. One case resulted in intrauterine fetal death (IUFD) at 37.3 weeks. Tumor volume and the LVR at the time of intralesional bleeding diagnosis were 415.0 cm3 (IQR 362.6–541.4 cm3) and 15.0 cm2 (IQR 11.2–17.3 cm2), respectively. For cases with confirmed intralesional bleeding, LVR at bleeding diagnosis was significantly greater than the LVR of cases without haemorrhage (LVR: 14.5 ± 3.1 cm2 vs. 5.2 ± 4.5 cm2, p = 0.01). Due to the small number of cases, ROC analysis was not performed. However, a comparison of LVR between cases with confirmed intralesional bleeding and those without revealed that LVR exceeded 11 cm2 in all cases with intralesional bleeding. When compared to uncomplicated cases, the growth rate of LVR was significantly higher (2.6 cm2 per week (IQR 1.2–8.5) versus 0.3 cm2 per week (IQR −0.1–0.7), p = 0.005). Polyhydramnios was identified in all cases prior to the diagnosis of haemorrhage.

The mean gestational age at the time of MRI examination was 34.1 ± 3.2 weeks. Among the cases that underwent MRI (n = 8), 37.5% (3/8) demonstrated mild airway compression (TEDI 5–10.2 mm), while 25% (2/8) exhibited significant airway compression (TEDI 10.8–12.6 mm). Mode of delivery data were available for all cases. Six cases (66.7%) underwent an EXIT procedure, two cases (22.2%) resulted in vaginal delivery, and one case (11.1%) underwent uncomplicated caesarean delivery. The median LVR at the last prenatal visit (median 37.3 weeks, IQR 35.9–37.9 weeks) was significantly higher in those requiring tracheostomy after birth versus those who did not (9.2 cm2, IQR 6.7–15.0 vs. 1.0 cm2, IQR 0.2–3.4; p = 0.02). All cases with LVR > 5 had tracheostomy performed subsequently after birth. Postnatally, 77.8% (7/9) of cases were treated with sirolimus, and 88.9% (8/9) underwent sclerotherapy. All neonates survived.

Ultrasound images were available for all cases, while MRI scans were performed in three. Half of the lesions (4/8) were located on the left side of the thorax. Tumor volume and LVR at presentation were 96.6 cm3 (IQR 23.6–32.3) and 4.2 cm2 (IQR 1.3–7.0), respectively. Growth of individual cases is outlined in Table 2 and Figure 4B.

Similar to cervical lymphatic malformations, uncomplicated axillary lymphatic malformations exhibited an LVR growth rate of 0.2 cm2 per week of gestation. None of the axillary cases received prenatal sirolimus. There were three suspected cases with intralesional bleeding, of which one was confirmed. Gestational age at the time of bleeding was 28.7 weeks. In this case, the fetus required three intrauterine blood transfusions (IUT) and survived. The tumor volume and LVR at the time of bleeding were 118.6 cm3 and 4.4 cm2, respectively. Additionally, one suspected case of intralesional bleeding resulted in a fetal death at 22.4 weeks. However, a full autopsy report was not available for this patient.

Mode of delivery was available for six of seven liveborn fetuses. Four cases (66.7%) resulted in vaginal delivery, one of which was complicated by shoulder dystocia (birthweight 3480 g; no gestational diabetes). The remaining two cases (33.3%) were delivered via caesarean section; one due to failure to descend and the other due to a significant size of the LM. The median LVR at the last prenatal visit (median 37.9 weeks, IQR 35.4–39.1 week) was significantly lower in cases with successful vaginal delivery compared with those with shoulder dystocia or requiring caesarean section (n = 3) for obstructed labor (2.0 ± 1.0 cm2 vs. 7.3 ± 2.5 cm2; p = 0.03). Due to the small number of cases, ROC analysis was not performed. However, all cases with LVR > 4 cm2 had a complicated vaginal delivery or delivered via caesarean section.

Out of seven livebirths, postnatal follow‐up data were available for five cases, as two cases were lost to follow‐up after birth; 40.0% (2/5) of cases were treated with sirolimus, and 80.0% (4/5) underwent sclerotherapy while another case did not receive any treatment. All neonates survived after the treatment.

Correlation US and MRI

In eight cases, across 10 measurements, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) between LVR measured by US and MRI for cervical and axillary lymphatic malformations was 0.88 (95% CI: 0.65–0.96, p < 0.0001).

Discussion

Principal Findings

This study evaluated 18 fetuses diagnosed with non‐nuchal LM in the second half of pregnancy, comprising 10 cervical and 8 axillary lesions. Lesions exhibited consistent growth with gestational age (0.2–0.3 cm2 increase in LVR per week). Intralesional bleeding emerged as a rare but significant complication in non‐nuchal LM, with larger tumor volumes (cervical LVR > 11 cm2) and rapid growth rates strongly associated with bleeding. Polyhydramnios frequently preceded the haemorrhage. In cervical malformations, an LVR > 5 cm2 was associated with the need for tracheostomy after birth, whereas in axillary cases, an LVR > 4 cm2 was associated with failure to progress and shoulder dystocia. Postnatally, most neonates underwent sclerotherapy and/or sirolimus therapy.

Prior Similar Studies

Similar to previous publications, non‐nuchal macrocystic LM typically exhibits progressive growth with advancing gestational age and may continue to enlarge during the postnatal period [3, 4, 18]. None of the previous studies reported LM growth adjusted for fetal size or delivery complications. A few publications have documented prenatal intralesional haemorrhage in non‐nuchal lymphatic malformations, including three cases with axillary LM [18, 19, 20] and one case with a mediastinal lesion [21]. Details of these cases are summarized in Table 3. Similar to our cohort, fetal intralesional bleeding occurred during the third trimester, with significant enlargement of the LM when haemorrhage was diagnosed [19, 20, 21]. This may be due to larger masses having a tendency to bleed, or the bleeding itself causing a substantial increase in the size of the mass. We also observed that polyhydramnios frequently preceded the bleeding. This is likely due to lesion enlargement causing esophageal compression or possible transudation of fluid from the lesion into the amniotic cavity. Among the published cases, three neonates survived, while one succumbed to significant blood loss. When bleeding occurs, this can cause fetal anemia (elevated MCA‐PSV or low hemoglobin levels on blood sampling) and may progress to hydrops fetalis. Close monitoring of the fetus for these signs is crucial, and intra‐uterine transfusion should be considered when indicated. In our cohort, fetal transfusions were performed in two cases, thereby successfully prolonging the pregnancy and resulting in live births. Of note, in utero treatment with sclerotherapy is not recommended in cases of intralesional haemorrhage as it could exacerbate the bleeding [20].

Evidence on prenatal LM of the lower extremities remains limited [22, 23, 24, 25]. Similar to our cases, previously reported lesions involved the entire lower limb, extending into the pelvic and retroperitoneal regions, with all cases detected after mid‐gestation. Genetic testing in the form of karyotype was normal in all cases (results for Klippel‐Trenaunay‐Weber syndrome were not available). Due to the extensive involvement of the lesions, all cases opted for TOP. Interestingly, a recent study documented rapid growth of this type of the lesion, progressing from near non‐existence to an extensive lesion within 4 weeks [25]. There is also some evidence suggesting that these extremity lesions may originate in the retroperitoneal abdomino‐pelvic region before extending to the lower limb [26, 27].

Clinical Implications

Intralesional bleeding is a significant complication of cervical LM, which usually occurs in the third trimester. For small lesions (LVR < 11 cm2), periodic ultrasound monitoring is recommended. Conversely, for large lesions (LVR > 11 cm2), rapid growth, or cases associated with polyhydramnios, close surveillance and referral to a fetal tertiary care center is recommended due to the heightened risk of intralesional bleeding. If bleeding occurs, this can lead to fetal anemia, potentially requiring fetal transfusion.

Large cervical LM with an LVR > 5 cm2 is associated with the need for tracheostomy after birth. Similarly, axillary lesions with an LVR > 4 cm2 may be associated with obstructed labor or shoulder dystocia. These findings are critical in guiding parental counseling and multidisciplinary team preparation to optimize perinatal management and neonatal outcomes.

Strength and Limitations

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of LM in various locations: cervical, axillary, and extremity. To date, it represents one of the largest cohorts reporting LM intralesional bleeding, offering valuable insights into predictors such as LVR and their clinical implications. However, the sample size remains too small to perform complex statistical analyses. Another limitation of this study is that some fetuses received sirolimus therapy, which may potentially alter the natural history and growth trajectory of LM. However, exploratory analysis within our cohort did not demonstrate a significant difference in lesion growth between cases exposed and unexposed to sirolimus. An additional limitation is that the calculation of the LVR assumed an ellipsoid lesion configuration. However, LM lesions are often irregular, and asymmetrical in shape, which may limit the accuracy of the estimation using the ellipsoid assumption. Therefore, LVR measurements should be interpreted as approximations rather than precise volumetric assessments. Another key limitation is the lack of long‐term follow‐up, and parental decline of full autopsy, both of which may introduce bias and reduce the robustness of the conclusions.

Research Implications

Further studies with larger sample sizes and longitudinal follow‐up data on LVR are essential to validate these findings and enhance their generalisability. Future research could explore the efficacy of fetal therapies, such as sclerotherapy or sirolimus, for cervical LM with LVR > 5 cm2 to potentially reduce the need for tracheostomy after birth and for axillary LM with LVR > 4 cm2 to prevent complications during vaginal delivery. Additionally, in cases with LVR > 11 cm2, fetal interventions may be investigated as a means to mitigate the risk of intralesional bleeding and its associated complications.

Conclusions

Non‐nuchal LM tends to grow with gestation. LVR may be a valuable tool for tracking lesion growth and predicting perinatal outcomes including intralesional bleeding, need for tracheostomy after birth in cervical lesions and complicated vaginal deliveries in axillary lesions. However, these observations should be considered exploratory given limited sample sizes and require confirmation in larger prospective cohorts.
