Multivariate assessment of morphological diversity in Moringa oleifera Lam. across agro-climatic zones of Rajasthan
Authors: Rajpal Choudhary, Vikram Jeet Singh Tanwar, Rohit Jain, Sumita Kachhwaha, Shanker Lal Kothari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding the agro-climatic structure of morphological diversity is essential for the effective utilization and improvement of drumstick (Moringa oleifera Lam.), a multipurpose tree species of high nutritional and economic value. The present study aimed to evaluate morphological diversity and phenotypic variation of M. oleifera across ten agro-climatic zones of Rajasthan, India, and to identify key traits and morphotypes relevant for breeding, conservation, and climate-resilient cultivation. A total of 367 accessions were characterized using 17 morphological traits, including eight quantitative and nine qualitative descriptors. Quantitative traits were analyzed using non-parametric statistics and Principal Component Analysis (PCA), while qualitative traits were evaluated through Multiple Correspondence Analysis (MCA). An integrated approach using Factor Analysis of Mixed Data (FAMD) was employed to assess overall variation, followed by hierarchical and FAMD-based clustering. Correlation analysis was conducted to identify relationships among traits. The results revealed significant variations among agro-climatic zones for all quantitative traits (Kruskal–Wallis, p < 0.01), with moderate to high effect sizes (ε² = 0.07–0.26). PCA indicated that the first two components explained 56.9% of total variance, primarily driven by stem girth and leaf-related traits. MCA showed that qualitative variation was largely influenced by stem morphology, canopy size, and branching density. FAMD further confirmed the integration of qualitative and quantitative traits, with the first two dimensions explaining 56.4% of total variability. Correlation analysis demonstrated strong positive associations among leaf traits, indicating a coordinated leaf development system. Clustering analysis grouped the accessions into distinct morphotypes, with substantial overlap across agro-climatic zones, suggesting that morphological variation is governed by both environmental factors and inherent genetic diversity. Two major groups identified through FAMD represented broadly adapted and high growth morphotypes. From an applied perspective, accessions with larger canopy and higher leaf area are recommended for biomass-oriented breeding and cultivation in favourable environments, while stable, moderate-growth accessions are suitable for arid and semi-arid regions. The study also highlights leaf traits as reliable selection indices and emphasizes the need for stratified germplasm conservation across agro-climatic zones. This study provides a robust framework for understanding morphological diversity in M. oleifera and offers practical insights for its genetic improvement, conservation and climate-resilient utilization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12862-026-02544-6.

Introduction

Medicinal plants are vital for traditional and modern healthcare systems, serving as primary sources of bioactive compounds which are important for the prevention and treatment of a wide range of diseases [1]. M. oleifera Lam. (drumstick tree), an economically significant member of the family Moringaceae, is an ancient perennial species widely recognized for its adaptability and multifunctional value [2]. Native to the Indian subcontinent, the species exhibits remarkable resilience across ecological gradients ranging from humid tropics to arid deserts, reflecting its pronounced phenotypic plasticity and genetic heterogeneity [3–5]. The plant’s exceptional nutritive and medicinal properties, coupled with its agronomic versatility, have elevated its importance as a sustainable crop in both subsistence and commercial agroecosystems [6–9]. Its leaves, pods, and seeds are rich in proteins, vitamins, essential amino acids, and bioactive phytochemicals such as phenolics and flavonoids, while bark, roots, and flowers serve as traditional medicinal ingredients with antimicrobial, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory properties, contributing to its extensive use in food, pharmaceutical and nutraceutical industries [10–13].

In India, M. oleifera naturally grows across a wide range of agro-climatic zones, making it an ideal species for investigating morphological variation under contrasting environmental conditions [14]. Rajasthan, located in the northwestern part of the country, provides a unique ecological gradient comprising ten well-defined agro-climatic zones, ranging from hyper-arid western plains to sub-humid and humid southern regions [15]. These zones exhibit distinct gradients in rainfall (100–1100 mm), temperature (3–48 °C), and soil pH (6.0–9.2) creating distinct environmental conditions that influence plant growth and development [16–18]. Such heterogeneity provides an excellent natural framework for evaluating morphological traits structuring across environmental gradients within M. oleifera populations [19].

Previous studies have demonstrated that environmental conditions significantly influence morphological and biochemical traits in M. oleifera. Eka, Ahyar [20] established that leaf dimensions and antioxidant capacity varied significantly among accessions grown under distinct agroclimatic conditions, revealing environment-driven modulation of both morphology and metabolite content. Iqbal and Bhanger [21] earlier found that season and growth location markedly affected leaf antioxidant activity, reinforcing the influence of climate on plant biochemistry and form. Similar trends are echoed in studies linking phenotypic expression to edaphic factors; Sharma, Kachhwaha [22] correlated morphological variability in Rajasthan populations with variations in soil iron content, suggesting adaptive adjustments in leaf morphology and branching architecture to mineral availability. These investigations collectively highlight that M. oleifera exhibits ecophysiological flexibility that enables its persistence across extreme environments while maintaining high intra-population variability.

Morphological diversity in M. oleifera is not only of ecological interest but also has significant agronomic and economic implications. Several studies have established that accessions exhibiting greater leaf biomass and canopy density produce superior yields of protein and polyphenols, contributing to enhanced nutritional and commercial value [8, 23]. Kibasa, Vicent [23] reported that Moringa-fortified sunflower oil exhibited improved oxidative stability, while Shivanna, N [24] demonstrated nutritional enhancement of yoghurt supplemented with Moringa leaf powder. These applications link morphological performance—particularly leaf area index and canopy productivity—to industrial quality attributes. Similarly, Moringa biomass has been successfully explored for biofuel and biochar production [25] and for synthesis of eco-friendly nanomaterials [2, 26]. Therefore, systematic characterization of morphological diversity is essential for identifying superior genotypes and ensuring consistent raw material quality.

Despite increasing interest in the biochemical and molecular diversity of M. oleifera, comprehensive studies integrating morphological variation with agro-climatic gradients remain limited, particularly at a regional scale in India [11, 27–32]. Most of the existing studies have focused on a limited number of accessions or restricted geographic areas and limited morphological descriptors, thereby underrepresenting the full extent of environmental variability [3, 4, 19, 22, 33]. Moreover, integrative approaches combining qualitative and quantitative traits using robust multivariate analyses are still relatively scarce.

The present study addresses these gaps by evaluating morphological diversity in M. oleifera across all ten agro-climatic zones of Rajasthan using a large dataset of 367 accessions. A total of 17 morphological descriptors, including both qualitative and quantitative traits related to plant architecture and foliar characteristics, were assessed. Statistical analyses, including non-parametric testing, PCA, MCA, FAMD, and clustering, were employed to identify patterns of variation, key discriminant traits and morphotype groupings. By integrating morphometric data with agroclimatic zoning, the study aims to elucidate spatial trends in morphological diversity, identify key discriminant traits and highlight accessions exhibiting superior adaptive potential. The novelty of this investigation lies in its comprehensive coverage of all major agroclimatic zones within a single state, enabling comparative analysis across contrasting environmental regimes from arid deserts to sub-humid southern plains. It represents the first systematic attempt to combine multivariate morphological analysis with geoclimatic parameters for M. oleifera in Rajasthan. The findings provide a comprehensive phenotypic baseline for M. oleifera and contribute to the identification of region-specific elite genotypes. This work further supports future breeding, conservation, and climate-resilient cultivation strategies for this important multipurpose species.

Methodology

Study area and agro-climatic zonation

The present study was conducted across ten agro-climatic zones of Rajasthan, India, which collectively represent one of the most diverse ecological gradients in the country (Table 1). These zones span a wide environmental continuum, ranging from hyper-arid and arid regions in the western part of the state to semi-arid, sub-humid, and humid regions in the eastern and southern parts. Such variation provides an ideal natural framework for investigating environment-associated morphological differentiation in M. oleifera. The agro-climatic classification adopted in this study follows the framework defined by the Directorate of Economics and Statistics, Government of Rajasthan [18], which integrates key environmental parameters including annual rainfall, temperature extremes, soil characteristics, and land-use patterns [15, 17].

The zones differ markedly in climatic and edaphic conditions, with rainfall ranging from as low as 100 mm in hyper-arid zones to over 1000 mm in humid regions, and temperature extremes varying from approximately 3 °C in winter to above 48 °C in summer. Similarly, soil types vary from coarse-textured desert soils and aeolian sands in western zones to alluvial, black, and lithosol soils in eastern and southern regions. These gradients are known to exert strong influences on plant growth, architecture, and adaptive traits.

To ensure comprehensive ecological representation, sampling locations were systematically distributed across all ten agro-climatic zones, covering both west–east and north–south gradients of the state. Each zone was treated as an independent ecological unit for comparative analysis, allowing robust evaluation of agro-climatic effects on morphological variation. The sampling design was structured to capture intra-zone heterogeneity by including multiple locations within each zone, while maintaining consistency with the established zonal classification. This zonation framework provided a scientifically robust basis for assessing morphological diversity and enabled meaningful interpretation of trait variation in relation to environmental gradients. By integrating broad-scale agro-climatic variability with systematic sampling, the study ensures reliable characterization of environment-associated morphological patterns in M. oleifera across Rajasthan.

Plant material and sampling design

M. oleifera Lam. is abundantly distributed across the study area and is not classified as endangered, threatened or protected under national or state regulations. Plant material used in this study was collected from both public (roadside and common land) and private locations across different agro-climatic zones. Sampling from private lands was carried out with prior permission from landowners, ensuring compliance with local administrative norms. No sampling was conducted in protected, restricted, or conservation-designated areas. As the study involved non-commercial academic research on a non-protected plant species, no specific ethical approval or collection permit was required under the applicable regulations.

A total of 367 accessions were sampled, with zone-wise representation as follows: IA (33), IB (32), IC (30), IIA (45), IIB (57), IIIA (28), IIIB (20), IVA (39), IVB (38), and V (45). The variation in the number of accessions among zones reflects differences in field accessibility and plant availability rather than actual population density, ensuring broad ecological representation across the state. Sampling was carried out during March 2026, which corresponds to a transitional phenological phase in M. oleifera, where plants may exhibit heterogeneous reproductive states. During this period, some individuals bore flowers, others had developing pods, while many remained in a purely vegetative state. Due to this asynchronous and inconsistent expression of reproductive traits across accessions, characters related to flowers, pods, and seeds were excluded from the study to maintain uniformity and comparability of morphological data. The study therefore focused exclusively on vegetative traits, which are more stable, consistently expressed, and less influenced by short-term environmental or seasonal variation. Plants were randomly selected from both natural and cultivated populations. To avoid over-representation of closely related individuals and to ensure sampling independence, a maximum of three plants were collected from a single cluster, with adequate spatial separation maintained between clusters. Only healthy, disease-free and morphologically normal plants were included to ensure unbiased trait evaluation. Each sampled plant was treated as an independent biological replicate. Geographic coordinates (latitude and longitude) were recorded for all sampling sites to confirm agro-climatic classification and ensure accurate spatial representation.

The plant material collected and used in the present investigation was taxonomically identified by Dr. Pravin Mohil, Assistant Professor and Taxonomist, Department of Botany, University of Rajasthan, Jaipur, India. A voucher specimen of the identified plant material has also been deposited in the Rajasthan University Botanical Library (RUBL), University of Rajasthan, Jaipur, India. The voucher specimen deposition number is RUBL21778, and it is publicly accessible for future reference and verification.

Qualitative morphological characterization

Qualitative morphological characterization was conducted to assess variation in plant architecture, canopy organization, bark characteristics and overall structural features of M. oleifera across agro-climatic zones. A total of nine qualitative traits were evaluated using predefined categorical descriptors. The classification of traits and their respective categories was adopted from previously published studies on M. oleifera by Gandji, Tovissodé [5], Popoola, Bello [34], Palada, Patricio [35] Santhoshkumar, Roy Choudhury [36] and Sharma, Kachhwaha [3] and related tree species, with necessary modifications introduced to better capture the observed patterns of variation and diversity under Rajasthan’s agro-climatic conditions. These modifications ensured that the descriptors were both ecologically relevant and sufficiently sensitive to distinguish among accessions.

Branching habit was categorized into basal, medial and terminal, based on the predominant position of lateral branches along the main stem. Basal branching refers to branches emerging from the lower portion of the stem, medial branching from the middle region, and terminal branching from the upper portion. Branching density was classified as sparse, intermediate or dense, reflecting the overall number and compactness of branches within the canopy. Branching pattern described the orientation of branches and was categorized as erect, drooping, or inclined, indicating vertical, downward, or angular growth directions, respectively. Canopy-related traits were evaluated to capture crown architecture. Canopy size was categorized as narrow, medium or wide, based on the lateral spread of the canopy. Canopy density was classified as sparse or dense, indicating the degree of foliage compactness and light penetration within the canopy. Canopy shape was categorized as flattened or irregular, representing either a relatively uniform, horizontally expanded crown or a non-uniform, asymmetrical structure. Bark characteristics were included as stable structural traits. Bark texture was classified as smooth or scaly, based on surface characteristics observed visually. Bark colour was recorded across multiple categories, including creamish, creamish grey, grey, dark grey, blackish grey and whitish grey, reflecting the wide variation observed among accessions and providing additional discriminatory resolution. Stem morphology was categorized into small, medium, and large, based on overall stem thickness and structural robustness, serving as an indicator of growth vigor and biomass accumulation.

Quantitative morphological measurements

Quantitative morphological traits reflecting vegetative growth, structural vigor and foliar development were measured using standardized and reproducible procedures. A total of eight quantitative traits were evaluated, including stem girth, number of primary branches, number of secondary branches, twig length, number of leaves per twig, leaf length, leaf width and leaf area. These traits were selected to represent key components of plant architecture, biomass potential and photosynthetic capacity.

Stem girth was measured at a fixed height of 1 foot above ground level using a measuring tape to ensure consistency across all accessions. The number of primary and secondary branches was recorded through direct counting, providing an estimate of branching intensity and canopy complexity. Twig length was measured using a flexible measuring scale, and the number of leaves per twig was counted manually. Leaf morphometric traits were recorded from fully expanded leaves located at the basal portion of mature twigs to maintain uniformity in sampling position. Leaf length and leaf width were measured using a measuring scale, and leaf area was estimated using the formula: leaf area = leaf length × leaf width × correction factor. The correction factor was applied based on the leaflet length-to-width (L/W) ratio to account for variation in leaflet shape, where leaflets with L/W > 2.25 were assigned a factor of 0.72, those with L/W between 1.80 and 2.25 a factor of 0.75, and those with L/W < 1.80 a factor of 0.78. This adjustment improved the accuracy of leaf area estimation across different leaflet morphologies.

To ensure measurement reliability and account for within-plant variability, triplicate observations were recorded for twig length, number of leaves per twig and leaf dimensions (length and width of the same leaf) from representative mature twigs. Mean values of these replicated measurements were used for subsequent statistical analyses.

Data processing and statistical analysis

All the morphological data were compiled, curated, and checked for consistency prior to analysis. The dataset was analysed at two levels: individual accession level (n = 367) and agro-climatic zone level (n = 10), depending on the objective of the analysis. This dual-level approach enabled assessment of both within-population variability and broader zone-wise patterns.

The non-standardized raw dataset (n = 367 accessions) was used for descriptive and categorical analyses to preserve the original variability and distribution of traits. Quantitative traits were summarized using median, interquartile range (IQR), minimum and maximum values, while qualitative traits were expressed as frequency distributions. Differences among agro-climatic zones for quantitative traits were evaluated using the Kruskal–Walli’s test, and the magnitude of zonal effects was assessed using effect size (ε²). Associations between qualitative traits and agro-climatic zones were examined using the chi-square (χ²) test of independence. Multivariate analyses were performed on the raw dataset to explore patterns of morphological variation. PCA was applied to quantitative traits to identify major axes of variation and determine trait contributions. MCA was conducted on qualitative traits to examine associations among categorical variables. To integrate qualitative and quantitative traits, FAMD was employed, providing a unified ordination framework and enabling comprehensive interpretation of morphological diversity. Hierarchical clustering was performed using Euclidean distance and Ward’s D2 linkage method to group accessions based on morphological similarity, and clustering patterns were interpreted in conjunction with 5 initial FAMD dimensions.

For zone-level analyses, the dataset was aggregated into ten agro-climatic zones (n = 10), and appropriate transformations were applied to ensure comparability among variables. Quantitative traits were standardized using Z-score transformation, while qualitative traits were converted into proportional data and transformed using the centered log-ratio (CLR) transformation. The transformed dataset was used for Pearson correlation analysis to evaluate relationships among traits, and for PCA-based clustering of agro-climatic zones. Additionally, heatmaps were generated to visualize patterns of trait variation and similarity among zones and variables.

All statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi 2.7.12 (The Jamovi project, Australia). Multivariate analyses were conducted using the “Factor” and “snowCluster” modules for PCA and hierarchical clustering, while MCA and FAMD were performed using “FactoMineR” module. Non-parametric tests and descriptive statistics were carried out using the base statistical functions within Jamovi. Graphical representations, including bar graphs and scatter plots, were prepared using OriginPro 8.5 SR1 (OriginLab Corporation, USA).

Results

The geographical distribution of the 367 M. oleifera accessions sampled across Rajasthan demonstrated comprehensive representation of all ten agro-climatic zones, with the number of accessions per zone as follows: IA (33), IB (32), IC (30), IIA (45), IIB (57), IIIA (28), IIIB (20), IVA (39), IVB (38), and V (45) (Fig. 1). The sampling sites were spatially distributed along both west–east and north–south gradients, encompassing arid, semi-arid, sub-humid and humid regions (Figure S1 and S2). This ensured robust representation of intra-zone variability while minimizing sampling bias associated with localized environmental conditions. The large sample size and unequal yet representative zonal distribution provided a strong statistical foundation for subsequent qualitative, quantitative and multivariate analyses aimed at elucidating agro-climatic structuring and environment-associated phenotypic variation in M. oleifera.

Qualitative morphological variation

The distribution of qualitative morphological traits across M. oleifera accessions revealed substantial variation in plant architecture, canopy structure, bark characteristics and stem morphology (Figure S3). Among branching habits, medial branching was the most frequent (41.1%), followed by basal (29.7%) and terminal branching (29.2%). Branching density was largely skewed toward sparse types, which accounted for 50.4% of accessions, while intermediate (25.9%) and dense branching (23.7%) were less common. Similarly, branching pattern showed dominance of drooping architecture (48.8%), followed by erect (41.1%) and inclined forms (10.1%). Canopy-related traits exhibited marked heterogeneity. Narrow canopy size was predominant (54.8%), followed by medial (39.2%) and wide canopy types (6.0%). Canopy density was largely sparse (68.7%), with dense canopy observed in 31.3% of accessions. Canopy shape showed a higher frequency of flattened forms (59.9%) compared to irregular types (40.1%).

Bark characteristics further contributed to qualitative diversity. Scaly bark texture was dominant (64.9%), while smooth bark was observed in 35.1% of accessions. Bark colour exhibited high categorical variability, with creamish black (33.0%) and creamish grey (26.7%) being the most prevalent categories, followed by creamish (12.5%), grey (10.1%), and blackish grey (9.0%), while other colour classes occurred at low frequencies (< 5%). Stem morphology was relatively evenly distributed between large (43.3%) and medium (41.4%) categories, whereas small stem size was less frequent (15.3%).

The frequency distribution patterns indicate considerable morphological heterogeneity across accessions, with dominance of intermediate architectural traits and a tendency toward compact canopy structures. These patterns highlight the presence of broad phenotypic variation likely shaped by combined genetic and environmental influences across agro-climatic zones.

Comparison of agro-climatic zones based on qualitative morphological traits

A comparative assessment of agro-climatic zones revealed distinct patterns in the distribution of qualitative morphological traits, indicating clear environment-associated variation in plant architecture across Rajasthan (Fig. 2).

Arid and hyper-arid zones (IA, IB, IC) were characterized by a higher proportion of medial branching (43.3–57.6%) along with substantial representation of basal branching. These zones also exhibited a predominance of sparse to intermediate branching density (33.3–53.1%) and relatively higher frequencies of drooping and erect branching patterns. Transitional and dry zones (IIA and IIB) showed increased heterogeneity in trait expression, with medial branching remaining dominant (31.6–53.3%) but accompanied by higher variability in branching density and canopy traits. Sparse canopy density was particularly prominent (73.7–77.8%), while canopy shape showed a mixed distribution of flattened and irregular forms. Semi-arid eastern zones (IIIA and IIIB) exhibited distinct structural characteristics, with relatively higher frequencies of terminal branching (39.3–40%) and increased occurrence of inclined branching patterns (up to 21.4%). These zones also showed moderate to high canopy sparsity (50–57.1%) and strong dominance of scaly bark texture (70–85.7%).

In contrast, sub-humid and humid zones (IVA, IVB, V) displayed more expansive growth forms. Basal and medial branching were prominent (26.3–38.5%), while sparse branching density dominated strongly, particularly in IVB (71.1%). Canopy traits in these zones showed increased frequency of narrow to medial canopy sizes (44.4–71.1%) combined with high canopy sparsity (66.7–81.6%). Bark texture remained predominantly scaly (60.5–77.8%), while stem morphology showed a higher proportion of large and medium categories, particularly in Zones V and IVA. Across all zones, bark colour exhibited high variability without a clear zonal pattern, suggesting that this trait may be less strongly influenced by agro-climatic gradients compared to architectural traits.

Statistical significance of qualitative traits across agro-climatic zones

Chi-square analysis revealed that several qualitative morphological traits exhibited significant variation across agro-climatic zones, indicating non-random distribution patterns and strong environment-associated structuring. Among branching traits, branching density showed a statistically significant association with zones (χ² = 31.7, df = 18, p = 0.024), while branching pattern was also significantly structured (χ² = 32.9, df = 18, p = 0.017). In contrast, branching habit did not show significant variation across zones (χ² = 23.2, df = 18, p = 0.181), suggesting relatively uniform expression of this trait irrespective of agro-climatic conditions. Among canopy traits, canopy density exhibited significant zonal variation (χ² = 21.4, df = 9, p = 0.011), whereas canopy size (χ² = 26.3, df = 18, p = 0.094) and canopy shape (χ² = 12.9, df = 9, p = 0.169) were not significantly different across zones. Bark texture showed a strong and significant association with agro-climatic zones (χ² = 23.4, df = 9, p = 0.005), indicating its sensitivity to environmental variation. Similarly, bark colour displayed significant variability (χ² = 104, df = 81, p = 0.043), although with a large number of categories contributing to this variation. Stem morphology also differed significantly among zones (χ² = 29.2, df = 18, p = 0.046), highlighting its importance as a key structural trait influenced by environmental conditions.

Effect size estimates based on Cramér’s V indicated that the strength of association between qualitative traits and agro-climatic zones was generally in the low to moderate range, with values varying from 0.178 to 0.253 across traits. The highest effect size was observed for bark texture (Cramér’s V = 0.253), followed by canopy density (0.241) and branching pattern (0.212), suggesting that these traits are relatively more responsive to environmental gradients and contribute more strongly to agro-climatic differentiation. Branching density (0.208) and stem morphology (0.199) also showed moderate associations, indicating that structural and architectural traits are influenced by zone-specific conditions but are not exclusively determined by them. In contrast, lower effect sizes were observed for branching habit (0.178) and canopy shape (0.187), reflecting relatively weak associations and suggesting that these traits are more stable across environments and potentially under stronger genetic control. The moderate magnitude of effect sizes indicates that while agro-climatic factors significantly influence qualitative trait expression, a considerable proportion of variation remains attributable to intra-zone heterogeneity and underlying genetic diversity.

The results demonstrate that branching density, branching pattern, canopy density, bark texture, bark colour and stem morphology are key traits contributing to agro-climatic differentiation, whereas branching habit and canopy shape exhibit relatively stable expression across zones. These findings highlight the role of both environmental filtering and inherent variability in shaping morphological diversity in M. oleifera.

MCA of qualitative traits

MCA was performed to explore the structure of qualitative morphological variation among the 367 M. oleifera accessions. The analysis generated 18 dimensions, collectively explaining 100% of the total inertia. The first dimension (Dim 1) accounted for 14.13% of the total variance, followed by Dim 2 explaining 8.48%. Together, the first two dimensions explained 22.6% of the total variation, indicating moderate dispersion of qualitative traits across multiple axes rather than strong concentration along a single dominant gradient. Subsequent dimensions contributed progressively smaller proportions of variance, with Dim 3 (7.0%) and Dim 4 (6.75%) cumulatively increasing the explained variance to 36.4%. The first eight dimensions together accounted for 59.9% of total variability, while the first twelve dimensions explained 80.3%. The relatively lower variance explained by the first two dimensions indicates that qualitative traits exhibit higher categorical dispersion and reduced redundancy. The interpretation of MCA results was based primarily on the first two dimensions for visualization, while acknowledging the contribution of higher dimensions to overall variability. These findings highlight that qualitative morphological variation in M. oleifera is structured along multiple independent axes, reflecting combined influences of genetic diversity and environmental heterogeneity across agro-climatic zones.

The distribution of accessions across clusters revealed distinct but overlapping patterns among agro-climatic zones. Cluster I comprised a substantial proportion of accessions across all zones, with the highest representation observed in Zones V (20 accessions), IIB (23), and IIA (17). Interpretation of MCA dimensions based on trait contributions

The contribution of qualitative traits to the principal MCA dimensions was evaluated using squared cosine values (R²), revealing clear patterns of trait structuring across axes (Fig. 3c). These traits collectively represent overall plant robustness, canopy expansion and structural development, indicating that Dim 1 captures a gradient of architectural complexity ranging from compact to more developed growth forms. In contrast, branching habit (R² = 0.1018) and canopy density (R² = 0.0730) contributed weakly to this dimension, suggesting limited influence on the primary axis of variation.

Dim 2 was structured mainly by canopy-related and distributional traits, with the strongest contributions from canopy density (R² = 0.3972) and canopy shape (R² = 0.2985), followed by branching density (R² = 0.2335) and bark colour (R² = 0.2183). Canopy size (R² = 0.1964) also contributed moderately, indicating that Dim 2 represents variation in canopy organization and spatial distribution rather than overall size or structural robustness. Traits such as stem morphology (R² = 0.0344) and bark texture (R² = 0.0298) showed minimal contribution to Dim 2, confirming that this axis is largely independent of structural growth characteristics. Branching pattern (R² = 0.1735 for Dim 1; 0.0906 for Dim 2) and branching habit (R² = 0.1018 for Dim 1; 0.0281 for Dim 2) exhibited relatively low contributions to both dimensions, indicating that these traits have limited discriminatory power in multivariate space.

Correlation analysis of morphological traits

To ensure biologically meaningful interpretation, correlations arising from compositional or mutually exclusive categorical variables (e.g., dense vs. sparse canopy, scaly vs. smooth bark) were excluded from analysis. The refined correlation assessment therefore focuses on genuine associations among morphological traits and their functional relationships (Figure S8).

Among quantitative traits, a strong and highly significant positive correlation network was observed within leaf-related parameters. Leaf width exhibited a very strong correlation with leaf area (r = 0.995, p < 0.001) and leaf length (r = 0.965, p < 0.001), while leaf length also showed a similarly strong association with leaf area (r = 0.967, p < 0.001). Twig length showed moderate positive correlations with leaf traits (r ≈ 0.25–0.46), indicating that longer shoots tend to support larger leaves and greater foliar development. This relationship reflects coordinated vegetative growth, where shoot elongation facilitates increased leaf area deployment. In contrast, total leaf number exhibited weak and sometimes negative correlations with individual leaf size traits (r ≈ − 0.30 to − 0.35), suggesting a trade-off between leaf size and leaf number. This indicates that accessions producing a higher number of leaves may allocate resources toward quantity rather than individual leaf expansion.

Branching traits showed relatively weak and inconsistent relationships with most quantitative variables, indicating that branching architecture is largely independent of leaf morphology. Secondary branching exhibited weak or negative associations with leaf traits and twig length, suggesting that increased branching complexity may slightly limit elongation growth and leaf expansion. Primary branching, on the other hand, showed minimal correlation with most traits, indicating stability and limited influence on overall morphological variation. Important relationships were observed between qualitative and quantitative traits, particularly in relation to plant size. Stem girth showed a very strong positive correlation with the large stem girth category (r = 0.895, p < 0.001) and significant negative correlations with medium (r = − 0.715, p < 0.05) and small stem categories (r = − 0.642, p < 0.05). This confirms strong agreement between continuous and categorical measurements of stem size and validates the consistency of trait recording. Canopy-related traits demonstrated moderate associations with growth parameters. Larger canopy size tended to be associated with increased leaf number, reflecting enhanced vegetative development, whereas narrower canopy forms showed weaker associations. Branching patterns also contributed to canopy organization, with erect branching generally associated with more structured canopy forms, while drooping and inclined patterns showed more variable relationships.

Similarly, bark colour categories such as creamish brown (–0.813) and dark grey (–0.811 on PC2) contributed to the separation of morphotypes, indicating their ecological and structural relevance.

The combined influence of vegetative and structural traits highlights strong agro-climatic structuring of morphological variation, with distinct trait syndromes corresponding to different environmental conditions. These results provide robust evidence that key traits such as leaf morphology, canopy size, branching density, and bark characteristics collectively drive the differentiation of morphotypes across Rajasthan.

Clustering of agro-climatic zones and driving traits

Cluster analysis based on PCA coordinates grouped the agro-climatic zones into five distinct clusters, reflecting underlying morphological differentiation driven by key vegetative and structural traits. The clustering pattern indicated that zones with similar trait syndromes were grouped together, while highly distinct zones formed separate clusters (Fig. 8b).

Cluster III, comprising IA, IC, IIB, IVA, and V, represented the largest group and was characterized by intermediate morphotypes. These zones were associated with moderate values of leaf traits (leaf length, width and area), balanced canopy structure (medial to narrow canopy) and intermediate branching density, indicating stable and adaptable growth forms. The grouping suggests convergence of these traits across diverse agro-climatic conditions. Cluster II included IB and IIA, which were primarily driven by higher contributions of branching organization and canopy structuring traits. These zones showed relatively higher values along PC2, associated with traits such as increased twig length, total leaf number and structured canopy architecture, indicating well-developed but not extreme growth forms. Cluster I was uniquely represented by IIIA and was strongly influenced by traits associated with compact growth. This cluster was characterized by negative associations with leaf size traits and positive association with traits such as dense or intermediate branching, compact canopy structure and reduced vegetative expansion. Its distinct positioning was primarily driven by strong contributions to PC2, highlighting unique structural adaptation. Cluster IV, represented by IIIB, was differentiated mainly by branching architecture and canopy density traits. This cluster was associated with higher secondary branching, specific branching patterns, and moderate canopy development, indicating a structurally distinct but not highly expansive morphotype. Cluster V, consisting solely of IVB, represented the most distinct and expansive morphotype. This cluster was strongly driven by high positive loadings on PC1, particularly leaf area, leaf width, leaf length and wide canopy traits, along with larger stem size. Its separation reflects enhanced vegetative vigor and structural development, likely associated with favourable agro-climatic conditions (Fig. 9).

Discussion

This study provides the first comprehensive, large-scale assessment of morphological diversity in M. oleifera across all agro-climatic zones of Rajasthan using an integrated multivariate framework (PCA, MCA, and FAMD). By combining qualitative and quantitative trait analyses across 367 accessions, the present work advances previous studies that were geographically restricted or limited to single data types and offers new insights into how environmental gradients structure phenotypic variation in a widely adaptable multipurpose tree species. This sampling provides a robust representation of intra-specific variability and confirms that morphological differentiation is structured along agro-climatic gradients rather than forming discrete, zone-specific groupings. These findings are consistent with the biological characteristics of M. oleifera as a cross-pollinated perennial species, where genotype × environment interactions play a significant role in shaping observable traits [37]. Similar levels of morphological heterogeneity have been reported from Africa and South Asia, where relatively modest environmental gradients were sufficient to generate multiple morphotypes within local populations [4, 5], supporting the interpretation that environmental filtering rather than geographic isolation underpins much of the observed variation.

A central outcome of this study is the identification of plant architecture as a key axis of agro-climatic differentiation. Traits such as stem morphology, canopy size, branching density and bark texture showed significant zonal structuring and contributed strongly to multivariate dimensions, indicating that these traits integrate long-term environmental influences [4, 34, 38, 39]. From a functional perspective, such architectural traits represent cumulative outcomes of growth allocation strategies, where plants balance resource investment between structural support, light interception, and hydraulic safety. In arid and semi-arid zones, the predominance of compact morphotypes—characterized by narrow canopies, sparse branching, and smaller stem size—likely reflects adaptive responses to water limitation, where reduced canopy surface minimizes transpirational demand and mitigates hydraulic stress [38, 40, 41]. Conversely, the occurrence of more expansive morphotypes with larger stems and wider canopies in sub-humid and humid zones indicates enhanced carbon assimilation and biomass accumulation under favourable moisture and nutrient conditions [19, 42, 43]. These patterns align with trait-based ecological theory, where plant functional traits shift predictably along environmental gradients to optimize resource use and survival. Similar trends have been reported in recent studies across tropical and semi-arid systems, where canopy architecture and stem traits were identified as key indicators of drought adaptation and growth performance [43–48]. The moderate effect sizes observed in the present study further suggest that while environmental filtering is significant, it does not fully constrain variation, allowing coexistence of multiple morphotypes within the same agro-climatic zone.

Leaf-related traits emerged as the dominant drivers of quantitative variation, with leaf length, width and area contributing most strongly to PCA and FAMD dimensions. The large effect sizes associated with these traits indicate high environmental sensitivity, confirming that foliar development is a primary target of phenotypic adjustment in response to agro-climatic conditions [35, 44–46, 49]. Mechanistically, variation in leaf size can be linked to physiological regulation of stomatal conductance, transpiration and photosynthetic capacity. Larger leaves in humid zones likely enhance light interception and carbon gain, whereas smaller leaves in arid regions reduce water loss and heat load, consistent with well-established ecophysiological principles [43, 50–52]. The strong positive correlations among leaf traits observed in this study further indicate coordinated developmental regulation, suggesting that leaf expansion operates as an integrated functional module [20, 31]. In contrast, the observed negative association between leaf size and leaf number reflects a classical trade-off in plant resource allocation, where investment in larger leaves is balanced by reduced leaf production, and vice versa. Such trade-offs are widely reported in recent ecological studies and represent adaptive strategies for optimizing carbon gain under different environmental constraints [28, 53]. The relatively weak correlations between branching traits and leaf parameters indicate partial modular independence between structural and functional traits, enabling flexible adjustment of canopy architecture without directly constraining leaf-level physiology [ [46]. This modularity likely contributes to the broad ecological amplitude and adaptability of M. oleifera.

Multivariate analyses provided robust and complementary evidence for the structuring of morphological diversity. PCA revealed that variation is largely organized along two principal axes: a primary gradient of leaf morphology and a secondary gradient of vegetative growth. MCA indicated that qualitative variation is primarily driven by structural traits, particularly stem size and canopy characteristics, while FAMD demonstrated effective integration of both trait types, identifying two dominant dimensions representing structural robustness and functional leaf traits. The consistency across these analytical approaches strengthens confidence in the observed patterns and highlights the value of integrative multivariate frameworks in studying complex trait systems. Comparable multivariate studies reported that architectural and vegetative traits accounted for 24–38% of total inertia or variance in Indian, Pakistani, and Mexican populations [4, 38, 39]. Principal component analyses from Mexico, Nigeria, Telangana (India) and Bangladesh demonstrated that the first three to five components typically explain 60–99% of total phenotypic variance, largely driven by stem girth, plant height, leaf abundance and pod traits [34, 39, 54, 55]. Clustering analysis further revealed that accessions form distinct but overlapping morphotypes corresponding to compact, intermediate and robust growth strategies. The presence of all agro-climatic zones within each cluster indicates that morphological variation is not strictly environment-specific but reflects a continuum shaped by both environmental and genetic factors. This finding is consistent with molecular and morphological studies that report weak correspondence between genetic clusters and geographic origin in M. oleifera, suggesting high gene flow and phenotypic plasticity across populations [3, 27, 29, 56–58].

The study highlights that agro-climatic conditions act as filters that influence the frequency and distribution of morphotypes rather than strictly determining their occurrence. This is evidenced by the moderate effect sizes and substantial intra-zone variability observed across traits. Such patterns underscore the role of micro-environmental heterogeneity and local adaptation in shaping phenotypic expression, and caution against oversimplified interpretations of morphological variation based solely on macro-climatic classification. Despite the robustness of the dataset and analytical approach, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The absence of molecular data limits the ability to disentangle genetic differentiation from phenotypic plasticity and future integration with genomic approaches would provide deeper insights into the genetic basis of observed variation. In addition, architectural traits such as branching density and canopy shape are sensitive to management practices including pruning and grazing, and unquantified differences in management history may have contributed to within-zone variability. Furthermore, fine-scale edaphic variation within zones such as soil salinity, organic matter and nutrient availability was not explicitly assessed and is known to significantly influence stem, leaf, and pod traits [38, 59].

The PCA-based clustering suggest that agro-climatic zone-specific strategies should be adopted for the selection and utilization of M. oleifera germplasm. Zones such as IVB, which are associated with expansive morphotypes characterized by larger leaf area and wider canopy architecture, are recommended for high biomass production and intensive cultivation systems. In contrast, zones like IIIA, representing compact morphotypes with reduced vegetative growth, are more suitable for cultivation under arid and resource-limited conditions where stress tolerance is critical. The presence of intermediate clusters across most zones highlights the availability of broadly adaptable genotypes, which can be utilized for multi-environment cultivation and breeding programs. Therefore, future breeding, conservation, and cultivation efforts should prioritize zone-specific trait selection while also exploiting intermediate morphotypes for developing climate-resilient and widely adaptable varieties.

Further, the identification of key trait syndromes provides a valuable basis for targeted selection and breeding. Leaf traits, due to their strong contribution to overall variability and functional significance, can serve as reliable selection indices for biomass production and nutritional applications. Accessions exhibiting larger leaf area, higher secondary branching and expanded canopy architecture are likely to be advantageous in high-input or favourable environments, whereas compact morphotypes with reduced canopy and structural investment are better suited for arid and resource-limited conditions. These findings have direct implications for climate-resilient cultivation and agroforestry systems, particularly in dryland regions where adaptive trait selection is critical [60, 61].

Conclusion

The present study provides a comprehensive assessment of agro-climatic structuring of morphological diversity and phenotypic variation in M. oleifera across Rajasthan. The results revealed substantial morphological variability among accessions, with key traits such as stem morphology, canopy size, branching density and leaf characteristics playing a central role in defining overall diversity patterns.

From an applied perspective, the findings provide clear and actionable directions for breeding, conservation and climate-resilient cultivation programs. For breeding purposes, accessions characterized by large stem size, wider canopy and higher leaf area should be prioritized for biomass production, leaf yield and nutraceutical applications. In contrast, accessions exhibiting moderate growth, stable branching and narrower canopy should be selected for arid and semi-arid regions where resource-use efficiency and stress tolerance are critical. Hybridization strategies can be designed by crossing robust, high-yielding accessions with stress-tolerant morphotypes to develop ideotypes combining productivity and resilience. For conservation, a stratified germplasm collection strategy is recommended, ensuring representation of accessions from all agro-climatic zones and both major clusters identified through FAMD. For cultivation and climate adaptation, zone-specific recommendations can be implemented. In arid zones (e.g., IA, IB, IIIA), accessions with moderate canopy and branching density should be promoted due to their stability under limited water availability. In relatively favourable zones (e.g., IVB and V), accessions with enhanced leaf development and wider canopy can be utilized for intensive cultivation and higher biomass production.

Future research integrating molecular markers, genomic tools and fine-scale environmental data will be essential to disentangle genetic and environmental components of variation and to enhance the utilization of this species in sustainable agroecosystems under changing climatic conditions.
