Rural general surgical provision from the perspective of twenty‐two rural general surgeons: a thematic analysis
Authors: Jessica A. Paynter, Kirby R. Qin, David Hunter‐Smith, Janelle Brennan, Warren Rozen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAustralia continues to suffer from a geographical maldistribution of general surgical services with only 20% of general surgeons working rurally despite 29% of Australia's population residing outside major metropolitan centres. This qualitative study explored the impact of human capacity and infrastructure upon rural general surgery provision.MethodsThis qualitative study employed semi‐structured interviews of rural general surgeons. Participants were recruited via the Royal Australasian College of Surgeons (RACS) Rural newsletter and purposive sampling. All interviews were conducted between January 2023 and April 2023. Transcripts were transcribed, de‐identified, and thematically analysed.ResultsTwo female and 20 male rural general surgeons were interviewed from seven of the eight Australian State or Territories. Four main themes were identified which reflected the impact of human capacity and infrastructure upon Australian rural general surgery provision: (1) small hospital syndrome – and the impact, (2) the rural general surgeon identity, (3) infrastructure and disempowerment and (4) over‐reliance on visiting surgeons.ConclusionImproving access to equitable general surgical care for rural Australians requires appropriate infrastructure and a well‐trained, sustainable multidisciplinary surgical team (human capacity). A greater understanding of the issues may help drive rational, long‐term supportive solutions.

Introduction

The provision of rural general surgery continues to suffer from ‘urban excess and rural deficit’. It is known that rural Australians suffer from poorer health outcomes, and this is partly due to a maldistribution of medical care, including surgical specialists. 1 Unfortunately this is not a new issue. Whilst in an ideal world access to general surgical services would be easily amenable to policy and investment, this enduring issue has persisted not just in Australia, but in most countries globally. 2

For decades, the Royal Australasian College of Surgeons (RACS) has directed efforts towards increasing rural surgeon supply. 1 Whilst this is critical, general surgeons are not the only health professionals who provide surgical care, and human capacity is paramount to delivering surgical care. Human capacity is defined by an individual or team based level of knowledge, skills, technical expertise and ability. In surgery – human capacity refers to not only individual surgeons, but also team‐members of the surgical team. 3 In addition to human capacity, equitable general surgical care cannot be delivered without up‐to‐date infrastructure and technology. Infrastructure refers to the ‘built environment and supporting elements [of a hospital] and includes equipment, access, information technology (IT) and sustainability’. 4 General surgeons working in rural environment accept a need to work within constrained resources, often cooperating to share resources and making use of what they have, including repurposing and reusing, but there is still a recognized need for modern infrastructure. 5

This research aimed to qualitatively assess the impact of human capacity and infrastructure upon rural general surgical provision from the perspective of Australian rural general surgeons.

Methods

Study design

A qualitative interview study was conducted according to the COREQ‐32 framework (Appendix 1). Ethics approval was granted by Monash University (MU‐LREC 32829).

Participants, interview guide and interviews

An interview‐based study was undertaken with subsequent reflexive, thematic analysis. This was chosen as the optimal technique to provide a foundational knowledge of the nuanced domain of Australian rural general surgery. A semi‐structured interview guide was created, based on a recent Australian narrative review and following an initial trial on three local rural general surgeons. 6 Recruitment occurred via advertisment within the RACS rural newsletter. All interviews were audio‐recorded by the principal investigator (JAP) by video‐conferencing software between January and April 2023. All participants were fully qualified general surgeons practising rurally at the time of the interview. Rurality was determined by the Australian Statistical Geography Standard (edition 3) – Remoteness Areas classification. 7

Transcribing, coding and thematic analysis

Interviews were recorded, transcribed, deidentified and analysed using NVivo v12.0 – a qualitative data analysis software. Transcripts were coded independently by two researchers (JAP and JB) who developed and refined a thematic structure. Coded transcriptions and field notes were transformed into key themes. Concepts and notes developed during the interview process were revisited, discussed, refined and categorized into themes. The researchers discussed and arranged the findings until consensus was achieved about the validity and suitability of themes. Researcher reflexivity was maintained by keeping a reflexive journal and providing interview transcripts to individual participants for final approval.

Results

Two female and 20 male surgeons were interviewed from seven of eight Australian State and Territories. Eighteen graduated from RACS and four obtained their qualifications internationally. Further demographics have been omitted to protect participant anonymity as rural general surgery is a small professional community. Four key themes were identified from interviews of 22 participants. Key themes were supported by multiple subthemes (Fig. 1). Quotations in support of each theme are shown in Appendix 2.

Themes and subthemes.

Theme 1: small hospital syndrome

We have nominally termed this theme small hospital syndrome – but acknowledge that better nomenclature may exist. Small hospital syndrome refers to the environment where the rural general surgeon works. This was identified in their reflections of the challenges faced by surgeons in smaller, rural hospitals compared to larger, highly‐resourced hospitals.

Analysis identified limited opportunities for professional development amongst an overworked, exhausted and transient surgical workforce. As a result, it became easier to create a culture of pure survival rather than a thriving environment that champions constant improvement. Secondly, participants highlighted that the lack of regular and consistent critical care teams is a major limitation in smaller centres. The presence of transient peri‐operative support for severely ill and/or comorbid patients was a persistent impediment to their practice and workforce retention. Participants also voiced that a lack of critical care services prevented them from operating at their full scope of practice. Thirdly, participants identified that negative interactions with hospital administration as an obstruction to their work. Hospital politics is often more intimate and personal in smaller, rural hospitals. If not managed effectively, small hospital politics can ruin relationships and result in the loss of surgical services. Politics often play a larger role in small rural hospitals (compared to their larger metropolitan counterparts) due to close and frequent interactions between members of the surgical team and beyond. Staff are frequently only employed at a single institution and, thus, interpersonal disputes are difficult to avoid. Finally, participants felt limited in their scope of practice due to professional isolation. This was especially pertinent amongst individual surgeons who performed certain procedures at a low volume – they were not confident enough to take on cases in a rural setting without suitable professional support and backup.

Theme 2: rural general surgeon identity

This theme explored the motivations behind a rural general surgeon's work. First, rural general surgeons highlighted that their local community motivated them to upskill and broaden their scope of practice, but that they were often limited by a lack of a formalized process to enable change in practice. Second, despite often working in relative isolation, rural general surgeons still identified strongly with their institution of training (whether rural/regional, metropolitan, or overseas) when calling for help. If, however they trained internationally, who they called for help was much more limited. This was often reflected in their day‐to‐day practice, with transfer destinations often based on where training occurred. Third, in the event of patients requiring transfer to tertiary centres, participants reported that they were often disregarded as competent clinicians when speaking to metropolitan‐based retrieval systems. This resulted in inefficient patient care that also took time and attention away from other patients. Fourth, respondents had a strong motivation towards community advocacy. They wanted to ensure that rural patients and rural general surgical teams had a seat at the table when important policy decisions were made. This, however, often resulted in large volumes of pro bono work which often proved to be exhausting for the participants and a contribution to burnout. Finally, this cohort of surgeons were strongly motivated by the humanitarian impact that their surgical work had on underserved rural communities. They believed that their surgical skillset had far greater reach in the rural setting compared to a metropolitan area. This need to make a continuous impact in their community was core to their identity as rural surgeons.

Theme 3: infrastructure and disempowerment

This theme relates to the provision of technology and infrastructure required to deliver efficient, gold‐standard surgical care. Appropriate infrastructure is crucial in every step of the surgical pathway, including investigation, diagnosis, the surgical procedure and follow‐up. More and more, technology is also playing an important role in clinical documentation and collaboration. Surgeons expressed a general dissatisfaction in relation to rural surgical infrastructure. They reflected challenges encountered working with an inefficient IT system, a lagging of innovation, and inadequate access to up‐to‐date radiological and pathological services required for surgical decision making. The lack of access to current technology, both in and out of the operating theatre, can lead to a feeling of disempowerment. 8

Rural general surgeons stated that their clinical work was continuously disrupted by an inefficient IT system. These systems lagged behind those that they were exposed to during their training. Younger, newly‐graduated rural general surgeons who had recently trained across metropolitan hospitals lamented the non‐existence of electronic medical records. Where electronic records did exist, they often comprised multiple asynchronous systems that did not communicate with each other. Participants highlighted that rural centres were slower to introduce new technology which impacted their attempts to innovate within the operating field. Finally, participants examined the limitations in pathological and radiological services in rural communities. Radiology services were often lacking a locally‐based radiologist. Furthermore, interventional radiology services were extremely scarce. Surgeons expressed that pathological services were sometimes inadequate but acknowledged they had smaller populations and smaller throughfare to justify a larger service. Rural patients often had to travel great distances to perform certain investigations– resulting in delays to their ultimate date of surgery. There is also a general lack of consumer choice for pathology providers. Insufficient competition amongst pathological service providers in rural Australia has been previously raised at government and consumer levels with this lack of competition possibly stifling innovation. 9

Theme 4: over‐reliance on visiting surgeons

Although visiting or ‘locum’ surgeons fill essential gaps in the healthcare system, their presence can also have negative impacts. Participants scrutinized the impact of visiting surgeons in their rural communities. They highlighted that whilst visiting surgeons provided much‐needed service provision, they also generated increased costs due to locum rates, complicated resource allocation and left complications for local surgeons to manage. Participants emphasized the burden of ensuring continuity of care in the wake of transient visiting surgeons. Local rural general surgeons are forced to compensate for staff discontinuity which adds to their already heavy workload.

Participants expressed that newly‐graduated surgeons are more likely to value work‐life balance, and therefore it is difficult to recruit the critical mass of rural general surgeons to make on‐call commitments palatable. One participant commented: ‘Gone are the days of the single‐town surgeon who was always available and on call. That will never come back, that's done. Now we have this sporadic on‐call service maintained by whoever the state sends during the week, for a few days. And sure it's definitely better than nothing, but I think it's a stop‐gate, and we need to stop relying on it, it's a temporising measure. And generally they're newer [surgeons], not prepared and not that enthusiastic about being here’. Other participants also highlighted the over‐reliance of health departments and local administrators on FIFO surgeons, with sparse plans to recruit permanent staff. Visiting surgeons should demonstrate a greater commitment to the rural institution rather than just ‘creaming off the country’, with participants advocating for better professional relationships, particularly education for junior staff.

Discussion

Overcoming small hospital syndrome

A strong culture of safety, respect and empowerment is associated with improved clinical care. Many rural hospitals operate at a subsistence level due to staff shortages, financial constraints, under‐resourcing and geographic challenges. This may limit a unit or hospital from adopting a culture of continuous improvement. Recognizing this and creating necessary change requires time and persistence. For example, upskilling rural nurses and allied health staff will require remunerated time away from work and a culture that values continuous education and change. This is particularly difficult to implement in under resourced units with insufficient staffing and high staff turn‐over. 10

Participants identified a lack of critical care capacity at their rural institution as a major prohibitor of providing general surgery locally. The College of Intensive Care Medicine could implement rural policies and working groups in collaboration with the RACS Rural Surgical Section. A shared goal by the colleges may deliver a sustainable rural ICU workforce in alignment with the RACS Rural Health Equity Strategic Action Plan. Participating rural general surgeons emphasized that they are often restricted in the operations they can perform due to limitations in peri‐operative services (e.g., body mass index over 40 or children below age 10). This concern has been raised by the rural generalist anaesthetist community who feared that ‘[an] urban‐centric view informs what is considered safe rural practice without regard to patient access’. 11

Ensuring good governance of rural general surgical services will ensure that rural general surgeons can focus upon their principal job – operating and providing clinical care. Participants highlighted an increasing need for management to create rural positions that are appealing and attractive to newly graduated general surgeons, particularly females. These positions need to support the non‐professional needs of rural general surgeons (e.g., childcare, housing, and jobs for professional partners), flexible working conditions and diversity. 12 , 13

Redefining the rural general surgeon identity

Currently, the ability to develop a broad and extended scope of practice as a rural general surgeons is largely driven by individual surgeon motivation, with a previous study showing that 6 months of training is typically required to develop a subspecialist skillset. 14 A formalized fellowship program (Post Fellowship Education and Training in Rural Surgery) was launched by General Surgeons Australia (GSA) in 2021–2022, but this is currently on‐hold. Expansion or re‐initiation of this could assist with formalized development of a broad and extended scope of rural general surgery practice, given that there is little incentive for major tertiary hospitals to offer short term contracts to rural general surgeons compared to formal subspeciality trainees. 15

Rural general surgeons do need to call for help, and mentoring has been consistently being identified as an avenue to better support the rural general surgeon in this situation. Whilst mentoring is still advocated for in the RACS ‘Retain for Rural’ strategy and ‘Train for Rural’ strategy, it is worth highlighting that mentorship has previously been trialled in the failed Rural Surgical Training Program. 1 A review of this program identified an inadequate number of mentors, many trainees were never allocated a mentor, and 65% of mentors lost contact with their mentees. 16 Providing formal support is particularly important for overseas trained rural general surgeons who may lack local training mentors. Mentor and training networks could assist rural general surgeons in arranging inter‐hospital transfer. Surgeons from the larger hospital could even be part of the rural on call roster so there is mutual understanding of the capabilities and difficulties of each specific rural centre, particularly as no two rural services are the same.

Rural general surgeons were also concerned by the communications and interactions with retrieval staff. Given retrieval and trauma systems were intentionally designed to streamline services, a solution to overcoming this could be the opportunity for rural general surgeons to provide regular feedback following retrieval interactions and have greater stakeholder involvement. Some rural surgeons have undertaken advocacy and committee work, out of fear of only metropolitan focused surgeons advocating for rural general surgery provision, and this has contributed to increasing burn‐out for some participants. Remedying this includes an ongoing use of video conferencing, which has occurred since COVID‐19, and also mandated co‐option of a rural representative on RACS and GSA, which has allowed more rural surgeons to be involved at the policy level rather than leaving it for the few who were willing to stand for higher committees, for example state and national councils. 1

Given the increased diversity in trainees, it is imperative that this diversity is represented in the rural general surgical workforce. This will require increasing flexible recruitment and training of female general surgeons. 12 , 13 This also extends to cultural diversity, where hopefully, the community will soon start seeing the positive effects of the Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Selection Initiative. 17 An interesting insight is that interview participants were primarily motivated to work rurally for humanitarian reasons. Whilst traditionally, the provision of rural general surgery has relied heavily on International Medical Graduates and return of service obligations (i.e., Bonding), the desire to work rurally seems to be becoming a personalized, positive choice for Australian graduates, and more needs to be done to support this. 13 Increased financial incentives have been shown to have the greatest effect on long‐term retention. Financial incentives would recognize the rural general surgeon's increased working and on‐call hours, limited private surgical work due to a lack of rural private hospitals, and fewer professional development opportunities. 2

Improving infrastructure

Improving IT and diagnostic service provision for rural general surgeons largely lies outside the scope of a single institution. Participants valued clinical systems that talked to each other, were usable, and generated high quality health data, as current systems reduced the rural general surgeon's work efficiency, contributed to increased clinical administration duties and work‐related fatigue. The development of state‐wide electronic medical records and medical imaging sharing may help to assist with service provision.

Participants commonly cited decreased access to interventional radiology which is increasingly being used in many situations to avoid operative management, as well as providing support for image guided biopsy and pre‐procedure localisation, for example hook‐wires for breast lesions. This requires radiologists to be on site at a rural hospital, even in part‐time capacity, to deliver these services. This is in contrast to a move towards remote reporting, which only provides part of the radiology service that rural general surgeons require. Rural hospital contracts to imaging providers should take into account the need for on‐site radiology outreach to ensure relatively simple and common procedures can still be performed in the rural setting. This could be an area of collaboration for both the Australian and New Zealand Rural Radiology Special Interest Group and RACS Rural to develop guidelines about what key services should be available at smaller rural hospitals.

Finally, the pathology sector is highly consolidated and competitive, with a large focus on quality service provision due to strict accreditation practices. Barriers to increased rural pathology services stem from heavy regulation, large expenses required to build and develop laboratories, collection centre costs, transport costs, obtaining tenders and necessity scale and scope of pathology services. At the moment there is little competition in smaller rural centres between pathology providers which often results in decreasing services due to higher costs required for transportation of specimens. Enabling competition between public hospital pathology and private pathology providers may enable improved consumer choice which could drive improvements in service delivery. Ultimately, government regulation or funding may be required, to ensure equitable access for rural communities, taking into account less financial margins for placing laboratories and collection centres in smaller towns, and increasing costs of specimen transfers.

Combating the over‐reliance on visiting surgeons

Our research suggests that visiting surgeons (locum, outreach, FIFO) provide both opportunities and challenges for the resident rural general surgeons. A solution to this could include the aforementioned rural‐metropolitan networks. These could ensure formalized, reciprocal arrangements are in place to enable professional development, ready locum coverage, education (research and policy support), training of the junior workforce and multidisciplinary team environments with quality audit assurance for the rural general surgeon, and thereby ease professional isolation. This would also ensure that metropolitan general surgeons had an understanding of the services available in rural centres, alongside gaining the ability to operate in a different environment. Good outreach involves mutual understanding, and a commitment to provide support to the rural centre, rather than using an outreach visit as an opportunity to increase income and referral networks whilst leaving the local surgeons to ‘clean up’ any complications and not provide on call support for inter‐hospital transfer.

The key to reducing rural over‐reliance on locum surgeons lies in the successful recruitment and retention of permanent staff. Our team has published extensive research on strategies that may help to achieve this. 6 , 12 , 13 A recent report by McGrail et al. highlighted the role of universities in addressing rural workforce shortages. 18 They suggested a seven‐point approach that could be applied to surgeons‐in‐training: enact a social mission statement for rural service, select for rural workforce outcomes, make medical training locations more accessible for rural people, highlight rural medicine in medical school curricula, invest in rural training pathways, facilitate rural prevocational and specialist training, and evaluate and recommit to the social mission. Importantly, preferencing those with rural backgrounds has already been adopted by GSA in their selection of surgical trainees. Rural general surgeons should continue to have ongoing representation and stakeholder engagement with rural clinical schools and Regional Training Hubs, to ensure that they facilitate opportunities for advocacy, research and education – particularly as surgeons‐in‐training navigate training pathways.

The strength of this research is that it gathered rich data from a specific target cohort that provides rural general surgical care. The research team was diverse, encompassing both rural and metropolitan surgeons, across a range of specialities and qualifications. Limitations include selection bias in the recruitment of surgeons – as those keen to advocate for change may have self‐selected, and this may not be generalizable to all Australian rural general surgeons. Interviewing other members of the rural surgical team may be a useful future study.

Conclusion

This qualitative research has identified four themes that reflect the provision of rural general surgery in Australia. These include the nuances of working in a smaller hospital, understanding the rural surgeon identity, challenges in infrastructure and the need for mutually beneficial outreach. Our hope is that this project provides insight and acts as a guide for service improvement in the future.
