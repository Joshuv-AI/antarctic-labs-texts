Multi-factorial analysis of smoking behaviour and cessation process
Authors: Ahmet Gurbuzel, Nurgul Naurzvai, Can Ozturk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and Objectives:Tobacco use is a major global health concern, contributing to six of the eight most common causes of death worldwide. In Turkey, the average number of cigarettes smoked per day by individuals over 15 years old surpasses the global average. To mitigate this public health issue, it is crucial to develop strategic education models and state policies. Additionally, closely monitoring patients who seek smoking cessation services can improve outcomes by identifying specific smoking patterns and tailoring treatments accordingly. This study aimed to analyze the smoking behavior, characteristics, and cessation outcomes of patients aged 25-65 years who visited the smoking cessation outpatient clinic.Methods:This cross-sectional prospective study included 307 patients. We collected socio-demographic data, prior smoking cessation experiences, addiction levels, and conducted medical examinations to assess health status and medical history. This information was used to develop personalized treatment and follow-up plans.Results:The mean age of the patients was 42±11 years, with 74% being married and 55% being male. At the end of a three-month period, the smoking cessation rate was 49% for women and 33% for men. Notably, patients who started smoking at a median age of 19±5 years had higher cessation rates compared to those who started at 17±4 years. The most common reasons cited for starting smoking were environmental, social, and curiosity factors.Conclusion:Our findings suggest that a personalized approach to smoking cessation, which takes into account factors such as age, gender, and comorbidities, may be more effective in helping patients quit smoking. Future research should focus on long-term outcomes and the impact of specific interventions tailored to individual patient characteristics.

INTRODUCTION

Cigarette smoking remains a primary driver of preventable disease and mortality worldwide, with its detrimental impact on public health is undeniable.[1] Turkey, which is grappling with alarmingly high smoking rates, particularly among adult males, has embarked on a concerted effort to address this pervasive public health concern.[2] According to the World Health Organization and national health data, Turkey has made substantial progress in tobacco control, but smoking rates remain concerning.[3] In 2019, the adult smoking rate in Turkey was estimated to be around 31%, with the rate among men notably higher at 41–43% and the rate among women at 13–14%.[4] Furthermore, this public health challenge is exacerbated by the concerning prevalence of smoking among youth as a 2017 survey indicated that nearly 7% of adolescents aged 13–15 reported using cigarettes. The addictive nature of smoking, largely attributed to nicotine, results in a powerful physical, behavioural, and psychological dependence that can be challenging to overcome.[34] The World Health Organization recognises nicotine addiction as a chronic disease and highlights nicotine’s critical role as a psycho-active substance that can contribute to mental and behavioural disorders.[5] Recognising the multifaceted nature of nicotine addiction, a successful smoking cessation strategy must incorporate a comprehensive approach that addresses the various components of this chronic condition. To effectively initiate and support the smoking cessation process, healthcare professionals should begin by thoroughly assessing the individual’s level of nicotine addiction as this information is crucial for guiding the selection and implementation of appropriate interventions and support measures. Indeed, studies have consistently demonstrated the efficacy of behavioural therapies, such as cognitive-behavioural therapy and motivational interviewing, in helping individuals overcome the psychological and behavioural aspects of smoking addiction.[345] Beyond individual-level interventions, population-level strategies targeting the prevention of smoking initiation and the promotion of smoking cessation are crucial for achieving a meaningful and sustainable reduction in smoking rates. Comprehensive tobacco control policies, such as the enforcement of smoke-free environments, the restriction of tobacco advertising and promotion, and the provision of accessible and affordable smoking cessation services, have been shown to significantly contribute to the reduction of smoking prevalence. This prospective cohort study aimed to better understand the factors that influence sustained smoking cessation outcomes in participants at a smoking cessation out-patient clinic.

METHOD

This prospective cohort study included 307 participants aged 25 to 65 who met the inclusion criteria out of 378 applicants. The study was conducted at a smoking cessation out-patient clinic, with approval from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Gazi University. The Fagerström Nicotine Dependence Test (FTND) was employed to assess smoking dependency severity. A power analysis based on smoking cessation prevalence yielded a result of 0.914 at a 95% confidence interval. Patients with severe renal and hepatic insufficiency were excluded. Those not attending follow-up sessions or with missing data were also excluded. Data were gathered through face-to-face meetings using the “Smoking Cessation Treatment Support Program” questionnaire form, consisting of seven sections and 32 questions. The initial meeting recorded demographic data, smoking habits, and FTND scores. Follow-up sessions on the 15th, 30th, 45th, 60th, and 90th days recorded vital signs, weight changes, CO levels, and any difficulties experienced during cessation. The main outcome variable was sustained smoking cessation, defined as intentional resistance to smoking urges lasting at least 3 months. The SPSS 25.0 statistical package was used for analysis. Descriptive statistics, Kruskal–Wallis analysis, Pearson Chi-square, Yates Corrected Chi-square Test, Mann–Whitney U Test, and Spearman correlation tests were applied. Data were categorized by age, education, marital status, pack-years, age of smoking initiation, annual cost of cigarettes, and other relevant factors.

RESULTS

The study included 307 participants, with diverse age groups with mostly married participants [Tables 1-7]. Only 1/3 of all participants had a university education, indicating various educational backgrounds. Most of the patients had a smoking history of 20 or more pack-years, signifying a substantial smoking intensity [Tables 2-6]. Almost half of the patients started smoking before the age of 18. More than 2/3 of the patients smoked freely at home and had another smoker at home, highlighting potential challenges for cessation [Tables 8-11]. More than half of the participants (56%) attempted to quit smoking once before the current cessation. 12% of the participants attempted to quit twice, and 2% of them attempted to quit thrice, which emphasises the challenging nature of quit smoking. 35% reported prior abstinence of more than 1 year, showcasing resilience. Primary reasons for quitting included fear of getting sick (60%) and economic concerns (19%). Difficulties faced during cessation included irritability (51%), excessive desire to smoke (50%), and mouth sores (24%). 43% of the participants had a smoker spouse at home, and in contrast to this, 62% of the participants had colleagues disturbed by smoking, emphasising strong social positive and negative factors for cessation. Overall, 40% achieved sustained smoking cessation, indicating both challenges and successes in the cessation journey [Graphics 1-3 and Table 12]. Factors influencing cessation success included gender (P = 0.004), age at first smoking (P = 0.009), and annual cost of cigarettes (P = 0.022). Environmental factors like having another smoker at home (P < 0.001) and having a smoker spouse (P = 0.001) significantly impacted cessation success. Family members’ attitudes also played a role, with those disturbed more likely to quit (P = 0.026) [Tables 13-15].

DISCUSSION

This study provides a thorough investigation of various factors associated with smoking cessation, drawing on data collected from routine polyclinic procedures over a one-and-a-half-year period.[36] The present research aims to examine the relationship between smoking behaviour and various demographic factors within a middle-aged population. Existing literature has established a higher prevalence of smoking among males, which is consistent with the observed demographic characteristics of the current sample.[78910] The extant research has consistently identified socio-economic status as a key determinant of cigarette smoking,[11] and the current investigation will build upon these findings by exploring the complex interplay between socio-economic factors and the observed socio-demographic patterns of smoking. The disproportionately higher representation of male smokers in the study sample reflects the broader socio-cultural context, where smoking has traditionally been more prevalent among men compared to women in the country.[11] This observation aligns with a substantial body of evidence from prior studies demonstrating significant disparities in smoking prevalence across a range of socio-demographic dimensions, including gender, race, income, and educational attainment.[101112]

A noteworthy finding is the significant difference in quit rates between genders, with women exhibiting a higher success rate in quitting. This contradicts global trends and raises questions about potential cultural or regional influences on quit smoking behaviour.[313] The association between advancing age and higher success rates in quitting smoking is consistent with previous research.[13] The emphasis on the 45–65 age group underscores the importance of considering age as a factor in cessation program design.[1314] The correlation between married individuals and higher application rates for smoking cessation is in line with expectations.[4] Interestingly, the study found no significant relationship between education level and cessation status in Turkey, which contrasts with the global trend.[3] Beyond the demographic and socio-economic dimensions, this study delved deeper into understanding the diverse motivations and drivers behind smoking behaviour in the Turkish context. The examination of alternative smoking methods, such as the use of rolled cigarettes, and the economic considerations that motivate quitting provided valuable insights into the multifaceted nature of smoking patterns and cessation efforts in the Turkish population.[3] The study’s findings suggest that a complex interplay of factors, including emulation, curiosity, and economic concerns, underlie smoking initiation in this population.[3413] Notably, the study also uncovered the significance of post-meal smoking behaviour and the challenges posed by previous unsuccessful quit attempts, highlighting the pivotal role of behavioural and psychological factors in the smoking cessation process.[4] The findings of this study underscore the multifaceted nature of smoking cessation in the Turkish context. A range of interconnected factors, including health concerns, existing medical conditions, and economic considerations, shape the decision and ability to quit smoking. Understanding these nuances is crucial for developing targeted and effective smoking cessation interventions tailored to the specific needs and cultural context of the Turkish population.[3413]

The exploration of alternative smoking methods and the economic motivation for quitting among rolled cigarette smokers provides valuable insights into the diverse reasons behind smoking behaviour. The identification of emulation, curiosity, and economic concerns as key drivers for smoking initiation highlights the complex and multifaceted nature of this behaviour.[15] The acknowledgment of the influence of cigarette costs as a significant motivator for smoking cessation aligns with broader public health efforts to address the economic factors that contribute to this behaviour and encourage individuals to quit.[15] Understanding the nuanced behavioural and psychological factors associated with smoking cessation, such as the role of post-meal smoking routines and the challenges posed by previous quit attempts, is crucial for tailoring interventions that can effectively address the individualised complexities of this issue. The prominence of health concerns, existing diseases, and economic considerations as key motivations for quitting underscores the intricate interconnections between individual perceptions of health, personal financial circumstances, and broader societal influences that shape the decision to abstain from smoking.[131516] The recognition of irritability and excessive desire to smoke as prominent difficulties during the cessation process emphasises the need for personalised strategies in managing withdrawal symptoms, such as close follow-up and dose adjustments in treatment approaches. The acknowledgment of the influence of the social environment on quitting success and the correlation with nicotine addiction scores highlights the multifaceted nature of smoking cessation, where individual factors and broader social contexts converge to impact an individuals, ability to successfully abstain from smoking.[1517] The emphasis on smoking-related respiratory symptoms and their correlation with pack-years underlines the importance of addressing not only the behavioural aspects but also the physiological consequences of smoking in cessation programs. The comparative success rates of different pharmacological methods, such as varenicline, bupropion, and nicotine replacement therapy, provide valuable insights into the effectiveness of various treatment options.[1317] Furthermore, the incorporation of psychotherapeutic approaches and their associated success rates add depth to the discussion, showcasing the comprehensive range of interventions that can be employed to support individuals in their smoking cessation journey.[1819] The multifaceted exploration of alternative smoking methods and the economic motivation for quitting among rolled cigarette smokers has revealed the complex interplay of individual, social, and environmental factors that shape smoking behaviour and influence the challenges faced in achieving successful cessation.[17181920]

The pursuit of smoking cessation is a multi-layered endeavour, shaped by a tapestry of interconnected elements that collectively influence an individual’s capacity to successfully overcome nicotine addiction. Close follow-up and dose adjustments have been recognised as critical interventions, underscoring the significance of personalised care and monitoring in the cessation process.[20] The acknowledgment of how an individual’s social environment can impact their quitting success, coupled with the observed correlation between nicotine addiction scores and this success, highlights the intricate relationship between social and individual factors in the complex process of smoking cessation.[18] The emphasis on smoking-related respiratory symptoms and their correlation with pack-years further emphasises the importance of addressing not only the behavioural aspects of smoking but also the physiological consequences in cessation programs. The comparative analysis of the success rates of different pharmacological methods, such as varenicline, bupropion, and nicotine replacement therapy, provides valuable insights into the relative effectiveness of these treatment options. The inclusion of psychotherapy and its associated success rate adds depth to the discussion on the multifaceted nature of cessation interventions, underscoring the potential benefits of combining pharmacological and behavioural approaches. Intriguingly, the study’s findings reveal that the benefits of smoking cessation go far beyond simply abstaining from the habit, as evidenced by the observed improvements in respiratory function, as measured by the FEV1/FVC ratio. Furthermore, the assessment of lipid profiles adds a holistic dimension to the understanding of health outcomes, with the data indicating a negative correlation between pack-years and HDL levels.[2021]

The holistic approach to smoking cessation, encompassing behavioural, physiological, and pharmacological interventions, holds the promise of unlocking more effective and sustainable pathways to overcoming nicotine addiction and improving overall health outcomes.[17202122] The alignment of post-treatment cessation rates with broader country-level data supports the external validity of the study. The emphasis on professional guidance and the caution against unproven methods reflect a commitment to evidence-based practices. The concluding emphasis on the role of healthcare professionals in taking smoking histories, providing information, and directing individuals to smoking cessation clinics underscores the public health implications of the study’s findings. CONCLUSION

Smoking cessation is a complex and multifaceted process that requires a comprehensive understanding of the various factors influencing an individual’s ability to quit successfully. This study aims to provide a comprehensive insight into the demographic, behavioural, and environmental factors that contribute to smoking cessation among adults. The study’s findings highlight the importance of considering individual characteristics and environmental influences when designing effective smoking cessation interventions. These findings underscore the need for personalised approaches to smoking cessation as individual factors such as self-efficacy, motivation, and impulsivity can significantly impact the success of cessation efforts. Additionally, the study emphasises the importance of parsing the smoking cessation process into distinct “milestones,” such as achieving initial abstinence, maintaining abstinence without a lapse, and avoiding relapse. This nuanced understanding of the cessation process can inform the development of more targeted interventions that address the specific challenges individuals face at each stage.
