Cancer-testis antigen ACRBP: Cytotoxic response to its HLA-A2 restricted peptide and immune features in ovarian cancer
Authors: Xia Zeng, Li-Na Lin, Xiaoquan Li, Wei-Xia Nong, Feng Li, Li Lan, Ping Wang, Ai Ye, Qing-Mei Zhang, Bin Luo, Ying-Ying Ge, Xiao-Xun Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWhile our prior study identified the HLA-A *0201-restricted ACRBP epitope peptide and demonstrated its capacity to generate cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) in vitro, the clinical relevance of the peptide-induced T cell reactivity in ovarian cancer (OC) patients and the in vivo anti-tumor efficacy of these CTLs remain unexplored. In this study, dendritic cells were sensitized with ACRBP peptide (ALLVLCYSI) and co-cultured with autologous CD8+T cells to induce the production of specific cytotoxic T lymphocytes (Pep-CTLs). The anti-tumor effects of Pep-CTLs were evaluated in SCID mice bearing human ovarian cancer (OC) OVCAR-3 cells. Concurrently, we co-cultured ALLVLCYSI peptide with peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from OC patients (HLA-A2+, ACRBP+) and assessed the number of specific T cells using ELISPOT assays. The immunological impact of the ACRBP peptide against human OC was validated through both in vitro and in vivo experiments. These findings establish a preclinical foundationfor developing ACRBP peptide-based vaccines in OC immunotherapy. To further elucidate ACRBP’s role in OC treatment, the study analyzed single-cell RNA sequencing data from 8 OC patients and bulk RNA sequencing data from the Cancer Genome Atlas Project (TCGA) comprising 308 ovarian cancer cases. This analysis aimed to explore the heterogeneity among ACRBP-expressing tumor cell populations and to investigate the correlation between ACRBP expression and immune molecule expression (including MHC and chemokines) alongside chemotherapy response. These insights furnish a theoretical framework supporting the future application of ACRBP in tumor immunotherapy and strategies to prevent immune escape.

Introduction

Ovarian cancer (OC) is one of the most common malignant tumors of the female reproductive system, ranking third among gynecological cancers in terms of incidence. However, due to the lack of early symptoms, OC is often diagnosed at an advanced stage, leading to a poor prognosis.1,2 Despite continuous improvements in treatment methods in recent years, the 5-year survival rate for ovarian cancer remains only 43.3%, with little change over the past 20 years.2–4 Therefore, there is an urgent need for better early diagnostic methods and treatment strategies to improve patient survival rates.

In recent years, the implementation of tumor immunotherapy has alleviated some of the challenges in cancer treatment.5 Tumor immunotherapy works by activating the body’s immune system to target and treat cancer. Tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) are one of the primary targets of immunotherapy, with one class of TAAs being cancer-testis antigens (CTAs). CTAs are abnormally expressed in various malignant tumors, but they are not present in normal tissues, except for the testis and placenta. CTAs have been shown to be closely related to tumor cell viability, proliferation, metastasis and invasion, and possess immunogenic properties. Therefore, CTAs are considered one of the best target antigens for designing therapeutic cancer vaccines, adoptive T cell therapies, and other immunotherapeutic approaches.6–9

ACRBP (also known as OY-TES-1 or CT23) is a member of the CTA family10 and is primarily expressed in the spermatogenic cell lineage, with no expression detected in normal adult ovarian epithelium. Several studies have confirmed that ACRBP is aberrantover expressed in OC tissues. For example, Tammela et al. found that the positive expression rates of ACRBP mRNA and protein in OC were 23% and 60%, respectively, through RT-PCR and immunohistochemistry. Additionally, they analyzed serum from 21 OC patients and found that in the 10 patients with ACRBP-positive cancer tissue, one patient’s serum was tested positive for ACRBP antibodies.11 Fan et al. used ACRBP antibodies on OC tissue microarrays and found that 81% of OC tissues expressed ACRBP.12 Lin et al. also confirmed that ACRBP mRNA and protein expression in OC tissues was significantly higher than in normal tissues, especially in epithelial OC, and that ACRBP expression was significantly related to the cancer stage and chemotherapy sensitivity. Furthermore, serological analysis showed that 28.5% of the serum from 56 OC patients was tested positive for ACRBP antibodies.13 Whitehurst et al. found that high expression of ACRBP was closely associated with increased proliferation of OC cells, shorter survival time, and faster recurrence in OC patients.14 These findings make ACRBP a promising target for immunotherapy in OC.

In recent years, researchers have been exploring the potential of ACRBP epitope peptides as targets for tumor immunotherapy. Okumura et al. identified an HLA-A24-restricted epitope peptide of ACRBP (KTPFVSPLL) and confirmed that CTLs activated by this peptide could specifically kill the small cell lung cancer cell line LK79 (CT23+, HLA-A24+).15 Safavi et al. combined immune-dominant epitopes of ACRBP and SYCP1 to develop a novel multi-epitope peptide vaccine for the treatment of mouse melanoma and triple-negative breast cancer. The results demonstrated that the vaccine could activate the immune system and exhibit anti-tumor effects.16,17 These findings suggest that utilizing ACRBP antigen peptides for tumor immunotherapy holds potential for future applications.

In previous studies, our research group identified the HLA-A *0201-restricted epitope peptide of ACRBP, ALLVLCYSI, and confirmed that CTLs induced by this peptide could specifically kill target cells in vitro.18 Based on this, the current study incubates ALLVLCYSI peptide with PBMCs from OCpatients to assess the patients’ T cell reactivity to the peptide. Additionally, animal experiments are conducted to observe the effect of CTLs induced by ALLVLCYSI peptide on OC cells. Furthermore, single-cell sequencing data of OC tissues and TCGA database were utilized to characterize the ACRBP-expressing tumor cells and to explorethe influence of ACRBP on the expression of MHC molecules and chemokines in tumor cells. This research lays the foundation for further exploration of the potential application of ACRBP-based tumor immunotherapy for OC.

Material and method

Peptide synthesis

The ACRBP epitope peptide ALLVLCYSI was synthesized by Dangang Biotechnology Co., Ltd., with a purity of over 95%. The synthetic peptide was dissolved in DMSO (Aladdin, China), and then diluted to a concentration of 1 mg/ml with sterile deionized water. The diluted peptide solution was stored at −80°C for future use.18

Animals and cell lines

Severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) mice (female, 5-6 weeks old) were obtained from Beijing Vital River Biotechnology Co., Ltd. and housed in a sterile environment at the Animal Center of Guangxi Medical University. All animal experimental protocols in this study were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Guangxi Medical University.

The human OC cell line OVCAR-3 was obtained from the Shanghai Cell Collection of the Chinese Academy of Science.

Preparation of PBMCs

The peripheral blood samples were collected from HLA-A2+ healthy donors. All experiments were conducted following the guidelines and regulations of the Ethical Review Committee of Guangxi Medical University and informed consent was obtained from all donors. PBMCswere isolated from blood samples through the employment of Ficoll/Hypaque density-gradient centrifugation technique.18

The peripheral blood samples were collected from OC patients who underwent surgery at the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University between November 2019 and January 2022. Patients who were HLA-A2 and ACRBP positive were selected by flowcytometry and ELISA methods.13,18 PBMCs of OC patients were isolated and cryopreserved in a solution of fetal bovine serum supplemented with 10%DMSO, then stored in a liquid nitrogen environment.

Enzyme-linked immunospot assay (ELISPOT)

After thawing PBMCs from OC patients (HLA-A2+, ACRBP+), the cell concentration was adjusted to 3 × 106 cells/mL using RPMI 1640 complete medium. The cells were cultured at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 12 hours. PBMCs were transferred into a 96-well ELISPOT kit (Dakewe, Beijing, China) to detect IFN-γ, with 3 × 105 cells per well. The experimental wells were treated with 10 μg/mL of the ACRBP peptide ALLVLCYSI, with negative control wells (containing the same amount of cell suspension as the experimental wells) and positive control wells (containing the same amount of cell suspension as the experimental wells and 10 μL of PHA), as well as blank control wells containing only medium. The cells were cultured for 20 hours in a 37°C, 5% CO2 incubator. After incubation, the cells were collected, and the number of IFN-γ secreting antigen-specific T cells was measured according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After drying the plate, the spots were counted using an ELISPOT counter. Each spot corresponds to one activated T cell that can secrete IFN-γ, referred to as a spot-forming cell (SFC). The antigen peptide-reactive T cells were considered positive if the SFC value in the experimental group minus 2 times the SFC value in the negative control group ≥0 .05%, and negative if the value was < 0.05%, with the result set to 0. The positive control (PHA-stimulated wells) SFC should be ≥ 500/3 × 105 cells.

Induction of peptide-specific CTL in vitro

CD14+cells and CD8+T lymphocytes were isolated from healthy volunteer PBMCs via magnetic bead separation. The CD14+ cells (i.e., monocytes) were cultured with rhGM-CSF (100 ng/mL) and rhIL-4 (50 ng/mL) for 5 days to induce differentiation into immature dendritic cells, followed by the addition of rhTNF-α (50 ng/mL) and continued culture for 3 more days to induce DC maturation. After 4–5 hours of incubation with 25 μg/mL ofthe ALLVLCYSI peptide, the mature DCs were mixed with autologous CD8+T lymphocytes (DC:T = 1:10) and cultured. On the second day of culture, IL-2 (final concentration of 20ng/mL) was added, and the cells were observed and gently mixed daily. On day 7 and day 12, antigen peptide-loaded DCs were added at the same ratio for stimulation. During the culture period, the medium was half-changed every 2–3 days, and IL-2 was replenished. On day 13, CD3 and CD28 antibodies and IL-2 were added, and the cells were cultured for an additional 2 days before being collected as the ALLVLCYSI peptide-induced CTLs (Pep-CTLs). A control group without peptide, referred to as NP-CTL, was also included. The specific procedure is as described in our previous report.18

Tumor implantation and CTL cell transfer

1 × 107 OVCAR-3 human OC cells in the logarithmic growth phase were subcutaneously inoculated into the intoaxillary region of SCID mice to observe and record tumor formation. When the tumor volume reached approximately 80 mm3, the tumor-bearing SCID mice were randomly divided into three groups: Pep-CTL group, NP-CTL group, and control group (PBS group). Based on the experimental grouping, the corresponding CTL cells or PBS were injected into the tail vein of the mice every 7 days, with each injection containing 3 × 107 cells, for a total of three injections (Figure 3A). The mice were weighed and their tumor size was measured and recorded every 2–3 days. Seven days after the third injection, the mice were sacrificed by blood collection via the eyeball, followed by the isolation of mouse serum and dissection of major organs and tumors. The tumor tissues were weighed and the tumor inhibition rate was calculated as [(average tumor weight of control group – average tumor weight of treatment group)/average tumor weight of control group]. The tissues were fixed and prepared into paraffin sections.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Tissue paraffin sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated and microwave-heated in a 10 mmol/L citrate buffer (pH 6.0) by microwave heating for 15 minutes. Endogenous peroxidase activity blocked using 10% hydrogen peroxide for 10 minutes. After a30-minute incubation with 5% BSA at room temperature, the sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with human CD34/Ki67/CD8/Granzyme Bantibodies (1:200, Long Island Biotech, China). Following this, horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated anti-human IgG (Long Island Biotech, China) was applied for 30 minutes at room temperature. Detection was performed using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) (Maixin Bio, China), and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin.19

The results were observed under a microscope. For each tumor tissue sample, 3 high-power fields (×200 magnification) were randomly selected. The proportion of positive cells was quantified via AipathWell™ Pathological Image Analysis Software (v3.0, Servicebio) using eitherthe positive cell percentage (number of positive cells/total number of cells × 100%) or the density of positive cells (number of positive cells/area of the tissue to be tested). Finally, the average positive cell rate or average positive cell density of each group was used to prepare the bar charts and perform the statistical analysis.

TUNEL assay

Tissue paraffin sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, subjected to antigen retrieval, and endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked as described in the immunohistochemistry method. Sections were incubated with 50μL Equilibration Buffer at room temperature for 30 minutes. After buffer removal, 56 μL of TdT buffer was added, and the sections were incubated at 37°C for 2 hours. Following PBS washing, 100 μL of Streptavidin-HRP reagent was applied for 30 minutes at 37°C. After PBS washing, 100 μL of freshly prepared DAB was added, and the reaction was stopped with water after color development.

The results were observed under a microscope. For each tumor tissue sample, 3 high-power fields (×200) were randomly selected. The proportion of positive cells was quantified via AipathWell™ Pathological Image Analysis Software (v3.0, Servicebio) using positive cell percentage (number of positive cells/total number of cells × 100%) Finally, bar graphs were plotted based on the average number of positive cell percentage in each group, and statistical analyses were performed.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Mouse serum from each group was used to detect the levels of cytokines TNF-α or IFN-γ using ELISA kits (Cloud-Clone Corporation, Wuhan, China). For each well, 50 μL serum samples or different concentrations of standards were added, followed by 100 μL of HRP-conjugated antibody. After incubation at 37°C for 60 minutes, the wells were washed five times. Then, 50 μL of substrate A and 50 μL of substrate B were added and incubated for 15 minutes at 37°C. After that, 50 μL of stop solution was added, and the absorbance at 450 nm (OD value) was measured within 15 minutes. A standard curve was drawn based on the concentration of the standards and OD values, and the TNF-α or IFN-γ concentration in the serum was calculated.18,20

Dataset and the processing of the data

To characterize ACRBP-expressing tumor cells at single-cell resolution, corresponding transcriptomic data from Gene Expression Omnibus dataset (GSE211956) was used. This dataset includes primary tumors from 8 patients with high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC).21

The single cell RNA-seq was processed and analyzed with Seurat (Ver 4.2.0). First, each sample was converted into a Seurat object utilizing the function of CreateSeuratObject. Genes were excluded if they were expressed in less than 3 cells. Low-quality cells were filtered out when cells contain fewer than 200 genes, more than 6000 genes and more than 15% mitochondrial unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) counts. Expression levels were normalized employing the following algorithm: expression level = log [1 + (UMIA ÷ UMITotal) × 10000]. After variable genes were identified, “anchor-based” integration was used to reduce the differences caused by experimental batches and donors, etc. The integrative data were then scaled and subjected to principal component analysis. Eleven clusters were generated subsequently by finding clusters ‘FindClusters(resolution = 0.5)’ and finding neighbors ‘FindNeighbors (dims = 1:30).’ The clusters include 3 major types of cells, namely tumor cells, immune cells (myeloid cells and lymphocytes), and stromal cells.

Cell clustering

Cells in single cell sequencing data were grouped into 11 clusters and visualized using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) construction. To identify the above cell types, mean expressions of the acknowledged markers21,22 were calculated for each cluster using ‘FindAllMarkers’ function in Seurat. The used markers are as follows: 1) tumor cell: EPCAM, CLDN3, KLK7;2) lymphocyte:CD79A, DERL3, MZB1, IGHG1, IGLC3, IGLC2, IGHG3, IGKC, CD3D, CD3E, CD8A, NKG7, CD2; 3) myeloid cell: CD68, LYZ, AIF1, CD14, C1QA, S100A9, S100A8, C1QB, FCGR1A, CSF1R, AIF1, FCER1G, CD163, C1QC, CCL3, FCGR3A, CD36; 4) fibroblast: CD68，LYZ, CD14，C1QA, SPP1, S100A9, S100A8, C1Q8，CSF1R, AIF1, FCER1G, CD163, C1QC, CCL3，FCGR3A, CD36; 5) mesothelium: KRT19, KRT18, MSLN, UPK3B. The cluster identity was determined at an expression cutoff greater than 0.75. Red blood cells (cluster 2) and a doublet cluster (cluster 9) that simultaneously expressed cancer cells and T cells were removed from further analysis. The remaining clusters were categorized into 3 major types: stromal cells (clusters 7, 1 and 8), immune cells (cluster 3 and cluster 5), and tumor cells (clusters 0, 4, 6, and 10). Correlation analysis based on gene expression programs was used to confirm the results of clustering. For clusters that belong to the same category (eg., cluster 1 and 8) or are similar in terms of origin (eg., mesothelium and tumor cells)23 are grouped together, Such observation is considered to confirm the results.

Fibroblasts were further divided into two subtypesbased on the known subtype markers of cancer-associated fibroblast (CAF).24 Clusters 1 and 8 were respectively defined as cancer-associated myofibroblasts (CAFmyo) and endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition CAFs (CAFEndMT). CAFEndMT not only expressed high levels of CAFmyo markers (such as DCN, TAGLN, ACTA2, MYLK, MYH11) but also showed elevated expression of canonical markers of endothelial cell (such as CLDN5, VWF, RAMP2, SLC9A3R2, RGS5), suggesting that CAFEndMT may be involved in the transition process from CAFmyo to endothelial cells, which is consistent with the previous reports.24,25

Determination of cell cycle and expressions of MHCs and chemokines

To assess the phase of cell cycle for ACRBP-expressing tumor subcluster, the ‘CellCycleScoring’ function in the Seurat package was used with the default parameters. Enrichment scores for phases S and G2M were calculated based on the average expressions of canonical marker gene sets in different subclusters. Cell cycle phases were then assigned to the subclusters according to the scores.26

For study of the average expressions of MHCs and chemokines for each cell type, gene sets of above molecules proposed by Hornburg et al22 were applied for this assay. For MHCs, the gene set included the following: HLA-A, HLA-B, HLA-C, HLA-E, HLA-G, HLA-DQA1, HLA-DMA, HLA-DRB1, HLA-DPA1, HLA-DQB1, HLA-DOB. For chemokines, the gene collection was supplemented as Table S1. The average expressions of the 2 gene sets in each cell type were calculated by ‘AddModuleScore’ function27in Seurat using the default settings. The Wilcoxon rank sum test was adopted for statistical analysis.

Differential expression analysis and functional enrichment analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) analysis wasperformed using the “FindAllMarkers” function of Seurat. Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG)functional enrichment analysis of each cluster were implemented using a web server DAIVID28 by entering the DEGs as input gene list. The harvested GO terms and KEGG pathways with an adjusted P value corrected by Benjamini-Hochberg algorithmsmaller than 0.05 were regarded as significant. The DEGs and top terms of annotated functions for each subcluster were visualized as volcano plot and bar chart respectively using package of ggplot2 (Ver 3.4.4).

Analysis of bulk RNA sequencing data

Bulk RNA sequencing data containing 308 ovarian cancer patients in the Cancer Genome Atlas Program (TCGA) was retrieved from UCSC xena TCGA data hub. The patient samples were arranged in increasing order of the ACRBP expression level. And the top 15% and the bottom 15% of patients were chosen as low-ACRBP expressing group and high-ACRBP expressing group respectively. We compare the expressional differences of each gene within the aforementioned MHC and chemokine gene sets between the 2 groups by the Wilcoxon rank sum test. The results are presented using violin plots by package of ggplot2.

Spearman correlation analysis

Correlation analysis was conducted to explore the potential association between immunological responses and chemotherapy efficacy. Subclusters are classified into 2 groups according to therapeutic response of patients. The ones derived from patients with good or partial response were combined as a group, and the group was assigned a value of 1. By contrast, the ones originated from patients with poor response were designated as the other group and the group was assigned a value of 0. The Spearman correlation test was performed with R software (Ver. 4.1.3). And the result was shown by scatter plot with package of ggplot2 (Ver 3.4.4).

Statistic analysis

The data were recorded in a database using Excel. Continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (x‾ ± s). Comparison among multiple sample means was conducted using analysis of variance (ANOVA). P-values < 0.05 were defined as significant.

Results

Genotype and expression of HLA-A2 were confirmed for OC cell line

The HLA-A genotype of OVCAR-3 cells was determined by extracting DNA for HLA-A gene typing, and the expression of HLA-A2 molecules on the cell surface was assessed by flowcytometry, with the specific steps detailed in our previous report.18 The results confirmed that the HLA-A alleles in the OVCAR-3 cell line were A02:01:01:01 and A29:02:01:01, and the HLA-A2 molecule expression on the cell surface was positive at a rate of 31.5%, indicating that the cells were HLA-A *0201 positive (Figure 1). ALLVLCYSI peptide can induced T-cell responses in OC patients

To investigate whether the HLA-A *0201-restricted epitope peptide ALLVLCYSI can induce T-cell immune responses in OC patients, we selected 31 OC patients who were HLA-A2 positive and obtained their PBMCs. After adding the ALLVLCYSI peptide to the PBMCs, we used the ELISPOT assay to measure the number of T cells secreting IFN-γ in the PBMCs of OC patients (i.e., the number of spot-forming cells, SFC). The response was defined as positive if the SFC value of the experimental group minus 2 times the SFC value of the negative control group was ≥ 0.05%. The results (Figure 2) showed that among the 31 OC patients’ PBMCs, the positive response rate for T cells specific to the ALLVLCYSI peptide was 18.91% (7/31). The ALLVLCYSI peptide was shown to induce T cell activation in the PBMC of some OC patients, producing a cellular immune response. Pep-CTL treatment in tumor-bearing mice can reduce tumor volume

First, we observed the overall effect of adoptive CTL immunotherapy on tumors in tumor-bearing mice. According to the experimental grouping, mice were intravenously inoculated with the corresponding CTL or PBS via the tail vein, and the adoptive immunotherapy process is shown in Figure 3A. Tumor volumes were measured, and tumor growth curves were plotted (Figure 3B). Before treatment (0-14 days), there was no significant difference in tumor volume growth among the groups. After treatment, tumor volumes continued to increase in all groups, but the growth rate of tumors in the Pep-CTL treatment group was significantly slower than in the other groups. Specifically, on day 21 after treatment (35 days after tumor inoculation), the tumor volume in the Pep-CTL group was smaller than that in the NP-CTL group (P < .05) and the PBS group (P < .01), with statistically significant differences. On day 21 after treatment, tumors were harvested and weighed, with results shown in Figure 3C. The average tumor weight in the Pep-CTL group was lower than that in the NP-CTL group and the PBS group, with a statistically significant difference compared to the PBS group (P < .01). Compared to the PBS group, the tumor inhibition rate in the NP-CTL group was 35.15%, while that in the Pep-CTL group was 49.75%. Compared to the NP-CTL group, the tumor inhibition rate in the Pep-CTL group was 22.51%. These results confirm that CTLs induced by the ALLVLCYSI peptide can produce specific anti-tumor effects in vivo. Pep-CTL treatment in tumor-bearing micecan affect angiogenesis in tumor

Since angiogenesis is one of the characteristics for evaluating tumor growth, we assessed the vascular density of tumor tissues in tumor-bearing mice by detecting the expression of CD34 in tumor tissues using IHC. Immunohistochemical brown staining was used to indicate vascular endothelial cells expressing CD34. A larger brown-stained area suggested a greater number of blood vessels, which is more conducive to tumor growth. As demonstrated in Figure 3D, the Pep-CTL group exhibited significantly reduced tumor micro-vessel density, evidenced by the lowest proportion of CD34+ cells (3.22% ± 2.17%) among all treatment groups (P < .05). These results indicate that Pep-CTL can inhibit angiogenesis in tumor tissues of tumor-bearing mice.

Pep-CTL can infiltrate tumor tissues in tumor-bearing mice

After confirming that Pep-CTL inhibits tumor angiogenesis, whether it can effectively attack tumor cells in vivo also requires assessment of CTL infiltration into the tumor tissue and its functional activity. To this end, IHC was employed to detect the content of CD8+ cells and GZMB-positive cells in tumor tissues, respectively. The results (Figure 3D) showed that in the Pep-CTL treatment group, the average density of infiltrating CD8+T cells (CTL) in tumor tissues was 1.98 ± 0.47 cells per 104 pixels, and with a relatively high level of GZMB expression (4.09 ± 1.0 cells per 104 pixels), which was significantly higher than that in the NP-CTL group. In contrast,no infiltrating T lymphocytes were detected in the tumor tissues of the PBS group. This indicates that after intravenous injection of Pep-CTL, some CTLs infiltrate into the tumor tissues of mice and exert cytotoxic effects on tumor cells.

Pep-CTL treatment in tumor-bearing mice can affect the proliferation or apoptosis of tumor cells

CTL infiltration is a prerequisite for exerting direct antitumor effects, while the ultimate cytotoxic function of CTLs must be reflected in their impact on tumor cell proliferation and apoptosis. Therefore, IHC was used to examine the expression of Ki67 protein, a tumor proliferation marker, in tumor cells. The results shown in Figure 3D indicate that the expression of Ki67 in the Pep-CTL group was statistically lower than that in the NP-CTL group (P < .05) and the PBS group (P < .01) respectively. Additionally, the TUNEL assay results in Figure 3E demonstrated that the number of apoptotic cells in the tumor tissues of the Pep-CTL group was higher than in the NP-CTL group (P < .01) and the PBS group (P < .01), with statistically significant differences. These findings suggest that Pep-CTL exerts its antitumor effects by inhibiting tumor growth and promoting tumor cell apoptosis in tumor-bearing mice.

TNF-α and IFN-γ levels were elevated in the serum of tumor-bearing mice

Serum from tumor-bearing mice was collected 21 days after CTL treatment, and the levels of TNF-α and IFN-γ in the serum were measured by ELISA. The results, as shown in Figure 4, indicated that the IFN-γ level in the serum of tumor-bearing mice treated with Pep-CTL was the highest, with a statistically significant difference compared to the PBS group (P < .01). The TNF-α level was elevated, but no statistically significant differences were observed between the groups. OC with high ACRBP expression exhibits significant proliferative characteristics and reduced response of chemotherapy

Although the tumor inhibition rate of Pep-CTL against subcutaneous neoplasms in vivo was 49.75% (significantly higher than controls), the overall immune response efficiency remained below 50%. These findings imply the presence of immunosuppressive factors compromising CTL-mediated tumor control in vivo.

To investigate the impact of ACRBP expression on the tumor-killing efficacy of CTLs, we performed scRNA-seqon primary tumor cells from 8 patients with high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC), aiming to characterize the molecular features of ACRBP-positive tumor cells. Among the eight patients, poor chemotherapy response scores were observed in individuals 1, 7, and 8; good responses in individuals 2, 3, and 5; and partial sensitivity in patients 4 and 6.

Using the established marker genes (see Methods), we first classified the tumor cells and defined them as cell clusters 0, 4, 6, and 10 (Figure 5A,B). These tumor cells were derived from solid tumors, including ovarian cancer (OC), which are mainly epithelial malignancies.23 Overall, these malignant cell clusters showed significant transcriptional similarity (Figure 5C) and were correlated with mesothelial cells (cluster 7) (Figure S1A). Meanwhile, no significant association was found between the tumor cell clusters (0, 4, 6, 10) and the patient origin (Figure S1B). Pathway enrichment analysis revealed the unique oncogenic characteristics of each cell cluster. In cluster 0, the bicellular tight junction pathway was significantly enriched (Figure S1B), and this mechanism has previously been confirmed to be associated with the induction of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in hepatocellular carcinoma.29 Cluster 6 and cluster 10 were respectively associated with the PI3K – Akt signaling pathway and the AP − 1 transcription factor, both of which promote tumorigenesis (Figure S1B),30,31 indicating that they play specific oncogenic roles in the process of tumor development.

Notably, cluster 4 exhibited distinct characteristics compared with other tumor cell clusters. Cluster 4 was not only related to cancer-associated pathways but also closely associated with the cell cycle pathway (Figure S1B). Moreover, this cluster highly expressed ACRBP (Figure 5D) and the previously reported proliferation-related DNA repair genes (Figure S1C),21 suggesting its stronger proliferative ability. This characteristic of high ACRBP expression in cluster 4 forms a sharp contrast with clusters 0, 6, and 10 in terms of oncogenic signals and proliferative features. In terms of immune-related phenotypes such as T-cell immune activation and chemoresistance, sub-clustering needs to be further carried out.

The subclustering yielded 10 cell populations (Figure 5E). Cell populations with similar transcription patterns were exhibited by heatmap (Figure 5F). Interestingly, subclusters sharing similar transcriptional profiles showed physical proximity in the UMAP visualization (Figure 5E). Resistant cancer cells often display an accelerated cell cycle and high proliferation capacity, we consequently analyze the distribution of the cell cycle in the tumor cell populations. Subclusters 0, 3, 5, 7, and 9 each contained over 40% G2/M-phase cells (Figure S1D), consistent with the functional enrichment of cell cycle pathways in cluster 4 (Figure S1B). Notably, subcluster 3 exhibited high ACRBP expression (Figure 5G). Functional enrichment analysis revealed that subclusters 0 and 3 upregulated cell cycle and transcriptional regulation pathways (G2/M-phase signatures, Figure 5H/J), while subcluster 2 was enriched in ribosome-related functions (G1-phase features, Figure 5H and S1E). Remarkably,subclusters 0, 2, and 3 were exclusively derived from chemotherapy non-responders. Collectively, these results are in line with our conventional understanding of the characteristics and behaviors of resistant cancer cells in terms of cell cycle distribution and functional enrichment. Compared to subcluster 0, 2 and 3,subcluster 6 comprised 34% of cells from chemotherapy non-responders and 66% of cells from responders, therefore it was classified as chemotherapy-sensitive group (Figure 5I). Despite displaying cell cycle markers (Figure 5H), subclusters 5, 7, 9 (high G2/M scores) and 4, 6 (high G1 scores) were functionally enriched in T-cell immune activation pathways, such as expression of major histocompatibility complex (MHC), and antigen presentation (Figures 5H/J and S1F). This data suggests that cell cycle-related genes may participate in diverse functions, with no single pathway prevailing the enrichment profile. Strikingly, subclusters 4, 5, 6, 7, and 9 originated from chemotherapy-sensitive patients (good/partial responders) and showed upregulated T-cell activation pathways. However, the significantly enriched pathways of subclusters 1 and 8 were not related to the immune response, even though the cells in these subclusters are all from partial responder (patient 4) (Figure S1G). Spearman correlation analysis was performed and the result demonstrated a significant positive association between immune response intensity of tumor cells and clinical outcomes (Spearman r = 0.6547, P = .0399; Figure 5K). Given that ACRBP is significantly expressed in subcluster 3, these data collectively indicate that high ACRBP expression in tumor cells is associated with tumor cell proliferation, lack of T-cell immune activation, and chemoresistance – findings that are consistent with the results of the Spearman analysis.

ACRBP-expressing tumor cells exhibit downregulation of MHC and chemokines

Tumor cells expressing ACRBP displayed deficient T-cell immune activation. Since MHC expression on tumor cells critically impact their recognition by Tcells, we investigated whether ACRBP influences MHC levels by performing differential gene analysis on subclusters 0, 2, and 3 that collectively lacked T-cell immune activation. Subclusters 0 and 3 showed significant suppression of HLA expression, accompanied by downregulated immune responses (Figures 6A,B). For subcluster 2, reduced expressions of IGHG1 and IGHG4 impaired complement activation and antigen-antibody binding (Figure 6C). As these subclusters originated exclusively from chemotherapy non-responders, these findings aligned with the data of Spearman correlation analyses. ACRBP-expressing tumor cells downregulated expressions of MHC and chemokine.

To further investigate MHC expression differences between ACRBP expressing subcluster 3 and chemotherapy-resistant (subclusters 0, 2) or partially sensitive (subcluster 6) subclusters, we analyzed average MHC expression across these cells. The results revealed that subcluster 3 exhibited significantly lower MHC expression compared to subclusters 0, 2, and 6. This suppression persisted when compared to the average expression of the remaining 9 subclusters (Figure 6D, left panel) and in direct pairwise comparisons (Figure S2A). Given that downregulation of chemokine ligands in tumor cells promotes immune evasion, chemokine expression across subclusters was analyzed parallelly. ACRBP expressing subcluster 3 showed reduced chemokine ligand expression, consistent with the observed MHC suppression patterns (Figure 6D, right panel, Figure S2B).

To validate these findings, we re-clustered all tumor clusters (0, 4, 6, 10), generating 11 new subclusters (Figure S3A). Subcluster 5, which highly expressed ACRBP and originated from patient 8 (Figures S3B,C), was enriched in G2/M-phase cells (Figure S3D). The subcluster 5 exhibited significantly lower MHC and chemokine ligand expression compared to other subclusters respectively, including chemotherapy-resistant subclusters 0, 2, and 4 (derived from non-responders; Figures S3E,H). Taken together, these data aligned with the previous research.

To validate our findings in a larger cohort, we analyzed bulk RNA-seq data from 308 ovarian cancer patients obtained from TCGA. High-ACRBP expressing tumors displayed downregulated MHC class I molecules (e.g., HLA-B, HLA-G, HLA-F, and HLA-H; Figure 6E), reduced expression of 10 chemokines (Figure 6F) and antigen presentation-related genes (TAP1, CANX; Figure S4) compared to low-ACRBP expressing tumors.

In summary, both single-cell and TCGA data consistently show that high ACRBP expression may be associated with the downregulated expression of MHC and chemokines and correlate with immune evasion. These findings preliminarily clarify the impact that ACRBP has on the efficacy of CTL therapy in vivo, and also provide theoretical support for optimizing the in vivo targeting efficiency of Pep-CTL.

Discussion

CTAs are a class of TAA restrictedly expressed in male germ cells and various tumor tissues, with minimal expression in other normal adult tissues. Consequently, tumor immunotherapy targeting CTAs has garnered significant research interest.32 To date, 38 CTA molecules have been identified as associated with OC, including ACRBP.33 Studies have indicated that ACRBP is highly expressed in OC tissues and is closely related to cancer cell proliferation, chemosensitivity, tumor staging, and prognosis. Additionally, high ACRBP expression in OC tissues can induce adaptive immune responses in some patients.12–14 Recent advances have been made in targeting ACRBP for tumor immunotherapy,16–19 suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target in OC.

Among various tumor immunotherapy strategies, CTL-based immunotherapy remains a focus in adoptive cell immunotherapy.34 Dendritic cell-cytotoxic T lymphocyte (DC-CTL) immunotherapy, a key approach, leverages DCs, the most potent antigen-presenting cells capable of initiating primary immune responses by presenting antigens to naive T cells. Numerous studies demonstrate the enhancement of DC antigen-presenting capacity and promotion of CTL antitumor effects through tumor antigen induction in vitro. DC-CTL immunotherapy has shown excellent safety and efficacy in treating various malignancies,improving immune function, inducing immune memory, and achieving durable antitumor effects.35,36 Among these, CTA peptide-pulsed DC-CTL therapy has also shown promise and broad application potential.37,38

In previous research, we demonstrated that DC-CTLs based on the ACRBP peptide ALLVLCYSI specifically kill tumor cells in vitro.18 However, further verification is needed to determine if this peptide effectively induces T-cell immune responses in OC patients. We co-cultured the ALLVLCYSI peptide with PBMCsfrom HLA-A2+ and ACRBP+OC patients, and assessed IFN-γ-secreting T cells using the ELISPOT assay. Results indicated that this antigenic peptide stimulates specific T-cell responses in some patients, with a positivity rate of 18.91%. Coupled with anti-ACRBP antibody detections in some OC patients,11,13 this suggests the immunogenicity of the ALLVLCYSI peptide, providing a basis for future tumor peptide vaccine studies. Nevertheless, some patients failed to activate CD8+ T cells post-stimulation or the number of activated T cells is low potentially due to factors including ACRBP expression levels, patient physiological status, and clinical characteristics (e.g., histopathological type, clinical stage, drug resistance, and prognosis), necessitating further investigation.

Concurrently, we infused ALLVLCYSI peptide-induced specific DC-CTL (Pep-CTL) into SCID mice bearing OVCAR-3 human OC cells (HLA-A0201+, ACRBP+), and evaluated Pep-CTL’s antitumor effects by examining tumor size, tumor tissue vascular density, cell proliferation and apoptosis, and cytokine levels (IFN-γ and TNF-α) in mouse serum. Compared to the control group, Pep-CTL enhanced antitumor effects against OC cells in mice, implying potential applications in solid tumor treatment. However, the specific tumor inhibition rate was only 22.51% after CTL adoptive therapy, with limited CTL infiltration in tumor tissues and low serum IFN-γ and TNF-α levels. This could be attributable to factors such as: (1) The tumors in mice were sizeable (approx. 80 mm3) at the time of CTL therapy, and the limited infused CTL numbers did not achieve a higher inhibition rate. IHC further confirmed the minimal CTL infiltration via tail vein injection, restricting antitumor effects. This accords with reports suggesting that adoptive immunotherapy is potent but necessitates abundant T cell injections, as only 1–2% infiltrate tumor tissues,39 with tumor-infiltrating T lymphocytes (TILs) positively correlating with antitumor effects and prognosis.40 Therefore, enhancing T cell infiltration is thus key for improving adoptive immunotherapy for solid tumors.41,42 (2) Tumor cells possess immune evasion mechanisms, such as downregulating ACRBP, MHC I molecules, chemokines, co-stimulatory molecules, and upregulating PD-L1.43 Our previous work revealed heterogeneous ACRBP expression in OC tumors,13 and this study confirmed low MHC I molecule expression (31.5% HLA-A2 expression in OVCAR-3 cells), contributing to ineffective antigen presentation and compromised CTL cytotoxicity. Therefore, strategies to enhance tumor antigen and MHC I molecule expression may bolster CTL efficacy. (3) The tumor microenvironment’s immunosuppressive factors, like FOXP3+ regulatory T cells, M2 macrophages, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and inhibitory cytokines (e.g., IL-10, TGF-β), suppress CTL activity, impeding tumor elimination.44–46

As mentioned earlier, although OVCAR-3 cells express ACRBP, only about 31.5% of cells express the HLA-A2 molecule. To investigate the expression of MHC molecules and chemokines in high ACRBP-expressing OC cells, we first analyzed single-cell RNA sequencing data from 8 OC tissues.Within the tumor cell populations, cluster 4, showing markedly high levels of ACRBP expression, was subdivided into 10 subclusters for characterization and uncovered the molecular heterogeneity. Notably, some subclusters (4, 5, 6, 7, and 9) showed significant enrichment of upregulated pathways related to cellular immune responses, and these subclusters were exclusively derived from patients with complete or partial responses to chemotherapy. Correlation analyses confirmed the positive association between these immune-active subclusters and favorable chemotherapy outcomes.However, two other subclusters (1 and 8), both originating from patient 4, lacked enrichment of immune-related pathways. Subcluster 1 exhibited functional enrichment in p53 signaling, while subcluster 8 showed significant association with ciliary movement. Platinum-based chemotherapeutic agents are known to induce DNA crosslinking and double-strand breaks,47 which activate the p53 pathway to either repair DNA damage or trigger apoptosis when damage is irreparable.22,47 This may explain why patient 4 achieved partial therapeutic response through enhanced p53 signaling despite lacking immune-mediated benefits. Subcluster 3, with the highest ACRBP expression among 10 subclusters, exhibited upregulated functions related to cell growth and proliferation but no immune-related activity. This suggests that high ACRBP expression in tumor cells might be associated with cellular proliferation and negative regulation of immune responses. Subsequent comparison of MHC and chemokine expression across the 10 subclusters revealed that subcluster 3 had the lowest expression levels of both molecules among all subgroups, implying a potential link between ACRBP and downregulation of MHC/chemokine expression. To verify these findings, we re-clustered all the tumor clusters (0, 4, 6, 10) into 11 new subclusters. Subcluster 5, derived from patient 8 (a poor chemotherapy responder), showed the highest ACRBP expression. Most cells in this subcluster were in G2/M phase and exhibited significantly lower MHC/chemokine expression compared to other subclusters.This data therefore confirmed the previous observations. Analysis of bulk sequencing data from 308 OC patients in TCGA additionally demonstrated that the high-ACRBP group had significantly reduced expression of MHC class I molecules, antigen presentation-related molecules (TAP1 and CANX), and chemokines compared to the low-ACRBP group. Notably, TAP1 downregulation has been reported as an immune evasion mechanism in other cancers.48 These research findings indicate the following: (1) High ACRBP expression in tumor cells may be associated with chemotherapy responsiveness and cell proliferation. It is therefore critically important to investigate the relationship between specific gene expression profiles and established tumor biomarkers in OC for advancing diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. Additionally, mutations in tumor biomarkers also play a central role in the diagnosis and treatment guidance of epithelial ovarian cancer. For instance, there is an established association between breast cancer-related gene (BRCA) mutations and response to platinum-based chemotherapy in ovarian cancer patients. Specifically, BRCA1/2 gene mutations can be used to assess the risk of onset; carriers of these mutations are sensitive to platinum-based chemotherapy, enabling accurate guidance of PARP inhibitor maintenance therapy and providing a basis for family genetic risk screening.44,49 In this study, the subpopulation with the highest ACRBP expression was derived from patients with poor chemotherapy efficacy. It can thus be inferred that if further in-depth investigations are conducted in the future to explore the association between ACRBP expression levels and the mutation status of other tumor biomarkers (such as BRCA1/2), as well as the correlations with chemotherapy sensitivity and clinical prognosis, it will provide an important basis for the precise formulation of personalized treatment strategies for ovarian cancer, with practical clinical guiding value. (2) ACRBP-high OC cells may evade immune surveillance through dual mechanisms: reduced antigen presentation to avoid T cell recognition and diminished chemokine production to limit T cell recruitment. This explains the limited T cell infiltration and suboptimal tumor suppression observed in ACRBP-targeted adoptive therapy in tumor-bearing mice.Based on these bioinformatic insights, our future research will focus on strategies to enhance T cell infiltration into tumor sites and improve the efficacy of adoptive immune cell therapy for solid tumors.

In recent years, the widespread application of immunotherapy in the field of oncology has offered new hope for treating of OC. However, the efficacy of most immunotherapeutic approaches in OC remains limited. For example, although immune checkpoint inhibitors have achieved breakthroughs in the treatment of various solid tumors, their single-agent or combination regimens have not improved the overall survival rate of patients with newly diagnosed, platinum-sensitive, or platinum-resistant ovarian cancer.44,50 This phenomenon may be related to the generally existing “immune-cold” tumor microenvironment in ovarian cancer. For instance, some ovarian cancer cells have low PD-L1 expression and low tumor mutational burden; some OC tissues lack T-cell infiltration and are heavily enriched with various immunosuppressive cells. These may be some of the reasons for the low sensitivity of OC to immune checkpoint inhibitors.44,51–54 Therefore, modifying the ovarian cancer microenvironment, such as increasing the infiltration of specific CD8+ T cells,54,55 may be closely related to patients’ prognosis, survival, and ability to respond to treatment. According to Galassi C,43 apart from perturbations of migration of CD8+T cells and inhibition of antigen recognition by CD8+ T cells, tumor cells also achieve immune evasion by other ways. For example, malignant cells produced immunosuppressive factors to dampen the activation and differentiation of T cells and promote T cell exhaustion. These immunosuppressive factors included cytokines and metabolites, such as vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA), transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGFB1), and adenosine. However, whether OC cells with high ACRBP expression also upregulated the expression of the above immunosuppressive factors to reshape the immunosuppressive microenvironment remains to be further analyzed. Based upon these mechanisms, future research on ACRBP epitope peptide-based DC-CTL adoptive immunotherapy could explore the following strategies to enhance therapeutic efficacy: (1) Co-administration of agents that enhance intratumoral chemokine production (e.g., CXCL9/10 agonists), anti-angiogenic drugs to normalize tumor vasculature, or T-cell recruiting agents to promote the infiltration of T lymphocytes into tumor tissues, reverse the “immune-cold” microenvironment, and enhance the effectiveness of adoptive immunotherapy.40,56–58 (2) Augmentation of tumor antigen presentation: pharmacological interventions to upregulate MHC class I molecules (e.g., IFN-γ analogs) and co-stimulatory signals (e.g., CD80/86 agonists) on tumor cells, enhancing antigen presentation capacity and subsequent CD8+ T cell activation/cytotoxicity.59–61 (3) Combining strategies such as promoting CD8+ T cell proliferation, reducing the production of immunosuppressive factors in tumor tissues, and blocking immune checkpoint molecules,thereby maintaining the functional activity and persistence of T cells.62,63

The integration of these multistrategies may synergistically amplify antitumor immunity and address current adoptive immunotherapy limitations to ultimately achieve durable remission in OC.
