Medication adherence and associated factors among chronic heart failure patients on follow-up in north Shewa public hospitals, Oromia region, Ethiopia
Authors: Dursa Hussein, Abyot Kebede Jima, Leta Adugna Geleta, Ketema Gashaw, Derara Girma, Seifu Mohammed Ibrahim, Meron Seyoum Lakew, Bedo Megersa Kumbe, Befekadu Tesfaye Oyato, Getu Siyum, Addis Adugna Senbate
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic heart failure affects approximately 26 million people globally. World Health Organization data show that only approximately half of chronically ill patients in developed countries adhere to recommended medication, with even lower rates in developing countries. Medication adherence is critical for managing chronic heart failure symptoms, delaying disease progression, and preventing hospitalizations. However, poor adherence increases rehospitalization, morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs.ObjectiveTo assess medication adherence and associated factors among chronic heart failure patients on follow-up at North Shewa Public Hospitals, Oromia Region, Ethiopia, in 2023.MethodsThis institutional-based cross-sectional study was conducted from March 1 to April 30, 2023, G.C. A total of 603 individuals were selected consecutively among those who underwent chronic OPD after being proportionally allocated to five hospitals in the zone. The data were collected using an interviewer-administered questionnaire and a medical chart review. The data were entered into Epi-data version 3.1 and then exported to SPSS version 26 for analysis. The multivariable logistic regression model included variables with a P value < 0.25 in the bivariate analysis. The degree of association was expressed using an adjusted odds ratio (AOR) with a 95% confidence interval (CI) at a P value < 0.05.ResultsAmong the 603 patients, 56% had optimal medication adherence, with a 95% CI of 52.1 to 60. Being able to read and write (AOR: 2.20; 95% CI: 1.34, 3.61), having a secondary education (AOR: 1.97; 95% CI: 1.06, 3.67), having community-based health insurance (AOR: 1.82; 95% CI: 1.22, 2.71), not having comorbidities (AOR: 1.82; 95% CI: 1.18, 2.52), taking several drugs < 2 (AOR: 2.11; 95% CI: 1.20, 2.45), not adding salt when cooking (AOR: 1.72; 95% CI: 1.20, 2.45), and asking a doctor or nurse without fear (AOR: 1.87; 95% CI: 1.03, 3.40) were factors associated with medication adherence among CHF patients.ConclusionThis study revealed that 56% of chronic heart failure patients had optimal medication adherence. Factors associated with higher adherence included higher education, community health insurance, lack of comorbidities, fewer medications, avoiding added salt, and comfortable communication with providers. Health professionals should provide education to strengthen medication adherence.Keywords: Chronic heart failure, Medication adherence, Associated factors, Ethiopia

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) include several disorders of the blood vessels and heart [1]. Cardiovascular diseases, including chronic heart failure (CHF), remain a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. Effective risk assessment and management are crucial for preventing adverse cardiovascular events [2]. CHF is a clinical syndrome characterized by symptoms such as shortness of breath, persistent coughing or wheezing, ankle swelling, and fatigue that may be accompanied by the following signs: jugular venous pressure, pulmonary crackles, increased heart rate, and peripheral oedema [3]. CHF manifests as inadequate cardiac output or abnormal filling pressure and is the leading cause of morbidity and mortality in developed and developing countries [4–6].

Heart failure (HF) is a dangerous cardiovascular illness, but its natural course can be altered with suitable medication. The amount to which patients take drugs as recommended by health care providers and as agreed upon in the patient’s treatment plan is known as medication adherence [7]. A person’s behaviour must meet the medication schedule prescribed by a medical professional, and adhering to chronic medications is one of the key factors in achieving treatment effectiveness [8, 9]. A patient is considered adherent if they take 80% of their prescribed medicine [10]. A study conducted in Pakistan revealed that approximately 80% of HCPs considered medication adherence to be extremely important in the clinical setting [11]. While effective management strategies are crucial for improving the prognosis of HF patients with reduced ejection fraction, medication adherence plays a pivotal role in achieving optimal outcomes [12].

The World Health Organization classifies the determinants of poor medication adherence into five categories: socioeconomic factors, factors associated with the health system, disease-related factors, therapy-related factors, and patient-related factors [13]. Medication adherence is influenced by a variety of factors, including patient-provider communication, patient knowledge and attitudes, and patients’ cognitive, psychological, and financial support. It depends on the individual’s willingness and ability to adhere to a medication schedule [8, 9]. Education level, residence, employment status, and health insurance possession showed significant associations with adherence [14], and nonadherence was significantly associated with the feeling that one would become more ill upon stopping medication use, the importance of accepting the reasons for taking medication, and the importance of following physicians’ instructions [15]. It also associates treatment attitudes, medication side effects, satisfaction with medication, and perception with adherence [16].

There are several studies on medication adherence and related topics, but most of them were performed in Western societies and achieved contradictory results [17, 18]. Patients with HF have poor adherence, which affects clinical outcomes, contributes to decreased physical function and a greater risk for death, and increases the unnecessary cost of patient care [11, 17–20]. Although many publications on CHF discuss risk factors, etiologies, diagnosis, and clinical manifestations, there are a limited number of studies on factors associated with medication adherence among CHF patients using the Morisk 8 scale [21].

Therefore, medication nonadherence is a major problem for HF patients because of reduced physical function, increased hospitalization, decreased treatment efficacy, increased morbidity and mortality. This study aimed to determine the level of medication adherence and factors associated with medication adherence among CHF patients followed up in the North Shewa Zone Public Hospital.

Methods and materials

Study design, period, and setting

An institutional-based cross-sectional study was conducted from March 1 to April 30, 2023, G.C. in the North Shewa zone, Oromia regional state, central Ethiopia. The study included CHF patients who were followed up at public hospitals, namely Selale University Compressive Specialized Hospital, Kuyu General Hospital, Dera Primary Hospital, Muk-Turi Primary Hospital, and Shano Primary Hospital, all located in the North Shewa zone of the Oromia Regional State. The capital city of the Zone, Fitche, is approximately 114 km away from Addis, the capital city of Ethiopia. The zone consists of 14 districts and 291 kebeles. As of 2020, the zone’s population was 1,639,587 people, including 362,841 women, living in 78,700 households. The study was conducted in an area with 5 hospitals, 63 health centres, and 267 health posts. The total number of CHF patients in the zone in 2023 was 679.

Population

All CHF patients who had attended a chronic care follow-up clinic at North Shewa Public Hospital were included in the population. The study population consisted of consecutively selected CHF patients who fulfilled the inclusion criteria.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

All CHF patients who were at least 18 years old or older received treatment for at least 6 months at North Shewa Public Hospitals for chronic follow-up during the data collection period.

Exclusion criteria

All CHF patients were seriously ill and unable to provide information.

Sample size determination

The sample size was determined using the single population proportion formula for the Epi Info STAT CALC cohort, version 7.2.4, based on the assumptions of a 95% confidence level (CL), a proportion of medication adherence compliance patients with CHF of 64.8%, and a 4% marginal error from the study conducted in the Oromia Region, West Ethiopia [19], among CHF patients. Additionally, considering a 10% nonresponse, the sample size was 603.

Sampling procedure

The sample size was proportionally allocated to five [5] hospitals in the zone based on the number of heart failure patients. Patients were selected from among patients who had visited the chronic OPD consecutively.

Data collection procedures (tools, techniques and personnel)

Data were collected by using a structured questionnaire and interviewing study subjects by modifying the tools of previous studies and by reviewing the literature of similar studies (23, 33, 56). A card review was performed to obtain clinical information about the patients, such as the NYHA class, the drug they consumed, and the period of hospitalization. The questionnaire had four parts: sociodemographic characteristics, clinical characteristics of heart failure, self-care behaviours and medication adherence. Ten BSc nurses collected the data by assigning 2 data collectors per hospital who were trained for four days on how to fill in the data and handle the documents following the objective of the study. Continuous monitoring and supervision were performed during data collection by the senior MSc nurse.

Study variables

Dependent variable

Medication adherence.

Independent variables

Demographic status

Age, sex, marital status, occupation, level of education, place of residence, wealth index, and community health insurance.

Clinical variables

Clinical symptoms of chronic heart failure, New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional classification, comorbidity, weight gain, and treatment received.

Self-care behaviour adherence

Follow status, therapeutic communication, smoking status, salt-free diet, alcohol use.

Follow-up status

We determined follow-up status by reviewing patient records and conducting interviews.

Therapeutic communication

We assessed therapeutic communication through patient-provider interviews and observation of communication patterns.

Smoking status

Smoking status was assessed through self-reported questionnaires.

Salt-free diet

We measured adherence to a salt-free diet using a validated food frequency questionnaire.

Alcohol use

Alcohol consumption was assessed through self-reported questionnaires.

Operational definition and definition of terms

Medication adherence

Medication-taking behaviours were measured by the MMAS-8, a self-reported scale and associated factor [18, 22, 24–28].

Optimal adherence

a medication adherence of the MMAS scale with a medication adherence score of 6 and greater than the 6 cut-off points on the MMAS-8; [18, 22, 24, 26–28].

Poor medication adherence

was defined as medication adherence on the MAAS scale with a medication adherence < 6 of the cut-off point on the MMAS-8 [18, 22, 24, 26, 27].

Self-care behaviour adherence

Self-care involves behaviours such as taking medications, monitoring and interpreting symptoms, keeping appointments, and contacting healthcare providers when needed [19].

Wealth index

The best agreement with the full wealth index minimum kappa statistic of 0.75 is based on the assumption that an underlying continuum of economic status exists that is related to the wealth of a household [29, 30].

Data quality assurance

The questionnaire was pretested on 5% of the sample of CHF patients who were followed up at Chanco Primary Hospital. The questionnaire was modified to enhance the consistency of understanding by the respondents as well as by the data collector. The data collectors were trained by the principal investigator about the general purpose of the study and the data collection procedures. The structured questionnaire was prepared first in English and then translated into Afan Oromo and Amharic by language experts. Finally, the data were checked for completeness before being entered into computer software for analysis.

Data processing and statistical analysis

Each questionnaire was reviewed for completeness and coded after data collection and data entry into Epi-Data version 3.1. The data were then exported to SPSS version 26 for editing, cleaning for inconsistencies, checking for missing values, and finally analyzing the data. Descriptive statistics, such as frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation, were calculated. Binary logistic regression was performed to assess the crude relationship between the independent variables and the dependent variable. All variables with a p-value < 0.25 were considered candidates for multivariate logistic regression to control for possible confounding effects [31].

To determine the independent influence of each variable on the outcome variable, multivariable logistic regression was used. Multicollinearity was checked using variance inflation factors (VIFs) and tolerance tests, with VIFs less than 5 and tolerance tests less than 1 serving as cut-off points for diagnosing multicollinearity. Model fitness was assessed using the Hosmer and Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, with the results indicating a good fit (p-value = 0.8). The wealth index was analyzed using principal component analysis (PCA), resulting in the creation of three components through varimax rotation and eigenvalues greater than 1. The model explained 72.5% of the total variance. The final results of the associations are presented as adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and a p-value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved, and an ethical approval letter was provided by the Ethical Review Board of Selale University (reference number IRB/778/15). The study was performed according to the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki on medical research. Written informed consent was obtained from every study subject before the data collection. All the information collected from the study participants was handled confidentially by omitting their identification.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of participants

In a study of heart failure patients, 603 participants participated, for a 100% response rate. The average age of the respondents was 52.65 years (SD = 15.47). Among them, 321 (53.2%) were female. Of the total respondents, 323 (53.6%) were unemployed, and 239 (39.6%) had no formal education. Over half of the participants (55.5%) lived in urban areas, and 308 (51.1%) were married. Regarding health insurance, 383 (63.5%) had no community-based coverage. Additionally, 241 (40%) of the participants were classified as poor (Table 1).

Clinical factors of chronic heart failure

In this study, among the 603 respondents, 510 (84.6%) mentioned experiencing signs and symptoms of heart failure. A majority of them (80.1%) were aware of how these symptoms affected their daily lives. The most commonly reported symptom was shortness of breath (50.6%), followed by persistent coughing (44.9%). Many participants did not experience weight gain (58.7%), ankle swelling (51.6%), or fatigue after minor exertion (77.8%). Regarding medication adherence, 503 (83.4%) reported taking their prescribed medication regularly. The most common comorbidity was hypertension (44.6%), and the majority of participants (80.8%) were taking fewer than two medications. Diuretics were taken by 421 (69.8%) of the respondents, and a significant proportion (53.7%) had stage III heart failure (Table 2).

Self-care behaviours factors

Among the study subjects, 380 (63%) reported not adding salt to their food while cooking, and 133 (22.1%) followed a salt-free diet when eating out. The majority of participants (80.9%) were nonsmokers, and 361 (59.9%) did not consume alcohol. Of the respondents, 184 (75.7%) believed that drinking alcohol hurts heart failure. Many participants (62%) had discussions about their illness with family members, and 333 (55.2%) visited a health facility after these discussions. A significant percentage (69%) would have followed their appointment schedule as instructed. Open communication with healthcare providers was reported by 443 (73.5%) participants, and 544 (90.2%) participants felt comfortable asking questions. Regarding pharmacy access, 343 (56.7%) reported having access at any time, and 356 (59%) found the instructions given by pharmacists to be clear (Table 3).

Medication adherence

According to the results of this study, more than half of the study participants, 337 (56%), had optimal medication adherence (95% CI: 52.1, 60), while 266 (44%) had poor medication adherence (95% CI: 40, 48) (Fig. 1). Of the total study subjects, 308 (51.1%) participants forgot to take their medication sometimes, and 150 (24.9%) missed it without reason for more than two weeks. Two hundred sixty (43.1%) participants sometimes forgot to bring their medication when they travelled from home, 168 (27.3%) stopped taking their medication without telling a doctor when they felt it was worse for them, and 213 (35.3%) stopped taking their medication when they felt better. Two-thirds of the respondents (73%) took all of their medication yesterday. Medication administration was inconvenient for 289 (47.3%) patients, and 285 (47.3%) patients sometimes forgot to take all their medication (Table 4).

Factors associated with medication adherence

Binary logistic regression was conducted to identify the associations of the independent variables with the dependent variables. Thirteen variables were significantly associated with medication adherence at P < 0.25. The variables were education status, occupational status, community-based health insurance, mentioned signs and symptoms, signs of fatigue after minor exertion, comorbidity, number of drugs, not adding salt to food when cooking, drinking alcohol, a subsequent appointment schedule, open communication with a health care provider, feelings about asking a doctor or nurse, and a wealth index, which were associated with medication adherence among chronic heart failure patients according to bivariate analysis.

To adjust for potential confounders, those variables that were significant in the bivariate analysis were entered into the multivariate logistic regression. The results revealed that educational status, community-based health insurance, comorbidity status, number of drugs, not adding salt to food when cooking, and asking a doctor or nurse without fear were factors associated with medication adherence among CHF patients (p < 0.05).

Participants who had completed secondary education were 1.97 times more likely to have medication adherence than those who had no formal education (AOR: 1.97; 95% CI: 1.06, 3.67). Respondents who could read and write were 2.20 times more likely to have medication adherence than those who had no formal education (AOR: 2.20; 95% CI: 1.34, 3.61). Respondents who had community-based health insurance were 1.82 times more likely to have medication adherence than those who had no community-based health insurance (AOR: 1.82; 95% CI: 1.22, 2.71).

Respondents who had no comorbidities were 1.72 times more likely to have medication adherence than patients with comorbidities (AOR: 1.72; 95% CI: 1.18, 2.52). Participants who had taken several drugs ≤ 2 were 2.11 times more likely to have medication adherence than participants who had taken several drugs > 2 (AOR: 2.11; 95% CI: 1.35, 3.31). Participants who did not add salt when cooking were 1.72 times more likely to have medication adherence than those who added salt to food when cooking (AOR: 1.72; 95% CI: 1.20, 2.45). Respondents who had asked a doctor or nurse about it without fear were 1.87 times more likely to have medication adherence than those who had asked questions without fear (AOR: 1.87; 95% CI: 1.03, 3.40) (Table 5).

Discussion

This study assessed medication adherence and associated factors among CHF patients in the North Shewa Zone Public Hospital in 2023. In this study, the optimal medication adherence was 56% [95% CI: 52.1, 60], which is lower than that reported in studies performed in western Ethiopia (64.86%), the United States of America (77.9%), and Sari (60.3%) [13, 18, 32]. However, these percentages are greater than those reported in studies conducted in Addis Ababa (48.6%), Khartoum State, Sudan (49%), Thailand (50.0%), and Yemen (45.8%) [22, 33–35]. The difference may be because patients’ backgrounds, education level, setup, and levels of awareness may affect medication adherence in CHF patients [33]. Additionally, this may be due to the country’s regulations and healthcare systems, medication availability and cost, and varying economic levels of the mentioned countries.

The study revealed that educational status is a statistically significant variable and indicated that CHF patients who had a secondary education were 1.97 times more likely to have medication adherence than those who had no formal education, while those who were able to read and write were 2.20 times more likely to have medication adherence than those who had no formal education. This finding is similar to those of studies conducted in Gondar, Northwest Ethiopia, and the Oromia region, West Ethiopia [19, 36]. This suggests that patients who are educated might adhere to their medications more closely since they are more informed about their medical conditions.

Respondents who had community-based health insurance were 1.82 times more likely to have medication adherence than those who had no community-based health insurance. This is comparable with studies conducted in Gondar, Ethiopia, Tanzania, and Khartoum State, Sudan [14, 22, 37]. This might be because community-based health insurance systems often provide coverage for essential medications, making them more affordable and accessible for insured individuals. This can significantly improve their ability to regularly obtain and take the medications they need.

Respondents who had no comorbidities were 1.72 times more likely to have medication adherence than patients with comorbidities. This is consistent with a study conducted in Gondar, Ethiopia, and other studies in Northwest Ethiopia [37, 38]. This may be due to the complexity of treatment regimens; patients with multiple comorbidities often require a larger number of medications, which can increase the complexity of the treatment regimen. This complexity can make it more challenging for patients to adhere to their medication schedules and dosing instructions, but this is in contrast with the findings of a study performed at Jimma University Specialized Hospital and Pakistan [21, 39], where patients with comorbidities had better medication adherence than those without comorbidities. The difference may be because the studies were conducted in different geographical locations (Gondar, Ethiopia, vs. Jimma University Specialized Hospital, Ethiopia, and Pakistan), which may have different patient demographics, healthcare systems, and access to resources that could impact medication adherence.

Participants who had taken several drugs ≤ 2 were 2.11 times more likely to have medication adherence than participants who had taken several drugs > 2. This study is comparable to a study conducted at Jimma University Specialized Hospital in Southwest Ethiopia and a study in Khartoum State, Sudan [21, 22]. This may be due to pill burden and polypharmacy. Taking a larger number of medications, often referred to as polypharmacy, can result in a greater “pill burden” for patients. This increased pill burden has been associated with reduced medication adherence, as patients may find it more difficult to manage and remember to take multiple medications consistently.

Participants who did not add salt when cooking were 1.72 times more likely to have medication adherence than those who added salt to food when cooking. This study was consistent with a study performed in West Ethiopia [13]. This may be due to health consciousness and lifestyle factors. Individuals who refrain from adding salt to their food when cooking may be more health-conscious and attentive to their overall well-being. This health-focused mindset may also translate to better medication adherence, as they are more motivated to follow prescribed treatment regimens.

Respondents who had asked questions about a doctor or nurse without fear were 1.87 times more likely to have medication adherence than those who had asked questions about fear. This study is in parallel with a study performed in the West Indies showing that nonadherence is related to poor patient-physician communication and insufficient explanations of medication regimens [15]. This could be because patients who communicated with a physician or nurse paid more attention to the communication between themselves and health professionals than to those who communicated with fear about disease and medication.

Strengths and limitations of the study

Strengths of the study

Adherence to medication in this study was assessed using the Morisky eight scale, which minimizes recall bias and can improve the likelihood of unintentionally false reporting that may be brought to the attention of a health professional.

Limitations of the study

The study is cross-sectional because the data represent one point in time, which limits the ability to establish causality or determine the temporal sequence of events. The study relied on self-reported medication adherence, which may be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. The study only focused on CHF patients who had follow-ups at North Shewa public hospitals not representing the entire population in North shewa Public Hospitals.

Conclusion

This study revealed that 56% of chronic heart failure patients had optimal medication adherence. Medication adherence was affected by different factors, such as educational status, community-based health insurance, comorbidities, number of drugs taken, not adding salt when cooking, and asking a doctor or nurse without fear. Even if more than half of the study patients adhere to their medications as prescribed, generating awareness at each visit is still essential for effectively managing medication adherence among CHF patients. Therefore, health professionals should provide education to strengthen medication adherence.
