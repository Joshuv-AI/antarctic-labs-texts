High-latitude teleconnections drive subtropical marine bioproductivity at the dawn of the Antarctic ice sheet
Authors: Alexandra Villa, Stephen R. Meyers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe impact of Antarctic cryosphere dynamics on global marine ecosystems, under the anticipated elevated atmospheric pCO2-levels of our future, is a question of broad societal importance. Newly acquired geologic data from the subtropical South Atlantic provide the first evidence of a highly synchronized 40-kyr-scale teleconnection between Antarctic ice sheet evolution, physical oceanography, and subtropical marine bioproductivity following the Eocene–Oligocene Transition, under pCO2-levels that exceed that of today. This work underscores the sensitivity of the marine-based ice sheets of Antarctica to oceanic heat delivery—with linkages to meridional temperature gradients and pCO2—yielding important implications for marine bioproductivity in our future warming world.

The Eocene–Oligocene Transition [EOT; ~34 Ma; (1–3)] represents a critical phase in the global progression from a greenhouse to an icehouse world, characterized by the development of permanent continental ice sheets on Antarctica, an up to 5 °C decrease in sea surface temperatures (SST) at high latitudes, a reduction in atmospheric carbon dioxide (pCO2), and a drop in global sea level of 70 m (3). Following the EOT, stratigraphic and climate proxy data indicate that the Antarctic ice sheets (AIS) waxed and waned during subsequent glacial and interglacial conditions throughout the early Oligocene, resulting in global sea level changes of up to 25 m (1–5). However, many details of the early history of the AIS are uncertain. Major questions remain regarding the mechanisms driving variable ice sheet and paleoceanographic responses and links to other global phenomena such as opening and closing of ocean gateways and secular reductions in atmospheric pCO2. As a consequence, the role of the AIS in both driving and responding to early Oligocene climate on different time scales (e.g., 102 to 107 years) remains ambiguous in many regards.

Evaluation of the deep ocean record of benthic foraminifera δ18O (5) and AIS proximal stratigraphic records (4) suggests a progressive transition from terrestrial ice sheets during the EOT to more aerially expansive marine-based ice sheets in the early Oligocene, with subsequent episodes of growth and retreat of the AIS from its marine margins over millions of years. During times of greater abundance of marine-based or marine-terminating ice sheets, there exists the potential for enhanced ice–ocean interactions, including the influence of meridional delivery of oceanic heat that is modulated by 40 kyr obliquity “meridional gradient” forcing (6, 7). Ref. 5 used this theoretical framework to leverage benthic foraminifera marine δ18O obliquity sensitivity as a measure of the abundance of marine vs. terrestrial-based ice sheets on Antarctica throughout the Oligocene and Miocene, where stronger obliquity sensitivity was associated with increased presence of marine-based ice.

The role of a marine-based AIS in driving and/or responding to global climate dynamics of the early Oligocene is a critical uncertainty. In the present study, we hypothesize that times of enhanced ice–ocean interaction should manifest as pronounced paleoceanographic responses that are transmitted throughout the global oceans, yielding strong high–low latitude teleconnections. We present a sediment record from the subtropical South Atlantic (International Ocean Discovery Program Exp. 390 and 393 (8); (Fig. 1) that allows us to test this hypothesis, and more generally, to assess the impact of the EOT and subsequent AIS evolution on subtropical marine bioproductivity during the early Oligocene.

We evaluate subtropical South Atlantic bioproductivity using the barium proxy (16–18), and a suite of supplementary proxies, from a new sediment record collected during joint IODP Expeditions 390 and 393 (8). Site U1558 is located in the western South Atlantic (30° 53.7814’S, 24° 50.4822’W) on the western flank of the Mid-Atlantic Ridge at a water depth of 4,334 m. In the present day, Site U1558 is bathed by the northbound flow of Antarctic Bottom Water (AABW) and southbound flow of North Atlantic Deep Water (NADW). Site U1558 recovered strata spanning the middle Eocene (U1558F Biozone E9 Morozovella aragonensis; 42.6 Ma) through the Miocene (U1558F Biozone M3 T Catapsydrax dissimilis; 17.51 Ma). The interval of focus in the present study includes Unit IIC (latest Eocene) and Unit IIB (early Oligocene). Unit IIC is a pink to pinkish gray nannofossil chalk with clay interbedded, and Unit IIB is a pink to pinkish gray nannofossil ooze with clay. Paleomagnetic and biostratigraphic data indicate a relatively complete record of deposition throughout the study interval with no obvious hiatus (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). This record provides a unique opportunity to develop a high-temporal resolution (subprecession-scale) subtropical South Atlantic paleoceanographic reconstruction spanning the EOT and earliest Oligocene.

Barium, biogenic silica, calcium carbonate, and other data from deep-sea sediment records have been widely used as paleoproductivity proxies (16–22), including at sites spanning the EOT (10–13, 15, 23–25). For example, an increase in bioproductivity during the EOT has been previously noted by changes in biogenic barium fluxes, opaline silica fluxes, and carbonate fluxes in the Southern Ocean at the Agulhas Ridge ODP Site 1090 (13); (Fig. 1 and SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Yet, limited evidence is available for astronomical-scale changes in paleoproductivity during the EOT and early Oligocene in the low-latitude Atlantic (e.g., high-resolution datasets at the subprecession scale). One notable study is that of (12), which investigated the Western Equatorial Atlantic ODP Sites 925 at Ceara Rise (Fig. 1) and proposed eccentricity-scale variability in paleoproductivity; however, the temporal sampling resolution of that data series (40 kyr) does not permit assessment of obliquity and precession-scale variability, and aliasing potentially impacts the evaluation of eccentricity-scale signals.

In the present study, a suite of X-ray fluorescence (XRF) scanning elemental data were collected at a nominal 2-cm resolution on the Site U1558 composite splice using Hole A and F (26), providing a median data sampling resolution finer than 2.5 kyr throughout the study interval. Paleomagnetic and biostratigraphic data provide a robust preliminary time scale (8); (SI Appendix, Fig. S1), and the paleomagnetic-derived sedimentation rates (SI Appendix, Table S3) yield initial constraints for cyclostratigraphic evaluation of XRF data with the TimeOpt astrochronologic testing and calibration method (27, 28). XRF-scanning methods, details of the paleomagnetic/biostratigraphic time scale, and a description of the cyclostratigraphic methods are contained in the SI Appendix.

The Ba/Al data (Fig. 2B) from Site U1558 indicate a marked increase concurrent with the ~1‰ shift in δ18Obuik (Fig. 2A) that defines the initial expansion of the AIS, known as “Oi1” (1). This subtropical bioproductivity proxy from Site U1558 also reveals an amplification of the astronomical response, following Oi1. Notably, strong monochromatic 40 kyr obliquity-driven variability emerges in the Ba/Al (Figs. 2B and 3 A and B) bioproductivity proxy, following an initial interval characterized by mixed eccentricity, obliquity, and precession response (Figs. 2B and 3C). Analysis of wt.% K, a measure of clay content that is similar to but antiphased with wt.% CaCO3, (SI Appendix, Fig. S3) reveals markedly different astronomical responses; the interval following Oi1 is characterized by a progression from short eccentricity/precession forcing of wt.% K (Fig. 3G), to mixed short eccentricity/obliquity/precession forcing (Fig. 3 E and F). The presence of a dominant monochromatic 40 kyr bioproductivity cycle in Ba/Al (Fig. 3 A and B) contrasted with the more complex spectral signature of the local depositional facies signal observed in wt.% K (Fig. 3 E and F) is consistent with a far-field teleconnection driving marine bioproductivity, rather than a more localized regional forcing mechanism.

The timing of the occurrence of a strong 40 kyr obliquity response in Ba/Al (Figs. 2B and 3 A and B) is commensurate with enhanced benthic δ18O obliquity sensitivity in the megasplice (Fig. 2D). This leads us to conclude that emergence of the first marine-based ice sheets is linked to an amplified influence of meridional gradient forcing of atmospheric/oceanic processes that simultaneously impacted heat delivery to the ice sheet margin and influenced the delivery of key nutrients to the subtropical South Atlantic. The strong monochromatic 40 kyr response of bioproductivity (Fig. 3 A and B) differs however from the mixed eccentricity/obliquity/precession response observed in the benthic foraminifera δ18O megasplice data (from Site 1218; Fig. 3 I and J), suggesting that integrated changes across the entire AIS were not responsible for the teleconnection to subtropical bioproductivity. This necessitates a mechanism for changes in nutrient delivery to the subtropics that is distinct from the overall ice volume and sea level response; for example, a linkage specifically involving marine-based ice dynamics and subtropical bioproductivity.

Hypotheses for the subtropical Atlantic 40 kyr bioproductivity teleconnection fall into two broad categories: 1) mechanisms attributable to changes in the rate of intermediate water production in regions of the Southern Ocean sensitive to marine ice sheet forcing processes (32) resulting in a greater flux of key nutrients to the lower latitudes (henceforth “intermediate water production”), and 2) mechanisms attributable to changes in nutrient export from the Southern Ocean related to changes in composition, such as dust delivery of nutrients into the Southern Ocean (33) (henceforth “intermediate water composition”). A combination of these two hypotheses is also possible. It should be noted that obliquity-scale variability was previously identified in Ba/Al and Si/Al data from a younger mid-Oligocene Tropical North Atlantic dataset at the shallower Ceara Rise Leg 154 Site 926 (34), through a 360 kyr stratigraphic interval and that work proposed high-latitude bottom-water production as a possible driver of the low-latitude bioproductivity cycles. Astronomical-scale teleconnections between the Southern Ocean and lower latitude bioproductivity have also been proposed in the Pleistocene (33, 35, 36).

Southern Ocean nutrient export to the subtropics—driven by obliquity gradient controls on intermediate water production and/or composition–is a plausible source of the 40 kyr subtropical bioproductivity signal. It is estimated that up to three-quarters of the primary production in the modern ocean, north of 30oS, is supported by the Southern Ocean overturning circulation (32, 33), underscoring the scale of its global influence. More specifically, the 40 kyr obliquity bioproductivity teleconnection could be attributed to the mechanism described by ref. 5, which implicates obliquity-driven meridional temperature gradients and resultant atmosphere/ocean interactions as controls on the delivery of oceanic heat to marine-based ice sheets. Under this scenario, high obliquity results in the advection of warm water into ice-shelf cavities and grounding zones, promoting marine-based ice sheet loss and enhanced freshwater delivery to the ocean, with resultant consequences for intermediate water production rate. Distinct from this mechanism, recent modeling experiments of ref. 37 demonstrate that increased meridional wind speed drives enhanced dense shelf water production and abyssal overturning, via mechanisms attributable to offshore advection of sea ice. Strong meridional winds enhance coastal polynyas, resulting in a greater production of sea ice and associated brine rejection, while also increasing heat loss to the atmosphere. The northward export of sea ice also serves as a freshwater contribution to lower latitudes, influencing the formation of Antarctic Intermediate Waters (AAIW) and Subantarctic Mode Water (SAMW) that deliver nutrients to low latitudes (32). Thus, if obliquity-controlled temperature gradients impact the strength of regional meridional winds, this also provides a means to generate linked 40 kyr intermediate-water production and subtropical bioproductivity signals.

Alternatively, changes in the nutrient composition and ecological dynamics at intermediate water production sites provide a plausible mechanism to generate a 40 kyr subtropical bioproductivity signal. For example, ref. 33 demonstrates a mechanism whereby changes in the aeolian delivery of the micronutrient iron to the Southern Ocean can impact the export of silicic acid in SAMW to low latitudes. According to this hypothesis, obliquity-scale changes in atmospheric circulation and aridity—coincident with the emergence of marine-based ice sheets—could influence aeolian iron delivery to the Southern Ocean, modulating diatom silicic acid uptake in the Southern Ocean, and silicic acid export to the lower latitudes where it modulates bioproductivity at Site U1558. During the latest Eocene through earliest Oligocene, δ30Si data from Maud Rise [ODP Site 689, Fig. 1, (14)] suggest a doubling of the silicic acid concentration in intermediate depth water (~1,500 m) of the Southern Ocean, peaking at ~31.5 Ma, underscoring the importance of diatom productivity and proliferation at this time. At Site U1558, the noisier and weaker obliquity signal observed in Si/Al data does not seem to lend support to this mechanism (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). However, biogenic silica dissolution during early diagenesis may mask the silica productivity signal at this site. For example, studies of early diagenesis have revealed that seabed sites with sedimentation rates <2 cm/kyr are characterized by nearly complete biogenic silica dissolution (38, 39); sedimentation rates through the study interval at Site U1558 are <1.3 cm/kyr. Other studies do demonstrate enhanced biogenic silica accumulation within this time interval (Site 1090 and Site 744; refs. 13 and 15; SI Appendix, Fig. S4).

The astronomical variations at Site U1558 are superposed on longer-term secular changes in sediment flux (Fig. 4). The initiation of the EOT interval is demarcated by a decrease in calcium carbonate content and mass accumulation rate (MAR), and an increase in the concentration and MAR of terrigenous derived aluminum, potassium, and silica (Fig. 4 and SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Intervals of dissolution due to carbonate undersaturation have been widely noted in previous studies of the EOT from the Atlantic and Pacific [e.g., Site 1218; (10)]. The observed pulse in the MAR of Al, K, and Si at Site U1558 (Fig. 4) is most likely an artifact of the spatial resolution of the reconstructed sedimentation rates in this interval, as the coarse resolution of the paleomagnetic time scale can mask a short-term decrease in sedimentation rate at this stratigraphic level due to dissolution of carbonate (SI Appendix, Figs. A pronounced increase in bioproductivity proxies at Site U1558 is observed at Oi1 (BioBa MAR, Ba/Al, Ca MAR, Br/Al), the first glacial expansion event of the AIS (Fig. 4). Background biogenic barium accumulation rates increase sevenfold from 51.4 to 363 μg/cm2/kyr across Oi1, and calcium accumulation rates approximately double from 0.233 g to 0.477 g/cm2/kyr. Our evaluation of Br/Al, an XRF proxy for marine organic matter content (40, 41), documents a contemporaneous increase across Oi1. Increases in bioproductivity across Oi1 have been observed in previous studies from the Atlantic, Pacific, and Southern Ocean (SI Appendix, Fig. S4), although at a lower temporal resolution than provided in the present study.

The bioproductivity fluxes at Site U1558 are sustained at high levels throughout the early Oligocene (Fig. 4), but notably, the highest Ca MARs occur during the Early Oligocene Glacial Maximum (EOGM), consistent with an increase in ocean alkalinity (42) and/or an ecological shift that favors carbonate bioproduction. Following the EOGM, the first marine-based ice sheets emerge, as documented by stratigraphic evidence from the Antarctic Cape Roberts Project 3 Core [CRP-3; (4)] and benthic foraminifera δ18O obliquity sensitivity assessment (5)—coinciding with the interval during which we observed a strong monochromatic 40 kyr forcing of bioproductivity (Ba/Al and Br/Al data) at Site U1558 in the subtropical western South Atlantic (Fig. 4 and SI Appendix, Fig. S2). This time interval also approximately coincides with an increase in the diatom/radiolarian ratio observed in Pacific Sites U1333, U1334, and 1218 (11); (SI Appendix, Fig. S4), suggesting far-field bioproductivity influences in the Pacific.

For the entire portion of the Oligocene evaluated here, we note that Site U1558 Ca MAR, BioBa MAR, and Ba/Al generally remain elevated above that of the late Eocene. In contrast, there is a reduction of Br/Al in the youngest interval at U1558 (<32 Ma; Fig. 4), suggesting a decrease in marine organic matter preservation to levels more representative of the Eocene, even under conditions of generally elevated bioproductivity. This suggests a change in redox state of the water mass, a topic of future evaluation.

Changes in ocean circulation and AIS growth leading up to, during, and following the EOT have widely been associated with the timing of the opening and closing of the Drake and Tasman passages (12, 3, 43). Prior to the opening of these passages (>~50 to 35 Ma), the subtropical western South Atlantic received warm southbound waters sourced from the Pacific Ocean and warm northbound diverted to southbound waters from the Agulhas (44–46). With the opening of the Drake and Tasman passages, and the establishment of the proto-Antarctic Circumpolar Current, a global ocean circulation reorganization emerged (3, 44–46), which manifested as marine bioproductivity increases through global teleconnections (Fig. 4 and SI Appendix, Fig. S4). In this present work, we document this stepwise shift in bioproductivity at U1558 and also the emergence of a sustained teleconnection between subtropical bioproductivity and high-latitude astronomical forcing of the newly established marine-based AIS. The discovery of this strong bioproductivity teleconnection that is distinct from the global climate response (e.g., δ18Obenthic) and the local depositional system response (e.g., wt.% K) emphasizes the need for careful attention to the astronomical forcing of subtropical bioproductivity and its connection to cryosphere evolution. Furthermore, the spatial-temporal variability observed in proxy records from the Pacific Ocean, Southern Ocean, and Atlantic Ocean (SI Appendix, Fig. S4) hints at underlying mechanisms related to ocean dynamics that center on the Southern Ocean and its global influence (SI Appendix, Text 3.2).

Previous work (5) established a detailed perspective of the evolution of Antarctica’s marine vs. terrestrial ice sheets over the Oligocene–Miocene, using the δ18Obenthic obliquity sensitivity paleosensor (Fig. 2D) combined with stratigraphic/paleobiologic data surrounding Antarctica. The recent study of ref. 47 provides additional evidence in support of the enhanced obliquity sensitivity of marine-based ice sheets, using AIS-proximal ice-rafted debris records. The present study provides another piece of the climate puzzle, linking the emergence of the first high-latitude marine-based ice sheets in Antarctica to obliquity-forcing of subtropical bioproductivity, thereby identifying a key teleconnection that is a component of early Oligocene climate dynamics. This work motivates additional high-resolution astronomical-scale reconstructions of marine bioproductivity across the EOT and throughout the Oligocene and Miocene, to further assess the impact of ice sheet evolution on marine ecosystems and carbon sequestration under elevated atmospheric pCO2-levels that could be similar to those in our future (31).

Materials and Methods

Core sections were scanned at the IODP Gulf Coast Repository, with a nominal 2-cm sampling resolution, using acceleration voltages of 10 kV, 30 kV, and 50 kV. XRF count data were calibrated to quantitative compositional data via the analysis of 59 sediment samples using Inductively Coupled Plasma-Atomic Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-AES), Inductively Coupled Plasma-Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS), and quantitative benchtop XRF. A comprehensive multifaceted approach was implemented for cyclostratigraphic evaluation of the XRF data, using the available paleomagnetic time scale constraints, spectral analysis, and the TimeOpt method (27), via the software Astrochron (28). Prior to cyclostratigraphic evaluation, the XRF data were interpolated to its median spatial sampling resolution, and analyses were performed following removal of a linear trend from each analyzed stratigraphic interval. To help limit false positive detection of astronomical signals, we utilize more rigorous multiple testing P-values/confidence levels rather than the traditional P-values/confidence levels that are more commonly used in cyclostratigraphy. Additional information about the materials and methods can be found in the SI Appendix, including scripts for reproduction of the key cyclostratigraphic analyses.
