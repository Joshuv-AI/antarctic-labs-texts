Primary renal synovial sarcoma presenting with tumor rupture and extensive intra-abdominal dissemination: A case report and literature review
Authors: Yoon Seon Oh, Luciano Barajas, Chika Iguh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Primary renal synovial sarcoma is a rare malignant mesenchymal neoplasm that may pose significant diagnostic challenges. We report a 34-year-old man who presented with recurrent hemorrhage and hemoperitoneum from a large left renal mass. Following embolization and multivisceral resection, histologic examination revealed a high-grade spindle and round cell neoplasm initially favored to represent Ewing sarcoma. Molecular analysis identified an SS18SSX2 fusion, establishing the diagnosis of poorly differentiated synovial sarcoma. This case highlights the importance of molecular testing and raises the possibility that tumor rupture may contribute to atypical intra-abdominal dissemination.

Introduction

Synovial sarcoma is a malignant mesenchymal neoplasm characterized by the pathognomonic t (X; 18) (p11.2; q11.2) chromosomal translocation, resulting in fusion of the SS18 gene with one of the SSX genes, most commonly SSX1 or SSX2.1 Although it accounts for approximately 5 to 10% of all soft tissue sarcomas, synovial sarcoma most commonly arises in the deep soft tissues of the extremities of adolescents and young adults and only rarely occurs in visceral organs.2

Primary renal synovial sarcoma (PRSS) is an exceptionally uncommon manifestation of this disease and remains a significant diagnostic challenge due to its overlapping clinical, radiologic, and histopathologic features with a variety of renal neoplasms.3 Reported differential diagnostic considerations include sarcomatoid renal cell carcinoma, adult Wilms tumor, primitive neuroectodermal tumor, and other spindle cell sarcomas of the kidney.3 Definitive diagnosis typically requires integration of morphologic findings, immunohistochemistry, and molecular confirmation of SS18 rearrangement.3 A recent systematic review identified fewer than 200 reported cases in the literature, highlighting the rarity of this entity and the limited collective clinical experience available to guide management.4

PRSS typically presents as a large renal mass associated with flank pain, hematuria, or abdominal discomfort and is recognized for its aggressive clinical behavior, propensity for local recurrence, and metastatic potential.4 Hemorrhage and spontaneous tumor rupture are distinctly uncommon presentations and have been described only sporadically in the literature. We report a case of poorly differentiated primary renal synovial sarcoma presenting with recurrent hemorrhage, tumor rupture, and extensive intra-abdominal involvement, including pancreatic and omental extension. In addition to illustrating the diagnostic challenges posed by this rare entity, this case raises the possibility that tumor rupture may contribute to atypical patterns of intra-abdominal dissemination.

Case presentation

A 34-year-old male presented with acute left flank pain and was found to have a large left renal mass (Fig. 1) complicated by hemoperitoneum, for which he underwent initial interventional radiology embolization. Several weeks later, the patient presented with severe left flank pain, nausea, and vomiting concerning for recurrent hemorrhage. Imaging demonstrated progressive enlargement of the renal mass from prior studies with central necrosis and associated mass effect on adjacent structures, including the pancreas and descending colon. Small-volume ascites was also present. No definite metastatic disease was identified on initial imaging.

The patient subsequently underwent repeat embolization followed by exploratory laparotomy with en bloc resection. Intraoperatively, the mass was noted to be ruptured with associated hemoperitoneum and was densely adherent to the descending colon, small bowel, omentum, and posterior abdominal wall. The procedure included left nephrectomy, distal pancreatectomy, splenectomy, extended left colectomy with colostomy creation, and small bowel resection.

Gross examination demonstrated a large composite specimen consisting of the left kidney, colon, distal pancreas, and omentum with a hemorrhagic cavity measuring 12 × 11.5 × 4 cm adherent to surrounding structures (Fig. 2a and b). Immunohistochemical studies demonstrated tumor cells positive for CD56 (Fig. 3d), CD99 (Fig. 3e), caldesmon, and vimentin, and negative for desmin, S100, chromogranin, synaptophysin, CD34, p53, and cytokeratins (AE1/AE3, OSCAR). Molecular analysis by next-generation sequencing identified an SS18::SSX2 fusion; subsequently, a diagnosis of synovial sarcoma (FNCLCC grade 3) was made. Pathologic staging at the time of diagnosis was pT4b, pN0, pM1 (metastatic involvement of the pancreas and omentum).

The postoperative course was complicated by prolonged ileus and intra-abdominal hemorrhage requiring return to the operating room for hematoma evacuation and drain placement. The patient was subsequently discharged after clinical stabilization. During the postoperative period, the patient was readmitted with an intra-abdominal fluid collection requiring image-guided drainage and antibiotic therapy and underwent thoracentesis for a left pleural effusion.

Following recovery, the case was discussed at an institutional multidisciplinary conference, and the patient was referred to medical oncology for consideration of systemic therapy. The patient was consented for initiation of combination chemotherapy with Adriamycin, ifosfamide, and mesna (AIM regimen). However, he did not attend scheduled appointments for central venous access placement and chemotherapy initiation. At the most recent follow-up approximately four months after surgery, the patient reported doing well clinically. Surveillance CT demonstrated indeterminate retroperitoneal nodules measuring up to 8 mm and a 2.5 cm heterogeneous nodule adjacent to the gallbladder, for which continued radiologic surveillance was recommended. The patient had not initiated the recommended AIM chemotherapy regimen because of uncertainty regarding treatment benefit but expressed willingness to re-establish oncology follow-up.

Discussion

Primary renal synovial sarcoma (PRSS) is a rare malignant mesenchymal neoplasm that may present significant diagnostic challenges due to overlapping clinical, radiographic, and histologic features with other renal malignancies. Synovial sarcoma most commonly arises in the deep soft tissues of the extremities near large joints, whereas primary renal involvement is distinctly uncommon.1,3,5 Patients frequently present with large renal masses demonstrating cystic degeneration, hemorrhage, and necrosis, often leading to an initial clinical impression of renal cell carcinoma or other hemorrhagic renal lesions.4,6 In some cases, hemorrhagic presentation may mimic Wunderlich syndrome, ruptured angiomyolipoma, traumatic/perinephric hematoma, or hemorrhagic renal cystic lesions, further complicating diagnosis 7, 8, 9.

Histologically, primary renal synovial sarcoma may demonstrate spindle cell, round cell, or poorly differentiated morphology and may overlap with other spindle cell and small round blue cell neoplasms of the kidney, including Ewing sarcoma/primitive neuroectodermal tumor, sarcomatoid renal cell carcinoma, adult Wilms tumor, malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor, and other undifferentiated renal sarcomas.5,10,11 In the present case, the tumor was initially favored to represent Ewing sarcoma based on morphologic findings prior to molecular characterization, demonstrating the diagnostic complexity of these lesions.

Molecular testing plays a critical role in establishing the diagnosis of synovial sarcoma, particularly in morphologically ambiguous cases. Synovial sarcoma is characterized by the recurrent chromosomal translocation t (X; 18) (p11.2; q11.2), resulting in SS18::SSX fusion transcripts, which are considered defining molecular alterations of this entity.3 In our case, next-generation sequencing identified an SS18::SSX2 fusion, confirming the diagnosis of synovial sarcoma. Although the tumor demonstrated immunoreactivity for CD56, CD99, caldesmon, and vimentin, the immunophenotype alone was not sufficiently specific to establish the diagnosis, particularly given the initial morphologic concern for Ewing sarcoma. Prior studies by Yang et al. and Huang et al. similarly demonstrated that these tumors commonly express CD99, BCL2, vimentin, EMA, and CD56, while lacking specificity for any single immunohistochemical marker.6,11 This case therefore illustrates the importance of integrating morphology, immunohistochemistry, and molecular testing in the evaluation of unusual renal spindle and round cell neoplasms.

Another notable feature of this case was the extensive rupture-associated intra-abdominal involvement. Although hemorrhagic and cystic degeneration are recognized features of these tumors, presentation with spontaneous renal rupture, retroperitoneal hemorrhage, or hemoperitoneum appears to be uncommon 7, 8, 9,12. Prior reports by Pichler et al. and Stamm et al. described primary renal synovial sarcoma presenting as retroperitoneal hematoma or hemorrhagic renal masses initially suspected to represent benign or traumatic processes rather than aggressive sarcoma (Table 1).8,9 Similarly, Sairam et al. reported a diagnostically challenging hemorrhagic presentation initially managed as a perinephric hematoma before definitive pathologic diagnosis was established (Table 1).7 In the present case, recurrent hemorrhage, repeated embolization procedures, frank tumor rupture, and extensive intra-abdominal adhesions contributed to a particularly aggressive clinical presentation. Histologic identification of tumor within retroperitoneal ruptured tumor contents further raises the possibility that tumor rupture contributed to the unusual intra-abdominal distribution pattern identified at resection. Compared with previously reported hemorrhagic presentations, the extent of intra-abdominal involvement in the present case appears particularly extensive, with pancreatic and omental involvement identified at the time of resection (Table 1). The pattern of pancreatic and omental involvement in this case is also unusual and raises important considerations regarding the mechanism of tumor spread. Synovial sarcoma most commonly metastasizes to the lungs, followed by bone and lymph nodes, whereas intra-abdominal involvement is distinctly uncommon.4 Metastatic involvement of the pancreas by sarcoma is itself rare overall, and omental involvement has been infrequently described in synovial sarcoma. While the pancreas and omentum were classified pathologically as metastatic sites, the operative and pathologic findings in this case raise the possibility that at least part of the intra-abdominal disease burden may have represented rupture-associated dissemination or peritoneal seeding rather than purely hematogenous metastatic spread. Supporting findings include documented tumor rupture with hemoperitoneum, extensive tumor adherence to surrounding intra-abdominal structures, involvement of retroperitoneal ruptured tumor contents, and associated serosal inflammatory and granulation tissue changes in adjacent bowel and omental specimens. Although it is not possible to definitively distinguish metastatic spread from rupture-associated dissemination in this setting, this case suggests that tumor rupture may contribute to atypical intra-abdominal distribution patterns in aggressive renal sarcomas.

Management of primary renal synovial sarcoma often requires multimodal therapy including radical surgical excision and consideration of systemic chemotherapy. Due to the rarity of the disease, standardized treatment guidelines remain limited, although aggressive surgical resection remains the cornerstone of therapy.4,13 Chemotherapeutic regimens commonly include ifosfamide- and doxorubicin-based protocols, which have demonstrated activity in synovial sarcoma and advanced primary renal synovial sarcoma.4,13 Several prior reports have additionally described prolonged survival or remission following aggressive multimodal treatment combining surgery and systemic chemotherapy.4,6 The extensive local and intra-abdominal involvement in the present case required complex multidisciplinary surgical management followed by consideration of systemic therapy. Following recovery, the patient was referred to medical oncology and consented for treatment with Adriamycin, ifosfamide, and mesna (AIM regimen). A limitation of the present report is the relatively short postoperative follow-up period, which precludes meaningful assessment of long-term oncologic outcomes, recurrence patterns, and survival. Although surveillance imaging has identified indeterminate lesions requiring continued follow-up, the clinical significance of these findings remains uncertain.

Overall, this case highlights several important clinicopathologic features of primary renal synovial sarcoma, including diagnostic difficulty, aggressive hemorrhagic behavior, tumor rupture, and an unusual pattern of intra-abdominal involvement. The extensive rupture-associated dissemination observed in this case expands the spectrum of reported manifestations of this neoplasm and raises important considerations regarding mechanisms of spread in large hemorrhagic renal sarcomas. This case also demonstrates the importance of molecular testing in establishing the diagnosis of unusual renal spindle and round cell neoplasms and supports consideration of synovial sarcoma in the differential diagnosis of large hemorrhagic renal masses in young. Recognition of rupture-associated dissemination may additionally have important implications for understanding atypical intra-abdominal spread patterns in aggressive renal sarcomas.
