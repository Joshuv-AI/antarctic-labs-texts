Applying a Robust Deep Learning Model to Assess the Remaining Amount of Foods With High Glycemic Index in Taiwanese Hospital Diabetic Meals: A Focus on Rice and Congee, With Milk as a Low-Glycemic Reference
Authors: Hsien-Yu Fan, Pey-Rong Chen, Ya-Ting Kuo, Yu-Tien Chang, Kuo-Liong Chien, Yung-Chi Lu, Shih-Lin Hung, Shih-Ming Chuang, Ying-Yueh Chu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveBuilding on our previous success in artificial intelligence–driven food classification, this study aims to enhance portion size estimation methods, with a particular focus on rice and congee, using milk as a low-glycemic index reference in selected Taiwanese hospitals.MethodsWe used a RealSense D435i depth camera to capture both 2-dimensional (2D) and 3-dimensional (3D) images of rice, congee, and milk served in standard hospital bowls. The images were labeled and classified by a convolutional neural network, Xception, whereas food weight and volume were estimated using 25 depth points with multiple perceptron (MLP) models. MLP models were trained on 80% of the data set and tested on the remaining 20%, with predictions refined through a weighted ensemble of selected MLP models. We validated the MLP model performance through 3 comparisons: 1) MLP with traditional statistical methods (linear regression), 2) MLP with clinical dietitians, and 3) MLP performance across 3 different hospital settings.ResultsThe Xception model accurately identified foods with high glycemic index and recognized milk as a low-glycemic reference, achieving a training accuracy of 1.0 with the loss approaching zero by epoch 22. Test accuracy stabilized early with consistently low loss. MLP models effectively predicted food weights, with models E, F, and G yielding the lowest mean squared errors for rice, congee, and milk, respectively. Across all food types, MLP models consistently outperformed traditional linear regression, showing lower mean absolute percentage error (MAPE: 0.3%–3.3%) than linear regression (MAPE: 4%–9.2%). When compared with estimates from 6 experienced dietitians (MAPE: 39%–41%), the MLP models also demonstrated markedly higher accuracy. Performance remained stable across 3 hospital sites, indicating strong generalizability.ConclusionsThe Xception and MLP models effectively quantify foods with high glycemic index and identify milk as a low-glycemic reference, demonstrating strong predictive performance and generalizability. Further studies are warranted to extend this approach to other food types.Keywords: food quantification, glycemic index, Xception model, MLP model, depth imaging

Introduction

Rice and congee are clearly classified as the food with high glycemic index [1]. Although milk has a lower glycemic impact than rice and congee, its glycemic index is still higher than that of vegetables. Due to the shortage of clinical dietitians in Taiwanese hospitals, many patients do not receive personalized nutritional care. For example, patients with diabetes, like other patients, are often given fixed portions of these foods. Their actual intake is difficult to determine because not all patients complete the meals.

Hospital meals are typically well-planned to meet nutritional needs, but inpatients, especially the elderly, often experience reduced appetite due to the discomfort from treatments [2,3]. As a result, patients may consume less food than what is provided, leading to insufficient intake. Despite this, physicians often prescribe glucose-lowering medications based on the assumption that patients are eating their entire meal. When a patient eats less rice but still takes the prescribed medication, they risk hypoglycemia [4,5]. This highlights the urgent need for more personalized nutrition and medication plans to prevent such adverse effects.

With advancements in image recognition technology, it is now possible to assess the amount of leftover food on patients’ trays using plate collection systems [6,7]. In this study, we focused on rice and congee, using milk as a low-glycemic index reference, and simulated various leftover scenarios. Using 2-dimensional (2D) and 3-dimensional (3D) photography, we developed a deep learning framework that combines convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for food classification and multilayer perceptrons (MLPs) for predicting leftover portion sizes [8]. Building on our previous success in food classification using artificial intelligence (AI) [9], this study further aimed to estimate leftover amounts by integrating MLP models. We validated our findings through the following: 1) comparing the performance of MLP models with traditional statistical methods (linear regression); 2) evaluating the accuracy of MLP predictions against estimates made by clinical dietitians; and 3) assessing the generalizability of the model using data sets from 3 different hospitals.

Methods

Equipment

The Intel RealSense Depth Camera D435i was mounted on a VIJIM LS25 multifunctional desktop stand (aluminum; maximum load: 1.5 kg; clamp range: 0–50 mm; weight: 1 kg), as illustrated in Figure 1 [10]. This camera was used for dietary assessment in the context of precision nutrition, offering both 2D and 3D imaging capabilities. It enabled accurate food classification and quantification of portion sizes, food volume, and weight. With a depth resolution of ≤1280 × 720 pixels and an 87° × 58° field of view, the camera captured high-quality depth information, allowing for precise calculations of food dimensions. Operating within a range of 0.3–3 m and achieving a depth accuracy of <2% at a 2-m distance, the device had a minimum depth measurement distance of ∼38 cm at maximum resolution, making it particularly well-suited for personalized nutrition assessments where precise intake measurements are critical for tailoring nutritional interventions.

Data set preparation for leftover liquid and solid food in hospitals

To apply the RealSense depth camera for precise food assessment, a data set was prepared using standard white bowls from National Taiwan University Hospital, with volumes of 450 mL for rice and milk and 670 mL for congee. The study focused on capturing images of common hospital foods such as white rice, congee, and fresh milk. Each food item was divided into specific portions to create data sets for AI models (Supplemental Table 1). For example, a full bowl of rice (320 g) was split into nutritional servings ranging from 1 of 8 (20 g) to 8 of 8 (160 g), photographed in 3 different arrangements (leveled, vertical, and random) for a total of 600 images, later divided into 480 training images (80%) and 120 testing images (20%). Similarly, congee (500 g) and fresh milk (360 mL) were also divided into various portions with corresponding images captured for AI training and testing. For congee, 764 images were collected, split into 665 images for training and 99 for testing, whereas milk generated 1099 images, split into 955 for training and 144 for testing. An empty bowl, along with bowls containing various portions of milk, congee, and rice, were also captured for reference. The captured data were used to develop MLP models to estimate food volume and weight based on the image and depth data provided by the RealSense camera.

Labeling and model integration for food recognition and weight estimation

To further enhance the data set’s utility for food recognition and weight estimation, each red-green-blue (RGB) image captured in the study was labeled using LabelMe [11]. Each image was assigned a specific index: 0 for an empty bowl, 1 for milk, 2 for congee, and 3 for rice. In addition to labeling, the actual weight of these food ingredients was meticulously recorded to facilitate accurate modeling. These indices served as outputs for the CNN-based model designed for image recognition. The outputs generated by the CNN model were then associated with the recorded weights of the food ingredients, enabling integration with MLP model. This multi-MLP approach used ensemble methods, specifically weighted averaging voting, to produce a refined estimate of the weight of each food ingredient. This comprehensive labeling and model integration process ensured that the AI models developed could reliably predict food volumes and weights based on the images processed, thereby enhancing the accuracy of dietary assessments in the context of precision nutrition.

Classification of food images for CNN training

Before the CNN model was trained, food images were organized and annotated. The data were then cleaned by removing duplicates and deleting blurry, overly dark, overly bright, or other poor-quality photographs. To increase data diversity, each image was randomly rotated to make the model invariant to orientation, and the images were flipped horizontally or vertically. Brightness, contrast, and saturation were adjusted as well. The images were resized to a standard input size, 299 × 299 pixels, as required by the CNN. For data splitting, the data set was divided into training and test sets, usually in an 80:20 ratio for CNN models (Supplemental Table 2). The data set used in this study was divided into training and test sets across 4 categories: empty, fresh milk, congee, and rice. The training set included 27 images of empty plates, 184 images of fresh milk, 113 images of congee, and 190 images of rice. The test set comprised 7 images of empty plates, 46 images of milk, 28 images of congee, and 48 images of rice. This distribution ensured a balanced representation of each category for model evaluation. In this study, the Xception model was used as the backbone of the classifier model. Input images were resized to 299 × 299 pixels with 3 RGB channels, and the model was configured to classify images into 4 categories (e.g., rice, congee, milk, and empty bowl) [12]. Pretrained weights from ImageNet were used to initialize the model. To fine-tune the model for our specific task, all layers except the dense layers were frozen during training. The dense layer consisted of 1024 units with a rectified linear unit activation function. The model was trained over a series of epochs, during which both training accuracy and loss, as well as test accuracy and loss, were closely monitored. Training accuracy and loss were used to evaluate how effectively the model was learning from the training data, with an expectation of increasing accuracy and decreasing loss over time. Test accuracy and loss were measured to assess the model’s ability to generalize to new, unseen data, with well-generalized models showing similar metrics between training and test sets, indicating minimal overfitting.

Depth abstraction

The weight or volume of food ingredients is highly dependent on depth information. Given the nonlinear and complex shape of a bowl, accurately calculating its volume can be challenging. In this work, we proposed a novel approach for estimating the weight and/or volume of ingredients by using depth data captured by the D435i camera.

First, the center of the bowl is identified using the RGB image, and its corresponding depth is recorded from the depth image. An additional m points were then selected, starting from the center and moving outward in a clockwise spiral, gradually increasing radius toward the bowl’s edge. Their depth values were similarly recorded. This process is showed in Figure 1. If any point is recorded as zero or null, a nearby point was selected as a substitute. These 25 depth points were then abstracted and used as input features for the next step.

Multilayer perceptron

The 25 abstracted depth points were used as input for the MLP model. To train and evaluate the model, we compiled a data set that included different food types: milk, porridge, and rice (Supplemental Table 3). The training set comprised 191 instances for milk (including 955 cases), 133 instances for porridge (including 665 cases), and 58 instances for rice (including 290 cases). The test set included 48 instances for milk (with 144 cases), 33 instances for porridge (with 33 cases), and 15 instances for rice (with 15 cases). The model’s input layer consisted of 25 data points. The network architecture included 3 hidden layers with varying numbers of nodes. The model used a regression approach for data analysis. Models A and B featured a simple architecture with a dense layer of 100 units followed by a dense layer with 1 unit, with model A using stochastic gradient descent and model B using the Adam Optimizer (Supplemental Table 4). Model C introduced an additional 10-unit dense layer, whereas model D included layers with 100 and 20 units. Model E had a deeper structure with dense layers of 64, 32, and 16 units. For congee and milk images, more complex architectures were implemented with model F containing layers of 64, 128, and 64 units, and model G comprising layers of 128, 256, and 128 units. Models C through G were all optimized using Adam Optimizer.

Validation analysis

In validation 1, we used 25 data points and applied traditional statistical methods (linear regression) to predict food weight (grams per milliliter). RMSE and MAPE were calculated, and the results were compared with those from the MLP model. In validation 2, 6 registered dietitians estimated leftover food amounts. We calculated RMSE and MAPE based on their estimates and compared the accuracy of the MLP model with that of the dietitians. In validation 3, we collected additional data from 2 other hospitals to assess the model’s generalizability. RMSE and MAPE were used to evaluate performance across different hospital data sets.

Results

Performance evaluation of the Xception model for classifying foods

The training and test accuracies showed distinct patterns throughout the epochs. During the initial epochs (0–10), the training accuracy exhibited considerable fluctuations, ranging from 0.4 to 0.9 (Figure 2). However, after approximately epoch 10, the fluctuations diminished, with accuracy stabilizing between 0.9 and 1. By approximately epoch 22, the training accuracy consistently reached 1, indicating near-perfect performance on the training data. In contrast, the test accuracy began at 0.7–0.8 during epoch 0, with notable oscillations between 0.9 and 1 throughout epochs 0–10. After epoch 10, the test accuracy stabilized and maintained a value of 1, suggesting that the model was able to generalize well to unseen data after sufficient training.

Training and validation accuracy and loss of the Xception model.

Similarly, the training loss demonstrated significant variability during the initial epochs (0–10), fluctuating between 0.2 and 1.4. However, after epoch 10, the training loss consistently decreased, stabilizing between 0 and 0.2. By approximately epoch 22, the training loss reached 0, indicating minimal errors in the model’s predictions on the training data. The test loss, initially between 0.6 and 0.8 during epoch 0, showed similar fluctuations between 0 and 0.2 throughout epochs 0–10. After epoch 10, the test loss gradually decreased and stabilized near 0, reflecting the model’s effectiveness in minimizing errors and its robustness in generalizing to new data.

Quantitative analysis of leftover rice

Across all models, the predicted weights closely matched the true weights, with minimal deviations observed (Table 1). For example, at a true weight of 40 g, the predictions ranged from 40.0 to 41.0 g across the models. Similarly, for a true weight of 320 g, the predictions varied from 319.4 to 321.0 g. Model E demonstrated the lowest training and test MSEs of 3.7 × 10−5 and 8.3 × 10−5, respectively, indicating the best overall performance in terms of accuracy among the models. In contrast, model D showed slightly higher training and test MSE values, suggesting a marginally lower prediction accuracy. Overall, all models exhibited high accuracy in predicting rice weights, with model E outperforming the others.

The MLP model consistently outperforms traditional linear regression across all 3 food types—rice, congee, and milk (Table 5). For example, in the case of rice, the RMSE dropped from 41.31 (linear regression) to 18.28 (MLP), and the MAPE improved from 9.17% to 3.13%. Similar improvements were observed for congee and milk, suggesting that the MLP model provides a more precise estimation across diverse food textures.

Discussion

Main findings

The Xception model demonstrated strong performance in classifying foods with high glycemic index and milk as a low-glycemic reference, showing steadily increasing accuracy and decreasing loss during training, suggesting effective learning and minimal overfitting. In our quantitative analysis of hospital leftover foods, MLP models accurately predicted the weights of rice and congee, as well as the volume of fresh milk. Across all 3 food types, the MLP models consistently outperformed traditional linear regression, showing lower RMSE and MAPE values. Furthermore, when compared with estimates from 6 experienced dietitians, the MLP models again showed superior accuracy. Notably, the model’s performance remained consistent across 3 different hospital sites, indicating strong generalizability.

Compared with previous studies

The present study demonstrated significant advancements in the accuracy of estimating nutrient intake from GI foods, specifically rice, congee, and milk, compared with previous research, including that of Lu et al. [6] and Tagi et al. [7]. Lu et al. [7] developed an AI-based system that used RGB plus depth image pairs and a multitask contextual network for food segmentation. Their system achieved a high correlation coefficient (>0.91) with ground truth measurements and reported MREs of <20%. However, their methodology did not specifically address the quantification of high-GI foods, which could limit its applicability for precise nutritional assessment in this context. In contrast, our study specifically targeted high-GI foods and used CNN models A through E for food image recognition. The MSEs observed in our study were considerably lower, with values consistently <1, and in many instances, reaching precision levels ≤2 or 3 decimal places. For instance, the MSEs for rice across the different models ranged from 3.7 × 10−5 to 7.0 × 10−5, reflecting a substantial improvement in quantitative accuracy for nutrient estimation. Moreover, Tagi et al. [7] evaluated an AI model for estimating leftover liquid foods, reporting mean absolute errors of 0.25 for thin rice gruel and 0.63 for fermented milk, with RMSEs of 1.55 and 0.89, respectively. Although their study indicated that AI estimation surpassed visual methods in accuracy, the MAE and RMSE values for thin rice gruel were higher than the precision achieved in our study (Supplemental Tables 5–7). Overall, the results from our study suggest that the proposed approach offered a marked improvement in the precision of nutrient estimation for high-GI foods(Supplemental Table 8), outperforming methodologies by both Lu et al. [6] and Tagi et al. [7]. This enhanced accuracy underscores the effectiveness of our models in providing more reliable assessments of high-GI food intake.

Clinical implications

In Taiwanese hospitals, the meal offerings are relatively standardized, with rice, congee, and milk being the primary foods that significantly impact blood glucose concentrations. Rice and congee, in particular, are almost always provided daily to patients. However, diabetic inpatients often have poor appetites, leading to incomplete meal consumption despite being prescribed hypoglycemic medications or insulin. This mismatch frequently results in hypoglycemia. Additionally, due to the shortage of hospital dietitians and the fact that nutritional consultations often require out-of-pocket expenses, not all patients opt to consult with a dietitian. This study offers a potential solution to the shortage of dietitian resources in Taiwanese hospitals. By using CNN and MLP models to identify and quantify the remaining grams of high-GI foods when meals are returned, our MLP model has achieved a margin of error of <3% in gram estimation. If integrated into the hospital’s real-time electronic medical record system, this approach could enable physicians to prescribe more accurately, preventing frequent hypoglycemic episodes during hospitalization.

Strengths and limitations

This study presents several strengths. First, the use of the Intel RealSense depth camera D435, combined with a controlled photographic setup, ensured consistent and high-quality image data for analysis, which is critical for reliable model training and evaluation. Second, the application of the Xception model and multiple MLP architectures allowed for a robust comparison of different machine learning approaches in predicting food quantities. The high accuracy achieved by these models, particularly in predicting rice, congee, and milk quantities, highlights the potential of AI-driven methods for precise food intake assessment in clinical settings. Additionally, the systematic preprocessing of image data, including augmentation techniques, contributed to the models’ generalization capability, reducing risk of overfitting and enhancing their applicability to real-world scenarios.

Despite the promising results, this study has some limitations. The data set used, although carefully curated, may not fully represent the diversity of hospital food items, which could limit the generalizability of the findings to other settings or types of meals. Furthermore, the study’s focus on specific food types like rice, congee, and milk means that the models may require further validation and potentially retraining to handle a broader range of foods with varying textures and appearances. Another limitation is the reliance on a single imaging device, which, although standardized in this study, may not capture the same level of detail in different environments or under varying lighting conditions. Finally, the study’s findings are based on controlled experimental conditions, and additional research is needed to assess the models’ performance in more dynamic, real-world hospital environments where factors such as varying plate sizes, different food consistencies, and patient handling of food could impact accuracy. However, in our ongoing efforts, the MLP models have undergone repeated validation not only through comparison with traditional statistical methods such as linear regression but also by benchmarking against experienced clinical dietitians’ estimates and testing across 3 different hospital sites. In all cases, the MLP models demonstrated consistently lower prediction errors, indicating improved robustness and generalizability. These validations help to address some of the initial limitations and support the potential practical application of AI-based food consumption assessment in diverse hospital settings.

Conclusions

The Xception model effectively identified foods with high glycemic index and recognized milk as a low-glycemic reference from 2D images. Subsequent analysis using MLP models on the corresponding depth images achieved high accuracy in predicting the weights of rice, congee, and the volumes of milk. Model E provided the best performance for rice weight prediction, whereas models F and G were superior for congee and milk, respectively. These results demonstrate the robustness of the combined Xception and MLP approach in food quantification, with potential for further refinement in predicting lower quantities and extending the method to additional food types.
