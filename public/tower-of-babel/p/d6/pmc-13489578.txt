Quality of Life and Its Determinants among Jordanian Patients with Multiple Sclerosis: A Cross-sectional Analytical Study
Authors: Najah Essam Mahadin, Abdullah Sulaiman Odeh Alshobaki, Mahmoud Mohammed Mahmoud Hamdan, Amro Osama Khalil Salem, Obieda Husam Hussein, Nour Amin Tarawneh, Aya Amin Tarawneh, Rashed Omar Alqaisi, Ahmad Samer Abu Khashab, Waqar Abdul-Qahar Al-Kubaisy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a non-traumatic disability that affects the central nervous system and reduces quality of life (QoL). No study from Jordan has specifically assessed the QoL in patients with MS.Objectives:To evaluate QoL among Jordanian MS patients and examine its association with sociodemographic and medical characteristics.Methods:This cross-sectional analytical study included adult Jordanian MS patients (aged ≥18 years) who attended the MS Unit at Albasheer Governmental Teaching Hospital, Amman, between December 2023 and July 2025. Data was collected through face-to-face interviews using the validated 26-item WHOQOL-BREF questionnaire.Results:A total of 300 patients with MS were included (mean age: 35.3 ± 11.1 years; female: 68.7%). The overall mean QoL score was 57.2 ± 15.2, indicating generally low QoL. Physical and psychological domains had lower mean scores (49.7 ± 18 and 57.8 ± 18.4, respectively). In the univariate analysis, significant risk factors for poor QoL were parental consanguinity (OR: 1.88, 95% CI = 1.01–3.45) and smoking (OR: 1.74, 95% CI = 1.08–2.77). In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, factors independently associated with lower odds of higher QoL were smoking (OR = 0.454, 95% CI: 0.236–0.871; P = 0.018), having one comorbidity (OR = 0.278, 95% CI: 0.108–0.714; P = 0.008), and secondary progressive MS (OR = 0.260, 95% CI: 0.084–0.798; P = 0.019).Conclusions:The overall QoL of Jordanian multiple sclerosis patients is poor, particularly in the physical and psychological domains. Factors independently associated with lower odds of higher QoL were smoking, having one comorbidity, and secondary progressive multiple sclerosis, and thus interventions targeting these risk factors may improve the QoL.

INTRODUCTION

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a major cause of non-traumatic disability worldwide.[1] It is a progressive autoimmune disorder that leads to demyelination and inflammation in the central nervous system.[2] The disease shows variable symptoms due to immune attacks and axonal injury.[3] Patients may suffer from sensory-motor, visual, cognitive, bladder, sexual, and psychological problems such as depression and anxiety.[4] Globally, more than 1.8 million people have MS,[5] with young adults and women more commonly affected.[3678] Prognosis can vary with age; one study found that complete recovery ranged from 87.4% in the youngest age group (0–18 years) to 68% in the oldest age group (>44 years).[7] Although there have been advances, its exact cause is unclear, with the most likely causes being genetics, environment, and possible viral infections.[9]

Clinically, MS is divided into types based on severity and progression. Benign MS is the mildest, with minimal disability for years (EDSS ≤3 within 10 years).[10] Clinically Isolated Syndrome (CIS) is a single neurological episode lasting ≥24 hours and may precede MS.[11] Relapsing–remitting MS (RRMS), the most common type, alternates between relapses and remissions.[12] Early-onset RRMS (i.e., occurring before the age of 18 years) is associated with more frequent relapses, faster disability, and severe progression.[13] Secondary progressive MS (SPMS) develops from RRMS, with gradual worsening even during remission.[14] Primary progressive MS is less common, requires longer observation, and often has poorer outcomes.[15] RRMS is generally milder than primary progressive MS and progressive-relapsing MS, but needs early, accurate diagnosis for better management.[16]

The World Health Organization defines health as “a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being, not merely the absence of disease”.[17] Quality of life (QoL) is described as an individual’s perception of their life position within cultural and personal contexts.[18] Patients with MS have significantly reduced QoL,[236] and report lower QoL than the general population.[19] Factors associated with QoL include age, sex, disease duration, MS type, smoking, and comorbidities.[20]

In Jordan, MS prevalence is about 39 per 100,000 people.[21] Few studies from Jordan have explored the challenges faced by patient with MS. One qualitative study reported on challenges such as disease progression, healthcare barriers, miscommunication, delayed diagnosis, poor understanding of management, and physical and psychological symptoms.[22] However, no study has specifically assessed the QoL in Jordanian MS patients. Therefore, this study was conducted to determine the QoL of Jordanian MS patients and to identify sociodemographic and medical factors associated with impaired QoL. Understanding the impact of MS on the QoL of patients is essential for both patients and healthcare providers.

METHODS

Study design, setting, and population

This cross-sectional analytical study included adult Jordanian MS patients (aged ≥18 years) from the MS Unit at Albasheer Governmental Teaching Hospital, Amman, which is the only referral center for MS patients in Jordan, and was conducted between December 2023 and July 2025. Patients with pre-existing psychiatric disorders were excluded.

Written informed consent was obtained from each participant, and verbal consent was obtained from healthcare providers. Prior to participation, all participants were provided with information about the study, including its purpose and procedures. Participants were informed that their participation was entirely voluntary, that they could decline to participate or withdraw from the study at any time without any consequences, and that their responses would be treated confidentially and analysed anonymously. Participants were also assured that no personally identifiable information would be disclosed in any reports or publications. No incentives or compensation were provided for participation.

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from Mutah University Committee (Ref no.: 1122023), Jordanian Ministry of Health (Ref no.: MOH/REC/2023/243; date: August 2, 2023), and from the hospital director (Ref nos.:119922 and 10450; date: August 6, 2023).

The sample size was calculated using OpenEpi version 3.01, assuming the prevalence of poor QoL among MS patients to be 50% (as no prior studies had been conducted in Jordan) and setting a type I error rate (α) at 0.05. The minimum calculated sample size was 292.

Sampling technique and data collection

A systematic random sampling method was employed based on hospital records, with a sampling interval of five; an interval of five was chosen to achieve the required sample size from the about 1400 patients with MS recorded in the hospital. Face-to-face interviews were conducted at the outpatient clinic waiting room during routine follow-up visit before the scheduled medical consultation.

A two-part structured questionnaire was used to elicit data. The first part of the questionnaire collected sociodemographic information (e.g., age gender, etc.) and medical history (e.g., chronic diseases and autoimmune conditions). In the second part, QoL was assessed using the validated Arabic version of the WHOQOL-BREF.[18] The WHOQOL-BREF has good reliability and contains 26 items across four domains: physical (7 items), psychological (6 items), social (3 items), and environmental (8 items). Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (very poor) to 5 (very good). Domain and overall scores were converted to a 0–100 scale following the WHOQOL-BREF scoring guidelines. In addition, each participant’s weight and height were measured to calculate the body mass index (BMI), expressed in kg/m2.

Data analysis

Data were entered into Excel and analyzed with SPSS version 27. Descriptive statistics included means, standard deviations (SDs), frequencies, and percentages; inferential statistics included t-tests, ANOVA with post hoc tests, Chi-square tests, odds ratios, correlations, reliability analysis (Cronbach’s alpha) and multivariable binary logistic regression. Significance was set at P < 0.05. QoL was categorized as poor (<60) or good (≥60) depending upon Silva et al. (2014).[23] This threshold was adopted to enhance the clinical interpretability of the results by identifying a distinct subgroup of vulnerable patients. To ensure data robustness, QoL was also analyzed as a continuous variable during the initial descriptive phase.

RESULTS

Demographic and clinical characteristics

A total of 300 Jordanian patients MS were included in the study. The mean age of the participants was 35.3 (±11.1) years (range: 18 to 92 years) and the mean BMI was 25.7 ± 5.0 kg/m2 (range: 15 to 52 kg/m2). More than two-thirds of the patients were female (68.7%), of whom 72% had a regular menstrual cycle. Most patients lived in the central region (88%), had a university degree (52.7%), were married (58.3%), had access to a caregiver at home (78.7%), were non-smokers (58.0%), and unemployed (67.3%). Among smokers, 65.9% had been smoking for >5 years, while among unemployed patients, 37.2% reported being unemployed due to MS. In addition, most patients had no non-autoimmune comorbidity (76.3%) and no other autoimmune disease (86.3%) [Table 1].

Quality of life scores

The WHOQOL-BREF in our study showed good overall internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha: 0.799). For the individual domains, the reliability coefficients ranged from 0.658 to 0.799. The overall mean QoL score was 57.2 ± 15.2, indicating a generally low level of QoL. The social and environmental domains had the highest mean scores (61.3 ± 20.9 and 60.2 ± 19.6, respectively), while physical and psychological domains had lower mean scores (49.7 ± 18 and 57.8 ± 18.4, respectively). From the 26 questionnaire items, the highest mean score (3.7 ± 1.2) was observed for both items “How satisfied are you with your personal relationships” and “How safe do you feel in your daily life.” Conversely, the lowest mean score (2.7 ± 1.2) was recorded for the item “To what extent do you feel that physical pain prevents you from doing what you need to do.” The mean QoL score was non-significantly higher among females (57.8 ± 14.7) than males (56.1 ± 16.3) (t = −0.905; P = 0.366) [Table 2]. In total, 167 (55.7%) and 133 (44.3%) patients were classified as having poor and good QoL, respectively.

Quality of life and sociodemographic variables

A statistically significant but weak negative correlation was observed between QoL and both age (r = −0.172, P = 0.003) and BMI (r = −0.155, P = 0.007). Additionally, a significant difference in mean QoL scores was found across the three education levels (F = 5.552, P = 0.004). Participants with a university degree reported the highest mean QoL score (59.5 ± 15.1), whereas those with a high school education or less reported the lowest (53.2 ± 15.3). Post hoc analysis revealed a significant difference between these two groups (P = 0.003). The mean QoL scores significantly increased with higher household income levels (F = 8.086, P = 0.001). Patients with a monthly income of <500 JD had significantly lower QoL scores than those with monthly incomes of 500–1000 JD (P = 0.01) and >1000 JD (P = 0.001). A significant positive correlation was also found between household income and QoL (r = 0.219, P < 0.001). No significant differences in mean QoL scores were found based on caregiver status (t = 1.923, P = 0.055) or employment status (t = −1.572, P = 0.117) [Table 3].

Patients with parental consanguinity reported significantly lower mean QoL scores (52.0 ± 15.0) than those without consanguinity (t = 2.417, P = 0.016). Similarly, patients with MS who smoked had significantly lower QoL scores (54.1 ± 16.4) than non-smokers (59.5 ± 13.9) (t = 2.983, P = 0.003). Although mean QoL scores tended to decline with longer smoking duration, the difference was not statistically significant (F = 1.040, P = 0.356), and no significant correlation was found between smoking duration and QoL (r = −0.137, P = 0.127) [Table 3]. No significant differences in QoL scores were found based on region of residence (F = 1.039, P = 0.372. Additionally, MS disease duration was not significantly associated with QoL [Table 3].

When assessed according to the category of QoL (i.e., good: ≥60; poor: <60), previously married individuals (widowed/divorced) had a significantly higher prevalence of poor QoL (84.6%) than unmarried and married participants (χ2 = 6.06, P = 0.048). Similarly, patients with parental consanguinity demonstrated a significantly higher rate of poor QoL (69.8%) than those without parental consanguinity (55.2%) (χ2 = 4.03, P = 0.044), and were nearly twice as likely to have poor QoL (OR = 1.88, 95% CI: 1.01–3.45). Patients who smoked had a significantly higher proportion of poor QoL (63.5%) compared to non-smokers (50%) (χ2 = 5.39, P = 0.02), and they were nearly twice at risk to have poor QoL (OR = 1.74, 95% CI: 1.08–2.77). Poor QoL was non-significantly more prevalent among residents from the southern region (63.3%; χ2 = 1.893, P = 0.639) and those who were unemployed (58.4%; χ2 = 1.893, P = 0.169) [Table 4].

Quality of life and clinical variables

A significant association was observed between the number of non-autoimmune comorbidities and QoL scores (F = 18.1, P < 0.001), with post hoc analysis confirming the result (P < 0.001). In contrast, the number of autoimmune diseases other than MS had no significant effect on QoL (F = 0.468, P = 0.627). [Table 3]. When assessed according to the category of QoL, a significant association was found between MS type and QoL, with the highest rate of poor QoL among those with the SPMS (74.1%) (χ2 = 20.610, P < 0.001). Poor QoL was non-significantly more prevalent among patients with a family history of MS (58.9%; χ2 = 0.248, P = 0.618, respectively). In addition, no significant association was observed between the nature of the first MS symptom and QoL classification (χ2 = 4.604, P = 0.200) [Table 4].

Multivariate binary logistic regression analysis

All variables that were significant in the univariable analysis were included in the multivariate binary logistic regression model. Multivariable logistic regression showed that smoking (odds ratio [OR] = 0.454, 95% CI: 0.236–0.871; P = 0.018), having one comorbidity (OR = 0.278, 95% CI: 0.108–0.714; P = 0.008), and SPMS (OR = 0.260, 95% CI: 0.084–0.798; P = 0.019) were independently associated with lower odds of higher QoL [Table 5].

DISCUSSION

This study revealed a generally low QoL among Jordanian patients with MS, with more than half of the cohort (55.7%) classified as having poor QoL. While participants scored highest in the social and environmental domains, they demonstrated the lowest scores in the physical and psychological dimensions. In the multivariable logistic regression, smoking, have one comorbidity, and SPMS were significant independent factors associated with lower QoL. In our study, most participants were female (68.7%), consistent with findings of a previous study on MS.[6] In general, MS globally is more prevalent in women than men.[24] The mean age of participants with MS was 35.3 ± 11.1 years, which aligns with those reported previously[67] and reflects the well-documented trend of MS predominantly affecting young adults, a factor that can significantly influence individual productivity and broader economic outcomes.[25]

Participants demonstrated a low overall QOL score (57.2 ± 15.2), supporting similar findings from studies conducted in Australia,[26] the United States,[19] and the West Bank, Palestine.[27] Reduced QoL among individuals with MS is commonly associated with functional limitations, disruption of social and familial roles, and decreased engagement in daily activities. In addition, the findings that physical and psychological domains yielded the lowest scores in our study is consistent with global patterns. In a systematic review, Gil-González et al.[2] reported that these dimensions are typically the most affected in MS patients regardless of geographical location, as they directly capture the burden of functional impairment and emotional distress associated with the disease.

The relatively moderate scores observed in the social domain might reflect the robust family-based caregiving networks that characterize Jordanian society.[22] In our local context, the extended family often acts as a primary support system, potentially buffering patients against the social isolation and psychological distress that frequently accompany MS in more individualistic cultures.

Multiple factors influence QoL in individuals with MS, including disease characteristics and sociodemographic variables. In the present study, participants with higher education levels reported significantly better QoL, consistent with previous finding.[28] This may be attributed to increased disease awareness, greater adherence to treatment regimens, and more frequent engagement with healthcare services among educated patients.[29]

The current study found no significant association between employment status and QoL, which contrasts previous finding.[30] Nonetheless, our study found that employed MS patients had a higher mean QoL score and 50% had “good” QoL, which is consistent with finding from Kuwait, where employed/previously employed MS patients reported higher QoL than those who had never worked.[31] Similarly, a systematic review found that that maintaining activity, particularly through employment, acts as a protective factor against poor QoL.[2] However, in our study, about two-thirds of the unemployed patients stated that their unemployment was unrelated to MS. This may be partially explained by the demographic profile of the sample, in which two-thirds of participants were female. In the Jordanian cultural context, women are less likely to participate in the formal workforce.[22] In addition, in the current study, about one-third and one-fourth of participants had low educational level and were aged <25 years, respectively, and thus potentially had limited opportunity/eligibility for employment.

Among unemployed MS patients, 58.4% had poor QoL, potentially due to lower household income, as a significant association was also noted between lower income and lower QoL, and higher income and better QoL. This is consistent with the literature,[3132] suggesting that higher income is associated with better QoL in MS patients, possibly because of better capacity to manage the high financial burden of care associated with MS.[33] Interestingly, income was not a significant independent predictor of QoL in the multivariable analysis. This could be explained by the universal health insurance coverage available to most MS patients in Jordan, which facilitates equitable access to essential disease management. Such a system likely mitigates the impact of socioeconomic disparities, ensuring that clinical management remains standardized regardless of a patient’s financial status.

Notably, participants who were previously married (widowed/divorced) reported significantly poorer QoL compared to their married or never-married counterparts. This could be related to reduced social and familial support, as well as limited access to caregivers, which are protective factors for QoL among individuals with chronic conditions.[34] Non-smokers had significantly higher mean QoL scores than smokers, which aligns with previous finding,[35] wherein MS patients who smoked had lower QoL and more severe symptoms, such as fatigue. The lower QoL observed in smokers could be associated with a higher burden of comorbid conditions, such as cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes mellitus and depression.[363738] Consistent with this, we also found that having one comorbidity was a significant independent risk factor of lower QoL in MS patients.

Patients with a disease duration >10 years (n = 23) demonstrated significantly higher rates of poor QoL. This finding could be related to the cumulative effects of disease progression and the associated increase in disability over time.[39] Age also appeared to negatively correlate with QoL in our study, further reinforcing the impact of long-term disease burden on patients’ well-being.

Our findings showed that the highest rate of poor QOL was observed among patients diagnosed with SPMS, with 74.1% reporting poor QoL. This is consistent with Gross et al. (2017),[40] who noted that individuals with SPMS often experience more severe neurological impairment, longer hospital stays, and increased healthcare needs, all of which can negatively impact QoL. Our study found that patients with the SPMS subtype reported the lowest QoL scores. While SPMS is typically associated with older age and longer disease duration, our regression model confirmed that the SPMS subtype was independently associated with significantly lower QoL.

No significant association was found between family history of MS and QoL, in agreement with previous finding.[41] This may be due to greater disease awareness and preparedness among individuals with a family history of MS, potentially mitigating its impact on their perceived QOL. We also found no significant differences in QoL based on region of residence. This may reflect the relative homogeneity of the population across regions in Jordan, as well as the centralized nature of MS care, with most patients receiving services from a national referral center. Of note, the variability in access to advanced disease-modifying therapies remains a pivotal factor in the Middle Eastern context. Disparities in insurance coverage and the high costs of newer therapies can impact long-term clinical outcomes and overall QoL,[39] a factor that warrants further detailed investigation in future studies.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The study was conducted at a single national referral center, and thus patients with milder disease who attend irregularly or seek private care may have been underrepresented, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, logistical challenges, particularly the geographic distance between our university and the central referral hospital in Amman, resulted in time constraints, which may have affected the recruitment process. Furthermore, this study did not account for physical disability or psychological symptoms such as fatigue, depression, and anxiety, which are known predictors of QoL. Future research should incorporate these multidimensional variables to provide a more comprehensive assessment.

CONCLUSION

This study found that patients living with multiple sclerosis (MS) in Jordan experience generally poor QoL, particularly in the physical and psychological domains. Factors independently associated with lower odds of higher QoL were smoking, having one comorbidity, and secondary progressive MS. Further studies can assess if comprehensive interventions targeting modifiable risk factors and comorbidities in patients with MS, smoking cessation programs and awareness campaigns and psychosocial and mental health services, improve the QoL of MS patients.
