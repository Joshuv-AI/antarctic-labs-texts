Ambulatory electrocardiogram-detected ST-segment elevation and ventricular tachycardia in coronary vasospasm

Introduction

Vasospastic angina (VSA) is an underrecognized cause of myocardial ischemia characterized by transient coronary artery spasm and a wide spectrum of clinical presentations.1 Symptoms may be atypical, intermittent, or nocturnal, often mimicking noncardiac conditions or other arrhythmogenic syndromes, which can delay diagnosis. When unrecognized, coronary vasospasm may precipitate myocardial infarction, malignant ventricular arrhythmias, syncope, or sudden cardiac arrest.1 Diagnosis can be challenging and may require provocative testing or demonstration of dynamic ischemic changes, while management focuses on trigger avoidance, risk factor modification, and vasodilator therapy.2 We report a case of recurrent syncope associated with transient ST-segment elevation and non-sustained polymorphic ventricular tachycardia captured on ambulatory monitoring, ultimately attributed to coronary vasospasm.

Case report

History of presentation

An African American woman in her 60s presented to the emergency department after her first witnessed syncopal episode. Shortly after midnight, she got out of bed to get a cool towel for her chronic burning chest discomfort and collapsed to the floor. She reported a long-standing history of intermittent nocturnal near-syncope associated with chest discomfort, palpitations, diaphoresis, and dyspnea. Her partner reported observing transient stiffness and unresponsiveness lasting approximately 60 seconds without convulsive activity. The patient rapidly returned to baseline mental status and sustained a minor right temporal scalp injury. There was no incontinence, tongue biting, or exposure to toxins.

Family history was notable for a twin sister with recurrent syncopal episodes similar to the patient’s presentation, another sister who died from myocardial infarction following coronary artery bypass grafting, her mother who died from complications of ischemic heart disease, and her father with an unspecified aneurysm. She reported consuming 2–3 alcoholic drinks daily, including several beers the evening before presentation. She worked remotely in an office-based administrative role.

Vital signs and physical examination were unremarkable except for a small, superficial abrasion to the right periorbital region. Initial evaluation, including troponin levels, serum ethanol, head computed tomography, and laboratory studies including potassium, magnesium, and calcium levels, was unremarkable. Her ischemic evaluation was essentially unremarkable; therefore, she was discharged home with the same regimen, with counseling to modify risk factors, including diet and lifestyle modifications, and outpatient follow-up schedules.

In the weeks following discharge, the patient developed progressively frequent episodes of burning chest pain accompanied by lightheadedness and transient stiffness, often triggered by hot showers and relieved with cold compresses. She remained fully conscious, and antacid therapy was ineffective. She was readmitted for further evaluation.

Medical history

She has a history of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and a 17-pack-year smoking history. Home medications included aspirin 81 mg, atorvastatin 10 mg, hydrochlorothiazide 12.5 mg, metoprolol succinate 25 mg daily, and sublingual nitroglycerin as needed.

Differential diagnosis

This patient presented with witnessed syncopal episodes with prior multiple episodes of near syncope associated with chest pain and dyspnea in the setting of risk factors, including being a postmenopausal female, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and smoking; therefore, coronary artery disease is high in the differential diagnosis. Other potential diagnoses included primary cardiac arrhythmias and inherited arrhythmogenic syndromes, including atrial arrhythmias, ventricular arrhythmias, Stokes–Adams attacks due to bradycardia, Brugada syndrome, long QT syndrome (LQTS), and other repolarization abnormalities. Given convincing symptoms, she was admitted for the workup, considering coronary artery disease unless proven otherwise, and to monitor for any arrhythmias while in the hospital with telemetry.

Investigations

Transthoracic echocardiography performed during admission revealed mildly reduced left ventricular systolic function with an ejection fraction of 45%–50%. A treadmill exercise stress test achieved 7.2 metabolic equivalents, with 1.5-mm ST-segment depressions in the inferior and lateral leads that rapidly normalized during recovery. The test was terminated after 6 minutes due to fatigue, without symptom reproduction. Her calculated Duke Treadmill Score was −1.5.

Given another presentation with persistent symptoms, she was readmitted for further workup. Coronary computed tomography angiography was performed, demonstrating mild (25%–49%) non-obstructive disease of the left anterior descending, left circumflex, and right coronary arteries. In the absence of obstructive coronary disease, a 48-hour ambulatory Holter monitor was obtained. It demonstrated transient ST-segment elevations followed by episodes of non-sustained polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (Figure 2), along with brief episodes of nocturnal Wenckebach atrioventricular block felt to be physiologic. Due to concerns of masked Brugada syndrome, based on precordial T-wave changes on the initial electrocardiogram (ECG) and transient ST-segment elevations during Holter monitoring, a repeat ECG was performed with high right precordial lead placement, but it did not show a diagnostic Brugada pattern. She was re-admitted for invasive coronary angiography, which demonstrated moderate proximal right coronary artery narrowing that resolved with intracoronary nitroglycerin (Figure 3), consistent with coronary vasospasm. Procainamide challenge testing for Brugada syndrome was subsequently performed and was negative. Acetylcholine provocation testing was not pursued due to the high clinical likelihood of coronary vasospasm and concern for provoking malignant ventricular arrhythmias in the setting of recent syncope and documented ventricular tachycardia.Figure 3Coronary angiogram showing severe vasospasm of the right coronary artery after engaging the artery, which subsequently resolved with intracoronary nitroglycerin administration.

Management

Given documented ventricular tachycardia associated with syncope, an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) was implanted. Metoprolol and hydrochlorothiazide were discontinued, and therapy was transitioned to diltiazem (titrated to 240 mg daily) and isosorbide mononitrate 30 mg daily.

She was also extensively counseled about diet, focusing on the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension eating plan, lifestyle modifications(including cessation of tobacco smoking), and avoidance of triggers for vasospastic episodes. Outcome and follow-up

At the 9-month follow-up, the patient reported marked symptomatic improvement with medication adherence, with only rare breakthrough episodes when doses were missed. Sublingual nitroglycerin effectively relieved symptoms during 1 episode. Device interrogation showed no recurrent ventricular arrhythmias. She successfully discontinued tobacco use and was counseled on strict medication compliance and avoidance of vasospastic triggers, including alcohol, cold exposure, emotional stress, caffeine, and illicit substances.

Discussion

VSA remains a diagnostic challenge because of its intermittent presentation and the frequent absence of objective findings outside of spontaneous episodes. To improve diagnostic consistency, the Coronary Vasomotion Disorders International Study Group established standardized criteria that emphasize nitrate-responsive angina, transient ischemic electrocardiographic changes during spontaneous episodes, and coronary artery spasm documentation.1 When spontaneous ischemic ECG changes are captured, such as transient ST-segment elevation during resting angina, a definitive diagnosis of VSA can be established without the need for provocative spasm testing. Provocative testing with intracoronary acetylcholine or ergonovine is most useful when spontaneous episodes are not documented, and diagnostic uncertainty remains, particularly in patients with rest angina and unobstructed coronary arteries. In patients with a high pre-test probability of VSA who demonstrate spontaneous ischemic ECG changes and associated ventricular arrhythmias or syncope, the diagnosis may be supported by clinical, electrocardiographic, and angiographic findings without the need for additional provocative testing.

The Japanese Coronary Spasm Association (JCSA) risk score is a clinically derived tool used to stratify the risk of major adverse cardiac events in patients with definite VSA.3 The score incorporates 7 variables, including out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, smoking, rest angina, significant organic coronary stenosis, multivessel spasm, ST-segment elevation during symptoms, and β-blocker use. In a large contemporary international registry, the JCSA score demonstrated consistent prognostic value in both Japanese and Caucasian patients.4 In our African American patient, the calculated JCSA score was 6, reflecting smoking, rest angina, ST-segment elevation during symptoms, and β-blocker use. This placed her in the high-risk category and was concordant with her presentation complicated by ventricular tachycardia and syncope, suggesting potential applicability beyond the predominantly Japanese derivation cohort despite limited external validation. The JCSA score should therefore be viewed as an adjunctive risk-stratification tool in patients with VSA rather than a standalone predictor of adverse clinical outcomes.

Management of VSA centers on targeted vasodilator therapy and aggressive risk factor modification.2,5 Calcium channel blockers (CCBs) inhibit coronary smooth muscle contraction and reduce anginal episodes and adverse cardiovascular events; both dihydropyridine and nondihydropyridine agents are effective, with combination therapy considered in refractory cases when hemodynamics permit. Short-acting nitrates are recommended for acute symptom relief. In contrast, long-acting nitrates may be added when symptoms persist despite CCB therapy, with attention to nitrate tolerance and the limited prognostic benefit of chronic use. Adjunctive therapies, such as statins, are recommended for their beneficial effects on endothelial function and cardiovascular risk reduction, alongside strict modification of precipitating factors, including smoking cessation, control of hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes, and avoidance of known triggers. β-Blockers are generally avoided due to the risk of exacerbating coronary vasospasm through unopposed α-adrenergic activity.2,5 In our patient, medication adjustments followed these principles: metoprolol and hydrochlorothiazide were discontinued, and therapy was transitioned to diltiazem with the addition of long-acting isosorbide mononitrate in addition to statin therapy. This approach led to marked symptomatic improvement and aligns with contemporary management of high-risk VSA.

Given the malignant arrhythmic manifestation in this case, the role of ICD therapy is an important consideration. Patients with VSA who experience ventricular arrhythmias or syncope represent a high-risk subgroup with an ongoing risk of recurrent, unpredictable, and potentially fatal events. Although aggressive risk factor modification and optimized vasodilator therapy with CCBs and nitrates can reduce ischemic episodes, ventricular arrhythmias may recur despite appropriate medical treatment. Observational data suggest that recurrent life-threatening arrhythmias occur in 19%–24% of patients with VSA, often without preceding ischemic symptoms, highlighting the limitations of medical therapy alone.6, 7, 8 Consistent with this risk profile, contemporary guideline statements note that ICD implantation may be considered in patients with coronary vasospasm who survive sudden cardiac arrest or experience sustained ventricular arrhythmias, acknowledging the potential for recurrence despite vasodilator therapy.9 In this patient, the decision to pursue ICD implantation was made through a shared decision-making process acknowledging the limited data surrounding secondary prevention of arrhythmias in VSA.9 Factors supporting ICD implantation included a long-standing history of resting near-syncopal anginal episodes, documented ventricular tachycardia temporally associated with transient ST-segment elevation, smoking history, β-blocker use, and concern for recurrent ventricular arrhythmia despite optimized medical therapy. A wearable cardioverter-defibrillator (WCD) was also considered a potential bridge strategy when assessing the response to vasodilator therapy. Current guideline statements acknowledge the role of WCDs in clinical scenarios where the arrhythmogenic substrate may be transient or reversible, including during optimization of medical therapy.9 However, given the unpredictable nature of recurrent arrhythmias in VSA despite medical treatment, permanent ICD implantation was ultimately favored over a temporary WCD strategy in this case. With continued vasodilator therapy, avoidance of vasospastic triggers, and ICD protection against sudden cardiac death, the long-term prognosis is favorable.

Beyond coronary vasospasm, the differential diagnosis in this case included additional arrhythmogenic conditions such as Brugada syndrome, LQTS, and other repolarization abnormalities. These conditions may mimic or coexist with VSA, and distinguishing among them is critical because the management substantially differs. Brugada syndrome was actively considered due to the patient’s recurrent syncope, precordial ECG abnormalities, transient ST-segment elevations associated with ambulatory ventricular arrhythmia, and family history of syncopal events. However, repeat ECG acquisition with high-right-precordial lead placement and subsequent procainamide challenge testing did not demonstrate a Brugada pattern. The QT interval was normal, whereas the corrected QT interval (QTc) was borderline prolonged on the recorded ECG. Ambulatory monitoring demonstrated an average QTcB of 433 milliseconds, with QTcB of >460 milliseconds during 8.33% of the recording period, although the significance of these findings remains uncertain. Nevertheless, the constellation of dynamic ST-segment elevations during syncopal episodes, nitrate-responsive angina, and multiple risk factors for coronary vasospasm favored ischemia-mediated ventricular arrhythmia secondary to coronary vasospasm.

Conclusion

This case highlights VSA as an important and potentially underrecognized cause of malignant ventricular arrhythmias and syncope. Ambulatory electrocardiographic monitoring was instrumental in establishing the diagnosis by capturing transient ischemic changes preceding ventricular tachycardia. Optimized vasodilator therapy, strict risk-factor modification, and ICD placement for secondary prevention represent a comprehensive strategy to reduce recurrent arrhythmic events and sudden cardiac death in high-risk patients.
