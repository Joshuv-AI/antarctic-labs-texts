Isolated abdominal aortic dissection: Key role of computed tomography in diagnosis and management, and the etiological dilemma (spontaneous vs iatrogenic)—case report
Authors: Safouane Lissane Eddine, Iliass Khlifi Taghzouti, Mounir Salek, Aberrahmane Ibenyahia, Mohamed Ayez, Soufiane Aberwil, Nabil Ziani, Said Adnor, Zakaria Chahbi, Soukaina Wakrim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIsolated infrarenal abdominal aortic dissection (IAAD) is a rare vascular condition confined to the infrarenal segment of the abdominal aorta, often presenting with nonspecific symptoms that make clinical diagnosis difficult. We report the case of a 67-year-old hypertensive man with a history of femoral coronary angioplasty and L4-L5 lumbar disk surgery who presented with intermittent lower-limb claudication. CT angiography demonstrated a strictly infrarenal aortic dissection at the level of the prior lumbar surgery, characterized by an intimal flap separating a preserved true lumen from a well-defined false lumen extending to the aortic bifurcation, associated with an aneurysmal dilatation and no suprarenal or iliac artery involvement. Multiplanar and 3-dimensional reconstructions were essential for precise morphological assessment, evaluation of distal perfusion, and therapeutic decision-making. This case highlights the atypical clinical presentation of IAAD and underscores the pivotal role of imaging in diagnosis and management planning, while suggesting that prior femoral coronary angioplasty may represent a contributing factor; early recognition is crucial to guide appropriate conservative or endovascular treatment and optimize patient outcomes.Keywords: CT angiography, Infrarenal aorta, Isolated abdominal aortic dissection, Vascular imaging

Introduction

Isolated abdominal aortic dissection (IAAD) is a rare condition, accounting for less than 2% of all aortic dissections [1]. The infrarenal form is characterized by an intimal tear confined to the infrarenal aortic segment, forming a true and false lumen without extension to the suprarenal aorta or common iliac arteries [2].

Patients with IAAD are often asymptomatic or present with nonspecific symptoms such as back or abdominal pain, making clinical diagnosis difficult [3]. Conventional imaging may suggest dissection but often fails to assess its full extent or the characteristics of the intimal flap.

Computed tomography (CT), particularly CT angiography, is the reference imaging modality for diagnosis and management of IAAD. It provides detailed visualization of the intimal flap, distinguishes the true from the false lumen, identifies mural thrombosis or calcifications, and detects potential complications such as aneurysmal dilation or impending rupture [4].

Our case is distinctive for the incidental discovery of a strictly infrarenal dissection confined to a short segment, with a well-demarcated false lumen and a preserved true lumen of normal caliber. This report underscores the rarity and frequently silent nature of IAAD and demonstrates the decisive contribution of CT imaging for accurate diagnosis and management planning, including risk assessment and therapeutic decision-making [5].

Case presentation

A 67-year-old man with well-controlled hypertension on dual therapy (angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor and calcium channel blocker) had a past history of femoral access coronary angioplasty 4 years prior to admission, with no peri-procedural complications, and lumbar disk herniation surgery at L4-L5 two years later. He had no history of diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, connective tissue disease, vasculitis, or known aortic pathology, was a nonsmoker, and reported no family history of aortic aneurysm, dissection, or premature cardiovascular disease

The patient presented with intermittent claudication of the lower limbs after walking more than 200 meters. These symptoms had been present for 16 months and had progressively worsened in frequency and intensity over the preceding 3 months.

On physical examination, blood pressure was 128/73 mmHg and heart rate 68 bpm. Peripheral pulses were symmetrical, and motor and sensory functions were intact. The abdomen was soft, with no palpable pulsatile mass. Laboratory investigations were unremarkable, including hemoglobin 14.2 g/dL (normal 13-17 g/dL), white blood cell count 6.8 × 10⁹/L (normal 4-10 × 10⁹/L), platelet count 245 × 10⁹/L (normal 150-400 × 10⁹/L), C-reactive protein 2 mg/L (normal <5 mg/L), serum creatinine 0.9 mg/dL (normal 0.7-1.2 mg/dL), with no evidence of inflammatory, infectious, or acute ischemic processes. Despite the absence of significant clinical or biological abnormalities, the persistence and progression of claudication prompted an abdominal and lower-limb CT angiography to investigate a vascular etiology—whether spontaneous or secondary to previous procedures.

CT angiography revealed a localized dissection of the infrarenal abdominal aorta at the L3-L4 level, associated with aneurysmal dilatation (Fig. 1). The thin intimal flap created a double lumen configuration. The true lumen, of preserved caliber and continuous with the aortic lumen, was well opacified, while the larger posterior false lumen, with its rounded outpouching mimicking a saccular aneurysm, enhanced synchronously and partially compressed the true lumen (Fig. 2, Fig. 3). The dissection extended for approximately 23 mm to the aortic bifurcation without crossing it. There was no contrast stasis, mural hematoma, rupture, or complicated calcification.

Axial aortic CT showing the true lumen (T), false lumen (F), and intimal flap (green arrow).

Sagittal aortic CT showing the true lumen (T), false lumen (F), and intimal flap (green arrow).

The dissection remained strictly infrarenal, with no proximal extension to the suprarenal aorta or distal involvement of the common iliac arteries. The aorta exhibited diffuse medial calcification without significant stenosis. Distal arterial perfusion was preserved, with homogeneous and symmetric enhancement of both lower limbs (Fig. 4). A complementary thoracic CT angiogram excluded additional dissection.

3D reconstruction from aortic CT angiography showing diffuse medial calcifications of the aorta and lower-limb arteries with preserved distal runoff, with the level of the aortic dissection indicated by the blue arrow.

Imaging was decisive for confirming the diagnosis of an atypical infrarenal dissection, allowing comprehensive morphological assessment including lumen configuration, flap localization, absence of acute complications, and preservation of distal perfusion—all critical for management decisions.

After multidisciplinary discussion, a conservative management strategy was adopted. This approach focused on strict blood pressure control, optimization of cardiovascular risk factors, and close clinical and imaging surveillance. Endovascular repair was considered a secondary option, to be pursued only in the event of symptom progression, aneurysmal enlargement, compromised distal perfusion, or development of complications.

At short-term follow-up, the patient remained hemodynamically stable, with no worsening of claudication or emergence of new symptoms. Follow-up imaging showed stability of the dissection without extension or aneurysmal progression (Fig. 5). A scheduled imaging control at 6 months is planned. This favorable outcome supports conservative management in selected cases of uncomplicated isolated infrarenal abdominal aortic dissection and underscores the central role of CT angiography in diagnosis, risk stratification, and longitudinal follow-up.

Discussion

Aortic dissection has an annual incidence estimated between 3 and 5 cases per 100,000 people [6], while the isolated abdominal form is exceptional, representing 1.7% of all aortic dissections and 4.2% of type B dissections [7]. It predominantly affects patients over 60 years old, often hypertensive and with a smoking history, sharing an epidemiological profile similar to type B dissections [8]. Recent studies confirm this rarity and the absence of specific risk factors beyond hypertension and smoking [9].

From a radiologic standpoint, multidetector CT angiography is the gold standard [10]. It allows detailed multiplanar and volumetric analysis, including axial slices, multiplanar reformations (MPR), maximum-intensity projections (MIP), and 3D volume renderings (VR). The key finding is an intimal flap separating a true and a false lumen. The true lumen is typically smaller, enhances earlier, and remains contiguous with the outer wall, whereas the false lumen is wider, enhances later, and may contain partial thrombus [10].

Partial thrombosis of the false lumen has major prognostic implications. In the prospective cohort by Wu et al. [11], a completely thrombosed false lumen was associated with improved survival, whereas a patent false lumen carried a higher risk of progression. VR and MIP reconstructions are particularly useful for delineating flap extension and relationships with collateral branches, while thin axial slices remain crucial to avoid diagnostic pitfalls—such as confusing dissection with mural thrombus, calcified atheroma, or a thrombosed aneurysm. Recognition of a continuous intimal flap and 2 communicating lumina is diagnostic of dissection [10].

In our case, the dissection was strictly infrarenal, with a spiral intimal flap extending to the aortic bifurcation without iliac involvement or complications. The atypical clinical presentation—intermittent claudication without abdominal or back pain—illustrates that IAAD may manifest with distal ischemic symptoms when iliac arteries are involved, which was not the case here. About one-third of IAADs are asymptomatic and discovered incidentally, while half present with abdominal or lumbar pain [12]. Claudication symptoms are rarely described, adding further uniqueness to this case.

Therapeutic management is generally well established: medical treatment with strict blood pressure control is recommended for uncomplicated forms, with close imaging surveillance [13]. Endovascular or surgical options are reserved for cases with persistent pain, visceral or peripheral ischemia, or patent false lumen. Contemporary series report high technical success rates with endovascular repair (EVAR) and low short-term morbidity and mortality [14]. In our patient, the absence of complications justified conservative treatment with close follow-up, consistent with current recommendations.

The possible link with prior femoral coronary angioplasty warrants further discussion. Iatrogenic aortic dissections secondary to femoral access during catheterization have been described [15], but they typically occur early—immediately or within weeks after the procedure—and most often involve the ascending aorta or arch (type A dissections). Purely abdominal dissections are exceedingly rare [15]. A delayed occurrence several years later, as in this case, is exceptional and unreported to date. The absence of abdominal aortic imaging following angioplasty makes it impossible to formally exclude this mechanism.

Moreover, the presence of a localized aneurysmal dilatation at the level of the dissection may indicate a structural weakness of the aortic wall, as aneurysmal degeneration is a recognized risk factor for spontaneous abdominal aortic dissection [16].

In contrast, lumbar disk surgery 2 years earlier appears even less plausible as a causal factor, as vascular complications from such procedures are rare, acute, and usually recognized intraoperatively.

Therefore, while an iatrogenic origin related to prior femoral angioplasty cannot be completely ruled out, it remains highly unlikely. Given the patient’s chronic cardiovascular risk factors, this IAAD is far more consistent with a spontaneous etiology.

Conclusion

This case highlights the crucial role of imaging—particularly CT angiography—in the diagnosis and management of IAAD. CT angiography confirms the diagnosis by visualizing the intimal flap and double-lumen configuration, assessing the extent of dissection, and evaluating false-lumen patency—key elements for therapeutic strategy.

Although rare, IAAD may occur spontaneously or secondary to endovascular procedures such as coronary angioplasty and should be considered in cases of unexplained lower-limb ischemic symptoms. Tailored management—whether conservative or endovascular—optimizes prognosis.

This observation reinforces established knowledge: IAAD predominantly affects long-standing hypertensive patients, conservative management with regular imaging follow-up is safe in uncomplicated cases, and endovascular repair remains an effective option for progressive or complicated dissections.
