Acute on Chronic Respiratory Event Score
Authors: Robert J Graham, Ryohei Shibata, Francisco Herrera Carillo, Luis Enriquez, Rebecca Steuart, Christopher J Russell, Lauren R Sorce, Christopher D Baker, Zhaozhong Zhu, Jonathan M Mansbach, MyTRaCh Investigators
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

Children with tracheostomy and home ventilation (HV) are at high risk of morbidity and mortality from acute respiratory infections (ARIs).1,2 American Thoracic Society guidelines have helped optimize the safety of children with HV at home.3 There remains, however, limited evidence to guide clinicians’ oversight for safe home management of ARIs and no systematic means of evaluating outpatient illness severity.4 Indeed, existing intensive care severity scores (eg, Pao2/Fio2, Spo2/Fio2 ratio calculations, ROX index, Oxygenation Index, Oxygen Saturation Index) are not applicable due to the (1) lack of laboratory assessments in the home (eg, blood gases) and (2) differences in oxygen delivery mechanisms between home (eg, liter flow) and hospital (eg, Fio2). Establishing an objective measure of ARI severity for children with HV would not only help stakeholders communicate about the severity of an ARI, but also gauge response to interventions and establish thresholds for presentation to acute care. The objective of this analysis was to develop an outpatient ARI severity score for children with tracheostomy and HV.

Methods

From March 2022 to October 2023, we enrolled children using transtracheal HV into a prospective, multicenter cohort study, the Multicenter Tracheostomy Research Collaboration (MyTRaCh), to examine ARI pathobiology. Caregivers collected serial tracheal aspirates and participated in scheduled interval and ARI-associated clinical reviews. We excluded children with cystic fibrosis, immunodeficiencies, primary ciliary dyskinesia, and those living in long-term care facilities due to the potential for different colonization patterns and triage thresholds. Site teams collected demographics and medical history through structured caregiver interviews and medical chart reviews. All sites had institutional review board approval (IRB-P00027933).

An ARI was defined as follows: (1) new respiratory symptoms (eg, rhinorrhea, cough) for > 24 hours, (2) increased tracheal secretions over baseline for > 24 hours, and (3) increased oxygen requirement over baseline or periprocedure (eg, suction). This definition aligns with the 2025 definition of ARI recently published by the BrighT STAR Respiratory Collaborative.5 Post-ARI caregiver interviews and medical record review confirmed ARI-associated clinical outcomes (ie, time to ARI resolution [primary outcome], emergency department visits, hospitalizations) and respiratory support changes.

Using the clinical expertise of MyTRaCh investigators and readily available home respiratory support variables, we developed the Acute on Chronic Respiratory Event Score (ACRES) (Fig 1). Based on clinical severity and thresholds of support safely provided at home, changes in ventilator settings from baseline were worth either 1 or 2 points. The variables and points assigned were vetted for face validity by the pulmonary, hospital medicine, and intensive care clinicians working as investigators at the 13 MyTRaCh sites. We determined the ACRES threshold that best differentiated low vs high severity ARI based on the maximum log rank statistic and clinically relevant change. We compared low- and high-severity ARIs using the log-rank test. Cox regression models were developed for sensitivity analyses. We conducted statistical analyses using R version 4.3.2 (R Foundation).

Results

Sixty-three participants met inclusion criteria for ACRES evaluation. The median age at enrollment was 5.8 years (interquartile range [IQR], 2.8-10.0 years), 60% were male, 81% were non-Hispanic White, and 76% had only or additional public insurance. The median age of tracheostomy placement was 4.8 months (IQR, 2.4-16.8 months). Of the 15 represented primary diagnosis categories, the top 3 were the following: (1) acquired or congenital central neurologic condition (n = 38, 60%), (2) neuromuscular disorders (n = 16, 25%), and (3) chronic lung disease of prematurity (n = 15, 24%).

Of the 122 ARIs, the median ACRES score was 2 (IQR, 2-4). There were 85 low-severity ARIs (70%) (ACRES, 0-3 points) and 37 high-severity ARIs (30%) (ACRES, ≥ 4 points). Compared with low-severity ARIs, children with high-severity ARIs were more likely to require an emergency department visit (36% vs 54%, P = .080) and were significantly more likely to require hospitalization (34% vs 56%, P = .021). Moreover, children with high severity had significantly longer time to ARI resolution (Fig 2). These findings were consistent across sensitivity analyses.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to use acute changes from baseline respiratory support, including duration of ventilation, oxygen provision, and ventilator settings, to create an outpatient ARI severity score—ACRES. There are implicit challenges of gauging acute illness severity when it is superimposed on chronic respiratory failure (CRF) requiring differing amounts of ventilator support. Our initial validation of ACRES established scoring thresholds, differentiating low- vs high-severity ARIs that were significantly associated with both hospitalization and longer time to ARI resolution. Although further validation will be required, ACRES successfully integrates baseline ventilator support and provides a much-needed objective outpatient ARI severity score for research standardization and assessment of this heterogeneous, high-risk population.

ARIs are the leading cause of hospitalization and death in children with tracheostomy and HV.1,2 Multiple recent consensus statements, including expert panel recommendations for pneumonia management in children with neurologic impairment,6 the BrighT STAR Respiratory Collaborative,5 and the 2025 American Thoracic Society guidelines on tracheostomy care,7 highlight the need for rigorous studies to help guide the management of ARIs in this high-risk population. As the first outpatient ARI severity of illness score for children with tracheostomy and HV, ACRES begins to address this knowledge gap.

Although the present study is limited by a small sample size, to our knowledge, MyTRaCh represents the largest study in this population. Although performance in condition-specific cohorts (eg, neuromuscular-derived CRF vs primary lung disease) is needed, the heterogeneity of underlying conditions and variability of baseline severity in the inception study and performance of ACRES with independent ARI management at each site speaks to the utility of this instrument. Longitudinal evaluation with serial ACRES assessments and integration of additional tools, including home end-tidal CO2, which is currently not standard care, may lead to further refinement of ACRES. Additional studies in adults with CRF could also be pursued.

Ultimately, ACRES provides a much-needed outpatient ARI severity score for this vulnerable, understudied population. ACRES holds potential as both a research reference point and clinical tool. Although establishing thresholds for in-person acute care evaluations for a child on HV should be individualized, incorporating factors such as distance, personnel and equipment resources, goals of care, and parent/provider preference, ACRES may help families and clinicians communicate about the severity of illness.8 Indeed, with further study, ACRES may be 1 component of a homecare early warning system, as used in hospital, and would complement other clinical assessment by families and home nursing.
