Tactile perception and tolerability thresholds of TMS characterized by intensity across locations and frequencies
Authors: Neketa Nesmith, Megan Senda, Yang Hou, Kirthin Dev, Austin M. Spitz, F. Andrew Kozel, Kevin A. Johnson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTranscranial Magnetic Stimulation (TMS) involves pulsed magnetic fields that pass through the scalp to stimulate the brain, with incidental stimulation to superficial nerves and muscles. This study characterizes the scalp sensations of TMS by location (over modified BeamF3 prefrontal, THREE-D prefrontal, right orbitofrontal, medial prefrontal, motor, and parietal cortical targets, as well as the knee) and by frequency (1 Hz, 10 Hz, or iTBS), with increasing levels of stimulation intensity.MethodsStudy participants (n = 18) each provided ratings for 7 locations, with 3 frequencies per location. We adapted quantitative sensory testing method of limits, where participants identified the sensory threshold, the annoyance threshold, and the tolerability threshold, for each trial as TMS intensity increased.ResultsFor location, sites distal to the facial nerves and muscle (Knee, P3, M1, mPFC) were more tolerable, followed by BeamF3, with the THREE-D and AF8 locations as least tolerable. For frequency, we found that 1 Hz was significantly more tolerable than 10 Hz and iTBS. iTBS was more annoying than 10 Hz but only marginally different in tolerability.ConclusionsThe sensation and tolerability of TMS is dependent on stimulation location, and frequency, with increasing stimulation intensity.SignificanceFrom a research perspective, the tactile sensations can be a problematic confound, particularly when stimulation approaches an unpleasant or painful level. Clinically, providers need data on adjustments to stimulation parameters to improve tolerability and efficacy. This study provides TMS researchers and clinicians with empirical data to inform respective endeavors.

Introduction

TMS involves pulsed magnetic fields that pass through the scalp to stimulate the brain, with incidental stimulation to superficial nerves and muscles (Hallett, 2007; Rossi et al., 2009). As the magnetic field decays exponentially from the TMS coil, energy impacting the scalp is greater than energy reaching the brain (Salinas et al., 2007). Researchers and clinicians focus on TMS brain effects, which vary with parameters including location, frequency, and intensity (Caulfield and Brown, 2022; Lefaucheur et al., 2020). However, the tactile sensations also vary with stimulation parameters (Kaur et al., 2019; Meteyard and Holmes, 2018; Peterchev et al., 2017). Individual anatomy adds variability related to positioning and the stimulation intensity set by motor threshold (MT) (Johnson et al., 2013). Experienced TMS users know that tolerability varies by scalp location (e.g., sites closer to facial muscles and cranial nerves are less tolerated), although a full understanding is limited by sparse data (Abler et al., 2005; Meteyard and Holmes, 2018). Experienced users of TMS also discuss that tolerability varies by frequency (e.g., 1 Hz is clearly more tolerable than 10 Hz, and 10 Hz may be more tolerable than intermittent theta-burst, iTBS), with only limited empirical data to support assumptions (Humaira et al., 2021; Kaur et al., 2019). Formal quantitative evaluations of multiple locations, frequencies, and intensities are a gap in the research literature. Given the significant implications for research and clinical applications of TMS, we developed methods and characterized tactile sensations associated with common scalp locations and stimulation frequencies.

Scalp sensations are a challenging confound for neuroscience research, particularly when stimulation is unpleasant or painful (Holmes and Meteyard, 2018). Researchers use TMS to probe brain function, including motor physiology and cognitive processing (Lisanby et al., 2000; Valero-Cabre et al., 2017). Painful peripheral sensations can alter corticomotor excitability and MT (Chowdhury et al., 2022; Rohel et al., 2021) and impact attention (Eccleston and Crombez, 1999; Gong et al., 2019; Meteyard and Holmes, 2018). Physiological measures, such as cardiac responses, are proposed as biomarkers of brain target engagement (Dijkstra et al., 2023; Iseger et al., 2021). However, noxious sensory stimuli impact cardiac responses (Battaglia et al., 2024). Sensations generally become a confounding variable when discomfort or pain differs between comparisons.

For clinical research, scalp sensations pose a challenge to establishing appropriate control conditions (Arana et al., 2008; Braga et al., 2021; Duecker and Sack, 2015). Sham TMS without tactile sensation risks unblinding participants, while a sham with sensation may have physiological effects that contribute to therapeutic benefit. Neuromodulation of cranial nerves can potentially treat neurologic and psychiatric disorders (Powell et al., 2023). Additionally, noxious stimulation may activate opioidergic systems with therapeutic implications (King et al., 2013; Williams et al., 2018). The THREE-D study established iTBS as equivalent to 10 Hz stimulation for treating depression (Blumberger et al., 2018). Notably, the study used a different mean location from the OPT-TMS study (Johnson et al., 2013), with a high rate of reported headaches. TMS clinical studies have not provided sufficient information about tactile discomfort (e.g., intensity, duration) to fully evaluate impact.

Clinically, scalp discomfort is a known side effect (Loo et al., 2008; O'Reardon et al., 2007; Rossi et al., 2009). Mitigating methods have not been implemented broadly (Borckardt et al., 2006b; Rossi et al., 2009). Fortunately, most patients acclimate quickly and tolerate a full course of treatment (Anderson et al., 2009; Borckardt et al., 2013; Humaira et al., 2021; Loo et al., 2008; O'Reardon et al., 2007). Acclimation could result from physiological adaptation, psychological reappraisal, reduced attention, or even affective brain effects (Borckardt et al., 2013). Clinicians employ methods to reduce discomfort (e.g., ramping up stimulation intensity gradually, adjusting treatment parameters, or using distraction). Empirical knowledge is needed to guide clinical decision making, balancing efficacy and tolerability.

This study aimed to systematically characterize the tolerability of TMS across clinically relevant scalp locations (over modified BeamF3 prefrontal, THREE-D prefrontal, right orbitofrontal, medial prefrontal, motor, and parietal cortical targets) and commonly used stimulation frequencies (1 Hz, 10 Hz, and iTBS), evaluated at increasing levels of stimulation intensity (machine output). Secondarily, we evaluated the knee as a tactile surrogate for safe TMS parameter testing. Based on cranial anatomy and prior experience, we hypothesized that TMS will be less tolerable at the THREE-D prefrontal and orbitofrontal locations relative to the modified BeamF3 prefrontal location. Based on experience and limited data, we hypothesized that iTBS will be less tolerable than 10 Hz.

Methods

Participants and procedures

We recruited volunteers, ages 18 years and over, from the local community. Participants first provided informed consent, as approved by the institutional review board. We administered a safety screening (Keel et al., 2001), collected self-report questionnaires, and conducted a brief medical history. Exclusion criteria covered contraindications for TMS and conditions that interfere with sensory testing. We registered the study with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06354686). This was an exploratory pilot study, and we did not perform a priori power calculations to estimate the required sample size.

Prior to sensory testing, we determined each participant's resting MT using the maximum-likelihood strategy (Borckardt et al., 2006a; Mishory et al., 2004) and visual observation of the right-hand. We individually searched for the M1 position and coil orientation (typically 45° relative to the midline) that produced the strongest digit movement in the right hand. We never exceeded the individual's 120% MT in subsequent testing for safety considerations (Chen et al., 1997; Rossi et al., 2009). We used a MagPro R30 TMS System (MagVenture A/S, Farum, Denmark) with a Cool-B70 figure-8 coil. On this system, the population average MT is 39% of the maximum machine output (internal data and manufacturer correspondence). For comparisons across different machines, we define this 39% level as 100% of the population average MT on the system (sMT). So, a setting of 20% (19.5 rounded up) on the study TMS system equates to 50% sMT (i.e. 50% of the population average MT). For clarification, we have provided a conversion table in Supplementary Material (1. Table of Intensity by Machine Output, realized di/dt, sMT and %MT).

After MT determination, we marked scalp locations on a fabric cap with neuronavigation registration. We then conducted sensory testing with each participant at 7 locations, with 3 frequencies per location (21 trials per participant). We used a hierarchical randomization design; considering order effects, possible local induced sensitivity, and efficiency. We randomly selected from two different location sequences, subsequently randomizing the three stimulation frequencies for each location, with details in Supplementary Material (2. Randomization Order). Testing typically occurred over a single session (full visit was approximately 3 h).

Location

We provide figures for coil placement in Supplementary Material (3. Testing Locations). Before testing scalp locations, we first performed sensory testing on the knee. Knee testing provided an opportunity for participants to acclimate to the sensations and rating procedures. We placed the coil just above the patella, with the handle down parallel with the lower leg.

For scalp locations, we registered each participant's head to the MNI template using Brainsight stereotaxic neuronagivation (Rogue Research Inc., Montreal, Quebec, Canada), based on a combination of anatomic and EEG landmarks. We then approximated the THREE-D MNI location (−38, 44, 26) (Blumberger et al., 2018) and recorded MNI coordinates for other locations tested. Participants wore a fabric cap with all target locations marked for testing.

The six scalp locations tested were the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex by the modified Beam method (“BeamF3”) (Mir-Moghtadaei et al., 2015), the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex by THREE-D coordinates (“THREE-D"), the right orbitofrontal cortex using Downer methodology (AF8) (Feffer et al., 2018), the medial prefrontal cortex found by EEG Fz convention (“mPFC”), the left primary motor cortex localized during MT determination (“M1”), the left parietal cortex using inverted modified Beam measurements (“P3”). For both BeamF3 and THREE-D, the coil was oriented 45° from midline, handle pointing posterior, following standard clinical convention for treating major depressive disorder. For AF8, the coil was oriented as referenced (Feffer et al., 2018). For mPFC, the coil was oriented along the midline, handle pointing posterior, similar to clinical orientation for treating obsessive-compulsive disorder. For M1, the coil was oriented parallel to the midline, handle pointing posterior (to match mPFC orientation and to reduce hand movement that may be a confounding distractor). For P3, the coil orientation was flipped from BeamF3, handle pointing anterior. We created two testing sequences for scalp locations, avoiding sequential testing of the two dorsolateral prefrontal sites, and we randomly assigned participants to a sequence.

Frequency

We tested three different frequencies at each location in randomized order. The 1 Hz train consisted of stimulation ON for 4 s, 1 pulse per second, then stimulation OFF for 11 s. The 10 Hz train consisted of stimulation ON for 4 s, 10 pulses per second, then stimulation OFF for 11 s. The iTBS train consisted of stimulation ON for 2 s, 5 triplet pulses per second (each pulse consisted of 3 pulses at 50 Hz), then stimulation off for 13 s.

Threshold testing

We utilized Quantitative Sensory Testing (QST) method of limits (Cruz-Almeida and Fillingim, 2014), with aspects of the McGill Pain Questionnaire (MPQ) (Melzack, 1975). The method of limits assesses thresholds (sensory or pain) by increasing the stimulus intensity until a threshold is reached. The MPQ contains qualitative descriptors that can be linked to the intensity of discomfort or pain. We focused on the evaluative domain, that increases through “annoying”, “troublesome”, “miserable”, “intense”, to “unbearable”. From clinical experience, we selected “annoying” and “troublesome/miserable” as appropriate levels to gauge discomfort and tolerability. We avoided “unbearable” given practical concerns of headache with repeated testing and ethical considerations.

In a single continuous trial, we used an increasing method of limits to establish a “sensory threshold", an “annoyance threshold", and a “tolerance threshold". In the single trial, we administered two trains of the assigned frequency at 30% sMT (machine output = 11%), then two trains at 40% sMT (machine output = 15%), and subsequently continued with two trains at each subsequent 10% sMT increment (machine output = 19%, 23%, 27%, etc.) until the tolerance threshold was reached (or 120% of the individual's motor threshold as a safety limit). We instructed participants to identify the sensory threshold as the first train where a tactile sensation was perceived (noting that tactile sensation should be differentiated from auditory perception). We instructed participants to identify the annoyance threshold, after the sensory threshold was identified, as the first train where the tactile sensation was perceived as annoying (descriptor based on the MPQ, Group 16). We instructed participants to identify the tolerability threshold, after the annoyance threshold was identified, as the first train where the tactile sensation was perceived as troublesome or miserable (descriptors based on the MPQ, Group 16). At the conclusion of each trial, participants also provided a numeric pain rating (0–10 scale, 0 being none and 10 being worst) and rated the sensation based on MPQ (adjectives from groups 1, 2, 4, 5, 8, 9, 12, 13, 16, 17, and 20).

Data analysis

We first examined the distributional properties of the dependent variables at each level of the within-subject factors. Skewness and kurtosis values were inspected to assess departures from normality. Consistent with recommended guidelines (±1.96); (Kim, 2013), all variables demonstrated acceptable normality, with only a few instances of moderate kurtosis (between 2 and 7). We used multilevel modeling (MLM; i.e., mixed effects model) to test our primary aims, given that MLM is robust to moderate deviations from normality and can appropriately account for correlated observations within participants.

MLM was used to evaluate effects of stimulation location (Knee, BeamF3, THREE-D, AF8, mPFC, M1, P3) and stimulation frequency (1 Hz, 10 Hz, iTBS) on sensory, annoyance, and tolerability threshold values. Repeated observations across stimulation conditions were nested within individuals, and random intercepts accounted for individual variability. Type III mixed-effects ANOVA with Wald χ2 tests was used to show the main effect of each independent variable. For significant omnibus effects, pairwise comparisons were conducted to identify specific differences between stimulation conditions, with Tukey's correction applied to control for multiple comparisons. Models were first estimated with both main effects and the Location × Frequency interaction term; because the interaction was not statistically significant across outcomes, final models included only the main effects. Multilevel models were estimated in R (RStudio) using lme4; Type III Wald χ2 tests were conducted with the car package, and Tukey-adjusted pairwise contrasts were obtained via emmeans.

In a secondary analysis, we calculated the proportion of participants that could tolerate stimulation at clinically relevant levels of 100% sMT and 120% sMT. We used a generalized estimating equations (GEE) analyses with a binomial distribution and logit link to assess tolerability as a function of stimulation location and frequency, computed in IBM SPSS Statistics version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). An independent working correlation structure was used.

Results

Participant characterization

Of 20 individuals who consented to participate in the study, 18 completed the procedures and included in the analysis (1 dropout due to scheduling, 1 was unable to complete due to discomfort).

The community sample had a broad age range from 18 to 82 years of age, with mean age of 34.8 years (SD = 21.6 years). There were 12 female and 6 male participants. Symptoms of depression and anxiety were overall minimal to mild (PHQ-9: range 0–16, mean = 2.8, SD = 4.4), (GAD-7: range 0–19, mean = 2.8, SD = 4.6). Most participants had no-to-little recent pain of significant impact (PROMIS Pain Interference Short Form 6b: range 6–18, mean = 7.8, SD = 3.4).

The mean scalp distance from BeamF3 to THREE-D as measured on a marked headcap was 3.7 cm (SD = 0.8 cm, min = 1.4, max = 5.1). The mean scalp MNI coordinates for BeamF3 were − 50.4, 28.2, 56.6 (SD x = 4.4, y = 6.3, z = 4.3), which we estimate correspond to a cortical location of −44, 22, 51. We estimate the scalp location of the THREE-D target as −44, 50, 32. The direct line distance between estimated scalp locations is 3.3 cm, compared to the measured 3.7 cm along the curved scalp.

The average MT for participants was 48.5% of maximum machine output (range 36–69, SD = 9.0). This study average MT is greater than the established system MT of 39% maximum machine output, so the converted study average is 124% sMT (range 92–177, SD = 23). This would indicate that the study average MT was 24.4% higher than that broader population average.

Annoyance thresholds

In 15.9% of all trials (60 of 378), stimulation was stopped at or near the participant's 120% motor-threshold safety limit before the annoyance threshold was reached. In these cases, the stopping value was treated as the annoyance threshold in analyses. Across locations, early stops were most common at the knee (15 of 60), P3 (15 of 60), M1 (12 of 60), and mPFC (9 of 60) sites. Early stops occurred most frequently during 1 Hz stimulation (36 of 60). Accordingly, annoyance thresholds may be modestly underestimated, particularly for 1 Hz stimulation.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate potential habituation, sensitization, or expectancy effects associated with repeated stimulation during the session. Multilevel models were re-estimated adjusting for actual trial order (1−21), randomized location-order sequence, and randomized frequency-order sequence. Results remained substantively unchanged. The main effects of stimulation frequency and location remained statistically significant for sensory thresholds (frequency: χ2(2) = 32.04, p < .001; location: χ2(6) = 72.63, p < .001), annoyance thresholds (frequency: χ2(2) = 134.44, p < .001; location: χ2(6) = 197.60, p < .001), and tolerability thresholds (frequency: χ2(2) = 93.74, p < .001; location: χ2(6) = 159.66, p < .001). Neither randomized location-order nor frequency-order sequence was significantly associated with any threshold outcome (all ps > 0.05). Trial order was not significantly associated with sensory (p = .096) or tolerability thresholds (p = .125), although a modest association emerged for annoyance thresholds (χ2(1) = 6.70, p = .010), suggesting slight changes in annoyance ratings over the course of the session. Overall, these findings indicate that the observed effects of stimulation frequency and location were robust to session progression and randomized stimulation order.

Discussion

The location of stimulation has a significant impact on the tolerability of TMS, due to cranial nerve and muscle involvement. The least tolerable stimulation sites were AF8 and THREE-D which are close to facial muscles and nerves, followed by BeamF3; with mPFC, M1, P3, and Knee collectively as most tolerable. Our findings support the hypothesis that TMS is less tolerable at the THREE-D prefrontal and orbitofrontal locations relative to the modified BeamF3 prefrontal location. Our results align with a study mapping 43 sites with single-pulse, fixed-intensity stimulation in the context of task performance (Meteyard and Holmes, 2018). However, our study tested multiple frequencies across a broad range of intensities, relevant to clinical interventions. Considering participant burden, we limited the number of sites tested and only tested one coil orientation per site. Research shows that coil orientation impacts tolerability (Tani et al., 2021), and our experience suggests the small adjustments in scalp location and coil angle may substantially improve patient comfort (especially if the coil is directly over a cranial nerve).

The frequency of stimulation also has a significant impact on the tolerability of TMS. Clinicians generally consider 1 Hz stimulation to be more tolerable than 10 Hz (Berlim et al., 2013). Our threshold results and qualitative ratings (MPQ) support this, aligning with a study testing 1 Hz and 10 Hz at three locations (Kaur et al., 2019). Clinician perspectives on 10 Hz versus iTBS are clouded by utilization of different stimulation intensities. Our prior experience and a secondary analysis of the THREE-D study suggest that 10 Hz may be slightly less painful than iTBS (Humaira et al., 2021). Our current study found that while iTBS is more annoying than 10 Hz stimulation, the tolerability at therapeutic levels is marginally different in thresholds and qualitative ratings (MPQ). We did not find strong support for our hypothesis that TMS theta burst is less tolerable than 10 Hz, although we did not test at train durations equivalent to therapeutic sessions (e.g. 120% MT iTBS ≥3 min) (Blumberger et al., 2018). To our knowledge, this is the first study to directly compare 1 Hz, 10 Hz and iTBS within-individuals. Considering participant burden, we did not test 5 Hz or other frequencies. When 10 Hz is not tolerable, some clinicians consider 5 Hz as an alternative (Philip et al., 2015). Novel frequency patterns that produce desired brain effects, while improving the sensory experience, is an opportunity for future development.

We characterized the overall mean sensory threshold as 40.2% sMT with a relatively low amount of variability. Similar to MT as a reference point for brain stimulation, sensory threshold may be a reference point for peripheral interventions, linked to clear biological effects on the superficial muscle and nerves. Peripheral magnetic stimulation may be used to treat pain (Bedder and Parker, 2023; Leung et al., 2014), and thus sensory thresholds could inform this application. Peripheral stimulation of cranial nerves is emerging for psychiatric conditions (Powell et al., 2023), and reports of low intensity TMS may have relatively more sensory impact than brain effects (Makale et al., 2023).

We characterized the overall mean annoyance threshold as 87.6% sMT with considerable variability. The annoyance threshold likely has the greatest relevance for studies where attention is important. We infer that annoying sensory stimulation represents a level that distracts from other sensory and cognitive processes. We found effects of both location and frequency on the annoyance threshold. Studies comparing location and frequency, especially above 90% sMT, may be confounded by tactile components of TMS.

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, some testing was terminated early due to safety guidelines. As a result, group means likely underestimate true tolerability thresholds, particularly for conditions in which participants reached the safety ceiling before reporting intolerability. Second, we tested only two trains before adjusting to a higher intensity. Within a longer session, repeated stimulation could become more tolerable with adaption or more troublesome with sensitization. A caveat of our methodology is that we defined tolerability from the McGill Pain scale anchors of “troublesome or miserable” on an item that extends up to “unbearable”. We selected the lower valence for relevance in real-world situations. Tolerance can vary based on perceived benefits or appraisal of harm. For instance, the motivation to tolerate discomfort may be lower for a healthy research participant relative to a patient seeking treatment for a disabling condition. Of note, our population had a higher MT (48.5% of maximum machine output) than the manufacturer provided MT (39%) used to calculate sMT, and we have also seen a higher MT (46.9%) in a separate study on mental health conditions on the same device (Senda et al., 2023). The difference may result from MT methodology or from population differences. This does not impact relative conclusions but could impact empirical values generalized to other populations. We only tested one coil type, and sensations may vary with the broader range of field characteristics (Deng et al., 2013).

Conclusions

This study quantifies the impact of location and frequency, with increasing stimulation intensity, on the sensation and tolerability of TMS. Stimulation becomes less tolerable at near facial muscles and nerves (i.e., prefrontal locations). Low-frequency stimulation at 1 Hz is much more tolerable than 10 Hz and iTBS, while the tolerability of 10 Hz over iTBS is marginal. Researchers should consider the impact of sensation and tolerability on study designs. Perceived sensory thresholds on the scalp can provide a rough estimate of fields strengths needed to induce neuronal effects, and qualitative descriptions may help understand the types of sensory fibers activated. Sharp, tingling pain may link to fast-conducting A-delta fiber activation, whereas dull or slower throbbing pain may link to slow C-fiber mediated nociception (Basbaum et al., 2009). The knee may be a safe, tactile surrogate for some parameter testing (e.g. no seizure risk or brain effects). Clinicians should be aware of the impact of location, frequency, and stimulation intensity on treatment tolerability. Clinical guidelines recommend a “ramp-up” of stimulation to improve tolerability as patients acclimate to stimulation (Trapp et al., 2025), and our data provides benchmark levels to start stimulation. When a patient is unable to tolerate a specific parameter set, the clinician should be well-versed in possible adjustments to improve tolerability in conjunction with any data regarding the impact to efficacy. We acknowledge the limitation of a single-session study in generally healthy individuals, and there is a need for additional data to further inform research and clinical practice.
