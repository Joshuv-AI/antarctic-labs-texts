Clinical characteristics of nontuberculous mycobacterial infections among people with presumptive tuberculosis in India: a multicenter study (2021–2025)
Authors: Surendra Kumar Sharma, Vishwanath Upadhyay, Alladi Mohan, Ravindra Kumar Dewan, Rajesh Solanki, Kamal Kishore Chopra, Babaji Ghewade, Digambar Behera, Ravindra Mohan Pandey, Ajoy Kumar Verma, Ashish Ranjan, Raj Narayan Yadav, Kaushal Kumar Dwivedi, Pranav Patel, Rangineni Jayaprada, Kusum Sharma, Jay Bhanushali, Dhruminkishan Prajapati, Mahmud Hanif, Monica Patel, Manjula Singh, Seyed Ehtesham Hasnain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundDisease caused by nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) is often misdiagnosed as tuberculosis (TB) in India. Comprehensive studies on NTM are lacking, therefore, the present study aimed to describe clinical features, underlying conditions, species distribution, and treatment outcomes of NTM disease among people with presumptive TB (individuals with symptoms suggestive of TB) in India.MethodsThis prospective study (2021–2025) was conducted across seven tertiary care centres in India. People older than or equal to 12 years, irrespective of their human immunodeficiency virus status, diagnosed with NTM disease were included. Individuals with concurrent TB were excluded. Participants were recruited consecutively from individuals with presumptive pulmonary TB (PTB) and extrapulmonary TB at participating centres. Clinical, radiological, and laboratory data were collected from study centres under supervision of dedicated clinicians. Line probe assay (LPA) and Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) were used for NTM diagnosis. Recent guideline-based treatment (GBT) was administered to people diagnosed with NTM disease. Main outcomes of this study included clinical and radiological characteristics, species distribution, and treatment outcomes of NTM pulmonary disease (NTM-PD) and extrapulmonary NTM (EP-NTM) disease.FindingsAmong 71,143 individuals with presumptive TB, 230 (0·32%) had NTM disease (191 pulmonary and 39 extrapulmonary). In NTM-PD, common underlying conditions included previous PTB (80·6%), malnutrition (47·1%), and vitamin D deficiency (43·5%). M. avium complex (25·7%), M. abscessus complex (24·3%), and M. kansasii (20·9%) were the most frequently isolated NTM species. LPA and WGS showed 97·6% concordance in diagnosing NTM. Of 155 individuals with NTM disease with complete treatment details, 137 (88·4%) completed therapy; mean culture conversion time was 94·3 days for NTM-PD. Poor treatment outcome (9 deaths, 9 lost to follow-up) was noted in 18/155 (11·6%) treated individuals.InterpretationNTM disease among people with presumptive TB is an emerging public health problem in India. Integrating rapid molecular diagnostic tests for early diagnosis and GBT into National Tuberculosis Programmes is essential to improve outcomes by reducing diagnostic and treatment delays.FundingIndian Council of Medical Research.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Before this study, PubMed, Scopus, IndMed, Google Scholar, and the Cochrane Database were searched for reports published between Jan 1, 1981, and Jan 31, 2021, using terms related to nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM), pulmonary and extrapulmonary disease, India, risk factors, treatment outcomes, diagnosis, and species identification. Most studies from India and other high tuberculosis-burden settings were retrospective, single-centre, or laboratory-based, with limited clinical detail and little information on species-level distribution, diagnostic delay, or treatment outcomes.

Added value of this study

This is a prospective multicentre study from India to assess the proportion of NTM disease among people with presumptive TB, providing details on clinical and radiological profile, microbiological characteristics, species distribution, and treatment outcomes of both pulmonary and extrapulmonary nontuberculous mycobacterial disease. NTM disease proportion was 0·32% among 71,143 people with presumptive TB, pulmonary disease accounted for 83% of cases, and previous tuberculosis, malnutrition, and vitamin D deficiency were common in NTM-pulmonary disease. Diagnostic delay was substantial. Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC), Mycobacterium abscessus complex (MABC), and Mycobacterium kansasii were the predominant species isolated. Treatment was completed in 137 of 155 treated individuals, and the mean culture conversion time in NTM-pulmonary disease was 94·3 days. Subspecies-level characterisation of M. abscessus complex showed that line probe assay had 97·6% concordance with whole-genome sequencing.

Implications of all the available evidence

Taken together with existing literature, these findings show that NTM disease is an under-recognised cause of morbidity in high tuberculosis-burden settings and should be integrated more deliberately into diagnostic and treatment pathways. There is a need for earlier suspicion, species-level molecular diagnosis, guideline-based therapy, and stronger laboratory capacity within National Tuberculosis Programmes. Future research should try to evaluate direct molecular testing from clinical specimens, species-specific treatment outcomes, long-term relapse, resistance patterns, and the feasibility of national NTM surveillance and registry systems.

Introduction

Despite the ubiquitous presence of nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) in our environment, diseases caused by these organisms are not commonly encountered.1,2 NTM are generally considered to be less virulent than Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb), nevertheless, NTM can cause disease in both immunocompromised and immunocompetent patients.2, 3, 4 While cystic fibrosis, bronchiectasis, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) are important risk factors for the development of NTM pulmonary disease (NTM-PD) in Western and European countries,3 lung damage due to past history of pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) is an important associated condition for development of NTM-PD in High Burden Countries (HBC) for TB.5 True prevalence of NTM disease in HBC remains uncertain.5 In India, where a large proportion of population has lung tissue damage following PTB, NTM disease may represent an underdiagnosed cause of chronic pulmonary illness.5

In India, where considerable attention and resources are being dedicated for TB control or elimination,2 people with NTM disease are often misdiagnosed and treated as TB. Furthermore, the treatment of NTM disease is challenging because of the wide diversity of NTM species, each with distinct virulence, pathogenic potential, and antibiotic susceptibility patterns. In contrast to PTB, which is managed with standardised short-course regimens, NTM-PD requires species-specific, prolonged, multidrug-therapy. The treatment duration for extrapulmonary NTM disease (EP-NTM) is generally shorter than that for NTM-PD. Most of the previously published studies from India are mostly single-centre, retrospective or laboratory-based and often focus on either pulmonary or extrapulmonary isolates.5, 6, 7 Studies describing clinical characteristics of isolated NTM species/subspecies, including clinical and radiological features, risk factors, and details of treatment outcomes are lacking.5, 6, 7

This represents an important research gap, where NTM disease is frequently misdiagnosed as TB because of overlapping clinical and radiological features and acid-fast smear positivity, leading to delays in diagnosis and institution of appropriate treatment.2,5 Better understanding of the burden, species distribution, and clinical profile of NTM disease is needed to improve recognition and management in routine practice.6 This present study was conducted to determine the proportion of NTM disease among people with presumptive TB, and to summarise underlying factors, clinical and radiological features, geographical distribution, and outcome of guideline-based multidrug treatment regimens8, 9, 10 among people with NTM-PD and extrapulmonary NTM disease.11

Methods

This prospective observational study was conducted in multiple tertiary care centres across India from January 2021 to April 2025; the last patient was recruited in March 2024, and treatment follow-up continued until April 2025 (Supplementary Figure S1). The study was conducted in 7 centres across India, New Delhi included 3 sites (New Delhi Tuberculosis [NDTB] Centre; National Institute of Tuberculosis and Respiratory Diseases [NIRTD]; Jamia Hamdard (Deemed to be University) [study co-ordination centre]); Other sites were Postgraduate Institute of Medical Education and Research (PGIMER), Chandigarh, BJ Medical College (BJMC), Ahmedabad, Jawahar Lal Nehru Medical College (JNMC), Datta Meghe Institute of Medical Sciences (DMIMS), Sawangi, Wardha, Maharashtra; Sri Venkateswara Institute of Medical Sciences (SVIMS), Tirupati, Andhra Pradesh. The molecular experiments were carried out at NITRD, New Delhi and Sharda University, Noida, Uttar Pradesh, which was the seventh site. The coordination site was Jamia Hamdard (Deemed to be University) in New Delhi, and participating centres were purposively selected from six geographical regions of India, considering the availability of diagnostic and treatment facilities and prior experience in managing people with NTM disease.

The study included all individuals with presumptive Pulmonary TB and presumptive extra-pulmonary TB (EPTB), older than or equal to 12 years, regardless of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) status, who had tested positive in NTM culture from at least two out of three separate expectorated sputum specimens obtained separately at least one week apart, revealing the same NTM species/subspecies.8, 9, 10

Presumptive PTB included individuals with symptoms such as cough ≥2 weeks, fever, weight loss, haemoptysis, or an abnormal chest radiograph.11 Presumptive EPTB included individuals with organ-specific signs and symptoms, and/or constitutional symptoms.12 Individuals were excluded if the first sputum sample was non-diagnostic, and then a second sputum sample was obtained one week later for repeat testing.8 Individuals with active TB and NTM co-infection, and who did not consent to participate in the study/unwilling to come for follow-up visits, were also excluded. Details on other variables like clinical presentation, nutritional status,13 radiographic features, laboratory findings, time-lapse between symptom onset and accessing medical advice, were comprehensively documented using a structured case-record proforma under the supervision of dedicated clinicians at each study centre. Diagnostic algorithm for including confirmed individuals with NTM disease is provided in Supplementary Figure S2.

Pulmonary and extrapulmonary specimens from people with presumptive-TB were processed at respective recruitment sites. Initially, all specimens underwent Xpert MTB/RIF (Cepheid, Sunnyvale, USA) or Truenat (Molbio Diagnostics Private Limited, Goa, India) testing per the manufacturer's instructions and protocols mandated by the National TB Elimination Programme (NTEP).14 Acid-fast bacilli (AFB) smears were prepared using the Ziehl-Neelsen method and the bacillary load was graded as per guidelines.11 Sputum aliquots were decontaminated using N-acetyl-l-cysteine-sodium hydroxide method and inoculated on Lowenstein-Jensen solid media and liquid culture using BACTEC MGIT 960 system (BD Biosciences, New Jersey, USA). Mycobacterial cultures were grown in MGIT tubes containing 7H9 medium supplemented with oleic acid-albumin-dextrose-catalase and polymyxin B, amphotericin B, nalidixic acid, trimethoprim, azlocillin (PANTA) (Becton Dickinson, New Jersey, USA). The immunochromatography assay ([ICA SD MPT64TB Ag kit, Standard Diagnostics, Gyeonggi-do, South Korea]) distinguished M. tuberculosis from NTM in positive cultures. NTM isolates were subjected to species identification. At first level, line probe assay (LPA) GenoType® CM/AS assay was performed, which is based on the 23S rRNA gene for species identification. Then, at second level, for drug-resistance testing, the NTM-DR kit was applied, which interrogates the erythromycin ribosomal methylase 41 (erm[41]) and rrl (23S rRNA) genes for macrolide resistance, and the rrs (16S rRNA) gene. The erm[41] gene analysis facilitates differentiation of M. abscessus subspecies. Then, at third level, WGS was utilised to evaluate the accuracy of LPA results for species/subspecies identification. Mycobacterium fortuitum and Mycobacterium senegalense were reported as separate species consistent with clinical reporting standards.15

Culture isolates negative on ICA were subjected to DNA extraction using the Genolyse kit (Hain Lifescience, Nehren, Germany), followed by species identification with GenoType common mycobacteria/additional species (CM/AS) kit version 2·0 (Hain Lifescience, Nehren, Germany), using PCR amplification and hybridisation to identify NTM species via colourimetric visualisation as per the manufacturer's instructions (Hain Lifescience NTMLPA brochure). Genotypic drug-susceptibility testing (DST) was done using GenoType NTM-DR assay (Hain Lifescience, Nehren, Germany) to detect species including M. avium complex (MAC), Mycobacterium chelonae, and the M. abscessus complex (MABC), and their resistance to aminoglycosides (kanamycin, amikacin, gentamicin) and macrolides (clarithromycin, azithromycin) through rrs and rrl gene mutations, respectively. The erm[41] gene was analysed for MABC macrolide resistance, following the manufacturer's instructions (Hain Lifescience NTM-DR LPA brochure). Phenotypic DST using the microdilution method (Sensititre™ SLOWMYCO2/RAPMYCO2 Susceptibility Testing Plate, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA) was performed selectively in people with poor treatment response, persistent culture positivity, relapse, or suspected treatment failure, where additional microbiological guidance was considered clinically necessary.

DNA for WGS was extracted using the bead-beating method with the FastPrep-24TM instrument (MP Biomedicals, California, USA). Mycobacterial cultures were heat-killed, pelleted, and lysed in lysis matrix-B tubes via bead beating. Supernatant DNA was purified with AMPure XP beads (Agencourt, Beckman Coulter, California, USA) and eluted in buffer (Qiagen, Venlo, Netherlands). DNA concentration was measured using a Qubit 2·0 fluorometer, normalised to 0·2 ng/μL. Libraries were prepared using the Nextera XT DNA Library Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, USA) with 14 PCR cycles, normalised to 4 nM, and pooled. The library was denatured, diluted to 12·5 pM with 1% PhiX control, and sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq instrument (Illumina, San Diego, USA). FASTQ files from Illumina MiSeq sequencing were analysed using Mykrobe software (Illumina, San Diego, USA) to identify SNPs and lineages. WGS served as a comprehensive tool to validate the accuracy of LPA, particularly in MAC and MABC identification.

Individuals fulfilling clinical, radiological, and microbiological diagnostic criteria were initiated on guideline-based treatment (GBT). Regimens were directly tailored according to the identified species and DST profile. People included in the study were administered GBT consisting of macrolide (usually azithromycin 500 mg once daily), ethambutol 25 mg/kg once daily, rifampicin 450 mg daily if body weight is < 50 kg and 500 mg if body weight is > 50 kg, isoniazid 300 mg daily along with pyridoxine 10 mg daily for 6 weeks; later treatment was modified based on treatment response. Amikacin 15 mg/kg/day daily was administered on alternate days for the initial 3–4 months.8, 9, 10 People infected with slowly growing mycobacteria were treated with macrolide- and ethambutol-based regimens, and those with rapidly growing mycobacteria received combinations including amikacin, linezolid, and clofazimine. People with NTM-PD and EP-NTM disease were followed up at the respective study centres at 1- or 2-month intervals, according to patient convenience. At each visit, clinical assessment, imaging as indicated, and relevant blood investigations were done for treatment monitoring and safety; additional visits were arranged earlier for worsening symptoms, adverse events, or treatment intolerance. In people with NTM-PD, sputum was collected every second month to assess smear and culture conversion. Individuals with EP-NTM disease underwent clinical and imaging assessments during monthly follow-up visits. These people were meticulously monitored for adverse events (AEs) and severe adverse events (SAEs) while they were receiving treatment.

Statistical analysis

Data were recorded using a structured proforma and managed using MS Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, USA), and all entries were double-checked. Descriptive statistics are presented. Continuous variables are summarised as mean (standard deviation [SD]). Categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages. A good outcome was defined as completion of treatment with clinical improvement, microbiological response (including sputum culture conversion in NTM-PD and site-specific clinical or radiological improvement in EP-NTM disease), and radiological improvement or resolution on follow-up imaging. Statistical analysis was carried out using Stata Statistical Software: Release 16. College Station (TX): StataCorp LLC; 2019.

Of the 230 NTM isolates, 126 (54·8%) were slowly growing mycobacteria, and 104 (45·2%) were rapidly growing mycobacteria (Table 4). Discussion

This prospective multicentre study, conducted between 2021 and 2025 across seven sites from northern, central, western, and southern regions of India, presents data on NTM disease among people with presumptive TB. The study also summarises underlying clinical conditions, microbiological, radiographic details, species distribution, and guideline-based treatment outcomes of NTM-PD and EP-NTM disease.

In the present study, 0·32% of the 71,143 people with presumptive TB had NTM disease. Previous Indian studies have reported a wide variation in the proportion of NTM disease among individuals with presumptive TB symptoms, ranging from 0·37%–32·1%.5, 6, 7,15,16 Observations from our study are consistent with findings reported from other Indian studies5,6,16 conducted in comparable settings. This contrasts with studies from other HBC like China (1·6%–56·9%), Vietnam (8·5%), Indonesia (15%), Iran (0·1%–72%), African nations (0·2%–28%), and Brazil (52·2%),16,17 where differences are likely related to study design, diagnostic criteria, and case ascertainment.16,17 Many earlier studies from India and across the globe reported only microbiological isolation of NTM without assessing clinical relevance or treatment outcomes, making interpretation difficult.16,17 The proportions of NTM-PD (83%) and EP-NTM (17%) in our study are comparable with previous Indian reports18 and recent data from the USA.19

In our study, the overall delay for the final diagnosis was 222 days. This is significantly longer than the 30–45 days reported in the studies from USA and South Korea.8, 9, 10,19 Delay in Indian settings can also be attributed to overlapping clinical-radiographic features with TB, a low index of suspicion for NTM disease, a lack of adequate laboratory facilities for NTM diagnosis, and a low threshold for administering empirical anti-TB treatment.20 Further, we observed the mean interval between NTM disease diagnosis and treatment initiation to be approximately two weeks, whereas studies from the USA and Japan have reported delays of 3–6 weeks.17 This can be attributed to the closely supervised research setting in the present study.

The demographic and clinical profile of people with NTM disease observed in this study also has practical implications. Most people with NTM disease were in the third to fifth decades of life, and male predominance was seen, similar to other HBC settings.21 In contrast, low TB-burden countries have reported a higher proportion of females with NTM disease, often with underlying structural lung disease.8, 9, 10 In our study, previous lung damage from TB was the most common associated condition, followed by malnutrition and vitamin D deficiency.22,23 This is clinically important because it suggests that in India, NTM disease commonly arises in a population with post-TB structural damage and nutritional vulnerability. The high frequency of cavitary disease and extensive radiographic involvement further supports the view that many individuals present late in the disease course. These findings are relevant for TB control programmes because people with persistent symptoms and abnormal imaging despite negative M. tuberculosis testing should prompt consideration of NTM disease. The species distribution in this study also provides useful diagnostic and public health information. MAC was the most common species, followed by MABC, M. kansasii, and M. fortuitum. Subspecies characterisation of MABC added further clinical value, particularly because M. abscessus subsp. abscessus showed clarithromycin resistance in one patient carrying the T28 sequevar of erm. The high concordance between LPA and WGS supports the use of molecular methods for rapid species identification in routine practice.24 In a high TB-burden country, where clinical overlap with TB is common, species-level identification is essential for choosing appropriate treatment and avoiding unnecessary anti-TB therapy.

Although the geographic variation in species distribution observed across centres is interesting, it should be interpreted cautiously. Differences in local climate, soil, and water exposure may influence environmental NTM ecology, but the present study was not designed to formally test ecological determinants. Therefore, these observations should be regarded as descriptive rather than causal. Further environmental and molecular epidemiological studies are needed to clarify these patterns.

Treatment duration was significantly longer in NTM-PD than in EP-NTM. Treatment completion was achieved in 137 of 155 people with NTM disease (88·4%), which was similar to that reported worldwide.17,25 The mean culture conversion time in the present study was 3 months compared with approximately 2 months reported from the USA,17,25 possibly due to a higher bacillary load in cavitary disease. In this study, 6·6% of individuals who were initiated on treatment died, which is lower than the 15%–30% mortality reported in studies from the USA and South Korea.17,25 This may reflect earlier treatment initiation, fewer comorbidities, and younger age at presentation. Deaths were more common in EP-NTM than in NTM-PD, possibly because of a greater comorbidity burden.

The strengths of present study include its multicentre design across diverse Indian regions, strict adherence to American Thoracic Society (ATS) diagnostic criteria,8,10 prospective collection of nutritional and comorbidity data, and species-level confirmation by WGS. This study has a few limitations. It was a facility-based study conducted among people with presumptive tuberculosis attending tertiary care centres, so the findings may not be fully generalisable to the wider community or to primary-care settings. Phenotypic DST was performed only in selected individuals with poor treatment response. In addition, the absence of long-term follow-up limited the assessment of relapse, and referral bias from tertiary care centres may have underrepresented milder cases. The lack of formal analysis for poor outcomes also limits causal inference, especially because the number of poor outcome events was small. Larger multicentre studies with longer follow-up are warranted to confirm these findings and improve generalisability.

In conclusion, this study shows that NTM disease is an under-recognised cause of morbidity among people with presumptive TB in India. The findings support earlier suspicion of NTM disease in people with persistent mycobacterial symptoms, use of species-level molecular diagnosis, and timely initiation of appropriate treatment. These observations have direct implications for clinical practice, tuberculosis control, and future policy planning within India's NTEP.
