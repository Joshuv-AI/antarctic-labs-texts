A Probable Culture‐Specific Disorder of Pathological Weight Gain: Psychocultural Inversion of Anorexia Nervosa in Southern Morocco, Mauritania, and the Sahel
Authors: Mounir Jaafari, Abdi Cheikh Mohamed Fadel, Ahmed Gourani, Rachid Aalouane, Ismail Rammouz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights We describe a young woman with a compulsive drive to gain weight, characterized by fear of weight loss, distorted body image, and deliberate use of weight‐inducing strategies.Her presentation reflects a pathological internalization of culturally valued corpulence, exceeding socially accepted behaviors and associated with psychological distress.This case may represent a probable culture‐specific disorder and a psychocultural inversion of anorexia nervosa, highlighting the need for culturally informed diagnostic frameworks.

To the Editor,

In several Saharan and Sahelian societies, including Southern Morocco and Mauritania, corpulence remains an esthetic and social ideal associated with prosperity, fertility, health, and marital desirability, and is often actively encouraged through dietary and pharmacological practices (1, 2). We present a case of a young woman whose compulsive pursuit of weight gain appears to reflect not just culturally congruent behavior, but a pathological internalization of local ideals. Her presentation may represent a probable culture‐specific disorder, as conceptualized within the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM‐5‐TR) framework of Cultural Concepts of Distress. Clinically, this case also evokes a psychocultural inversion of anorexia nervosa (AN), defined by an intense fear of weight loss, distorted body image, and maladaptive behaviors aimed at inducing obesity rather than thinness.

A 22‐year‐old woman from Tantan, in southern Morocco, was admitted for recurrent suicidal ideation despite an outwardly calm and composed affect. She lived with her parents and two brothers in a traditional family with a modest income. She was markedly obese (BMI = 44.1 kg/m2), well groomed, and appeared emotionally detached from her reported suffering. Her speech was coherent, affect incongruent, and there were no psychotic or mood features suggestive of major affective or psychotic disorders. She frequently changed psychiatrists and exhibited inconsistent treatment adherence.

She insisted on obtaining amitriptyline and olanzapine, explicitly citing their weight‐promoting effects. Her request raised suspicion of factitious disorder or malingering, particularly as she minimized the potential health consequences of obesity. On further exploration, she admitted to deliberately fabricating psychiatric symptoms to obtain psychotropic medications specifically for their appetite‐stimulating properties and potential to induce weight gain. She also reported long‐term use of corticosteroids, antihistamines, and hypercaloric diets, all aimed at increasing body mass. Her preoccupation with weight gain began in adolescence and intensified over time, becoming central to her self‐esteem and social identity.

In her sociocultural context, certain weight‐gain behaviors—such as taking antihistamines or consuming high‐calorie foods—are normalized and sometimes even encouraged. However, her feigning of psychiatric illness and her use of psychotropic medications to gain weight were not considered acceptable or typical by her family. Although she did not exhibit overt social conflict, she experienced inner distress related to her perception of her thinness, a strong desire to conform to cultural beauty ideals, and a fear that her inability to achieve a “desirable” physique would diminish her marriage prospects. She was convinced that gaining weight would increase her attractiveness and her chances of getting married, reflecting both cultural pressures and internalized motivations.

From a diagnostic perspective, although body dysmorphic disorder and other specified feeding or eating disorder (OSFED) were initially considered, this case differed significantly. Her behavior was not driven by a fear of physical deformity (as in body dysmorphic disorder), nor did it meet the criteria for AN or OSFED, where fear of weight gain and dietary restriction predominate. These characteristics do not correspond to the eating disorders listed in the DSM‐5‐TR. While AN involves a pursuit of thinness often linked to internalized values such as control, discipline, or self‐esteem, this case illustrates a psychocultural inversion: the internalization of local beauty standards that valorize obesity, leading to excessive and unhealthy behaviors In her case, the internal motivation revolved around achieving social status and femininity by becoming heavier, rather than thinner.

The phenomenon appears embedded in a broader cultural environment that valorizes corpulence. In Mauritania and parts of southern Morocco, the practice known as leblouh—the force‐feeding of girls to prepare them for marriage—remains culturally significant, linking body size to marital desirability and family honor (3). Contemporary variants increasingly involve the misuse of pharmacological agents, such as corticosteroids and psychotropic drugs, to achieve rapid weight gain (4). A recent study in Laayoune found that 90% of Sahrawi women had, at some point, wished to gain weight, highlighting intense sociocultural pressure toward corpulence (3). This pressure stands in stark contrast to the thinness ideal in Western societies, where AN reflects an internalization of values like control and restraint (5).

This culture‐specific expression of psychopathology underscores how sociocultural norms shape the content and direction of body‐image disturbances. Recognizing such culturally mediated disorders can help clinicians differentiate between culturally congruent behaviors and pathological manifestations, while avoiding the misapplication of Western diagnostic frameworks in non‐Western contexts. Although the cultural formulation interview—a DSM‐5‐TR tool for exploring patients' cultural perceptions of illness—was not formally used in this case, we plan to incorporate it into ongoing field and qualitative investigations in Guelmim, Laayoune, and Nouakchott (6). These studies aim to assess the prevalence, clinical patterns, and cultural underpinnings of similar presentations. While treatment should not seek to change cultural values per se, it must address individual distress and maladaptive behaviors in a culturally sensitive manner. This includes psychoeducation about health risks, support for patient insight, and involvement of family members who influence health decisions. A clearer understanding may eventually justify formal recognition of this probable culture‐specific disorder and guide the development of therapeutic frameworks adapted to the sociocultural realities of North‐West Africa.
