Biomimetic ferritin nanocages for synergistic co-delivery of metformin and rapamycin restore neurodevelopmental homeostasis in autism spectrum disorders
Authors: Yizhe Shen, Lele Yu, Liujiao Wang, Jilu Jin, Cheng Yu, Yuan Fan, Yue Lang, Huashan Xu, Byron C. Jones, Yishi Liu, Jiaying Wu, Siyuan Gao, Fuxue Chen, Shini Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a multifactorial neurodevelopmental disorder with limited treatment options, largely due to its complex etiology and the inadequate delivery of therapeutics to the central nervous system. Herein, we report a novel biomimetic nanocomposite, HFn@M/R, designed for the synergistic co-delivery of metformin (Met) and rapamycin (Rapa) to restore neurodevelopmental homeostasis in ASD. Heavy-chain ferritin (HFn) nanocages, produced via an Escherichia coli expression system, were employed as a dual-drug carrier owing to their high drug loading capacity and intrinsic blood-brain barrier permeability via transferrin receptor 1 targeting. Comprehensive physicochemical characterization confirmed structural integrity, optimal drug loading, and redox/pH-responsive release under pathological conditions. In neuronal models, HFn@M/R restored mitochondrial membrane potential, enhanced AMPK-CREB-BDNF signaling, and suppressed mTOR hyperactivation and autophagic blockade. In a valproic acid-induced rat model of ASD, HFn@M/R achieved robust brain accumulation, ameliorated behavioral deficits, and normalized hippocampal electroencephalogram patterns. Transcriptomic analyses further revealed that HFn@M/R modulated key neurodevelopmental, metabolic, and immune pathways, underscoring its capacity to orchestrate a multi-target therapeutic network. Collectively, our findings establish HFn@M/R as a promising precision nanomedicine platform for ASD treatment, with potential applicability to a broad range of neurodevelopmental and neuroinflammatory disorders.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03760-w.

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a complex neurodevelopmental condition characterized by social communication deficits, repetitive behaviors, and limited interests, with its prevalence showing a yearly increase [1, 2]. While genetic mutations and environmental factors contribute to its pathogenesis, the interplay among mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and neuroinflammation further exacerbates neuronal damage [3–8]. Current therapies, including behavioral interventions and symptom-specific medications such as risperidone and aripiprazole, offer limited efficacy against core ASD symptoms and often produce adverse effects [9, 10]. Thus, there is an urgent need for targeted therapies addressing the multifactorial etiology of ASD.

Rapamycin (Rapa) targets the mTOR signaling pathway, a serine/threonine kinase, a central regulator of protein synthesis, cell growth, and synaptic plasticity by integrating nutrient and growth signals [11, 12]. In ASD, aberrant hyperactivation of mTOR has been documented in both clinical cohorts and preclinical models [13–15]. Mechanistically, mTOR complex 1 (mTORC1) overactivation suppresses autophagic flux evidenced by elevated p62/SQSTM1 levels [16]. This autophagy deficiency not only exacerbates protein aggregation but also triggers TRAF6-dependent NF-κB signaling, linking mTOR dysregulation to neuroinflammation, a hallmark of ASD [17]. Rapa, a macrolide mTOR inhibitor, ameliorates these pathological cascades by restoring autophagic clearance and attenuating neuroinflammation [13]. In Tsc1-deficient mice, Rapa rescues social interaction deficits and reduces neuronal hyperexcitability, thus highlighting its therapeutic potential in ASD [18, 19]. Its clinical potential is hindered by poor aqueous solubility, systemic immunosuppression, and limited blood-brain barrier (BBB) penetration [19]. Given the high heterogeneity of ASD, treatment strategies relying on single-drug approaches that target specific molecular pathways often fail to achieve optimal therapeutic outcomes.

Complementing the mTOR inhibition of Rapa, metformin (Met), a first-line AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) activator, targets mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress which are the key contributors to ASD pathophysiology [20, 21]. In anxiety disorders, Met improves symptoms by activating AMPK in inhibitory neurons of the medial prefrontal cortex [22]. AMPK activation not only enhances mitochondrial biogenesis but also suppresses mTORC1 via regulatory-associated protein of TOR (Raptor) phosphorylation, creating a synergistic axis for autophagy induction [23]. Importantly, AMPK activation induces phosphorylation of cAMP response-element binding protein (CREB) which in turn drives the expression of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) [24–26]. Clinical studies demonstrate BDNF dysregulation in ASD patients, with preclinical models showing hippocampal neuroprotection upon BDNF administration [27, 28]. Given that AMPK-driven CREB activation not only restores BDNF signaling but also establishes regulatory crosstalk that balances mTOR-dependent proteostasis, concurrent targeting of AMPK-CREB-BDNF and mTOR pathways offers a two-pronged approach to restore neurodevelopmental homeostasis. Notably, research has shown that Met-Rapa co-treatment exhibits a synergistic protective effect against testicular torsion-detorsion-induced ischemia/reperfusion injury by significantly alleviating oxidative stress, inhibiting apoptosis, and improving histopathological damage [29]. Consequently, investigating the synergistic interactions between Met and Rapa across distinct neurobiological pathways may pave the way for an innovative therapeutic strategy that enhances the efficacy of ASD interventions while simultaneously reducing drug-related toxicity. Despite this synergy, systemic delivery of both drugs faces challenges, including rapid clearance and insufficient brain accumulation.

Biomimetic nanocarriers, such as heavy-chain ferritin (HFn), offer a breakthrough in central nervous system (CNS) drug delivery. The hollow nanocage of HFn enables high drug-loading capacity [30, 31]. Crucially, its H-subunit binds transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1), highly expressed on brain endothelial cells, facilitating receptor-mediated transcytosis across the BBB [32]. Fan et al. reported that HFn-loaded doxorubicin (Dox@HFn) achieves significant higher efficacy than free Dox, effectively suppressing glioblastoma growth [33]. Additionally, the intrinsic antioxidant properties of HFn mitigate neuroinflammation by sequestering free iron, a contributor to reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation in ASD [34]. Despite these advances, the potential of HFn in neurodevelopmental disorders remains untapped.

Herein, to address the urgent need for precision therapy in ASD, we engineered a novel biomimetic nanocomposite formulation (HFn@M/R) for multi-target synergistic treatment (Scheme 1). Specifically, we employed an Escherichia coli (E. coli) expression system to synthesize HFn as a delivery vector, which was co-loaded with Met and Rapa. The nanocomposite is engineered to bind the TfR1 on brain endothelial cells via its heavy-chain subunit, facilitating its traversal of the BBB and subsequent entry into neuronal cells. Following internalization, the acidic lysosomal environment triggers ferritin disassembly, thereby enabling the efficient release of both therapeutic agents. Met activates AMPK and the downstream cAMP signaling cascade, while Rapa inhibits mTOR to promote autophagy, resulting in complementary neuroprotective effects that collectively alleviate the core symptoms of ASD. This innovative strategy not only provides a promising platform for the precision treatment of ASD but also demonstrates potential applicability to other neurological disorders, thereby offering a robust theoretical and technical foundation for personalized intervention and precision medicine in ASD.

Result and discussion

Synthesis, characterization, and redox/pH-responsive drug release of HFn@M/R

HFn nanocages were expressed in E. coli and purified for dual-drug encapsulation. Both passive diffusion loading and pH-induced deconstruction methods were employed into consideration to evaluate the loading efficiency of HFn for Met and Rapa (Fig. 1A). The results demonstrated that pH-induced disassembly achieved superior drug loading (61 Met and 12 Rapa molecules per HFn) compared to passive diffusion (48 Met and 5 Rapa molecules) (Fig. S1A-C), with comparable protein recovery rates (Fig. S1D). Consequently, the pH-driven method was selected for further studies.

Structural integrity of HFn@M/R was confirmed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), which showed uniform spherical morphology for both HFn and HFn@M/R (Fig. 1B). Sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) confirmed a molecular weight of ~ 21 kDa for HFn and HFn@M/R (Fig. 1C), consistent with the reported size of HFn [35]. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) revealed a hydrodynamic diameter of 26 nm for HFn@M/R, slightly larger than HFn (23 nm) (Fig. 1D). Zeta potential measurements demonstrated strong electronegativity (− 42.33 ± 3.32 mV for HFn versus (vs.) − 31.10 ± 6.62 mV for HFn@M/R), ensuring colloidal stability (Fig. 1E). Circular dichroism (CD) spectra confirmed preserved α-helical secondary structures in HFn@M/R (Fig. 1F). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) identified carbon (C) (68.19% in HFn; 76.66% in HFn@M/R) and oxygen (O) (31.1% in HFn; 23.34% in HFn@M/R) as dominant elements (Fig. 1G). Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy validated drug encapsulation, with characteristic peaks for Met (C = NH at 1636 cm⁻¹) and Rapa (C-O-C at 1124 cm⁻¹) in HFn@M/R (Fig. 1H). UV-vis spectroscopy further confirmed dual-drug loading via absorption peaks at 234 nm (Met) and 278 nm (Rapa) in HFn@M/R (Fig. 1I). Drug-loading optimization studies identified a 1:2 Met: Rapa mass ratio as optimal, achieving 123 Met and 19 Rapa molecules per HFn while retaining 34% protein activity (Fig. 1J and Fig. S1E). At this drug concentration ratio, maximal loading (160 Met and 22 Rapa per nanocage) and refolding activity (46%) were achieved at an HFn concentration of 2 mg/mL (Fig. 1K and Fig. S1F). Furthermore, HFn@M/R exhibited excellent stability within 7 days (Fig. S2).

Previous studies have demonstrated that individuals with ASD exhibit significantly reduced antioxidant capacity in the brain, accompanied by markedly increased oxidative stress [36]. To investigate the drug release kinetics of HFn@M/R under pathophysiological conditions, phosphate buffer saline (PBS) containing 1 mM H₂O₂ and 0.5% Tween 80 were employed to simulate cerebral oxidative stress, while PBS buffers at pH 5.0 and 7.4 were implemented to mimic physiological environments. Met exhibited minimal release (14%) at pH 7.4 but accelerated release at pH 5.0 (61% cumulative release at 110 h) and under oxidative stress (86%) (Fig. 1L). Rapa displayed sustained release across conditions, with highest efficiency under oxidative stress (Fig. 1M). In summary, HFn@M/R demonstrated effective drug release in response to both pH variations and oxidative stress conditions, indicating its potential responsiveness to the pathological environments associated with ASD.

Biocompatibility and targeting mechanism of HFn@M/R

The biocompatibility and targeting efficacy of HFn@M/R were systematically evaluated. Cytotoxicity assays in human neuroblastoma cells (SH-SY5Y), mouse brain endothelial cells (bEnd.3), and rat microglial cells (GMI-R1) cells demonstrated above 85% viability across tested concentrations (0.1–10 µM), confirming minimal toxicity of both HFn and HFn@M/R (Fig. S3A, B). Hemolytic analysis revealed concentration-dependent erythrocyte damage by free Met and Rapa combinations, with hemolytic activity remaining below clinical safety thresholds (Fig. S4). Notably, HFn@M/R completely prevented drug-induced hemolysis, demonstrating protective function of protein shell (Fig. S4). The TfR1-mediated targeting mechanism of HFn was elucidated through structural and functional analyses. AlphaFold 3 modeling revealed that apical heavy-chain domains of HFn engage TfR1 via four key contact regions, enabling clathrin-dependent endocytosis (Fig. 2A). Western blot confirmed comparable TfR1 expression in SH-SY5Y and bEnd.3 cells (Fig. 2B, C). Flow cytometry (FCM) showed significant higher uptake of HFn-Cy5.5 than free Cy5.5 in SH-SY5Y cells (Fig. 2D, E), a trend mirrored in bEnd.3 cells (Fig. S5A, B). TfR1 antibody competition assays significantly reduced HFn-Cy5.5 internalization (Figure. 2 F and Figure. S5C), confirming receptor specificity. To assess BBB penetration, a transwell co-culture system using bEnd.3 brain endothelial cells in the upper chamber and SH-SY5Y neuronal cells in the lower chamber was established (Fig. 2G). HFn-Cy5.5 exhibited time-dependent accumulation in SH-SY5Y cells, achieving 3.3-fold higher fluorescence intensity than free Cy5.5 at 24 h (Fig. 2H, I and Fig. S6). Near-infrared imaging and quantitative region of interest (ROI) analysis revealed that HFn@ICG (indocyanine green), a fluorescent surrogate for HFn@M/R, achieved robust brain accumulation within 1 h post-injection, with radiance significantly higher than that of the free ICG group, peaking at 8–12 h and persisting for 24 h, whereas free ICG showed no detectable brain signal (Fig. 2J and Fig. S7). Collectively, these results establish HFn@M/R as a TfR1-driven, BBB-penetrant nanocarrier with favorable biosafety, confirming its potential as an effective carrier for CNS-targeted delivery.

In vitro therapeutic effects of HFn@M/R

To assess the neuroprotective effects of HFn@M/R, SH-SY5Y cells were treated with valproic acid (VPA), a well-established ASD model inducer linked to mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress [37, 38]. Dose-response assays identified 20 mM VPA as the optimal concentration, reducing cell viability by 50% (Fig. 3A). Co-treatment with HFn@M/R restored viability in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3B). Comparative studies revealed that HFn@M/R outperformed free Met (VPA + Met), Rapa (VPA + Rapa), or their combination (VPA + M&R), achieving higher cell viability (Fig. 3C). These findings align with previous reports indicating that Met mitigates Rapa-induced apoptosis, further supporting the efficacy of HFn@M/R in preserving neuronal cell viability against VPA-induced cytotoxicity [39].

High concentrations of VPA are known to induce mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress, which can disrupt energy metabolism, impair neural development, and exacerbate brain dysfunction [40, 41]. 5,5’,6,6’-Tetrachloro-1,1’,3,3’-tetraethylbenzimidazolylcarbocyanine iodide (JC-1) analysis revealed VPA-induced mitochondrial dysfunction through a 26.3% elevation in green fluorescence intensity, indicative of mitochondrial membrane potential depolarization (Fig. 3D, E). While free Met and Rapa showed partial mitochondrial membrane potential restoration, their combination achieved a bit higher attenuation of fluorescence signal (Fig. 3D, E). Notably, HFn@M/R treatment significantly reduced green fluorescence to levels comparable to the control group (Fig. 3D, E), indicating that HFn@M/R effectively restores mitochondrial membrane potential disrupted by VPA. This mitochondrial rescue was further confirmed through quantification of NADH dehydrogenase 1 (ND-1) encoded mtDNA copies, which returned to control level in HFn@M/R treated groups (Fig. 3F). These findings establish the unique capacity of HFn@M/R to counteract VPA-induced mitochondrial destabilization through coordinated membrane potential regulation and genomic maintenance.

Additionally, Western blot analysis indicated that VPA suppressed AMPK phosphorylation (p-AMPK/AMPK), CREB activation (p-CREB/CREB), and BDNF expression (Fig. 3G, H). While free Met or Rapa partially restored these markers, HFn@M/R nearly normalized p-AMPK, p-CREB, and BDNF to those of the control (Fig. 3G, H). Concurrently, VPA hyperactivated mTOR (elevated p-mTOR/mTOR) and accumulated p62 which inhibited autophagy, both reversed by HFn@M/R (Fig. 3I, J). The mTOR inhibition mediated by Rapa synergized with Met’s AMPK activation to enhance autophagic flux, as evidenced by reduced p62 levels. Collectively, these results demonstrate that HFn@M/R synergistically integrates mitochondrial stabilization, AMPK-mTOR pathway modulation, and autophagy restoration through its dual-drug cargo, offering a multifunctional therapeutic strategy to counteract VPA-induced neurotoxicity.

HFn@M/R attenuates oxidative stress, apoptosis, and neuroinflammation

Mitochondrial dysfunction triggers ROS overproduction that overwhelms cellular antioxidant defenses, initiating oxidative cascades. In VPA-treated SH-SY5Y cells, confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) images revealed intense ROS-associated green fluorescence (Fig. 4A), quantified by FCM as 1.8-fold elevation vs. controls (Fig. 4B, C). Pharmacological interventions demonstrated dose-dependent ROS attenuation, with Met achieving 8.0% reduction, Rapa 35.3%, M&R 37.1%, and HFn@M/R 46.6% decrease relative to VPA-treated cells (Fig. 4B, C). Mitochondrial permeability transition provokes cytochrome C release and intrinsic apoptosis activation [42]. Compared to VPA-treated cells, apoptosis levels were substantially reduced in cells co-treated with Met, M&R, or HFn@M/R, whereas Rapa alone failed to mitigate this effect (Fig. 4D, E). Notably, mitochondrial-derived DAMPs (mtDNA/ATP) orchestrated neuroinflammatory signaling through interleukin-6 (IL-6)/tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α)/nuclear transcription factor-κB (NF-κB) upregulation [43]. Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis in GMI-R1 cells confirmed VPA-induced cytokine upregulation in IL-6, TNF-α and NF-κB (Fig. 4F-H). While Met failed to suppress inflammation, Rapa-containing regimens displayed anti-inflammatory efficacy proportional to treatment complexity, with HFn@M/R achieving maximal cytokine reduction (Fig. 4F-H).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Met predominantly exerted anti-apoptotic effects by preserving mitochondrial integrity, whereas Rapa selectively targeted neuroinflammatory cascades through suppression of mtDNA/NF-κB-driven cytokine production. The synergistic interplay of these mechanisms became evident in combination therapies (M&R and HFn@M/R), which synergistically equilibrium redox level, attenuated both apoptosis and inflammation with amplified efficacy. Notably, the HFn-based co-delivery system further potentiated this synergy, likely through enhanced intracellular bioavailability and spatiotemporal coordination of drug actions.

Therapeutic efficacy of HFn@M/R in ASD rat models

Inspired by the excellent biocompatibility and therapeutic efficacy of HFn@M/R demonstrated in vitro, it’s in vivo therapeutic potential was further evaluated. Comprehensive systemic safety assessments, including complete blood counts (CBC), blood biochemistry (liver and kidney function markers), serum cytokine profiling (IL-6 and TNF-α), and histopathological analysis of major organs, revealed no evidence of hematopoietic toxicity, organ dysfunction, inflammatory response, or tissue damage following a 7-day treatment regimen with HFn@M/R, confirming their excellent biosafety profile (Fig. S8, S9 and S10).

ASD rats was modeled by administering VPA to pregnant rats on gestational day 12.5 [44]. Neurodevelopmental assessments of the offspring, including plane correction, cliff avoidance, negative geotaxis, and swimming ability, revealed that VPA-exposed offsprings exhibited significantly delayed performance compared to wild-type (WT) controls in all tests (Fig. S11). Post-weaning, male offspring were stratified into different groups (VPA, VPA + Met, VPA + Rapa, VPA + M&R, VPA + HFn@M/R) and subjected to a 7-day regimen (Fig. 5A).

Behavioral tests underscore the superior efficacy of HFn@M/R in mitigating ASD-like deficits in animal model. In the three-chamber social test, VPA-exposed rats exhibited pronounced social avoidance, which was reversed by HFn@M/R treatment to levels comparable to those of the WT group (Fig. 5B, C and Fig. S12A, B). Elevated plus maze and open field test revealed that HFn@M/R significantly reduced anxiety-like behaviors, increasing open-arm exploration time (Fig. 5D, E) and center-zone activity (Fig. 5F–H). Additionally, the Y-maze task demonstrated that HFn@M/R restored spontaneous alternation rates to 65.1%, thereby mitigating repetitive behaviors (Fig. 5I, J). Finally, Morris water maze trials further confirmed cognitive rescue, with HFn@M/R-treated rats locating the hidden platform more rapidly (Fig. S12C) and spending significantly more time in the target quadrant during probe trials compared to the VPA group (Fig. 5K–M).

The hippocampus is critically involved in learning, memory, and emotional regulation, with its pathological alterations being strongly implicated in ASD pathogenesis [45–47]. In vivo multi-channel electroencephalography (EEG) recordings were performed in the hippocampal CA3 region of ASD rats to characterize the neuromodulatory function of HFn@M/R. VPA-exposed rats exhibited elevated local field potential (LFP) amplitudes with heightened signal fluctuations (Fig. 5N), indicative of pathological hyperexcitability. HFn@M/R administration effectively suppressed these aberrant amplitudes to near-physiological levels. Spectral analysis revealed that VPA-exposed rats showed increased power density across δ (0.5–4 Hz), θ (4–8 Hz), α (8–12 Hz), β (12–30 Hz), and Hγ (30–100 Hz) bands compared to controls (Fig. 5O and Fig. S13). HFn@M/R treatment reversed these pathologic oscillations, achieving power reduction across all bands (Fig. 5O and Fig. S13). Crucially, total energy intensity in the CA3 region was restored (Fig. 5P), indicating that HFn@M/R treatment effectively normalizes abnormal EEG patterns and reduces neuronal hyperexcitability in the hippocampus of VPA-induced ASD rats. Collectively, the comprehensive evaluation of HFn@M/R in a VPA-induced ASD model demonstrates its excellent biocompatibility, effective brain targeting, and superior therapeutic efficacy in improving behavioral and cognitive deficits.

HFn@M/R alleviates hippocampal neuronal damage and neuroinflammation in ASD rat models

Pathological abnormalities in hippocampal neurons have been observed in patients with ASD, potentially linked to neuronal damage [48]. Building upon the behavioral and EEG improvements observed in ASD rats treated with HFn@M/R, effects on hippocampal neuronal integrity were further investigated. Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) staining revealed significant increase in apoptotic neurons in the VPA-exposed group compared to WT, which was reduced in HFn@M/R-treated rats (Fig. S14). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and Nissl staining confirmed severe neuronal damage in VPA rats, including cellular swelling, disorganized architecture, and hyperchromatic nuclei (Fig. S14). Treatment with HFn@M/R restored neuronal density and morphology to near-WT levels, outperforming free Met, Rapa, or their combination (Fig. S14). Western blot analysis further revealed marked reductions in the p-AMPK/AMPK and p-CREB/CREB ratios alongside decreased BDNF expression in the hippocampus of the VPA group (Fig. 6A, B), indicating suppressed AMPK-CREB-BDNF signaling cascades. Concurrently, the VPA group exhibited mTOR hyperactivation and accumulated p62 aggregates (Fig. 6C, D), reflecting impaired autophagic flux. Notably, HFn@M/R treatment reversed these molecular perturbations, normalizing p-AMPK, p-CREB while restoring BDNF expression (Fig. 6A, B). The intervention concurrently suppressed mTOR overactivation and reduced p62 accumulation, indicating recovered autophagy functionality (Fig. 6C, D). These findings, consistent with in vitro mechanistic studies, confirm that HFn@M/R exerts a dual regulatory effect on the AMPK and mTOR pathways, thereby mitigating ASD-related neuropathology.

Neuroinflammation plays a critical role in ASD pathogenesis, often involving the activation of glial cells in the brain [49–51]. Immunohistochemistry for Ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1 (Iba1, a microglial marker) and Glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP, an astrocytic marker) revealed a significant increase in activated microglia and astrocytes in the CA1, CA3, and dentate gyrus (DG) regions of the hippocampus in VPA-exposed rats (Fig. 6E-H). While treatment with free Met, Rapa, or their combination resulted in a modest reduction in glial activation, HFn@M/R markedly decreased the number of activated glial cells, indicating a substantial attenuation of neuroinflammation (Fig. 6E-H). Collectively, HFn@M/R mitigates hippocampal neurodegeneration by synergistically restoring AMPK metabolic signaling, inhibiting mTOR-driven pathology, and suppressing neuroinflammatory cascades.

Mechanism analysis of HFn@M/R

Transcriptomic analysis provided a comprehensive overview of gene expression alterations across various treatment conditions (Fig. 7A, B). In the VPA-treated group, distinct molecular signatures were observed, with 98 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) upregulated and 214 downregulated compared to WT group (Fig. S15A and Table S1). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment highlighted immune-related processes, including antigen processing and presentation and positive regulation of immune responses (Fig. S15B), while Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis identified critical pathways such as RGI-like receptor signaling and NOD-like receptor signaling (Fig. S15C), both implicated in neuroinflammatory regulation [52, 53]. These findings underscore the association of VPA exposure with inflammatory activation and immune modulation. Comparative analysis of Met and Rapa interventions relative to the VPA group revealed divergent mechanistic pathways (Fig. S16A). Genes specifically modulated by Met were enriched in cellular homeostasis and metabolic adaptation, with notable cAMP-dependent protein kinase activity suggestive of AMPK modulation under energy stress (Fig. S16B, C). In contrast, Rapa uniquely regulated pathways linked to PI3K-Akt signaling, reflecting feedback-driven reprogramming post-mTOR inhibition, alongside autophagy-apoptosis crosstalk driving metabolic remodeling (Fig. S16D-E). These transcriptomic alterations align with our experimental outcomes. Moreover, the combined intervention of Met and Rapa synergistically targeted neuroinflammation processes (Fig. S17A), with GO terms such as neutrophil chemotaxis and KEGG pathways include IL-17/TNF signaling indicating balanced immune regulation (Fig. S17B, C). Concurrently, the enrichment of Advanced Glycation End-products-Receptor for Advanced Glycation End-products (AGE-RAGE) signaling and fluid shear stress pathways suggested oxidative stress mitigation and endothelial protection, thereby supporting neurovascular homeostasis (Fig. S17C).

Notably, the VPA + HFn@M/R group displayed distinct transcriptional remodeling characterized by 36 upregulated and 23 downregulated DEGs (Fig. 7A-C and Table S2), encompassing neurotransmitter regulators (Tph1, Oxt), metabolic/oxidative stress modulators (Alas2, Acsm5), and neurodevelopmental element (Foxp2) (Fig. 7D), aligning with ASD-relevant pathologies [54–58]. Transcriptomic profiling of the VPA + HFn@M/R group revealed significant enrichment of DEGs in pathways critical for neurodevelopment, metabolic adaptation, and immune modulation (Fig. 7E-H). GO analysis highlighted key biological processes, including calcium-mediated signaling and negative regulation of amyloid-beta formation, which enhance synaptic plasticity and mitigate neurotoxic protein aggregation (Fig. 7E, F). Additionally, enrichment in peptide metabolic processes and regulation of T cell receptor signaling underscored the dual role of HFn@M/R in maintaining neuropeptide homeostasis and fine-tuning immune responses within the neural microenvironment (Fig. 7E, F). Enrichment Circos plot analysis elucidated critical gene-pathway interactions: Tph1 drove serotonin synthesis via tryptophan metabolism, while Oxt and Cxcr1 linked to calcium signaling and T cell receptor regulation, respectively. Cnmd and Alas2 further emphasized roles in neuroprotection and metabolic adaptation (Fig. 7G). KEGG pathway corroborated these findings by demonstrating prominent enrichment in neuroactive ligand-receptor interactions (central to dopamine/serotonin signaling) and tryptophan metabolism, which governs serotonin synthesis, a critical modulator of ASD-related behavioral deficits (Fig. 7H, I). The cAMP signaling pathway, essential for neuronal survival, and pathways such as autoimmune thyroid disease and B cell receptor signaling, implicated in neuroinflammatory regulation, further supported the therapeutic potential of HFn@M/R (Fig. 7G, H). Moreover, the HFn@M/R-exclusive DEGs, distinct from all monotherapy groups, uniquely enriched pathways critical to extracellular matrix remodeling and metabolic specialization (Fig. S18A, B). Functional analyses further revealed unique neuroprotective mechanisms through enhanced purine catabolism, anti-inflammatory lipid signaling regulation, glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis-mediated synaptic stabilization, and folate-dependent metabolic coupling between one-carbon metabolism and neurodevelopmental processes (Fig. S18A, B). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that HFn@M/R intervention orchestrates a multi-target network, synergistically enhancing synaptic function, metabolic reprogramming, and immune-neuro crosstalk. Its unique capacity to regulate specialized neuroprotective and metabolic pathways distinguishes HFn@M/R as a multifaceted therapeutic strategy, addressing the complex interplay of neurodevelopmental, metabolic, and immune dysregulation in ASD.

Conclusion

In summary, our work establishes HFn@M/R as a versatile nanomedicine platform capable of addressing the multifactorial pathology of ASD through targeted, multi-modal intervention. By harnessing the unique BBB-penetrating properties of HFn nanocages to co-deliver Met and Rapa, we achieved precise modulation of both AMPK and mTOR-dependent pathways, key regulators of mitochondrial function, autophagy, and neuroinflammation. The comprehensive characterization confirmed the high drug-loading efficiency and stimulus-responsive release profile of HFn@M/R, while in vitro experiments demonstrated its ability to restore mitochondrial membrane potential, rebalance neurotrophic signaling, and alleviate oxidative stress and apoptosis. In a VPA-induced rat model, HFn@M/R not only exhibited robust brain accumulation and an excellent safety profile, but also significantly improved behavioral outcomes and normalized aberrant hippocampal electrophysiological patterns. Transcriptomic analyses further elucidated its capacity to rewire neurodevelopmental, metabolic, and immune networks, underscoring the mechanistic basis for its therapeutic efficacy. Collectively, these findings provide compelling evidence for the potential of HFn@M/R as a precision nanotherapeutic for ASD and lay a strong foundation for its future clinical translation in treating complex neurodevelopmental and neuroinflammatory disorders. Future work will focus on validating these findings in genetic ASD models, conducting extended chronic toxicity studies, and elucidating the precise nano-bio interactions at the cellular level to further optimize the platform for clinical translation.

Experimental section

Animals

All animal procedures complied with ethical guidelines approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shanghai University (ECSHU-2020-030). Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (WT, male: 300 g; female: 250 g) were acquired from the Chinese Academy of Sciences Experimental Animal Center and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with a 12 h light/dark cycle. Male and female rats were paired overnight (1:2 ratio), and pregnancy was confirmed by vaginal plug detection (designated gestational day 0.5). Pregnant females received intraperitoneal injections of 600 mg/kg VPA or saline on gestational day 12.5. Male offspring were selected for experiments due to the male-biased prevalence of autism-like phenotypes [59]. From postnatal (PN) days 35–42, ASD-model rats received daily tail vein injection treatments for 7 days. Experimental groups included: WT, VPA, VPA + Met (2.5 mg/kg Met), VPA + Rapa (2.5 mg/kg Rapa), VPA + M&R (2.5 mg/kg Met and 2.5 mg/kg Rapa), and VPA + HFn@M/R (injected with HFn@M/R nanoparticles equivalent to 2.5 mg/kg Rapa). All animals were euthanized humanely following institutional ethical guidelines.

HFn expression and purification

The synthetic HFn gene was cloned into pET-21a(+) and expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3) (BNCC, China). Cultures grown in ampicillin-supplemented Luria-Bertani (LB) medium (30 µg/mL) (BNCC, China) at 37 °C were induced with 0.5 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) (Yeasen, China) at Optical Density (OD) 600 0.6–0.8, followed by expression at 37 °C (4 h) or 15 °C (16 h). Bacterial lysates, prepared by ultrasonication (200 W, 5 s on/off cycles, 30 min), underwent thermal purification (70 °C, 15 min) and centrifugation (12,000 ×g, 30 min). The supernatant was sequentially purified via nickel-nitrilotriacetic acid (Ni-NTA) affinity chromatography and diethylaminoethyl (DEAE) ion-exchange columns (Yeasen, China). Purified HFn was dialyzed into storage buffer (50 mM Tris, 300 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, pH 8.0), filtered (0.22 μm), and stored at − 80 °C.

HFn@M/R Preparation and characterization

For passive loading, Met and Rapa (MedChemExpress, USA) at a 1:2 weight-to-weight (w/w) ratio were incubated with HFn (1 mg/mL) under agitation (25 °C, 24 h). Unbound drugs were removed by dialysis (molecular weight cut off [MWCO] 8–14 kDa, PBS, 24 h) and centrifugation (12,000 ×g, 10 min). For pH-driven assembly, the drug mixture was acidified to pH 2.0 (1 M HCl, 30 min agitation) with HFn, followed by neutralization (pH 7.4, 5 M NaOH). Purification mirrored the passive loading protocol. Morphology was analyzed by TEM (2% phosphotungstic acid staining). Hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential were measured via dynamic light scattering (0.02 mg/mL PBS). Structural integrity was verified by 12% SDS-PAGE (ACE Biotechnology, China) with Coomassie Blue (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and CD spectroscopy (0.5 mg/mL, 10 mM PBS, pH 7.4) (JASCO Corporation, Japan). XPS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and FTIR (KBr pellets, 4 cm⁻¹ resolution) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) characterized elemental composition and chemical groups.

UV-vis standard curves (234 nm for Met; 278 nm for Rapa) quantified drug concentrations. Structure prediction with alphafold 3

The interaction model between the ferritin nanocage heavy chain subunit and TfR1 was predicted using AlphaFold 3 [60]. Initially, the amino acid sequences of both the ferritin heavy chain and TfR1 were obtained from Universal Protein Resource (UniProt). These sequences were then input into the AlphaFold 3 prediction pipeline, which generated multiple structural models. The top-ranked model was selected based on predicted alignment error and predicted Local Distance Difference Test (pLDDT) scores, which indicate the confidence level of the predicted structure. The model was further validated by assessing the interaction interface using known binding residues from previous experimental studies as references. The resulting structural models were visualized using PyMOL, allowing for detailed examination of the binding interface and identification of key contact regions between the ferritin nanocage and TfR1.

Cell culture

SH-SY5Y, GMI-R1, and bEnd.3 were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). Cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Meilunbio, China). Cultures were maintained in a humidified incubator (Memmert, Germany) at 37 ℃ with 5% CO₂.

Cell viability assessment

Cell viability was quantified using Cell Counting Kit-8 (Beyotime Biotechnology, China; C0037). Cells in log phase (1 × 10⁵ cells/mL) were seeded in 96-well plates, treated with HFn or HFn@M/R (0–20 µg/mL equivalent HFn) at ~ 60% confluency, and incubated for 24 h. CCK-8 working solution (1:10 in serum-free DMEM) was added (110 µL/well), followed by 1 h incubation and absorbance measurement at 450 nm.

Hemocompatibility evaluation

Rat red blood cells (RBCs) were isolated via orbital blood collection and centrifugation (2,000 ×g, 10 min), washed until supernatant clearance, and reconstituted as 2% suspension in PBS. Equal volumes (0.2 mL) of RBC suspension and test solutions (including water/PBS controls) were incubated (4 h, RT). Hemoglobin release was quantified spectrophotometrically (540 nm) after centrifugation (3,000 ×g, 5 min).

Cellular internalization studies

For uptake analysis, SH-SY5Y and bEnd.3 cells (1 × 10⁵ cells/mL) were exposed to Cy5.5-labeled formulations (10 µL) in 6-well plates (1 h, 37 °C). Cells were trypsinized, washed 3 times with PBS, and analyzed by FCM (Beckman, USA). Blocking experiments incorporated 100 µL TfR1 antibody co-treatment before fixation (4% PFA, 20 min).

BBB penetration model

bEnd.3 cells were cultured on Transwell inserts (0.4 μm) (Merck KGaA, Germany) for 6 days to establish BBB monolayers. SH-SY5Y cells (lower chamber) were exposed to upper chamber-administered HFn-Cy5.5 (10 µL). Penetration efficiency was quantified via FCM (Beckman, USA) of recipient SH-SY5Y cells, with comparative assessment of free Cy5.5 vs. HFn-Cy5.5 at 24 h.

Neurotoxicity modeling and rescue

VPA-induced damage was established by treating SH-SY5Y cells (1 × 10⁵ cells/mL) with different concentration VPA (MedChemExpress, USA) for 24 h. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) was determined via CCK-8 (Beyotime Biotechnology, China; C0037). For rescue experiments, cells were treated with VPA (IC50 dose) combined with Met, Rapa, their combination, or HFn@M/R to viability assessment.

Mitochondrial membrane potential analysis

JC-1 probe (Beyotime Biotechnology, China; C2003S) was employed to assess mitochondrial membrane potential. SH-SY5Y cells (1 × 10⁵ cells/well in 6-well plates) were allowed to adhere for 24 h before pharmacological interventions. Post-treatment samples were trypsinized, pelleted (300 ×g, 5 min), and resuspended in JC-1 working solution (prepared per manufacturer’s protocol: 200× stock diluted 1:200 in 1× buffer). Following 20-minute incubation at 37 °C with periodic agitation, cells underwent three buffer washes (4 °C, 500 ×g centrifugation). Fluorescence ratios were detected using CytoFLEX FCM (Beckman, USA).

Detection of mitochondrial copy number

SH-SY5Y cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells/mL and grouped for drug treatment based on different drug treatments. Total DNA was isolated from SH-SY5Y cells after different drug treatments. Mitochondrial copy number was quantified using the mitochondrial-specific gene ND-1 as an indicator. ROS detection

SH-SY5Y cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells/mL and treated with drugs for 24 h. DCFH-DA (Beyotime Biotechnology, China; S0034S) (10 µM) was added to drug-treated SH-SY5Y cells for 30 min at 37 °C. After PBS washing, intracellular ROS levels were assessed via CytoFLEX FCM (Beckman, USA) and Zeiss LSM710 Meta confocal microscope (Zeiss, Germany) imaging.

Apoptosis assay

Cell apoptosis was evaluated using Annexin V-PE/7-AAD apoptosis detection kit (Yeasen, China; 40311ES60). SY5Y cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells/mL, and after 24 h of drug treatment, apoptosis was assessed. Annexin V-FITC detects phosphatidylserine on the membrane of early apoptotic cells, while 7-AAD is used to differentiate between live cells and late apoptotic cells.

In vivo biosafety evaluation

WT SD rats (Control [saline], Met, Rapa, Met + Rapa, HFn@M/R) received 7-day intravenous treatments. Orbital blood samples underwent hematological profiling. Following euthanasia, major organs (brain, heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys) were harvested, PBS-perfused, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Paraffin-embedded tissues were sectioned (5 μm), H&E-stained, and imaged for histopathological analysis.

Enzyme-Linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6 and TNF-α) in the rat serum were quantified using commercial sandwich ELISA kits (HUABIO, China; #ER0004 and #ER0007). The serum samples were brought to room temperature and diluted appropriately with the provided diluent. All standards and samples were assayed in duplicate. 100 µL of each standard and sample was added to the antibody-precoated wells and incubated according to the kit instructions. After a series of washes, biotinylated detection antibody and subsequently avidin-HRP conjugate were added. The colorimetric reaction was developed using tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate and stopped with a stop solution. The OD of each well was immediately measured at 450 nm with a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The cytokine concentrations were calculated by interpolating the mean OD values against the standard curve generated from the known standards.

Fluorescence imaging

ASD rats received intravenous ICG (MedChemExpress, USA) or HFn@ICG (4 mg/kg). Cerebral biodistribution was monitored at 1–24 h post-injection using a PerkinElmer IVIS Lumina III imaging system (PerkinElmer, USA).

Early developmental assessment in offspring

Postnatal developmental progression was monitored from birth (designated PN 0), with weekly body weight measurements recorded. Eye-opening status was evaluated starting at PN 12, scored as 0 (both eyes closed), 1 (one eye open), or 2 (both eyes open). Behavioral milestones were systematically assessed using standardized protocols: The plane correction required pups to fully right themselves within 3 s when placed supine on a smooth surface. For cliff avoidance, pups positioned at a table edge were observed for successful 90° turning within 15 s. Negative geotaxis was tested on a 30° inclined glass surface, measuring the latency to complete a 180° rotation into a head-up orientation. Swimming ability was evaluated at PN 8, 10, 12, and 16 by releasing pups into a water-filled basin for 5–10 s, with posture and limb coordination scored as 0 (head and nose submerged), 1 (head raised but nose submerged), 2 (head and nose above water but ears submerged), 3 (water at mid-ear level) and 4 (head, nose, and ears fully raised).

Behavior tests

The behavior of the rats was evaluated through a series of tests, including the open field test, Y-maze, elevated plus maze, three-chamber social interaction test, and Morris water maze. All tests were conducted with the support of Shanghai Yuyan Instruments (China). The open field test utilized an arena divided into 16 grids, with total locomotion distance and time spent in the central 4-grid zone recorded during 10-min to quantify anxiety-like behavior. In the Y-maze, spontaneous alternation rates were calculated during 10-min explorations of three symmetrical arms. The elevated plus maze measured open-arm time proportion (%) over 10 min. For the three-chamber social interaction test, rats underwent four phases: habituation (5 min in central chamber), free exploration (5 min), social novelty exposure (10 min with an unfamiliar rat in the left chamber), and social preference testing (10 min with a second stranger in the right chamber). Chamber occupancy times were tracked using automated tracking software. Spatial learning and memory were evaluated in the Morris water maze across a 5-day acquisition phase (escape latency to a submerged platform) and a probe trial (quadrant occupancy % and platform crossings after platform removal).

Immunohistochemical staining

Deparaffinized sections underwent antigen retrieval in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) buffer (100 °C, 20 min). Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% H₂O₂ (5 min). After blocking, sections were incubated with Iba1 antibody (Abcam, UK; ab178846) or GFAP antibody (Abcam, UK; ab7260) (4 °C, overnight) and HRP-conjugated secondaries (RT, 1 h). 3,3’-Diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate (5 min) was used for chromogenic detection, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining and ethanol/xylene dehydration.

TUNEL staining

Tissue sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and treated with proteinase K (20 µg/mL, 37 °C, 20 min). After Triton X-100 permeabilization, TUNEL reaction mix was applied (2 h, dark).

Nissl staining

Sections were stained with 1% toluidine blue (MedChemExpress, USA) (30 min), rinsed, dehydrated through graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and mounted for neuronal morphology analysis.

Electrophysiological experiments

Under isoflurane anesthesia, rats were stereotaxically implanted with hippocampal CA3 electrodes (coordinates: 4.2 mm posterior to bregma, 4.1 mm lateral, 4.0 mm ventral). Local field potentials (δ, θ, α, β, γ bands) were recorded for 1 h post-recovery. Signals were analyzed using MATLAB 2019a (wavelet/Pwelch functions) for spectral power quantification.

Transcriptome sequencing

Hippocampal RNA was extracted, sequenced (Illumina Novaseq 6000), and analyzed via DESeq2 (|log2FC|≥1, p < 0.05). Functional enrichment (GO/KEGG) and clustering were performed on differentially expressed genes.

Statistical analysis

All results in this study were recorded as Mean ± SEM. Statistical analysis was performed by two-tailed Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA followed by post hoc Tukey’s test to identify significant differences. Differences were considered significant when p<0.05. *p<0.05, **p<0.01, ***p<0.001. All statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism8.0 software.
