Single-particle atomic-scale strain-gradient engineering for high-performance fuel cells
Authors: Dafu Zhao, Zisheng Tang, Jinfeng Liu, Zhiyi Hu, Zhiwen Yin, Jieheng Lv, Xiaobin Liao, Xiaoqian Wang, Yingfei Liu, Damin Liu, Lihua Chen, Bao-Lian Su, Dongyuan Zhao, Yong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Practical electrochemical energy conversion requires electrocatalysts that coordinate multiple elementary steps at spatially distinct active sites, yet atomic-level control of such site-specific reactivity within a single heterogeneous particle remains challenging. Here we propose and realize atomic-scale gradient strain as a design concept for heterogeneous electrocatalysis. Using Pd@Pt core-shell tetrahedra as a model system, we construct a continuous lattice-strain gradient across individual 3–4 atomic-layer Pt {111} epitaxial shells, where lattice-mismatch-driven compression relaxes from edges to center regions. This single-particle strain gradient, spanning approximately −8% to −2%, spatially links *O2 activation at highly compressed sites with *OH weakening at moderately compressed sites through kinetically accessible intermediate redistribution. The catalysts exhibit competitive oxygen reduction reaction performance, with mass and specific activities of 2.19 A mgPt⁻1 and 3.01 mA cm⁻2 at 0.9 V vs reversible hydrogen electrode, while retaining 91% activity after 20 k cycles. In membrane electrode assemblies, they achieve 0.57 A mgPt⁻1, and peak power densities of 2.10 W cm⁻2 in H2 − O2 and 1.16 W cm⁻2 in H2−air, with over 90% performance retention after 20 k cycles.

Introduction

The transition to sustainable energy technologies relies on electrocatalysts that combine high activity and durability with minimal loading of scarce noble metals1–4. Meeting these demands requires atomic-scale control of active sites, where tailored interfacial structures and lattice strain can reshape adsorption energetics and reaction pathways5–8. Among emerging strategies, single-atom catalysts (SACs) maximize metal utilization9–13, but their practical implementation is limited by low loading and thermodynamically driven aggregation governed by the Gibbs–Thomson effect14,15. These constraints have focused attention on atomically thin catalytic layers, which can simultaneously preserve high noble-metal utilization and structural stability while enabling precise tuning of strain and electronic structure16,17.

Core–shell nanostructures provide a particularly attractive platform because a well-defined lattice interface can impose compressive or tensile strain within only a few atomic layers at the surface, thereby tuning adsorption energies and accelerating rate-determining steps18–21. Lattice mismatch between dissimilar cores and shells imposes compressive or tensile strain within atomically thin surface layers, thereby tuning adsorption energies and accelerating rate-determining steps22,23. The catalytic response of such strained architectures depends critically on the exposed facets: Pt {111} surfaces, for example, are more than twice as active as Pt {100} for the ORR owing to their coordination geometry and oxygen-binding characteristics24. Realizing facet-specific strain coupling thus requires deterministic control of morphology and orientation. However, multistep deposition often generates nonuniform or overly thick shells that relax the intended strain field and obscure the relationship between strain, facet, and activity25. Consequently, simultaneous atomic-level control of strain and facet orientation remains a central challenge, particularly for engineering spatially differentiated active sites rather than uniformly strained surfaces.

More fundamentally, the prevailing paradigm of strain engineering in electrocatalysis treats strain as a single-valued, spatially uniform descriptor21,26. This paradigm is intrinsically mismatched to multistep reactions such as ORR, which involves the sequential evolution of *O2, *OOH, *O, and *OH intermediates27,28. Uniform strain shifts the metal d-band center in one single direction29–31, improving at most one elementary step while compromising others. The consequence is a persistent adsorption–desorption conflict: strengthening binding to activate *O2 often impedes *OH desorption, reflecting the Sabatier-type trade-off that has long limited ORR catalyst design32,33. In this light, the key missing capability is not merely stronger or weaker binding but spatially programmable binding, the ability to impose atomically resolved, site-differentiated reactivity within a single coherent nanoparticle to coordinate sequential reaction steps, for which a strain-gradient strategy provides a viable route. By allowing lattice distortion to vary continuously over nanoscale distances, a gradient strain field can generate spatially differentiated electronic states and adsorption strengths that are inaccessible to uniform strain or purely compositional tuning34–36. If realized with atomic precision, such a strategy would elevate strain from a global, averaged parameter to a local, programmable surface field capable of orchestrating multistep chemistry.

Here, we establish a continuous, atomically defined lattice-strain gradient across the surface of individual Pd@Pt core-shell tetrahedra. Using a one-pot solvothermal synthesis, we construct Pd@Pt core-shell tetrahedra with controlled Pt {111} facet orientation, where Pd–Pt lattice mismatch generates a three-dimensional, facet-coupled compressive gradient strain across 3–4 Pt {111} atomic layers. XAFS and HAADF-STEM strain mapping confirm the atomically thin epitaxial Pt shell and the designed gradient configuration. DFT calculations support a strain-gradient-driven local-cooperative mechanism in which highly compressed edge regions render the *O2−to−*OOH step thermodynamically more favorable in the highest-compression regime, while low migration barriers indicate kinetically accessible surface redistribution of *OH across strain-differentiated Pt sites, and the moderately compressed region is more favorable for weakening *OH binding and facilitating the final removal of oxygenated intermediates. This strain-differentiated surface is associated with competitive ORR activity: the Pd@Pt core-shell tetrahedra deliver a mass activity (MA) of 2.19 A mgPt−1 and a specific activity of 3.01 mA cm−2 at 0.9 V vs RHE. When assembled into MEAs, the Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra achieve peak power densities of 2.10 W cm−2 (H2–O2) and 1.07 W cm−2 in H2–air after 20 k cycles (−7.8% from 1.16 W cm−2). They also deliver a MA of 0.57 A mgPt−1, a current density of 0.40 A cm−2 at 0.8 V, and a voltage loss of 10 mV, meeting the DOE 2025 targets. This work shifts the conceptual basis of strain engineering from uniform interfacial distortion to single-particle, atomic-scale strain-field programming, supporting gradient strain as a design principle for efficient and durable electrocatalysts in multistep energy-conversion reactions.

Results

Material synthesis and structural characterizations

Figure 1a schematically illustrates a one-pot strategy for synthesizing Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra featuring a gradient-strained Pt {111} shell with a thickness of 3−4 atomic layers. Simultaneous reduction of platinum (II) acetylacetonate [Pt(acac)2] and palladium (II) acetylacetonate [Pd(acac)2] in N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF) using polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) and formaldehyde (HCHO) at 180 °C led to the formation of the tetrahedral architecture (See Supplementary Information for details). In this system, DMF served as the solvent, PVP moderated surface energies and facet exposure, and HCHO acted as a mild reductant that favored the preferential formation of {111} facets. Because Pd has a lower surface energy than Pt, Pd(acac)2 was reduced first, yielding well-defined Pd tetrahedra with the assistance of HCHO. Once the Pd framework was established, Pt2⁺ ions were subsequently reduced and Pt atoms deposited epitaxially onto the Pd {111} facets, forming a continuous, atomically thin Pt shell. The substantial surface-energy contrast between Pd and Pt suppressed Pt interdiffusion into the Pd core, thus ensuring a distinct core–shell architecture37. Direct structural evidence for this evolution is provided by HAADF-STEM imaging and EDS mapping, which clearly track the transition from Pd tetrahedra to a uniform Pd@Pt core–shell architecture characterized by continuous Pt deposition across {111} facets of the Pd core (Figs. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) confirms that the Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra exhibit consistent size and morphology across the sample (Fig. S4). Aberration-corrected HAADF-STEM reveals the atomic structure of individual tetrahedra, which have an average edge length of ≈25 nm (Fig. 1b). Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) patterns acquired along the [111] zone axis (the insets of Fig. S5) reveal a high-quality, periodic face-centered cubic (fcc) Pt lattice extending across the entire {111} facets, confirming an atomically thin Pt layer coherently aligned with the underlying Pd framework. The simulated Pt atomic model along the [111] zone axis matches well with the experimental atomic arrangement (Fig. S6). Notably, the contact regions between {111} planes differ from those in pure Pd tetrahedra because Pt atoms selectively deposit on Pd {111} facets, generating distinct edge features. This contrast is likely attributed to the significant difference in atomic numbers between Pd and Pt. EDS elemental mapping (Fig. S7) of an individual Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedron further confirms the precise alignment of Pt atoms on {111} facets of the Pd tetrahedra, leading to the formation of a distinct core–shell structure.

3D tomography reconstruction based on HAADF-STEM imaging was performed to visualize the spatially resolved atomic arrangement of the Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra. At the initial orientation of 0°, the tetrahedra predominantly feature {111} facets, with edges defined by {110} facets (Fig. 1d). The reconstructed structural units and images at different rotation angles (Figs. S8 and S9) demonstrate uniform Pt atomic deposition on the {111} facets without agglomeration. A cross-sectional view (Fig.1c) reveals a distinct Pt layer (≈1 nm) at the interface, corroborated by rotational and ortho-slicing analyses (Supplementary Movie 1 and Fig. S10). HAADF-STEM (Fig. 1e) and EDS mapping (Fig. 1f−h) further confirm a ≈1.2 nm Pt layer at the outer edges of the Pd@Pt core–shell structure, confirming the precise alignment of Pt atoms on the {111} facets. Magnified images of the selected region in Fig.1i (shown in Fig.1j, k) further illustrate that the Pt shell comprises 3 − 4 atomic layers, which appear brighter than the Pd core due to the higher atomic number of Pt. This brightness contrast clearly distinguishes the Pt shell from the Pd core, enabling accurate thickness measurements. Together, these characterizations confirm an atomically, uniformly distributed Pt shell on the {111} facets. To elucidate the formation mechanism of this architecture, we systematically varied the concentrations of HCHO and PVP within an optimal range. This adjustment effectively stabilizes the {111} facets and balances the kinetics of Pd reduction and Pt deposition. However, either a deficiency or excess of these additives disrupts this balance, leading to irregular morphologies (Figs. S11–S13). Specifically, in the absence of HCHO, the {111} facets were not effectively stabilized, which inhibited tetrahedra formation. With 5 μL mL−1 HCHO, tetrahedra formation was only partially promoted, still resulting in irregular morphologies (Figs. S11a, b and S12). Increasing the HCHO concentration to 10 μL mL−1 produced uniform tetrahedra, while excessive HCHO (15 μL mL−1) caused irregular Pt deposits on the Pd tetrahedra surfaces (Figs. S11c, d). A similar concentration-dependent effect was observed for PVP: insufficient PVP (20 mg mL–1) led to aggregation, the optimal amount (30 mg mL−1) enabled uniform Pt deposition and well-defined core–shell tetrahedra, while excessive PVP (40 mg mL−1) disrupted the morphology (Fig. S13). These observations confirm that precise control over HCHO and PVP is essential for obtaining uniform Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra with atomically thin Pt precisely aligned on the {111} facets.

The core–shell structure of the Pd@Pt tetrahedra was further confirmed by selectively etching the Pd core, yielding Pt hollow tetrahedra (Figs. S14 and S15). The resulting product consists of Pt hollow tetrahedra that largely preserve the original morphology and crystallographic orientation, with Pt atoms forming an atomically thin, well-defined surface layer (Fig. S16). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Fig. S17) analysis revealed that the Pt:Pd atomic ratios for the Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra and the Pt hollow tetrahedra are approximately 1:3.5 and 15.7:1, respectively, which are approximately consistent with the ICP-OES and EDS results (Fig. S18). Lattice spacing measurements indicate that the Pt (111) facets on the surface of the Pd tetrahedra have a spacing of 0.22 nm (Figs.S19a, b), which is denser than the typical Pt (111) spacing of 0.23 nm observed in the Pt hollow tetrahedra (Figs.S19c, d). This reduced spacing arises from compressive strain (≈−4.3%) induced by the slight lattice mismatch between the Pd core and Pt shell. Integrated intensity profiles (Fig. S19e) and X-ray diffraction (XRD, Fig. S20) further validate this strain by demonstrating a smaller Pt (111) lattice distance and notable negative shifts for the Pt diffraction peaks relative to the hollow tetrahedra.

X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) was employed to characterize the Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra. The Pd K-edge and Pt L3-edge XANES spectra closely match those of the corresponding metal foils (Fig. 2a, b). In the Fourier-transformed extended X-ray absorption fine structure (FT-EXAFS, phase-uncorrected), a dominant metal–metal peak appears at ≈2–3 Å. At the Pd K-edge of Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra (Fig. 2c), Pd–Pd bonding remains foil-like, with Pd–Pt coordination also detected, showing that the Pd core preserves a bulk-phase character in tight contact with the Pt shell. At the Pt L3-edge of Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra (Fig. 2d), compared with Pt foil, the main R-space peak appears at a ≈ 0.10 Å shorter distance and lower amplitude, consistent with contracted Pt–Pt bonds and reduced coordination due to interfacial Pt–Pd scattering. Wavelet-transform (WT) at the Pt L3-edge features intensity concentrated in the high-k region of metal–metal scattering (Fig. 2e), overlapping with the Pt–Pt feature of Pt foil and broadened by Pt–Pd scattering. At the Pd -K edge (Fig. 2f), the WT mirrors Pd foil, confirming a metallic Pd core, and likewise features k-broadening arising from interfacial Pd–Pt coordination. Quantitative EXAFS fitting analysis supports Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra with metallic Pd core and atomically thin Pt shell on {111} facets. The χ(k)k3 oscillations at the Pd K-edge and Pt L3-edge display foil-like phase and amplitude, entirely different from oxide references (Fig. S21), confirming metallic Pd and Pt. EXAFS fits in k-space, and R-space closely match the experimental spectra (Figs. S22 and S23). At the Pt L3-edge of Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra (Table S1), EXAFS fitting determined a Pt–Pt bond distance of 2.66 ± 0.03 Å with a coordination number of 5.8 ± 0.7, together with a Pt–Pd contribution (R = 2.69 ± 0.09 Å, CN = 2.1 ± 0.3). Compared with Pt foil (R = 2.76 ± 0.04 Å, CN = 12), the shorter Pt–Pt distance and reduced total CN (≈8.0) indicate that the Pt shell is atomically thin, reduced in coordination, and subject to compressive strain. At the Pd K-edge of Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra (Table S2), Pd−Pd bonding is retained at 2.73 ± 0.06 Å, consistent with metallic Pd foil, while Pd–Pt coordination is detected (R = 2.69 ± 0.09 Å), matching the Pt–Pd bond length obtained at the Pt L3-edge and confirming tight core–shell contact across the interface. Overall, EXAFS quantifies an unstrained Pd core and a 3−4 atomic-layer Pt shell that undergoes interfacial contraction, concentrating compressive strain in the Pt shell and providing direct structural evidence for sub-nanometer strain distribution.

From this displacement field, εxx and εyy strain maps (Fig. 3d, e) quantify normal components along x and y, revealing that the larger Pt lattice contracts to match the smaller Pd lattice beneath38,39. The εxy shear map (Fig. 3f) and rotational map ωxy (Fig. 3g) further reveal localized lattice distortion and strain accommodation, most pronounced at the edges and corners, where the Pt shell deforms most strongly to maintain coherence with the Pd core. To evaluate the reproducibility and universality of the strain-gradient feature, we re-prepared a batch of Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra and applied the same aberration-corrected HAADF-STEM/StatSTEM workflow to three independently selected particles (Figs. S24–S26). For each particle, we performed low-magnification imaging of the analyzed edge/corner regions, high-resolution atomic-scale imaging of the Pt shell, relative atomic displacement mapping, εxx and εyy strain mapping, and εxy and ωxy mapping. All three particles reproducibly exhibit the same edge-to-center compressive-strain gradient, characterized by inward atomic displacement, strongest compression near the edges and corners, and gradual relaxation toward the inner facet region. Importantly, the quantitative strain range of approximately −8% to −2% is consistently observed across all measured particles, demonstrating that this edge-to-center compressive-strain gradient is a universal structural feature of the Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra rather than an artifact of a single particle. In contrast, the Pt hollow tetrahedron shows no apparent gradient strain, including at the particle edge (Figs. S27 and S28). This comparison further indicates that the observed strain distribution in the Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra is not an intrinsic feature of the tetrahedral geometry alone, but is associated with the presence of the Pd core and the Pd–Pt core–shell interface.

Collectively, these results suggest that the compressive strain in the Pt shell is primarily generated during epitaxial Pt growth on the smaller Pd lattice, whereas the strain gradient arises from the non-uniform accommodation of this mismatch strain over the tetrahedral geometry. EXAFS shows that the Pt–Pt distance decreases from 2.76 Å in Pt foil to 2.66 Å in Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra, corresponding to an average compressive strain of ~−3.6%. Consistently, HAADF-STEM reveals a reduced Pt (111) spacing from ~0.23 nm in Pt hollow tetrahedra to ~0.22 nm in Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra, corresponding to a local compressive strain of ~−4.3%. The EXAFS-derived value lies within the local strain range resolved by StatSTEM (−8% to −2%), with the largest strain accommodation localized at the edges and corners. This agreement strengthens the reliability of the strain analysis and supports the existence of the strain gradient. Notably, after selective removal of the Pd core, the Pt hollow tetrahedra largely retain the original tetrahedral morphology and crystallographic orientation, yet no longer display the edge-to-center compressive strain gradient. These observations indicate that tetrahedral geometry alone is insufficient to generate the observed gradient; rather, the coherent Pd@Pt interface provides the primary source of compressive strain, while the tetrahedral edge/corner geometry governs its spatial distribution.

ORR performance evaluation

The ORR activity of Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra/C was benchmarked against Pt hollow tetrahedra/C and commercial Pt/C (20 wt% Pt on Vulcan XC-72, 2–5 nm). Catalysts were uniformly supported on carbon and loaded onto glassy-carbon electrodes. Polarization curves (Fig. 4a) show the activity order Pt/C < Pt hollow tetrahedra/C < Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra/C. The Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra/C shows a positive shift in half-wave potential, implying a lower overpotential and faster ORR kinetics; this performance is enabled by the gradient compressive strain in the Pt shell. Moreover, cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves (inset of Fig. 4a) revealed two distinct potential regions corresponding to the underpotentially deposited hydrogen (HUPD, H+ + e− = HUPD, 0–0.37 V vs RHE) and the adsorbed hydroxyl species (OHad, 2H2O = OHad + H3O+ + e−, >0.7 V vs RHE)40. Notably, the onset potential for OHad formation exhibited a positive shift compared to that on commercial Pt/C and Pt hollow tetrahedra/C, suggesting relatively weaker OHad chemisorption on the Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra/C41. Furthermore, Koutecky–Levich (K–L) plots (J−1 vs ω−1/2) of Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra/C are linear (Fig. S29), indicating first-order kinetics in dissolved O2. The electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) calculated by HUPD for the Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra/C is 69.8 m2 gPt−1, slightly lower than that of Pt hollow tetrahedra/C (77.4 m2 gPt−1) and commercial Pt/C (74.7 m2 gPt−1), indicating that the higher ORR performance of the Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra/C does not arise from a larger accessible surface area. Instead, comparison with the Pt hollow tetrahedra/C suggests that maintaining the Pd-core-supported compressive strain gradient in the atomically thin Pt shell is critical for enhancing the intrinsic site activity. Fig. S30 shows Tafel slopes of 49.76 mV dec⁻1, 71.51 mV dec⁻1, and 83.36 mV dec⁻1 for the Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra/C, Pt hollow tetrahedra/C, and Pt/C, respectively. The lower Tafel slope clearly indicates the presence of favorable {111} surface gradient compressive strain, which improves ORR kinetics. Additionally, the ECSA was further evaluated by measuring the electrooxidation of carbon monoxide (CO stripping). Due to the stronger binding interaction of COad with the Pt {111} surface of the Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra/C, the ECSA values obtained from CO stripping were expectedly higher than those derived from HUPD measurements. Indeed, the ECSA value from CO stripping is 106.2 m2 gPt−1 (Fig. S31), and the ratio of ECSACO:ECSAHUPD was calculated to be 1.46, supporting the formation of an atomically thin Pt surface on the Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra/C42,43. As shown in Fig. 4b, the Pd@Pt core-shell tetrahedra/C deliver a specific activity (SA) of 3.01 mA cm⁻² at 0.9 V vs RHE, which is 2.3 and 8.6 times that of Pt hollow tetrahedra/C (1.32 mA cm−2) and Pt/C (0.35 mA cm−2), respectively. Furthermore, the MA of the Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra/C reaches 2.19 A mgPt−1 at 0.9 V vs RHE, which is higher than that of Pt hollow tetrahedra/C and Pt/C. Together with the specific-activity trend, this result further indicates that the activity advantage of the Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra/C is closely associated with the Pd-core-stabilized compressive strain gradient in the Pt shell.

The ORR electrocatalytic durability of all catalysts was evaluated through linear potential sweeps ranging from 0.6 to 1.1 V at a sweep rate of 100 mV s−1 in O2-saturated 0.1 M HClO4 solutions. After 20 k cycles, the Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra/C exhibits only an 8 mV negative shift in the half-wave potential (Fig. 4c), indicating good durability. The SA and MA of the Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra/C are decreased by only 10% and 9%, respectively. The TEM images collected at the same magnification before (Fig. S32a) and after 20 k cycles of the durability test (Fig. S32b) show that the Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra/C largely preserve their tetrahedral morphology and particle size after cycling, without obvious aggregation or structural collapse. This result is consistent with the role of the Pd core in helping maintain the structural stability of the catalyst. Additional post-ADT HAADF-STEM, STEM-EDS, and StatSTEM analyses (Fig. S33) further show that the cycled Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra/C retain the ordered atomically thin Pt shell and an edge-to-center compressive-strain gradient, indicating that the key atomic-scale structural features are largely preserved after 20 k cycles. In comparison, the Pt hollow tetrahedra/C exhibit a larger loss in SA (11%) and MA (17%) along with a greater negative shift of 22 mV in the half-wave potential after 20 k cycles (Fig. S34). However, the commercial Pt/C exhibited larger degradation, with a 70 mV negative shift in half-wave potential and losses of 72% (MA) and 71% (SA) after 10 k cycles (Fig. S35). TEM corroborates this degradation, initially uniform nanoparticles (Fig. S32c) coarsen into aggregates after testing (Fig. S32d), consistent with Ostwald ripening and the attendant loss of active surface area. By contrast, the improved durability of Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra/C arises from the coherent interface between the precisely aligned {111} facets of Pt shell and Pd core. Lattice mismatch induces a compressive strain gradient, balancing surface stress and inhibiting Pt atom migration and Ostwald ripening. The core–shell architecture further limits acid dissolution and agglomeration, while the Pd tetrahedral scaffold provides mechanical robustness. Collectively, these features contribute to improved corrosion resistance and durability.

Membrane electrode assembly (MEA) full cell evaluation

To evaluate device-level performance, the Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra/C were integrated as the cathode in proton exchange membrane fuel cell (PEMFC) MEAs (Fig. 4d) under identical iR-free conditions. Both cathode and anode of Pt loadings were fixed at 0.1 mgPt cm−2, with commercial Pt/C serving as the benchmark. The Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra/C consistently deliver higher current densities across the kinetic region (>0.7 V), reflecting more efficient utilization of active sites and improved reaction kinetics (Fig. 4e). Under H2–O2 conditions (Fig. 4f), the Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra/C initially exhibit a peak power density of 2.10 W cm−2, which decreases slightly to 1.95 W cm−2 (−7.1%) after 20 k cycles. In contrast, Pt/C drops from 1.58 to 1.35 W cm−2 (−14.6%). Under the more demanding H2–air operation (Fig. 4g), the Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra/C initially delivers a peak power density of 1.16 W cm−2, retaining 1.07 W cm−2 (−7.8%) after 20 k cycles. Pt/C, however, decreases markedly from 0.88 W cm−2 to 0.70 W cm−2 (−20.5%). These results indicate stable MEA performance of the Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra/C under H2–air operation after 20 k cycles.

Furthermore, the MA of Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra/C reaches 0.57 A mgPt−1 (Fig. 4h), compared with 0.35 A mgPt−1 for Pt/C, and is above the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) begin-of-life (BOL) target (0.44 A mgPt−1). After 20 k cycles, Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra/C retains 0.49 A mgPt−1, compared with 0.35 A mgPt−1 for Pt/C, and remains above the U.S. DOE end-of-life (EOL) target (0.26 A mgPt−1). It also exhibits nearly threefold slower peak power density degradation than Pt/C. As shown in Fig. 4i, after 20 k cycles, the Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra/C surpass the U.S. DOE durability targets for both current density at 0.8 V and voltage loss at 0.8 A cm−2. At 0.8 V, Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra/C decreases from 0.45 to 0.40 A cm−2, remaining substantially above the U.S. DOE target (>0.30 A cm−2), whereas Pt/C drops from 0.28 to 0.22 A cm⁻2, falling below the target. After cycles, the voltage loss at 0.8 A cm−2 for Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra/C remains as low as 10 mV, well below the U.S. DOE limit (<30 mV), in sharp contrast to Pt/C (40 mV), which exceeds the limit. Collectively, these metrics position the Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra/C as a competitive PEMFC cathode, combining high intrinsic activity with long-term durability. The ability to exceed multiple DOE performance and durability targets simultaneously highlights the effectiveness of strain-gradient engineering as a design strategy for PEMFC cathode design.

Theoretical calculation and mechanism analysis

To gain mechanistic insight into how the experimentally observed local compressive-strain variation influences ORR energetics, we performed DFT calculations on Pt (111) slabs under different compressive strain levels. These calculations are intended to examine how compressive strain affects representative Pt (111) surface energetics, rather than to reproduce the full three-dimensional Pd@Pt interface, the continuous strain field. The calculated trends therefore provide mechanistic support for strain-dependent ORR energetics. In general, both surface strain and ligand effects can influence the catalytic behavior of a core–shell nanostructure. In the present case, however, the explicit Pt-skin/subsurface-Pd comparison indicates that the ligand effect is substantially weaker than the strain effect and does not alter the overall strain-dependent trend (Fig. S36). This is consistent with previous studies that treated Pt shells thicker than ~0.6 nm primarily in terms of surface strain18,31. Accordingly, the calculated strain window (−5% to −2%) was selected to capture the representative compressive regime most relevant to the energetic trends discussed below. Electron localization function (ELF) analysis (Figs. 5a and 5b, S37–S42; and Table S3) suggests that the high-strain regions at the edges ( − 5% to −4%) enhance Pt–Pt orbital overlap and create a more delocalized electronic environment, which is consistent with stronger O2 adsorption in the highest-compression regime. In contrast, the moderately strained regions at the center (−4% to −2%) reduce the *OH binding energy through local electronic reconstruction. Work function (WF) analysis (Fig. 5b and S37a; and Table S4) demonstrates that compressive strain gradients increase WF from 5.70 eV (unstrained Pt) to 5.76 eV (−5% strain). This results from lattice contraction, which shortens Pt−Pt bond lengths and enhances orbital overlap. Projected density of states (PDOS) analysis (Figs. S43–S52) further reveals that the gradient strain modulates the orbital contributions and spin-split features near the Fermi level, thereby regulating the d-band center and adsorption properties. As shown in Fig. 5c and Table S5, the d-band center of Pt (111) shifts monotonically to lower energies with increasing compressive strain, decreasing from approximately −2.52 eV at −0.5% strain to −2.69 eV at −5% strain, progressively moving away from the Fermi level. This monotonic d-band shift provides a useful descriptor for the moderate-strain regime, particularly for the weakening of *OH binding. However, in the highest-compression regime, the adsorption behavior deviates from a simple linear d-band picture because local structural distortion becomes significant44,45. Notably, although uniform compressive strain is well known to improve the ORR performance of Pt, the present DFT results are more consistent with a strain-differentiated energetic landscape than with a single near-optimal uniform compressive strain. Rather than shifting the energetics of all ORR intermediates uniformly, the compressive strain gradient generates distinct local adsorption environments across the surface. The gradient strain, with its spatially differentiated electronic structural features, contrasts sharply with traditional uniform strain systems that rely on single d-band modulation. This strain-differentiated adsorption/desorption landscape may help alleviate the conventional Sabatier trade-off46–48.

Comparative analysis of adsorption sites on the gradient compressive Pt (111) surface (bridge, hollow, and top sites) identified the bridge site as the most favorable with an adsorption energy of −0.716 eV (Fig. S53). In the strain range of −2% to −4%, the *O2 adsorption energy on the Pt (111) surface remains stable at ≈−0.7 eV, indicating moderate adsorption strength. Consistent with this trend, the optimized side-view adsorption configurations show only mild and continuous relaxation from 0% to −4% compression, whereas a much more pronounced local uplift and rumpling of the top-layer Pt atoms appear at −4.5% (Fig. S54), indicating the onset of adsorbate-induced local surface rearrangement in the highest-compression regime. When the compressive strain reaches −4.5% to −5%, however, the adsorption energy becomes substantially more negative, indicating markedly stronger *O2 adsorption in the highest-compression regime49. Free-energy analysis further shows that, for most strain states, the *O2−to −*OOH step remains uphill, whereas at −4.5% and −5% compression this step becomes downhill (Figs. 5d, e and S55–S60; and Tables S7 and S8). This behavior indicates that the highest-compression regime is governed by a distinct nonlinear response, rather than by a simple linear strain dependence. Consistently, H2O adsorption (Figs. S61 and S62), which serves as an auxiliary probe of surface–adsorbate interaction, weakens progressively from 0% to −4% compression but deviates from this monotonic trend at −4.5% and −5%, further supporting that the highest-compression regime no longer follows the simple moderate-strain adsorption picture. By contrast, the moderately compressed region (−2% to −4%) is more favorable for weakening *OH binding and facilitating the final removal of oxygenated intermediates.

Figure 5f and Supplementary Movie 2 illustrate the strain-gradient catalytic mechanism, in which a hierarchical network of local sites provides complementary functions for different ORR elementary steps. In the highest-compression regime, the Pt (111) surface shows markedly stronger *O2 adsorption, and the *O2−to − *OOH step becomes thermodynamically favorable at −4.5% and −5% compressive strain. At the same time, the highest-compression region also thermodynamically stabilizes *OH more strongly. However, migration-barrier calculations show that oxygenated intermediates remain kinetically mobile on compressed Pt surfaces, with maximum migration barriers of approximately 0.19 eV at −2% compressive strain and 0.13 eV at −5% compressive strain (Fig. S63). This barrier is substantially lower than the uphill free-energy cost for local *OH−to−*H2O removal at the highest-compression sites (+0.52 eV at −4.5% and +0.71 eV at −5%), indicating that surface redistribution is energetically more accessible than local OH removal at these sites. Importantly, the lower barrier in the highest-compression regime indicates that the most highly compressed sites are not kinetically inaccessible dead-end sites, even though *OH is more strongly stabilized at these sites in the thermodynamic sense. Independently, the moderately compressed region (−2% to −4%) provides a more favorable thermodynamic environment for weakening *OH binding and facilitating the final removal of oxygenated intermediates. In this way, the strain gradient is more rigorously understood as enabling complementary local thermodynamic functions linked by kinetically accessible surface redistribution across strain-differentiated Pt sites.

Discussion

We demonstrate an atomic-scale strain-gradient strategy by synthesizing Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra in a one-pot solvothermal process, enabling deterministic growth of 3–4 Pt {111} atomic layers that host a continuous compressive gradient from −8% to −2%. The synergistic roles of HCHO and PVP guide epitaxial Pt deposition while suppressing Pd–Pt interdiffusion, yielding architecturally uniform tetrahedra with an intrinsically programmed strain field. XAFS and HAADF-STEM strain mapping directly resolve this facet-coupled, edge-to-center lattice-strain gradient, confirming atomic-level control of spatially differentiated surface states within one coherent lattice. Combined with the experimentally resolved strain gradient, the DFT analysis supports a local-strain-based interpretation in which different compressive-strain levels may contribute differently to ORR intermediate energetics. This structure shows competitive ORR and MEA performance, delivering mass and specific activities of 2.19 A mgPt−1 and 3.01 mA cm−2 with 91% retention after 20 k cycles and MEAs power densities of 2.10 W cm−2 (H2–O2) and 1.07 W cm−2 (H2–air), exceeding DOE 2025 targets. More broadly, this work shifts strain engineering from uniform, particle-averaged tuning to single-particle, atomic-scale strain-gradient programming, offering a general paradigm for efficient and durable electrocatalysis in multistep energy-conversion reactions.

Methods

Chemicals

Platinum(II) acetylacetonate (Pt(acac)2, 98%), Palladium(II) acetylacetonate (Pd(acac)2, 99%), Ferric chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O, 98%), potassium bromide (KBr, 99.95%), anhydrous ethanol (C2H5OH, AR, ≥99.7%), polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP, Mw=58,000), and perchloric acid (HClO4, AR, 70.0–72.0%) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, AR, 99.9%), formaldehyde (HCHO, AR, ≥99.5%), and hydrochloric acid (HCl, AR) were sourced from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Commercial carbon black (XC-72R) was provided by Cabot Corporation, and Nafion (5%) was obtained from Macklin. Commercial Pt/C catalyst (20 wt%, 2–5 nm Pt nanoparticles) was supplied by Johnson Matthey (JM) Corporation. All chemicals were used as received without any further purification. Deionized (DI) water, with a resistivity of 18.2 MΩ·cm at 25 °C, was employed in all experiments.

Preparation of the Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra and Pt hollow tetrahedra

Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra with gradient strain were synthesized via a solvothermal method. Typically, 20 mg of Pd(acac)2 (0.070 mmol) and 300 mg of PVP were dissolved in 10 mL of DMF under ambient atmosphere, corresponding to concentrations of 2 mg mL−1 for Pd(acac)2 and 30 mg mL−1 for PVP. The mixture was subjected to ultrasonication at room temperature for 30 min to obtain a clear solution. Subsequently, HCHO (10 μL mL−1, corresponding to 100 μL in the 10 mL DMF reaction solution) was added to the metal precursor solution under vigorous stirring to ensure homogeneous distribution of the additive. Finally, 10 mg Pt(acac)2 (0.025 mmol) was injected into the mixture, corresponding to a concentration of 1 mg mL−1 and a precursor Pd:Pt molar ratio of approximately 3:1, followed by another 30 min of ultrasonication at room temperature, resulting in a yellow transparent solution. The resulting solution was transferred into a 25 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and sealed without additional inert-gas purging, and then heated at 180 °C for 10 h to conduct the solvothermal process, producing a colloidal solution. The product was collected by centrifugation, washed twice with ethanol and three times with DI water, and subsequently dispersed in ethanol for further use.

Pt hollow tetrahedra were prepared by selectively etching the Pd cores from the Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra. This was achieved using an aqueous etching solution consisting of ferric chloride (FeCl3) and hydrochloric acid (HCl) at pH = 0.55. In a typical procedure, 300 mg of KBr (15 mg mL−1), 50 mg of PVP (2.5 mg mL−1), 25 mg of FeCl3 (1.25 mg mL−1), 0.3 mL of concentrated HCl, and 20 mL of DI water were combined in a glass vial. The mixture was heated to 100 °C in an oil bath under magnetic stirring. Then, 20 mg of Pd@Pt tetrahedra was introduced into the mixture, corresponding to a concentration of 1 mg mL⁻1. After 3 h of reaction, the product was collected by centrifugation, washed twice with ethanol and three times with DI water, and finally dispersed in ethanol for further use.

Material characterization

The phase composition of the electrocatalyst samples was primarily characterized by XRD (D8-ADVANCE) using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54056 Å) at a scanning rate of 5°/min, with diffraction peaks recorded in the range of 30° to 80°. The samples were dispersed in solution and deposited on ultrathin carbon films prior to imaging. The morphology and size distribution of the catalysts were observed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM-1400Plus) at an accelerating voltage of 120 kV. For detailed structural and compositional analysis, high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images and selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns were obtained using a Talos F200S field emission transmission electron microscope. High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) measurements were performed simultaneously at an accelerating voltage of 120 kV to probe the atomic arrangement and elemental distribution within the samples. Further STEM imaging and EDS analysis were conducted on an FEI double Cs corrected Titan Themis G2 microscope operated at 300 kV with an X-FEG electron gun, which allowed for ultra-high-resolution imaging and precise elemental mapping. The convergence semi-angle of the probe was set to 17.8 mrad, and the inner and outer collection angles of the STEM detector were 48 and 200 mrad, respectively. The screen current used for HAADF-STEM imaging and EDS analysis was 50 pA, ensuring high-quality imaging and accurate elemental mapping. Surface electronic structures were measured using an Escalab 250Xi X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS) equipped with an Al-Kα monochromatic X-ray source, an MgKα/AlKα dual anode X-ray source, and a Thermo Scientific Kα spectrometer. The actual Pt loading in the catalysts was quantified using an inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer (ICP-OES). XAS measurements at the Pt L3-edge and Pd K-edge were performed at beamline I06 of the Diamond Light Source (UK). The spectra were collected under high-vacuum conditions to minimize air absorption. The incident X-ray beam was focused to a spot size of approximately 0.5 × 0.5 mm2, ensuring uniform illumination of the sample during data acquisition.

3D tomography reconstruction and analysis

To obtain a detailed 3D reconstruction of the nanostructures, a series of high-resolution STEM-HAADF images was acquired at 4° intervals, covering a tilt range from −52° to +64°. A total of 30 STEM-HAADF images and 90 EDS elemental mapping images were collected for comprehensive tomographic analysis, ensuring precise registration across all tilt angles. The 3D reconstruction was then performed using an iterative back-projection algorithm, which allowed for the accurate restoration of the 3D structure. To further refine the reconstructed model, a discrete tomography approach was utilized. The final 3D model was validated by comparing the reconstructed shape with 2D projections at the initial tilt angles, confirming the accuracy and consistency of the reconstruction. Dynamic visualization techniques, such as rotation, filtering, and ortho-slicing, were applied to examine the internal features of the nanostructure, ensuring the reliability and precision of the reconstructed model.

Atomic-column positions were determined by model-based fitting of two-dimensional Gaussian functions to the individual atomic columns in the image. The refined fits provide the center coordinates of the atomic columns together with the corresponding fitting standard deviations, which were used for subsequent strain analysis and uncertainty estimation.

A well-resolved internal region near the center of the analyzed Pt {111} facet was selected as the reference region because the atomic columns are clearly resolved and the FFT pattern is clean and periodic. Electrochemical measurements

The room temperature was 25 ± 2 °C. The electrolyte was stored protected from light, and 0.1 M HClO4 was freshly prepared by dilution before use. DFT models and calculations

All spin-polarized density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)52. The generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional was used to describe exchange-correlation interactions53. A plane-wave cutoff energy of 520 eV was employed. The electronic and ionic convergence criteria were set to 1.0 × 10−5 eV per atom and 0.02 eV Å−1, respectively. Structural relaxations were carried out using the conjugate gradient algorithm. Brillouin-zone sampling was performed using a 3 × 3 × 1 Monkhorst–Pack k-point mesh. Long-range dispersion interactions were described using the DFT-D3 method with BJ damping54. A four-layer Pt (111) slab containing 64 Pt atoms in total was constructed. The bottom two layers were fixed, whereas the upper two layers and all adsorbates were fully relaxed. Compressive strain was imposed by proportionally scaling the in-plane lattice constants a and b of the Pt (111) slab, while keeping these strained in-plane lattice parameters fixed during relaxation. The optimized atomic coordinates of the computational models are provided in Supplementary Data 1.

The Pt (111) slab under compressive strain was used as a local model to isolate the effect of compressive strain on the surface energetics and electronic structure of Pt. A Pt-skin/subsurface-Pd control model was additionally constructed to evaluate the possible electronic contribution from the Pd core. The electrode-potential effect was not introduced explicitly in the DFT structural optimization. Instead, it was accounted for in the ORR free-energy analysis within the computational hydrogen electrode framework55, from which the free-energy diagrams at U = 0 and U = 1.23 V were constructed.

In the experiment, the local strain in the Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedron is spatially distributed and spans approximately −8% to −2%. In the DFT calculations, a uniformly Pt (111) slab under compressive strains was used as a simplified local model to isolate the strain effect. Accordingly, the strain range of −5% to −2% was chosen as a representative compressive regime relevant to the key energetic changes discussed in the main text, rather than as a one-to-one mapping of the full experimental strain distribution.

MEA preparation and fuel cell testing

The fuel cell device performance tests, including polarization curve recording and ADT, were conducted using a PEMFC test station (ALTF-1800) integrated with an electrochemical workstation (DH7002A). The membrane electrode assemblies (MEAs) for the fuel cells were fabricated using the ultrasonic spray coating method. Both the anode and cathode electrodes were fabricated using carbon paper (TGP-H-060, Toray) as the gas diffusion layer. The anode utilized commercial Pt/C catalyst (JM, HiSPEC 4000, 40 wt%), while the cathode employed Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra catalysts (40 wt%), with 15 wt% PTFE emulsion added to enhance hydrophobicity.

The anode catalyst ink (commercial Pt/C) and cathode catalyst ink (Pd@Pt core–shell tetrahedra) were separately prepared by dispersing the catalysts into a mixed solvent of DI water (0.6 mL) and isopropanol (4.5 mL), with Nafion solution (5 wt%) added to ensure that the ionomer content accounted for 25 wt% of the solid mass. Both inks were sonicated in an ice bath for 1 h at a power of 100 W to ensure complete dispersion of the catalysts. A 12 μm-thick GORE M788.12 proton-exchange membrane was used as received without further pretreatment. Subsequently, the anode and cathode inks were separately deposited onto both sides of the membrane using an ultrasonic spray method (JY-W50LD, SONIC Co., 50 kHz nozzle). During deposition, the membrane was fixed on a heating platform at 80 °C. The spraying path followed a serpentine pattern with a 2 mm pitch, with each layer offset by 1 mm and rotated 90° to ensure uniform coverage. The final noble metal loading on both the anode and cathode was approximately 0.10 mg cm−2. The prepared MEAs were assembled by hot-pressing the catalyst-coated GORE membrane with two sheets of carbon paper at 120 °C and 1.0 MPa for 3 min, with an active area of 4.0 cm2. The performance of the MEAs was evaluated under the following conditions: 80 °C, 100% relative humidity (RH), and a backpressure of 150 kPa (absolute pressure). The gas flow rates were H2–O2 = 300/300 sccm or H2–air = 300/1000 sccm, with a humidifier used to maintain stable humidity. ADT was conducted using a trapezoidal waveform, cycling between 0.6 V and 0.95 V (each potential held for 3 s) at 80 °C and 100% RH, with H2–N2 = 200/200 sccm.
