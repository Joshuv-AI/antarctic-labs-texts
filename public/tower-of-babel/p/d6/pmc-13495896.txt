Nanofiber‐Supported Decellularized Matrix Membrane Creates a Biomimetic Biochemical and Biophysical Niche for Intestinal Organoid Epithelium
Authors: Jaeseung Youn, Hyeonji Kim, Nam Than, Juhyeok Kim, Jangwon Yoon, Dohui Kim, Hyun Jung Kim, Dong Sung Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTReconstructing intestinal organoid‐derived epithelium on porous membranes is a promising strategy for building in vitro intestine models that combine biological complexity with experimental accessibility. A central challenge is designing membranes that reproduce the biochemical and biophysical cues of the native intestinal microenvironment. Here, we report a nanofiber‐supported decellularized extracellular matrix (NaDE) membrane engineered to emulate these key features. Specifically, a colon‐specific NaDE (C‐NaDE) membrane is fabricated by integrating porcine colon‐derived decellularized extracellular matrix (CdECM) hydrogel with an ultra‐thin nanofiber scaffold. The resulting membrane preserves a collagen‐rich extracellular matrix (ECM) composition and exhibits basement membrane‐level stiffness (∼230 kPa), thereby providing a supportive niche for intestinal epithelial reconstruction. Colon organoid‐derived epithelium cultured on the C‐NaDE membrane shows enhanced expression of genes associated with stem cell maintenance and epithelial proliferation. Notably, the C‐NaDE membrane also increases markers related to secretory epithelial lineages that are typically underrepresented in conventional synthetic stiff membrane systems, including enteroendocrine, tuft, and Paneth cell populations. These findings demonstrate that integrating organ‐specific matrix composition with tissue‐relevant mechanics can improve membrane‐based intestinal epithelial models. The C‐NaDE membrane, therefore, provides a physiologically enhanced platform for studying intestinal biology, disease mechanisms, barrier function, and drug responses.Keywords: colon, decellularized extracellular matrix, intestine, nanofiber membrane, organoid

Introduction

Intestine, a crucial component of the gastrointestinal system, is responsible for absorbing water, electrolytes, and nutrients, as well as facilitating host–microbiome interactions. Significant efforts have been made in recent years toward the in vitro reconstitution of the in vivo intestine, providing deeper insights into its physiology, associated disorders, and applications in drug absorption testing [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. Intestinal organoids, derived from induced pluripotent or adult stem cells, have emerged as a promising approach to replicate in vivo intestinal physiology in vitro. These organoids exhibit the remarkable ability to encompass diverse epithelial cell lineages, including stem cells, enterocytes, goblet cells, enteroendocrine cells, and Paneth cells, which are essential for intestinal functions such as absorption, barrier integrity, and mucus secretion [6, 7, 8, 9, 10].

Traditionally, intestinal organoids have been cultured in dome‐shaped extracellular matrix (ECM) hydrogels, such as the widely used commercial product, Matrigel. Matrigel is favored due to its availability and composition of several key extracellular proteins, predominantly laminin, along with growth factors that support organoid growth and differentiation. Recently, a more advanced alternative, intestinal tissue‐derived decellularized ECM (dECM) hydrogel, has been introduced [11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. The dECM hydrogel offers a more physiological ECM composition, including various types of collagens and abundant proteoglycans, while providing mechanical properties that better support the formation, growth, and maintenance of organoids [16]. Intestinal organoids cultured in dECM hydrogel exhibit elevated gene expression associated with key aspects of intestinal physiology, such as stemness, mucus production, and Wnt signaling [13].

Despite the promise of dECM hydrogel for culturing intestinal organoids as an in vitro intestine model, the inherent three‐dimensional (3D) architecture of these organoids presents challenges. Their enclosed hollow‐sphere structure and the polarization of epithelial cells, with the apical side facing the lumen, necessitate complex techniques like micro‐perforation with a microneedle to access the lumen [17, 18, 19, 20]. As a result, conducting experimental procedures on 3D intestinal organoids, such as absorption assays or studies of host–microbiome interactions, requires labor‐intensive effort and technical proficiency. This highlights the need for methods to improve accessibility to the lumen of 3D intestinal organoids.

One promising approaches involves reconstituting intestinal organoids into a 2D epithelium by dissociating the 3D intestinal organoids and seeding the cells onto a porous membrane coated with Matrigel, a mouse tumor ECM protein mixture [21, 22, 23, 24]. The multiple lineages of intestinal cells from the dissociated organoids reorganize into a polarized 2D intestinal epithelium, with the basal side attached to the membrane. This configuration offers excellent accessibility to the lumen, making it ideal for a wide range of important experiments, including permeability tests, host–microbiome interaction studies, and disease modeling [25 , 26].

However, while physiological microenvironments are well‐replicated in 3D organoid cultures with advanced dECM hydrogel, [13] most 2D intestinal organoid epithelium models are still reconstituted on synthetic polymeric membranes (e.g., polyester, polyethylene terephthalate (PET), polycarbonate (PC), or polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS)) [22 , 26]. These synthetic membranes, even when coated with Matrigel, have limitations in replicating the physiological microenvironment. Specifically, Matrigel's insufficient collagen composition and the nonphysiological biophysical properties of synthetic membranes, including their flatness and high stiffness (often 103–104 times higher than native tissue), may restrict natural epithelial growth and functions, such as stem cell maintenance and differentiation into diverse intestinal cell subtypes [27, 28, 29].

Given that dECM hydrogels offer a highly physiological material capable of replicating the biochemical and biophysical microenvironments of specific in vivo tissues or organs, we hypothesized that fabricating a dECM membrane would be a promising strategy for reconstructing a physiologically enhanced 2D organoid‐derived epithelium. In this study, we developed a nanofiber‐supported dECM (NaDE) membrane, specifically targeting the colon, by processing porcine colon‐derived dECM (CdECM) hydrogel onto an ultra‐thin nanofiber scaffold, referred to as C‐NaDE. The C‐NaDE membrane closely mimics the biochemical and biophysical properties of intestinal tissue, particularly colon tissue, including its complex ECM composition and basement membrane‐level stiffness (∼100 kPa) [30]. When culturing human colon organoids‐derived epithelium (COE) on the C‐NaDE membrane, we observed profound biochemical and biophysical responses, evidenced by alterations in cellular morphology and gene expression profiles. Cells within the COE cultured on the C‐NaDE membrane exhibited a smaller, denser morphology, along with a significant upregulation of mRNA expression related to stemness maintenance and rare secretory cell types (such as tuft, enteroendocrine, and Paneth cells), compared to those cultured on conventional membranes.

Results and Discussion

In vivo intestinal epithelial tissue is comprised of an epithelium and underlying ECM layers: the basement membrane and lamina propria. The basement membrane primarily comprises type IV collagen, laminins, and proteoglycans, while the lamina propria mainly consists of nanofibrillar collagen, such as type I (Figure 1a) [31 , 32]. To mimic this bilayer architecture and composition of intestine ECM, the C‐NaDE membrane is designed to incorporate two distinguishable layers: a CdECM hydrogel layer containing various ECM proteins similar to those found in the basement membrane, and an underlying type I collagen layer supported by an ultra‐thin nanofiber scaffold, as depicted in Figure 1b.

The fabrication of the nanofiber scaffold‐supported type I collagen layer involved an electrospinning process for the nanofiber scaffold, and following type I collagen fibrilization and immobilization. Utilizing an electrospinning system with a hollow cylindrical aluminum collector and a polymer solution containing 5% (w/v) polycaprolactone (PCL) and F‐127 (3:1 weight ratio) dissolved in 1,1,1,3,3,3‐hexafluoro‐2‐propanol, a sparse and ultra‐thin PCL/F‐127 nanofiber scaffold was fabricated in a free‐standing configuration (Figure 1c). Subsequently, a lamina propria‐like collagenous layer was reconstructed by coating the nanofiber scaffold with type I collagen fibrils. The CdECM hydrogel, derived from porcine colon tissue via a series of processes, including decellularization, sterilization, chopping, lyophilization, and enzymatic digestion, was then deposited onto the type I collagen layer supported by the nanofiber scaffold (Figure 1d). This process effectively recreated the basement membrane‐lamina propria interface in the form of a nanofiber‐supported membrane (Figure 1e,f). The electrospun nanofiber scaffold displayed a fiber diameter distribution primarily in the range of 500–900 nm and formed a micron‐scale porous network. (Figure S1). In addition, biochemical analysis confirmed that the CdECM was successfully decellularized, as evidenced by substantial DNA removal while preserving collagen and glycosaminoglycan (GAG) content. Rheological measurements showed that the CdECM hydrogel exhibits viscoelastic properties comparable to those of Matrigel (Figure S2).

The reconstruction of COE on the C‐NaDE membrane began with the seeding of dissociated 3D colon organoids. After approximately 7 days of culture on the C‐NaDE membrane, the seeded colon organoid‐derived epithelial cells formed a continuous epithelium and underwent proliferation and differentiation, resulting in polarized and columnar epithelial structures reminiscent of those observed in the in vivo intestine. Notably, the height of the COE was significantly increased when cultured on the C‐NaDE membrane compared to a conventional Matrigel‐coated PET membrane. While similar epithelial morphologies have been reported in previous gut‐on‐a‐chip systems driven by externally applied mechanical stimuli, the enhanced epithelial architecture observed in this study was achieved solely through the intrinsic biochemical and biophysical properties of the C‐NaDE membrane (Figure 1h). Immunofluorescence staining revealed that the COE on the C‐NaDE membrane not only exhibited natural morphology but also expressed key proteins essential for intestinal tissue functions, including those involved in physical and metabolic barriers (ZO‐1 and P‐gp), metabolism (CYP3A4), mucus secretion (MUC2), and signaling protein secretion (ChrA) (Figure 1i). In addition to morphological features, the establishment of apical–basal polarity is a critical determinant of functional intestinal epithelium. In vivo, colonic epithelial cells exhibit a well‐defined apical–basal organization, with distinct localization of membrane transporters and tight junction proteins that are essential for barrier integrity and vectorial transport. The immunofluorescence analysis confirmed the presence of apical–basal polarity in epithelial cells cultured on the C‐NaDE membrane. Specifically, P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp), a representative apical membrane transporter, was predominantly localized at the luminal surface, while ZO‐1, a tight junction protein, was clearly organized along apical lateral cell–cell junctions. These spatially restricted expression patterns indicate the formation of a polarized epithelial architecture (Figure S3). Together with the observed columnar morphology and increased cell density, these findings suggest that the C‐NaDE membrane supports not only structural organization but also functional epithelial characteristics that more closely resemble those of native intestinal tissue.

To determine whether the C‐NaDE membrane provides a physiologically relevant biochemical microenvironment akin to native colon tissue ECM, we conducted an in‐depth analysis of the CdECM hydrogel, a core component of the C‐NaDE membrane, focusing on its protein composition. Comprehensive proteomic analysis unveiled a wide range of colon‐specific matrisome and non‐matrisome components preserved within the CdECM hydrogel, which were markedly distinct from the proteomic profile of Matrigel.

In the CdECM hydrogel, collagen subtypes and proteoglycans accounted for approximately 68.97 % and 10.02 % of the total matrisome proteins, respectively, while glycoproteins made up 15.04 %. In contrast, Matrigel was predominantly composed of glycoproteins, which constituted over 94.57 % of its total matrisome (Figure 2a). Collagens, as the primary structural components of intestinal ECM, along with proteoglycans, play pivotal roles in regulating signaling pathways crucial for intestinal proliferation and tissue homeostasis. Furthermore, the heterogeneous composition of the ECM facilitates the regulation of various biological pathways, including adhesion, proliferation, and morphology, akin to the collaborative dynamics observed in an orchestral performance [33]. Thus, the CdECM hydrogel, with its rich diversity of collagen subtypes and proteoglycans, more physiologically replicates the in vivo colon biochemical microenvironment compared to Matrigel, which contains only 0.19 % collagens and 1.48 % proteoglycans.

Gene ontology (GO) analysis of the CdECM hydrogel and Matrigel further elucidated the dynamic effect of CdECM hydrogel's biochemical components on molecular functions, biological processes, and cellular components. Notably, genes associated with vital biological processes, including cellular processes, homeostatic regulation, and developmental processes, exhibited positive modulation by the CdECM hydrogel and interacted with each other (Figure 2b,c).

In summary, our findings highlight that the CdECM hydrogel offers a more comprehensive and abundant ECM protein composition than Matrigel, supporting a wider range of biological processes. This underscores its potential to create a more physiologically relevant biochemical microenvironment that closely mimics in vivo tissue.

In addition to measuring the biochemical composition of ECM proteins, we also assessed the stiffness of the C‐NaDE membrane to evaluate whether it provides a physiologically relevant biophysical microenvironment for intestinal organoid cells. Stiffness is known to influence cellular morphology, stemness, and mechanotransductive signaling [34 , 35]. The stiffness of the C‐NaDE membrane was measured using the ball‐indentation method, a technique commonly employed for characterizing thin hydrogel constructs. The C‐NaDE membrane exhibited a Young's modulus (E) of 230.96 ± 28 kPa, which is significantly lower than that of the conventional PET membrane (𝐸 ≈ 1 GPa) but closely matches the modulus of native intestinal tissue (𝐸 ≈ 100 kPa) (Figure 3a,b) [30 , 36 , 37].

While dECM has been used in developing various 2D epithelium models, such as those for lung or corneal tissues [38, 39, 40], most of these approaches involve coating dECM onto rigid PET membranes, subjecting cells to stiffness levels far greater than those found in vivo. In contrast, the C‐NaDE membrane, with type I collagen layer supported by the sparse nanofiber scaffold, provides a biophysical environment that more closely mimics physiological conditions, as it is a thin, free‐standing scaffold with stiffness in the order of 100 kPa.

To investigate whether intestinal cells can mechanosense the significantly lower stiffness of the C‐NaDE membrane compared to the PET membrane, we analyzed the morphological differences in cells cultured on the C‐NaDE membrane (with a CdECM layer supported by a type I collagen‐nanofiber scaffold) versus those on a CdECM‐coated PET membrane. Notably, vinculin, a key protein in cell‐ECM interactions, was found to be less abundant in cells on the C‐NaDE membrane compared to those on the CdECM‐coated PET membrane. Cells on the C‐NaDE membrane also exhibited smaller cytoplasmic areas and nuclei. Additionally, their cytoplasmic boundaries were sharper and more defined compared to cells on the PET membrane (Figure 3c,e). Quantitatively, the vinculin area in cells on the C‐NaDE membrane decreased to 0.29‐fold compared to that in cells on the PET membrane. The nuclei of cells on the C‐NaDE membrane exhibited decreased size, with area and perimeter reduced to 0.66‐fold and 0.80‐fold, respectively, relative to those on the PET membrane. In contrast, cell density, defined as the number of cells per unit area (mm2), was 4.77‐fold higher on the C‐NaDE membrane than on the PET membrane. Furthermore, the boundary shape index, calculated as the aspect ratio of the protruded area at the cell's cytoplasmic boundary, was 4.32 times higher on the C‐NaDE membrane, indicating sharper, more protruded cytoplasmic boundaries (Figure 3f–j).

Given that both membranes are coated with the same CdECM hydrogel, ensuring nearly identical biochemical microenvironments, the observed differences in cellular behavior are likely attributed to the distinct biophysical microenvironments created by the underlying layers, specifically, the type I collagen‐nanofiber scaffold of the C‐NaDE membrane versus the PET membrane. The smaller vinculin area, reduced cytoplasmic and nuclear sizes, higher cell density, and sharper cytoplasmic boundaries observed on the C‐NaDE membrane align with known epithelial cell responses to softer substrates [41, 42, 43]. These findings suggest that cells can indeed sense the lower stiffness of the C‐NaDE membrane, which is significantly less rigid than the PET membrane and more comparable to native tissue stiffness. In addition to these mechanobiological interpretations, the observed epithelial morphology on the C‐NaDE membrane also suggests improved physiological relevance. In vivo, colonic epithelial cells form a highly organized architecture composed of densely packed columnar cells with limited apical surface area. In contrast, epithelial cells cultured on rigid in vitro substrates are known to adopt a spread morphology characterized by increased cell area, enhanced focal adhesion formation, and reduced packing density [44, 45, 46]. In this context, the morphological features observed in cells cultured on the C‐NaDE membrane, including reduced cell spreading, smaller nuclear and cytoplasmic size, increased cell density, and more compact cellular organization, are consistent with epithelial phenotypes reported in compliant, tissue‐like environments. These features therefore more closely resemble the characteristic morphology of native intestinal epithelium compared to those observed on rigid PET substrates, supporting the physiological relevance of the C‐NaDE membrane as a culture platform.

Thus, we conclude that the physiologically relevant biochemical microenvironment provided by the CdECM hydrogel is synergistically enhanced by the biophysical cue provided by the soft collagen‐nanofiber scaffold of the NaDE membrane. This unique combination of biochemical and biophysical properties makes the C‐NaDE membrane a compelling system for developing physiological intestinal organoid epithelium models, offering a closer approximation of in vivo conditions than existing models.

To investigate the impact of the biochemical and biophysical properties of the C‐NaDE membrane on the COE, we conducted transcriptomic analysis using total RNA sequencing. This allowed us to directly compare the genetic expression profiles of COE cultured on the C‐NaDE membrane with those cultured on a conventional Matrigel‐coated PET membrane. A substantial number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified between the two groups (Figure 4a). Using a false discovery rate (FDR) cutoff of < 0.05 and a fold‐change threshold > 2, we identified a total of 3704 DEGs, consisting of 1617 upregulated and 2087 downregulated genes in the COE cultured on the C‐NaDE membrane.

GO analysis of biological processes revealed enrichment in categories associated with cell proliferation, including DNA replication and cell cycle regulation, in the COE grown on the C‐NaDE membrane. Specifically, genes related to the maintenance of the stem cell population were significantly upregulated in comparison to those of the COE cultured on the PET membrane (Figure 4b,c). In contrast, the conventional group shows significantly higher levels of metabolism and transport processes, indicating that a conventional membrane supports predominantly enterocyte lineage commitment while offering limited representation of other cell types (Figure 4b). Notably, the top 60 genes involved in epithelial morphogenesis showed marked differences between the two groups, further highlighting the distinct effect of the NaDE membrane on the epithelial morphology (Figures 1g,h, and 4c).

To gain further insight into how the C‐NaDE membrane affects intestinal cell subtypes, we compared the expression levels of marker genes for various intestinal cell subtypes (Figure 4d). Stem cell markers, such as LGR5, OLFM4, RGMB, SMOC2, and ACL2, were significantly upregulated in COE grown on the C‐NaDE membrane. Similarly, markers associated with transit‐amplifying (TA) cells, including MKI67, PCNA, TOP2A, MCM5, and CDK4, exhibited pronounced upregulation, indicating enhanced proliferative activity in the COE. These findings suggest that the C‐NaDE membrane, with its collagen‐rich biochemical composition and low biophysical stiffness, promotes stemness maintenance and proliferation, aligning with cellular responses typically observed in collagen‐rich and soft culture environments [13 , 28].

Interestingly, the C‐NaDE membrane also induced distinct changes in differentiation‐related gene expression. Enterocyte marker genes, such as ALPI, FABP2, PRAP1, SLC40A1, and MYO1A, were generally downregulated in COE cultured on the C‐NaDE membrane compared to the Matrigel‐coated PET membrane. However, the C‐NaDE membrane notably upregulated markers for several rare secretory cell types, including CHGA (enteroendocrine cells), TRPM5 (tuft cells), and LYZ (Paneth cells). These results indicate that the C‐NaDE membrane not only enhances stemness and proliferation but also directs the differentiation pattern toward rare secretory cell types, promoting a more diverse range of intestinal subtypes.

Intestinal organoid‐derived epithelium reconstructed on membranes is recognized as a promising in vitro model due to its diverse cellular composition and the practical accessibility of membrane‐based platforms. In these systems, the membrane plays a critical role as a culture substrate, significantly influencing the characteristics of the epithelium at both the cellular and tissue levels. To replicate intestinal physiology, the membrane must provide a physiologically relevant microenvironment in terms of its biophysical and biochemical properties.

In this study, we developed the C‐NaDE membrane using a CdECM hydrogel supported by a nanofiber scaffold. The C‐NaDE membrane replicates the ECM protein composition of colon tissue and exhibits basement membrane‐level stiffness of approximately 230 kPa. Our protein analyses and mechanical tests confirmed that the C‐NaDE membrane provides both biochemically and biophysically relevant microenvironments. Cells cultured on the C‐NaDE membrane responded to these environments, exhibiting distinct morphological and genetic profiles compared to cells grown on conventional synthetic PET membranes coated with mouse sarcoma‐derived commercial ECM.

One of the key findings was the significant upregulation of genes related to stem cell maintenance in the COE cultured on the C‐NaDE membrane. Since stiff substrates are known to limit intestinal stemness, this result highlights how the physiological stiffness of the C‐NaDE membrane likely drove the observed genetic changes. The presence of intestinal stem cells and their expression of key functions are crucial for replicating intestinal physiology in vitro, enhancing culture stability, and enabling more reliable long‐term studies [6, 47]. This improvement in stem cell maintenance on the C‐NaDE membrane is particularly important for applications such as disease research (e.g., inflammatory bowel disease) and drug toxicity testing, where the behavior of intestinal stem cells plays a central role in producing accurate results.

Additionally, the C‐NaDE membrane's ability to promote the differentiation of rare secretory intestinal cell subtypes, often underrepresented in conventional membrane cultures, is highly promising. TA cells in the intestinal epithelium typically differentiate into either absorptive enterocytes or secretory cells [48]. However, conventional membranes tend to induce differentiation into enterocytes dominantly, making it difficult to obtain rare secretory cell types. The elevated expression of markers for secretory cells, including CHGA, TRPM5, and LYZ, in COE cultured on the C‐NaDE membrane indicates that its biochemical and biophysical properties help overcome this limitation. Specifically, the increased markers for enteroendocrine cells, tuft cells, and Paneth cells—critical for systemic metabolism regulation, immune response, and stem cell niche maintenance—highlight the C‐NaDE membrane's potential for applications in intestinal pathophysiology studies, drug testing, and microbiome interaction research.

The present study was designed to develop and validate the C‐NaDE membrane and to demonstrate the combined effects of biochemical and biophysical cues on intestinal epithelium. At the same time, we recognize that elucidating the biological mechanisms underlying these effects is an important future research direction. Both the C‐NaDE and CdECM‐coated PET membranes present an ECM‐coated surface (Figures 1f and S4), suggesting that surface chemistry is partially shared. Accordingly, we focused our interpretation on stiffness and ECM composition as the primary factors contributing to the observed cellular and transcriptomic changes. Nevertheless, more detailed mechanistic studies will be essential to delineate how individual membrane properties, including ECM composition, microscale stiffness, and porosity, contribute independently or synergistically to epithelial morphogenesis and gene regulation. We consider such systematic investigations to be a valuable future direction, for which the C‐NaDE membrane provides a versatile and physiologically relevant platform.

Moreover, the C‐NaDE membrane is easily integrated into standardized platforms, such as culture inserts, similar to conventional porous membranes (Figure S5). In this study, we presented permeability assays and a Dextran sulfate sodium‐induced barrier disruption experiment (Figure S6) as proof‐of‐concept demonstrations, highlighting the potential of the membrane to support the reconstruction of physiologically relevant and functional in vitro intestinal models for applications in drug absorption studies, disease modeling, and mechanobiological investigations.

Conclusion

The C‐NaDE membrane reproduces key features of native colonic extracellular matrix, including a collagen‐rich composition and basement membrane‐level stiffness, thereby supporting reconstruction of a more physiologically relevant COE. Compared with conventional synthetic membranes, the C‐NaDE membrane promoted transcriptional programs associated with stem cell maintenance, epithelial proliferation, and increased representation of secretory epithelial lineages that are often limited in existing in vitro systems. In particular, markers related to enteroendocrine, tuft, and Paneth cell populations were enhanced, suggesting improved epithelial heterogeneity and lineage balance. These results indicate that simultaneous control of biochemical composition and biophysical mechanics is an effective strategy for advancing membrane‐based intestinal models. In addition, the compatibility of the C‐NaDE membrane with standard culture inserts and microfluidic devices supports its practical implementation in studies of epithelial barrier function, host–microbiome interactions, disease modeling, and compound‐response screening. Overall, the C‐NaDE membrane provides a versatile and physiologically enhanced platform for next‐generation in vitro intestinal research.

Materials and Methods

Colon Tissue Decellularization

Fresh porcine colon tissue, acquired from a local butcher shop, underwent decellularization using established physical and chemical methods. Initially, the submucosal layer was isolated from the colon tissue and cut into small pieces (less than 3 × 3 cm). These pieces were immersed in 70% v/v ethyl alcohol for 2 h to deactivate enzymatic activity. The tissue samples were then stirred in deionized (DI) water with 1% v/v penicillin/streptomycin (Hyclone, USA) for 30 min to remove impurities.

Next, the samples were treated with a 1% w/v sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS; BioShop, Canada) solution in 25 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA; Biosesang, Korea) for 6 h, followed by treatment with a 1% v/v Triton X‐100 (Biosesang, Korea) solution in 25 mM EDTA for 24 h to eliminate residual cells. The treated tissues were rinsed with DI water for 12 h, followed by rinsing with 100% isopropyl alcohol (Samchun, Korea) for 2 h to remove any remaining surfactants and lipids.

Finally, the tissues were stirred in a solution of 10 U/mL deoxyribonuclease (DNase; Sigma–Aldrich, USA) dissolved in 2.5 mM magnesium chloride for 2 h to remove DNA residues. The acellular tissues were sterilized with 0.1% v/v peracetic acid in 4% v/v ethanol for 2 h and then rinsed with DI water and phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS; Welgene, Korea). The samples were lyophilized overnight and stored at −80°C until further use.

For preparation, the lyophilized colon‐derived decellularized ECM (CdECM) was digested with pepsin (Sigma–Aldrich, USA) in a 0.5 M acetic acid solution (Merck Millipore, USA) and stirred at 700–1000 rpm for 5 days. The CdECM hydrogel was subsequently neutralized to a pH of 7.0–7.4 using a 10 M sodium hydroxide (NaOH; Biosesang, Korea) solution while kept on ice.

Proteomic Analysis

Proteomic analysis was conducted on lyophilized powders of Matrigel (Corning, USA) and CdECM using a Thermo Scientific Q Exactive Hybrid Quadrupole‐Orbitrap, paired with a Dionex U 3000 RSLCnano HPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Purified peptide samples were reconstituted in solvent A (water/acetonitrile (98:2 v/v), 0.1% formic acid) and injected into the LC‐nano ESI‐MS/MS system. Initially, the samples were trapped in an Acclaim PepMap 100 trap column (100 µm × 2 cm, nanoViper C18, 5 µm, 100 Å) and washed for 6 min with 98% solvent A at a flow rate of 4 µL min−1. Subsequently, separation was achieved using an Acclaim PepMap 100 capillary column (75 µm × 15 cm, nanoViper C18, 3 µm, 100 Å) at a flow rate of 300 nL min−1. The LC gradient involved 2%–35% solvent B over 30 min, 35%–90% over 10 min, 90% solvent B for 5 min, and finally 5% solvent B for 15 min. MS data were collected using Xcalibur software (version 3.1). The Orbitrap analyzer scanned precursor ions within the mass range of 350–1800 m/z, with a resolution of 70 000 at m/z 200. For collision‐induced dissociation, the 15 most abundant precursor ions were selected with a normalized collision energy of 32. Mass data acquisition was automated using Proteome Discoverer 2.2.

Protein identification was performed using Thermo Proteome Discoverer (version 2.4.1.15). MS and MS/MS data were searched against the Mus musculus UniProt database for Matrigel and the Sus scrofa UniProt database for CdECM. Trypsin was used as the protease, allowing up to two missed cleavages. Static modifications included carbamidomethylation of cysteines, while dynamic modifications included oxidation of methionine, N‐terminal acetylation, and N‐terminal methionine excision. Precursor mass tolerance was set at 10 ppm, and fragment mass tolerance at 0.02 Da. Filtering criteria included a false discovery rate (FDR) of < 1%.

The identified proteins in Matrigel and CdECM were compared with an Atlas of human genes. GO biological process analysis was performed on the identified and overlapping proteins. Statistical significance was determined using an FDR filter and p < 0.05, with Fisher's exact test applied to calculate raw p‐values.

C‐NaDE Membrane Fabrication

To prepare a PCL/F‐127 nanofiber scaffold, a mixture of PCL (Mn = 80 000 g mol−1; Sigma–Aldrich, USA) and Pluronic F‐127 (Sigma Aldrich, USA) in a 3:1 (w/w) ratio was dissolved in 1,1,1,3,3,3‐hexafluoro‐2‐propanol (Sigma–Aldrich, USA) at a concentration of 5 wt%. Electrospinning was performed using a hollow cylindrical collector with a diameter of 2 cm, under an applied voltage of 15 kV. The distance between the needle and the collector was maintained at 20 cm, with a solution flow rate of 1 mL h−1. The resulting nanofiber scaffold was then immersed in 70% ethanol and rinsed twice with PBS prior to use.

To create a type I collagen layer over the nanofiber scaffold, it was immersed in a 0.15 mg mL−1 solution of rat tail type I collagen (Corning, USA) dissolved in PBS. After 1 h of incubation at 37°C, the excess collagen solution was removed [49]. A 0.8 mg mL−1 CdECM solution was prepared by dissolving CdECM hydrogel in PBS, and 100 µL of this CdECM solution was applied to the type I collagen‐coated nanofiber scaffold and incubated for 3 h at 37°C.

For the control groups in Figure 3, a PET membrane (0.4 µm pore size) from Transwell (Corning, USA) was sequentially coated with 0.15 mg mL−1 type I collagen and 0.8 mg mL−1 CdECM, incubating at 37°C for 1 and 3 h, respectively. For the control groups in Figures 1 and 4, the PET membranes were coated with a 0.8 mg mL−1 Matrigel solution and incubated for 3 h at 37°C.

Intestinal Organoids Culture

Human colon organoid line C04 was obtained from the 3D Organoids Core at Baylor College of Medicine (USA). To expand the colon organoid cells, the organoids, embedded in 30 µL of Matrigel, were cultured in 500 µL of complete medium in a 24‐well plate (Corning, USA) within a humidified CO2 incubator at 37°C. The complete medium for organoid expansion consisted of a 1:1 mixture of high Wnt medium (3D Organoids Core, Baylor College of Medicine, USA) and advanced Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM)/F‐12 (Gibco, USA), supplemented with 1% amphotericin B (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The medium was refreshed every other day to promote optimal organoid growth.

When organoids were ready for seeding onto the C‐NaDE membrane, the Matrigel was washed with DPBS, and 300 µL of TrypLE (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was added to each well. The plate was incubated at 37°C for 10 min. Afterward, the organoids were harvested into a 15 mL conical tube and pelleted by centrifugation at 100× g for 5 min at 4°C. Subsequently, 1 mL of TrypLE Express (Gibco, USA) was added to the tube, and the sample was incubated at 37°C for an additional 2 min to dissociate the organoids into single cells or small cell fragments. Gentle pipetting was performed to ensure proper dissociation, followed by the addition of 13 mL of Advanced DMEM/F‐12 to dilute the TrypLE. After another round of centrifugation at 100× g for 5 min at 4°C, the pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of complete medium supplemented with ROCK inhibitor (Y27632; Calbiochem, Germany).

The dissociated organoid cells were seeded onto the CdECM‐ or Matrigel‐coated Transwell culture insert (24‐well size; PET membrane 0.4 µm pore size; Corning, USA) or C‐NaDE membrane‐integrated culture insert at a density of approximately 3 × 105 cells mL−1. After allowing 6 h for cell attachment, any unattached cells were removed, and fresh complete medium was added to both the apical (100 µL) and basolateral (700 µL) sides. The medium was replaced every other day until the organoids formed a complete COE on the membranes, which typically took 4–5 days. After COE formation, cultures were maintained for an additional 7 days, with Advanced DMEM/F‐12 medium in the apical compartment and complete medium in the basolateral compartment, replacing the media every other day.

Immunofluorescent Imaging

For immunofluorescence imaging of the COE after culture, the following protocol was employed: (1) cells were fixed with 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature (RT); (2) permeabilization was carried out using 0.3% (v/v) Triton X‐100 solution (Sigma–Aldrich, USA) for 15 min at RT; and (3) cells were blocked with 1% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Sigma–Aldrich, USA) for 1 h at RT. PBS washes were performed between each step. The fixed cells were then incubated with primary antibodies diluted in PBS at 1% (v/v) for 1 h at RT. The primary antibodies used in this study were ZO‐1 (61‐7300; Invitrogen, USA), P‐GP (MA5‐13854; Invitrogen, USA), CHRA (ab45179; Abcam, UK), LYZ (ab108508; Abcam, UK), and MUC2 (PA5‐21329; Invitrogen, USA). Following five PBS washes, cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488 or 568 secondary antibodies (A‐11011 or A‐10680; Invitrogen, USA), diluted in PBS to 0.5% (v/v), for 1 h at RT. After three additional PBS washes, cell nuclei were stained with DAPI (Thermo Fisher, USA), and F‐actin was visualized using Alexa Fluor 680‐conjugated phalloidin (A22286; Invitrogen, USA), both diluted to 0.5% (v/v) in PBS and incubated for 1 h at RT. The immunofluorescence‐stained cells were visualized using a confocal microscope (FV3000; Olympus, Japan) and analyzed with Imaris software (Oxford Instruments, UK).

Total RNA Sequencing Analysis

For total RNA sequencing, COEs cultured on C‐NaDE and Matrigel‐coated PET membranes for 12 days were dissolved in TRIzol (Invitrogen, USA) and frozen. Total RNA sequencing was outsourced to LAS Inc. (South Korea) for analysis. RNA sequencing data were initially processed using the STAR aligner (v2.7.8a) [50] to map raw sequencing reads to the human reference genome (GRCh38). The resulting BAM files were quantified using the Rsubread package (v2.12.3) in R to obtain raw gene expression counts [51]. DEG analysis was performed using DESeq2 (v1.38.3), with genes having an adjusted p value < 0.05 considered significantly differentially expressed [52]. Gene set enrichment analysis of the DEGs was conducted using the clusterProfiler package (v4.6.2) to identify significantly enriched GO terms [53]. Heatmaps of the top DEGs were generated using ggplot2 (v3.4.3) to visualize expression patterns across samples.

Scanning Electron Microscopy Analysis

The sample was fixed in a 2.5% (w/v) glutaraldehyde solution (Sigma–Aldrich, USA) prepared in DI water for 10 min. After gently rinsing with DI water, the sample was dehydrated through a graded series of ethanol (30%, 50%, 70%, 90%, and 100% in DI water, v/v). It was then dried overnight at 4°C and sputter‐coated with platinum using a 15 mA electric current for 120 s. The surface microstructure of the C‐NaDE membrane was examined using a field‐emission scanning electron microscope (FE‐SEM; SU6600; Hitachi, Japan).

Statistical Analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using a t‐test to compare two different groups of data, and a two‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for comparison among multiple groups. All data are expressed as means (±s.e.m).
