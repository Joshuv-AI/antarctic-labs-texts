Signal-to-noise ratio enhancement for MEMS resonant sensors with potential barrier adjustable stochastic resonance
Authors: Junhui Wu, Guangya Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The signature of stochastic resonance is that additional noise surprisingly enhances the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). A noise-adaptive system that learns to add an optimal amount of noise to trigger stochastic resonance and improve SNR is known as adaptive stochastic resonance. However, the current stochastic resonance mechanism fails when environmental noise exceeds the optimal noise level, as any additional noise merely worsens the SNR. In this case, instead of adding noise, stochastic resonance can be facilitated by adapting the potential energy landscape of the bistable system. Here, we propose a novel approach to enhance SNR in noisy environments, involving a potential adjustable microelectromechanical systems resonator. A periodic signal with an amplitude of 0.28 Vrms is buried in ambient noise, emulated by a white noise signal with amplitude ranging from 0.7 Vrms to 4 Vrms. Experimental results show that when the ambient noise exceeds 1 Vrms, adding additional noise leads to a decline in SNR. However, SNR enhancement induced by stochastic resonance is experimentally demonstrated by tuning the potential well of the resonator. This advancement highlights the feasibility of potential adjustable systems to overcome the limitations of conventional noise adjustable stochastic resonance methods in noisy environments. The proposed mechanism is further applied to detect the frequency of 2.7 nN periodic forces with various waveforms.

Noise is typically regarded as detrimental in most scientific and engineering contexts, and significant efforts have been made to reduce or eliminate its impact. However, under certain conditions, introducing a controlled amount of noise can actually enhance system performance1–3. One of the interpretations of this counterintuitive effect is known as stochastic resonance, a concept initially proposed to explain the periodicity of ice ages4. Stochastic resonance describes how, in some nonlinear systems, the addition of noise can improve the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) in response to weak periodic signals5,6. This phenomenon typically requires three key elements: a bistable system characterized by a double-well potential, a weak periodic input signal, and a tunable noise source. As illustrated in Fig. 1a, when only a weak periodic signal is applied, the system remains confined within a single potential well, resulting in a low-amplitude response. When artificial noise is introduced, the system occasionally crosses the energy barrier between wells. At an optimal noise level, the system transits between wells at the same frequency as the input signal and produces a markedly higher output amplitude, thus enhancing the SNR. Since its initial proposal in a climate model4, stochastic resonance has been observed across a broad range of scientific disciplines. Early studies demonstrated its presence in electrical circuits7–9, nonlinear dynamical systems10–12, mechanical vibrators13,14, and biological sensory systems such as those in crayfish15, crickets16, and paddlefish17. In recent years, stochastic resonance has also drawn attention in semiconductor devices, including photodetectors18, memristors19, and optomechanical systems20,21. Beyond these foundational studies, stochastic resonance has been applied in a variety of practical domains, including communications22,23, energy harvesting24, machine fault detection25–29, and image processing30,31. This growing body of research underscores the versatility and potential of stochastic resonance across both theoretical and applied contexts, which has also been reported in nonlinear microelectromechanical systems (MEMS)23,32,33.

MEMS technology has significantly advanced the capabilities of miniaturized devices, particularly by leveraging nonlinear behaviors. These advancements have led to the development of diverse nonlinear MEMS devices, including sensors34–37, tunable resonators38, and systems exhibiting chaotic dynamics39–41. Additionally, coupled nonlinear MEMS structures have been investigated to enhance performance characteristics, especially sensitivity42–46. Bistability is a fundamental requirement to enable stochastic resonance and has been implemented in MEMS through various methods. Traditional mechanical approaches often rely on buckled beams47–49 or compliant micromechanisms50–52. However, these purely mechanical structures typically lack tunability, which is crucial for compensating for fabrication imperfections in mass production. Bistability has also been achieved using external magnetic53,54, electrostatic55–57, and electrothermal58,59 actuation. While effective, these devices are often designed as binary switches and do not exhibit complex dynamic behavior. Another approach involves utilizing Duffing-type nonlinearity in electromechanical and optomechanical systems20,40,60–62, where bistability arises from vibration amplitude bifurcation. Although stochastic resonance-induced signal amplification has been demonstrated in such systems23,32, the resulting vibration bistability is highly frequency-dependent and limited to a narrow bandwidth, restricting its broader applicability. In this work, we employ a bistable MEMS resonator with an electrostatically tunable potential energy landscape. An integrated piezoresistive transducer enables continuous, real-time position sensing, allowing for in-depth investigation of the nonlinear dynamic behavior.

In this study, we experimentally demonstrate stochastic resonance in an adjustable MEMS resonator by introducing noise into the system. The potential energy landscape of the resonator can be reconfigured into a double-well shape to create two stable states, as shown in Fig. 1b. However, traditional stochastic resonance approaches face limitations under high-noise conditions. As illustrated in Fig. 1c, noise-adjustable methods rely on tuning the noise intensity to an optimal level (denoted as D2), where the SNR is maximized. When the environmental noise already exceeds this optimal level, it is expected that the addition of further noise degrades the SNR, which is confirmed by our experimental results. To overcome this limitation, we propose a potential-adjustable stochastic resonance strategy using the tunable bistable MEMS resonator. Compared to other MEMS-based implementations of stochastic resonance, a key advantage of the proposed approach is its applicability across a wide frequency range below the natural frequency of the resonator, enabled by the position bistability. Instead of adjusting the noise level, we modify the potential energy barrier between the two states to achieve optimal system performance. As illustrated in Fig. 1d, this approach enables stochastic resonance to occur even under strong ambient noise by adapting the system’s potential energy landscape. The results demonstrate that potential-adjustable stochastic resonance offers a promising alternative to improve signal detection in noisy environments where conventional methods are ineffective.

Results

The MEMS resonator used in this study integrates both an electrostatic actuator and a piezoresistive displacement sensor, enabling precise control and accurate measurement. A bistable system is realized by electrostatically tuning the potential energy barrier. Stochastic resonance is demonstrated with this configuration without applying extra environmental control. Experimental results show that when environmental noise is already high, further increasing noise does not improve the SNR. Instead, SNR enhancement is achieved by increasing the energy barrier between the two stable states and inducing stochastic resonance. The proposed potential-adjustable stochastic resonance is applied for frequency detection of a 2.7 nN periodic force. Forces of three waveforms, including square, sine, and triangle, are tested. Significant SNR enhancement is observed when the excitation frequency remains below the natural frequency of the resonator. This finding underscores a key advantage of the potential-tunable MEMS resonator: it enables effective stochastic resonance even in the presence of strong ambient noise, where conventional noise-adjustable approaches fail.

As shown in Fig. 2f, the device integrates two piezoresistors for position sensing, each measuring 220 μm in length and 3 μm in width. The operating principle of the position sensor is analogous to that of strain gauges. In conventional strain gauges, resistance changes primarily result from geometric deformation, whereas in piezoresistors, the dominant factor is a change in material resistivity under applied mechanical stress64. When the shuttle moves upwards, the top piezoresistor in Fig. 2f is under compression, while the bottom piezoresistor is under tension, resulting in opposite changes in their electrical resistance. By measuring the difference in resistance, the position of the resonator can be continuously monitored with high sensitivity. Fig. 2d shows the MEMS chip bonded to a printed circuit board using aluminum wires, enabling stable electrical interfacing for actuation and signal readout.

The electric potential distribution near the fixed and movable combs is simulated using the finite element method, as shown in Fig. 3b, at resonator positions of −6 μm, 0 μm, and +6 μm. When the resonator moves to the right, the distance between the fixed and movable fingers decreases, increasing the local electric field gradient. Consequently, the rightward electrostatic force at +6 μm is greater than at 0 μm or −6 μm. Beyond +6 μm, the electrostatic force saturates, as additional overlap between the comb fingers hardly increases the electric potential gradient. The electrostatic force further increases only when the movable and fixed fingers are about to pull in, which could result in a short circuit.

Traditionally, the overlap distance of the fixed and movable fingers of the comb drive is designed to operate within the saturation region, where the generated electrostatic force remains stable and approximately linear, depending only on the applied voltage difference between the fixed and movable fingers. For example, the engaged comb drives used for actuation are designed with a 40 μm finger overlap. In this study, however, we intentionally exploit the electrostatic stiffness, defined as the first derivative of the electrostatic force with respect to displacement, to reshape the potential energy landscape of the MEMS resonator.

Experiment results of noise-adjustable stochastic resonance

Traditional noise-adjustable stochastic resonance is experimentally demonstrated using the bistable MEMS resonator by introducing additional pseudo-random bit sequence (PRBS) noise into the system. DC voltages of 70.11 V and 70.77 V are applied to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${V}_{l}^{+}$$\end{document}Vl+ and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${V}_{l}^{-}$$\end{document}Vl-, respectively, to achieve a symmetric potential energy landscape. A 20 Hz sine wave with an amplitude of 0.4 V serves as the periodic input signal. To evaluate the influence of environmental noise, the input is constructed by combining a sine wave with a white noise signal that emulates ambient noise. The effect of additional noise is studied by varying the amplitude of the PRBS signal, which is used to distinguish it from the background white noise. The response of the MEMS resonator is recorded across a range of PRBS amplitudes under three ambient noise conditions: 0.7 Vrms, 1.0 Vrms, and 3.0 Vrms. The output signal is sampled over a 20-s duration at a rate of 100 kSa/s. The SNR is computed in MATLAB and plotted as a function of PRBS amplitude to evaluate the influence of additional noise on system performance.

As shown by the orange curve in Fig. 4a, the SNR increases with the amplitude of the additional noise, reaching a peak value of −1.55 dB when the root mean square (RMS) noise level is 0.6 V. Beyond this point, the SNR begins to decline. The time-domain response and corresponding power spectral density for suboptimal noise intensity are presented as Fig. 4b(i) and c(i), respectively. Under this condition, the resonator does not consistently transition between the two potential wells within each cycle of the periodic signal. Discussion and conclusion

This study experimentally demonstrates the effectiveness of a potential-adjustable MEMS resonator in enhancing signal detection through stochastic resonance, particularly under high-noise conditions where traditional noise-adjustable methods fail. By tuning the electrostatic potential landscape via DC locking voltages applied to non-engaged comb drives, a controllable double-well potential is realized. This tunability allows the system to maintain bistability and achieve resonance even when environmental noise exceeds the optimal level. Experimental results reveal that the traditional noise-adjustable stochastic resonance mechanism does not improve SNR when the environmental noise exceeds a threshold value. The relationship between SNR enhancement and locking voltage is systematically characterized across a wide range of noise intensities. The results confirm that adjusting the potential barrier provides a reliable strategy to improve signal quality under varying noise levels. Furthermore, the proposed resonator successfully detects ultra-low-amplitude periodic forces by reshaping its potential energy landscape, enabling the use of stochastic resonance for frequency detection at signal levels previously undetectable in the linear regime.

The primary advantage of potential-adjustable stochastic resonance lies in its effectiveness under high-noise conditions. Unlike other MEMS implementations that rely on Duffing-type nonlinearity, where vibration amplitude-based bistability is confined to a narrow frequency range, the proposed approach supports operation across a broader frequency range, from DC up to the natural frequency of the resonator. Additionally, when the locking voltage remains below the bifurcation threshold, the device retains Duffing-type nonlinear characteristics, allowing reconfiguration into amplitude bistability if desired. However, a trade-off of the proposed mechanism is the reduction in stiffness required to form a bistable potential, which results in a relatively low natural frequency at the stable states, typically only a few hundred hertz, much lower than conventional MEMS resonators. In the current design, the device is fabricated using a commercial MEMS fabrication process with a minimum feature size of 3 μm. Future work will focus on scaling the resonator down to the nanometer scale, offering several key advantages. First, the device footprint will be significantly reduced, enabling easier integration into complex systems. Second, nanoscale resonators typically exhibit higher operating frequencies, enhancing their performance in high-speed applications. Third, the required actuation voltage can be substantially lowered, improving energy efficiency and compatibility with low-voltage electronic systems.

Overall, this work establishes a robust hardware framework to implement potential-adjustable stochastic resonance and demonstrates its practical advantages in high-noise environments. The approach offers promising opportunities for signal detection in microscale sensing applications. Future work will focus on shrinking the footprint of the potential-adjustable resonator to the nanometer scale, improving the scalability and integration in compact nonlinear sensing platforms.
