Cross‐Tissue Specificity of Pediatric DNA Methylation Associated With Cumulative Family Stress
Authors: Meingold H. Chan, Sarah M. Merrill, Fizza Fatima, Julie L. MacIsaac, Jelena Obradović, W. Thomas Boyce, Michael S. Kobor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCumulative family stress (cumulative FS), characterized by mother‐reported co‐occurring stressors in a family context, may be biologically embedded through DNA methylation (DNAm) and contribute to later health outcomes, including psychopathology. To illuminate tissue specificity in association with cumulative FS across two accessible tissues, we compared epigenome‐wide DNAm associations in buccal epithelial cells (BECs; n = 218) and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs; n = 51) from 7‐ to 13‐year‐old children in Canada, accounting for sex, age, predicted cell‐type proportion, and genetic ancestry. We found that higher levels of cumulative FS were associated with DNAm at seven sites, primarily in stress‐ and immune‐related genes, only in PBMCs but not in BECs. Matched tissue samples (n = 50) also indicated limited cross‐tissue concordance. These associations were partly explained by negative but not positive mother–child relationship. Our findings suggested that PBMC DNAm can be used as a marker for biological embedding of cumulative FS and highlighted cross‐tissue specificity of the associations of DNAm with cumulative FS.

Introduction

Stressful family environments in early life are risk factors for long‐term negative health and behavioral outcomes, including poorer physical health, stress dysregulation, and psychopathology. These early experiences may “get under the skin” in part through a number of physiological pathways in a process known as biological embedding, in turn influencing the course of children's development and health (Boyce and Kobor 2015; Hertzman 1999). Such biological embedding is potentially underpinned by epigenetic regulation, that is, processes that may alter gene expression without changing the underlying DNA sequence. The best‐characterized epigenetic mark in humans is DNA methylation (DNAm), specifically methylation at cytosine–guanine dinucleotides (CpGs), which is relatively stable and can be measured easily, and is therefore commonly used to study the biological embedding of early‐life experiences (Aristizabal et al. 2020). At the fundamental level, DNAm plays a significant role in cellular differentiation during embryogenesis and tissue differentiation, leading to distinct DNAm profiles across tissues and cell types. Therefore, DNAm associated with social experience could be reflected differently, dependent on the types of biological samples used in the analyses (Aristizabal et al. 2020; Jiang et al. 2015).

Although severe early‐life adversity, such as childhood trauma, abuse, and poverty, has been a primary focus of human DNAm research and was often shown to be associated with DNA signatures (Parade et al. 2016), daily experience of more prevalent yet still stressful familial factors, especially cumulatively, may also be linked to health and development. For example, subjective report of financial stress and parental psychopathology are more common adverse exposures among children (18.8% and 7.1%, respectively) than physical or sexual abuse (4%) (Calthorpe and Pantell 2021; Grummitt et al. 2021). In fact, recent research has shown that cumulative family stress (cumulative FS) is associated with biomarkers of inflammation, cardio/metabolic systems, and endocrine systems (Chiang and Bai 2022; Deighton et al. 2018). DNAm was proposed as a potential biological pathway underlying associations between cumulative FS and health outcomes, with emerging evidence found in the prenatal, infant, and early childhood periods (Barker 2018; Unternaehrer and Meinlschmidt 2016). These very early time points have been of interest for studying biological embedding due to its sensitivity to environmental exposures (Jones et al. 2018). However, it is also important to understand whether and how cumulative FS can be reflected by DNAm during later childhood, which represents a developmental period of important psychosocial transitions, heightened stress, and peak years for onset of common psychopathologies (Mundy et al. 2015; Washington 2009).

Epigenetic Associations of Cumulative FS

The biological embedding of severe psychosocial adversity, such as childhood maltreatment or other traumatic events, through DNAm as one potential pathway have been widely studied (Gladish et al. 2022; Zhang and Liu 2022). However, the sensitivity of DNAm to more prevalent, albeit less severe, types of psychosocial stress during childhood still requires further scrutiny (Obradović et al. 2012). These include stressors in the family context, such as maternal depression, parenting stress, and financial stress, all of which have been linked to health and developmental outcomes in children (Obradović et al. 2012). Previous research primarily assessed the independent associations of single stressors with pediatric development, yet children are rarely exposed to only one type of stressor, especially given the high level of co‐occurrence between familial risk factors (Goulter et al. 2021). Therefore, a cumulative risk perspective is increasingly being adopted to conceptualize and examine aggregate roles of co‐occurring stressors and thus provide a more comprehensive representation of children's adverse experiences in the family context (Bush et al. 2018; Essex et al. 2013; Goulter et al. 2021). Using this cumulative environmental risk approach, both cross‐sectional and longitudinal associations have been identified with a wide range of negative child health and behavioral outcomes, including internalizing and externalizing problems (Goulter et al. 2021), neurocognitive development (J. Kim et al. 2022; Payne et al. 2021), and psychosocial adjustment (Santini et al. 2021). These cumulative risks were also shown to be biologically embedded in children, including observed associations with biomarkers of inflammation (e.g., C‐reactive protein [CRP]) and stress reactivity (e.g., cortisol reactivity and autonomic nervous system) (Deighton et al. 2018; Goulter et al. 2021; Iob et al. 2020; Lacey et al. 2020; Ouellet‐Morin et al. 2021; Wiens et al. 2020).

Research on biological embedding of postnatal exposures has also identified differential DNAm with aspects of FS (Zhang and Liu 2022), including associations between increased DNAm on NR3C1 (specifically exon 1F), encoding the glucocorticoid receptor, with postnatal parental stress reported across seven human studies (Turecki and Meaney 2016; Zhang and Liu 2022). Additionally, higher levels of subclinical parental psychiatric symptoms, such as depression, anxiety, or general internalizing/externalizing symptoms, were linked to differential DNAm of candidate genes related to glucocorticoid and dopamine regulation in offspring, including NR3C1 exon 1F, 11b‐HSD2, and SLC6A3 (Cimino 2018; Cimino et al. 2019; Conradt et al. 2016; Gardini et al. 2022; Hill et al. 2019). Subjective report of financial stress is also linked to differential DNAm in candidate genes, such as genes encoding serotonin, for example, 5HTTLPR (Beach et al. 2014) and SLC6A4 (Provenzi et al. 2016), and related to neurodevelopment (e.g., BDNF) (Cerutti et al. 2021). Nonetheless, there is a substantial debate regarding the technical and statistical approaches underpinning these findings (e.g., lack of effect size cutoff and/or statistical adjustment with cell type proportions) and in turn the biological meaning and interpretability of these early findings have been questioned (Jones et al. 2018; Vinkers et al. 2015). Furthermore, these studies rarely accounted for the potential roles of the parent–child relationship quality in these processes. Psychological studies have shown that a stressful family environment may often be associated with lower levels of supportive caregiving behaviors and poorer parent–child relationship quality (Jackson and Choi 2018). Similar to FS, these behaviors and interactions could be reflected at the molecular level. For example, some human studies provided support for the linkage of certain caregiving behaviors, both positive (e.g., mother–child bonding, warmth, responsiveness, and touch) and negative (e.g., harsh parenting), with DNAm in children (Brody et al. 2021; Houtepen et al. 2018; Merrill et al. 2019; Moore et al. 2017; Unternaehrer and Meinlschmidt 2016), which could help explain the DNAm associations with psychosocial stress at the broader family level.

Most of these aforementioned studies relied on a similar set of candidate genes and only limited research has been conducted in a genome‐wide context (Merrill et al. 2019; Unternaehrer and Meinlschmidt 2016). However, one challenge faced by the field of social epigenetics is the poor reproducibility of EWAS findings, which in part could be explained by the heterogeneities of measures. Among the handful of studies that took an EWAS approach to investigating the biological embedding of FS in early life, there are also heterogeneities in how FS was operationalized and measured across studies (Zhang and Liu 2022). Specifically, some studies investigated the differential DNAm of multiple types of stressors separately (Dunn et al. 2019; Houtepen et al. 2018), while some examined their combined or cumulative effects (Bush et al. 2018; Essex et al. 2013; Papale et al. 2018; Smith et al. 2011; Tay et al. 2019). Nonetheless, the overall biological pathways related to these CpGs shared a common theme of stress response, brain development, and immunity (Zhang and Liu 2022). Most relevant to the present study are the two previous EWASs regarding the cumulative effect of multiple types of FS (including maternal depression, financial stress, and parenting stress) in children (Bush et al. 2018; Essex et al. 2013). These studies also found differential DNAm linked to FS in genes related to neurodevelopment and immune regulation.

Cross‐Tissue Investigation of DNAm

Another challenge to comparing and interpreting findings across EWASs is the different peripheral tissue sources. Buccal and blood samples represent two readily accessible tissue candidates commonly used in human epigenetic studies. While human epigenetic studies are most often performed using blood (Smith et al. 2015; Yousefi et al. 2022), buccal samples can be obtained less invasively from cheek swabs and are used more frequently in pediatric studies (Parade et al. 2021). These two tissue types are both of interest for studying biological embedding of social experience, albeit for different reasons. Specifically, most DNAm studies on children's exposure to FS have been conducted with buccal epithelial cells (BECs) (Bush et al. 2018; Carola et al. 2021; Cimino 2018; Conradt et al. 2016; Essex et al. 2013; Garg et al. 2018; Houtepen et al. 2018). BECs originate from a similar germ layer to brain cells during early development and while somewhat controversial, they have been suggested to serve as a surrogate tissue for the brain, so are of interest in psychosocial research due to their potential proximity to human brain function and psychological symptoms, especially in children (Lowe et al. 2013; Smith et al. 2015). On the other hand, blood samples are of interest for analysis of immune function and inflammation. There is ample evidence for links between stress and immune system outcomes, including inflammatory processes and biomarkers (Seiler et al. 2020). Therefore, blood samples are excellent candidates for analysis related to stress exposure (Jones et al. 2018).

Given the tissue specificity of DNAm profiles due to its role in cellular differentiation and maintenance of cellular identity, findings regarding DNAm associations could be specific to the tissue in which they are examined (Jiang et al. 2015). Cell type differences represent a contributor to DNAm profile variations in tissues. Buccal samples contain two main cell types, that is, BECs and neutrophils, whereas blood samples primarily consist of immune cells and the actual cell‐type composition depends on the sample type. For example, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) consist of lymphocytes and monocytes. Furthermore, genetic influences occur through methylation quantitative trait loci (mQTLs), which are genetic variants strongly associated with DNAm level of nearby CpGs. mQTLs can also be tissue specific and may drive differential DNAm between tissues (Islam et al. 2019; Jiang et al. 2015).

Concordance (or the lack thereof) of DNAm patterns across tissues has been studied (Islam et al. 2019; Jiang et al. 2015; Loke et al. 2021; Ollikainen et al. 2010). The differences across tissues within adult individuals were found to be larger than that across individuals within the same tissue (Jiang et al. 2015). In pediatric samples, a comprehensive cross‐tissue comparison of DNAm in matched pediatric BECs and PBMCs was conducted using the same cohort as the present study, the Gene Expression Collaborative for Kids Only (GECKO) (Islam et al. 2019). This previous study indicated that 36% of CpGs across the genome were differentially methylated across tissues, which was validated in a separate pediatric cohort. Differentially methylated CpGs increased to 76.2% when only focused on those associated with mQTL, supporting the importance of tissue‐specific genetic influence.

Closely relevant to the current study are the cross‐tissue analyses between blood and buccal DNAm associations with retrospectively reported childhood adversity in adults, which showed a moderate to high correlation of the regression coefficients of the top 1000 CpGs in blood and buccal (Houtepen et al. 2018). However, these top CpGs were selected without a statistical significance cutoff and could include spurious associations. Indeed, when comparing significant DNAm associations across matched tissues from the same individuals, a larger number of associations with social variables were identified in PBMCs than BECs in adult (Jiang et al. 2015). Although there is a scarcity of cross‐tissue comparison of the association with FS in pediatric samples, one could speculate similar discordance may be observed. Given that the identified significant associations could vastly depend on the tissue source selected (Jiang et al. 2015), it is key for epigenetic studies to investigate the extent to which the associations are tissue‐specific or concordant. More specifically, it is crucial to address if DNAm from a specific tissue source is more sensitive to early‐life stress in children, which can help inform which tissue source, or if both, is appropriate for capturing biological embedding of cumulative FS.

The Present Study

The present study took a cumulative risk approach to examine the biological embedding of mother‐reported cumulative FS, which could spill over to influence the whole family environment and experiences of the children as posited by the family stress theory (Repetti and Bai 2025). We studied this through DNAm in two tissues commonly obtained in pediatric DNAm research, that is, BECs and PBMCs in 218 and 51 school‐age children, respectively, in the Canadian GECKO cohort (Islam et al. 2019). Specifically, we investigated (1) the epigenome‐wide associations of cumulative FS with DNAm in both tissues, (2) tissue specificity and cross‐tissue concordance of DNAm associations in relation to cumulative FS with the 50 matched tissue samples that provided this unique opportunity, and (3) the roles played by mother–child relationship quality in explaining the association between cumulative FS and DNAm in children.

First, we hypothesized that DNAm levels, especially in genes involved in stress‐related pathways, may be associated with cumulative FS. Second, given the strong tissue specificity of DNAm based on both biological function and as demonstrated in previous research, we expected to find unique associations of DNAm with cumulative FS for each tissue type and stronger associations in PBMCs. Finally, we explored whether mother–child relationship quality, especially negative relationship given the context of stress, would explain some of the observed associations between DNAm and cumulative FS.

Methods

Participants

This study included 219 mother–child dyads representing a subsample of the GECKO cohort, a prospective Canadian cohort of 402 families with 6‐ to 11‐year‐old children designed to examine how social environment was related to child development and gene regulation. Mothers were recruited from Vancouver, British Columbia, and provided informed consent for the collection of survey data from themselves as well as buccal DNA samples from their children (age range 6–11 years, n = 376) and/or blood DNA samples in a follow‐up study (age range 7–13 years; 0.5–2.3 years after buccal sample collection, n = 92). This DNAm substudy was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Board at the University of British Columbia.

The analyses were conducted based on a subsample of children that met the selection criteria of having complete meta data on relevant variables for this study and DNAm data from BECs (n = 218; 47.2% female) and PBMCs prepared from whole blood samples (n = 51; 43.1% female), with both BEC and PBMC DNAm available from 50 children (44.2% female). The ethnic identities of mothers and children were reported by the mothers using a multiple‐choice item with 25 possible options, including “multiracial/multiethnic” and “other,” which were then collapsed into six groups: White, Black, Asian, First Nation, multiracial, and other. Mothers primarily self‐reported as White (48.2% and 64.7% for buccal and blood samples, respectively), married (75.2 %), and college‐educated (87.2 %) (full demographic information is presented in Table 1). In this sample, 52.8% of children were classified as White according to maternal reports. Children were aged 6.98–12.97 years (mean = 9.38, SD = 0.97) and 7.00–12.94 years (mean = 10.8, SD = 1.14) at the time of buccal and blood sample collection, respectively.

Measures

Cumulative FS

Measures of cumulative FS were obtained from mothers through self‐reported surveys based on a composite of three FS measures, that is, maternal depressive symptoms, parenting stress, and financial stress, which were also used as indicators of cumulative FS in previous pediatric DNAm studies (Bush et al. 2018; Essex et al. 2013). Maternal depressive symptoms were assessed by the Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression (CES‐D) Scale (Radloff 1977), which showed good reliability with these data (Cronbach's alpha = 0.83). Maternal parenting stress was measured by the Parenting Role Overload scale, which included five items regarding the amount of stress the mother experiences on a 5‐point scale (e.g., “How often do things you do add up to being too much?”), and the average score was then calculated (Essex et al. 2013). This scale also showed good reliability (Cronbach's alpha = 0.72). Maternal financial stress was assessed based on the average of four items (e.g., “How often have you thought about any current or future money problems?”) on a 5‐point scale with good reliability (Cronbach's alpha = 0.80). The scores from these three scales were significantly positively correlated (r = 0.22–0.36, p<0.001). A composite score of cumulative FS was created by summing the standardized scores of the three scales.

Mother–Child Relationship Quality

Mother–child relationship quality was assessed through both survey reports and interviews with mothers. To leverage the rich information from all parenting‐relevant scales, principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted to reduce the dimensions. Measures included two surveys, the Parent Feelings Questionnaire (PFQ) (Deater‐Deckard and Panneton 2017) and Parent–Child Conflict Tactics Scale (CTSPC) (Straus et al. 1998), along with the modified Five‐Minute Speech Sample (FMSS) interview (Caspi et al. 2004; McGuire and Earls 1994).

The PFQ included 34 items eliciting responses regarding parents’ feelings about their relationship with their children. Specifically, the negative parent–child relationship subscale was created by averaging the mother's rating of 13 negative statements on a 5‐point Likert scale (e.g., “My child and I fight or argue more than I would like to”). Two additional subscales for parental emotions related to their children, positive and negative emotions, were created by averaging the mother's rating on a 10‐point frequency scale for 10 positive (e.g., happy) and 10 negative (e.g., angry) emotions, respectively. The reliability was good for most PFQ scales (Cronbach's alpha = 0.722–0.907), while that of negative emotions was subthreshold (0.638).

The CTSPC assessed parental behaviors during conflict with their children. Mothers were asked to indicate how often they used each behavior on a 7‐point scale (0 = never; 6 = more than 20 times in the past year). Ratings higher than 2 were later recoded to represent chronicity, with 3–5, 6–10, 11–20, and > 20 times in the past year coded as 4, 8, 15, and 20, respectively. Two subscales from the CTSPC, nonviolent discipline (Cronbach's alpha = 0.752) and psychological aggression (Cronbach's alpha = 0.526), were used in the analysis.

In the modified FMSS, mothers were asked to describe their children in the first 5‐min section of the interview and then describe their relationship with their children in the second 5‐min section. Speech samples were recorded and transcribed verbatim for later coding by three trained raters. Six variables were coded from the speech samples, among which positive comments, negative comments, general warmth, and general negativity were used in the analysis. The interrater reliability was good (agreement = 0.68–0.96).

PCA with oblique rotation was conducted using nine variables related to parenting behaviors. The scree plot suggested a two‐factor solution (Figure S1). The first two PCs explained 54.13% of the total variance. The first factor, negative mother–child relationship quality, accounted for 35.8% of the variance, with negative parent–child relationship, parental negative emotion, nonviolent discipline, psychological aggression, general negativity, and negative comments loaded onto the factor (Table 2). The second factor, positive mother–child relationship quality, accounted for 18.3% of the variance, with positive parent–child relationship quality, positive emotions, positive comments, and general warmth loaded onto the factor (Table 2). Scores of the two PCs were extracted for downstream analysis.

DNAm and Genetic Data

All procedures were approved by the institutional review board at the University of British Columbia. Full details of sample collection, extraction, DNAm and genotype profiling, and data preprocessing are presented in the Supporting Information. Briefly, buccal samples were obtained from children using Isohelix Buccal Swabs (Cell Projects Ltd., Kent, UK) and blood samples were collected at a follow‐up visit 8 months to 2.06 years later. PBMCs were isolated from the blood samples through centrifugation, washed, and resuspended in R10 medium as described previously (Islam et al. 2019; Miller et al. 2009). DNA extracted from BECs and PBMCs were applied to the Illumina Infinium HumanMethylation450K BeadChip DNAm array. Saliva samples were also collected from children to extract DNA for genome‐wide single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) profiling using the Illumina Infinium PsychChip BeadChip genotyping array. The SNP data were subjected to multidimensional scaling (MDS), and the first three MDS variables, explaining ∼80% of the variance (Figure S2), were included in subsequent analyses to account for genetic ancestry. Child ethnicity reported by the mothers was significantly correlated with the first MDS variable (Spearman's rho = −0.40, p = 1.8e‐09) (Figure S2).

All available DNAm data including three tissue types and samples not used in the present study (376 BECs, 92 PBMCs, 55 dried blood spots) were preprocessed together to reduce the impact of batch effects. After preprocessing and removal of samples (technical replicates, duplicate samples from the same individuals, and siblings), 218 BECs and 51 PBMCs (all with genotype data), of which 50 were matched from the same individuals, and 485,577 probes in each sample were used for subsequent analyses. Note that the number of samples was different from that published previously (Islam et al. 2019) due to different selection criterion, the nature of our interrogation, and the updated QC pipeline. To reduce the testing space and address the equal significance likelihood assumption of the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) false discovery rate (FDR) control method (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995; Korthauer et al. 2019), only 270,133 and 204,753 variable probes were included in subsequent analyses for BECs and PBMCs, respectively.

Data Analysis

Computational Cell‐Type Proportion Estimation

To account for cell‐type heterogeneity, a well‐established driver of interindividual DNAm variation (Jones et al. 2018), we used DNAm‐based methods to computationally estimate cell‐type proportions in BECs (Zheng et al. 2018) and PBMCs (Houseman et al. 2012). Six immune cell types (B, NK, CD4T, CD8T, monocyte, and neutrophil cells) and eight cell types (epithelial cell and fibroblast in addition to the six immune cell types) were estimated for PBMCs and BECs, respectively (Figure S3). Epithelial cell proportion (mean = 82.11%, SD = 13.30%), was included as a covariate in subsequent analyses of BECs. For PBMCs, three PCs accounting for 92.57% of the overall variation of cell‐type proportions were included as covariates in DNAm models.

EWAS With Robust Linear Regression for Cumulative FS

Child characteristic covariates known to be significantly associated with DNAm levels, including sex, age, genetic ancestry, and cell type, were included in the model (Jones et al. 2018). The robust linear regression model used for the site‐by‐site EWAS and candidate analysis was as follows: CpG∼FamilyAdversity+Sex+Age+BatchEffect+CellType+GeneticMDS1+GeneticMDS2+GeneticMDS3+ε

All variable probes (at least 5% differences in methylation levels across samples in the fifth and 95th percentile) were included for the exploratory EWAS (BECs: 270,133; PBMCs: 204,753). Given the large number of tests, we used the BH FDR control method for multiple‐test correction (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995). A medium‐confidence statistical threshold was set at FDR < 0.20 corresponding to approximately p < 2.00e‐04 and < 2.00e‐05 in our candidate analysis and EWAS, respectively, and a high‐confidence statistical threshold at FDR < 0.05 corresponding to p < 1.245e‐7 in EWAS, to determine significant associations between DNAm at each site with cumulative FS.

The change in DNAm level associated with cumulative FS was quantified by the change in DNAm value, or beta value (Δβ) (Jones et al. 2018). In our analyses, Δβ was calculated by extracting the beta coefficient of cumulative FS from the robust linear regression models described above, representing the change on the y‐axis (β value) divided by the x‐axis (cumulative FS) while controlling for other covariates, and then multiplying the coefficient with the range of cumulative FS values between the fifth and 95th percentiles to reduce the effects of outliers. The resulting Δβ value represented the change in β value at each CpG associated with cumulative FS while adjusting for other covariates. We also set a technical threshold of |Δβ|> 0.03, as it was likely to be greater than technical noise given the observations in previous studies using pediatric samples, the same DNAm array, and similar preprocessing pipelines (Merrill et al. 2019, 2021). We used BACON, a Bayesian method, to check for bias and inflation in our EWAS models (van Iterson et al. 2017), which showed no inflation of the two EWAS models in our analyses (estimated inflations of 0.95 and 0.96 for BECs and PBMCs, respectively) (Figure S4).

Candidate Gene Analyses With Robust Linear Regression for Cumulative FS

For a priori candidate analyses, we identified a list of biologically relevant candidate genes reported to be related to early‐life psychosocial adversity and stress response: NR3C1, FKBP5, SLC6A4, MAOA, GAD1, SLC6A3, COMT, OXTR, BDNF, and ADCYAP1R1 (Mourtzi et al. 2021; Schiele et al. 2020). These genes were then used to search for 20 additional target genes sharing biological pathways but studied less frequently in previous candidate analyses using the web‐based gene network and functional annotation tool GeneMANIA (http://genemania.org/) with max resultant genes set to 20 and gene ontology biological process‐based weighting. A total of 30 target genes resulting in 366 and 323 variable probes for BECs and PBMCs, respectively, were included in candidate analyses (Tables S1 and S2). The same analytical models, statistical threshold (FDR < 0.20), and technical threshold (|Δβ|> 0.03) were used for these analyses.

Characterization of Identified Differentially Methylated Sites Associated With Cumulative FS

CpGs found to be significantly associated with cumulative FS were annotated to genes, genomic regions, and chromosomal and map locations based on UCSC Genome Browser (GRCH37/hg19) (Price et al. 2013). We explored the biological functions and gene network interactions of the annotated genes using the GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/) database, EWAS Atlas (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/ewas), and GeneMANIA (http://genemania.org/).

Cross‐Tissue Concordance and Specificity

DNAm profiles are highly tissue‐specific even within the same individual (Islam et al. 2019; Jiang et al. 2015). We examined the concordance of DNAm levels across (1) all overlapping CpGs and (2) at the identified CpGs across BECs and PBMCs from the same individuals by Pearson's correlation analysis. In addition, we examined whether the CpGs identified in one tissue were also differentially methylated in the other when restricted to a smaller testing space with lower multiple‐testing burden. Cross‐tissue information of identified CpGs was extracted from a previous study involving thorough cross‐tissue comparison in the same cohort (Islam et al. 2019). Furthermore, we assessed the blood–buccal correlation using the Iowa Methylation Array Graphing Experiment for Comparison of Peripheral Tissue and Gray matter (IMAGE‐CpG) database (http://han‐lab.org/methylation/default/imageCpG) (Braun et al. 2019).

To characterize the identified sites in the brain, we also examined their variability in brain and correlations between DNAm levels in blood and brain using BECon (Blood–Brain Epigenetic Concordance) (https://redgar598.shinyapps.io/BECon/) (Edgar et al. 2017) and covering BA 7 (superior parietal lobule), BA 10 (frontal cortex), and BA 20 (temporal cortex) and IMAGE‐CpG (Braun et al. 2019) covering the amygdala, hippocampus, and temporal cortex. The average correlations across brain regions were reported.

mQTL Analysis

Genetic variations at some SNPs known as mQTLs are previously reported to be associated with DNAm levels of nearby CpGs (Islam et al. 2019; Smith et al. 2014). We determined whether the identified CpGs were associated with any mQTLs based on previous examination conducted in GECKO (Islam et al. 2019). However, since the list of DNAm sites previously interrogated was selected based on overlap across discovery and validation cohorts, not all DNAm sites investigated in the current study were included. For the sites that were not included, we used the same statistical approach as previously described to identify mQTLs (Islam et al. 2019).

Contribution of Mother–Child Relationship Quality to DNAm

We conducted contribution analysis to examine the potential contributions of mother–child relationship quality to DNAm levels at the identified CpGs, as in previous DNAm studies (Merrill et al. 2021). The percentage contributions for negative and positive relationship quality PCs (described above) were calculated as follows: βFamilyAdversityBaseModel−βFamilyAdversityAdjustedModelβFamilyAdversityBaseModel×100

Here, the base model was the linear regression model used for candidate analysis or EWAS discovery, while the adjusted model was the base model after including a relationship quality PC as a covariate. This was conducted for each CpG of interest identified in candidate analysis and EWAS. Two separate sets of analyses were conducted for negative and positive maternal parenting factors to examine their individual roles. All data analysis steps were performed in R version 4.2.0 and Bioconductor version 3.15.0.

DNAm‐Based Inflammatory Markers Predictors

To examine whether the identified CpGs were associated with immune responses in our samples, two DNAm‐estimated inflammatory markers, including interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) and CRP, were calculated using predictors previously developed by two independent groups with different multiethnic cohorts (Stevenson et al. 2021; Wielscher et al. 2022). A total of 35 and 1511 CpGs were used for the predictions of IL‐6 and CRP, respectively, with seven CpGs overlapping across the predictors. The predicted values were the linear combination of the DNAm levels at these CpGs multiplied by the corresponding estimated coefficients previously reported.

The data for buccal samples have been deposited in NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) (accession number GSE137903) (McEwen et al. 2020). The analysis scripts (R Markdown) used in the analyses and supporting the findings are available from the first author upon reasonable request.

Tissue Specificity of DNAm Across BECs and PBMCs

Although BECs had a larger set of variable probes than PBMCs, comparison of the overall Δβ distributions across the 186,618 overlapping variable probes in both tissues showed that PBMCs had larger Δβs (i.e., larger differences in DNAm associated with cumulative FS) (Δβ mean = −0.0035, SD = 0.017, median = −0.0028) than BECs (mean = 0.00075, SD = 0.0097, median = 0.00039) (Figure S5). Across tissues, there was a significant but weak correlation between Δβ of all overlapping CpGs (r = 0.043, p < 2.2e‐16) (Figure S5).

To investigate the cross‐tissue signals within individuals associated with cumulative FS, we leveraged the matched tissue samples from 50 children. We first tested the correlations of DNAm levels across matched BECs and PBMCs at the seven cumulative FS‐associated CpGs identified in PBMCs (Table 4). One of the seven sites (cg17755321) was invariable in BECs, and therefore cross‐tissue concordance was not examined. Significant positive correlations were found for only two of the six sites that were variable in both tissues, cg22659953 and cg22664962 (Figure 3), indicating tissue specificity. Only one of the six CpGs, cg17487741 annotated to RCBTB1, was significantly associated with cumulative FS with nominal p < 0.05 and| Δβ |> 0.03| in both tissues (Table 5), but it should be noted that the FDR after BH correction was 0.226 (i.e., larger than our statistical threshold) in BEC.

Post Hoc Characterization of CpGs Associated With Cumulative FS

Since the identified differential DNAm signals in PBMC were most relevant to stress and the immune system, we conducted post hoc analyses to examine the associations between DNAm levels at these sites and DNAm‐estimated inflammatory markers, including IL‐6 and CRP. Estimated IL‐6 and CRP were highly correlated (r = 0.817, p = 2.56e‐13) (Figure S6). Among the seven significant sites, we found that two sites (cg00052684 annotated to FKBP5 and cg23950278) were significantly and negatively associated with both estimated IL‐6 and CRP (rs = −0.318 and −0.596, ps = 0.023 and 3.936e‐06, respectively; Figure S6), and one additional site (cg04611670 annotated to MEGF6) was also significantly and negatively correlated with estimated IL‐6 (r = −0.304, p = 0.030).

It is also important to assess how these differential DNAm signals in blood, mostly relevant to stress and the immune system, may be reflected in the brain, which would have more proximal implications for psychiatric outcomes (Braun et al. 2019; Edgar et al. 2017). As blood is a peripheral tissue and its DNAm level may not always be directly related to that in the brain, we examined post hoc the blood–brain DNAm concordance using BECon (Edgar et al. 2017) and IMAGE‐CpG (Braun et al. 2019) (Table S5; Figure S7). While two CpGs (cg00052684 and cg23950278) were not available in the BECon database, DNAm at cg22664962, cg17487741, and cg22659953 showed positive correlation with that in brain (mean correlations across all brain regions: r = 0.40, 0.14, and 0.09, respectively). This is supported by the IMAGE‐CpG database, which assessed different brain regions than BECon, two of these three CpGs (cg22659953 and cg22664962) also showed significant positive correlations across brain and blood (Spearman's correlation coefficients across all brain regions: rho = 0.456, p = 0.039 and rho = 0.555, p = 0.010, respectively). In contrast, DNAm at cg04611670 and cg17755321 in blood showed negative correlations with that in brain (mean correlations across all brain regions: r = −0.17 and −0.14, respectively). These correlations were all above the 50th percentile of all CpG correlation values, suggesting that these CpGs were more strongly correlated in brain and blood than average; in particular, cg22664962 was in the 90th percentile.

Negative Mother–Child Relationship Contributed to Relation of DNAm at SLC6A3‐Related Medium‐Confidence CpG With Cumulative FS

We examined the contribution of mother–child relationship quality to the association between children's DNAm and cumulative FS at the seven significant CpGs identified in PBMCs. The two mother–child relationship quality PCs created from parenting variables available in this cohort were correlated with cumulative FS in the expected direction, with the negative relationship PC showing a positive correlation and the positive relationship PC showing a negative correlation with cumulative FS, although they did not meet the significance threshold (Pearson's r = 0.178, p = 0.226; r = −0.126, p = 0.391, respectively). Nevertheless, given the significant DNAm associations with mother‐child relationship quality and the strong link between cumulative FS and mother–child relationship quality suggested in the literature (Jackson and Choi 2018; Lovejoy et al. 2000), we examined the contributions of relationship quality to the relation of DNAm and cumulative FS as a possible suppressor or amplifier (). Negative relationship quality showed a contribution of 21.69% to the association between cumulative FS and PBMC DNAm at cg2265995 (Table 6). However, no significant contribution was observed for positive mother–child relationship quality (Table S6).

Discussion

Cumulative FS may get “under the skin” of children through biological pathways and influence their long‐term health and development. Here, we examined DNAm in school‐age children associated with mother‐reported cumulative FS in two readily accessible peripheral tissues, BECs and PBMCs, and found significant associations primarily in PBMC DNAm, suggesting tissue specificity. One of these associations was partially explained by negative mother–child relationship quality, indicating the contribution of more direct family factor to the biological embedding of the family environment.

Children's Blood DNAm in Stress‐ and Immune‐Related Genes Were Associated With Cumulative FS

To investigate the associations of DNAm with cumulative FS, we first examined stress‐related candidate genes. One high‐confidence and one medium‐confidence DNAm CpGs associated with cumulative FS passed our statistical and technical thresholds in children's PBMCs, but only one buccal DNAm CpG passed the statistical but not the technical threshold. One identified CpG was annotated to FKBP5, a stress‐responsive gene in gene networks linked to GR signaling and inflammatory response (Wu et al. 2010) and an important regulator of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis (Pratt and Toft 1997). Although this specific CpG has not been identified in previous studies related to adversity, DNAm sites residing within FKBP5 have been robustly linked with early‐life adversity and stress‐related psychiatric symptoms, such as posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and other anxiety disorders (Bierer et al. 2020; Klengel et al. 2013; Matosin et al. 2023; Unternaehrer and Meinlschmidt 2016; Zannas et al. 2016). Another CpG identified in PBMCs resides in SLC6A3 encoding dopamine transporter, which has been a key player in investigations on genetic factors involved in a range of psychiatric disorders, including mood disorders, PTSD, schizophrenia, drug addiction, and oppositional defiant disorder in adults (Salatino‐Oliveira et al. 2018; Van Der Zwaluw et al. 2009). Along the same lines, the specific SLC6A3‐annotated CpG identified in our study was linked to fetal alcohol spectrum disorder in two previous EWASs (Lussier et al. 2018; Portales‐Casamar et al. 2016). Differential methylation of CpGs in SLC6A3 has also been associated with emotional and behavioral functioning in children (Carola et al. 2021; Cimino 2018; Ding et al. 2017; Xu et al. 2015) and other adverse environmental conditions, including child abuse and maternal smoking (Joubert et al. 2016; Yang et al. 2013). Therefore, our candidate gene investigation replicated some previous DNAm findings related to early‐life adversity and emotional functioning, and supported our hypothesis of the biological embedding of cumulative FS through DNAm in stress‐associated genes.

Beyond the stress‐related candidate CpGs, we discovered one high‐confidence and four medium‐confidence DNAm CpGs linked to cumulative FS using an epigenome‐wide approach in PBMCs. Several of these identified CpGs in PBMCs are associated with immune function or reside within or near immune‐related genes. Specifically, differential DNAm in TNF, a key immune gene encoding tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF‐α) (Louis et al. 1998), has been linked to early‐life experience, such as childhood maltreatment (Chiang and Bai 2022; Deighton et al. 2018; Miller et al. 2011) and protective parenting, and later proinflammatory responses (Provenzi et al. 2016; Unternaehrer and Meinlschmidt 2016). In particular, the TNF‐annotated CpG identified in the present study was also reported in a study on DNAm associations with mother–infant attachment style (Merrill et al. 2021), corroborating its role in biological embedding of early social experience. Similarly, DNAm in RCBTB1 encoding RCC1 and BTB domain‐containing protein, a disease‐related gene (e.g., various cancers) mainly expressed in basophils, was reported to be linked to negative environmental exposures, such as air pollution (Eze et al. 2020). Both RCBTB1 and MEGF6 were also suggested to be involved in inflammation and immune‐related gene networks, while one of the intergenic CpGs identified here (cg23950278) also resides close to TRAF6 encoding TNF receptor‐associated factor. In addition, differential DNAm of the two intergenic CpGs was implicated in early‐life exposures, such as maternal folic acid supplementation and placental microbes (Joubert et al. 2016; Tomlinson et al. 2017). Taken together, our findings indicated connections between early‐life stress and DNAm signals in inflammation‐ and immune‐related genes. As all signals were discovered in PBMCs, containing a high proportion of immune cells, it was unsurprising to find significant associations in immune‐related genes.

Our results were consistent with existing theoretical models and accumulating evidence of dysregulated stress and immune responses in relation to early cumulative FS. Early‐life stress, especially in the family environment, has long been linked to elevated risk of psychopathology (Humphreys et al. 2019; A. W. Kim et al. 2020; Moriarity et al. 2023). One pathway was proposed to involve the disruption of stress response tendencies and long‐term dysregulated adaptations to stress due to daily stressful experiences at home, which in turn heighten the risk of psychopathology later in life (Gardini et al. 2022; Merrill et al. 2019; Unternaehrer et al. 2020). Our findings added to the existing literature by suggesting that DNAm in genes involved in the stress responses (e.g., FKBP5) may underlie these dysregulated adaptations, leading to increased susceptibility to psychopathology.

Furthermore, early‐life adversity and dysregulated stress have been linked to poor physical health, especially through inflammation and immune pathways (Chiang and Bai 2022). Environmental programming models, such as the Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD) (Barrett 2017) and the Predictive–Adaptive Response model (Gluckman et al. 2005), posited that children may develop phenotypes considered adaptive to stimuli in their early environment but contribute to later adverse health outcomes. Indeed, our post hoc analyses supported that higher levels of DNAm‐estimated CRP and IL‐6 were associated with lower DNAm levels at two differentially methylated CpGs, which was linked to higher cumulative FS. These consistent associations across two inflammatory markers estimated with DNAm tools developed from different cohorts and by different groups gave us more confidence that these differentially methylated sites were linked to inflammation in these children. These results aligned with previous studies showing that exposure to cumulative FS in early life may induce a chronic proinflammatory state in children, reflected by changes in the proportion of immune cells in blood, increased cytokine levels, and DNAm of key immune‐related genes (Beach et al. 2015; Chiang and Bai 2022; Miller et al. 2011) as well as elevated levels of both CRP and IL‐6 in children (Miller and Chen 2010; Slopen et al. 2013). A recent meta‐analysis showed that the association between childhood stress and inflammation became stronger across the life course (Chiang and Bai 2022). The present study provided further support for the contribution of early cumulative FS to the inflammatory state and immune health of children through differential DNAm at key immune‐related genes, although further studies are required to replicate these results and investigate their potential functional significance.

Cross‐Tissue Concordance Across BECs, PBMCs, and Brain

We found that although BECs were more variable than PBMCs, as reported previously (Islam et al. 2019), PBMCs had stronger associations with cumulative FS. These findings align with both the tissue‐specific variability in DNAm and the stronger signals of social factors reflected in PBMCs than BECs DNAm, as reported in previous cross‐tissue comparisons (Jiang et al. 2015). It is worth noting that despite the smaller sample size in our PBMC samples, which is often associated with less statistical power to detect effects, PBMCs were still the primary tissue in which we identified significant differential DNAm linked to cumulative FS. One possible explanation is that the power issue may have been alleviated because larger effect sizes, another major component of power calculations, were detectable in blood, as indicated by the greater overall Δβs in PBMCs than in BECs. These larger effect sizes in blood could be due to its stronger relevance to immune and inflammatory processes associated with cumulative FS. Furthermore, given the link between stress and immune health, our findings suggested that DNAm in PBMCs, especially in genes involved in regulating immune function and inflammation, may readily reflect the biological embedding of early‐life stress. Our findings highlighted the importance of choosing a tissue relevant to the variable of interest, and in the case of cumulative FS, blood may be the most suitable sample type for future investigations.

We found limited concordance in differential methylation associated with cumulative FS. No CpGs were consistently, significantly associated with both matched PBMCs and BECs, even when narrowing down to only the seven CpGs already identified in PBMCs. This is unsurprising given the classification of these identified CpGs in a previous study on cross‐tissue comparisons using the same cohort (Islam et al. 2019). Four out of the seven identified CpGs (cg22659953, cg04611670, cg17487741, and cg17755321) were reported to be differentially methylated across BECs and PBMCs, defined as FDR < 0.05 and |Δβ|> 0.05 (Islam et al. 2019), supporting that DNAm at these identified CpGs were discordant across tissues. Only one identified CpG, cg22664962, was reported to be variable across individuals and highly correlated between BECs and PBMCs (i.e., informative sites; rho > 0.47) (Islam et al. 2019). The discordance in DNAm associations, or the significant associations in PBMC and the lack thereof in BEC, converges with existing findings of substantially distinct DNAm profiles across these tissues (Islam et al. 2019; Jiang et al. 2015).

The blood–brain concordance database indicated that most DNAm CpGs associated with cumulative FS in PBMCs were variable and significantly correlated with that in the brain. PBMCs were reported to share similarities in gene expression with the cerebellum across 4000 unique genes (Andrews and Neises 2012) and similar DNAm markers related to reward, stress responses, and psychopathologies, as identified in postmortem adult brain tissue (Colin and Van Lint 2009). Therefore, PBMC DNAm patterns associated with cumulative FS may also be reflected in brain DNAm, which could contribute to the later development of psychopathology. Further studies are required to determine the relation of DNAm in the developing brain to that in blood as a more readily accessible peripheral surrogate tissue.

Negative Mother–Child Relationship Contribute to the Association of Children's Blood DNAm With Cumulative FS

Given that daily experience of children may be more directly related to mother–child relationship quality, which can be negatively impacted by adverse family environments, the present study explored the roles of the mother–child relationship quality on biological embedding of cumulative FS in children. Our results showed that, at one identified CpG residing in SLC6A3, negative mother–child relationship explained a substantial portion of the associations of DNAm with cumulative FS. Therefore, it is tempting to speculate that negative mother–child relationship may serve as at least a partial behavioral pathway for the linkage of cumulative FS with DNAm patterns implicated in adult psychopathology and poorer emotional and behavioral functioning in children (Cimino et al. 2021). This aligns with the prevailing hypothesis regarding the connections between early adversity, negative parenting, and later psychopathology (Cicchetti and Toth 2016; Jackson and Choi 2018; Miller et al. 2011). Nonetheless, the lack of significant correlations between negative mother–child relationship and cumulative FS in our sample was somewhat encouraging, as it suggested that mother–child relationship quality was not always determined by exposure to adversity. Indeed, the identification of negative mother–child relationship as a possible contributing factor illuminates potential avenues for future interventions, such as family therapy to support positive mother–child relationship and thus mitigate any potential negative link between cumulative FS and children's biological patterns.

Limitations

This study had several limitations that should be taken into account when interpreting our findings. First, the current study was conducted with families who lived in Vancouver, Canada—a primarily White, affluent, and well‐educated metropolitan area. We acknowledge that our sample could be considered a rather advantaged group. The experience of cumulative FS and how it may be associated with children's development can be highly context‐specific, so the present findings may not be generalizable to children from less advantaged families and more diverse racial and socioeconomic backgrounds (Warner et al. 2023). Furthermore, we noted that some subscales (e.g., CTSPC psychological aggression) had suboptimal internal reliabilities in our current sample. Nonetheless, this psychometric issue could be partially mitigated by our analytical approach of creating PCs from multiple measures reflecting similar underlying constructs and using that for downstream analyses. Second, we adopted a moderately lenient threshold for multiple‐test correction across all analyses (FDR < 0.20), thus accepting some false positives. This threshold was implemented along with a stringent, robust technical threshold of |Δβ|> 0.03 (Merrill et al. 2021), adding confidence to our findings, as the difference observed was greater than technical noise likely resulting from the array methodologies used in the analyses. Nonetheless, given the small sample size and the thresholds used, further studies are required to corroborate these findings. Third, all data were obtained at the same time point, and therefore the associations between cumulative FS and DNAm were cross‐sectional and correlational in nature. Although we hypothesized that the DNAm patterns may have been induced by cumulative FS, it is entirely possible that these DNAm patterns in children could exist prior to exposure to stress or merely reflect the consequence of an existing phenotype, also known as reverse causation (Lappalainen and Greally, 2017). Furthermore, these patterns could emerge through passive gene‐environment correlation, in which parental genome could be linked to both FS and the child genome and in turn have a genetic influence on child DNAm (McAdams et al. 2023). Alternatively, it is possible that they co‐occurred due to other factors, such as unmeasured environmental exposure that associated with both FS and child DNAm. Therefore, these associations should not be interpreted as causal relationships and should be investigated longitudinally in future studies to scrutinize their temporal order and relations over time. Forth, despite collecting samples from the same individuals, it should be noted that PBMCs were collected at a slightly later time point than BECs. Prior literature has suggested that there are stronger tissue‐ than age‐related differences within individuals (Islam et al. 2019; Jaffe and Irizarry 2014), especially in a relatively short timeframe. Nonetheless, as age and development are important drivers of DNAm differences, we controlled for children's age at sample collection in both models. Further studies with matched samples collected at the same time point are needed to address the potential confound of age at collection time in a more rigorous fashion. Finally, our cumulative FS construct was only derived from maternal reports, and therefore did not consider the paternal or child's perspective regarding the social environment. Future studies adopting a more family system perspective with collection of data from multiple family members to gain a comprehensive understanding of the family environment will be beneficial to understand the relations of DNAm with biological embedding of cumulative FS.

Conclusions

Despite the limitations outlined above, our study contributed to the existing literature regarding the associations of DNAm with cumulative FS by comparing the patterns in two common tissues in school‐age children. This cross‐tissue comparison suggested that PBMCs may be a relevant tissue type for identifying DNAm markers of early psychosocial stress. Extending previous studies on cumulative FS and DNAm, our robust analyses with consideration of important biological factors driving DNAm, including cell‐type heterogeneity, genetic ancestry, and effects of genetic variants, supported the link of such exposure with DNAm, especially in stress‐ and immune‐related genes. Further, we showed that direct parenting factors, such as mother–child relationship quality, represented possible pathways underlying this relation as potential avenues for future interventions.
