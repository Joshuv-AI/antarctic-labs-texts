Comparison of the efficacy of different types and intensities of extracorporeal shock wave therapy for lateral epicondylitis: a systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Ran Jin, Haijing Wang, Wenwen Lv, Hengqiang Cheng, Yanru Li, Qingqiang Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo evaluate the efficacy of different types and intensities of extracorporeal shock wave therapy (ESWT) for lateral epicondylitis using a network meta-analysis.MethodsFollowing a systematic retrieval of major databases (e.g. PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Scopus, and Cochrane Library) from their respective inception dates up to September 2025, relevant RCTs and cohort studies on ESWT for lateral epicondylitis were identified, with data analyzed in R software (version 4.4.3).ResultsThe analysis included 11 RCTs and 2 cohort studies involving 938 patients with lateral epicondylitis. Network meta-analysis demonstrated that medium-intensity radial ESWT (rESWT-M) ranked highest in improving visual analog scale (VAS) scores at 1 month post-treatment, with a surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) of 100%. Significant benefits were also observed for rESWT-L [SMD = − 1.91, 95% CrI (− 2.34, − 1.47)], fESWT-M [SMD = − 1.80, 95% CrI (− 2.45, − 1.15)], fESWT-H [SMD = − 1.74, 95% CrI (− 2.38, − 1.10)], and sham ESWT [SMD = − 1.74, 95% CrI (− 2.30, − 1.19)], when compared to waiting for therapy. fESWT-L excelled in long-term pain relief (SUCRA = 99.06%) and RM score improvement (SUCRA = 99.81%); it also significantly reduced VAS scores at 6 months post-treatment versus sham ESWT [SMD = − 2.10, 95% CrI (− 2.72, − 1.50)]. rESWT-L was most effective on DASH (SUCRA = 97.15%) and PRTEE (SUCRA = 97.66%) scores. Furthermore, sham ESWT significantly outperformed waiting for therapy on post-treatment PRTEE scores [SMD = − 5.97, 95% CrI − 6.93, − 5.02].ConclusionThe findings indicate distinct advantages for different ESWT types and intensities in managing lateral epicondylitis. rESWT-M offers superior short-term analgesic effects, whereas fESWT-L is more beneficial for long-term pain relief. Overall, low-energy ESWT appears to yield the most favorable therapeutic profile. Validation through additional high-quality RCTs is recommended, as the current evidence is limited by the scarcity and suboptimal quality of existing research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-026-06984-0.

Introduction

Lateral epicondylitis (tennis elbow) is a chronic overuse injury involving inflammation near the origin of the common extensor tendon at the lateral epicondyle of the humerus. Typically resulting from repetitive wrist movements, the condition primarily presents as lateral elbow pain which may impair wrist function [1]. The prevalence of lateral epicondylitis ranges from 1 to 3% among individuals aged 35–54 years [2].

Although typically self-limiting, nonoperative management remains the first-line approach, with surgery considered only after conservative treatments fail. Nonoperative treatments include local immobilization, pharmacotherapy, local injections, and physical therapy [3]. Extracorporeal shock wave therapy (ESWT), a noninvasive physical treatment effective for tendinopathy-related pain, has been increasingly used in recent years for this condition [4]. Based on the mode of generation, ESWT can be categorized into focused ESWT (f-ESWT) and radial ESWT (r-ESWT), each available at different energy levels. Shock waves exert direct mechanical effects and induce cavitation during propagation, leading to a cascade of biological responses, including modulation of inflammatory processes, promotion of neovascularization, and facilitation of tissue repair, thereby achieving anti-inflammatory, analgesic, and functional improvement effects [5].

Although ESWT has been shown to improve pain intensity and functional outcomes in lateral epicondylitis, clinical applications are highly heterogeneous—varying by device and energy delivery—with no consensus on optimal treatment parameters [6–9]. Moreover, previous meta-analyses have focused on comparing ESWT with alternative therapies (such as injections and ultrasound therapy) rather than differentiating among ESWT regimens themselves [10–12]. This limitation, coupled with the inherent constraints of pairwise meta-analysis to simultaneously compare multiple interventions, underscores the necessity for a systematic review and network meta-analysis that can synthesize all available evidence and rank interventions by efficacy. This study was therefore designed to systematically assess and compare ESWT modalities by type and intensity, with the goal of providing robust evidence for clinical guidelines.

Materials and methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses extension for network meta-analyses (PRISMA-NMA) guidelines [13], with its protocol registered in the PROSPERO database (CRD420251155935).

Literature search and study selection

A systematic search of PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library covered all records from inception to September 2025. To identify potentially eligible studies, the search strategy combined keywords, MeSH terms, and free-text variations for “lateral epicondylitis” and “shockwave,” with no restrictions on blinding, language, or publication date. Supplementary Table S1 details the full strategy.

Two investigators conducted the literature screening independently. Following initial import and deduplication in EndNote 21, titles and abstracts were assessed for relevance. Studies deemed potentially eligible proceeded to a full-text review for final eligibility assessment. Any selection disagreement was settled by a third investigator.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data extraction was performed independently by two investigators using a standardized form developed in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third investigator if needed. The form captured the following parameters: author, publication year, participant demographics, study design, intervention type and intensity, and outcomes. Outcomes extracted included VAS, DASH, PRTEE, and RM scores, reported as mean ± standard deviation.

RCT bias was assessed independently via the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool version 2 (RoB 2) [15], followed by cross-checking. Any discrepancy was resolved through consensus involving a third investigator. Five domains were evaluated: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result. Each signaling question was answered as Yes (Y), Probably Yes (PY), Probably No (PN), No (N), or No Information (NI). Studies were considered at high risk of bias if at least one domain was rated as high or probably high risk.

Cohort study quality was rated with the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) [16], applicable for both cohort and case–control studies. The NOS assigns scores across three domains: selection of the study groups, comparability of the groups, and outcome assessment. A total score out of 9 is generated, with studies categorized as high (7–9), medium (4–6), or low (< 4) quality.

Statistical analysis

All computations and visualizations were implemented via R software (version 4.4.3) with the packages gemtc 1.1-0 and igraph 2.2.1. Continuous outcomes, including post-treatment VAS, DASH, PRTEE, and RM scores, were expressed as standardized mean differences (SMD) with 95% credible intervals (CrI). To model the data, both consistency and inconsistency models were constructed within a Bayesian framework using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods, running four chains with 25,000 burn-in iterations for convergence. Model convergence was subsequently confirmed by examining the Potential Scale Reduction Factor (PSRF). For intervention networks forming closed loops, the node-splitting method was employed to evaluate local inconsistencies between direct and indirect evidence. In cases where closed loops were absent, the Deviance Information Criterion (DIC) was used to compare the fit of consistency versus inconsistency models and to detect significant global inconsistency. Treatment rankings were estimated using the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA, range 0–100%). Heterogeneity was measured by I2 statistic (low: < 25%, moderate: 50%, high: > 75%). Fixed-effects models were used for low heterogeneity. To assess publication bias, funnel plots were examined and Egger’s test was performed in case of sufficient studies. Meta-regression explored the impact of study design on heterogeneity and outcomes.

Results

Literature screening results

The initial search yielded 1849 records. After duplicate removal, 917 articles underwent preliminary screening, through which reviews, case reports, animal studies, and other irrelevant publications were excluded, leaving 115 records. Full texts were unavailable for 43 records. Subsequent full-text assessment led to the exclusion of studies that were neither RCTs nor cohort designs or that lacked relevant post-treatment outcome data (VAS, DASH, PRTEE, or RM scores). Quality assessment results

Application of the Cochrane RoB 2.0 tool to the 11 included RCTs revealed the following: All studies mentioned randomization, with seven specifying sequence generation methods (random number tables, computer-generated randomization, envelope method), and four only stating “random” without further details. Allocation concealment was reported in five studies; six studies did not report allocation details. Risk of bias in the randomization process was rated low in nine studies and unclear in two. Participant blinding was implemented in nine studies, leading to a low risk of bias due to deviations from intended interventions in ten studies, with one rated as unclear. The risk from missing outcome data was low in ten studies and unclear in one. For outcome measurement, nine studies were at low risk and two were unclear regarding potential bias from assessor knowledge. Selective reporting bias was considered low in eight studies, while three were unclear due to insufficiently detailed analysis plans. Notably, no study was assessed as having a high risk of bias in any domain. A fixed-effects model was used for the NMA (I2_cons = 31.82%). Further assessment indicated satisfactory model convergence (multivariate PSRF = 1, Supplementary Fig. S1A), and global consistency was adequate (consistency model DIC = 13.34 vs. inconsistency model DIC = 13.32). The SUCRA ranking for improving immediate post-treatment VAS was as follows: fESWT-M (SUCRA: 94.74%), rESWT-M (SUCRA: 52.9%), rESWT-L (SUCRA: 52.36%), and waiting for therapy (SUCRA: 0%) (Supplementary Fig. S3A). Pairwise comparisons in the league table showed that, relative to waiting for therapy, fESWT-M [SMD = − 1.52, 95% CrI (− 2.02, − 1.02)] resulted in a significant reduction in immediate post-treatment VAS scores; Similarly significant reductions were observed for rESWT-M [SMD = − 1.29, 95% CrI (− 1.89, − 0.69)] and rESWT-L [SMD = − 1.29, 95% CrI (− 1.69, − 0.89)], with differences reaching statistical significance. Furthermore, fESWT-M showed a potential trend towards superior efficacy in reducing immediate post-treatment VAS scores compared to rESWT-M [SMD = − 0.23, 95% CrI (− 0.55, 0.10)] or rESWT-L [SMD = − 0.23, 95% CrI (− 0.53, 0.08)]. Study design, as per meta-regression, did not significantly affect this outcome. Considering the SUCRA ranking, fESWT-M was identified as the optimal shockwave parameter for improving immediate post-treatment VAS. Effects of different interventions on immediate VAS and corresponding mean differences (95% CrI) are detailed in Supplementary Table S3A and Supplementary Fig. S4A. Assessment via a comparison-adjusted funnel plot revealed approximately symmetrical point distribution, indicating the possibility of no potential publication bias (Supplementary Fig. S5A).

One-month post-treatment VAS

Six interventions were compared for VAS at 1 month post-treatment, and the network plot is presented in Fig. 3B.

A fixed-effects model was used (I2_cons = 11.03%). Model convergence was satisfactory (multivariate PSRF = 1, Supplementary Fig. S1B), and global consistency was adequate (consistency model DIC = 14.50 vs. inconsistency model DIC = 14.48). SUCRA rankings were: rESWT-M (100%), rESWT-L (65%), fESWT-M (51.16%), fESWT-H (42.94%), Sham ESWT (40.9%), and Waiting for therapy (0%) (Supplementary Fig. S3B). Pairwise comparisons showed that, compared with Waiting for therapy, fESWT-M [SMD = − 1.80, 95% CrI (− 2.45, − 1.15)], rESWT-M [SMD = − 3.65, 95% CrI (− 4.41, − 2.90)], rESWT-L [SMD = − 1.91, 95% CrI (− 2.34, − 1.47)], fESWT-H [SMD = − 1.74, 95% CrI (− 2.38, − 1.10)], and Sham ESWT [SMD = − 1.74, 95% CrI (− 2.30, − 1.19)] significantly reduced VAS at 1 month, with statistically significant differences. Compared with rESWT-M, rESWT-L [SMD = 1.75, 95% CrI (1.13, 2.37)], fESWT-M [SMD = 1.86, 95% CrI (1.07, 2.63)], fESWT-H [SMD = 1.91, 95% CrI (1.14, 2.68)], Sham ESWT [SMD = 1.91, 95% CrI (1.21, 2.62)], and waiting for therapy [SMD = 3.65, 95% CrI (2.90, 4.41)] were less effective in reducing 1-month VAS, all statistically significant. Meta-regression showed no significant effect of study design on 1-month VAS. Based on SUCRA rankings, rESWT-M was identified as the optimal shockwave parameter for reducing VAS at 1 month. Effects of different interventions on 1-month VAS and corresponding mean differences (95% CrI) are provided in Supplementary Table S3B and Supplementary Fig. S4B. Publication bias was evaluated via a comparison-adjusted funnel plot, which demonstrated approximate symmetry, suggesting the possible absence of potential publication bias. Egger’s test further confirmed the absence of significant asymmetry (t = − 1.07, df = 8, P = 0.3155), indicating no apparent publication bias (Supplementary Fig. S5B).

Three-month post-treatment VAS

Six interventions were compared for VAS at 3 months post-treatment, with the network plot shown in Fig. 3C.

A random-effects model was applied (I2_cons = 84.57%). Model convergence was satisfactory (multivariate PSRF = 1, Supplementary Fig. S1C). Through node-splitting analysis, it was found that direct and indirect comparisons yielded statistically significant differences (Supplementary Fig. S2A). SUCRA rankings were: fESWT-L (81.88%), rESWT-M (69.2%), fESWT-M (62.08%), fESWT-H (33.51%), Sham ESWT (31.29%), and rESWT-L (22.04%) (Supplementary Fig. S3C). Pairwise comparisons suggested that fESWT-L may provide superior reduction in 3-month VAS compared with fESWT-M [SMD = − 0.36, 95% CrI (− 2.01, 1.27)], rESWT-M [SMD = − 0.26, 95% CrI (− 2.00, 1.48)], rESWT-L [SMD = − 1.19, 95% CrI (− 2.84, 0.41)], fESWT-H [SMD = − 1.05, 95% CrI (− 3.33, 1.19)], and Sham ESWT [SMD = − 1.01, 95% CrI (− 2.30, 0.22)], though none reached statistical significance. Meta-regression indicated that study design was not a significant source of influence on this outcome. Thus, although SUCRA ranking suggested fESWT-L as the optimal shockwave parameter for 3-month VAS, its clinical relevance is limited. Effects of different interventions on 3-month VAS and corresponding mean differences (95% CrI) are detailed in Supplementary Table S3C and Supplementary Fig. S4C. A comparison-adjusted funnel plot showed approximate symmetry, pointing to the possible non-existence of potential publication bias for 3-month VAS (Supplementary Fig. S5C).

Six-month post-treatment VAS

Four interventions were compared for VAS at 6 months post-treatment, with the network plot shown in Fig. 3D.

A random-effects model was utilized for NMA (I2_cons = 74.65%). Model convergence was satisfactory (multivariate PSRF = 1, Supplementary Fig. S1D), and global consistency was adequate (consistency model DIC = 15.76 vs. inconsistency model DIC = 15.84). SUCRA rankings were: fESWT-L (99.06%), fESWT-M (57.66%), Sham ESWT (36.97%), and fESWT-H (6.31%) (Supplementary Fig. S3D). Pairwise comparisons indicated that, compared with Sham ESWT, fESWT-L [SMD = − 2.10, 95% CrI (− 2.72, − 1.50)] significantly reduced 6-month VAS scores. Compared with fESWT-L, fESWT-M [SMD = 1.55, 95% CrI (0, 3.09)] and fESWT-H [SMD = 2.95, 95% CrI (1.31, 4.58)] were less effective in reducing VAS, with statistically significant differences. Comparisons of fESWT-M [SMD = − 0.55, 95% CrI (− 2.21, 1.08)] and fESWT-H [SMD = 0.85, 95% CrI (− 0.67, 2.37)] versus Sham ESWT were not statistically significant. Meta-regression showed no significant influence of study design on 6-month VAS. Based on SUCRA rankings, fESWT-L was identified as the optimal shockwave parameter for reducing 6-month VAS. Effects of different interventions on 6-month VAS and corresponding mean differences (95% CrI) are provided in Supplementary Table S3D and Supplementary Fig. S4D. Comparison-adjusted funnel plot indicated approximate symmetry, implying the likely absence of potential publication bias (Supplementary Fig. S5D).

Post-treatment DASH

Three interventions were compared for post-treatment DASH, with the network plot described in Fig. 3E.

A fixed-effects model was utilized (I2_cons = 0%). Model convergence was satisfactory (multivariate PSRF = 1, Supplementary Fig. S1E), and global consistency was adequate (consistency model DIC = 3.99 vs. inconsistency model DIC = 3.99). SUCRA rankings were: rESWT-L (97.15%), fESWT-M (48.03%), and exercise (4.82%) (Supplementary Fig. S3E). Pairwise comparisons showed that, compared with exercise, rESWT-L [SMD = − 0.65, 95% CrI (− 1.24, − 0.06)] significantly reduced post-treatment DASH scores. fESWT-M [SMD = − 0.18, 95% CrI (− 0.43, 0.07)] versus exercise showed no significant difference. Compared with fESWT-M, rESWT-L [SMD = − 0.47, 95% CrI (− 1.00, 0.06)] showed a trend toward DASH reduction but without statistical significance. Based on SUCRA rankings, rESWT-L was identified as the optimal shockwave parameter for reducing post-treatment DASH. Effects of different interventions on DASH and corresponding mean differences (95% CrI) are presented in Supplementary Table S3E and Supplementary Fig. S4E. Approximate symmetry was observed in the comparison-adjusted funnel plot, supporting the conclusion of probably no potential publication bias (Supplementary Fig. S5E).

Post-treatment PRTEE

Three interventions were compared for post-treatment PRTEE, with the network plot shown in Fig. 3F.

A fixed-effects model was applied (I2_cons = 0%). Model convergence was satisfactory (multivariate PSRF = 1, Supplementary Fig. S1F), and global consistency was adequate (consistency model DIC = 5.30 vs. inconsistency model DIC = 5.32). SUCRA rankings were: rESWT-L (97.66%), Sham ESWT (52.34%), and waiting for therapy (0%) (Supplementary Fig. S3F). Pairwise comparisons indicated that, compared with waiting for therapy, rESWT-L [SMD = − 6.27, 95% CrI (−7.17, − 5.38)] and Sham ESWT [SMD = − 5.97, 95% CrI (− 6.93, − 5.02)] both notably reduced post-treatment PRTEE scores, with statistically significant differences. Additionally, rESWT-L [SMD = − 0.30, 95% CrI (− 0.64, 0.05)] suggested a potential superiority over Sham ESWT in reducing PRTEE scores, though not statistically significant. Meta-regression showed no significant influence of study design on PRTEE. Based on SUCRA rankings, rESWT-L was identified as the optimal shockwave parameter for reducing post-treatment PRTEE. Effects of different interventions on PRTEE and corresponding mean differences (95% CrI) are presented in Supplementary Table S3F and Supplementary Fig. S4F. Comparison-adjusted funnel plot indicated approximate symmetry, meaning publication bias may not exist (Supplementary Fig. S5F).

Post-treatment RM scores

Three interventions were compared for post-treatment Roles and Maudsley (RM) scores, with the network plot shown in Fig. 3G.

A fixed-effects model was applied for NMA (I2_cons = 0%). Model convergence was satisfactory (multivariate PSRF = 1, Supplementary Fig. S1G), and global consistency was adequate (consistency model DIC = 4.36 vs. inconsistency model DIC = 4.35). Based on SUCRA rankings, fESWT-L (99.81%) ranked highest, followed by Sham ESWT (36.57%) and rESWT-L (13.62%) (Supplementary Fig. S3G). Pairwise comparisons indicated that, compared with Sham ESWT, fESWT-L [SMD = − 0.77, 95% CrI (− 1.26, − 0.28)] significantly reduced post-treatment RM scores. Compared with rESWT-L, fESWT-L [SMD = − 0.90, 95% CrI (− 1.54, − 0.26)] also significantly reduced post-treatment RM scores. Based on SUCRA rankings, fESWT-L was identified as the optimal shockwave parameter for reducing post-treatment RM scores. Effects of different interventions on RM scores and corresponding mean differences (95% CrI) are provided in Supplementary Table S3G and Supplementary Fig. S4G. Comparison-adjusted funnel plot indicated approximate symmetry, confirming there might be no potential publication bias (Supplementary Fig. S5G).

Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the efficacy of ESWT with different types (focused vs. radial) and energy levels (low, medium, high) for lateral epicondylitis via NMA. Outcome measures included pain, function, and overall clinical efficacy, specifically VAS, DASH, PRTEE, and RM scores, which are internationally recognized assessment tools for lateral epicondylitis and accurately reflect the clinical effects of ESWT interventions [30].

Regarding pain relief, NMA demonstrated that immediately post-treatment, fESWT-M, rESWT-M, and rESWT-L were all superior to waiting for therapy, with no statistically significant differences among them. This finding aligns with the results reported by Magdalena Stania et al., showing comparable early efficacy between focused and radial ESWT [31]. At 1 month post-treatment, rESWT-M was identified as potentially the most effective intervention for pain relief, suggesting that rESWT-M may rapidly induce analgesia. This rapid analgesic effect is likely attributable to the “hyperstimulation analgesia” mechanism [32], in which shockwaves overstimulate peripheral nerve fibers, blocking nociceptive signal transmission to the central nervous system. Additionally, compared with fESWT, rESWT has a wider area of action [20], potentially covering superficial nociceptors more effectively and resulting in faster pain reduction. However, the optimal parameters changed over time. At 3 months post-treatment, fESWT-L exhibited a promising, yet not statistically significant, superior efficacy over other interventions. At 6 months post-treatment, fESWT-L showed significant superiority over fESWT-M, fESWT-H, and Sham ESWT. This may be explained by the selective effect of low-energy shockwaves on unmyelinated nerve fibers, which reduces and disperses pain mediators and harmful metabolites, prevents local nerve reinnervation, and alters pain transmission processes. An additional long-term analgesic effect is achieved through the reduction of CGRP- and SP-positive neurons in the dorsal root ganglia [33]. Furthermore, ESWT promotes new collagen synthesis and neovascularization within the tendon [31], contributing to sustained therapeutic effects lasting up to 24 weeks [34]. f-ESWT allows energy to be more concentrated on deep target tissues [21]. Animal studies have shown that repeated ESWT sessions (two or three treatments) increase pain thresholds and prolong analgesic duration compared with a single treatment [35, 36]. Overall, for pain relief, the meta-analysis supports the short-term superiority of rESWT-M, whereas fESWT-L appears more advantageous for long-term analgesic effects.

Beyond pain relief, functional recovery is a critical indicator for evaluating the therapeutic efficacy of lateral epicondylitis. In the assessment of comprehensive efficacy, including both pain relief and functional improvement (DASH, PRTEE, RM scores), rESWT-L and fESWT-L demonstrated significant advantages. rESWT-L exhibited certain superiority in improving DASH and PRTEE scores, suggesting its unique effect in enhancing upper limb function. For RM scores, fESWT-L outperformed rESWT-L and Sham ESWT. Combined with the long-term analgesic effect of fESWT-L, these results further confirm the therapeutic superiority of fESWT-L in the management of lateral epicondylitis. It is noteworthy that, regarding overall efficacy, low-energy shockwaves—whether focused or radial—generally appeared superior to medium- or high-energy protocols. During the literature screening and based on the included studies, it was observed that high-energy ESWT studies were fewer in number compared with low- or medium-energy studies. This may be due to the ability of low-energy ESWT to induce multiple beneficial tissue responses and related metabolic effects [4], its better patient tolerability, higher compliance, and reduced post-treatment pain, facilitating early rehabilitation exercises and daily activity, thereby promoting comprehensive functional recovery. Conversely, as energy levels increase, the pain induced by shockwaves becomes more pronounced, often necessitating local anesthesia during treatment [37]. Notarnicola et al. reported that low- and medium-energy shockwaves can induce nitric oxide (NO) release, which exerts analgesic, pro-angiogenic, and anti-inflammatory effects, reduces fibrosis, and promotes musculoskeletal tissue regeneration and repair [38–40]. In contrast, high-energy shockwaves may cause fibrinoid necrosis in normal tendons, peritendinous fibrosis, and inflammatory cell infiltration, potentially damaging tendon tissue [41]. Clinically, the use of energy flux density > 0.28 mJ/mm2 for tendinopathy treatment is generally not recommended [42].

However, previous work by Magdalena Stania et al. found no significant differences in pain or function between rESWT and fESWT for tendinopathies [37]. Larisa Ryskalin et al. also noted that protocol heterogeneity, including factors like device type, session details, and energy levels, hinders protocol standardization [43]. This study is the first to stratify ESWT by intensity (energy level) and type and to apply NMA for more comprehensive and detailed comparisons, thereby expanding the research scope. Specifically, the NMA integrates quantitative data from RCTs and cohort studies in lateral epicondylitis, systematically evaluating the effects of different ESWT types and intensities.

Limitations include: (1) An uneven distribution of studies across ESWT types, and limited outcome measures for some modalities, possibly affecting the results; (2) slight variations in definitions of “low,” “medium,” and “high” energy levels, especially for rESWT (pressure values not directly convertible to energy units), posing a methodological challenge despite standardization; (3) differences in intervention intensity, duration, and cycle among studies, potentially introducing potential bias; (4) inclusion of cohort studies may affect statistical results, although subgroup analyses did not detect significant influence of study design on outcomes.

In summary, ESWT proves to be a highly efficacious treatment for pain and functional improvement in lateral epicondylitis. Different types and intensities of ESWT have distinct clinical value. For patients seeking short-term pain relief, rESWT-M may be the preferred choice, whereas fESWT-L appears optimal for long-term pain control and functional improvement. Regarding upper limb function, rESWT-L demonstrated significant benefits. The statistical models in this study were well-constructed, and outcome measures remained stable and reliable, providing robust evidence for clinical decision-making. Given the limitations, further high-quality, large-sample clinical trials are warranted to verify these findings and determine the optimal ESWT type, intensity, and treatment cycle. It is recommend that future rESWT studies report energy levels in terms of energy flux density, ensuring more precise and objective guidance for clinical practice.
