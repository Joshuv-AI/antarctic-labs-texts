Association between gut microbiota diet index and cardiovascular disease in US adults: a cross-sectional study of NHANES 2007–2018
Authors: Yun Lu, Siyuan Cheng, Yunchen Han, Shaojie Han, Hao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DI-GM), a recently developed metric designed to evaluate diet quality in relation to gut microbiota, has an uncharted connection with cardiovascular diseases (CVD).MethodsWe collected cross-sectional data from 8,533 participants in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) conducted between 2007 and 2018. Cardiovascular disease (CVD) was defined as a combination of self-reported physician diagnoses and standardized medical status questionnaires. Dietary recall data were utilized to calculate the DI-GM), which includes components that are both beneficial and unfavorable to gut microbiota. All analyses incorporated NHANES sample weights, stratification, and primary sampling units to account for the complex multistage probability sampling design. Logistic regression and Restricted cubic splines (RCS) were employed to explore the associations among DI-GM, beneficial gut microbiota, and CVD.ResultsThe control group had significantly higher DI-GM and beneficial gut microbiota score than the disease group (p < 0.05). Inverse associations were observed between DI-GM, beneficial gut microbiota score and the prevalence of cardiovascular disease (OR = 0.93, 95% CI = 0.89–0.97; OR = 0.88, 95% CI = 0.83–0.92). Moreover, RCS showed a linear relationship between DI-GM and cardiovascular disease.Subgroup analysis revealed stronger inverse associations between DI-GM and CVD among older individuals or those with hypertension or diabetes.ConclusionsA higher DI-GM was associated with a lower prevalence of CVD. Future studies are supposed to validate these findings by employing longitudinal studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05993-5.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is a leading cause of mortality and loss of healthy life years, ranking as the foremost factor in global disease burden and significantly contributing to health decline and elevated healthcare expenditures [1]. Although secondary prevention and management strategies for CVD have expanded and improved over recent decades, it continues to result in approximately 17.7 million deaths each year (31% of global deaths), meaning that in the United States, one out of every three deaths is attributed to CVD [2].

The gut microbiota is essential for human health, influencing various aspects such as immune function, metabolic regulation, and neurobehavioral traits. Numerous studies have previously highlighted the association between gut microbiota and cardiovascular disease, demonstrating that gut microbial communities can regulate cardiovascular system function and metabolism through metabolites such as bile acids and amino acids [3]. Given that diet is a key determinant of human gut microbial composition, changes in dietary habits can alter the gut microbiota, rendering dietary interventions an area of increasing interest.

Recently, Kase et al. reviewed 106 articles regarding the relationship between adult diet and gut microbiota, conducting a comprehensive literature review that identified 14 dietary components influencing gut microbiota either positively or negatively [4]. They subsequently developed a novel gut microbiota diet index to assess diet quality associated with maintaining a healthy gut microbiota [4]. Furthermore, the Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DI-GM) demonstrates a positive correlation with urinary enterolignans and enterolactone (markers of gut microbiota diversity), suggesting an association between this index and gut microbiota diversity.

The Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DI-GM) exemplifies interdisciplinary collaboration and knowledge synthesis across the fields of nutrition, microbiology, medicine, and epidemiology [4]. It can be inferred that the Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DI-GM) effectively identifies dietary patterns that are either beneficial or detrimental to gut microbiota and holds promise as a standardized tool for evaluating balanced diets for gut health. Nonetheless, studies investigating the relationship between the Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DI-GM) and cardiovascular disease remain insufficient.

Therefore, this study analyzed the data from NHANES 2007–2018 and hypothesized that consuming foods beneficial to the gut microbiota might reduce the risk of CVD, thereby providing new epidemiological insights into reducing the incidence of CVD.While prior studies have examined DI-GM in relation to stroke and mental health outcomes using NHANES data, no published study has investigated the association between DI-GM and broad CVD outcomes (encompassing congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease, angina, heart attack, and stroke as a composite endpoint) in a nationally representative U.S. adult population spanning 2007–2018. The present study fills this gap and extends the existing evidence base.

Method

Data source and participants

NHANES, initiated by the U.S. National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), is a nationwide survey conducted every two years. It primarily monitors the health and nutrition status of the American population to gain a comprehensive understanding of modern disease trends and to inform public health policy development. All of the NHANES data is accessible to the public and can be downloaded freely through: https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.htm. For this analysis, we used cross-sectional data from 59,842 participants across six NHANES cycles (2007–2008, 2009–2010, 2011–2012, 2013–2014, 2015–2016, and 2017–2018). The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) participants aged < 20 years (n = 25,072); (2) participants with incomplete CVD data (n = 168); (3) participants without relevant dietary intake information (n = 3,963) and those lacking covariates included in the study (n = 22,106). After manual screening, we ultimately selected 8,533 participants for subsequent analysis. The inclusion and exclusion criteria in this study are summarized in the flowchart (Fig. 1).

Assessment of dietary index for gut microbiota

According to the scoring criteria outlined in the study by Kase et al., fourteen food items and nutrients were identified as constituents of the Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DI-GM). These constituents include avocado, broccoli, chickpeas, coffee, cranberries, fermented dairy products, fiber, green tea, soybeans, and whole grains, which are regarded as beneficial components. Conversely, red meat, processed meat, refined grains, and diets high in fat (constituting 40% or more of total energy intake) were classified as unfavorable components. Dietary recall data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2005–2018 were employed to calculate the Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DI-GM), with the components and scoring criteria detailed in Supplementary Table 1. Regarding dietary fiber, total fiber intake (in grams per day) was derived from the NHANES dietary data using the USDA Food and Nutrient Database for Dietary Studies (FNDDS). Fiber sources included vegetables, fruits, whole grains, legumes, nuts, and seeds reported across all food items in the 24-hour dietary recall. A score of 1 was assigned to items deemed beneficial to gut microbiota when consumption met or exceeded the sex-specific median; otherwise, a score of 0 was allocated. Conversely, for items classified as unfavorable to gut microbiota, a score of 0 was assigned if consumption met or exceeded the sex-specific median or constituted 40% or more of total energy intake (for high-fat diets); otherwise, a score of 1 was assigned. The scores were subsequently aggregated to compute the overall Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DI-GM), which ranges from 0 to 13. This included scores for items beneficial to gut microbiota, which ranged from 0 to 9, and those classified as unfavorable to gut microbiota, which ranged from 0 to 4. The Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DI-GM) was further stratified into four categories: 0–3, 4, 5, and ≥ 6 [4].

Outcome definitions

Cardiovascular disease status was assessed through individual interviews, utilizing self-reported physician diagnoses in conjunction with standardized medical history questionnaires. Participants were specifically asked: ‘Has a doctor or other healthcare professional ever informed you that you have been diagnosed with congestive heart failure (CHF), coronary heart disease (CHD), angina pectoris, heart attack, or stroke?’ Those who responded affirmatively to any of these conditions were classified as patients with cardiovascular disease (CVD). Consequently, the outcome variable was dichotomized for subsequent analysis.

Covariates

Several potential confounding variables were considered, informed by both established research findings and clinical expertise, including age [5]; sex(men/women) [5]; race (Mexican American, Other Hispanic, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, Other Races) [5]; marital status (Married/Cohabiting, singlea) [5]; education level (< High school, > High school, High school) [5]; energy intake (kcal/day) [6]; serum low density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-cholesterol) (mg/dL) [7]; Poverty Income Ratio (≤ 1.30, 1.31–3.50, and ≥ 3.50) [5]; physical activity (vigorous/moderate recreational activities for at least 10 min continuously in a typical week) [8]; Smoke [9]; Drinking alcohol [9]; hypertension [10]; and diabetes mellitus [11]. Smoking status was categorized as never, former, and now using two questions: “Have smoked at least 100 cigarettes in your life” and “Do you smoke now?” [9]. Self-reported drinking status was categorized as follows: never (consumed < 12 drinks in a lifetime), former (consumed ≥ 12 drinks in one year but not in the last year, or did not drink in the last year but consumed ≥ 12 drinks in a lifetime), mild (≤ 1 drink per day for females and ≤ 2 drinks per day for males), moderate (≤ 2 drinks per day for females and ≤ 3 drinks per day for males), or heavy (≥ 3 drinks per day for females and ≥ 4 drinks per day for males) [9]. Fasting serum samples were obtained through venipuncture to measure low-density lipoprotein cholesterol. Participants consumed 75 g of glucose immediately after fasting samples were taken, followed by an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) conducted 2 hours later. The mean diastolic and systolic blood pressure (mmHg) was calculated from three readings taken for each participant. Participants were classified as hypertensive if they met any of the following criteria: (1) systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, (2) self-reported “having been told they have high blood pressure,” “having been told twice they have high blood pressure,” or responded “yes” to taking prescription medication for hypertension [12]. Participants were classified as diabetic if they met any of the following criteria: (1) fasting blood glucose level in laboratory tests ≥ 7.0 mmol/L; (2) blood glucose level > 11.1 mmol/L during OGTT; (3) random blood glucose level > 11.1 mmol/L; (4) HbA1c level > 6.5%; (5) use of diabetes medications; (6) self-reported diagnosis of diabetes [13].

Statistical analysis

Mean ±standard deviation for continuous variables, the P-value was calculated by the weighted linear regression model; n (%) for categorical variables, the P-value was calculated by the weighted chi-square test.

Survey-weighted multiple logistic regression analysis, accounting for NHANES sampling weights, stratification, and primary sampling units, was used to evaluate the relationships between DI-GM and the prevalence of CVD, and three models were constructed. No adjustment for covariates in Model 1. Model 2 was adjusted for the age, sex, race, PIR, marital status, education level. Model 3 was adjusted for the Model 2 variables plus smoking, alcohol use, hypertension, DM, physical activity, serum LDL-cholesterol, and energy intake.Multicollinearity among covariates was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), and all covariates yielded VIF values below 5, indicating no substantial multicollinearity.

We applied restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis with three knots, positioning them at the 10th, 50th, and 90th quantiles of the DI-GM distribution, to model curves and evaluate the potential non-linear relationship between DI-GM and CVD. The choice of three knots was made on the basis of Harrell’s recommendation, which balances flexibility and overfitting risk and is generally appropriate for sample sizes > 1,000 with moderate event rates. The subgroup analysis was performed using multivariate logistic regression stratifying by. An interaction term was also used to explore the heterogeneity of the association between different subgroups using the log-likelihood ratio test model.

R software version 4.2.3. (Core Team, Vienna, Austria) was used to conduct all the statistical analyses. p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Our study included 8,533 participants, and the overall weighted prevalence of CVD was 8.75%. Table 1 displays the baseline characteristics of the study population. Participants with CVD were more likely to be older, male, Non-Hispanic White, equal to less than high school, less energy intake, less activity, smokers, obese, and have hypertension or diabetes. Furthermore, participants with CVD were more likely to have lower DI-GM (p < 0.05).

Participants across DI-GM categories

All participants were categorized into four groups according to their DI-GM score values (0–3, 4, 5, ≥ 6). Among the 8,533 participants, the distribution of the total DI-GM score was as follows: 1,596 participants (18.7%) had a score of 0–3, 1,823 (21.4%) had a score of 4, 1,960 (23.0%) had a score of 5, and 3,154 (37.0%) had a score of ≥ 6. The mean DI-GM score was 5.01 (SD 1.72) in participants without CVD and 4.79 (SD 1.67) in those with CVD, indicating a left-skewed distribution with the majority of participants concentrated in the higher score categories.The clinical characteristics of the four groups are shown in Table 2. Participants in the group where DI-GM scores are 6 or above vs. other three groups were older and more likely to be female; less smoking, activity, Non-Hispanic Black, BMI; higher education level and PIR; and less CVD cases (p < 0.05).

Association between different dietary live microbe groups and CVD

As shown in Table 3, each point increase in DI-GM was associated with an 7% decrease in the prevalence of CVD [odds ratio (OR): 0.93, 95% confidence interval (CI) = 0.89–0.97]. The above association remained significant in the model 2( OR: 0.89, 95% CI: 0.84–0.94, and p < 0.001 ) and model 3(OR: 0.91, 95% CI: 0.87–0.96, and p < 0.001). After grouping DI-GM, participants with DI-GM ≥ 6 were significantly negatively correlated with the prevalence in model 1(OR: 0.74, 95% CI: 0.60–0.92, and p = 0.006), Similarly, the aforementioned association remains statistically similar in model 2(OR: 0.64, 95% CI: 0.51–0.81, and p < 0.001) and model 3(OR: 0.69, 95% CI: 0.60–0.92, and p = 0.006). Additionally, the prevalence of CVD decreased significantly as the beneficial to gut microbiota increased (OR: 0.88, 95% CI: 0.83–0.92, and p < 0.001), while the association between the unfavorable to gut microbiota and CVD was not significant. Furthermore, through adjustment for model 2 and model 3, the associations between CVD and beneficial to gut microbiota still significant. Notably, in Model 3, the association between unfavorable components for gut microbiota and CVD also reached statistical significance (OR = 0.89, 95% CI = 0.82–0.97, p = 0.009), whereas this association was not significant in Models 1 and 2, this attenuation to significance pattern after full covariate adjustment may reflect confounding by lifestyle and metabolic factors that mask the underlying association in unadjusted analyses.

Subgroup analyses

The results of the subgroup analyses can be seen in Fig. 3. Initially, variations in the DI-GM are linked to the incidence of CVD in populations characterized by hypertension, advanced age, non-Hispanic Black and White ethnicities, and higher Poverty Income Ratio (PIR ≥ 3.5). Additionally, a correlation between the DI-GM and CVD incidence is observed across male, female, diabetic, and non-diabetic populations. However, this association does not differ between diabetic and non-diabetic groups (p for interaction = 0.702). Conversely, a statistically significant variation in this association is noted between genders (p for interaction = 0.016). This suggests that the association between the DI-GM and the probability of CVD occurrence varies by gender. Based on the composition of the DI-GM, a further subgroup analysis was performed by categorizing it into components beneficial to gut microbiota and unfavorable to gut microbiota. The trend in the subgroup analysis for components beneficial to gut microbiota aligns with that of the DI-GM. In the case of components unfavorable to gut microbiota, a significant association with CVD occurrence is observed among individuals with diabetes, hypertension, as well as among males, older individuals, and those in the middle Poverty Income Ratio category (1.31–3.50). However, no statistically significant differences were found within any of the subgroups.

Discussion

Among the 8533 NHANES participants included in our study, we first observed that increases in DI-GM, DI-GM ≥ 6 group, and beneficial to gut microbiota were significantly and negatively associated with the prevalence of CVD. Based on this phenomenon, to our knowledge, we demonstrated the first time a linear association between DI-GM and CVD using the RCS, while both beneficial and unfavorable to gut microbiota were still linearly associated with CVD.

In recent years, there has been increasing attention to the effects of diet on gut microbiota and its potential role in cardiovascular conditions. However, the interplay between individual diet, gut microbiota, and cardiovascular disease is multifaceted, involving numerous factors and complexities [14]. Consequently, there is an urgent need for a comprehensive metric to evaluate typical dietary intake based on its impact on gut microbiota. In response to this need, the Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DI-GM) was introduced. In relevant research, the Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DI-GM) is described as comprising components selected based on their positive or negative effects on gut microbiota. These impacts are reflected in variations in gut microbial diversity indices, production levels of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), and the abundance of specific bacterial populations [4].

The focus and development of the Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DI-GM) have contributed to its distinctiveness compared to previous indices that explore how diet influences gut microbiota composition. Furthermore, the DI-GM can evaluate both the quality of diet concerning gut microbiota health and the overall nutritional quality of dietary intake; this capability may stem from the incorporation of individual foods rather than food groups in its development process [15]. The integration of specific foods into the DI-GM may aid in the formulation of more accurate dietary guidelines.

Fermented dairy products are among the unique dietary components in the Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DI-GM). Fermented dairy primarily includes fermented milk products, such as yogurt and kefir, as well as cheese. Increasing evidence supports the role of fermented foods in enhancing gut microbiota [16]. Fermented dairy products, including yogurt, cheese, and kefir, contain lactic acid bacteria and lactic acid, and have been shown to increase the abundance of Lactobacillus spp. in the gut, potentially overcoming gut dysbiosis [17]. Additionally, the consumption of fermented dairy products is believed to benefit cardiovascular health by lowering diabetes risk and increasing high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol levels [18]. A cohort study conducted in a Swedish population found that fermented dairy intake was negatively associated with cardiovascular mortality, while non-fermented dairy intake was linked to increased cardiovascular disease mortality [19]. Another crossover study demonstrated that fermented dairy intake raised HDL levels in 29 women with hypocholesterolemia. The HDL concentration exhibited a significant increase of 0.3 mmol/L (p = 0.002), and the LDL/HDL cholesterol ratio decreased from 3.24 to 2.48 (p = 0.001) [20]. A recent meta-analysis confirmed this association, indicating that fermented dairy consumption has either a beneficial or neutral effect on cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk [21].

The underlying mechanisms for these observations may involve lipids with specific functions. For instance, the intake of milk polar lipids has demonstrated specific anti-inflammatory properties in mouse models [22]. Reductions in macrophage infiltration in white adipose tissue, along with lower plasma levels of endotoxin transport proteins, have been observed [23]. Furthermore, mice fed milk polar lipids may exhibit a stronger colonic barrier, associated with an increase in mucus-secreting goblet cells in the colon. Strengthening the gut barrier helps prevent endotoxins from entering the bloodstream, thereby blocking multiple inflammatory pathways [24]. Conversely, the high calcium content in dairy products may also serve as a contributing mechanism. Clinical research indicates that higher calcium levels in dairy products may partially offset the cholesterol-raising effects of dairy fat on total and LDL cholesterol through mechanisms such as enhancing fecal fat excretion and altering bile acid circulation, without reducing HDL cholesterol [25, 26].

Similarly, chickpeas and soybeans are among the beneficial components of the DI-GM. These components include soy oligosaccharides and raffinose, which can selectively enhance the growth of bifidobacteria [27]. Bifidobacteria, one of the earliest colonizers in the human gut that accompany humans throughout life, offer extensive cardiovascular health benefits [28]. This benefit arises from metabolites produced by bifidobacteria, such as indole-3-lactic acid, which alleviates oxidative stress by promoting the dissociation of Nrf2 from Keap1 and facilitating its translocation to the nucleus, thereby activating the antioxidant enzyme system [29]. Moreover, acetate generated by bifidobacteria serves as a fermentation substrate that can indirectly produce butyrate via cross-feeding interactions. Butyrate, one of the most potent anti-inflammatory agents among known gut metabolites, engages in immune signaling by stimulating anti-inflammatory factors (IL-10, TNF-β) and promoting T cell differentiation [30]. Notably, the abundance of bifidobacteria directly influences the integrity of the gut barrier. Previous research utilizing mouse models indicated that a high-fat diet (HFD) decreases bifidobacteria levels, subsequently compromising gut barrier integrity [31]. However, short-chain fatty acids produced by bifidobacteria contribute to gut barrier protection by facilitating tight junction protein assembly and suppressing inflammasome activation [32]. In conclusion, the consumption of chickpeas and soybeans may offer cardiovascular protection through their effects on gut microbiota metabolism.

Our subgroup analysis revealed a significant correlation between the DI-GM and the incidence of cardiovascular diseases in both older populations and individuals with hypertension. In older adults, this may be attributed to alterations in gut microbiota composition and function that occur with aging, leading to a general decrease in diversity and abundance [33]. Furthermore, decreased production of metabolites, such as short-chain fatty acids, which are crucial for maintaining gut barrier function in older populations, may result in increased intestinal permeability, facilitating the entry of bacteria or their metabolites, such as lipopolysaccharides (LPS), into the bloodstream, which can trigger systemic inflammatory responses and immune activation [34]. In individuals with hypertension, an increase in Gram-negative bacteria, such as Prevotella and Klebsiella, in the gut may exacerbate inflammation and immune responses [35]. Additionally, dysbiosis in hypertensive patients can lead to decreased production of short-chain fatty acids and may disrupt neurogenic mechanisms regulating blood pressure [36]. These pathophysiological processes can promote the development of cardiovascular diseases; thus, consuming foods within the DI-GM that support the stability of gut microbiota may enhance gut microbiota composition and reduce the incidence of cardiovascular diseases. Consequently, the association between the DI-GM and CVD incidence is more pronounced in these groups.

Red meat, processed meat, and high-fat diets are classified as unfavorable components of the Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DI-GM), with studies suggesting various mechanisms through which these foods contribute to dysbiosis. High-fat diets have been associated with reduced microbial diversity and increased gut permeability [37]. Specifically, high-fat diets rich in meat-based proteins may promote unique and less diverse populations of sulfur-metabolizing bacteria, due to their elevated sulfur content derived from sulfur-containing amino acids and organic sulfurs found in preservatives [38]. Additionally, dietary components such as choline and L-carnitine, which are prevalent in red and processed meats, have been linked to gut microbiota that facilitate the biosynthesis of trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), a metabolite implicated in atherogenesis [39]. These adverse components have also been confirmed in subgroup analyses to be related to the incidence of cardiovascular diseases among individuals with diabetes, hypertension, and the elderly [40].

To our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate the relationship between the Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DI-GM), a dietary quality index associated with gut microbiome diversity, and cardiovascular diseases. The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) implemented rigorous quality control measures in data collection and employed a complex sampling design, allowing for an assessment of this association within a large and diverse adult population in the United States. Furthermore, sensitivity analyses, including subgroup analyses, enhanced the reliability of the research findings.

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. First, reliance on self-reported cardiovascular disease diagnoses introduces information bias in the collected cardiovascular outcome data. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of causal relationships between DI-GM and cardiovascular diseases. Additional prospective research and randomized controlled trials are essential to determine causality. Third, the DI-GM was assessed using a single 24-hour dietary recall, which may not accurately capture habitual dietary intake at the individual level. This limitation is particularly relevant for certain DI-GM components—such as chickpeas, avocados, and cranberries—that tend to be consumed sporadically rather than on a daily basis, potentially misclassifying individuals’ typical dietary patterns.Fourth, the DI-GM is a proxy index based on dietary intake of foods previously linked to gut microbiota composition, rather than a direct measurement of microbial diversity or abundance. The absence of microbiome sequencing data (e.g., 16 S rRNA or metagenomic profiling) in NHANES means that the inferred relationships between diet, gut microbiota, and CVD cannot be biologically validated at the individual level, and the index should be interpreted as a dietary surrogate rather than a measure of the gut microbiome itself. Furthermore, as with many studies, the potential for confounding effects arising from unmeasured variables or residual measurement error due to unknown confounders cannot be entirely ruled out. Additionally, our conclusions are confined to the American population and cannot be generalized to other regions due to differences in dietary habits. Ultimately, even when accounting for numerous confounding variables, including demographic characteristics, lifestyle, diet, and specific diseases, some confounding factors may still affect the results.

Conclusion

The newly proposed DI-GM, which reflects the dietary quality index associated with gut microbiota diversity, was inversely associated with the prevalence of cardiovascular diseases. Furthermore, this association is particularly pronounced in older adults and individuals with hypertension. Given the strong association between diet, gut microbiota, and cardiovascular diseases, future studies on DI-GM and dietary interventions for patients with cardiovascular diseases are crucial for reducing the prevalence of this condition.
