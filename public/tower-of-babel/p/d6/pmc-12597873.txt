Loss of Dmrta1 alters CD8+ T cell activation and resistance to influenza virus infection
Authors: Hiroyuki Kondo, Kunihiro Otsuka, Junko Morimoto, Hideki Arimochi, Shin-ichi Tsukumo, Koji Yasutomo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe generation and function of effector and memory CD8+ T cells are crucial for effective immune responses and long-term immunity. Using gene expression analysis, we found that doublesex- and mab-3-related transcription factor like family A1 (Dmrta1), a member of the DMRT family of transcription factors, is highly expressed in activated and memory CD8+ T cells. In this study, we investigated the role of Dmrta1 in the activation and differentiation of CD8+ T cells. The Dmrta1-deficient (Dmrta1-KO) mice showed an equivalent number of thymic and splenic T cells compared with wild-type mice. Dmrta1 deficiency in T cells resulted in impaired early activation of CD8+ but not CD4+ T cells and reduced expression of granzyme B in CD8+ T cells following influenza virus infection. Although virus-specific CD8+ T cell numbers and cytotoxicity in the lung were comparable between wild-type and Dmrta1-deficient mice during primary infection, Dmrta1-KO mice exhibited a transient accumulation of virus-specific CD8+ T cells in the spleen with reduced cytotoxic activity. Upon secondary challenge, memory CD8+ T cells from Dmrta1-KO mice showed persistent defects in granzyme B expression and cytotoxic function. These findings demonstrate that Dmrta1 modulates the early activation of naïve CD8+ T cells and supports the cytotoxic functionality of both effector and memory CD8+ T cells, particularly in secondary lymphoid organs, with significant implications for antiviral immunity.Keywords: CD8, cytotoxicity, DMRTA1, T cell activation

Introduction

The generation of effector and memory CD8+ T cells is a fundamental aspect of the adaptive immune response, playing a crucial role in the ability to combat infections and provide long-term immunity.1,2,3,4 Effector CD8+ T cells are responsible for the immediate response to pathogens, exhibiting potent cytotoxic activity that targets and eliminates infected cells.2,5,6 Following the resolution of an infection, a subset of these effector cells transitions into memory CD8+ T cells, which persists in the body and confer enhanced protection against subsequent encounters with the same pathogen.7,8,9,10 In addition to CD8+ T cells, innate immune cells such as natural killer cells, dendritic cells, macrophages, and neutrophils play essential roles in the early defense against viral infections by producing inflammatory cytokines and promoting antigen presentation.11 These components contribute to the orchestration of adaptive immunity, including CD8+ T cell responses.

Dmrta1 (Doublesex- and Mab-3-related transcription factor A1) is a member of the DMRT family of transcription factors, which are characterized by a conserved DM domain.12,13,14 This family of transcription factors plays crucial roles in sex determination and differentiation across various species, from invertebrates to vertebrates.13,15 In mammals, DMRT proteins are involved in various developmental processes, including gonadal development and neural differentiation.15,16,17,18,19 Despite the established roles of DMRT family members in development, the specific functions of DMRTA1, particularly within the immune system, remain less well characterized.

Herein, we found Dmrta1 was highly expressed in activated CD8+ T cells and antigen-experienced memory CD8+ T cells. The deficiency of Dmrta1 disturbs the early activation of CD8+ but not CD4+ T cells following T cell stimulation with anti-CD3 monoclonal antibody. Furthermore, the expression of granzyme B was reduced by Dmrta1 deficiency after influenza virus infection. Although after influenza virus infection, virus-specific CD8+ T cells in the lungs are comparable between control and Dmrta1-deficient mice, more tetramer+CD8+ T cells exist in the spleen of Dmrta1-KO mice 7 d after infection. Even after secondary infection with the influenza virus, the expression of granzyme B was low in Dmrta1-deficient mice. Those data suggest that Dmrta1 regulates early activation of CD8+ T cells.

Materials and methods

Mice

A loxP gene fragment was inserted into the mouse Dmrta1 locus using the CRISPR-Cas9 method to establish Dmrta1-flox mice (C57BL/6J background) (Supplementary Fig. 1A) by Dr S. Takahashi (University of Tsukuba).20 To confirm correct gene targeting, the Dmrta1-floxed allele was fully sequenced, and no off-target mutations were observed at the editing site. Additionally, to minimize background variation, Dmrta1-flox mice were backcrossed to the C57BL/6J background for at least six generations. Primer sequences were as follows: Dmrta1-loxP, forward primer 5′- GCTGTCCTTTGGGTTTTTGA -3′ and reverse primer 5′- GTGGGCTGTCATTTTGTCCT-3′ and Cd4−Cre forward primer 5′- TCTCTGTGGCTGGCAGTTTCTCCA -3′ and reverse primer 5′- TCAAGGCCAGACTAGGCTGCCTAT -3′. Cd4−cre transgenic mice were gifts from M. Kubo (RIKEN), OT-1 TCR transgenic mice were purchased from Immuno-Biological Laboratories, and CD45.1+ mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, Maine, USA). Cd4−Cre+Dmrta1flox/wt and Cd4−Cre+Dmrta1flox/flox mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions and used for experiments at 8 to 10 wk of age. In this study, Cd4−Cre+Dmrta1flox/wt mice were used for comparison with the Dmrta1-KO mice. No difference was observed between Cd4−Cre+Dmrta1flox/wt and C57BL/6J mice (Supplementary Fig. 1F). All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the guidelines established by the animal research committee of Tokushima University.

Viruses and infections

Influenza virus A/PR/8 (PR8; H1N1) was provided by Dr S. Sawa (University of Kyushu). The influenza virus was grown by the MDCK cell line (CELL BANK: RCB0995), and the virus titer was measured using the plaque assay, and the viruses were stored at −80°C. For primary infection, mice were anesthetized using isoflurane and infected intranasally with 100 pfu of influenza virus PR8(100 pfu was the lethal dose for mice). For second responses against the influenza virus, splenocytes from influenza virus-infected CD45.2+(Cd4−Cre+Dmrta1flox/wt mice or Cd4−Cre+Dmrta1flox/flox mice) were transferred into CD45.1+ mice. Recipient mice were infected intranasally with 100 pfu of influenza virus the next day. After 8 d of infection, recipient mice were sacrificed and analyzed for the lungs and spleens.

Flow cytometric analysis and cell sorting

For the analysis of thymocyte and splenocyte, the thymus and spleen were crushed with a slide glass. For the analysis of lung cells, minced lung was digested with 0.125% Collagenase D (Roche) in the presence of 0.01% DNase I (Roche). Single-cell suspensions were stained with antibodies specific for TCRβ (BioLegend, clone H57-597), CD8α (BioLegend, clone 53-6.7), CD45 (BioLegend, clone 30-F11), CD4 (BioLegend, clone RM4-5), CD62L (BioLegend, clone MEL-14), CD44 (BioLegend, clone IM7), CD69 (BioLegend, clone H1.2F3), Klrg1 (BioLegend, clone 2F1/KLRG1), CD127 (BioLegend, clone A7R34), and CD103 (BioLegend, clone 2E7). For intracellular staining, we used eBioscience™ Intracellular Fixation & Permeabilization Buffer Set and Granzyme B (BioLegend, clone GB11). Influenza virus-specific CD8+ T cells were detected using major histocompatibility complex class I tetrameric complexes specific for the H-2Db–restricted immunodominant epitope of the influenza virus nucleoprotein (DbNP366–374; ASNENMETM) (MBL). For influenza virus-specific CD8+ T cells staining, the cells were incubated for 30 min at 4 °C, washed, and then stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies for specific surface markers for 20 min at 4 °C. After 3 additional washes with fluorescence-activated cell sorting buffer, the cells were analyzed using a FACSCanto II (BD Biosciences). Additionally, these cells were collected using a Sony cell sorter MA900 (Sony).

In vitro T cell stimulation

Splenocytes (1 × 106 cells) were stimulated in the presence of Ultra-LEAF™ purified anti-mouse CD3 antibody (Biolegend, clone 17A2) for 1 d using a flat 96-well plate. After the stimulation, these cells were stained with antibodies specific for CD4, CD8-α, and CD69.

In vitro memory CD8+ T cell differentiation and gene overexpression using a retrovirus

For in vitro memory CD8+ T cell differentiation, OT-1 transgenic mice were sacrificed, and OT-1 T cells (1 × 106 cells) in the spleen were stimulated in the presence of a peptide (OVA peptide257-264: SIINFEKL, 1 μM) for 1 h. After 1 h of stimulation, half of the supernatant was removed, and fresh medium was added and incubated for 1 d. Then, OT-1 T cells were infected with retrovirus encoding the Dmrta1 gene and incubated in the presence of interleukin-15 (20 ng/ml; BioLegend) for 7 d. The retrovirus was prepared via the transfection of pMXs-IRES-GFP harboring the Dmrta1 gene into Plat-E packaging cells with GeneJuice Transfection Reagent (Millipore). After changing the medium at 24 h, the supernatant was collected 48 h after transfection. Polybrene transfection reagent (Millipore) was added to a final concentration of 8 µg/ml. OT-1 T cells were cultured in 24-well plates for 1 d, and the retroviral supernatant was added. The plates were centrifuged at 2,600 rpm for 90 min at 32 °C. After centrifugation, half of the supernatant was removed, and fresh RPMI complete medium was added.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the lungs using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). RNA from sorted CD8+ T cells was extracted using the RNeasy Plus Micro kit (Qiagen). First-strand cDNA was synthesized from total RNA using the PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (Takara Bio). Real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis was performed using the StepOne Real-Time PCR System and SYBR™ Green PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Primer sequences were as follows: Dmrta 1, forward primer 5′- GGAGCCGGTCAGTCCCAACT -3′ and reverse primer 5′- AATGTAGTCTCTGGCCCAC -3′, Gapdh, forward primer 5′- ACTCCACTCACGGCAAATTC -3′ and reverse primer 5′- TCTCCATGGTGGTGAAGACA -3′, and Influenza-Matrix protein, forward primer 5′- GGACTGCAGCGTAGACGCTT -3′ and reverse primer 5′- CATCCTGTTGTATAGAGGCCCAT-3′.

Cytokine detection

Cytokine concentrations were measured using the LEGENDplex™ Mouse Anti-Virus Response Panel (13-plex) (BioLegend). Briefly, we incubated mouse serum from infected mice with the Mouse Anti-Virus Response panel premixed beads on a plate shaker. After incubation, Mouse Anti-Virus Response panel detection antibodies were added to the mixed solution and incubated on a plate shaker. After incubation, the mixed solution was analyzed using a FACSCanto II (BD Biosciences).

Histological evaluation

After mice were sacrificed, lung was collected and fixed using 10% phosphate-buffered formalin (pH = 7.2). Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded lung sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin. The pathological score was defined as the total score of 4 following criteria; (1) inflammation (0–3): no changed, mild, moderate (lymphocytic or neutrophilic infiltration) or severe (formation of abscess or inducible bronchus-associated lymphoid tissue), (2) edema (0–2): no changed, focal peribronchial edema or widely peribronchial edema, (3) hemorrhage (0–2): no changed, focal alveolar hemorrhage or widely alveolar hemorrhage, (4) fibrosis (0–2): no changed, partly fibrotic changed or fibrosis.

In vivo cytolytic assay

We pulsed splenocytes derived from CD45.1+ mice with 10 mg/ml Influenza NP peptide (ASNENMETM: Genescript), OVA peptide (SIINFEKL: Genescript), or No peptide for 1 h at 37 °C and labeled them with 1,000 dilution, 3,000 dilution, or no stained of CFSE (Cayman Chemical) for 30 min at 37 °C, respectively. Influenza-infected B6 mice (CD45.2+ mice), 7 d after infection, received 5 × 106 of each targeted cell population via intravenous injection. After 20 h, we detected CD45.1+ cells from the lungs and spleens and analyzed the expression of CSFE.

Western blotting

The cell lysates extracted from splenocytes using radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (Nacalai tesque) containing Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche) were boiled with dodecyl sulfate sample buffer, separated using sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, and transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (Merck Millipore). Membranes were probed with anti-DMRTA1 antibody (GeneTex) using a 500 dilution and anti-β-ACTIN antibody (Proteintech) using a 5,000 dilution, and then with secondary antibodies conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (Jackson ImmunoResearch) using a 10,000 dilution. Signals were detected using the Chemi-Lumi One (Nacalai Tesque), and pictures were taken using the Odyssey XF Imaging system (LI-COR).

Statistical analysis

The data of results are expressed as mean ± SD. One-way ANOVA was performed for multiple comparisons, while Student t-test was used for comparisons between 2 groups.

Results

Dmrta1 is highly expressed in activated CD8+ T cells and memory CD8+ T cells

We purified T cell subpopulations in the thymus and spleen in naïve mice and influenza virus-specific tetramer+CD8+ T cells in the spleen and lungs in influenza virus-infected mice and tested Dmrta1 expression in various subpopulations. Dmrta1 was expressed in splenic effector and central memory CD8+ T cells in uninfected mice and tetramer+CD8+ T cells in lungs of influenza virus-infected mice but low in thymocytes and naïve CD8+ T cells in the spleen (Fig. 1A). Memory T cells could be divided by the expression of CD49d, and CD49dlow memory T cells are antigen-inexperienced, and CD49dhigh memory T cells are antigen-experienced.19 We purified splenic CD49dlow and CD49dhigh memory CD8+ T cells and tested Dmrta1 expression in these cells. Dmrta1 was highly expressed in CD49dlow memory CD8+ T cells compared with CD49dhigh memory CD8+ T cells (Fig. 1B). From these results, Dmrta1 is highly expressed in activated CD8+ T cells and antigen-inexperienced memory CD8+ T cells.

Dmrta1 deficiency affects initial T cell activation in vitro

As Dmrta1 null mice show developmental defects,13 we established Dmrta1fl/fl mice and deleted Dmrta1 in T cells by CD4−Cre transgene (Supplementary Figs. 1A–D). Dmrta1-KO mice, in which Dmrta1 is deleted in T cells, showed no significant differences in the total number of thymocytes (Figs. 2A, B) or the frequency of thymocyte subpopulations (Figs. 2C, D) compared with wild-type mice (CD4−Cre+Dmrta1fl/wt). Similarly, the numbers of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Figs. 2E, F) and the proportions of CD44-CD62L+ naïve (Nv), CD44+CD62L+ central memory (CM), and CD44+CD62Llow effector memory (EM) T cells were comparable between Dmrta1-KO and control mice for both CD4+ (Figs. 2G, H) and CD8+ T cells (Figs. 2I, J). However, upon T cell stimulation with anti-CD3 monoclonal antibody, CD69 expression was lower in CD8+ T cells from Dmrta1-KO mice than in controls after 1 d, whereas CD4+ T cells exhibited no difference (Figs. 2K, L, and Supplementary Fig. 1F). Dmrta1 plays crucial roles in sex determination. Thus, we investigated whether Dmrta1-KO mice show sex differences in CD69 expression. However, no difference was observed in CD69 expression upon T cell receptor (TCR)-mediated stimulation of CD8+ T cells between male and female Dmrta1-KO mice (Supplementary Fig. 1G). These results suggest that DMRTA1 is dispensable for T cell development in the thymus but plays a role in regulating the early activation of CD8+ T cells in vitro.

Susceptibility to primary influenza virus infection was decreased in Dmrta1-KO mice

We tested whether Dmrta1 deficiency changes the susceptibility to influenza virus infection to test the functional significance of the early activation defect in CD8+ T cells in Dmrta1-KO mice. We infected 100 pfu of influenza virus in both wild-type and Dmrta1-KO mice and monitored body weight and mouse survival. The body weight loss was comparable between wild-type and Dmrta1-KO mice (Fig. 3A). The survival in Dmrta1-KO mice was slightly increased compared with wild-type mice (Fig. 3B). The virus mRNA in the lungs 7 d after infection (Fig. 3C) and pathological scores 5, 7, and 14 d after infection (Fig. 3D) were similar between both groups. We also tested serum inflammatory cytokines and chemokines 7 d after infection, and no significant difference was observed in any molecules between both groups (Fig. 3E). These results indicated that Dmrta1 deficiency in T cells showed higher influenza virus resistance than control mice.

The number of virus antigen-specific T cells in the lungs of Dmrta1-KO mice was also equivalent to those of wild-type mice, 7, 14, 35, and 60 d after infection (Figs. 4C, D). However, the expression of granzyme B in tetramer+ cells was lower in Dmrta1-KO mice than in control mice 7 d after infection (Figs. 4E, F). Next, we investigated whether the slightly decreased expression of granzyme B in Dmrta1-KO mice affects the killing activity of influenza virus-infected cells. The carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE)-labeled spleen cells pulsed with influenza NP peptide were transferred into control and Dmrta1-KO mice infected with the influenza virus. The frequency of CFSEhigh cells in the lungs of both control and Dmrta1-KO mice was significantly decreased compared with uninfected mice (Figs. 4G, H). However, the frequency of CFSEhigh cells was comparable between control and Dmrta1-KO mice.

As Dmrta1 was highly expressed in virus-specific CD8+ T cells in the lungs 42 d after infection (Fig. 1B), we next tested the number of CD103+CD69+ tissue resident memory CD8+ T cells in the lungs. The cell number of CD103+CD69+ in tetramer+ cells was comparable between wild-type and Dmrta1-KO mice (Figs. 4I, J). These results indicated that Dmrta1 deficiency slightly disturbs granzyme B expression in CD8+ T cells but did not affect the number and killing activity of CD8+ T cells in the lung under influenza infection.

Dmrta1 deficiency in the spleen temporarily increased the number of antigen-specific CD8+ T cells in the spleen

We next tested whether the reduction of early CD8+ T cell activation in Dmrta1-KO mice affected the number of CD8+ T cells in the spleen. The number of tetramer+CD8+ T cells in the spleen was significantly increased 7 d after infection in Dmrta1-KO mice compared with wild-type mice (Figs. 5A, B). However, 14, 35, and 60 d after infection, the number of tetramer+CD8+ T cells in the spleen was comparable between wild-type and Dmrta1-KO mice. We next tested the expression of CD62L in tetramer+ cells in the spleen. The cell number of CD62L+tetramer+ cells on Dmrta1-KO mice was temporarily increased 7 d after infection (Figs. 5C, D). However, the frequency of CD62L+tetramer+ cells did not change in Dmrta1-KO mice compared with wild-type mice (Figs. 5C, D). In vivo cytotoxicity assays revealed a reduced frequency of CFSEhigh target cells in the spleens of infected Dmrta1-KO mice compared with controls (Figs. 5E, F).

Cytotoxicity of memory CD8+ T cells is also impaired in Dmrta1-KO mice

We sought to define the functions of DMRTA1 in secondary responses. We purified spleen cells from wild-type or Dmrta1-KO mice infected with the influenza virus (day 35 after influenza infection). Those spleen cells were transferred into CD45.1 mice, and influenza virus were then infected (Fig. 6A). The susceptibility against the infection (Fig. 6B) and number of CD8+ T cells and tetramer-positive cells in donor cells (Figs. 6C, D) 8 d after infection was comparable between mice in which T cells from wild-type and Dmrta1-KO mice were transferred. However, the expression of granzyme B was lower in Dmrta1-KO than wild-type T cells 8 d after infection (Figs. 6E, F). These results indicate that splenic memory CD8+ T cells generated in Dmrta1-KO mice have impaired functions against a second infection.

Discussion

The differentiation of effector and memory T cells requires complex gene regulation.1,3 To identify key regulators of these pathways, we analyzed the function of DMRTA1, which is highly expressed in activated CD8+ T cells and CD49low memory CD8+ T cells (antigen-inexperienced). Conditional deletion of Dmrta1 in T cells impaired early CD8+ T cell activation and certain functional parameters during both primary and secondary influenza virus infections, while unexpectedly conferring a slight increase in survival following primary infection. These findings highlight DMRTA1 as a modulator of CD8+ T cell activation and functional differentiation, with potential implications for virus-specific immune responses.

Dmrta1 contributes to the early activation of CD8+ T cells, both in vitro and in vivo, during initial and secondary responses to influenza virus infection. This impairment in early activation is characterized by reduced expression of activation markers such as CD69 and a diminished ability to produce granzyme B. These defects suggest that Dmrta1 is involved in modulating the transcriptional landscape necessary for full activation of CD8+ T cells. One possible mechanism is that Dmrta1 directly regulates the transcription of genes essential for CD8+T cell activation, cytotoxicity, recruitment, and proliferation. Further studies involving chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing could provide deeper insights into the specific genes regulated by Dmrta1.

Despite impaired early activation, Dmrta1-KO mice exhibited a transient increase in the number of tetramer+CD8+ T cells in the spleen seven days post-influenza infection. This phenomenon suggests that Dmrta1 might influence the migratory capacity of CD8+ T cells or their retention in specific organs. Possibly, defective activation in the lungs alters the trafficking cues of CD8+ T cells, leading to their accumulation in the spleen. The normalization of CD8+ T cell numbers at later time points further supports the idea that Dmrta1 affects the initial priming phase rather than long-term maintenance of T cell populations.

Although Dmrta1-deficient CD8+ T cells exhibited impaired early activation in vitro, their cytotoxic function in the lungs during influenza virus infection was largely preserved. Given the critical role of CD8+ T cells in controlling the influenza virus at the site of infection, this finding suggests that Dmrta1 is not essential for mounting effective cytotoxic responses in the pulmonary environment. This tissue-specific preservation of function may reflect compensatory mechanisms in vivo, such as strong inflammatory cues or redundant activation pathways in the lung that can override the early activation deficits observed under in vitro conditions. In contrast, a mild but reproducible reduction in cytotoxic activity was observed in the spleen of Dmrta1-KO mice. This discrepancy raises the possibility that Dmrta1 may play a context-dependent role in regulating CD8+ T cell effector differentiation or maintenance outside the primary site of infection. Rather than indicating an intrinsic defect in splenic CD8+ T cell function per se, this reduction may reflect differences in T cell activation thresholds, antigen exposure, or migration dynamics between the lung and secondary lymphoid tissues. Thus, the observed phenotype, preserved pulmonary cytotoxicity despite impaired in vitro activation and reduced splenic cytotoxicity, may be reconciled by considering the distinct immunological contexts of each tissue. In the highly inflamed lung environment during active infection, Dmrta1-deficient CD8+ T cells may still receive sufficient activation signals to acquire cytotoxic function. Moreover, in the spleen, where antigenic stimulation is likely weaker or indirect, the intrinsic activation defect may be more apparent. Further studies examining T cell receptor signaling strength, transcriptional profiles, and tissue trafficking behavior in Dmrta1-KO mice will be necessary to fully delineate these mechanisms.

Dmrta1 belongs to the Dmrt family, which has several family proteins.11,12,13 The function of Dmrta1 might be overlapped with Dmrta2 and Dmrt3 because Dmrta1 single-deficient mice had no clear abnormalities, but Dmrta1, Dmrta2, and Dmrt3 triple-gene-deficient mice had abnormalities in embryos.16 In CD8+ T cells, it remains possible that Dmrta2 and Dmrt3 compensate for Dmrta1 function in CD8+ T cells, thereby mitigating the effects of Dmrta1 deficiency.

In conclusion, our study identifies Dmrta1 as a previously unrecognized regulator of CD8+ T cell activation and functional differentiation. The impaired granzyme B expression and altered activation dynamics in Dmrta1-deficient CD8+ T cells underscore the need for further mechanistic studies to elucidate the molecular networks controlled by this transcription factor. Such insights may provide novel opportunities for modulating immune responses in the context of infection, immunotherapy, and vaccine design.
