Synthetic lethality targets in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma: prevalence and limitations of liquid biopsy detection in a minority-serving cancer center
Authors: Mahmoud Abdelsamia, Yafang Li, Daniel Eastwood, Ian Rabinowitz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsSynthetic lethality (SL) exploits genetic vulnerabilities in cancer cells, offering therapeutic opportunities beyond conventional targets. While BRCA/poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibition is FDA-approved for pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), the prevalence of other actionable SL targets remains poorly characterized.Patients and methodsWe retrospectively analyzed 260 PDAC patients at the University of New Mexico Comprehensive Cancer Center (2017–2022); 74 underwent somatic sequencing (54 tissue, 17 liquid biopsy) and 117 underwent germline testing. The cohort included 11% Native American patients. We characterized eight SL gene targets and compared mutation detection between biopsy methods.ResultsTP53 mutations were identified in 73% of patients and CDKN2A was the most prevalent SL target (46%), followed by SMAD4 (24%), MTAP (15%), and ARID1A (14%). Liquid biopsy significantly underdetected key alterations: KRAS detection decreased from 93% (tissue) to 47% (liquid, p < 0.001) and SMAD4 from 33% to 0% (p = 0.004). Native American patients showed significantly lower TP53 mutations than White patients (38% vs 79%, p = 0.023).ConclusionsMultiple SL targets beyond BRCA exist in PDAC. Liquid biopsy inadequately detects critical alterations, particularly deletions. Native American patients demonstrated preliminary molecular differences warranting validation in larger studies. Tissue-based molecular profiling will be essential for comprehensive patient selection as SL-targeted therapies advance.KEYWORDS: Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, synthetic lethality, CDKN2A, liquid biopsy, Native American, precision oncology, molecular profiling, PARP inhibitors

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is the fourth leading cause of cancer-related death in the United States, with an annual incidence of approximately 64,000 cases and 51,000 deaths, resulting in a dismal 5-year survival rate of 9% for metastatic disease [1]. This high mortality-to-incidence ratio reflects both poor early detection and limited therapeutic options. Notably, immunotherapies targeting programmed death-1 (PD-1) and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) have shown minimal efficacy in PDAC, unlike their success in other malignancies [2].

Whole-exome and whole-genome sequencing of large PDAC cohorts has identified four major driver mutations: KRAS (90%), TP53 (50–75%), CDKN2A (30–40%), and SMAD4 (30%), which collectively account for the majority of somatic alterations in this disease [3]. However, these genes have proven largely undruggable through conventional therapeutic approaches, necessitating alternative strategies.

Synthetic lethality (SL) offers a promising framework for targeting these otherwise intractable alterations. SL occurs when combined disruption of two genes causes cell death, whereas inactivation of either gene alone remains viable [4]. Therapeutically, this concept exploits the pharmacologic inhibition of one gene product in cells where the synthetic lethal partner is genetically inactivated. This approach is particularly relevant for PDAC, where passenger deletions of critical metabolic enzymes are common. Cancer cells tolerate these deletions through compensatory expression of partially redundant paralogues, creating a selective dependency that can be therapeutically exploited [5]. Targeted inhibition of the remaining paralogue thus becomes lethal to cancer cells while sparing normal tissues that retain both functional copies [6].

The only clinically validated SL strategy in PDAC involves PARP inhibitors for patients with BRCA1/2 mutations, approved by the FDA in 2019 [7,8]. However, large-scale functional genomic screens using CRISPR libraries have revealed numerous additional SL interactions, suggesting this approach may be widely applicable beyond BRCA [4–6]. Multiple genes with established SL vulnerabilities are altered in PDAC, including CDKN2A (creating thymidylate synthase (TYMS) dependency) [9], SMAD4 deletions (creating malic enzyme 2 (ME2)/ malic enzyme 3 (ME3) vulnerability) [10], MTAP deletions (creating PRMT5 dependency) [11], ARID1A (creating ARID1B dependency) [12], and ATM (sensitizing to PARP/ATR inhibition) [13]. The therapeutic landscape for KRAS-mutant PDAC is rapidly evolving, with direct KRAS targeting now emerging as a clinically viable strategy. While no KRAS inhibitor is currently FDA-approved specifically for PDAC, sotorasib and adagrasib are approved for KRAS G12C-mutant disease—a subtype representing only 1–2% of PDAC cases, severely limiting their applicability [14,15]. For the broader KRAS G12D-mutant population (~35–40% of PDAC), MRTX1133 is in Phase I/II trials with potent preclinical activity [16], and setidegrasib (ASP3082), a first-in-class KRAS G12D-targeted proteolysis-targeting chimera (PROTAC) degrader, demonstrated an objective response rate (ORR) of 24% and a median overall survival (OS) of 10.3 months in a Phase I study [17]. Most significantly, daraxonrasib (RMC-6236), a pan-RAS inhibitor targeting the active GTP-bound state across G12D, G12V, G12R, Q61H and wild-type RAS – covering >90% of PDAC—demonstrated an ORR of 35%, median progression-free survival (PFS) of 8.5 months, and median OS of 13.1 months in second-line RAS G12-mutant PDAC in a Phase I/II study, markedly exceeding historical benchmarks of ~2–3 months PFS and ~5–7 months OS with second-line chemotherapy [18]. The Phase III RASolute 302 trial demonstrated groundbreaking results, with daraxonrasib significantly improving overall survival compared to standard chemotherapy in previously treated metastatic PDAC; median OS was 13.2 versus 6.6 months (HR 0.40, p<0.001) and median PFS was 7.3 versus 3.5 months (HR 0.45, p<0.001) in the RAS G12 population, with a favorable safety profile and treatment discontinuation due to adverse events in only 1.2% versus 11.2% with chemotherapy [19]. As direct KRAS inhibition advances, understanding co-occurring SL vulnerabilities in KRAS-mutant tumors becomes increasingly clinically relevant. Despite this promise, the prevalence of actionable SL targets in real-world PDAC populations, particularly in underrepresented ethnic groups, remains poorly characterized. Furthermore, the optimal molecular profiling approach—tissue versus liquid biopsy—for identifying these vulnerabilities has not been systematically evaluated. We therefore sought to: (1) determine the prevalence of genes with known SL vulnerabilities in our diverse patient cohort, (2) compare mutation detection rates between tissue and liquid biopsy, and (3) examine molecular patterns across ethnic groups to inform precision medicine strategies in PDAC.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

We retrospectively reviewed 398 patients with PDAC seen at the University of New Mexico Comprehensive Cancer Center (UNMCCC) between 2017 and 2022. Approval was obtained through institutional IRB exemption (Study ID: 24-145). Medical records were de-identified using standard protocol via an Honest Broker. Patients were eligible if they had a confirmed PDAC diagnosis, were evaluated at UNMCCC during the study period, and underwent genetic testing. Of 398 reviewed patients, 260 were eligible, and 155 (59%) underwent genetic testing.

Data collection and genetic testing

We extracted demographic and clinical variables from medical records including age, sex, race/ethnicity, smoking history, disease stage at diagnosis, genetic testing platform, and specimen type. The treating physician chose the sequencing platform based on clinical availability. The majority of somatic sequencing was performed using Foundation One CDx (70/74 patients), which employs next-generation sequencing (NGS) of 324 genes to detect base substitutions, insertions/deletions, copy number alterations, and gene rearrangements on multiple specimen types including formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue, peripheral whole blood, and bone marrow aspirate [20]. Invitae Hereditary Cancer Panel analyzes 70 genes associated with hereditary cancer predisposition, focusing on germline mutations [21]. Seventy-four patients underwent somatic sequencing: 54 tissue biopsy, 17 liquid biopsy; 3 patients were excluded (2 with multiple specimen types, 1 with unspecified specimen type). One hundred seventeen patients underwent Invitae germline testing; 36 patients received both platforms.

Synthetic lethality gene identification

We identified eight genes with established or emerging SL interactions based on two criteria: (1) documented prevalence in PDAC based on large genomic databases including The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) [3], and (2) published evidence of a specific synthetic lethal vulnerability: BRCA1/2 [7,8], CDKN2A [9], SMAD4 [10], MTAP [11], ARID1A [12], ATM [13], BLM [22], and MYC [23,24]. For each gene, we recorded the type of alteration (mutation, deletion, or amplification) and specimen source.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for demographic and clinical variables. Confidence intervals for somatic mutation prevalence were calculated using the Clopper-Pearson exact method; germline testing results are reported as prevalence rates. For mutation comparisons between tissue and liquid biopsy samples, as well as between Native American and White patients, chi-square tests were used to compare proportions between groups. Fisher’s exact test was applied when any cell count was less than 7. Odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals were calculated for comparisons between ethnic groups. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 without adjustment for multiple testing, given the exploratory and hypothesis-generating nature of the study. All analyses were performed using SAS software version 9.4 (The SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Results

Patient Demographics and testing patterns

We identified 260 eligible patients with PDAC seen at UNMCCC between 2017 and 2022. The median age was 68 years, 58% were male, and 24% had a smoking history. Disease stage distribution included 38 resectable (15%), 76 locally advanced unresectable (29%), and 146 metastatic (56%) cases. Only 155 of 260 eligible patients (59%) underwent genetic testing. Among these, 117 had germline testing via Invitae, 74 had somatic tumor sequencing, and 36 received both platforms.

The somatic sequencing cohort (n = 74) included 58 White/Anglo (78%), 8 American Indian/Alaska Native (11%), 4 Asian (5%), 1 Black/African American (1%), and 3 unknown/declined race (4%). Twenty-one patients (28%) were Hispanic/Latino ethnicity, recorded independently of race. For tissue versus liquid biopsy comparisons, 71 patients with single specimen types (54 tissue, 17 liquid) were analyzed after excluding 3 patients with ambiguous specimen type. For ethnic molecular pattern comparison, Native American (n = 8) and White (n = 58) patients were compared, excluding other racial groups due to small sample sizes. Synthetic lethality targets

Among 74 patients who underwent somatic next-generation sequencing, we identified genes with known SL vulnerabilities (Table 2). CDKN2A was the most common alteration, found in 34 patients (45.95%, 95% confidence interval (CI) 34.29–57.93%), including 15 mutations and 19 deletions. CDKN2A loss creates vulnerability to TYMS inhibition. The PAn-canceR Inferred Synthetic lethalites (PARIS) machine learning tool identified TYMS as a synthetic lethal partner with CDKN2A [9], and cladribine—an FDA-approved drug for multiple sclerosis and hairy cell leukemia—has been identified as a potentially repurposable agent for CDKN2A-mutant tumors through the Synthetic Lethality Knowledge Graph (SLKG) [25].

SMAD4 alterations occurred in 18 patients (24.32%, 95% CI 15.10–35.69%), comprising 16 mutations and 2 homozygous deletions. Importantly, only the homozygous deletions—not the mutations—create the ME2/ME3 metabolic vulnerability. SMAD4 is located on chromosome 18q21, less than 200 kb away from ME2. Dey et al demonstrated that loss of ME2 in the SMAD4 locus creates a cancer-specific metabolic vulnerability upon targeting of ME3. ME3 depletion selectively kills ME2-null PDAC cells consistent with synthetic lethality [10]. In our cohort, only 2 of the 18 SMAD4-altered patients (2.7%) had homozygous deletions that would create this ME2/ME3 vulnerability.

MTAP deletions were present in 11 patients (14.86%, 95% CI 7.66–25.04%). MTAP loss co-occurs with CDKN2A deletion and creates dependency on protein arginine methyltransferase 5 (PRMT5). MTAP deficiency sensitizes cells to PRMT5 knockdown, and combination of PRMT5 inhibitors with type I PRMT inhibitors (GSK3368715) reveals robust synergistic anti-proliferative effects in MTAP-deficient cancer cell lines [11]. In early clinical trials with PRMT inhibitors, 29% of patients achieved stable disease [26].

ARID1A alterations were found in 10 patients (13.51%, 95% CI 6.68–23.45%), comprising 9 mutations and 1 deletion. ARID1A is a specific vulnerability in ARID1A-mutant cancers through synthetic lethality with its paralogue ARID1B [12]. Loss of ARID1A creates selective dependency on ARID1B, making ARID1B inhibition a potential therapeutic strategy.

MYC amplifications were found in 5 patients (6.76%, 95% CI 2.23–15.07%). C-MYC overexpression is common in pancreatic cancer and associated with chemoresistance, intra-tumor angiogenesis, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and metastasis [24]. Combined inactivation of CTPS1 and ATR is synthetically lethal to MYC-overexpressing cancer cells, providing a novel therapeutic strategy [23].

ATM showed 2 somatic mutations (2.70%, 95% CI 0.33–9.42%). ATM deficiency generates genomic instability that sensitizes PDAC cells to therapy-induced DNA damage, and PARP inhibition diminishes cell viability in ATM-deficient PDAC cells [13].

Major driver mutations

TP53 mutations were detected in 54 of 74 patients (72.97%, 95% CI 61.39–82.65%). KRAS mutations were found in 60 of 74 patients (81.08%, 95% CI 70.30–89.25%). Among identifiable subtypes, the distribution was G12D (26 patients, 43%), G12V (17, 28%), G12R (10, 17%), with other subtypes each representing ≤2 patients. Notably, only one patient (1.7%) harbored the druggable G12C mutation, consistent with PDAC’s characteristically low G12C prevalence compared to lung cancer (14%) and colorectal cancer (4%) [27,28]. The predominance of G12D mutations (43%) highlights the urgent need for G12D-targeted therapies; MRTX1133, a selective KRAS G12D inhibitor, is currently being evaluated in Phase I/II trials and our cohort’s 26 patients with G12D mutations would potentially be eligible for such targeted approaches [29,30]. Among Hispanic/Latino patients with KRAS-mutant tumors in our cohort, G12D was the predominant subtype, observed in 10 of 21 patients (47.6%). In contrast, G12C was not identified in any Hispanic/Latino patient (0/21), while G12V was observed in 4 of 21 patients (19.0%). Further stratification by Native American race was not performed because of the small subgroup size (n = 8), which precluded reliable subtype-specific estimates.

Germline testing findings

Germline testing via Invitae identified genetic alterations in 60 of 117 patients (51%). Seven patients had BRCA1/2 mutations, representing the only FDA-approved SL target in PDAC, eligible for PARP inhibitor therapy. The prevalence of germline BRCA mutations (5.98%) aligns with published estimates ranging from 0.7–5.7% for BRCA2 and 0.3–2.3% for BRCA1, with higher rates in Ashkenazi Jewish populations (up to 19%) [31]. The median age of BRCA1/2 mutation carriers was 53 years, substantially younger than the cohort median of 68 years. Additionally, nine patients had ATM mutations (7.69% of germline-tested patients) and three had BLM mutations (2.56%). BLM loss combined with BRCA1-BARD1 deficiency causes a synergistic growth defect in human cells, suggesting potential therapeutic relevance [22].

Tissue versus liquid biopsy comparison

Liquid biopsy demonstrated significantly lower mutation detection rates across all major genes (Table 3). KRAS detection was markedly reduced: 47% (8/17) in liquid biopsy versus 93% (50/54) in tissue biopsy (p = 0.0001), representing a greater than 2-fold difference in sensitivity. Similarly, TP53 detection was 47% versus 85% (p = 0.001), SMAD4 0% versus 33% (p = 0.004), and CDKN2A 18% versus 56% (p = 0.010), representing a 3-fold reduction. Among SL targets, MTAP deletions showed a trend toward lower detection in liquid biopsy (0% vs 20%, p = 0.06), and ARID1A alterations showed a similar trend (0% vs 19%, p = 0.10). Critically, SMAD4 was completely absent from all 17 liquid biopsy samples (0%), despite being present in one-third of tissue samples, indicating that liquid biopsy is inadequate for comprehensive molecular profiling of SL targets in PDAC.

Ethnic differences in molecular patterns

Native American patients (n = 8) demonstrated significantly different molecular profiles compared to White patients (n = 58) (Table 4). Most notably, TP53 mutations were found in only 38% (3/8) of Native American patients compared to 79% (46/58) of White patients (odds ratio (OR) =0.164, 95% CI 0.036–0.750, p = 0.023), representing a 6-fold difference. SMAD4 mutations were completely absent in Native American patients (0/8, 0%) compared to 29% (17/58) in White patients (p = 0.101). KRAS mutations showed a non-significant trend toward lower prevalence in Native Americans (63% vs 85%, OR = 0.303, 95% CI 0.064–1.432, p = 0.152). CDKN2A mutations occurred in 25% of Native American patients versus 48% of White patients (OR =0.385, 95% CI 0.077–1.924, p = 0.245). These findings are preliminary and hypothesis-generating given the small sample size (n = 8), with wide confidence intervals reflecting substantial uncertainty. Larger, inclusive genomic studies are needed to confirm or refute these signals.

Discussion

This study characterized SL gene vulnerabilities in 74 PDAC patients from a minority-serving cancer center who underwent somatic next-generation sequencing. Our prevalence findings are consistent with The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data (Table 5): CDKN2A (46% vs 41% TCGA), SMAD4 (24% vs 27%), MTAP (15% vs 13%), ARID1A (14% vs 6–8%), ATM (2.7% vs 3%), and MYC (6.8% vs 5%). Two key findings emerged with important clinical implications: liquid biopsy significantly underdetects mutations critical for SL-targeted therapy selection, and Native American patients demonstrated preliminary molecular differences that require validation in larger cohorts.BRCA1/2 mutations remain the only FDA-approved SL target in PDAC following the POLO trial, which demonstrated maintenance olaparib benefit in germline BRCA-mutated metastatic PDAC that had not progressed after platinum-based chemotherapy [7,8]. However, only 247 of 3,315 screened patients (7.5%) harbored germline BRCA mutations [32], highlighting the critical need for additional targetable vulnerabilities. In our cohort, BRCA1/2 alterations were found in 7 of 117 germline-tested patients (6.0%), consistent with published rates and reinforcing that the vast majority of PDAC patients require alternative therapeutic strategies.TP53 mutations were the most prevalent alteration in our cohort, identified in 73% of patients with striking mutational heterogeneity. TP53 has long been considered “undruggable,” making synthetic lethality an especially attractive strategy [33]. TP53 loss disables the G1/S checkpoint, forcing tumor cells to rely on S/G2 checkpoints mediated by the ATR–CHK1–WEE1 axis; inhibiting these kinases drives TP53-mutant cells into mitosis with unrepaired DNA, causing mitotic catastrophe and selective cell death [34,35]. Preclinical data in pancreatic cancer xenografts showed that WEE1 inhibition combined with gemcitabine produced a 4-fold enhancement in tumor regression selectively in TP53-deficient models [36], consistent with a Phase II trial of adavosertib in TP53-mutant platinum-resistant ovarian cancer that achieved an ORR of 43% [37]. For the 27% of patients in our cohort retaining wild-type TP53, MDM2 inhibitors represent an emerging strategy—by preventing p53 degradation in tumors with MDM2 amplification, these agents have advanced to clinical trials, with AMG232 in Phase I and idasanutlin (RG7388) having reached Phase III development [38,39]. Platinum-based chemotherapy has been explored in TP53-mutant tumors, though the relationship is complex—while some studies report enhanced platinum sensitivity with TP53 loss, others demonstrate resistance, with outcomes varying by mutation subtype and tumor type [40–42]. Notably, liquid biopsy detected TP53 mutations in only 47% of cases compared to 85% by tissue biopsy (p = 0.001), underscoring that tissue-based profiling is essential for accurate identification of patients who may benefit from these emerging strategies. Beyond BRCA and TP53, CDKN2A represents the most prevalent SL vulnerability, with TYMS identified as a synthetic lethal partner through machine learning approaches. The clinical translation of this vulnerability remains in early stages — TYMS inhibitors including 5-FU and pemetrexed are widely FDA-approved for various cancers with preclinical data supporting preferential killing of CDKN2A-deficient cells, though no CDKN2A-stratified clinical trials have yet been conducted in PDAC [9]. Cladribine, FDA-approved for multiple sclerosis and hairy cell leukemia, has been identified through the SLKG as a potentially repurposable agent with in vitro sensitivity in CDKN2A-loss cells [25]. Given its prevalence in our cohort and the availability of existing FDA-approved drug candidates, this gene-drug relationship represents a high-priority target for prospective clinical investigation in PDAC. MTAP deletions were present in 15% of our cohort. In our cohort, all 11 MTAP-deleted patients (100%) harbored concurrent CDKN2A loss, consistent with the chromosomal proximity of MTAP and CDKN2A on 9p21.3. MTAP loss creates dependency on PRMT5 through accumulation of its substrate MTA, rendering MTAP-deleted cells selectively sensitive to PRMT5 inhibition [11,43]. While first-generation PRMT5 inhibitors failed due to dose-limiting toxicities, a new generation of MTA-cooperative PRMT5 inhibitors has overcome this limitation. MRTX1719 demonstrated partial responses with 30–60% tumor burden reductions in patients with MTAP-deleted solid tumors in interim Phase I/II results [44]. Combinatorial CRISPR screening further identified that PRMT5 inhibition combined with MAPK pathway inhibitors leads to complete responses in CDKN2A/MTAP-null, RAS-active tumors in vivo [45], a particularly relevant finding as all 11 MTAP-deleted patients in our cohort also harbored KRAS mutations.ARID1A alterations were present in 14% of our cohort, all representing loss-of-function variants. ARID1A loss creates selective dependency on its paralog ARID1B through synthetic lethality within the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex [12]. No FDA-approved therapy exists specifically for ARID1A-deficient tumors, but multiple mechanisms of synthetic lethality are under clinical investigation. ARID1A loss upregulates EZH2 activity, and EZH2 inhibition selectively kills ARID1A-mutant cancer cells [46,47]; tulmimetostat, a next-generation dual EZH2/EZH1 inhibitor, has shown confirmed responses in ARID1A-mutant cohorts in an ongoing Phase I/II trial (NCT04104776) [48]. ARID1A deficiency also increases replication stress and dependence on ATR-mediated DNA repair, with ceralasertib demonstrating clinical activity in ARID1A-deficient solid tumors (NCT03682289) [49]. Furthermore, ARID1A loss impairs homologous recombination repair, enhancing sensitivity to PARP inhibition, particularly when combined with temozolomide, which potently induces replication stress and tumor regression in ARID1A mutant preclinical models [50]; an ongoing trial is evaluating talazoparib in ARID1A-selected cohorts (NCT04550494). Additionally, ARID1A mutations have been associated with enhanced response to immune checkpoint blockade [51,52], a relationship warranting prospective investigation in PDAC. Critically, liquid biopsy detected no ARID1A alterations in our cohort (0% vs. 19% in tissue, p = 0.10), meaning all potentially eligible patients would be missed by liquid biopsy-based screening.SMAD4 alterations occurred in 24% of our cohort, comparable to total SMAD4 inactivation rates of 20–55% reported in large cohorts, which include mutations (~20–22%), loss of heterozygosity, and deletions [3,53,54]. However, a critical therapeutic distinction must be emphasized: total SMAD4 alterations encompass multiple mechanisms of inactivation, but only homozygous (deep) deletions create the ME2/ME3 synthetic lethality vulnerability [10]. In our cohort, merely 2 of 18 SMAD4-altered patients (2.7% of total cohort, 11% of SMAD4-altered cases) had homozygous deletions. While precise homozygous deletion frequencies are inconsistently reported in the literature, studies suggest rates substantially lower than total alteration frequency [55,56], with some series finding homozygous deletions in only single-digit percentages of cases. This distinction is critical for clinical trial design: screening for “SMAD4 alterations” alone would substantially overestimate the eligible patient population for ME2/ME3-targeted therapies by approximately 9-fold in our cohort. The remaining 16 patients with SMAD4 mutations or heterozygous loss would not be expected to benefit from ME2/ME3 inhibition, as they retain partial SMAD4 function. Clinical translation of this vulnerability remains in early stages – no FDA-approved therapy or registered clinical trial currently selects patients based on SMAD4 deletion as a synthetic lethality biomarker. However, selective ME3 inhibitors have demonstrated potent growth inhibition in SMAD4-deficient PDAC cell lines preclinically, with synergistic effects observed in combination with the MEK inhibitor trametinib [57]. Beyond ME2/ME3, additional preclinical vulnerabilities in SMAD4-deficient PDAC have been identified including PARP1 inhibition combined with radiotherapy, mTOR/AKT pathway inhibition, and STAT3 inhibition with multiple STAT3 inhibitors showing selective efficacy against SMAD4-deficient PDAC cells [58–60], though none have reached SMAD4-specific clinical trials. Future trials targeting this vulnerability must therefore specifically select patients with confirmed homozygous SMAD4 deletion rather than any SMAD4 alteration. The synthetic lethality framework extends well beyond PDAC and has achieved clinical validation across multiple malignancies, providing important context for the vulnerabilities identified in our cohort. The BRCA1/2–PARP inhibitor interaction remains the paradigmatic example, with multiple PARP inhibitors FDA-approved across ovarian, breast, prostate, and pancreatic cancers [61,62]. Beyond DNA damage response (DDR) based targets, VHL loss in clear cell renal cell carcinoma creates dependency on HIF-2α, exploited by belzutifan – an FDA-approved HIF-2α inhibitor that achieved a 49% objective response rate in VHL-associated renal cell carcinoma (RCC) [63]. Emerging SL interactions include microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H)/mismatch repair deficient (dMMR) tumors exploiting WRN helicase dependency, and ATM loss creating vulnerability to ATR inhibition [64,65], with several additional SL targets in clinical development. The significant underdetection of mutations by liquid biopsy has critical clinical implications. Copy number alterations (SMAD4, MTAP, CDKN2A deletions) are inherently more difficult to detect through cell-free DNA analysis compared to point mutations [66]. PDAC tumors are characterized by dense desmoplastic stroma with limited vascularity, which may restrict the release of tumor DNA into circulation [67]. The circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) fraction in PDAC patients tends to be lower than in other solid tumors, particularly in early or intermediate stages of disease [68]. The complete absence of SMAD4 and ARID1A detection in all liquid biopsy samples is particularly concerning, as it would miss patients eligible for targeted approaches. The 3-fold underdetection of CDKN2A and >2-fold underdetection of KRAS mutations would exclude patients from eligibility for emerging targeted therapies and clinical trials. Notably, the 0% SMAD4 detection rate must be interpreted with caution given the small liquid biopsy cohort (n = 17); differences in tumor burden, disease stage, and individual ctDNA shedding efficiency among these patients may independently contribute to this finding beyond platform limitations, and a prospective adequately powered study is needed before this finding can guide clinical decisions. Patients undergoing molecular testing to guide therapeutic decision-making should receive tissue-based NGS whenever possible. The significantly lower TP53 mutation rate in Native American patients (38% vs 79%, p = 0.023) represents a novel finding with potential precision medicine implications. New Mexico’s unique demographics (11% Native American vs 1.3% nationally) enabled this observation [69]; Native Americans represent less than 1% of most genomic databases. While the small sample size (n = 8) necessitates cautious interpretation, these findings are exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and require validation in larger, multi-institutional cohorts before any biological conclusions can be drawn.

Beyond targeted therapies exploiting synthetic lethality, improvement in PDAC outcomes may come from advances in immunotherapy and early detection. Recent phase I data demonstrated that an individualized mRNA neoantigen vaccine (Autogene cevumeran) combined with atezolizumab and chemotherapy generated durable T cell responses in resected PDAC patients, with vaccine responders showing significantly delayed recurrence compared to non-responders (median RFS not reached versus 13.4 months, p = 0.007), though this approach is limited to early-stage disease [70]. Novel early detection strategies are also emerging, including protease-cleavable peptide probes that, when combined with CA19-9, achieved 85% sensitivity for stage I PDAC with 96% specificity in the PAC-MANN assay [71]. Additionally, large-scale CRISPR screens across 930 cancer cell lines have identified 500 gene dependencies including 42 synthetic lethal targets, expanding the therapeutic landscape beyond the genes examined in our study [72].

Strengths and limitations

This study has notable strengths. First, it examines a unique population with 11% Native American representation compared to less than 1% in most genomic databases [69]. Our identification of preliminary differences in TP53 mutation rates in Native American patients represents a hypothesis-generating finding warranting further investigation. Second, we systematically characterized synthetic lethality targets with rigorous statistical analysis including 95% confidence intervals. Third, our direct tissue versus liquid biopsy comparison provides evidence-based guidance for molecular profiling strategies.

This study has several limitations. Our cohort consists of patients who underwent clinical genomic testing rather than a random population sample, introducing selection bias that may influence mutation prevalence estimates. Additionally, censoring bias may exist if patients with particularly aggressive mutation profiles experienced rapid disease progression before genomic testing, potentially leading to underrepresentation of certain lethal mutation combinations. The retrospective design, single-institution setting, and modest sample sizes further limit statistical power for subgroup analyses. Not all patients underwent genetic testing, and wide confidence intervals reflect substantial uncertainty in estimates. The 40% of eligible patients who did not undergo genomic testing likely reflects the evolving landscape of molecular profiling during the study period (2017–2022); prior to the emergence of multiple biomarker-driven targeted therapies in PDAC, the clinical imperative for routine genomic testing in PDAC was not well established, and testing decisions were largely at physician discretion. This temporal limitation, combined with access barriers inherent to a minority-serving community cancer center, underscores the broader challenge of genomic testing equity and supports our central finding that comprehensive tissue-based NGS should be pursued in all PDAC patients. Despite these limitations, this work provides valuable insights into SL targets in a diverse PDAC population.

Conclusions

Multiple SL targets beyond BRCA exist in PDAC, with CDKN2A, SMAD4, MTAP and ARID1A most prevalent. Liquid biopsy is inadequate for comprehensive SL profiling due to significant underdetection of critical alterations, particularly copy number changes. Preliminary molecular differences were observed in Native American patients, highlighting critical gaps in cancer genomics research for underrepresented populations and the urgent need for larger inclusive studies. As SL-targeted therapies advance beyond BRCA/PARP inhibition, tissue-based molecular profiling will be essential for identifying eligible patients. This work may encourage pharmaceutical researchers to prioritize development of inhibitors targeting CDKN2A-TYMS, SMAD4-ME3, MTAP-PRMT5, and ARID1A-ARID1B synthetic lethal partnerships given their high prevalence in PDAC.
