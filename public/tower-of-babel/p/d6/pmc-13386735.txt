Sociodemographic Characteristics Associated with PCSK9 Inhibitor Use in Individuals with Possible Familial Hypercholesterolemia in Alberta: A Cohort Study
Authors: Samuel E Fineblit, David M Vickers, David JT Campbell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProprotein convertase subtilisin/kinase type 9 inhibitors (PCSK9is) significantly lower low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c) level and improve cardiovascular outcomes, but they remain underutilized, potentially due to access-related barriers. This study examines clinical and sociodemographic factors associated with PCSK9i prescriptions in Alberta, Canada.MethodsA population-based study was conducted using data from the Alberta Kidney Disease Network. Adults with possible familial hypercholesterolemia (LDL-c ≥ 5 mmol/L) and suboptimal LDL-c control despite high-intensity statin therapy were included. Individuals with ≥ 3 dispensations of PCSK9i formed the treatment cohort. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed based on the identified variables, including neighbourhood income, material and social deprivation, sex, age, urban vs rural residence, and specialist involvement.ResultsAmong 15,286 eligible individuals, only 150 (0.98%) were prescribed PCSK9is. In multivariable analysis, those receiving PCSK9is were less likely to be female (adjusted odds ratio (OR) 0.50, 95% confidence interval (CI) [0.30, 0.81], P = 0.005), had higher baseline LDL-c levels (adjusted OR 1.47, CI [1.23, 1.74], P < 0.001), had higher rates of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (adjusted OR 5.58, CI [1.63, 14.50], P = 0.003), and were more likely to have seen a specialist physician (adjusted OR 6.64, CI [2.69, 21.50], P < 0.001). They were also more likely to reside in a higher-income neighbourhood.ConclusionsPCSK9is are markedly under-prescribed in patients with severe hypercholesterolemia in Alberta. Prescription patterns have distinct differences by sex, specialist access, and neighbourhood income. Policy changes to improve equitable access, especially for disadvantaged populations, may help to reduce cardiovascular risk and improve outcomes.Keywords: alirocumab, evolocumab, PCSK9 inhibitor, cardiovascular disease, cholesterol, prescriptions, socioeconomic deprivation

Monoclonal antibody proprotein convertase subtilisin/kinase type 9 inhibitors (PSCK9is) are indicated for use in individuals with familial hypercholesterolemia or atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) who have not achieved low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c) targets despite maximally tolerated statin therapy. These medications result in significant LDL-c reduction and improved cardiovascular outcomes.1,2 However, they remain significantly underutilized in those who may receive benefit.3

A number of variables may contribute to determining those who are prescribed PCSK9is. These may include patient age, sex, and the care providers involved in their treatment. Higher rates of PCSK9i insurance coverage rejections have been reported previously in propensity-matched women, compared to men, in the US.4 However, other data have shown similar rates of PCSK9i use regardless of sex.3 Physician attachment and specialist involvement may be important factors in determining who is prescribed PCSK9is, given that a prior or special authorization process typically is required to obtain coverage for these high-cost medications.5,6 Given that specialist care is often more readily available in urban settings, rural vs urban residence may also contribute to determining who is prescribed PCSK9is.

The relatively high cost of PCSK9is may be another contributor to the underutilization of these medications. The patchwork of insurance coverage for medications in Canada, and the gaps within this system, may result in financial barriers to care. Individuals without coverage or significant financial means may be unable to benefit from high-cost medications, despite having medical indications for treatment. Individuals with low incomes are less likely to have private drug insurance and are more likely to spend more than 5% of their household income on medication, suggesting that they may be disproportionally impacted by the adverse effects of suboptimal access to medication.7, 8, 9 Previous work has demonstrated that individuals with full coverage for their medications following myocardial infarction have improved cardiovascular outcomes, compared to those who face copayments or other cost sharing, indicating the potential for patient-borne medication expenses to result in disparities.10 However, despite this evidence, significant gaps remain in the literature evaluating the impact of socioeconomic factors on access to prescription medication and associated health outcomes in Canada.11 Although lower income has been found to be associated with a higher rate of PCSK9i insurance coverage rejections in the US, the impact of financial status on access to PCSK9is has not been reported previously in Canada.4

In this study, we sought to investigate factors associated with prescriptions for PCSK9is in Alberta, among those with a probable indication for their use.

Methods

We conducted a population-based study using the Alberta Kidney Disease Network (AKDN) database to identify adults (aged ≥ 18 years) registered with Alberta Health from September 28, 2015 (the date of first PCSK9i availability in Canada) to the end of the available data period (March 31, 2021). The AKDN database allows for data linkage at the individual level among a number of provincial health datasets.12 This database includes data for more than 99% of Alberta residents. Demographic data, administrative health data from discharge abstracts and physician claims, pharmacy dispensation data, and laboratory data from the AKDN repository were used to identify exposures, covariates, and outcomes. This research was completed with research ethics approval from the University of Calgary Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board (REB-16-575). The authors confirm that patient consent is not applicable to this article, as this study was completed using administrative data, and for this reason, the research ethics board did not require individual patient consent. This study was completed without third-party funding.

Individuals aged ≥ 18 years with a documented LDL-c level ≥ 5 mmol/L on at least one occasion within the study period were identified as having possible familial hypercholesterolemia (as these individuals would receive at least 3 points on the Dutch Lipid Clinic Network score).13 They were included if their LDL-c level was ≥ 2.5 mmol/L despite high-intensity statin use, assumed present if atorvastatin ≥ 40 mg or rosuvastatin ≥ 20 mg had been dispensed within 3 months before the LDL-c measurement. This threshold was selected because it represents an indication for escalation of management, based on the 2016 and 2021 Canadian Cardiovascular Society lipid guidelines.14,15 The PCSK9i cohort included those with ≥ 3 dispensation records for evolocumab or alirocumab; others were classified as the non-PCSK9i cohort.

We compared clinical and demographic characteristics between cohorts, including age, sex, urban vs rural residence, physician attachment (defined as ≥ 3 visits with the same family physician), and specialist involvement (defined as any consultation with a general internist, endocrinologist, or cardiologist). Urban residence was defined as an address within the geographic boundaries of Calgary or Edmonton. For the purpose of this analysis, any other address was classified as rural. Socioeconomic status was measured using neighbourhood income quintile at the dissemination-area level, by linkage to census data and the 2016 Pampalon Material and Social Deprivation Indices.16, 17, 18

To stratify the cohort by socioeconomic status, we used a combined version of the Pampalon Index that reflects the relative “deprivation” of small geographic areas (dissemination areas) relative to the local community or wider society.16,17 The Pampalon Index is derived from 6 items collected in the Canadian census—here, the 2016 census—that are associated with the health effects of both material and social deprivation. Material deprivation includes items related to education, income, and employment, and social deprivation is based on items related to marital status, living alone, and single-parent families. Principal component analysis generates 2 factor scores for specified dissemination areas that are then grouped into quintiles.16

To combine the material and social components of the Pampalon Index into a single “deprivation” indicator, we used the second of 2 grouping strategies suggested by Gamache et al.19 to create “quintiles.” This approach has the advantage of creating clear deprivation profiles whilst preserving the quintile groupings of the original index, which are as follows: quintile 1—socially and materially privileged; quintile 2—average material and social deprivation; quintile 3—socially deprived but materially privileged; quintile 4—socially privileged but materially deprived; and quintile 5—socially and materially deprived.

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) was defined by a history of myocardial infarction, stroke and/or transient ischemic attack, or peripheral vascular disease, using validated coding algorithms from the University of Calgary Interdisciplinary Chronic Disease Collaboration.20 Subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate the impact of baseline LDL-c level and the presence of CVD as possible confounding variables, as appropriate.

Pharmacy dispensation data were used to assess lipid-lowering therapy. Statin use was defined as described above. Use of other medications was defined as ≥ 3 dispensations within the observation window. The association between PCSK9i use and LDL-c level achieved was assessed.

We developed a predictive model for a binary outcome variable of PCSK9i use, with age, sex, baseline LDL-c level, CVD status, categorical neighbourhood income, combined material and social deprivation indices (quintiles), rural vs urban residence, and specialist involvement included as independent variables.

To evaluate the model’s performance on unseen data, our entire dataset was partitioned randomly into training and testing subsets via stratified random sampling. Stratified sampling ensured that the partitioned datasets had matching distributions of patients taking PCSK9i medication.

The logistic regression model was fitted to training data, and we estimated the model’s accuracy (the proportion of total correct predictions) and the area under the receiver operating curve (which measures the model’s ability to distinguish between the 2 classes of the outcome in the testing data) to evaluate the model’s performance. We also assessed the model’s fit using McFadden’s R-squared.

Results

Study population

A total of 15,286 individuals met the inclusion criteria for the study, with an average age of 54.7 years. Among these individuals, 52.6% were female, and 47% resided in rural areas. The mean baseline LDL-c level was 5.14 mmol/L. A total of 286 individuals (1.9%) had CVD. A small proportion of the cohort, specifically 150 individuals (0.98%), were prescribed PCSK9is over the course of the study. The remaining 15,136 individuals (99.02%) did not receive at least 3 dispensations of PCSK9is and were categorized as the non-PCSK9i cohort. Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics

Individuals prescribed PCSK9i were younger, with an average age of 51.6 ± 11.9 years, compared to the non-PCSK9i cohort, who had an average age of 54.9 ± 11.2 years (P < 0.001).The PCSK9i cohort had slightly higher mean baseline LDL-c levels (5.64 mmol/L vs 5.14 mmol/L), although this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.11).

The proportion of female patients in the PCSK9i cohort was 31%, compared to 54% in the non-PCKS9i cohort (P < 0.001), despite the fact that mean baseline LDL-c levels were slightly higher in the female subgroup (Supplemental Table S1). Although ASCVD was more prevalent in men, only 11% of all individuals prescribed PCSK9is had a history of ASCVD, suggesting that this too may not explain the observed difference. Specialist involvement and/or consultation was more frequent in the PCSK9i group (97%) than in the non-PCSK9i group (76%; P < 0.001).

In the unadjusted analysis, socioeconomic factors, including neighbourhood material deprivation and average neighbourhood income, were associated with prescription for PCSK9is (Fig. 1). Those in the PCSK9i cohort had lower material deprivation scores (−0.010 ± 0.039) compared to those in the non-PCSK9i cohort (0.002 ± 0.038; P < 0.001). The difference in social deprivation index was not statistically significantly different between the 2 groups (P = 0.081). Higher mean neighbourhood income was observed in the PCSK9i cohort (69,447 ± 34,521 CAD$) compared to the non-PCSK9i cohort ($59,405 ± $26,030; P < 0.001). Rates of rural residence were similar between the groups.

After multivariable analysis, an association was found between PCSK9i use and higher index LDL-c levels (adjusted odds ratio (OR) 1.47, 95% confidence interval (CI) [1.23, 1.74], P < 0.001), as well as higher rates of ASCVD (adjusted OR 5.58, CI [1.63, 14.50], P = 0.003). Also present was a negative association with female sex (adjusted OR 0.50, CI [0.30, 0.81], P = 0.005). Specialist involvement was also associated with PCSK9i use (adjusted OR 6.64, CI [2.69, 21.50], P < 0.001).

An association was present between receiving PCSK9is and higher neighbourhood incomes, although this relationship did not appear linear in the multivariable analysis. Compared to individuals residing in neighbourhoods with mean household income < $85,000, those with neighbourhood income of $85,000-$157,000 had a higher rate of PSCK9i use (adjusted OR 2.10, CI [1.03, 4.11], P = 0.034). A similar association was noted comparing individuals with neighbourhood income of $158,000-$230,000 (adjusted OR 7.77, CI [1.20, 28.73], P = 0.008) to those in the lowest neighbourhood income quartile. A trend toward a higher level of PCSK9i use was also observed comparing those in the highest neighbourhood income quartile (> $230,000) to those in the lowest, but this difference was not statistically significant in the context of a high degree of variability driven by low sample size in the highest income quartile (adjusted OR 3.11, CI [0.17, 15.94], P = 0.278).

Of the 8 independent variables included in the logistic regression model, a patient’s index LDL-c level, their cardiovascular disease status, and having specialist involvement (in that order) were the 3 largest influences on the model’s ability to predict PCSK9i use (Supplemental Fig. S1). A patient’s age and their combined material and social deprivation quintile had the least influence over the model’s predictive ability.

Lipid-lowering therapy and LDL-c reduction

The effectiveness of PCSK9is in reducing LDL-c level was evident in the data. For those who received PCSK9is, the mean LDL-c level achieved was lower, at 2.55 mmol/L (± 1.70), compared to 3.41 mmol/L (± 1.28) in the non-PCSK9i cohort (P < 0.001). This reduction in LDL-c level is consistent with the known efficacy of PCSK9is in managing hypercholesterolemia.1,2

Discussion

This study demonstrates the overall underutilization of PCSK9is in severely hypercholesterolemic individuals with a probable indication for the use of these medications. Fewer than 1% of the eligible population in this study appeared to be undergoing long-term treatment with PCSK9is, despite having LDL-c levels that exceeded recommended targets. The findings highlight several key determinants associated with PCSK9i prescription, including baseline LDL-c level, sex, specialist involvement, cardiovascular disease history, and income.

The association of higher rates of PCSK9i use with higher baseline LDL-c level, as well as history of ASCVD, in the multivariable model was not surprising, given the higher risk of future CVD events associated with these clinical parameters. Involvement of specialist physicians in a related discipline also was strongly associated with PCSK9i use. This finding aligns with previous research suggesting that specialists are more likely to prescribe high-cost medications such as PCSK9is, due to their having greater familiarity with these treatments and the process to secure insurance coverage for them.6 However, the reliance on specialist care may inadvertently create additional disparities in access.5 Therefore, efforts aimed at improving access to specialized care or enhancing the familiarity of primary care providers with PCSK9is could help reduce these disparities.

Sex also appeared to correlate with prescription dispensation patterns. Women were less likely to receive PCSK9is, with only 31% of those receiving PCSK9is being female, compared to 54% in the general cohort. Although the exact reasons for this discrepancy remain unclear, it did not appear to be fully explained by baseline LDL-c levels, the relative prevalence of CVD observed, or other variables included in the logistic regression model. Previous data have shown that lower rates of PCSK9i access in women can be associated with worse cardiovascular outcomes.9 Future studies should investigate the potential reasons behind this gender disparity and explore strategies to ensure equitable access to lipid-lowering therapies for men and women.

Higher mean neighbourhood income also was associated with an increased likelihood of PCSK9i use. This association is consistent with prior research highlighting the barriers faced by people experiencing economic disadvantage in accessing high-cost medications.4,7 Further research, including individual-level data on income and insurance coverage, is needed to better understand the nonlinearity of this finding. An interesting finding is that although a similar association was noted with material deprivation on the unadjusted analysis, this did not persist after adjustment for other variables. Nonetheless, the neighbourhood income findings support the notion that financial barriers may contribute to underutilization of these effective therapies. This finding is concerning, given that Canadians with low income have a higher prevalence of CVD, as it suggests that individuals with greater need for lipid-lowering therapies may face increased difficulty in accessing them.21

Although previous data have demonstrated reduced access to other aspects of healthcare for rural populations, this was not seen in the current study.22 A possible reason for this is that lipid management is a predominantly lab-based discipline, and therefore conducive to virtual care. The presence of a single, integrated health system in Alberta during the study period, with standardized province-wide referral and management pathways, also may have contributed to the similar rates of PCSK9i use seen in rural and urban residents. However, further research is needed to better understand this finding.

In the unadjusted analysis, age was inversely associated with PCSK9i prescription, with younger age associated with a higher rate of PCSK9i use. However, this finding did not persist after adjustment for other variables, suggesting that the difference may have been driven by other confounding factors.

The clinical outcomes of PCSK9i use are well documented, and our study further supports their role in reducing LDL-c levels. Individuals receiving PCSK9is, on average, had lower LDL-c levels at the last documented measurement in the study period. These reductions in LDL-c level are expected to translate into improved long-term cardiovascular outcomes.

The rate of CVD seen in this study was quite low, at 1.9%, despite a high-risk population, which may be explained by several factors. CVD may have been underestimated if no billing encounters occurred with the relevant diagnostic codes during the study interval, even if individuals had a more remote history of CVD. Additionally, all individuals included in this study were treated with high-intensity statin therapy, which would be expected to reduce the rate of CVD. Many individuals with a history of CVD would have been treated to LDL-c levels < 2.5 mmol/L, with statin therapy ± ezetimibe, and thus also would have been excluded from this study, thereby reducing the rate of CVD observed.

The strengths of this study include the use of population-level data, allowing us to examine group characteristics that are coincident with PCSK9i use broadly, not just at specialized centres. The individual-level linkages in these data facilitated observations of different types of variables, including demographic data, neighbourhood-level indicators of income and deprivation, clinical parameters, laboratory data, and prescription dispensation.

This study also has several limitations. First, this study used administrative health data. As a result, we relied on pharmacy dispensation data to assess whether or not patients were taking lipid-lowering medications, which may lead to some misclassification in those who had a lipid profile completed shortly after starting a new therapy, but before its full effect could be observed; those who were dispensed a medication but never took it; those who stopped a lipid-lowering therapy shortly after initiation; and those who received a therapy from a nonreporting pharmacy or as part of a clinical trial. Similarly, we were reliant on neighbourhood-level indicators of income and material deprivation as proxy measures of socioeconomic status. Information about insurance coverage, as well as patient and provider preferences and contraindications, was also unavailable, given the limitations of the administrative dataset used. Some of these factors may have contributed to the unexplained variation suggested by the small McFadden’s R-squared value noted in the logistic regression analysis.

Despite using a large population-level dataset, only a small number of individuals prescribed PSCK9is were captured. This small number is likely related to the strict entry criteria requiring both severe hypercholesterolemia and lipid levels that were above target despite therapy with a high-intensity statin, with or without ezetimibe. The number may also relate to the fact that the available data only extend to 2021. We suspect that prescription rates for PCSK9is have increased since that time, given the context of increased familiarity with these medications, major publications and guideline updates, expanded coverage, and reduced cost during the study interval.1, 2, 3,14,15 An updated analysis may be helpful to confirm the findings in this study when more recent data become available.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that PCSK9is are under-prescribed therapies for individuals with severe hypercholesterolemia in Alberta. Both clinical and sociodemographic factors were found to be associated with rates of dispensed prescriptions. This study demonstrates that access to specialist physicians strongly correlates with PCSK9i use, and strategies to improve specialist access, or familiarity with this treatment among primary care providers, may increase utilization in patients likely to benefit. Although systemic undertreatment of cardiovascular risk factors has frequently been described in women, further research is needed better understand the best ways to address this disparity as it pertains to PCSK9i use. The correlation of neighbourhood income with PCSK9i use also highlights the need for policies that address income-associated disparities in care. Given the substantial reduction in LDL-c observed with combination lipid-lowering therapies, including PCSK9is, efforts to improve access to these medications could have a significant impact on reducing cardiovascular risk and improving outcomes.
