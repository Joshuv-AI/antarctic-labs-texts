Viral hepatitis B co-infection among people living with HIV: a National cross-sectional survey in Togo in 2023
Authors: Roméo Mèdéssè Togan, Frédéric Iman Youa, Rogatien Comlan Atoun, Malanna Bigma, Rodion Yao Konu, Arnold Junior Sadio, Martin Kouame Tchankoni, Alassane OURO Medeli, Oumarou I Wone Adama, Palokinam Pitche, Gilles Wandeler, Didier Koumavi Ekouevi, Claver Anoumou Dagnra
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ContextHIV/HBV co-infection accelerates the progression of chronic hepatitis towards end-stage liver disease. In Togo, little is known about the prevalence of hepatitis B in people living with HIV (PLWH). The objective of this study was to estimate the determinants of hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection among PLWH in Togo in 2023.MethodsA cross-sectional study was carried out between May and October 2023 among PLWH receiving antiretroviral treatment in HIV care centers in the six health regions of Togo. Serological tests were used for the detection of HBV surface antigen (HBsAg), anti-HBs antibody, and total anti-HBc antibody. The HBV DNA viral load was measured in HBsAg positive PLWH. Logistic regression analyses were performed to assess associated factors with HIV/HBV co-infection.ResultsA total of 897 PLWH (74.9% women) were included. The median age was 45 years (IQR: 38-53). The prevalence of viral hepatitis B was 6.5%, 95% CI [5.0 - 8.3]. This prevalence was higher in northern Togo (12.0%) than in southern Togo (5.2%) (OR: 2,7 IC 95% 1,44 – 4,98). Among 58 HBsAg+ PLWH, 93% had an undetectable HBV viral load (<10IU/ml) on Tenofovir/Lamivudine/Dolutegravir. Among HBsAg-negative PLWH (n=100), 2% were vaccinated against HBV, 61% had been exposed to HBV and 37% were not protected against HBV. ConclusionHBV prevalence is high among PLWH, especially in northern Togo, and the HBV viral load is suppressed in almost all patients on ART treatment. One third of our study population was susceptible to HBV infection, and in urgent need of HBV vaccination.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11822-5.

Introduction

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection is a major public health concern in sub-Saharan Africa as it is the main cause of liver cirrhosis and cancer in the region [1–4]. In 2022, the World Health Organization (WHO) estimated that 254 million people were living with chronic hepatitis B. and that 1.2 million new infections and 1.1 million deaths occurred each year, mainly from cirrhosis or hepatocellular carcinoma [5]. That same year, only 13% of individuals living with HBV were aware of their status, and just 3% were receiving treatment [5].

In people living with HIV (PLWH), co-infection with the hepatitis B virus leads to a rapid progression of liver disease [6, 7]. Worldwide, the prevalence of HBV infection among (PLWH) was 7.4% in 2022 [5]. This prevalence among PLWH ranged from 5% to 28% in sub-Saharan Africa [8, 9].

In Togo, a West African country with a generalized HIV epidemic, the prevalence of HIV infection was 1.7% among adults [10]. In key populations, the HBsAg prevalence in Togo was 7.1% among men who have sex with men (2017) and 9.9% among female sex workers (2020) [9, 11]. Other studies in Togo have reported an HBV prevalence of 10.6% in women of childbearing age [12] and 10.9% in the general population [13]. The epidemiology of HIV/HBV co-infection is poorly described in Togo. The only available study conducted in 2011 in Lomé, among PLWH on ART reported an HBV prevalence of 9.7% [14]. This study was geographically limited and is now outdated, highlighting the need for nationally representative data.

Since 2010, WHO recommends HBV screening before starting ART, and the use of regimens consisting of nucleoside analogues (Lamivudine) and nucleotides (Tenofovir) for HIV/HBV co-infection [15]. First-line treatment for PLWH in Togo currently consists of Tenofovir, Lamivudine and Dolutegravir [16]. This treatment contains two drugs active against HBV. The aim of treatment is to control the HBV replication and prevent clinical complications such as cirrhosis, hepatic decompensation, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and death [17, 18]. However, only a few small studies have evaluated treatment outcomes among persons with HIV/HBV in West Africa and none has been conducted on Togo yet [6, 19, 20].

The fight against hepatitis B also involves vaccination, which is effective in preventing infection after exposure to HBV. There are few data on HBV vaccine coverage among PLWH [21].

The aim of this study was to estimate the prevalence of HBV co-infection among PLWH in Togo in 2023, to describe their HBV vaccination status, and to identify factors associated with HBV infection.

Methods

Study design and population

A cross-sectional study was carried out from May to August, 2023 in centers providing care for HIV, viral hepatitis and sexually transmitted infections in Togo. The sites were selected to ensure national representativeness, based on the size of their active PLWH cohorts and the availability of laboratory capacity. The study population consisted of persons with HIV on antiretroviral treatment (ART), aged 18 and over, followed at HIV treatment facilities, and having signed a written informed consent.

PLWH in life-threatening condition (medical emergency) and those younger than 18 years were excluded. Self-reported HBV-infected patients were not excluded. To ensure comparability, all participants underwent standardized HBV testing in this study, and only these laboratory results were used for prevalence estimation.

From May to August 2023, all patients who visited the care centers for follow-up visits related to their HIV status and who met the inclusion criteria were offered participation in the study, until the target sample size was reached.

The sample size was calculated on the basis of previous estimates by the WHO in its Viral Hepatitis Scorecard African Region report in 2021 and a study carried out in 2021, which had reported estimated prevalences of viral hepatitis B among PLWH of 13.0% and 9.7% respectively [14, 22]. The sample size was calculated assuming an expected prevalence of 10%, a 95% confidence level, a 2% margin of error, and a 10% adjustment for potential non-response or unusable data, leading to a minimum target of 864 participants. Recruitment by health region was proportional to the active file of PLWH patients [23].

Data collection

The questionnaire used in this study was developed on the basis of a literature review and previous studies on the subject. It was then adapted to the specific characteristics of our target population and validated by a committee of experts made up of specialists in these fields. Before being used in the study, the questionnaire was pre-tested to ensure its clarity, relevance and reliability. Any necessary adjustments were made following this pre-test to ensure the quality of the data collected (supplementary file 1).

The questionnaire was administered face-to-face by the medical doctor or nurse at the HIV treatment facility to all participants. It was supplemented by data extraction from patients’ medical records. The variables collected were: (i) sociodemographic characteristics: age, sex, marital status; (ii) HIV-related data: date of diagnosis, date of ART initiation, HIV-1 viral load if available in the file; (iii) hepatitis B-related data: history of viral hepatitis, Self-reported history of HBV infection, notion of hepatitis B vaccination and HBV risk behaviors.

Laboratory tests

A sample of 4 to 5 ml of whole blood was taken by venipuncture at the elbow for biological tests, after centrifugation and aliquoting into 1.5 ml micro-tubes. Rapid tests for HIV, syphilis and HBV were performed at the study sites. HBV viral load and HBV serological tests were performed at the National Institute of Hygiene, Togo’s national biological reference laboratory. HIV status was confirmed for all participants using the diagnostic algorithm according to national recommendations for HIV testing: the first test used was the SD BIOLINE HIV/Syphilis Duo (Abbott, Santa Clara, CA, USA) with HIV performance (sensitivity 99.9% and specificity 99.7%). The First Response HIV 1–2-0 Card Test (Premier Medical Corporation Pvt. Ltd, Maharashtra, India) (sensitivity 100% and specificity 100%) was used in case of positivity of the first test [24]. A third test, INNO-LIA HIV I/II Score (20T) (Fujirebio, Goteborg, Sweden) (HIV performance: 100% sensitivity and 96.2% specificity) was used in case of serodiscordance [25]. Rapid SD Bioline tests for HBsAg (Abbott) were carried out on site, in the laboratories of the study centers (HBV performance: sensitivity 100% and specificity 99%) [26]. For all patients diagnosed HBsAg-positive, viral load for the detection of HBV DNA were performed using the « Real-Time-PCR Test » COBAS® 4800 HBV test. HBV DNA results were classified as suppressed viral load (< 10 IU/mL), low viral load (10 to 2,000 IU/mL) or high viral load (> 2,000 IU/mL).

Among PLWH with a negative HBsAg test, one hundred were randomly selected for the determination of serological markers. Elisa tests for HBc and anti-HBs were carried out according to standard laboratory protocols.

Data processing and statistical analyses

Data were collected on an electronic tool developed on KoboCollect and exported in Excel format for statistical analysis using R software version 3.3.2. Continuous variables were presented as medians with their interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables as proportions. Prevalence estimates were reported with their 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Medians were compared using the Mann Whitney test, and categorical variables using Chi2 or Fisher’s exact test. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to study the association between HBsAg-positivity and explanatory variables, including socio-demographic and clinical characteristics as well as risk behaviors). For multivariate analysis, we included all variables for which the p-value was < 0.20 in univariate analysis. The top-down stepwise procedure was performed to obtain a final model. Analysis was performed at the 5% significance level.

Sociodemographic characteristics and history of HBV infection

The median age was 45 years (IIQ = [38–53]), 440 (49.1%) participants were from the Grand Lomé region, and 672 (74.9%) were female. The proportion of unschooled participants was higher among women than men (29.0% vs. 9.3%). The majority of participants lived in collective housing (53.4%). The median age at first sexual intercourse was 19 years, IIQ = [16–20], and 5.3% of participants had at least 2 partners, whereas 2.8% reported having sex with people of the same sex. Of all participants, 25/897 (2.8%) reported having been vaccinated against hepatitis B. This proportion was significantly lower among women (1.9%) than among men (5.3%). Of those vaccinated, 11/25 (47.8%) received at least three doses of vaccine. In addition, 24 (2.7%) of the participants self-reported a history of hepatitis B viral infection (Table 2).

Prevalence of viral hepatitis B infection

Of the 897 participants, 58 were HBsAg-positive, representing a prevalence of 6.5% (95% CI= [5.0 - 8.3]). The prevalence was higher in northern Togo 20/166, 12,0% (95% CI = [7.1, 17.0]), than in southern Togo 38/731, 5,2% (95% CI = [3.6, 6.8], p=0.002) (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Proportion of people living with HIV co–infected with HBV across sub-groups

Proportion of people living with HIV co–infected with HBV across sub-groups

Among the 24 participants who self-reported prior HBV infection, 18 had confirmation. When these 18 confirmed cases were excluded, the recalculated HBsAg prevalence was 4.6% (95% CI = [3.2–5.9]), compared to 6.5% (95% CI: [5.0–8.3]) when all cases were included. This difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.077).

In multivariate analysis, we found a significant difference in hepatitis B prevalence between the northern and southern regions of Togo (OR=2.7, 95% CI = [1.44 - 4.98], p=0.002). Of 58 HBsAg-positive participants, 54 (93.1%) had an undetectable HBV viral load, three had a viral load between 10 and 2000 IU/mL and one patient had a viral load of 21,480 IU/mL. Two of four individuals with a detectable HBV viral load also had a detectable HIV viral load despite an average duration of 12 months on dolutegravir/tenofovir/lamivudine-based treatment. Discussion

Summary of main results

The aim of this national survey carried out in Togo in 2023 was to estimate the prevalence of HBV among PLWH and explore associated factors. A total of 897 PLWH were included in HIV care sites in Togo. The overall prevalence of viral hepatitis B among PLWH was estimated at 6.5%. The prevalence was more than twice as high among PLWH in northern Togo compared to southern regions of Togo. HBV viral load was suppressed in 93% of HBsAg + PLWH. Of a sub-sample of 100 participants, one third had no serological sign of past exposure to HBV and only 2% (2%) were protected against HBV through vaccination. Our results highlight the need to strengthen hepatitis B prevention and vaccination strategies among PLWH in Togo.

Prevalence of HBV among people living with HIV

We report an overall HBV prevalence of 6.5% among PLWH in Togo in 2023. A study carried out in Lomé in 2011 reported a prevalence of 10.7%, twice as high as the prevalence we found in this city [14]. The population studied in 2011 probably included patients followed in a tertiary hospital, which could have introduced a selection bias and increased the observed prevalence.

In addition, another hypothesis to be explored by other studies would be that the potential drop in HBV prevalence between 2011 and 2023 in Lomé could be due to increased vaccination coverage, combined with improved prevention and treatment measures.

In our study, the HBV prevalence increased from south to north, with a prevalence above 10% in the northern half of the country, similar to a study carried out among MSM in 2011 [11]. The difference in HBsAg prevalence between regions of Togo could be explained by several factors, such as higher socio-economic status in the southern regions, which are generally more urbanized and have better access to health services. Other explanations could include differences in cultural practices such as scarification, and vaccination coverage [29, 30].

Our results are similar to those found globally in West Africa, where the prevalence of HBV among PLWH varies between 4% and 15% [8, 31, 32]. The observed prevalence remains high and calls for compliance with national and WHO recommendations on the systematic testing for HBV among PLWH. This is particularly true given that studies carried out in Côte d’Ivoire and Senegal reported that two-thirds of PLWH were not screened, showing that HBV screening of PLWH is poorly implemented in the African context [6, 33].

HBV viral suppression

HBV viral suppression was 93.1% in HIV/HBV coinfected participants, almost all (90%) of them receiving a regimen containing two HBV-active antiviral molecules (tenofovir and lamivudine). Similar results were reported in a study in Senegal, where 87.9% of patients had a suppressed HBV viral load. In contrary to our study, a significant proportion of the Senegalese individuals with HBV replication had not yet switched to a tenofovir-containing regimen. Overall, our results confirm good HBV management among PLWH: the majority of our participants with replicating HBV infection reported low treatment adherence. These results underline the importance of therapeutic education and clinical monitoring to effectively manage HBV replication in HIV-infected patients. Further research with larger sample sizes is needed to fully understand the factors influencing HBV viral load suppression in this population. These results also confirm the efficacy of dual therapy in countries with a high prevalence of HBV. The efficacy of tenofovir-containing regimens has also been demonstrated in several studies, notably in Malawi [34] and France [35]. A meta-analysis of 11 studies, including nine randomized clinical trials, showed that the virological response to HBV was superior with a combination of Tenofovir + Lamivudine compared with Lamivudine monotherapy (Relative Risk 1.42 95% CI 1.14–1.67). This confirms that the first line regimen of Tenofovir/Lamivudine/Dolutegravir is suitable for the African context [36]. However, more data are needed on the long-term clinical and biological monitoring, including for the assessment of renal toxicity of tenofovir [37, 38].

HBV vaccination

In a sample of 100 HBsAg-negative PLWH, 37.0% had no serological evidence of any exposure to HBV, and remain susceptible to contract the infection in the absence of preventive measures. Patassi et al. in 2011 reported a proportion of 27.8% in a population of 1,212 PLWH who were negative for all HBV viral markers. Similar results were reported in Côte d’Ivoire in 2012 and Chile in 2020, with 27.0% and 37.6% respectively of naïve PLWH likely to be infected with HBV [39, 40]. In Togo, HBV vaccination was integrated into the Expanded Program on Immunization (EPI) in 2008 for children under five years of age, with a schedule of three doses at 6, 10, and 14 weeks, but without a birth dose. All participants in our study were born before this introduction and therefore did not benefit from routine infant vaccination. Furthermore, catch-up vaccination for adults is available in pharmacies and health centers, requires direct payment, and is conditional on prior documentation of HBV seronegativity, a test that is not routinely performed among people living with HIV during their routine follow-up. These economic and structural barriers could explain the very low vaccination rate of 2% observed in this study. Our results highlight the need for routine vaccination strategies targeting HBsAg-negative people living with HIV to prevent HIV/HBV coinfection and its long-term complications.

Strengths

This study is the first nationally representative survey to cover all six health regions of Togo. A comprehensive assessment of the different stages of HBV management, including vaccination and treatment among PLWH. This study made it possible to compare the prevalence of HBV among PLWH in different regions and to estimate the suppression of HBV viral load among HBsAg-positive participants undergoing antiretroviral treatment. It also shed light on the status of HBV vaccination among PLWH in Togo.

Limitations

Our study has some limitations. First, we included participants who self-reported having been previously diagnosed with HBV. However, excluding the 18 cases with documented previous infection did not significantly alter the prevalence estimate (4.6% versus 6.5%, p = 0.077). This sensitivity analysis confirms the robustness of our results and highlights the importance of identifying both newly identified and previously known cases in order to reflect the true burden of HIV/HBV co-infection in Togo. Secondly, due to the high cost of viral hepatitis markers, serological exposure tests were only performed on a randomly selected subsample of patients. This may have reduced the statistical power needed to detect differences between regions. Furthermore, we did not collect information on cultural practices such as scarification, which could help explain the regional variations observed in HBV prevalence [41–43]. Finally, we did not assess HBV resistance mutations. Although resistance mutations have been reported elsewhere in Senegal, where 2 out of 58 participants carried such mutations.

Conclusion

HBV prevalence remains substantial among people living with HIV in Togo, with significantly higher rates observed in the northern regions. The majority of co-infected individuals achieved HBV viral suppression under tenofovir-based regimens. Nevertheless, more than one-third of PLWH remain susceptible to HBV infection. These findings highlight the urgent need to implement routine HBV screening among PLWH and to establish vaccination programs for eligible individuals within the framework of national HIV/AIDS control initiatives. Finally, further research is warranted to investigate the influence of specific sociocultural factors on HBV epidemiology in Togo and across West Africa.
