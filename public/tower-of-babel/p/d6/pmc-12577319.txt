Shoulder tip pain during and after caesarean section under spinal anaesthesia: Incidence, Severity, and associated factors in a tertiary hospital in Ethiopia
Authors: Ananya Abate Shiferaw, Fitsum Tamene Mamo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundShoulder tip pain (STP) is an underrecognized complication of cesarean section under spinal anesthesia, potentially impairing maternal comfort and early neonatal care. Despite the increasing cesarean delivery rate in Ethiopia, data on the incidence and contributing factors of STP are lacking. This study aimed to assess the incidence, severity, and associated factors of STP among women undergoing elective cesarean section under spinal anesthesia.MethodsA prospective cohort study was conducted at Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia between June 2023 and May 2024. A total of 165 women undergoing elective cesarean section under spinal anesthesia were enrolled. Data on demographics, perioperative variables, and STP characteristics were collected. Logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with STP incidence and severity. Significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsSTP occurred in 49.6% of participants, with 14.5% experiencing intraoperative and 38.8% postoperative pain. The right side was the most commonly affected (70.4%).Among those affected, 20.7% reported mild pain, 48.7% moderate pain, and 30.4% severe pain. Blood loss > 500 mL was significantly associated with STP incidence (AOR = 4.43, p = 0.001). Severity was influenced by prior cesarean history, intraoperative positioning, block height, and intra operative bleeding > 500 ml. No significant associations were found with age, BMI, gravidity, or duration of surgery.ConclusionThe incidence of shoulder tip pain during and after caesarean section is high and clinically significant in Ethiopia. Background

Caesarean section is one of the most common surgical procedures performed globally, accounting for over 21% of all deliveries, with rising trends observed worldwide [1]. In Ethiopia, caesarean delivery rates are increasing, especially in tertiary hospitals, often performed under spinal anaesthesia due to its favorable safety profile and ease of administration [2, 3]. Spinal anaesthesia is associated with benefits such as minimal neonatal exposure to anaesthetic agents and better maternal participation during childbirth [4–6]. However, it is not without complications [7].

Shoulder tip pain (STP) is a frequently overlooked complication that can occur during or after caesarean section under spinal anaesthesia [8]. Though commonly associated with laparoscopic surgery, recent studies suggest that shoulder tip pain is also prevalent following caesarean delivery, with reported incidence rates ranging from 26.6% to 49.5% [8–13]. This pain, often described as sharp and referred, is thought to result from phrenic nerve irritation due to factors such as subdiaphragmatic blood collection, air entrapment or peritoneal irritation [8–10]. It typically presents on the right side, may radiate to the neck or upper arm, and can be severe enough to limit movement and disrupt maternal-neonatal bonding and care [8, 9]. Despite its clinical significance, no published data exists on the incidence, severity or contributing factors of shoulder tip pain after caesarean delivery in Ethiopia or the broader African context. Understanding the magnitude of this problem is essential to inform better pain management protocols, enhance maternal outcomes and stimulate further research.

Ethiopia, the second most populous country in Africa, has a population exceeding 130 million. According to the 2019 Ethiopia Mini Demographic and Health Survey (EMDHS), the national cesarean section (C/S) rate is approximately 5%, with significantly higher rates in urban areas, reaching up to 24.1% in its capital - Addis Ababa. The absolute number of cesarean sections has been rising rapidly, in line with the expansion of maternal health services, increasing urbanization, and evolving obstetric care practices. Despite this trend, there is limited understanding of associated complications such as shoulder tip pain (STP), which, though not life-threatening, can impair maternal satisfaction, delay recovery, and hinder breastfeeding initiation [10].

As maternal well-being and patient-centered care gain attention in Ethiopia, perioperative pain assessment and management remain underdeveloped in routine practice. Given the rising cesarean delivery rates and the lack of routine evaluation of STP, this study is both timely and relevant, aimed at addressing a critical gap in maternal care. Specifically, it sought to determine the incidence and severity of intraoperative and postoperative STP in mothers undergoing elective caesarean section under spinal anaesthesia at a tertiary hospital in Ethiopia and to identify clinical and perioperative factors associated with its occurrence.

Methods

This study was approved by the Ethics Review Board of department of Anaesthesiology, Addis Ababa University, College of Health Sciences. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrolment.

Study design and setting

A prospective cohort study was conducted at Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital (TASH), Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, from 1 June 2023 to 1 May 2024. TASH is the largest tertiary referral and teaching hospital in the country, with a capacity of over 800 inpatient beds. It provides specialized surgical, medical, and obstetric care and serves as a major academic center affiliated with Addis Ababa University.

Study population

The study population included women aged 18 years and older who underwent elective cesarean section under spinal anesthesia during the study period. Participants were excluded if they had a history of shoulder or neck disorders, chronic pain conditions, chronic comorbidities, communication impairments, or had undergone major abdominal surgery other than cesarean section. Additionally, participants were excluded from the final analysis if they declined consent after enrollment, experienced failed spinal anesthesia, required conversion to general anesthesia, or if the procedure was converted to an emergency cesarean section.

Data collection

Eligible participants were enrolled sequentially. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire (Supplementary Material 1) developed by the research team based on a review of relevant literature and clinical experience in obstetric anesthesia. Prior to the main study, the questionnaire was pretested on five patients to assess clarity, comprehensiveness, and feasibility. Revisions were made accordingly. Data collection was performed by trained anesthesia residents and included patient demographics, preoperative information, and intraoperative variables such as surgical position, duration of surgery, estimated blood loss, sensory block level, and neonatal weight. Characteristics of shoulder tip pain, including its location and severity, were also recorded.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the incidence of shoulder tip pain (STP) either during surgery or within 24 h postoperatively. Secondary outcomes included the location of STP (categorized as left-sided, right-sided, or bilateral), severity of STP (measured using a Numeric Rating Scale [NRS] from 0 to 10 and categorized as mild (NRS 1-3), moderate (NRS 4-6), and severe (NRS 7-10)), and factors associated with its occurrence and severity. Intraoperative STP was assessed before patients were transferred to the post-anesthesia care unit, while postoperative pain was assessed within 24 h of surgery.

Sample size and power analysis

The required sample size was determined using a single population proportion formula, assuming a shoulder tip pain (STP) incidence of 26.6% [10], with a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error. After adjusting for the finite population of elective cesarean deliveries at the study center, the final sample size was calculated to be 165. A post-hoc power analysis was performed to evaluate whether this sample size was sufficient for multivariate logistic regression analysis. Using four predictors from the final model, an observed STP incidence of 49.6%, and a minimum clinically significant odds ratio of 2.0, the analysis demonstrated a power of 83%, indicating that the sample size was adequate to detect meaningful associations.

Data analysis

All data were entered and analyzed using SPSS version 27. Descriptive statistics were presented using frequencies and percentages. Bivariate logistic regression was used to identify potential risk factors for STP. Variables with a p-value < 0.25 were included in the multivariate logistic regression model. Statistical significance was considered at p < 0.05 with a 95% confidence interval. Model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. Ordinal logistic regression analysis was employed to identify factors associated with the severity of shoulder tip pain.

Incidence of shoulder tip pain

Shoulder tip pain was reported by 82 women (49.6%). Intraoperative shoulder tip pain was noted in 24 women (14.5%), and postoperative pain in 64 women (38.8%). Six women experienced both (3.6%) (Fig. 2). Right-sided shoulder pain was dominant (70.4%), followed by left-sided (23.8%) and bilateral (5.8%) (Fig. 3).

The anatomical distribution of shoulder tip pain among participants who experienced STP (N = 82)

All patients (24/165) who developed intraoperative shoulder tip pain (STP) received rescue analgesics during surgery. STP occurred in 54.8%, 37.9%, 42.9%, and 53.3% of women with sensory block levels at T10, T8, T6, and T4, respectively.

Factors affecting incidence of shoulder tip pain

Bivariate and multivariate binary logistic regression identified that intraoperative blood loss of more than 500 ml was significantly associated with shoulder tip pain (adjusted odds ratio 4.427; 95%CI 2.162–9.064; p = 0.001). Other factors, including age, body mass index, gravidity, previous caesarean sections, gestational age, neonatal weight, intraoperative position, sensory block level, and duration of surgery, were not significantly associated (See Table 3).

Although the association was not statistically significant, we observed a trend of increased STP incidence with longer surgery duration. Among patients whose surgery lasted less than 45 min (n = 19), only 26% (5/19) developed postoperative STP and none experienced intraoperative STP. On the other hand, in patients with surgery duration between 45 and 60 min (n = 128), postoperative STP occurred in 40% (51/128) and intraoperative STP in 15% (20/128). For those whose surgery exceeded 60 min (n = 18), 44% (8/18) developed postoperative STP and 22% (4/18) developed intraoperative STP.

Severity of shoulder tip pain

Among study participants who developed shoulder tip pain, 20.7% (n = 17/82) experienced mild pain, 48.7% (n = 40/82) had moderate pain, and 30.4% (n = 25/82) reported severe pain. Intraoperative pain was frequently severe, with 50% of affected participants reporting a pain score > 7. In contrast, postoperative pain was most commonly moderate, with 56.3% reporting a score between 4 and 6. (See Table 4)

Ordinal logistic regression was conducted to assess factors associated with the severity of shoulder tip pain. The model showed a good overall fit, with a significant model fitting chi-square (χ² = 287.588, p < 0.01), good goodness-of-fit (Pearson p = 1.000), high explanatory power (Nagelkerke pseudo R² = 0.906), and the assumption of proportional odds was satisfied (test of parallel lines p = 1.000). Several variables were significantly associated with pain severity. (See Table 5) Intraoperative bleeding of less than 500 mL was associated with a lower likelihood of experiencing severe shoulder tip pain (OR = 0.22, 95% CI [-2.87, -0.15], p = 0.03). The number of previous cesarean deliveries was also associated with pain severity. Compared to women with three previous cesarean sections, those with one prior cesarean had significantly higher odds of reporting severe pain (OR = 51.37, 95% CI [0.84, 7.04], p = 0.013), and those with two prior cesarean sections also had increased odds (OR = 24.19, 95% CI [0.02, 6.36], p = 0.049). This trend was not observed in women with no previous cesarean deliveries. Patients managed in reverse Trendelenburg (100 head-up) position had significantly lower odds of reporting severe shoulder tip pain compared to those positioned supine (OR = 0.07, 95% CI [-4.96, -0.35], p = 0.024). Regarding sensory block level, women who achieved a sensory level at T10 were less likely to report severe shoulder tip pain compared to those with T4 sensory level (OR = 0.03, 95% CI [-5.88, -0.98], p = 0.006). Other variables, including gestational age, neonatal weight, duration of surgery, and whether shoulder tip pain occurred intraoperatively or postoperatively, did not show statistically significant association with the severity of pain.

Discussion

This study identified a high incidence of shoulder tip pain among women undergoing elective caesarean section under spinal anaesthesia, with nearly half of participants affected either during or after surgery. Postoperative shoulder tip pain was more common than intraoperative pain, and the majority of pain was moderate in severity. These findings suggest that shoulder tip pain is a prevalent and potentially under-recognised complication in this population.

Our incidence findings are consistent with previously reported ranges of 26.6% to 49.5% in studies from Turkey and Iran [8–10]. The predominance of right-sided pain aligns with earlier reports, and may be explained by anatomical and physiological factors, including the distribution of phrenic nerve fibres and gravitational pooling of blood or air in the right subdiaphragmatic space [12–15].

The significant association between shoulder tip pain and intraoperative blood loss >500 ml supports hypotheses that peritoneal irritation, likely caused by subdiaphragmatic blood collection, contributes to phrenic nerve-mediated referred pain. Although previous studies have speculated on this mechanism [8, 14], our study provides supportive statistical evidence for this link. Other examined variables, including maternal demographics, gestational age, prior caesarean deliveries, and intraoperative positioning, were not significantly associated with incidence of shoulder tip pain.

This study revealed that the recommended sensory block level of T4 for caesarean section was achieved in only 15% of participants, indicating a potential gap in the quality of anaesthesia care provided. In theory, inadequate sensory block may result in peritoneal irritation, which is considered a possible cause of shoulder tip pain (STP). However, in our analysis, we did not find a statistically significant association between sensory block level and the occurrence of STP. The routine use of intrathecal opioids in our setting may have mitigated the effects of peritoneal irritation, thereby blunting the expected relationship. Interestingly, ordinal logistic regression analysis demonstrated that women who achieved only a T10 sensory block were significantly less likely to report severe STP compared to those who achieved a T4 block (OR = 0.03, 95% CI [-5.88, -0.98], p = 0.006). This counterintuitive finding suggests that factors other than block height may play a more prominent role in influencing STP severity. Further studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to better elucidate this relationship.

In our study, several clinical variables were significantly associated with the severity of shoulder tip pain following cesarean section under spinal anesthesia. Notably, intraoperative blood loss of less than 500 mL was linked to reduced odds of experiencing severe STP. This finding supports the hypothesis that larger volumes of intra-abdominal blood may irritate the peritoneum and diaphragm, triggering referred pain via phrenic nerve pathways [8, 10, 14]. Our results also revealed that women with one or two previous cesarean deliveries were more likely to report severe pain compared to those with three prior cesarean sections, while no such association was observed among women with no prior cesareans. This non-linear relationship could reflect on factors other than intra-abdominal adhesions, surgical difficulty, or neural sensitization from repeated procedures and warrant further exploration.

In addition, patients positioned in a head-up (reverse Trendelenburg) position intraoperatively were significantly less likely to report severe pain, consistent with prior evidence that this positioning may help reduce diaphragmatic irritation by minimizing cephalad blood pooling [8, 10, 14]. Similarly, the observed association between a lower sensory block level (T10) and reduced pain severity may be explained by the fact that higher blocks can potentially lead to intercostal muscle paralysis and impaired chest wall mechanics [16, 17], thereby increasing susceptibility to blood or air accumulation beneath the diaphragm.

These findings collectively highlight the multifactorial nature of STP and underscore the need for tailored perioperative strategies to mitigate this common postoperative discomfort. It also emphasise the need for increased awareness among anaesthetists and obstetric teams of shoulder tip pain as a significant postoperative burden. Active intraoperative management of bleeding and appropriate postoperative analgesia may help reduce its impact. Additionally, structured documentation and routine enquiry about this symptom during recovery assessments could improve recognition and care.

This study is, to our knowledge, the first in Ethiopia and sub-Saharan Africa to explore this topic using a prospective design. However, it is limited by its single-centre setting, modest sample size, and non-randomised observational design, which restrict generalisability and causal inference. Incomplete achievement of the recommended T4 sensory block and routine use of intrathecal opioids may have confounded the analysis of spinal block height and shoulder tip pain. Additionally, we were unable to assess factors such as surgical technique and had no follow-up to evaluate the persistence of pain. Randomised controlled trials comparing different surgical and anaesthetic techniques, as well as investigations into preventive strategies such as patient positioning, peritoneal suctioning, and pre-emptive analgesia, would add further clarity.

In conclusion, shoulder tip pain is a common but underappreciated complication during and after caesarean section under spinal anaesthesia. Greater awareness and targeted interventions may reduce its incidence and improve maternal recovery and satisfaction.
