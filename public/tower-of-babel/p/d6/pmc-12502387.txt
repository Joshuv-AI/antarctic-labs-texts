Study of socioeconomic differences of long-term PM2.5 exposure and population mortality risk - based on 345 cities from 2000 to 2019 in China
Authors: Surong Zhao, Xingyu Zhang, Ronghang Liu, Junyan Liu, Xiaoqian Shao, Wanru Zhao, Jiu Wang, Yang Zhao, Chunlei Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrevious studies have thoroughly evaluated the association between long-term exposure to PM2.5 and mortality, but the effect of socioeconomic status on the association remains controversial.MethodsWe utilized the data of all-cause mortality per year, concentration of PM2.5, socioeconomic and demographic characteristics for 345 cities in China from 2000-2019. We applied the Poisson generalized estimating equations (Poisson GEE) model to explore the association between PM2.5 and mortality in cities with different quartiles of demographic and socioeconomic characteristics.ResultsOverall, every 10 µg/m ³ increase in PM2.5, the RR was 1.082 (95% CI:1.058-1.106) for all-cause mortality, with residents of the Northeast and Southwest regions having a higher risk of death. Cities with the highest quartiles of gender ratio, age, dependency rate, and migration rate had relative risks of 1.110 (95% CI: 1.020-1.208), 1.075 (95% CI: 1.018-1.135), 1.101 (95% CI: 1.068-1.135), and 1.108 (95% CI: 1.053-1.165), respectively. Stratified analyses showed that the exposure-mortality associations were strongest in cities with both the lowest and highest socioeconomic status (SES) quartiles.ConclusionThe association between PM2.5 and mortality varied by socioeconomic status, demonstrating a U-shaped pattern with the highest mortality risk observed among residents in cities with both the lowest and highest socioeconomic levels.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24521-2.

Introduction

Ambient air pollution is a major global environmental and public health challenge due to its adverse effects on the health of populations. Among air pollutants, fine particulate matter (PM2.5, particles with an aerodynamic diameter of less than or equal to 2.5 μm) is a key risk factor [1–3], linked to millions of premature deaths annually [4–6].Increasing studies have indicated PM2.5 is associated to various health effects, including cardiovascular [7], respiratory [8], and circulatory diseases [3], as well as preterm births, low birth weight, and stillbirths [9]. Recent research have shown that even very low levels of PM2.5 exposure—well below national standards—can still have detrimental effects on public health [7, 10].

China’s rapid economic growth, urbanization, and industrialization have led to severe air pollution, with PM2.5 being a major concern [11]. According to China’s 2023 environmental bulletin, only 59.9% of 339 monitored cities met air quality standards, while 31.0% exceeded PM2.5 limits [12]. The key characteristics of PM2.5 pollution in Chinese cities are its high average concentrations and the high frequency of pollution episodes [13]. Annual PM2.5 concentrations (29–30 µg/m³ in 2021–2023) far exceeded WHO guidelines (< 5 µg/m³)(WHO,2021).

China’s rapid economic development has made it the world’s largest developing country, yet this growth has been accompanied by increasing income inequality and socioeconomic disparities [14]. Studies show these socioeconomic status (SES) differences significantly influence both PM2.5 exposure and mortality rates [15]. While SES is known to modify PM2.5-mortality relationships, the direction of this effect remains debated. Several Chinese and American studies report higher mortality risks among low-SES groups, attributing this to disparities in healthcare access and environmental protections [16–19]. Conversely, certain studies from China and South Korea suggest high-SES populations face greater exposure due to urban energy consumption patterns [14, 20, 21]. Additionally, mixed PM2.5-mortality relationships have been documented in both European and Chinese studies [22, 23].However, most existing studies have employed datasets with relatively limited temporal coverage [2]and exposure ranges [24], which may compromise their accuracy in accounting for spatiotemporal variations.

This study aims to examine mortality risks from long-term PM2.5 exposure and their socioeconomic variations across 345 cities 2000–2019 in China from 2000 t0 2019. The findings will help to identify vulnerable populations and inform targeted strategies to improve air quality and reduce mortality risks in China.

Methods

Data source

We employed a two-stage machine learning model to estimate annual ground-level PM2.5 at 0.1◦× 0.1◦ resolution, integrating CMAQ simulations, satellite data, emission inventories, and ground-based measurements. In Stage 1, we used a Random Forest model to predict a high-pollution indicator, defined by PM2.5 values exceeding two standard deviations above the monthly mean. SMOTE was also used to balance the training set. In Stage 2, another Random Forest model was trained to predict the residuals between CMAQ outputs and PM2.5 observations. Final PM2.5 estimates were obtained by correcting CMAQ simulations with these residuals.PM2.5 estimates validated to a high degree of accuracy (R2 = 0.83) [25, 26].We obtained the population density from the world gridded population at a spatial resolution of 0.1◦× 0.1◦ [26, 27].PM2.5 concentrations for the 345 cities are population-weighted concentrations, calculated by averaging PM2.5 concentrations across all grids within the city’s boundaries and weighting them according to the population density of all grids. We averaged the 2000–2019 daily PM2.5 data into annual average concentrations [28].

We obtained indicators of demographic characteristics such as gender ratio, average age, migrant rates, dependency ratio, and the population size of each city for 345 cities from China’s population census data. In this study, we use urbanization rate, Per capita GDP (PGDP), and rate of educational attainment to represent socioeconomic indicators. These data come from the Statistical Yearbook and the Statistical Bulletin of National Economic and Social Development of each city in China from 2000 to 2019, in addition we collect the annual all-cause mortality rate of each city, and the number of deaths in each city is obtained by multiplying the all-cause mortality rate of each city by the number of populations.

Statistical analyses

To account for potential overdispersion in data where the variance often exceeds the mean - a violation of the equidispersion assumption inherent to standard Poisson regression - we utilized Poisson generalized estimating equations (Poisson GEE) with robust sandwich standard errors. This analytical approach is specifically designed to handle repeated measures and within-cluster correlation [29]. Importantly, the GEE approach does not rely on the equidispersion assumption, and its robust variance estimator provides valid inference even in the presence of overdispersion. By employing this method, we ensured appropriate accounting for both the temporal correlations in our data and potential overdispersion effects that could otherwise bias the estimated associations between PM2.5 exposure and mortality outcomes. Based on China’s geographic location and current economic and social development, and with reference to the existing literature, we divided China into eight regions: eastern, central, western, southern, northern, northeastern, northwestern, and southwestern [30](Figure A. 1). We explored the annual average PM2.5 concentrations in each region and the strength of the association between long-term PM2.5 exposure and mortality. Meanwhile, we divided the 345 cities into four subgroups according to quartiles of demographic characteristic variables and socioeconomic characteristic variables based on the mean values from 2000 to 2019, and applied Poisson GEE modeling for each subgroup to derive the effect estimates (RR and 95% confidence intervals) for each subgroup. We then used meta-regression to explore whether the RR values were statistically different between subgroups. In this meta-regression model, RR values were used as the dependent variable and the corresponding subgroups as the independent variables. The meta-regression results will give a p-value, after which the process will be repeated for each indicator.

We also performed a sensitivity analysis to test whether the results were stable. First, we adjusted the model for ambient temperature and relative humidity. Second, we included PGDP in the model. To investigate potential lagged effects, we incorporated PM2.5 into the model using moving averages from the current year as well as from 1 to 2 years prior (lagged by 0–1 years and 0–2 years). This approach allowed us to compare the differences in effect estimates between the sensitivity models and the primary model.

All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4, with all tests being two-sided and a significance threshold set at 0.05.

Discussion

This study found that long-term exposure to PM2.5 significantly increases mortality risk, with populations in Northeast and Southwest regions of China being particularly vulnerable. Additionally, socioeconomic factors such as education level and urbanization modify the strength of this association. Our findings show a positive relationship between long-term exposure to PM2.5 and mortality risk. Specifically, for each 10 µg/m³increase in the annual average PM2.5 concentration, the overall mortality risk increased by 8.20%.

Our study identified a positive link between prolonged exposure to PM2.5 and mortality, aligning with previous studies carried out in China, further validating the global health risks posed by air pollution. While our study showed a 8.20% increase in all-cause mortality for every 10 µg/m³ increase in PM2.5. In comparison, a cohort study using data from the CHINA-PAR program reported that a 10 µg/m³ increment in PM2.5 exposure increased the risk of mortality by 11.1% [31]. Similarly, a 22-year cohort study conducted in China reported that 10 µg/m3 increment of annual average PM2.5 exposure was found to increase the likelihood of all-cause mortality by 20% in this population [32]. The risk estimates from these two studies are higher than those in our study, likely because their analyses focused on specific cities or specific subgroup, whereas our study covered a more comprehensive range of regions and populations in China. International studies have also shown that PM2.5 exposure was connected to mortality, with 12% in the United States [33],18% in Canada [34]per each 10 µg/m3increase in annual PM2.5 concentration.

Our study found that residents of Northeast and Southwest suffered more serious mortality risk related to PM2.5 compared with other regions. Existing studies have demonstrated that demographic changes and stagnant socioeconomic development in Northeast China [35] have led to severe population crisis challenges in the region, where the gravity centers of population aging have moved northeastward, resulting in an increasing proportion of elderly population in the total population [36]. Previous studies have shown that the increase in older population can amplify the health effects to air pollution [37]. Population aging significantly impacts the ambient particulate matter pollution-attributed mortality [38], and might caused by the progressive declines of physical function, self-care abilities, and general physical health with age [39].Compared with other regions, Southwest suffered lower average annual PM2.5 concentrations, while the mortality risk remains higher than other regions. One possible explanation is that the Southwest is a main area in China’s 21st-century “Western Development“ [40]and an important base for the non-ferrous metal industry and strategic reserves [41]. Regions with under the lower average PM2.5 concentrations, have higher health risks occurred for the population owing to toxic substances attached to the particles [42]. Furthermore, several international studies have shown that exposure to metals such as iron, copper, and zinc can have acute health effects and increase mortality in populations [43, 44].

In terms of stratified analysis of population characteristics, we found that cities with higher male-to-female ratios showed increased mortality risks associated with PM2.5 exposure, which is consistent with a European cohort study [45]. Additionally, a US cohort study reported a steady positive correlation between ambient PM2.5 and cardiovascular-related mortality among males [46]. The higher susceptibility in males may be explained by greater intensity of work-related outdoor PM2.5 exposure [47], as well as the fact that females tend to have healthier lifestyle habits than males [38], among other factors.For migrant population stratification analysis, we found that mortality were higher in areas with higher proportions of migrant population.PM2.5 exposure had a relation to increased health risks in migrant population [48]. One possible explanation for this is that the migrant population in China may refrain from visiting health facilities due to exposure to ambient air pollutants [49]. In the age-stratified analysis, populations residing in cities with higher proportions of elderly residents and young children exhibited increased health risks associated with air pollution exposure. An Italian time-series study similarly found that air pollution poses a greater health risk to the elderly and children [50].In the dependency-stratified analysis, we observed that people living in cities with the lowest and highest quartile showed heightened sensitivity to mortality related to PM2.5 exposure.

In terms of stratification by socioeconomic factors, our study found a stronger association between PM2.5 and mortality in low-SES and high-SES cities.On the one hand, a global study based on multi-year data from 163 countries showed a positive association between SES and health status [51]. A study in China also found that, people with low-SES may experience more severe negative health effects than people with high-SES as a result of air pollution [52]. One possible explanation is that low-SES people are at a higher risk of being exposed to specific occupational exposure and have a limited awareness of how to protect themselves from air pollution., which increases their risk of mortality [53].Additionally, population with low-SES are less likely to have access to better public transportation systems, stricter environmental regulations, easier access to environmental information, better health care and other better public services [23]. Moreover, They have a higher probability of being exposed to higher concentrations of air pollutants due to they live in poorer housing conditions [54].On the other hand, the association between PM2.5 exposure and population mortality risk remained strong in cities with high socioeconomic status, consistent with previous results. A study conducted in China suggested that the concentration of PM2.5 and mortality associated with PM2.5 in high-SES are much higher than those in low-SES [55].A plausible explanation may involve two key mechanisms. Firstly, the economic growth driving socioeconomic advancement often leads to increased energy consumption and corresponding rises in PM2.5 emissions, thereby exacerbating pollution exposure [11].Secondly, these cities typically experience population growth due to migration, which intensifies population exposure to air pollution and increases the health burden of air pollutants on residents [23, 56].Multiple studies have shown that even in high-SES urban areas, population growth may lead to elevated PM2.5-related mortality rates [55, 57].While higher socioeconomic status does provide populations with more resources to mitigate health risks, air pollution and population growth may offset the positive effects of improved living standards and healthcare [58]. The results of our study carry significant public health implications for China. Firstly, although the Chinese government has formulated and promulgated a series of policies to reduce ambient air pollutants emissions in recent years [39], PM2.5 remains strongly associated with increased mortality risk. Therefore, the government should establish stricter air quality standard [2] and help cities with fast-developing industrialization in switching to cleaner energy sources, such as liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) and electricity, in order to reduce emissions of ambient air pollutants [59].Secondly, China’s socioeconomic development is positively associated with social investment on environmental protection and health care [23, 60].However, due to rapid development, these low-SES areas are at risk of increased PM2.5 pollution levels in the future [39].Consequently, the government should formulate specific policies and laws to reduce the adverse health effect of air pollution in different types of cities in China.For example, improving the urban layout of low-SES areas (locating pollutant-emitting factories away from residential areas) [61, 62]and increasing in the rate of green planting in low-SES areas [63, 64]can reduce the risk of mortality, and enable people of low-SES to experience the same level of high-quality living conditions and healthcare as people of high-SES [65].Finally, the Chinese Government should pay more attention to the protection of susceptible populations to ambient air pollution, such as males, the elderly, and migrant population. In addition, the health awareness of residents, a healthful dietary pattern, and an appropriate physical activity, reduced mortality risk connected with long-term exposure to air pollutants [53, 66].

This study has several advantages. Firstly, to the best of our knowledge, existing studies of socioeconomic differences in mortality risk due to long-term PM2.5 exposure in China cover less time or fewer cities than our study. The study utilizes nationwide data from 345 cities in China from 2000 to 2019, with a long-term and large scale data, which enhances the statistical robustness of the findings. Secondly, it not only estimated the impact of long-term exposure to PM2.5 on mortality, but also evaluated the influence of socioeconomic status as a modifier of this relationship, thus preventing the underestimation of the effect of PM2.5 by ignoring socioeconomic factors [67]. Finally, this study found that populations of high and low socioeconomic status were more sensitive to the adverse effects of PM2.5, and recommended that the governments concerned should take targeted preventive measures in these areas.

This study also has several limitations. Firstly, because of its cross-sectional design, establishing a causal relationship between long-term exposure to PM2.5 and mortality is not feasible. Secondly, the data used in this study were at the city-level, preventing analysis of individual-level confounders (e.g., smoking, physical activity, etc.) that might have influenced the findings. Additionally, while our study included 345 cities, certain vulnerable subgroups (e.g., very young and elderly populations) could not be examined in finer detail due to data constraints. Despite these limitations, the study provides nationally representative estimates using advanced exposure modeling. Future studies with larger stratified samples or individual-level data could enhance the precision of these comparative analyses. Finally, due to data limitations, the potential association between other air pollutants and population mortality was not taken into account.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates a U-shaped relationship between PM2.5 exposure and mortality risk across different SES groups, with the highest risks observed among populations at both the low and high SES. Moreover, regions characterized by high gender ratios, disproportionate population structures, large proportions of migrant residents, and geographical locations such as Southwest and Northeast China exhibited greater susceptibility to PM2.5-related mortality. These findings highlight the importance of incorporating socioeconomic and demographic heterogeneity into air pollution control strategies. We recommend that the Chinese government develop targeted, region-specific policies to mitigate the health impacts of ambient PM2.5 particularly for vulnerable populations at both extremes of the socioeconomic gradient.
