Effectiveness of a nurse-led training program in improving the knowledge and skills in basic life support among newly hired nurses at the University Medical Center
Authors: Aida Baizulda, Akbota Amangeldiyeva, Gulsharat Metchenova, Ayaulym Maratkyzy, Assyltas Yeleussiz, Askhat Tasbulatov, Joseph Almazan, Jonas Cruz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBasic Life Support (BLS) is a critical competency for nurses, however its training and evaluation often lack consistency. This issue is particularly evident in Central Asia, where limited evidence exists on scalable and sustainable educational models within medical science education to ensure frontline readiness.ObjectiveThis study evaluated the effectiveness of a nurse-led, simulation-based BLS training program on the knowledge and skills of newly hired nurses at a tertiary academic medical center in Kazakhstan.MethodsA one-group pre-test and post-test design assessed the impact of a structured BLS training program at the University Medical Center in Astana, Kazakhstan. Fifty-two newly hired nurses were enrolled, of whom forty-nine completed all immediate post-intervention assessments. The intervention integrated theoretical instruction, simulation-based practical training, and skills assessments based on European Resuscitation Council guidelines. BLS knowledge was assessed via a 20-item multiple-choice test, and skills were evaluated using a validated checklist. Differences in outcomes across three time points, namely, pre-intervention, immediately post-intervention, and 1–2 months post-intervention, were analyzed using paired t-tests and repeated-measures analysis of variance.ResultsKnowledge scores significantly improved immediately after the intervention (M = 18.10, standard deviation [SD] = 2.52) compared to the pre-test (M = 8.71, SD = 3.01; p < 0.001). However, a significant decline was noted at 1 month (M = 11.53, SD = 2.52; p < 0.001). By contrast, practical skills showed continuous improvement, with mean scores increasing from 3.47 (SD = 1.27) pre-intervention to 13.10 (SD = 0.92) at 2 months (p < 0.001).ConclusionThis is one of the first studies in Central Asia to evaluate a nurse-led, simulation-based BLS program with follow-up. The findings offer practical implications for medical and nursing education by demonstrating that such models can improve early medical preparedness.

Introduction

Basic Life Support (BLS) refers to a series of time-sensitive, life-saving interventions designed to support breathing and circulation in individuals experiencing medical emergencies such as cardiac or respiratory arrest. These actions involve the timely identification of cardiac arrest, competent chest compressions, rescue breaths, and employ an automated external defibrillator (AED) when necessary.1 For most healthcare workers, especially in emergency or intensive care environments, mastery of BLS is imperative, not merely due to compliance but also because it fundamentally impacts the patient's wellbeing.2 BLS, when correctly executed, considerably improves survival and decreases neurological injury after cardiac arrest. Globally, sudden cardiac arrest (SCA) is a leading contributor to cardiovascular mortality, which poses a significant public health burden.3,4

According to the World Health Organization,4 approximately 17.9 million deaths occur annually due to cardiovascular diseases, many of which are related to acute cardiac events. Despite significant advancements in emergency response systems, survival rates for out-of-hospital cardiac arrests remain distressingly low, often falling below 10 %, primarily due to delayed or inconsistent BLS in real-world settings.5,6 Furthermore, the lack of infrastructure and insufficient funding has significantly undermined BLS training in recent years, as BLS trainings face rigid restrictions across under-resourced regions. These factors cumulatively weaken the training programs. Several studies have found these types of barriers in Palestine,7 Rwanda,8 and Pakistan,9,10 proving the existence of such problems in diverse healthcare systems. Therefore, providing timely and effective BLS training for healthcare providers is critical for improving outcomes in emergency situations.

In Central Asia, particularly in Kazakhstan, systemic challenges persist in emergency preparedness training. Rechel et al.11 observed that public funding remains inadequate, particularly in rural regions, whereas Messova et al.12 highlighted inconsistencies in simulation access, training quality, and gaps in instructor certification. These shortcomings pose significant obstacles to national health policy efforts towards integrating emergency care into standard clinical training.11 Furthermore, high internal migration rates and staffing imbalances between urban and rural areas have strained the delivery of emergency care.12 Alarmingly, workforce projections suggest a significant shortfall in general practitioners by 2030, raising concerns about the healthcare system's capacity to meet growing population healthcare demands.13 Thus, strengthening the skills of current healthcare providers, particularly nurses, is critical for sustaining frontline emergency care delivery. These gaps are further exacerbated by rural-urban workforce imbalances and projected shortages in general practitioners.6,7 Therefore, ensuring their readiness is a high priority. However, despite the proven effectiveness of nurse-led and peer-supported training approaches, few studies have evaluated their long-term impact in Central Asia.12,13 Existing studies, while demonstrating initial gains in knowledge and skills, often neglect retention outcomes beyond immediate post-training assessments.14, 15, 16 As a result, these constraints hinder the development of training programs that are both effective in practice and sustainable over time.

In light of these challenges, simulation-based training has gained recognition as a valuable educational approach. Unlike traditional methods, it provides hands-on experience in realistic clinical scenarios, fostering critical thinking, technical proficiency, and effective team coordination.7,8,17,18 When paired with peer mentorship, it can enhance engagement and educational outcomes. Although simulation-based training has been shown to enhance learning outcomes, few studies have evaluated its implementation in Kazakhstan, especially in the context of nurse-led instruction. Even fewer have assessed retention of BLS knowledge and skills beyond immediate post-training, a vital concern for sustainable clinical competence.

Guided by experiential learning theory19 and the principles of deliberate practice,20 this study addresses these gaps by evaluating the impact of a nurse-led, simulation-based BLS training program on the knowledge and practical competencies of newly hired nurses at a tertiary medical center in Astana, Kazakhstan. It also sheds light on an instructional program developed to comply with the national clinical practice guidelines, yet navigates the specific systemic challenges of the country's healthcare infrastructure. Lastly, this research strives to fill the policy gap on the evidence-informed policymaking pyramid concerning emergency response and healthcare system workforce planning.

Materials and Methods

Design

This study utilized a one-group pre-test and post-test design in examining the effectiveness of the nurse-led training program in improving the newly hired nurses’ knowledge and skills on BLS.

Samples and setting

The study was conducted at the University Medical Center (UMC) in Astana, Kazakhstan, which is the UMC Corporate Fund that was established in accordance with the decision of the Board of Trustees of the Autonomous Educational Organization Nazarbayev University (dated September 20, 2015, No. 20) based on subsidiaries of the National Medical Holding Joint Stock Company. The UMC Corporate Fund, with a total capacity of more than 778 inpatient beds and 500 outpatient visits per shift, has united three leading medical centers: Mother and Child Center, Heart Center, and Diagnostic Center. The UMC offers health and medical care across various specialties including obstetrics and gynecology, pediatrics, pediatric surgery, traumatology, urology, endocrinology, pulmonology, gastroenterology, neurology, oncohematology, cardiology, and cardiac surgery.

This study included nurses who were newly hired at any of the hospitals at the UMC. A sample size calculation using G∗Power version 3.1 was conducted to identify the minimum sample size for the study. Using a hypothesized effect size of 0.25 for a repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA, within factors) with three measurements and a 5 % type I error rate, the required sample size was 43 nurses to achieve a 95 % statistical power. However, to account for a potential attrition, 20 % was added to the sample size, yielding an actual sample size of 52 newly hired nurses. A convenience sampling technique was used to recruit the participants. Those who were hired at the UMC during implementation of the study were recruited to participate. The study included nurses who provide care to adult patients and excluded those who care for pediatric patients.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved by the NUSOM Research Ethics Committee (Re:2024Oct#02) and the UMC Local Ethics Committee. The conduct of the study adhered to the ethical standards of conducting studies among human subjects. Research implementation also strictly followed the policies and procedures of the Ethics Review Committees and the hospital. Administrative approval was sought from the UMC before starting the study. During the recruitment of participants, adequate information about the study was provided by the researchers. The information included the objectives of the study, processes involved, potential risks and benefits, expected participants of the subjects, and the voluntary nature of participation. The participants were also told that they could discontinue participation without any consequences. Researchers gave the subjects the opportunity to ask questions and receive clarification about the study, guaranteeing that all questions would be answered appropriately. The privacy of the respondents was protected by not collecting identifying personal information. However, unique codes were assigned to the participants to allow the pairing of data from the three data collection periods. To ensure the protection of the subject's privacy, the allocation of codes was done randomly and only the subject and the Principal Investigator (PI) had knowledge of the subjects' codes. The PI stored the surveys in a secured cabinet and the data on a password-protected computer. The subjects were asked to provide written informed consent before participating in the study.

Intervention and instrumentation: nurse-led BLS training program

The Nurse-Led BLS Training Program was designed with the primary goal of equipping all healthcare professionals with the necessary BLS-CPR knowledge and skills, including the use of an automated AED for a person in cardiac arrest. The training program was conducted by certified BLS nurse trainers (European Resuscitation Council [ERC] BLS/AED Provider) and constituted a detailed training plan encompassing both theoretical knowledge and clinical skills. This program was specifically intended for nurses who do not have any prior knowledge or a minimal level of knowledge of BLS-CPR. The course included training on how to use an AED when available, specifying the number of participants, the allocation of instructors, and the duration of the course, in line with ERC guidelines (2024).21 The program was divided into two main components: the theoretical component and the practical skills component (see Figure 1).Figure 1Flow of respondents and BLS training procedures.

Flow of respondents and BLS training procedures.

Theoretical Component: The training program covered BLS-CPR guidelines and procedure protocols, including critical steps such as assessing the condition of an unconscious victim, performing chest compressions and CPR, safely using an AED, placing an unconscious victim who is breathing on their own in a safe recovery position, and responding appropriately in situations where the victim is choking. The training also addressed the chain of survival as outlined by the ERC.21

The researchers administered a 20-question multiple-choice assessment to evaluate participants’ understanding and application of BLS protocols according to the latest guidelines. This assessment covered key aspects of BLS-CPR, including initial response actions, airway management, breathing assessment, chest compressions, and the use of AEDs. Correct responses were based on established resuscitation protocols, with higher scores awarded to those who selected the accurate answers, indicating a higher level of BLS-CPR knowledge and skill. Lectures and discussions were delivered in the training, which took approximately 4 h to complete.21

Practical Skills Component: The Practical Skills Component involved hands-on training sessions, demonstrations, and return demonstrations of BLS-CPR skills, rescue breaths, and AED usage on manikins. Utilizing mannequins and feedback, instructors evaluated the participants’ knowledge and understanding of the material. Certified BLS nurse trainers conducted and supervised this training, which took approximately 6 h to complete, including time for demonstration, practice, and assessment.21

The researchers employed a structured competency checklist that focuses on a series of skills critical to effective resuscitation. These competencies included checking the response, opening the airway, assessing breathing, calling emergency services, performing chest compressions, delivering rescue breaths, adhering to the correct compression/ventilation ratio, activating the AED, attaching pads, ensuring the area is clear, delivering a shock, following AED instructions, and placing the patient in the recovery position. The researchers assessed each skill using a binary “yes” or “no” format to determine successful completion. If a retest was required, the researchers once again recorded the outcome as “yes” or “no.”

Reliability of the tool

The BLS knowledge test and the skills checklist were adapted from the ERC,21 which are internationally standardized and widely used in clinical training. To ensure contextual appropriateness, both instruments were reviewed by three ERC-certified BLS instructors at the UMC, confirming alignment with the competencies required for frontline nursing practice. The knowledge test items were mapped onto the core BLS domains (initial response, airway, breathing, circulation, and AED use), supporting content validity. In the current sample of the study, the computed Kuder and Richardson Formula 20(KR20) value for the knowledge tests during the pretest was 0.51, whereas the computed KR20 post-test was 0.78. This indicated that the knowledge test had an acceptable reliability in this study. Inter-rater reliability was computed for the skills checklist using the data from the three independent evaluators. Intra-class correlation coefficients (ICC) were calculated, and the results showed that the ICC value for the skills checklist pre-intervention was 0.96 (95 % confidence interval [CI] = 0.93, 0.98), whereas the ICC values immediately after the intervention, 1 month, and 2 months after the intervention were 0.95 (95 % CI = 0.91, 0.97), 0.60 (95 % CI = 0.35, 0.76), and 0.22 (95 % CI = −0.19, 0.51), respectively. These results support the excellent reliability of the skills checklist in the pre-test and immediately after the intervention, whereas moderate reliability was observed 1 month after the test. The data did not support an acceptable inter-rater reliability of the checklist 2 months after the intervention.

Data collection

The recruitment of participants and data collection were conducted consecutively as newly hired nurses attended their first rotation in the hospital. Part of the training that newly hired nurses should complete is the BLS training. The researchers approached the new nurses during their orientation and asked if they were willing to participate in the study. Prior to enrollment, participants were asked during orientation whether they had previously completed any form of BLS training. This included formal certification courses (e.g., ERC/American Heart Association BLS Provider courses), institutional in-service sessions, or other structured BLS instruction within the past 2 years. Self-reported responses were recorded on the demographic questionnaire, and no verification through certification cards or training records was performed. They were told that if they decided not to participate in the study, they still needed to complete the training program and assessments as part of the routine training for newly hired nurses. Those who agreed to participate were asked to draw a random code. The subjects were instructed to remember the code and use the code in all of the tests and skill performances in the training program. The subjects were also instructed not to share the code with anyone. This allowed the pairing of the data of each subject in the different data collection period. The researchers ensured that the same procedures were followed in the different data collection periods. Data collections were performed from November 2024 to March 2025.

During data collection, the researchers integrated the data with the intervention to effectively evaluate its impact. The following steps were undertaken.

Pre-Test (Baseline Data Collection): At the start of the program, participants underwent a pre-test assessment to establish their baseline knowledge and skills before participating in the Nurse-Led Training Program. This initial test evaluated their existing BLS-CPR knowledge and skills, including the use of AED for a person in cardiac arrest. The test for knowledge as supervised by the researchers ensuring that the students’ scores accurately reflect their current level of knowledge. An observation checklist was used to evaluate the performance of the subjects. Three certified BLS nurse trainers independently evaluated the BLS skills of the subjects.

Training and Ongoing Assessment: Following the initial theoretical instruction, participants engaged in hands-on sessions, demonstrations, and return demonstrations of BLS-CPR skills, rescue breaths, and AED usage on manikins. The participants were instructed to film their performance of resuscitation measures on mannequins in various simulated scenarios after the training. Each clinical department required its employees to record and submit a minimum of three simulation videos per month directly to the instructor. The instructors independently reviewed each video and provided detailed feedback on the participants' skills in the form of a letter. This process enabled the instructor to track each participant's progress or regression using a simulation indicator.

Post-Tests: Immediate Assessment: The day following the completion of the training program, participants retook the same written test and underwent the same practical skills assessment (post-test 1). The knowledge and skills were evaluated again 1 month (post-test 2) and 2 months (post-test 3) after the training, strictly following the procedures in the prior data collection. This allowed for a direct comparison of pre- and post-intervention data, providing insight into the effectiveness of the training.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics, such as mean and standard deviation, were used in analyzing the BLS knowledge and skills of the subject before and after the intervention. For the BLS skills, the researchers computed the average scores from the three evaluators. Repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Greenhouse-Geisser correction was performed to examine the differences in the BLS skills between the three data collection times. The post-hoc pairwise comparisons with “Bonferroni adjustment” was used to compare between different data times. Due to the inability to achieve the required number of samples in the third data collection for the knowledge test to conduct a repeated measures ANOVA, paired samples t-test were performed to examine the knowledge scores between pre-test and post-test 1, and between post-test 1 and post-test 2. Before comparing the knowledge levels before and after the intervention, we examined the differences and relationships in pre-intervention knowledge based on demographic data using Pearson's product moment correlation and the Mann–Whitney U test as appropriate. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Analyses were conducted using SPSS version 24.0 (GraphPad Prism, La Jolla, CA, USA).

Reporting guidelines

This study adhered to the Transparent Reporting of Evaluations with Nonrandomized Designs (TREND) reporting guidelines. The completed TREND checklist was submitted as a supplementary file.

To examine the differences in the nurses’ BLS skills between the different time points of data collection, the pre-test and post-test data were analyzed using repeated measures ANOVA with Greenhouse-Geisser correction. The analysis to compare the BLS skills scores before the intervention, immediately after the intervention, 1-month and 2-month post-intervention was statistically significant (F2.75, 132.02 = 622.02, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.93). The post-hoc with Bonferroni adjustment revealed a significant improvement in the mean scores from pre-intervention to immediately after the intervention (mean difference = −7.92, p < 0.001, 95 % CI = −7.99, −6.59), 1-month (mean difference = −9.19, p < 0.001, 95 % CI = −9.96, −8.42) and 2-month post-intervention (mean difference = −9.63, p < 0.001, 95 % CI = −10.31, −8.94). No significant difference was revealed between the 1- and 2-month post-intervention. These findings indicate that the BLS training was effective in improving the BLS skills of the nurses immediately after the intervention, and the BLS skill level consistently improved until 1-month post-training.

Discussion

This study assessed how a nurse-led BLS training program impacted the knowledge and practical skills of newly hired nurses at an academic medical center. The findings offer several important insights into the effectiveness and sustainability of peer-led training interventions within clinical onboarding.

Specifically, consistent with experiential learning theory19 and the principles of deliberate practice,20 the results showed a significant improvement in nurses' BLS knowledge immediately after the nurse-led training intervention training, followed by progressive enhancement of practical skills over the course of 2 months. This is in agreement with one study from India, which reported that initially, 86.66 % of nurses had low knowledge of BLS, but after training, 58.33 % showed strong knowledge.18 Similarly, one study in Palestine noted that BLS training significantly increased nurses’ knowledge, with weighted mean scores improving from 52.2–75.1 % to 85.6–97.3 %, indicating a strong positive effect of the training.20 Likewise, in two separate experimental studies from Germany17 and Pakistan9 showed better retention of knowledge and skills after the BLS training, highlighting the importance of regular training. Unlike these prior studies, our research is among the first to evaluate a nurse-led, simulation-based BLS program in a Central Asian context, with follow-up data extending beyond immediate post-training. This offers rare insight into the feasibility and short-term retention outcomes of peer-led education models in under-resourced healthcare systems.

This study adds value by assessing retention up to 2 months post-training. While immediate post-intervention knowledge improved significantly, retention declined notably within 1 month, a pattern consistent with literature reporting knowledge decay within 3–6 months.8,9 It is important to note that knowledge retention was assessed only up to 1 month post-training; no additional knowledge assessment was performed at the 2-month mark. Therefore, the apparent upward trend in the 2-month data refers exclusively to practical skills performance and should not be interpreted as a resurgence in knowledge scores. This distinction is critical for accurately interpreting the longitudinal effects of the intervention. By contrast, practical skills continued to improve up to 2 months post-training, suggesting that hands-on simulation paired with guided mentorship may support more sustained performance gains.10,22 This short-term follow-up, although modest in duration, provides rare empirical insight into retention patterns in the context of nurse-led simulation training, a model that has been underexplored, particularly in Kazakhstan.11,12 This supports the feasibility of implementing scalable, peer-led educational models that can be integrated into routine onboarding processes.

Finally, the results revealed that the nurse-led BLS training program enhanced the knowledge and practical skills of newly hired nurses. Participants accomplished a high level of knowledge immediately after the course, demonstrating the effectiveness of a properly designed simulation instructional strategy. However, this gain dropped 1 month later, which aligns with the typical pattern of knowledge loss in the absence of reinforced intuitive practice. This is also in accordance with our earlier observations, which showed a sharp decrease in both theoretical and practical skills a few weeks to months after BLS training.14 Skills start declining at 2 weeks post-training, while knowledge retention dropped within 1–6 months post-BLS instruction.14 There is, however, some evidence that knowledge retention can last for up to 2 months among schoolteachers after undergoing a training session.23 The aforementioned studies support the phenomenon where, in the absence of progressively complex tasks or stimulating enriched learning opportunities, a learner may reach a performance plateau and stagnate the development of competence.22 To resolve this, further training should include complex simulation scenarios with periodic evaluations to maintain interest and exceed the established benchmarks of skill proficiency.

Limitations

This study had several limitations that may have influenced the interpretation of the findings. First, while 49 nurses participated in the initial post-training assessment, only 19 completed the 1-month follow-up knowledge test, and 15 completed the 2-month follow-up skill assessment. This reduced sample size limits the strength and generalizability of the long-term results. This research employed a one-group pre-test and post-test approach, enabling the assessment of knowledge and skills development within a single group over time. This design, while suitable for education intervention studies, lacks a control group, posing risks to internal validity such as testing effects, maturation, or other factors tending to influence outcomes regardless of intervention. Nonetheless, this design was selected due to logistical constraints and ethical responsibility to ensure all newly hired nurses with access to the training. To mitigate these limitations, the study employed multiple follow-up assessments and standardized training protocols to strengthen the reliability and consistency of the observed outcomes. While convenience sampling facilitated efficient recruitment during routine orientation periods, it is important to acknowledge its limitations. This non-random sampling method may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings to broader nursing populations. However, since the intervention was integrated into a mandatory training module and included all eligible newly hired nurses during the study period, the sample is representative of the typical onboarding cohort at health care setting. Another limitation relates to the measurement tools. Although the knowledge test and skills checklist were adapted from ERC's internationally validated guidelines and reviewed by certified BLS trainers, no additional psychometric analyses (e.g., construct validity or item difficulty evaluation) were conducted. Future studies should include more comprehensive validation to enhance the robustness of these instruments, particularly for assessing long-term outcomes. A further limitation of the present study was the relatively short follow-up period of 2 months. This limited duration may not fully capture long-term retention or skill decay. Future research should incorporate longer follow-up intervals (e.g., 6–12 months) to comprehensively assess the sustained impact of such training.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that a nurse-led, simulated BLS training program significantly improved the knowledge and practical skills of newly hired nurses at the academic healthcare facility. The results indicated significant immediate post-training improvements, particularly in theoretical knowledge and hands-on resuscitation skills. However, while skills were largely retained over time, knowledge declined within 1 month, highlighting the need for structured reinforcement strategies. These findings underscore the effectiveness of simulation and peer-led learning in clinical onboarding but also emphasize the importance of follow-up assessments and periodic refresher training to support long-term knowledge retention.

Implication for medical science education and development

This study highlights the need to integrate structured, simulation-based BLS training into the standard onboarding process for newly hired nurses. The immediate improvements in skills and knowledge along with the sustained retention of practical competencies, demonstrate that hands-on training is essential for clinical preparedness. Health care centers should prioritize regular simulation sessions, particularly in high-acuity departments, to help staff maintain essential life-saving skills. to help staff maintain essential life-saving skills. Embedding BLS competency checks within routine clinical evaluations could further enhance emergency response effectiveness, ultimately contributing to improved patient outcomes in cardiopulmonary arrest scenarios.

The decline in BLS knowledge observed 1-month post-training highlights the ongoing need for continued education to support long-term clinical competence. To support ongoing knowledge retention, nurse educators are encouraged to implement follow-up strategies such as quarterly assessments, digital learning platforms, or refresher modules to support knowledge retention. Additionally, involving experienced nurses in peer-led training sessions may increase engagement and foster a supportive learning environment. Such practices help maintain theoretical knowledge and procedural competence, while also promoting a collaborative and team-oriented learning culture.
