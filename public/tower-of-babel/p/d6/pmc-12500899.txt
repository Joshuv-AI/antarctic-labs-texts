Classifying metal passivity from EIS using interpretable machine learning with minimal data
Authors: Sanja Martinez, Izabela Martinez, Tomislav Ratković
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We present a data-efficient machine learning framework for diagnosing degradation of passive metallic surfaces using Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS). Passive metals such as stainless steels and titanium alloys rely on nanoscale oxide layers for corrosion resistance, critical in applications from implants to infrastructure. Ensuring their passivity is essential but remains difficult to assess without expert input. We develop an expert-free pipeline combining input normalization, Principal Component Analysis (PCA), and a k-nearest neighbors (k-NN) classifier trained on representative experimental EIS spectra for a small set of well-separated classes linked to distinct passivation states. The choice of preprocessing is critical: normalization followed by PCA enabled optimal class separation and confident predictions, whereas raw spectra with PCA or full-spectra inputs yielded low clustering scores and classification probabilities. To confirm robustness, we also tested a shallow neural network (NN) classifier on the same PCA-reduced input, which produced comparable but slightly less confident predictions than k-NN. The training dataset was comprised of five classes including abraded (ABR), cold-rolled (INT), mirror-polished (POL), citric (CPAS) and nitric acid (NPAS) passivated states on AISI 304 stainless steel. The external validation set included impedance spectra from distinct samples and literature-reported datasets spanning crevice zones, welds, mechanically abraded regions, as-received materials, and passive surfaces in low corrosivity media. The results supports the viability of interpretable, label-efficient classification of EIS spectra for corrosion diagnostics.

Introduction

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) is a cornerstone technique1,2 for probing electrochemical interfacial phenomena in systems ranging from energy storage devices to biomedical implants and structural metals. However, EIS interpretation traditionally relies on equivalent circuit modelling, a process that requires expert knowledge, iterative fitting, and strong prior assumptions. These limitations hinder reproducibility, slow down analysis, and constrain the use of EIS in real-time or high-throughput applications. To address these challenges, machine learning (ML) has emerged as a compelling solution. According to Web of Science, the number of publications applying ML to EIS data has increased sharply in recent years, driven largely by applications in battery diagnostics3.

Recent efforts have pursued two main automation strategies: enhancing EEC fitting through automated model selection3 or enabling model-free classification directly from spectral data4. The former improves efficiency but still depends on expert-defined model libraries and prior assumptions. The latter eliminates these dependencies by learning directly from spectral data, though it typically requires large experimental datasets4 or relies on synthetic augmentation5 to address data scarcity. Reliance on volume and augmentation is problematic in general6 because synthetic data, measurement noise, and inter-laboratory scatter distort meaningful spectral features7. The effect is likely to be more pronounced in stable EIS systems, such as passive metals, where intra-class variance is inherently low and inter-class differences are subtle. Similar constraints which have been addressed in other scientific domains8–10, support the validity of low-data learning as a principled strategy.

Here, we present an approach that combines principal component analysis (PCA)11 with either a shallow knearest neighbours (k-NN) or neural network (NN) classifier, demonstrating a compact and interpretable framework for classifying EIS spectra from passive materials using only a small number of spectra per class. The combined application of EIS and machine learning to passive metals remains rare in the literature12–17 despite the critical importance of detecting subtle changes in passivity. This gap underscores the need for new methodologies capable of resolving fine variations in impedance associated with passive film degradation, an objective directly addressed in this study. For example, Kurtz et al.17 show that passivity classification represents a highly relevant and promising use case for employing deep learning for surface state recognition on bioimplant alloys15–17. Their binary classification of clearly distinct states (intact vs. heavily corroded) demonstrates feasibility. In contrast, we propose a framework aimed at distinguishing subtle spectral differences in low-variance systems, such as variations in the degree of metal passivation. This makes the classification task significantly more challenging, yet also more relevant to practical scenarios where small spectral shifts indicate meaningful differences.

Furthermore, while PCA has been used in EIS studies for clustering or exploratory analysis, its use in classification also remains rare. Existing examples appear mostly in stimulus-driven systems such as electronic tongues18–20, food diagnostics21,22, or impedimetric sensors23, and typically involve PCA followed by discriminant analysis or support vector machines.

Despite the compact nature of many EIS datasets, reduced order, low-data approaches are nearly absent from EIS diagnostics. We fill that gap, not by exploring numerous classifiers, but by focusing on a purpose-built pipeline designed for sparse, low-variance electrochemical datasets. The described PCA + k-NN or NN workflow aligns with the philosophy of reduced-order modeling (ROM), which has gained momentum in computational physics and engineering for extracting low-dimensional representations from complex systems24–28. As articulated by Bollinger and Schaeffer24 ROMs that incorporate shallow NNs with an integrated model reduction layer offer a promising framework for high-dimensional systems where data are scarce. By projecting high-dimensional inputs onto low-dimensional, physically meaningful subspaces, these models significantly reduce the number of trainable parameters, helping avoid overfitting and improving generalizability. However, Bollinger and Schaeffer caution that separating dimensionality reduction from NN optimization may lead to suboptimal performance if the reduced space fails to preserve task-relevant directions, a limitation mitigated in our case by the clearly clustered class structure observed in the PCA domain. While recent ROM approaches integrate dimensionality reduction and learning into a single jointly optimized network, our method instead focuses on a preprocessing step that enables the use of kNN and NN. An additional novelty is applying PCA not only as a dimensionality reduction tool but also as a means of physical insight, visualizing loadings as a function of frequency to reveal the spectral regions most responsible for class separation. This directly supports the interpretability claims of our PCA-based, low-data approach.

It should be noted that our study is aimed at exploring purpose-built, low-data-compatible pipelines tailored for EIS. To conclude, this work demonstrates how a reduced-order, transparent approach, PCA followed by k-NN or NN, can yield stable, interpretable classifications even in data-scarce settings, offering a realistic path forward for EIS-based diagnostics.

Training dataset and workflow for supervised clustering and k-NN classification

To develop and validate a machine learning workflow for automated assessment of surface passivity, we constructed a structured training dataset comprising 20 spectra in total, four per condition, from AISI 304 stainless steel samples subjected to five well-defined surface treatments29. These treatments were designed to produce systematically varied passivation states, ranging from fully removed protective oxide to passivation-enhanced oxide. The surface states included: abraded (ABR), cold-rolled intact (INT), mirror-polished (POL), citric acid passivated (CPAS) and nitric acid passivated (NPAS).

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were performed using a localized, quasi-solid-state setup comprising a press-on cell and a paste-based electrolyte, with wireless data acquisition. The design of the measurement system, optimized for stability and portability, has been described previously29,30. More details are given in the experimental section. Such field-deployable configurations are increasingly recognized as essential for corrosion diagnostics under realistic conditions, as emphasized in recent comprehensive review31.

Each impedance spectrum was recorded across seven decades of frequency, producing Bode representations of the complex impedance components: log |Z| (log-transformed impedance magnitude) and phase angle versus log f. Each EIS spectrum was represented by 36 values of log|Z| and 36 values of phase angle. The dataset defines the mapping between spectral features and surface states. Each class corresponds to a distinct level of oxide enhancement or weakening, enabling supervised learning with interpretable and experimentally reproducible labels.

For each normalization type, the operations were applied separately to the set of all log |Z| values and to the set of all phase values. For example, in max-normalization every log |Z| value was divided by the maximum log |Z| across the dataset, and every phase value was divided by the maximum phase across the dataset. Figure 1 shows the raw spectra and the three normalized variants, calculated for four stainless steel samples in each of the five subgroups.

These types of normalization ensure comparable scaling between features while preserving inter-sample variability and meaningful amplitude differences between spectra. In contrast, the per-spectrum normalization strategy used in recent benchmarking study32 emphasizes spectral shape alone by removing inter-sample amplitude variation and suppressing any intra-class variance that originates from differences in signal magnitude. While this aids in isolating shape-based features for mechanistic classification, it limits applicability to systems where amplitude carries essential physical information, such as degradation state or oxide growth. Raw impedance spectra for all training and test samples, including log |Z| and phase angle data, are provided in the Data folder of the accompanying data and code repository (see Data availability).

Surfaces with intact passive films (NPAS, CPAS, POL, INT) exhibited high impedance at low frequencies, broad phase plateaus near 80° in the mid- to high-frequency range, and resistive behaviour at high frequencies, features that collectively reflect passive metal response. Typically, their Bode plots show a flat log |Z| profile at high frequencies, dominated by the solution resistance, followed by a slope near − 1 in the log |Z| versus log f representation at mid-to-low frequencies. This is accompanied by phase angles approaching − 90° over a wide low-frequency range, reflecting the nearly ideal capacitive behavior of the barrier oxide layer. These spectral features are widely recognized in the literature as signatures of intact passivity33. However, they also present analytical challenges: the absence of a clear low-frequency resistive plateau makes it difficult to extrapolate corrosion resistance using conventional equivalent electrical circuit (EEC) models, which rely on identifying a DC resistance limit. This underscores the need for alternative interpretation strategies when assessing passive systems via EIS.

Supervised clustering of impedance spectra in a PCA‑reduced feature space

After raw data normalization described in the previous chapter, PCA was applied to reduce the dimensionality of the dataset. Rather than functioning as a generic dimensionality reduction method, PCA revealed a structured and potentially interpretable projection of the impedance spectra, aligning with known electrochemical states and enabling supervised structure discovery. The first five principal components (PC1–PC5) were initially calculated, collectively explaining over 99% of the total variance. PC1, PC2, PC3, PC4, and PC5 accounted for 38.2%, 22.8%, 19.0%, 18.5%, and 1.03% of the variance, respectively. Before clustering, the principal components were scaled to the [− 1, 1] range.

The effect of normalization method and the PCA on supervised clustering performance is seen on silhouette scores, which measure how well each sample fits within its assigned cluster relative to other clusters35. Elimination of PC5 slightly improved the silhouette scores, so PC5 was excluded from further analysis. The remaining components (PC1–PC4) were retained for clustering and classification, as they supported interpretable, classwise separation. Silhouette coefficients for all four normalization methods confirmed robust clustering across classes, with the tightest clusters obtained for the max-normalized input having the highest average silhouette score of 0.96 (Fig. 3a–d). This result is consistent with the logic that max-normalization assigns equal starting weights to log |Z| and phase, after which PCA loadings naturally emphasize phase features (as shown later), and importantly, this type of normalization does not distort the overall spectral shapes. In contrast, raw spectra showed a much smaller average silhouette score, indicating that without normalization the clustering algorithm was more strongly influenced by absolute impedance magnitude rather than underlying shape, resulting in less cohesive clusters.

To assess whether the PCA-reduced features preserved meaningful physical structure, we applied hierarchical clustering using Euclidean distance and Ward’s linkage and compared the resulting dendrograms (Fig. 3e–h) for all four normalization methods between the PCA-reduced and full spectrum representations. In the PCA-reduced space, hierarchical clustering supported class structure, with ABR placed farthest from the passive classes, reflecting its distinct impedance behavior.

In contrast, clustering based on the full spectra without PCA yielded low silhouette scores (0.16–0.29) irrespective of normalization, reflecting reduced cohesion and overlap between classes (see Supplementary Fig.S1 in folder Supplementary figures of the accompanying data and code repository (see Data availability). These results highlight that PCA itself has a strong positive influence on clustering by filtering out noise and redundant dimensions, whereas normalization fine-tunes the separation by balancing feature scales. PCA provides a structured low-dimensional space where clustering boundaries align more closely with physical differences, while normalization mainly affects the relative weighting of features within that space. This evaluation step is critical, as recent studies have shown that seemingly minor preprocessing choices, can significantly impact the downstream performance of machine learning models, particularly when working with full spectrum data32.

The classspecific compactness ratios summarized in Table 1 further support the findings from the visual and silhouette analysis. The lowest (best) average compactness ratio was obtained for the max-normalized input (0.0366), indicating the tightest clustering of samples around their centroids. Among individual classes, ABR showed consistently low ratios across all normalization methods, while CPAS and POL exhibited higher ratios in the raw input, reflecting the weaker cohesion observed in the silhouette scores.

Global clustering metrics are presented in Table 2. Again, the max-normalized input outperformed the others, with the highest average silhouette score (0.96) and Calinski–Harabasz index (1452), and the lowest Davies–Bouldin index (e.g., 0.071), all indicating well defined and well separated clusters. In contrast, the raw input showed a reduced silhouette score and a higher Davies–Bouldin index, consistent with less cohesive clusters and increased overlap. These numerical results confirm that normalization method has a substantial impact on clustering quality, with max-normalization providing the most compact and separable structure in the PCA-reduced feature space.

Principal component interpretation and physical relevance

Principal component loadings (Fig. 5a, d, g and j) quantify how frequency-resolved features, log |Z| and phase angle, contribute to the variance captured by each principal component. These loadings provide insight into the physical mechanisms encoded in the spectra. The sign and shape of each loading curve indicate which spectral regions increase or decrease the component score, allowing interpretation of trends related to passivation and degradation. The accompanying box plots show the distribution of PC scores for each passivation class, derived from raw spectra (Fig. 5b, e, h and k) and max-normalized spectra (Fig. 5c, f, i and l), enabling visual assessment of inter-class separation and within-class variability39. Compared to raw spectra, max-normalized inputs yield box plots with tighter intra-class distributions and clearer inter-class separation across principal components, highlighting the benefits of normalization for enhancing PCA-based class discrimination.

PC1 (Fig. 5a) reflects dominant low-frequency variance, driven primarily by strong negative phase angle loadings below log f = 0, supported by log |Z| features in the same frequency range. This pattern aligns with barrier resistive behavior typical of passive oxide films: low log |Z| and suppressed phase angle values indicate degraded passivity, while high values reflect preserved film integrity, as exemplified in stainless steels by Monrrabal et al.40,41. PC1 clearly separates ABR from passivated states (Fig. 5c).

PC2 (Fig. 5d) emphasizes a mid-frequency phase angle peak near log f ≈ 1.5 (≈ 30 Hz), corresponding to the medium-frequency resistive-to-capacitive transition33 observed on spectra in Fig. 1. The cold-rolled (INT) and polished (POL) surfaces are maximally separated along this axis (Fig. 5f), suggesting differences in capacitive behaviour linked to surface preparation. In INT samples, the phase angle rise begins at higher frequencies, suggesting increased interfacial capacitance likely due to greater real surface area from cold rolling29. In contrast, POL samples exhibit the transition at lower frequencies, consistent with a smoother surface and lower interfacial capacitance. This difference is reflected in their normalized PCA scores, with INT scoring near 1 and POL near − 1 (Fig. 5f).

PC3 (Fig. 5g) captures asymmetric mid-frequency variance, characterized by opposing contributions from phase angle features in the range log f ≈ − 2 to 3. Like PC1, it includes a prominent positive loading near the phase angle maximum at log f ≈ 1.5 (≈ 30 Hz) but also shows a distinct negative contribution at lower frequencies around log f ≈ − 2. This pattern suggests that PC3 blends phase variance captured by PC1 and mid-frequency behaviour of PC2, while integrating additional low-frequency information and a more pronounced influence from log |Z|. This component effectively distinguishes CPAS from NPAS (Fig. 5i), highlighting its sensitivity to subtle shifts in the resistive–capacitive balance.

Although PC4 (Fig. 5j) accounts for a smaller proportion of total variance, it contributes meaningfully to class differentiation by isolating localized features, particularly in the phase domain, that helps separate all surface states (Fig. 5l). This component appears especially sensitive to subtle spectral deviations introduced by different surface treatments, including both mechanical and chemical modifications.

Together, the first four principal components localize class-relevant information along electrochemically meaningful axes. PC1 and PC2 predominantly reflect features influenced by resistive and capacitive behavior, while PC3 and PC4 appear to capture subtler variations potentially linked to oxide composition and interfacial differences. A deeper analysis of PCA loadings and their frequency-localized features would be required to conclusively map each component to specific electrochemical processes. Nonetheless, these trends suggest that PCA does not merely compress the data but highlights physically relevant variance, supporting interpretable, model-free classification. While it is immediately apparent that phase angle features dominate the PCA loadings, classification performance using phase-only spectra was significantly less successful. This suggests that subtle contributions from log |Z|, particularly at low and mid frequencies, also play a critical role and are more effectively leveraged through combined spectral analysis and dimensionality reduction.

To determine the optimal number of neighbours, we evaluated the PCA + kNN model using k = 1 to 8 nearest neighbours for each normalization method. The number of correctly classified samples, misclassified samples, and samples with tied probabilities (hesitant) as well as the average probability of the correct class are shown for each k value in Table 3.

The data highlight how both normalization and the choice of k influence the sharpness of class probabilities. For k = 1, all pipelines (raw as well as max-normalized, z-score, and robust) yield fully correct classifications with unit probability, because each sample is assigned to the class of its single nearest neighbour, resulting in an unambiguous decision regardless of preprocessing. As k increases, the classification confidence gradually degrades across all preprocessing pipelines, though the extent and onset of degradation vary. For raw spectra, correct predictions drop rapidly beyond k = 3, and the number of uncertain predictions increases, with the average probability of certain classifications falling to just 0.467 at k = 8. This indicates high sensitivity to k and a narrow optimal range. Max-normalized and robust normalized pipelines show identical behavior and remain stable across a wider range, maintaining perfect classification and full certainty up to k = 4. A significant drop in average probability occurs at k = 6, followed by a decline in accuracy at k = 8. Z-score normalization shows slightly lower robustness, sustaining unit probability only up to k = 3, though accuracy remains intact throughout the investigated k range.

To determine the most suitable pipeline, we identify the maximum k at which the classifier achieves 100% correct predictions, full certainty, and an average class probability of 1.0. According to this criterion, the optimal settings are: k = 1 for raw spectra, k = 3 for both max-normalized and robust normalized inputs, and k = 4 for z-score normalization. These thresholds represent the upper limits of stable classification performance before uncertainty or misclassification arises.

External validation of k-NN classification on test spectra and degradation tracking

To assess the practical utility and robustness of the classification model, we tested it on external datasets from the literature and from independent experiments on AISI 304 stainless steel and Ti–6Al–4 V alloys. These unseen spectra present variations not represented in the training set, offering a stringent test of the model’s ability to correctly classify mechanistically distinct states of passivity. We first applied both the PCA + k-NN to the set of spectra digitized from two studies by Monrrabal et al.40,41. This dataset, summarized in Table 4, uses the same material as the training set, AISI 304 stainless steel, but includes samples spanning a wide range of passive states. These include welded and creviced regions (common sites of localized corrosion)40samples exposed to NaCl solution and gel-based electrolytes with varying glycerol content modulating corrosivity40and cold-worked specimens containing different levels of martensitic phase, which influences corrosion susceptibility41. Monrrabal’s dataset thus provides a robust benchmark for evaluating model predictions across realistic surface degradation scenarios.

We also evaluated two additional datasets of our own. The first consisted of three spectra obtained from a nitric-acid-passivated AISI 304 stainless steel sample that was successively abraded to varying extents (denoted NPASABR 1 to 3), providing a controlled gradient of mechanical degradation. The second set comprised Ti–6Al–4 V spectra recorded both at open circuit potential (OCP) and after cathodic polarization at progressively more negative potentials (from − 500 mV to − 1300 mV). This series systematically reduces the native passive oxide layer, offering a well-defined model for electrochemical degradation of passivity of titanium alloys15–17.

As a widely accepted proxy for corrosion resistance in passive metals, low-frequency limit of impedance, hereby called the polarization resistance (Rp) is often used to quantify surface degradation. In Monrrabal’s equivalent circuit40,41 model, this corresponds to the fitted R2 parameter. The benchmark studies reported Rp values for each sample, and these are reproduced in Table 2 in decreasing order to serve as a reference for classification.

Figure 6 shows how test spectra distribute in the PCA-reduced space defined by the training data, under four different input types. Test spectra (colored diamonds) are projected into the same PC1–PC4 space used for training, and the background reflects the class decision boundaries derived from 1-nearest neighbor classification of the training set (k = 5). Among the normalization methods, only max-normalization (Fig. 6b) leads to clear separation of all five passivation states, with test spectra tightly clustered within the correct decision regions. In contrast, raw spectra (Fig. 6a) and z-score normalized inputs (Fig. 6c) show overlapping classes or drifted points, while robust normalization (Fig. 6d) partially captures structure but fails to isolate ABR or NPAS. These findings confirm that max-normalization not only improves class separability in the training space but also preserves generalizability across unseen spectra, supporting its use for subsequent classification steps.

Based on the Monrrabal’s benchmark Rp values and classification results obtained using the optimal pipeline (PCA-reduced max-normalized spectra with k = 4), it was possible to calculate the average and standard deviation of Rp values for each of the predicted classes. The ABR class, representing degraded passivity, exhibited the lowest average Rp of 0.82 ± 0.62 MΩ cm2 consistent with substantial barrier damage and active corrosion. The INT class, associated with intact passivity, showed a moderately elevated Rp of 6.66 ± 0.57 MΩ cm2 reflecting preserved but possibly vulnerable oxide layers. In contrast, the NPAS class, interpreted as enhanced passivity, had the highest average Rp of 18.00 ± 4.24 MΩ cm2 suggesting highly resistive surfaces with minimal ongoing corrosion.

The observed distribution of classes aligns well with physical expectations for corrosion resistance based on sample history and exposure.

One spectrum in the benchmark test set, which was not included in determining the Rp range for any class, corresponds to the 44% strain-induced martensitic stainless steel (44% M). It was classified as CPAS/NPAS, i.e., as exhibiting enhanced passivity, despite having a relatively modest Rp value of 2.1 MΩ cm2. This sample was excluded from defining Rp thresholds for the classes due to its ambiguous electrochemical character: it exhibited some features of enhanced passivity but with a relatively low Rp value. Its classification as CPAS/NPAS, reflects this ambiguity and is consistent with the original study41where it was also described as intermediate in corrosion resistance. Notably, it later showed the highest Shannon entropy in the NN output (Table 6), reinforcing its borderline status. This suggests that the classification of 44% M into the enhanced passivity group reflects real electrochemical improvements not fully captured by Rp alone, and that such borderline cases reinforce the classifier’s sensitivity to subtle passivation trends.

The NPAS class, defined by elevated Rp, corresponds to enhanced passivity states developed in low-corrosivity environments, such as 50% glycerol gel, which Monrrabal et al.40 show leads to better-ordered, less defective passive films and increased impedance compared to conventional NaCl-based aqueous electrolytes.

The absence of certain expected classes (e.g., POL or CPAS) in the test set reflects the limited diversity of the external dataset and underscores the importance of expanding the training data in future studies to encompass additional passivation states, including those modified by polarization or partial recovery. None of the validation samples exhibited the specular gloss and ultralow roughness characteristic of true mirror-polished surfaces. The absence of any predictions for POL demonstrates that the classifier does not assign unsupported labels, thereby reinforcing its interpretability. This alignment with inclusion of independent datasets reinforces the use of mechanistically grounded class definitions in interpretable machine learning frameworks for corrosion diagnostics.

Although our primary approach is based on classification, the updated analysis effectively introduces a regression-informed framework. Specifically, we used the benchmark dataset from Monrrabal et al. to define polarization resistance Rp thresholds for each class, and then assigned these values to our own samples according to their predicted class from the best performing max-normalization pipeline (Table 4). This allows estimation of Rp from class membership, effectively mapping spectra to Rp values via a class-based regression proxy (Table 4). This approach combines the robustness of categorical classification with the quantitative interpretability of regression, and could be expanded into a direct regression model in future studies with larger datasets.

Discussion

This study presents a fully experimental, expert-free machine learning pipeline for evaluating the electrochemical passivity of metallic surfaces directly from raw impedance spectra. The novelty of the approach lies in how data scarcity, rather than being a limitation, enables robust dimensionality reduction through principal component analysis (PCA). The compact yet highly structured training set yields clearly separated clusters with high classification probability and accuracy, reflecting well-defined passivation states encoded in distinct spectral shapes.

In this context, the k-nearest neighbours (k-NN) algorithm serves as a transparent classifier that relies on distances in the PCA-reduced space. The results clearly demonstrate that successful classification depends critically on preprocessing, particularly normalization and dimensionality reduction. Without PCA, class structure remains entangled and indistinguishable, and without proper normalization, classification becomes uncertain. The chosen pipeline, PCA applied to globally max-normalized spectra, provides sharply peaked class probabilities and robust separation, enabling clear distinction between intact, degraded, and enhanced passivity states. To test an alternative classifier, the same PCA-reduced input was also evaluated using a shallow neural network, which yielded comparable but slightly less confident predictions, supporting the overall robustness of the preprocessing strategy.”

Rather than attempting to model a continuum of all possible intermediate passivation states, this approach focuses on a small number of representative, high-quality spectra. PCA is used to extract dominant and interpretable spectral features that distinguish between key classes. The k-NN classifier then operates in this reduced feature space, assigning classes based on shape similarity. This strategy is particularly well suited to low-variance systems such as passive metals, where subtle spectral differences, though small, carry mechanistic significance. By concentrating on distinct regimes of passivity (enhanced, intact, and degraded), the method enables interpretable classification aligned with physical degradation states, rather than diluting predictive power across a continuous but ambiguous spectrum.

While this study focuses on passive metals, the approach is intended as a general conceptual outline for applying data-efficient, interpretable machine learning to electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) diagnostics more broadly. By emphasizing minimal but representative training data, PCA-based feature extraction, and shallow classifiers such as k-NN, the framework adopts a bottom-up strategy in which the training dataset can be transparently expanded. This includes systematic optimization of normalization methods, the number of retained PCA components, and component-wise scaling, allowing for tailored model refinement. The framework could be extended to other electrochemical systems that exhibit structured and classifiable spectral behavior. However, further validation is required to assess its robustness and transferability across diverse material classes and sensing applications.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy

Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were performed using the ReCorr® QCQ device in combination with a paste electrolyte cell, as described in detail elsewhere40. The setup comprises a compact potentiostat/frequency analyzer and a single carbon-based polymer counter electrode, within which a stainless steel SS316L pseudo-reference electrode is embedded. The reference and counter electrodes are separated by a thin epoxy resin layer.

The potential of the pseudo-reference electrode was verified against a saturated calomel electrode (SCE) daily, before and after measurements, yielding a stable offset of 60 ± 8 mV, confirming the reliability of the reference potential.

The metal tile sample served as the working electrode with direct electrical contact made with the crocodile clip.

The exposed area of the working electrode was limited to a 2.67 cm2 circular surface, to which 1.13 cm³ of paste electrolyte was applied, covered by the counter electrode/reference electrode lid29,30,43. Full construction details of the measurement cell, including all dimensions and a cross-sectional view illustrating the internal geometry and electrode layout, are provided elsewhere29,30.

The paste, similar in formulation to wax-based emulsions, consists of natural wax, water, and oil phases, with added carbon black and glycerol to ensure electrical conductivity (R_paste = 250 ± 10 Ω). Its preparation is described in detail elsewhere29. This paste formulation has previously demonstrated excellent spectral reproducibility in localized EIS measurements across multiple coatings and surface states, confirming its suitability for comparative and mechanistic impedance studies44.

EIS measurements were conducted over a frequency range of 10⁵ Hz to 10−2 Hz, using a 10 mV AC perturbation, with the DC bias set to the stabilized open circuit potential (OCP). OCP was monitored for 1000 s prior to EIS.

The test dataset included nitric-acid-passivated stainless steel subjected to sequential mechanical abrasion (grit 1200 paper) to simulate progressive surface deterioration, as well as Ti-6Al-4 V alloy samples exposed to stepwise cathodic polarization from open circuit potential down to − 1330 mV, with 1000 s holding periods at each potential prior to impedance measurement.

For external validation, we additionally used published spectra from Monrrabal et al.40,41who employed a gel-based quasi-solid electrolyte and press-on cell to acquire EIS data on a series of stainless steel components with varying surface treatments. These independent spectra serve as a representative validation set for testing the classifier on previously unseen data generated using a comparable localized measurement configuration.

Machine learning

Machine learning and clustering workflows were implemented using Orange (version 3.32.24), an open-source visual data mining platform34. All Orange workflow files (.ows) and documentation needed to reproduce the classification, and clustering results are available in the data and code repository (see Data availability). The workflows combines built-in Orange widgets with custom Python code developed for this study and integrated via Python Script widgets. Instructions are provided in Instructions for use.docx. The full source code for Orange widgets is available at: https://github.com/biolab/orange3.
