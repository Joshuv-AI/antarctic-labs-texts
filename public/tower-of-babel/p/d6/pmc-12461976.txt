A mouse model of stereotactic radiosurgery-induced neuroinflammation and blood-brain barrier compromise
Authors: Roberto J. Alcazar-Felix, Lin Cheng, Abhinav Srinath, Akash Bindal, David Tieri, Diana Vera-Cruz, Madison Yuan, Sammy Allaw, Nitha Aima Muntu, Dominic Montas, András Piffkó, Erik Pearson, Bader Ali, Peter Pytel, Issam A. Awad, Sean P. Polster
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) is a procedure that delivers high-dose single fraction, targeted radiation to treat brain pathologies. Brain radiation necrosis is a significant side effect of SRS, resulting in severe clinical sequelae such as seizure, hemorrhage, stroke, and neurological deficit. While focused radiation causes DNA damage and cell death, radiation necrosis is mostly mediated by vascular injury. Yet the effects of SRS on the neurovascular unit (NVU) cells—microglia, astrocytes, and endothelial cells—remain poorly understood. This study establishes a mouse SRS model using 15 to 60 Gy to characterize NVU stress, providing histological and transcriptomic profiles of radiation-induced damage. Our findings demonstrate blood-brain-barrier (BBB) disruption, inflammatory cell infiltration, and microvascular pathology. Spatial transcriptomics identified differentially expressed genes and cell-cell communication across NVU components, revealing a coordinated stress response involving immune modulation, barrier integrity, and tissue remodeling pathways. This model provides a mechanistic framework for developing strategies to mitigate BBB and NVU stress.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40478-025-02112-x.

Introduction

Stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) is a cornerstone treatment for a wide range of central nervous system (CNS) diseases in neurovascular and neuro-oncology care. By delivering a single, high-dose fraction of radiation to a precisely defined target, SRS achieves effective ablation of pathological tissue. While its clinical efficacy has been well established [1], the underlying cellular and molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood. Moreover, SRS can induce adverse radiation effects (AREs) in adjacent normal brain tissue, leading to complications such as vasogenic edema, neuroinflammation, ischemic injury, hemorrhage, and neuronal dysfunction [2, 3]. Clinical series estimate that up to 30% of SRS-treated patients develop AREs [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10], yet effective mitigation strategies remain limited [11]. Additionally, the variability in patient responses—where some individuals develop AREs despite identical radiation doses and volumes— suggests the involvement of host-specific factors, including systemic inflammation and neurovascular unit (NVU) susceptibility.

A leading hypothesis is that endothelial dysfunction plays a primary role in SRS-induced damage, resulting in vascular hyalinization, microvascular occlusion, and local ischemia [12]. This mechanism may explain why SRS is effective across diverse pathologies, including vascular malformations and tumors, which share endothelial-driven microvasculature [12]. Despite these insights, the broader impact of SRS on blood-brain barrier (BBB) integrity, neuroinflammation, and immune activation remains incompletely characterized and no robust animal model currently exists to systematically investigate these mechanisms. Therefore, establishing a mouse model of SRS-induced NVU damage is critical for elucidating the molecular and cellular cascades that drive AREs. Using state-of-the art histological and transcriptomic techniques, this study aims to characterize radiation-induced alterations in microglia, astrocytes, and endothelial cells to better define the pathophysiology of NVU dysfunction.

The clinical manifestations of AREs have been widely documented, with chronic encapsulated hematomas and pseudo-cavernomas often requiring surgical intervention. These lesions share histopathological and radiographic features with other endothelial dysmorphic diseases, such as cerebral cavernous malformations (CCMs) [13]. Previously, we identified shared molecular pathways between radiation-induced endothelial damage and CCM pathophysiology, implicating common signaling networks involved in NVU integrity, stress responses, and BBB regulation [2]. SRS has also been shown to induce microvascular permeability and immune activation, leading to MRI-detectable BBB disruption, perilesional vasogenic edema, and microhemorrhage [2, 14]. Current therapeutic interventions—such as corticosteroids, bevacizumab (anti-VEGF), pentoxifylline, hyperbaric oxygen, thalidomide, and vitamin E—primarily target the symptoms rather than the underlying mechanisms of AREs [15].

We hypothesize that SRS induces a coordinated inflammatory cascade within the NVU, leading to BBB dysfunction via shared signaling networks across microglia, astrocytes, and endothelial cells. Through histological and transcriptomic profiling, we aim to define the cellular and molecular events driving AREs and provide a mechanistic framework for understanding NVU dysfunction under radiation stress. This study not only offers insights into radiation-induced brain injury but also has implications for broader neurovascular pathologies involving endothelial damage, BBB dysregulation, and neuroinflammation.

Methods

Animals and anesthesia

Animal care and experiments were performed following the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Chicago, ACUP #72726. The study was planned and performed according to ARRIVE guidelines 2.0. All mice used in this work were female, Harlan Sprague Dawley (HSD): C57BL/6 background, and were purchased from Envigo (Indianapolis, IN). Female mice were selected to reduce confounding from male aggression and stress in group housing. Female mice have also been reported to demonstrate increased survival following irradiation, supporting their use in radiation injury models [16]. Mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions and used at 10 weeks of age upon irradiation. Mice were irradiated with a total of 15, 22.5, 35, 50, or 60 Gy (all doses are reported at the 50% isodose line), as described below. The resulting brain parenchymal changes were characterized by CT imaging and histology. For imaging and irradiation, anesthesia was induced using 4% isoflurane mixed with 21.5% oxygen and 78.5% nitrogen (air) and maintained with 2% isoflurane and air, administered via nosepiece at 2.5 L per minute. For study endpoints, euthanasia was performed with isoflurane overdose, with confirmation by decapitation.

Radiation delivery and simulation

Irradiation planning and simulation were performed with µ-RayStation 8B (Micro) (7.99.30.9336, RaySearch, Stockholm, Sweden) software. Radiation dose delivery to the 50% isodose line was calculated using Monte Carlo (GPU) algorithm. On day 0, mice were anesthetized and placed in a prone position on a plate in a small animal precision microirradiator (XRAD 225Cx, Precision X-ray Inc., North Branford, CT). Image-guided radiation delivery planning involved two sequential cone beam computed tomography (CT) images taken through a 2.0 mm Al filter including an initial scout (40 kVp, 0.5 mA, 0.3 mm isotropic voxels), followed by a higher resolution scan (60 kVp, 2.5 mA, 0.1 mm isotropic voxels). The beam target was placed where the temporomandibular joint at the thinnest portion of the skull and the posterior aspect of the clivus was observed on a coronal cut. The stereotactic target was then placed in the middle of the left hemisphere.

Stereotactic X-ray ionizing radiation was performed with a 5 mm aperture diameter lead collimator in a parallel-opposed (anteroposterior-posteroanterior) beam configuration, each delivering half the desired total dose at 225 kVp, 13 mA, and 0.3 mm Cu filter providing a dose rate of ~ 2.2 Gy/min. The animal support bed thickness was taken into consideration for effective radiation dose delivery. Total single doses of 15, 22.5, 35, 40, 42.5, 50, or 60 Gy to the 50% isodose line were delivered in this study. After radiotherapy, all mice were returned to their cages, with access to food and water ad libitum.

Imaging

Using the XRAD 225Cx onboard CT imaging, mice underwent in vivo serial contrast-enhanced scans at 2, 6, 8, 10, 12 or 18 weeks after irradiation (N = 3 per time point and per dose). A contrast (iohexol, 350 mg/mL, GE HealthCare, Chicago, IL) volume of 200 µl was retro-orbitally injected while under anesthesia before the scout scan for each CT follow-up scan.

3D Slicer software version 5.2.2 (www.slicer.org) was used to segment and quantify CT contrast-enhancing lesion volumes [17]. In the Volumes module, Display Window was set to 1,000; Level to 400, and Lookup Table to FullRainbow. To pinpoint the irradiated area, anatomical landmarks corresponding to the radiation delivery were identified. Subsequently, within the Segment Editor module, a new segmentation was created. Employing the threshold tool (range parameters − 70, 600), segmentation was performed on the first and last slices with visible contrast-enhancing lesion in the coronal, axial, and sagittal planes. Additionally, segmentation was performed every 3–6 slices between these endpoints. For skipped slices, segmentation was completed by interpolating between segmented slices using the Fill between slices tool with a 0.1 joint smoothing factor. Lesion volumes were then calculated using the Quantification/Segment Statistics module, and measurements were expressed in mm3. Dose-response curves were generated in order to follow CE lesion volumes over time.

Histology

Following euthanasia, the mouse brains were harvested and immersion-fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin (NBF) for 48 h at room temperature on an orbital shaker. Brains were sectioned into 1 mm thick coronal blocks and then embedded in paraffin as previously described [18]. Representative coronal irradiated 5 μm thick tissue sections were obtained from the block 2 mm ventral to the lambda. Tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histological characterization. H&E slides were then scanned using the Vectra Polaris brightfield scanning system (Akoya Biosciences, Marlborough, MA) at 40× magnification.

For ARE histological scoring, a grading system was adapted from Jiang et al. 2015 (Fig. 1A). Assessments were conducted per square on a grid with 500 × 500 μm ROIs using QuPath (v. 0.4.4) [19]. Immunofluorescent staining was done for B cells (B220+), T cells (CD3+), macrophages (CD163+, IBA1+), microglia (CD163−, IBA1+), and astrocytes (GFAP+). Antigen retrieval was performed at 100–115 °C for 15 min at pH 6 before B220 (cat. # 553085, RRID AB_394615, 1:150 dilution, BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ) and GFAP (cat. # ab68428, RRID AB_1209224, 1:100 dilution, Abcam, Boston, MA) staining and at pH9 before CD3 (cat. # ab135372, RRID AB_2884903, 1:75 dilution, Abcam), CD163 (cat. # ab182422, RRID AB_2753196, 1:500 dilution, Abcam) and Iba1 staining (cat. # ab178846, RRID AB_2636859, 1:3000 dilution, Abcam). Staining was completed with the Opal 6-Plex Manual Detection Kit (cat. # NEL861001KT, Akoya Biosciences). Immunofluorescence cell quantification was performed using the QuPath cell detection tool. For each cell type, an object classifier was trained by manually annotating true positive and false positive examples, enabling accurate and cell-type-specific identification.

Histological assessment of BBB permeability was detected by perfusion of EB dye (cat. # E2129-10G, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO). Five mice were irradiated with 35 Gy at the 50% isodose line. At 12 weeks post-SRS, EB at 2% weight/volume dilution in sterile normal saline was retroorbitally injected at 3 ml/kg. One hour after injection, the mice were perfused with normal saline until fluid flowing from the right atrium was colorless, followed by brain harvest. Brains were then fixed in 10% NBF for 48 h. To measure BBB disruption, brains were sectioned into 1 mm thick coronal blocks and areas with EB extravasation manually delineated using Image J [20]. EB dyed brains were imaged under fluorescence (excitation 570 nm, emission 640) using Lago X optical imaging system (Spectral Instruments, Tucson, AZ).

Spatial transcriptomics

Three coronal mouse brain sections, each representing the largest macroscopic lesion diameter observed in separate mice irradiated with 40 Gy at the 50% isodose line, were selected for transcriptomic studies at 6 weeks post-SRS. Formalin-Fixed Paraffin-Embedded tissue Sect. (5 μm) were processed following the Nanostring GeoMx DSP Manual Slide Preparation user manual (MAN-10150-03, RNA Slide Preparation). In brief, tissue slides were baked at 60 °C for 3 h and subjected to deparaffinization and rehydration, followed by target retrieval using antigen retrieval buffer (Tris-EDTA, PH9.0) for 20 min in a steamer. RNA target retrieval was accomplished by a digest with proteinase K (1ug/ml) for 15 min at 37 °C. Tissue was post-fixed in 10% NBF, followed by an overnight hybridization at 37 °C with the RNA probes (Mouse Whole Transcriptome Atlas). Excess probes were removed the next day washing the samples twice in stringent washes at 37 °C, followed by an incubation period in blocking Buffer W (Nanostring LOT#:2-23020032). After that, tissue was stained using conjugated primary antibodies targeting GFAP-AF488 (Invitrogen, RRID AB_10598515), Iba1-AF594 (Cell Signaling, RRID AB_2923427) and CD31-AF647 (Abcam, RRID AB_2857973) as well as the SYTO83 nuclear stain (Invitrogen, RRID N/A) at room temperature for 1 h.

For transcriptomics data acquisition, slides were placed on the GeoMx Digital Spatial Profiler (DSP) and scanned in 20x magnification. Based on immunofluorescence images, regions of interest (ROI) were selected. A segmented profiling strategy was used to define an ROI. Briefly, circular ROIs with a 650 μm diameter were used for GFAP and IBA1 corresponding channels. For CD31, ROIs were made around the vasculature using the drawing tool. Once an ROI was defined, fluorescence thresholds were tailored to allow histopathological informed masking of each desired cell population. ROIs were placed in a symmetrical fashion in the irradiated and control hemispheres along the mouse cerebrum. After ROI selection, oligonucleotide barcodes were collected into a 96-well plate by UV ablation while precisely separating compartments in each region. Sequencing libraries were prepared following the NanoString GeoMx DSP NGS Readout user manual (MAN-10153-03) by using the NanoString SeqCode Pack_G, H and B. Amplified libraries were pooled and purified using two rounds of Ampure XP magnetic bead cleanup (bead ratio: 1.2). Purified libraries were sequenced in an Illumina NovaSeq flowcell PE 2 × 50 bp at a depth of 100 reads per square micron of total ROI area. Of the 120 total ROIs, 95 passed the quality check and were used in the differential expression (DE) analysis.

Statistical analysis

For contrast-enhancing lesion volume data, lesion size curves were plotted over time by radiation dose given. Cell counts were compared across time using repeated measures Two-Way ANOVA analysis. Statistical tests and figures were prepared using PRISM software (version 10.1.1; GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA). DGE analysis was performed using a linear mixed effects model implemented in the GeoMxWorkflows package. The model included Hemisphere and Region as fixed effects and Brain as random effect. Differential expression was performed with Hemisphere as the grouping variable. Venn diagrams were created using Bioinformatics & Evolutionary Genomics online software (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/).

IPA was used to analyze differentially expressed genes, identifying significantly altered canonical pathways and upstream regulators. The statistical significance of each IPA pathway was determined using Fisher’s exact test (FDR < 0.05, abs (LFC) > 1). GSEA was conducted using the results from the linear mixed effects model. Enrichment was quantified using the normalized enrichment score (NES) with a false discovery rate (FDR) cutoff set at < 0.05 to define significance.

CellChat (version 2.1.2) was employed to infer and analyze cell–cell communication networks and information flow as previously described [21]. Briefly, the built-in CellChat database of ligand–receptor pairs was used to compute communication probabilities between CD31+, IBA1+ and GFAP+ cells, and bootstrapping approaches were applied to assess the statistical significance of each interaction (p < 0.05). Finally, enriched signaling pathways were identified based on CellChat’s internal inference methods to characterize the key communication axes driving cellular interactions.

Results

Establishing a mouse model of SRS damage over varying doses of radiation and time

Radiation was administered to a 5 mm left hemispheric target, with various doses and fixed volume calculations allowing for intra-target heterogeneity of < 10% (Fig. 2A). Simulated radiation distribution of 60 Gy to the isocenter showed a uniform dose distribution within the target volume in the left hemisphere and the contralateral hemisphere receiving 0 Gy (Fig. 2B). After radiation, mice were followed by serial assessment via cone beam CT scanning with venous contrast (Fig. 2C) and the lesions segmented (Fig. 2D). CE lesions developed as early as 2 weeks post-radiation with 50–60 Gy at the 50% isodose line, but were associated with high morbidity and mortality by 8 and 3 weeks, respectively (Fig. 2E). At 42.5 Gy, CE lesions appeared by 4 weeks and plateaued at 8 weeks without morbidity or mortality. Doses ≤ 35 Gy led to CE lesions only after > 10 weeks.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) assessment of irradiated brain with 15, 22.5 and 35 Gy, harvested at 6-, 12-, and 18-weeks post radiation, showed the progression of damage that occurs in three relative stages as assessed by damage Grades 1, 2, and 3 (Fig. 1A). For radiation damage grading, a scale was adapted from Jiang et al. (2015) [22]. Grade 1 showed mild histological changes associated with radiation injury, telangiectasias (< 100 μm diameter), and microhemorrhages. Grade 2 additionally presented vascular hyalinization, reactive astrocytosis (astrocytes with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm and long, branching cytoplasmic processes), and tissue loss. Grade 3 was defined by extensive tissue loss, cavernous vessels (> 100 μm), and/or fibrinoid vascular necrosis.

Dose and time-dependent radiation-induced damage and increased microglial and astrocytic activation in the brain

Histological analysis of brain tissue post-radiation revealed dose- and time-dependent radiation-induced damage. Brains collected at 6-, 12-, and 18-weeks post-radiation across varying doses showed a marked escalation in histological damage, with more severe radiation-induced lesions observed at higher radiation doses and later time points. As shown in Fig. 1B, the percentage of regions of interest (ROIs) containing histological signs of radiation-induced damage increased over time, with higher doses of radiation inducing a greater proportion of severe lesions (Grade 3). By 18 weeks post-35 Gy, the majority of lesions were classified as Grade 2 and Grade 3, indicating the effect of radiation over time.

Lesion volume demonstrated a correlation with the extent of radiation-induced lesions within the irradiated brain (Fig. 1C). A rapid increase in CE lesion volume was observed with rising percentages of radiation-affected tissue; however, the growth rate plateaued at higher levels, suggesting that while initial contrast-enhancing lesions were an early indicator of radiation injury, the histological severity of AREs continued to increase even as the CT contrast-enhancing lesion volume stabilized. The exponential decay model fitted to the data [Y = 64.36 (1-e^−0.1081X)] reflects this relationship, with lesion volume reaching a maximum despite continued histological deterioration in affected regions.

Histological examination at a dose of 35 Gy showed a distinct stepwise progression of tissue damage (Supplemental Fig. 1). Initially, microvascular damage with hyalinization was observed (Phase 1), followed by dilation and dysmorphism of affected vessels which maintained CD31 expression (Phase 2) (Supplemental Fig. 2). As radiation injury progressed, necrosis began to appear (Phase 3), characterized by eosinophilic regions with cell nuclei loss. Ultimately, extensive tissue loss and necrosis were evident (Phase 4), confirming the long-term degenerative effects of radiation on brain tissue. Immunofluorescent staining of microglia (IBA1+) and astrocytes (GFAP+) revealed cellular responses to radiation injury around dysmorphic microvasculature (Fig. 1D). Increased microglial and astrocytic activation was evident through the retraction of microglial processes and the envelopment of dysmorphic microvasculature by astrocytic processes, suggesting that these cell types play an active role in the brain’s response to radiation-induced damage.

Evans blue and optical imaging show increased blood-brain barrier permeability in the SRS model

Evans Blue (EB) dye perfusion revealed BBB disruption with increased vascular permeability limited to the irradiated targeted volume as observed in photographic images and optical imaging of the brain slices (Fig. 3A-B). Cone-beam CT 3D segmented volumetrics correlated with EB extravasation volumes quantified in coronal sections (R2 = 0.81, p = 0.0417) (Fig. 3C).

Brain damage by SRS at various doses elicits an inflammatory response within the brain in a dose-dependent manner

Brain sections were analyzed at 6- and 12-weeks post radiation with 35 Gy. Lymphocytic B220+ and CD3+ cell infiltration was observed at each time point in the irradiated hemisphere, with significant increased CD3+ cell infiltration compared to non-irradiated hemisphere (p = 0.0237) (Fig. 4B). GFAP staining revealed that although the number of total astrocytes did not change significantly (Fig. 4C), there were morphological astrocytic changes depending on the brain area (Fig. 4Ci-iv). In cortical irradiated regions, astrocytes hypertrophied and with longer processes (Fig. 4Civ). In deep brain, non-irradiated astrocytes had a rounded appearance with small processes covering small areas of tissue (Fig. 4Ci). In the irradiated deep brain, the number of these rounded astrocytes significantly decreased (p = 0.0234). Radiation induced deep brain astrocytes to display longer processes covering larger tissue areas (Fig. 4Cii).

IBA1+ cells showed a non-significant trend of increased cell numbers in the irradiated hemisphere over time (Fig. 4D). IBA1+ CD163 + co-stained cell numbers were significantly decreased by radiation (p = 0.0043) compared to non-irradiated hemisphere (Fig. 4D), especially in the optic nerve and meningeal compartments. In the non-irradiated hemisphere, IBA1+ CD163 + cell numbers significantly increased from 6 to 12 weeks post radiation (p = 0.0341). Radiation caused a morphological change of resting IBA1+ cells inducing loss of their ramified morphology into an activated state characterized by their ameboid shape (Fig. 4 Di_1, Dii_1, Ei_2, Eii_2). IBA1+ CLEC7A+ co-staining, further confirmed increased cell numbers of activated microglia in the irradiated hemisphere (p = 0.0101) (Fig. 4E).

GeoMx spatial transcriptome analysis of CD31, GFAP, and IBA1 positive cells show a unique signature of differentially expressed genes in response to radiation damage

Within these tissues, spatial transcriptomics utilizing the GeoMx Nanostring technology was completed on 20 ROIs in each irradiated mouse hemisphere, and 20 ROIs of the contralateral control hemisphere. Principal component analysis showed strong differential gene expression across all cell types (Fig. 5A). Pairwise DE analysis between the lesional hemisphere and the contralateral non-lesional brain hemisphere, irrespective of cell type identified 920 DEGs (FDR ≤ 0.05, |log(fc)| ≥ 0.5) (Fig. 5B, Supplemental File 1). SRS induced 466 DEGs in CD31+ cells, 536 DEGs in GFAP+ cells, and 828 DEGs in IBA1+ cells (FDR ≤ 0.05, |log(fc)| ≥ 0.5) (Fig. 5C, Supplemental File 1). A total of 79 DEGs were common between CD31+, GFAP+, and IBA1+ cells whereas 268, 316, and 599 DEGs were unique to each of them, respectively (Fig. 5D, Supplemental File 2).

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) in sorted CD31⁺ (endothelial), GFAP⁺ (astrocyte), and IBA1⁺ (microglia) populations revealed both shared and cell type–specific responses to stereotactic radiosurgery (Fig. 6A, Supplemental File 3). In endothelial cells, the enriched pathways indicated robust vascular remodeling and endothelial activation, accompanied by immune/inflammatory and cellular stress signaling (e.g., interferon response, coagulation, p53, and apoptosis). Astrocytes exhibited strong pro-inflammatory profiles, particularly centered on interferon and IL6–JAK–STAT3 signaling, along with evidence of metabolic reprogramming (including a pronounced downregulation of cholesterol homeostasis). Microglia displayed a pronounced immune activation, with marked enrichment of interferon-driven and inflammatory pathways, alongside stress and apoptotic signaling.

Bulk Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) identified 175 enriched pathways, broadly related to neuroinflammation, blood-brain barrier dysfunction, and intracellular/metabolic modifications (Fig. 6B, Supplemental File 4). Of these enriched pathways, 3 were significantly downregulated, while 67 were significantly upregulated. IPA analysis identified 3,520 upstream regulators—including proteins, nucleotides, and drugs—potentially modulating enriched pathways via target molecules (Supplemental File 4). Of these, 42 were predicted activators and 27 inhibitors, highlighting potential targets involved in intercellular communication and radiation-induced pathological or compensatory processes.

Spatial transcriptomics analyses were conducted on cell types implicated in radiation necrosis, namely endothelium, astrocytes, and microglia. IPA of CD31+ endothelial DEGs identified 261 enriched pathways, with 61 significantly upregulated and 5 downregulated, along with 4061 potential upstream regulators (174 significantly activating, 76 inhibiting) (Supplemental File 5). Astrocyte (GFAP+) analyses revealed 116 enriched pathways potentially involved in radiation necrosis, 29 significantly upregulated and 1 downregulated, and identified 3476 potential regulators (77 activating, 46 inhibiting) (Supplemental File 6). Microglial analyses further validated their established role, identifying 219 enriched pathways, 86 significantly upregulated and 4 downregulated, with 3002 upstream regulators (38 activating, 30 inhibiting) (Supplemental File 7), offering new mechanistic insights and context for interactions between microglia, astrocytes, and endothelium.

The roles of these DEGs within enriched pathways, with the potential for modification by upstream regulators begin to illustrate a pathobiological framework in the study of brain radiation necrosis. While this helps to focus subsequent studies with more directed hypothesis-generating findings, an important consideration is the communication between these cell types of interest.

Differential cellular communication pathways in response to irradiation revealed by CellChat

Using the CellChat R package, we compared signaling pathway information flow between irradiated and non-irradiated tissue to identify radiation-specific induced alterations. We found that nine pathways were significantly downregulated (ANGPTL, NPY, PERIOSTIN, COMPLEMENT, PTH, RBP4, KLK, WNT, and CX3C), whereas fifteen pathways enriched (CSF, GAS, PSAP, LIFR, SEMA3, PLAU, GALECTIN, SPP1, CXCL, NRG, CHEMERIN, PROCR, VISFATIN, MK, and GRN) in irradiated tissue (Fig. 7A). A consolidated view of intercellular signal strength and directionality (Fig. 7B) highlights robust upregulation of the neurotrophic factor PSAP and the proinflammatory cytokine SPP1, predominantly targeting GFAP+ cells. These signals likely represent a pro-repair and inflammatory response aimed at mitigating damage and coordinating glial activation. Conversely, GFAP+ cells also show strong downregulation of pleiotrophin (PTN) and neurotrophin (NT) signals originating from CD31+ and IBA1+ cells, underscoring the potential therapeutic benefit of PTN and BDNF to protect the NVU.

Dysfunctional signaling was further assessed by comparing the communication probabilities of each ligand-receptor pair among CD31+, GFAP+, and IBA1+ cells in irradiated versus non-irradiated tissue (Fig. 7C). In total, 43 interaction pairs were upregulated and 13 were downregulated, indicating a broad reconfiguration of cellular communication networks upon irradiation (Supplemental Fig. 8). Pathways associated with angiogenesis (ANGPT), inflammatory chemokine signaling (CCL, CXCL), growth/differentiation (FGF, BMP, IGF), and immune regulation (TGFβ, GALECTIN) became noticeably more active following radiation (Fig. 7C). By contrast, certain pathways critical for vascular homeostasis (EDN), metabolism (RBP4), and neurotrophic support (BDNF, PTN) showed reduced communication (Fig. 7D). Collectively, these data illustrate an extensive network-wide shift toward an inflammatory microenvironment in response to SRS-induced damage.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a mouse model of SRS-induced injury and characterized its effects using histopathological analysis, CT, and spatial transcriptomics. SRS is frequently used in clinical practice, yet its impact on the NVU and BBB demonstrates unexplained variability [23]. In contrast to whole-brain radiation therapy (WBRT), which induces cellular damage through cumulative DNA damage over multiple fractions of lower radiation doses, SRS administers a highly focused, high-dose radiation to targeted regions. Typical adverse events associated with WBRT include cognitive impairment, with comparatively less significant disruption of the NVU or BBB and a lower incidence of radiation necrosis.

To characterize dose-dependent effects of SRS on the NVU, we performed a comprehensive dose–response analysis across six radiation doses. These curves informed the selection of distinct doses for downstream cellular and transcriptomic analyses, based on the differential kinetics and radiographic evolution of lesions. For immunofluorescence studies, we selected a 35 Gy dose, which was associated with a more protracted latency period and a gradual, progressive increase in lesion volume over time. This trajectory provides an optimal window to capture dynamic cellular responses to radiation, allowing the characterization of temporal changes in the cellular microenvironment. The extended lesion evolution permitted serial histologic sampling at 6- and 12-weeks post-SRS to identify cellular alterations in neurovascular cell populations at different imaging stages. For spatial transcriptomic analysis, we selected 40 Gy dose as dose–response data indicated that this higher dose consistently produced contrast-enhancing lesions detectable by CT imaging at 6 weeks of SRS. Such a profile is advantageous for transcriptomic studies, as it ensures Grade 1 lesion formation with clear spatial demarcation, a prerequisite for precise spatial profiling.

Prior studies have also used doses ≥ 35 Gy to elicit reproducible BBB disruption and late AREs [24, 22, 25]. This dose represents peak irradiation doses commonly used in ablative prescriptions (e.g. trigeminal neuralgia, thalamotomy) within a radiated field with high dose fall off regions. It also provides a time-efficient and translationally relevant platform for evaluating candidate therapeutic interventions targeting the NVU. This strategy accelerates lesion development in mice, enabling screening of agents aimed at mitigating secondary AREs that arise at lower, clinically relevant doses but would otherwise require significantly longer latency periods to manifest in preclinical models. While no mouse model fully recapitulates human radiation necrosis, we selected this dosing paradigm to reliably induce BBB disruption detectable via contrast-enhanced imaging, thereby facilitating quantitative and reproducible assessment of treatment effects.

In early states of SRS induced NVU stress we propose a framework with cellular coordination of a microglia and astrocyte response to maintain BBB integrity. Our findings reveal a dynamic interplay of immune activation, vascular dysfunction, and tissue remodeling, with microglia serving as early responders, astrocytes contributing to BBB integrity and repair, and endothelial cells exhibiting radiation-induced dysfunction. By integrating histological, transcriptomic, and spatial analyses, we provide mechanistic insights into how these cellular interactions contribute to BBB disruption, inflammation, and early microhemorrhages. The human microglia atlas implies a microglia pathology-associated phenotype, known as “disease-associated microglia” (DAM), that shares signature clusters with those identified in mouse brains subjected to SRS [26]. These clusters overlap highly with human gene clustering associated with DAMs. This pro-inflammatory state is shared across multiple diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease, multiple sclerosis, epilepsy, and Lewy body disease. This is of key interest as these diseases have implications to systemic determinants such as peripheral immune regulation, brain-gut axis implications as well as potential modifiers of disease activity. SRS as a model of NVU stress is appealing for future study as well as the dynamic aspect of BBB opening and closing.

Consistent with previous research, our study demonstrates rapid activation of critical transcriptional and translational pathways regulating metabolic homeostasis, oxidative balance, and intercellular signaling following radiation [27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 3, 33, 34]. Specifically, endothelial cells exhibited pronounced dysregulation in pathways associated with vascular homeostasis, correlating closely with observed BBB disruption and vascular pathology. This finding is consistent with shared endothelial signaling pathways seen in diseases like cerebral cavernous malformations (CCMs), characterized by local inflammation and BBB dysfunction [2]. Astrocytes demonstrated marked enrichment in inflammatory signaling pathways, notably IL6–JAK–STAT3 signaling, alongside metabolic reprogramming. Microglia displayed significant activation, characterized by heightened immune response and antigen presentation, which supports their critical role in orchestrating neuroinflammation post-radiation.

Microglia emerged as key initial responders, showing increased activation and recruitment to injured regions, as indicated by CLEC7A + immunostaining. The numbers of microglia significantly increased with higher radiation doses and over time, peaking at 35 Gy, as revealed by immunofluorescence staining. This aligns with their dual role in initiating neuroinflammation and facilitating tissue repair [35, 36, 37, 38]. Nevertheless, this microglial response potentially exacerbates tissue injury by amplifying inflammatory processes, exemplified by heightened infiltration of T and B lymphocytes into irradiated tissue. Increased lymphocyte infiltration, together with elevated complement by IPA, implies an adaptive immune response triggered by radiation-induced antigen exposure, further intensifying local inflammation.

Our combined transcriptomic and intercellular communication analyses revealed distinct, cell type–specific pathway alterations with translational implications. The increased CCL2/8, ANGPT1/2, CXCL10/12, SPP1 and PSAP signaling indicates a coordinated microvascular and glial activation that may have both reparative and pro-inflammatory effects. In contrast, suppression of PTN and BDNF pathways reflects a loss of neurotrophic support that could exacerbate NVU vulnerability. Together, these findings offer mechanistic insights and identify candidate pathways for targeted interventions aimed at protecting BBB integrity, neuro-inflammation and modulating glial responses following SRS.

Our study has certain limitations. With a sample size of n = 3 (per time point and per dose), interpretation beyond absolute trends should be made with caution. While the mouse model provides controlled conditions for experimentation, its direct translational relevance to humans should be interpreted with caution. Further cross-species validation studies are necessary for translational applicability. Furthermore, the potential selection bias and imprecision inherent in transcriptomic analyses should be addressed in future research by incorporating complementary techniques, such as proteomics or metabolomics, to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the molecular landscape post-radiation. Although we developed a dose–response curve over a portion of the mouse lifespan, this does not replicate the longer clinical course of AREs in some patients or the formation of encapsulated hematomas. We have previously described the histopathologic features of human SRS-induced AREs, including vascular dysmorphism, cavernous-like malformations, lymphocytic infiltration, and glial reactivity [2] —features also observed in our mouse model. These features could potentially be approximated by using lower radiation doses and extending study endpoints, which we plan to explore in future work. Our model’s small, tightly collimated field produces a steep dose gradient with a limited sublethal ‘border zone’ compared with many clinical SRS plans. Concentric (core‑to‑periphery) analyses could be incorporated in future studies using annular ROI sampling and dosing schemes that generate broader gradient regions to better capture border‑zone biology. Finally, while this study focused on single-dose SRS, future work will also evaluate the differential NVU responses to alternative delivery modalities, including conventional fractionated radiotherapy, which may elicit distinct biological responses due to cumulative DNA damage, extended latency, and broader perilesional exposure. Future directions beyond this study include cognitive and behavioral implications of radiation damage, changes with fractionation schemes, as well as gradient border zone biology. These practical measures will inform response to therapeutics and more subtle biology of radiation damage.

In conclusion, our study presents a mouse model for investigating the effects of SRS on the NVU. We found that brain irradiation causes alterations of parenchymal architecture, induction of neuroinflammation, and BBB leakage. Our findings provide key insights into the mechanisms underlying radiation-induced brain damage, particularly in terms of the cellular and molecular responses of the NVU. This model of stress likely has similarities to other NVU stressors and this model is well suited as a non-invasive stress system with physiologic outcome parameters, namely the BBB. Understanding the differential roles of microglia, astrocytes, and endothelial cells in radiation-induced damage can inform therapeutic strategies aimed at mitigating AREs. Targeting microglial activation or modulating astrocytic responses could offer potential avenues for limiting inflammation and promoting tissue repair post-SRS or NVU stress. Additionally, preserving BBB integrity through therapeutic interventions targeting endothelial cells may help prevent or reduce the severity of neurovascular damage.
