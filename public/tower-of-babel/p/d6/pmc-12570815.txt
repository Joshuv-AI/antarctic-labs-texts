PharmacyGPT: exploration of artificial intelligence for medication management in the intensive care unit
Authors: Zhengliang Liu, Shaochen Xu, Zihao Wu, Brian Murray, Erin F. Barreto, Sheng Li, Wei Liu, Xiang Li, Tianming Liu, Andrea Sikora
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe purpose of this evaluation was to develop a novel framework for large language model (LLMs) iterative prompt optimization for the specialized domain of medication decision-making in the intensive care unit (ICU). This serves as a first step towards the use of LLMs as clinical decision support (CDS) tools capable of performing pharmacy related tasks.MethodsUsing a cohort of 1,000 adult patients managed in the ICU for greater than 24 h, an iterative optimization process in the GPT-4 framework was applied to enhance performance of various medication-related tasks, including patient disease state clustering by medication regimen, medication regimen generation, and outcome prediction with the intent to develop PharmacyGPT. Within this feedback loop, the input prompts were adjusted based on model performance in previous iterations.ResultsThe iterative prompt optimization process housed within PharmacyGPT was able to develop meaningful disease state clusters based on input information, develop medication regimens with inclusion of drug, dose, and frequency, and predict mortality with an accuracy of 0.75, precision 0.37, and recall 0.70.ConclusionIterative prompt optimization shows promise as a rapid means to improve LLM functionality to specific tasks, even in highly domain specific areas like medication management in the ICU. Such domain specific engineering may serve as a strategy to develop LLMs as viable CDS tools.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-025-03230-1.

Background

Large language models (LLMs) have shown remarkable potential in a multitude of applications across various healthcare domains [1–4]. To date, explorations of LLMs in diagnosis, enhancing medical knowledge, patient education, care coordination, patient triage, clinical trial referrals, and clinical note taking have been undertaken [5]. Despite their impressive generalization capabilities, LLMs have had limited clinical implementation and validation and likely require domain-specific engineering to be viable providers of clinical decision support (CDS) in complex fields, and methods to adapt LLMs in this manner remain under-investigated [6]. A key limitation for such domain-specific training is the lack of high quality training corpora and requirement for experts to provide clinical validation, which are resource-intensive and often rate-limiting [7, 8]. The lack of high quality datasets in the intensive care unit (ICU) is a well-known problem that has limited artificial intelligence (AI) uptake and is particularly true for granular data about medication use.

Prompt engineering, wherein generative AI is guided towards the desired outputs via detailed instructions, has emerged as a promising approach to provide a rapid LLM training mechanism for domain-specific fields [9–11]. In particular, applying few-shot training, a paradigm where the LLM is trained with only a small number of examples are provided by leveraging prior knowledge from a related task, is an efficient means of overcoming otherwise tedious dataset acquisition and processing. Moreover, dynamic prompting, which generates interaction prompts in real-time based on user input data, can overcome static approaches in a way that is both efficient but highly personalized to the task [9–11]. To test, the hypothesis that an iterative prompt engineering strategy has utility for domain-specific LLM training, medication use in the intensive care unit (ICU) was chosen.

Medication use in the ICU is notoriously complex but also extremely relevant to patient outcomes and a target of information technology (IT) related quality improvement efforts [12, 13]. Several key factors converge to create this clinical complexity: (1) patients are frequently prescribed 13–20 medications at any given time that can be titrated minute-to-minute [14, 15], (2) ICU medications are often designated as high-risk with narrow therapeutic indices and potential for drug-drug interactions, and (3) patients have rapidly changing end organ function altering pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties of mediations used. Beyond these clinical components, medications present a unique challenge for computer algorithms because of the unique alphanumeric combinations required for appropriate interpretation. For example, “cefepime 2 grams in 100 milliliters 0.9% sodium chloride intravenous every 8 hours” cannot be abbreviated to “cefepime” because the dose (2 g) and frequency (every 8 h) are required to know the patient received a sufficient amount of cefepime to treat their infection. Moreover, this “sufficient amount” cannot be known without knowing the patient’s renal function and infection type, with this dose potentially being appropriate for meningitis in a patient on renal replacement therapy but a six-fold overdose for a skin and soft tissue infection in a patient on intermittent hemodialysis. This is before well known data standardization challenges wherein different electronic health records (EHR) may use “cefepime 2gm in 100mL NS q8h” or “cefepime 2000 mg in 100mL 0.9% NS every 8 hours” etc. Even so, the causal role of medications on patient outcomes warrants AI methods development. Indeed, estimates indicate that unintended adverse drug events (ADEs) occur in 5% of the over 36 million hospitalized patients per year and unintended sentinel events occur at a rate of 38.8 events per 100 patients days in the ICU [16–19]. Each individual ADE doubles patient mortality, and the annual costs of treating ADEs across the US exceeds $1.5 billion [16, 17, 20–24]. These findings are particularly striking given that most ADEs are preventable [16, 17]. Given this complexity, high quality IT tools remains limited, and safe medication use in the ICU requires interprofessional healthcare team management including critical care pharmacists (considered domain experts) to ensure patient safety [25].

The purpose of this evaluation was to apply an iterative prompt optimization approach using few-shot training and dynamic prompting to support LLMs to conduct medication-related tasks (including mortality and severity of illness prediction using medication data and medication regimen generation given patient data). The goal was to establish a use case portfolio to inspire future research exploring LLM application to medication-related questions. This work has several contributions: (1) to open new dialogues for the application of LLMs for medication management, (2) to provide directions for investigators for future improvements in LLM-based medication management, (3) to evaluate the strengths and limitations of GPT-4 in medication management, (4) to provide insights for tailored data collection strategies to maximize the potential of LLMs in specialized fields, and (5) to present an effective framework and use case collection for applying LLM to medication management.

Methods

Data description

This study was reviewed by the University of Georgia (UGA) Institutional Review Board (IRB) and deemed to be exempt from IRB oversight (Project00001541), including waiver of consent. All methods were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the UGA IRB and the Helsinki Declaration of 1975. Patient data were obtained from the Carolina Data Warehouse, which houses EHR data from the University of North Carolina Health System. A random number generation was employed to identify 1,000 adult patients admitted to an ICU for ≥ 24 h between October 2015 and October 2020. Only data from the patient’s first ICU admission was included in the analysis.

The dataset contains patient demographics, medication information, and patient outcomes. Demographic characteristics included age, sex, admission diagnosis, severity of illness scores including sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) and Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) at 24 h. Medication information included the medication regimens in the first 24 h and the medication regimen complexity-intensive care unit (MRC-ICU) score at 24 h. The MRC-ICU is a validated score summarizing the complexity of prescribed medications in the ICU [26–28]. Additional medication information included consisted of drug, dose, route, duration, and timing of administration. Patient outcomes included mortality and ICU length of stay. Medication orders reflect what was administered to the patient by the multiprofessional team, but the presence of comprehensive medication management (CMM) to optimize those orders is unknown, given the retrospective nature of this analysis. Relevant methods and dataset description have been previously described [27].

Creating interpretable patient clusters

The first step was to generate high quality embeddings to be used for prompt engineering. This approach aimed to create accurate and interpretable patient clusters for use in clinical decisions. Interpretability was achieved based on meaningful categorization of patient disease states and separation of patient groups. The pseudo-code algorithm in Supplemental Fig. 1 describes the process of generating patient clusters using GPT-4 embeddings and hierarchical clustering from the dataset, which included age, sex, admission diagnosis and category, and ICD-10 based problem list. The title of Supplemental Fig. 1, “Diverse symptoms with neurological impact,” refers to one of the specific clusters identified during the clustering process, which is characterized by a range of symptoms related to neurological conditions.

In the 1000 ICU patients used, there were a variety of admission diagnoses and categories present, covering a broad spectrum of clinical conditions. The exact number of unique admission diagnoses and categories identified in the dataset was substantial, reflecting the complexity of ICU patient populations. The hierarchical clustering algorithm grouped these diverse diagnoses into interpretable clusters. The clustering process did not simply replicate the existing categories but rather identified meaningful groupings based on the embeddings, leading to 11 distinct sets as shown in Fig. 2. These clusters represent underlying patterns and relationships within the data that may not be immediately apparent from the original categories. This process enhances our understanding of patient profiles and supports the generation of more tailored medication plans.

In this method, we used GPT-3.5 embeddings because they are known for effectively capturing detailed relationships within complex data sets. This capability is essential for understanding the intricate profiles of ICU patients. Additionally, GPT-3.5 offers a good balance of performance and computational efficiency, making it ideal for the initial clustering phase before we use the more advanced GPT-4 for subsequent tasks. Patient information was first transformed into 1536-dimensional embeddings using GPT-3 [29, 30]. These embeddings were then used as input for a hierarchical clustering algorithm, which grouped patients based on the similarity of their embeddings. These patient clusters were created by embeddings generated by ADA-002 and clustered via agglomerative clustering techniques [31] and visualized using t-SNE [32] (t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding) [33]. The intent of this embedding and clustering process was to enhance the few-shot learning methods by providing a structured means to group patients based on the similarity of clinical features. GPT embeddings can support capturing complex relationships and patterns in data, which were then used by the hierarchical clustering algorithm to identify meaningful patient clusters: this approach helped in refining the model’s responses and ensuring that the generated medication plans were contextually relevant and clinically accurate.

Dynamic context and iterative prompt optimization

Prompting involves giving the LLM a specific input to elicit a relevant response, while in-context learning uses examples within the prompt to guide the model’s response. These concepts are central to AI’s ability to understand and generate text but also present challenges in optimizing prompt effectiveness and understanding the limitations of the model’s adaptability to new tasks. Optimizing prompting techniques can support LLM refinement without requiring additional training data or fine-tuning.

In this study, the 1000 ICU patient data was used solely as example prompts to interact with the LLM, demonstrating the model how to respond to specific clinical scenarios and what outputs were expected. There was no pre-training or retraining involved; instead, the base-model’s pre-existing knowledge was leveraged and refined through these prompts. In the iterative optimization process, the goal was to enhance the performance of the model when generating various patient-related outputs, such as prediction of mortality and APACHE II score as well as generation of the medication plan at 24 h. This dynamic context iterative optimization process is based on an iterative feedback loop, which adjusts the input prompts provided to the model based on its performance in previous iterations. Supplemental Fig. 2 outlines the algorithm for this iterative optimization process. Initially, a dynamic prompt containing N similar patient cases based on the variables age, sex, admission diagnosis and category, and ICD-10 based problem list to the GPT-4 model was used. Then, the ROUGE (Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation) score was calculated by comparing the model-generated output with the given example cases. The ROUGE score is an evaluation metric widely used in the field of NLP, especially in text summarization and machine translation. It is mainly used to evaluate the quality of automatically generated summaries or translations. The core idea of ROUGE is to evaluate candidate abstracts by comparing them to a set of reference abstracts (usually human-written) by measuring overlap of n-grams (or word sequences that appear in both summaries) with recall defined as number of n-grams appearing in both summaries divided by total number of n-grams in the reference summary. Responses exceeding the ROUGE threshold were considered positive, while those falling below were considered negative. Both positive and negative outputs were incorporated into the iterative prompt for subsequent cycles. The input prompt was modified for each iteration with the goal of improving the score. This was achieved by encouraging the model to produce a better response: “This is a good example, try to generate a better output” or by discouraging the model from producing the same type of output: “This is a bad example, try to generate a better output”, respectively. The iterative process continued until a predetermined number of iterations was reached. This approach enables GPT-4 to refine its responses throughout the iterative process, increasingly aligning with positive responses while avoiding negative ones. Thus, GPT-4 can achieve self-optimization through iteration.

Medication regimen generation

We employed GPT-4 to generate medication plans for ICU patients. Through the optimization method described, GPT-4 learned to improve its predictions and recommendations over time, as it was continually guided by the evaluation score and feedback from previous iterations. This approach enabled GPT-4 to adapt and refine its output based on a better understanding of the patient’s condition and the desired outcomes. The final prompt used can be seen in Supplemental Fig. 3.

These medications were then compared to the ground truth, defined as those medication plans actually prescribed at the 24 h using ROUGE scores. The ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2, and ROUGE-L were calculated. ROUGE-1 focuses on the overlap of words (1-grams), and ROUGE-2 is based on the overlap of bigrams (2-grams). ROUGE-L focuses on the Longest Common Subsequence (LCS). ROUGE-L does not require consecutive word sequence matching, but rather identifies the longest sequence of sequential matching words between the candidate abstract and the reference abstract.

Severity of illness and outcome prediction

GPT-4 and ChatGPT were queried to predict hospital mortality and severity of illness score (as measured by APACHE II) based on patient information including age, sex, admission diagnosis and category, and ICD-10 based problem list. APACHE II scores were categorized as < 10, 10–20, 21–30, or > 31. Prediction was measured using accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score. Accuracy is the ratio of correctly classified instances to the total number of instances. Precision focuses on the proportion of instances where the model predicts a positive outcome that actually turns out to be positive and is a measure of the accuracy of positive class predictions. Recall is the model’s ability to correctly identify positive classes and is the proportion of correct identifications by the model from all actual positive examples. The F1 score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, and it attempts to find a balance between the two. In an attempt to improve mortality prediction, different prompts were used including Rand-5, Freq-5, Bcat-rand-5, and Sim 5. Rand-5 randomly selects five demonstrations from the training set and incorporates them into the prompt. Freq-5 randomly selects one demonstration from the top five most frequent diagnosis categories. Bcat-rand-5 randomly chooses five samples with the same diagnosis category as the tested sample. Sim-5 selects the five most similar patient information records as demonstrations. Specifically, Freq-5, Bcat-rand-5, and Sim-5 attempted to align the diagnosis categories of the test set with those demonstrated in the prompts.

Discussion

Here, we present the first study to apply LLMs to a range of clinically significant problems in the realm of comprehensive medication management in the ICU using a novel training approach [26–28]. A dynamic prompting and iterative optimization approach that leverages the in-context learning capability of LLMs [29] by constructing dynamic contexts using domain-specific data was developed [34–38]. This method enabled the model to acquire contextual knowledge from semantically similar examples in existing data and improve its performance [29, 39]. This engineering approach is broadly applicable, with results showing potential LLMs to be used in highly specialized domains marked by technical language, if they are provided with carefully designed context and sample selection. This approach is promising given the specialization implicit in the ICU medication context, coupled with ‘life or death’ decision-making, and technical language not robustly represented in the original training corpus.

To enhance the quantitative evaluation of our model’s outputs, future work could transform these outputs into tabular data. This transformation would enable a more direct comparison of dosage and active principles. For instance, by comparing the generated medication regimens with the ground truth in a structured format, we could employ statistical methods to evaluate the accuracy and precision of specific dosage recommendations and active ingredient selections. This approach could provide a more objective assessment of the model’s performance and improve the appropriateness of current evaluation methods. Medications may require novel metric development for proper evaluation of LLMs. In this case, despite the scores being relatively low (as defined by ROUGE), upon expert review, it was notable that the regimens generated had correct combinations of medications, doses, frequency, and routes and had resemblance to regimens seen in the ICU setting. This ability to correctly concatenate alphanumeric combinations is notable. A novel metric (medication regimen complexity-intensive care unit, MRC-ICU) has been developed to meaningfully capture data about critically ill patient’s medications, similar to how SOFA or APACHE II work for severity of illness [26, 28, 40–53]. Development of this score was necessitated by the unique nature of medication data, and so too, a possibility exists that traditional NLP evaluation metrics are not a good measure of the effectiveness of the NLP algorithm for these tasks. Common NLP tasks include question answering (QA) and summarization, while specialized tasks might involve AI-generated prescription plans and outcome predictions [3]. Traditional NLP evaluation metrics, such as n-gram-based ROUGE scores [54] and METEOR scores [55], prioritize similarity between generated and reference texts rather than accuracy, safety, and adherence to clinical guidelines. Future metrics could assess the correctness of AI-generated prescriptions or the alignment of predicted outcomes with actual patient outcomes, considering sensitivity, specificity, and overall accuracy. Defining appropriate NLP tasks and developing tailored evaluation metrics for medication applications is necessary for the successful integration of LLMs in this domain and warrants future clinician collaboration and research.

To train AI models, high quality datasets are needed. In this case, low precision and recall scores are potentially due to the imbalanced dataset (e.g., alive patients outnumbering deceased patients by a 9 to 1 ratio). This imbalance has a tendency for the model to be biased towards the majority class, leading to predicting more patients as non-deceased: the result of this tends to be lower precision and F1 score (which balances precision and recall). Though the various prompts were designed to improve prediction performance, future analyses will require larger datasets with more balanced outcomes of interest. While it cannot be ruled out that performance is not due to the nature of the LLM itself (including its lack of domain specific training for ICU medications), future work will involve addressing this data imbalance (e.g., synthetic data generation, which has been successfully shown to improve outcome imbalance in ICU patient populations) and exploring other evaluation metrics better suited for imbalanced datasets to gain a more comprehensive understanding of model performance [45]. While this study used a one-of-a-kind dataset that incorporates comprehensive medication data for ICU patients, it is still relatively small by comparative AI standards. Furthermore, standardizing drug descriptions, including dosage numbers and frequencies, is crucial for creating an LLM-friendly datasets for medications. This standardization can help eliminate ambiguities and inconsistencies in the data, ensuring that LLMs can accurately interpret and generate relevant information about medications. Moreover, the inclusion of drug interactions, contraindications, and side effects can further enhance the LLM’s ability to provide safe and effective medication recommendations. The first steps towards standardizing ICU medication data for AI have begun [28]. Additionally, incorporating diverse data sources (e.g., electronic health records, clinical trial results, and relevant scientific literature), can provide LLMs with a more comprehensive understanding of the pharmacy domain. By integrating these diverse sources of information, LLMs can develop a more robust knowledge base, enabling them to generate better-informed responses and recommendations for various pharmacy tasks. Our study has demonstrated that LLMs can provide meaningful suggestions and identify patterns in complex ICU patient data, but they possess limitations that underscore the continued importance of human expertise in healthcare and careful evaluation of current capabilities. LLMs struggle to comprehend complex clinical information or produce nuanced and detailed clinical descriptions [56]. These models are primarily trained on textual data and may not have sufficient exposure to specialized medical terminology, complex concepts, or rare conditions, which are vital for effective decision-making in healthcare [56, 57]. Another area where LLMs face challenges (to some degree) is in interpreting time series data and numerical data [58–60]. The dynamic nature of healthcare particularly the ICU, where clinical status can change in minutes, often requires an understanding of changes in a patient’s condition over time and the ability to analyze numerical information, such as laboratory test results and vital signs. LLMs currently lack the sophistication to fully grasp these aspects of patient care. Training LLMs on domain-specific patient data is an established strategy for enhancing their performance in specialized domains [34, 47]. This targeted training described here has potential to lead to improvements in the model’s ability to generate relevant, accurate, and contextually appropriate responses for various medication tasks. Second, refining the model architecture to better align with the specific tasks and objectives in medication management may also lead to improvements. By designing customized architectures that cater to the unique requirements of medication applications, LLMs can become more effective in addressing domain-specific challenges [47]. This process may involve the development of task-specific layers, attention mechanisms, or other architectural components that are tailored to the needs of pharmacy-related tasks [37].

This type of domain specific training in the form of iterative prompts and domain specific reasoning engines has been undertaken in areas including radiation oncology (e.g., RadiologyGPT and RadOncGPT) [61, 62]. ImpressionGPT constructs dynamic prompts using similar medical reports as examples to guide impression generation from findings in the radiology reports [63]. ImpressionGPT also employs an iterative prompt optimization strategy that gives feedback to ChatGPT on its generated text to incrementally improve results. For clinical trial recruitment from radiology reports, the LLM integrated medical knowledge graphs and policy-based selection of logical reasoning examples into prompts. Both of these methods demonstrated the potential of careful prompt design and in-context learning to develop specialized capabilities in general purpose language models, even in the settings of minimal training data, that can be extended to other medical applications.

Every year, more than 4 billion prescription medications are dispensed in the United States, 1 billion medications are prescribed at physician office visits, and 350 million medications are given in hospital emergency departments [64]. This prodigious volume of medication use is unfortunately matched by high rates of medication errors, ADEs, and other unintended medication-related harm [65]. In the ICU, critical care pharmacists reduce ADEs by up to 70%, which improves patient-centered care, and reduces healthcare costs [25, 66]. Actions to prevent ADEs, improve resource utilization, and individualize patient care have demonstrated an estimated cost avoidance to pharmacist salary ratio of $3.3-$9.6:1 in the ICU setting [67, 68]. In the absence of sufficient clinical pharmacists (e.g., due to personnel or financial resource constraints), AI is a promising strategy to increase access to high quality medication expertise. Existing clinical decision support systems (CDSS) can reduce medication decision-making to one-size-fits-all rules based on rudimentary inputs (e.g., black box warnings in the package insert), which limits the ability to provide expert-level, individualized care. LLMs show the unique potential to process medication-related data, including handling alphanumeric combinations germane to the medication context that would be otherwise unsuitable or require extensive data cleaning. To date, no other technology has shown this potential, which has severely limited how medication data are incorporated into AI algorithms. This study marks the first steps to scoping how to apply LLMs to this much needed healthcare space; however, limitations are present, including the difficulty of establishing ‘ground truth’ of the most correct medication regimen. An ideal ‘ground truth’ scenario would be prospective enrollment of patients undergoing comprehensive medication management (CMM) by an expert, multiprofessional team with independent review for lack of medication errors and established positive outcomes for those patients. As such, high quality datasets would include this type of prospective validation in addition to granular patient information including temporal flowsheet data, laboratory data, and medication data in addition to patient outcomes. Such datasets would ideally be multi-center to maximize generalizability, though this requires the thoughtful use of common data models and potentially extensive data pre-processing. Additionally, while few-shot learning is a straightforward methodology to leverage pre-existing knowledge in LLMs, ultimately other fine-tuning methods for LLMs may be useful, including supervised fine-tuning, reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), and continual learning, particularly if combined with the aforementioned high quality datasets. Moreover, identifying strategies that allow for the model to retain learning (e.g., embedding persistent memory or leveraging a fine-tuning approach that allows the model to retain learning from previous interactions) are important steps to establish a clinical implementable form of PharmacyGPT. While ultimately, this type of rigor in conjunction with user-centered design and robust implementation science methodology is necessary for the clinical translation of LLM, this process is resource and time intensive and necessitates these scoping studies to understand strengths, limitations, and opportunities.

Conclusion

This paper introduces PharmacyGPT, which applies LLMs to a range of medication-related problems. The potential for improved patient outcomes and streamlined medication use is considerable. Future focus on the incorporation of expert knowledge, carefully designed training datasets, and development of tailored metrics for model evaluation is essential. By establishing a foundation for future research and development, PharmacyGPT has the potential to enhance the overall quality of medication use, while also contributing to a deeper understanding and more effective use of LLMs in specialized domains.
