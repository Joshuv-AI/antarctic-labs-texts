Signatures of local adaptation and maladaptation to future climate in wild Zizania latifolia
Authors: Yang Zou, Weidong Yang, Ruxue Zhang, Xinwei Xu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlobal climate change poses challenges to agricultural production and food security. Assessing the adaptive capacity of crop wild relatives to future climate is important for protecting key germplasm resources and breeding new crops. We performed population genomics, genotype-environment association analyses, and genomic offset assessment of Chinese wild rice, Zizania latifolia, a crop wild relative and potential new grain crop, based on 168 individuals from 42 populations. We found two genetic lineages in Z. latifolia, corresponding to the south and north of its range, that diverged during the Late Pleistocene. We also identified lineage-specific positively selected genes associated with flower development and flowering, seed shattering, pathogen defense response and cold tolerance. We further found that populations from southeastern China are the most maladapted to future climate and should be prioritized for conservation. Our findings provide important clues for leveraging existing genetic diversity to identify important germplasm resources and create climate-resilient crops.Subject terms: Molecular ecology, Evolutionary ecology

Introduction

Global climate change is exacerbating plant stress, limiting agricultural production and threatening global food security1. Integrating adaptive genes into crop varieties is an effective strategy for improving crop productivity2. Compared to cultivated plants, their wild relatives harbor higher genetic diversity and exhibit stronger environmental adaptability, making them invaluable repositories of natural genetic resources for breeding programs3. In addition to breeding crop varieties, another way to alleviate the global food crisis is through redomestication and de novo domestication of wild plants into new crops4. Although tens of thousands of plant species are edible to humans, only about 2,500 species from 170 taxonomic families have undergone some degree of domestication. Of these, fewer than 300 are considered to be fully domesticated5,6. The inclusion of neglected or underutilized plants in crop production systems may maintain food security and a more sustainable low-input agriculture by improving biodiversity and climate adaptability7.

Chinese wild rice (Zizania latifolia) is a perennial aquatic grass native to East Asia that belongs to the Oryzeae tribe within the Gramineae family (Supplementary Fig. 1a). It has an outcrossing mating system and well-developed rhizomes, and can reproduce both sexually through seeds and asexually through rhizomes and tiller buds8. It has historical significance as one of the most important cereals in ancient China from the Zhou Dynasty to the Tang Dynasty (from 771 BC to 907 AD)9. However, Z. latifolia has been cultivated as an aquatic vegetable for more than 2000 years because the young shoots of the plant become swollen, soft and edible after being infected by the fungus Ustilago esculenta (Supplementary Fig. 1b)9. The presence of this fungus prevents the cultivar from flowering, and asexual propagation via infected rhizomes is the only means of cultivar production10,11. After continuous long-standing endophyte infection, a significantly smaller repertoire of immune receptors was found in cultivated Z. latifolia compared to wild Z. latifolia11. Wild Z. latifolia is highly resistant to fungal infection, as evidenced by the fact that only five infected samples were found in 824 samples from 32 range-wide wild populations12. Zizania latifolia is also used as a tertiary gene pool for rice Oryza sativa, and gene transfer between them requires advanced techniques such as embryo rescue, somatic fusion, or genetic engineering13. The successful example of somatic hybridization between rice and Z. latifolia and the valuable traits of Z. latifolia for rice breeding14 give us a fact that could provide important genomic resources for rice improvement, facilitating the development of improved rice varieties with enhanced adaptability to diverse environmental challenges.

Wild Z. latifolia is also a potential new grain crop. Northern wild rice Zizania palustris, a close relative of Z. latifolia, has been domesticated as a commercial grain crop in North America in last century15. In addition, the high quality of Zizania grains16 and the elite agronomic traits found in Z. latifolia, disease and insect resistance, cold and flooding tolerance, and perenniality14,17 together suggest its high potential as a new grain crop. Breeding perennial food crops that do not require replanting after each harvest is a novel approach to addressing food security and environmental challenges18. Domestication of wild Z. latifolia has been attempted, but good target traits such as seed non-shattering have not yet been achieved and need to be improved in the future19.

Identifying genes associated with environmental adaptation in crop wild relatives can facilitate the development of improved crop varieties20. Predicting future climate-induced vulnerability will provide important clues for identifying germplasm resources that need priority conservation21. In this study, based on the recently published 547.38 Mb chromosome-level genome of Z. latifolia22, we combined whole-genome resequencing and environmental data to investigate local adaptation and future climate-induced vulnerability in wild Z. latifolia, a crop wild relative and potential new grain crop in East Asia. We performed whole-genome resequencing for 168 individuals of wild Z. latifolia collected from 42 populations across its range in China. We first inferred the genomic variation and demographic history of Z. latifolia. We then revealed signatures of local adaptation by identifying environment-associated genetic variants. Finally, we assessed the genomic offset of populations under future climate change. Our findings present a case study of using genomic data to detect genetic signatures of local adaptation and predict maladaptation to future climate, which can provide important information for utilization of crop wild relatives and domestication of potential new crops.

Results

Whole-genome resequencing and SNP calling

A total of 1.14 Tb of whole genome sequencing data were generated for the 168 individuals from 42 sampling locations of Z. latifolia across its distribution range in China (Fig. 1a). The raw reads were aligned to the chromosome-level genome of Z. latifolia as a reference22, resulting in a mapping rate of 99.72 ± 0.2% and an average sequencing depth of 12.4-fold (Supplementary Data 1). After stringent variant calling and filtering, a set of 3,695,324 high-quality SNPs was identified for further analysis. A survey of these SNPs in the Z. latifolia genome revealed that approximately 61.2% were located in intergenic regions and 4.0% in coding sequences (Supplementary Table 1).

Population structure

The admixture analysis showed that the 17 populations from the northern region always formed a separate genetic group when the K value changed from two to five, whereas when the 25 populations from the southern region divided into two, three, and four genetic groups, some populations were indecisive and grouped with different populations (Fig. 1b). When combined with the phylogenetic tree, only the genetic grouping of K = 2 was consistent with the topology of the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 1b, c). Therefore, the 42 Z. latifolia populations were divided into two genetic lineages reflecting geographical divergence, the Northern lineage and the Southern lineage. The principal component analysis (PCA) plot also supported the division into two groups (Fig. 1d). The northern and southern lineages exhibited similar levels of nucleotide diversity, with π values of 0.17 × 10−3 and 0.19 × 10−3, respectively, and showed low levels of genetic divergence between them, with an average genome-wide FST value of 0.092 (Fig. 1e). In addition, the decay of linkage disequilibrium (LD), as indicated by the decrease of r2 to half of its maximum value, showed a similar result in both lineages (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Demographic history

To explore the demographic history of Z. latifolia, we randomly selected one individual from each of the 42 populations (25 from the southern lineage and 17 from the northern lineage) for PSMC analysis. The effective population size (Ne) of both lineages increased from 0.6 to 0.2 Mya, decreased between 0.2 and 0.05 Ma, declined slowly until 0.015 Ma, and then increased slightly afterword (Fig. 2a). Furthermore, using the diffusion approximation method of δaδi, we evaluated 28 models with various gene flow scenarios and population size changes (Supplementary Fig. 3) to unravel the divergence history between the two lineages. Among them, model 7 showed the best fit based on the AIC values (Supplementary Table 2). The comparison of the simulated spectrum generated by the best-fit model with the observed spectrum verified the accuracy of our calculations (Supplementary Fig. 4). According to the best-fit model, the ancestral population with Ne of 1163 diverged into two lineages at approximately 0.095 Ma, with continuous asymmetric migration from the southern lineage to the northern lineage persisting throughout their historical divergence to the present. In addition, both lineages underwent a change in population size around 1200 years ago, with the southern lineage decreasing significantly, Ne from 40082 to 2620, and the northern lineage increasing slightly, Ne from 877 to 1375 (Fig. 2b).

Genes under positive selection

We performed both FST and XP-CLR tests to identify genes under positive selection in two lineages (Fig. 3a, b). In the southern lineage, a total of 390 positively selected genes (PSGs) were identified simultaneously using these two methods. Homologs of these genes in rice were used to elucidate their functions, and some candidate domestication-related genes were found (Supplementary Data 2). For example, ZlaHD1 (Zla03G004250) on chromosome 3R was an ortholog of the rice OsHD1 gene, which is related to flowering time23. The region containing ZlaHD1 showed significant differences between the two lineages (FST = 0.388, XP-CLR = 693.391). We also found that the region containing ZlaSSH1 (Zla09G002020) (FST = 0.295, XP-CLR = 401.25), an ortholog of the rice APETALA2-like transcription factor associated with seed shattering and seed size24. The LD heat map showed that these gene regions were highly linked (Fig. 3c). In addition, positive selection was evident for ZlaFTIP1 (Zla03G007370), which may affect flowering time; ZlaMADS4 (Zla13G005330), which plays an important role in floral development, especially lodicule development; and ZlaACS2 (Zla07G007960) and ZlaBBD1 (Zla02G020150), which are involved in pathogen defense response25–29.

In the northern lineage, only 77 PSGs were simultaneously identified by both FST and XP-CLR methods. Due to the limited number of PSGs, orthologous genes in the well-studied model plant Arabidopsis thaliana were used to elucidate their functions (Supplementary Data 3). Notably, among them, ZlaBRI1 (Zla01G014490), ZlaSIG5 (Zla01G031640), ZlaATTLP1 (Zla02G028110), ZlaACA8 (Zla06G012810), and ZlaADA2B (Zla09G005140) were identified as genes involved in response to cold stress30–34.

Environment-associated genetic loci

The Mantel test revealed significant positive correlations between genetic and geographic distances (r = 0.348, p = 0.001) and between genetic and environmental distances (r = 0.276, p = 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 5). The results of the MMRR analysis indicated that the effects of environmental factors were significant (p = 0.0157), whereas the effects of geographic factors were not significant (p = 0.4448). LFMM was used to explore genome-environment associations for 19 environmental variables (Supplementary Table 3). A total of 223,507 SNPs in 18,532 genes were identified as being significantly associated with at least one environmental variable. These genes included all of the above PSGs identified by both FST and XP-CLR methods. Four uncorrelated environmental variables with pairwise correlation coefficients |r| < 0.8 (Supplementary Fig. 6), namely Mean Diurnal Range (BIO2), Isothermality (BIO3), Max Temperature of Warmest Month (BIO5), and Precipitation Seasonality (BIO15), were selected for further analysis using RDA to identify genetic loci potentially associated with multivariate environmental predictors. The RDA analysis identified 10,355 SNPs in 1678 genes. The final selection of environment-associated genes was determined by comparing the results of the LFMM and RDA approaches, resulting in a total of 1451 genes identified simultaneously by both approaches. GO enrichment analysis revealed that these genes were involved in the regulation of various functions, including response to redox state, pollen development, and fruit development, and others (Supplementary Fig. 7 and Supplementary Data 4).

Genomic offset to climate change

We chose predictions of RONA for two environmental variables (Max Temperature of Warmest Month, BIO5, and Precipitation Seasonality, BIO15) as a representative outcome. As expected, for BIO5, RONA values of each population increased under more severe climate change scenarios with higher emissions (SSP370) (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Figs. 8, 9). In contrast to the observed RONA trends in response to temperature changes, RONA values associated with the precipitation-related variable BIO15 were relatively small, and not all populations’ RONA values increased under SSP370 (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Figs. 8, 9).

The predicted patterns of genetic offsets inferred by the gradient forest (GF) approach throughout the range of Z. latifolia in China were similar under SSP126 and SSP370, although a bit higher genomic offset occurred under SSP370 (Fig. 5a, b and Supplementary Fig. 10). The results for all three time periods showed that southeastern China had high local offsets (Fig. 5a, b and Supplementary Fig. 10), indicating that populations in this region are more vulnerable to climate change than those in other regions. This region also showed high reverse offsets (Fig. 5c, d and Supplementary Fig. 11), indicating that all populations throughout the current range of Z. latifolia in China may not be able to adapt to the future climate of southeastern China. The estimated forward offsets were generally low across the range of Z. latifolia in China, except for the northern part of northeastern China and a few small marginal patches of the current range of Z. latifolia in China, and similar patterns were shown for different dispersal constraints of the maximum allowable migration (Fig. 5c, d and Supplementary Fig. 12).

We identified three categories of genetic load, missense, synonymous, and LOF, across populations and found that populations from the northern lineage had higher genetic loads, on average, compared with populations from the southern lineage (Supplementary Fig. 13). The XZN population from the northern lineage had the highest genetic load, while the YY population from the southern lineage had the lowest genetic load (Supplementary Fig. 13). No correlation was observed between genetic load and genetic offset of populations (including local, forward, and reverse offsets) (Supplementary Figs. 14 and 15).

Discussion

Lineage divergence is commonly presented in widespread plant species35–37. In Chinese wild rice, two lineages corresponding to the northern and southern regions were identified in our study (Fig. 2), which is similar to previous studies based on microsatellite markers12,38. Going further than previous studies, we inferred the divergence time and demographic history of lineages. Based on the PSMC analysis, the population dynamics of this species indicated a continuous decrease in effective population size during the Late Pleistocene, leading to the divergence of the southern and northern lineages, which occurred at approximately 0.095 Ma, as inferred by the δaδi analysis (Fig. 2). The δaδi analysis also showed that the southern lineage experienced a drastic decline around 1200 years ago. This may be due to the fact that during the Tang Dynasty (618–907 AD), with the population explosion in the south and the great agricultural development, the promotion of lake reclamation and rice cultivation led to a drastic reduction of suitable habitats for the Chinese wild rice9. It is noteworthy that the level of genetic differentiation between the northern and southern lineages is low (FST = 0.092), which is also supported by the occurrence of some individuals with admixed genetic components (Fig. 1b) and the persisting gene flow between the two lineages throughout their historical divergence to the present (Fig. 2b). There are no obvious geographical barriers (e.g., high mountains) in the area between the two lineages (approximately latitude 38° N). Genetic exchange across this area has been demonstrated in other aquatic plants, supported by the occurrence of a single genetic lineage across this area, e.g. Sparganium stoloniferum39, Hydrilla verticillate40 and Myriophyllum spicatum10.

Natural selection plays a pivotal role in driving the evolutionary processes, divergence and local adaptation within species41. The Chinese wild rice exhibits a wide distribution across China, with our sampling encompassing latitudes ranging from 21° N to 49° N. The sampled environments vary considerably, with altitudes ranging from 550 to 3919 m (Supplementary Data 1), annual mean temperatures from 0.2 to 23.3 °C, and annual precipitation from 424 mm to 2041 mm. The MMRR analyses indicate that environmental factors have a greater influence on genetic divergence among Z. latifolia populations than geographic distance. To cope with diverse environments, the two lineages of Z. latifolia from the southern and northern regions have undergone adaptive evolution, resulting in shifts in allele frequencies at numerous loci. Using FST and XP-CLR methods, we identified 390 protein-coding genes that have undergone positive selection in the southern lineage. Among these genes, several genes (ZlaHD1, ZlaFTIP1, ZlaMADS4 and ZlaSSH1) associated with flower development, flowering, and seed shattering23,24,27,28 are potential target genes for the domestication of wild Z. latifolia as a new grain crop. For example, elimination of seed shattering is a primary focus of domestication of Z. latifolia, and its domestication will likely be accelerated via mutagenesis of ZlaSSH1, similar to what was done for Microlaena stipoides, an Australian wild relative of rice42. In addition, Z. latifolia as a natural genetic resource for rice breeding, the adaptive loci involved in pathogen defense response identified in the southern lineage and those related to cold tolerance identified in the northern lineage can contribute to rice breeding efforts. There are other genetic resources worthy of further exploration in Z. latifolia, such as finding high-altitude adaptation genes in the southern lineage, whose populations ranged from 623 m to 3919 m (Supplementary Data 1).

In addition to reflecting the history of adaptive evolution, the genetic structure of local adaptation also determines the response of populations to climate change43. The higher climate change maladaptation of Z. latifolia to temperature-related variables than to precipitation-related variables measured by RONA (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Figs. 8, 9) suggests that temperature is a more important environmental factor for aquatic plants. High importance of temperature-related factors for species distribution and population differentiation was also observed in other aquatic plants, two Myriophyllum species, two Stuckenia species and two Ranunculus species44, and two Oryza species45. When predicted by the GF approach, we found that populations located in southeastern China exhibited larger local genetic offsets than other areas, indicated a higher risk of maladaptation, which may be due to more drastic environmental changes in temperature-related variables in this area (Fig. 4a). The same reason may also be used to explain the high reverse genetic offset in southeastern China, indicating that the genetic variation currently available in the range of Z. latifolia in China may not be able to adapt to future climatic conditions in this area. The generally low forward offsets across the range of Z. latifolia in China indicates that most populations of Z. latifolia could minimize future maladaptation by migrating to new locations with suitable environments in the face of climate change. Migration may be facilitated by high levels of gene flow in Z. latifolia, supported by the low levels of population differentiation observed in this study. Based on the above results, we can conclude that most populations except for those from southeastern China are suitable as potential candidates for domestication into climate-resilient crops, and populations from southeastern China are in need of ex situ conservation collections to capture genetic diversity because of their high genomic offset to future climate change and unique genetic resources. Given that genetic offset is an actively developing field undergoing experimental validation46, and offset models assume that current genotype-environment associations are solely caused by local adaptation and will remain optimal in the future, and ignore other dynamics, such as gene flow of adaptive alleles, that could alter population maladaptation to future climate47, genetic offset estimates should be used cautiously to guide management decisions. Essential future work includes monitoring populations for climate change and common garden experiments for evaluating risk of maladaptation. We also examined whether the populations with higher genetic offset have increased genetic load and found no significant correlation between them, in contrast to the expectation that populations with high genetic load should be more vulnerable to climate change48. Similar results have been observed in other species21, which is not surprising because genetic offset and genetic load are estimated based on different sets of SNPs43.

In this study, based on population-level whole-genome resequencing data, we explored the local adaptation and maladaptation to climate change in Chinese wild rice, a crop wild relative and potential new grain crop in East Asia. We revealed two genetic lineages corresponding to the south and north of its geographical distribution. We also identified lineage-specific positively selected genes associated with local adaptation. By modeling the association between adaptive SNPs and environmental variables, we found that populations located in southeastern China are most maladapted under future climate scenarios. Overall, this study provides a genome-wide view of local adaptation and future climate-induced maladaptation in Chinese wild rice. Such information is key to protecting important germplasm resources and promoting the development of climate-resilient cultivars.

Methods

Sample collection, sequencing and variant calling

In this study, leaf material was collected from 168 individuals representing 42 wild populations of Z. latifolia in different basins encompassing its range in China. These populations thrived in diverse water bodies, including rivers, streams, marshes, and ditches. Random sampling was carried out with a minimum distance of 10 meters between collected plants to avoid sampling ramets connected by rhizomes from the same colony. The number of individuals sampled from each population ranged from two to six, depending on population size. The genomes of the 168 individuals were sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome of Z. latifolia22 using Bwa-mem v.0.7.1749 with default parameters. The resulting sequence alignment files were sorted and compressed using SAMtools v1.1350, and duplicate alignment reads were removed using Picard v1.92 (http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/). Raw single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were identified using the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK, v.4.2.0)51 and its subcomponents HaplotypeCaller, CombineGVCFs, and GenotypeGVCFs. Subsequently, a merged variant call format (VCF) file was generated. SNPs were filtered based on the following criteria: QD < 2.0 (filters out low-quality variants relative to coverage depth), MQ < 40.0 (excludes variants with poor alignment quality), FS > 60.0 (detects strand bias in variant representation), SOR > 3.0 (further identifies strand bias), MQRankSum < −12.5 (indicates lower mapping quality for alternate alleles), ReadPosRankSum < −8.0 (highlights variants often found at the ends of reads, suggesting artifacts). A core SNP dataset was defined by removing SNPs with more than two alleles, > 10% missing calls, and MAF < 5%. The annotation of SNPs was performed using SnpEff v 4.3t52. The final dataset comprised 3,695,324 high-quality SNPs across 168 individuals for subsequent analyses.

Population structure and phylogenetic analysis

The core SNP dataset was pre-processed by linkage disequilibrium pruning using Plink v1.9053 with a window size of 50 SNPs, a step size of 10 SNPs, and an r2 threshold of 0.05. This procedure resulted in a dataset of 608,507 SNPs, which was used for population structure and phylogenetic analysis. Principal component analysis (PCA) performed in Plink and ADMIXTURE v1.354 with default parameters were used to investigate the genetic structure of the population. In addition, the NJ phylogenetic tree was constructed based on the distance matrix using MEGAX55 and displayed using iTOL v.1.4.4 (http://itol.embl.de).

Genetic diversity, linkage disequilibrium and demographic history analysis

We calculated nucleotide diversity (π) for each lineage and genetic divergence (FST) between lineages using VCFtools v0.1.1356 with 100-kb sliding windows and a 20-kb step. Linkage disequilibrium (LD) decay for each lineage was identified for all pairs of SNPs using PopLDdecay v3.4157. The fluctuation of effective population size over time in two lineages of Z. latifolia was inferred using the Pairwise Sequential Markovian Coalescence (PSMC) model58 with the following parameter settings: -N25 -t15 -r5 -p “4 + 25*2 + 4 + 6” (where -N25 sets 25 iterations, -t15 sets the initial mutation rate to 15, -r5 sets the initial recombination rate to 5, and -p “4 + 25*2 + 4 + 6” defines time intervals with 4 fixed, 50 doubling, 4 fixed, and 6 fixed intervals), mutation rate of 1.3 × 10−8 mutations per site per year59, and one generation per year. We ran 100 iterations of PSMC to obtain 95% confidence intervals for demographic parameters. In addition, we tested alternative demographic histories of the species using the diffusion approximation method implemented in δaδi v.1.7.060, based on the analysis of the two-dimensional joint site frequency spectrum (2D-JSFS). Folded allele-frequency spectra were generated from the pruned linkage disequilibrium SNP dataset using easySFS (https://github.com/isaacovercast/easySFS) for all 2D-JSFS analyses. The optimization function involved four rounds of 10, 20, 30, and 40 replications each, with increasing the maximum iterations (3, 5, 10, and 15) and decreasing the fold-in parameter generation (3, 2, 2, and 1), resulting in 100 replications. Thirty models were generated to simulate different demographic scenarios for the two lineages, and the model with the best Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) was considered the best-fitting model.

Genes under positive selection

We used both a cross-population composite likelihood ratio (XP-CLR) test61 and VCFtools to identify genes under positive selection in each lineage. The XP-CLR scores were calculated by calculating population fixation statistics (FST) using 100-kb sliding windows with a 20-kb step. VCFtools was used to detect potential selective signals between lineages (genetic differentiation) using 100-kb sliding windows and a 20-kb step. The top 1% windows identified by both tests were considered as putative positively selected genes (PSGs) for each lineage. The LD blocks around genes under positive selection signals were further evaluated by calculating r2 between SNPs using PLINK and visualized using the R package LDheatmap62.

Identification of environment-associated genetic variants

Two methods were employed to investigate environment-associated genetic loci: latent factor linear mixed model (LFMM) and redundancy analysis (RDA). LFMM can efficiently estimate random effects caused by population history and isolation by distance when computing gene-environment correlations, and reduce the number of false-positive associations63, and RDA is one of the best-performing multivariant approaches for gene-environment associations and shows low false-positive rates64. LFMM, implemented in the R package LEA v3.3.263, was used to explore associations between allele frequencies and 19 environmental variables (resolution 2.5 arcmin) for the period 1970-2000 downloaded from the WorldClim v2.1 CMIP6 website (http://www.worldclim.org/). A false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of < 5% was applied as the significance cut-off. RDA was performed using the R package VEGAN65, taking into account four uncorrelated environmental variables (BIO2, BIO3, BIO5, and BIO15) with pairwise correlation coefficients |r| < 0.8. Outlier loci were identified using a cut-off based on standard deviation. Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was then performed on candidate genes by identifying significant GO terms (P < 0.05).

Mantel tests were performed to assess correlations between genetic differentiation (FST/1 − FST) and geographic/environmental distances. Both environmental and geographic distances among populations were calculated using PASSaGE2 software66. The R package “ecodist”67 was used to perform the Mantel tests. In addition, the multiple matrix regression with randomization (MMRR) analysis with 10,000 permutations was used to estimate the independent effects of the factors. The MMRR analysis was performed using the R package MMRR68.

Genomic offset to future climate change

We downloaded future environmental data (spanning 2041–2060, 2061–2080, and 2081–2100) for the 19 BIOCLIM variables from the WorldClim CMIP6 dataset (BCC-CSM2-MR) at each sampling location69. These future environmental datasets included two distinct shared socioeconomic pathways (SSPs), SSP126 (low emissions scenario) and SSP370 (medium emissions scenario), for each time period. Among methods for estimating the genomic offset of populations to climate change, two predominant and widely used methods47 were selected here: Gradient Forests (GF)70 and the risk of non-adaptedness (RONA)71.

We first used PYRONA v0.4.372 to calculate RONA values, which quantify the theoretical average change in allele frequency required to adapt to projected future climate scenarios. We used linear regression to establish a linear relationship between allele frequencies at significantly associated loci (identified simultaneously by both LFMM and RDA) and environmental variables73. For each locus, population, and environmental variable, we calculated the theoretical allele frequency change required to adapt to future climate conditions (RONA). A higher RONA value indicates a greater risk of maladaptation to climate change.

We then calculated genomic offset across the range of Z. latifolia based on a nonparametric, machine-learning GF analysis using “gradientForest” in R70,74. Unlike RONA, which is calculated for individual loci under specific environmental variables, the GF method is an extension of the random forest approach that simultaneously models the relationships between the collective impacts of numerous potentially adaptive loci and multiple climate variables. The genetic offset was quantified as a Euclidean distance metric between the current genomic composition and the projected genomic composition under future climates. Following the approach of Gougherty et al.75, we incorporated migration to assess potential maladaptation to future climate change and calculated three different formulations of genetic offsets: local, forward, and reverse offsets. The local genetic offset, assuming no migration between populations, quantified the alteration in genetic composition between the present and future climates at the same geographic location. The forward genetic offset, assuming unrestricted migration within China, was determined as the minimal genetic change between the present and future climates. The reverse genetic offset was calculated by identifying the minimal genetic change for any contemporary population within the current range that most closely aligned with the projected future climate of a specific location. To visualize these offset metrics simultaneously, we represented them as the red, green, and blue bands of an RGB image21,75. For the forward genetic offset, we conducted additional sensitivity assessments to understand how it responds to dispersal constraints. We tested how the forward offset changed under different scenarios where the maximum allowable migration was restricted to various distance categories, including 100, 200, 500, and 1000 kilometers, as well as scenarios where migration was unrestricted throughout China.

Genetic load assessment

We also assessed the genetic load carried by each population, which was used to test whether it was associated with genetic offset. Genetic load is expected to interfere with the gene function and reduce the overall viability of individuals76. We used SnpEff to categorize the derived allele mutations within coding regions of each individual into synonymous, missense, and loss of function (LOF) mutations (including nonsense and splice-site disrupting single-nucleotide variants). Genotypes of major homozygous alleles (present in more than 50% of the individuals) were used to represent the ancestral state. Database was built using the annotated reference genome of Z. latifolia22, and input files in VCF format were used to annotate SNPs, assigning a mutation category to each individual SNP. The ratio of the number of homozygous sites (two per site) to both homozygous and heterozygous sites (two per homozygous site and one per heterozygous site) for each category was evaluated to estimate the deleterious load76,77. Deleterious missense mutations were designated as those with a Grantham score of 150 or more78,79. To investigate the association between genetic load and genetic offset, the Spearman’s correlation coefficients were calculated between the three metrics of genetic offset under two climate scenarios (SSP126 and SSP370) in 2061-2080 and the above proxies of genetic load across the 42 populations.
