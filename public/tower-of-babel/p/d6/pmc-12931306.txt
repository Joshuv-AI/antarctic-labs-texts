Dynamic lysine acetylation and succinylation of platelet proteins regulates platelet storage lesion: mechanistic insights from multi-omics
Authors: Xin Liao, Chenlu Zhang, Min Chen, Jianfeng Zhou, Xinpeng Luo, Yidan Zhang, Xingping Hu, Zhicheng Wang, Li Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesPlatelet storage lesion (PSL) severely impairs platelet function during storage, presenting a major hurdle in transfusion medicine; however, the dynamic interplay between global proteomic changes and post-translational modifications (PTMs) underlying these functional deteriorations remains insufficiently characterized. Here, we report the first comprehensive multi-omics analysis integrating global proteomics, acetylomics, and succinylomics to dissect the molecular dynamics during platelet storage.MethodsWe performed quantification of global proteomics, acetylome and succinylome based on TMT-labeled LC-MS/MS analysis, combined with antibody-affinity enrichment and purification. Dynamic molecular changes and functional transformation of platelet were also characterized under proper conditions stored for 1, 3, 5, 7 days, respectively.ResultsWe systematically characterized 3,609 proteins, 1,308 acetylation sites, and 1,947 succinylation sites across multiple storage time points (D1, D3, D5, D7). We distinct temporal patterns of post-translational modifications, with succinylation showing more extensive coverage than acetylation in platelets. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed extensive metabolic reprogramming involving complement activation, energy metabolism, and cellular detoxification processes. The identification of specific motif patterns provided mechanistic insights into the functional specificity of these modifications. Random forest machine learning identified 20 core regulatory proteins representing critical nodes in PSL development. Furthermore, we employed real - time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT - QPCR) to measure the expression levels of key genes related to platelet function and PTM - associated pathways.ConclusionBy mapping the interplay between proteomic abundance shifts and PTM dynamics, this study provides a multidimensional understanding of PSL, establishing a foundational framework for optimizing storage protocols and enhancing transfusion safety.Keywords: Platelet storage lesion (PSL), proteomes, acetylation, succinylation

Introduction

Platelet transfusion is a cornerstone of modern clinical medicine and a key therapeutic strategy for patients with thrombocytopenia or platelet dysfunction. The quality of platelets is a critical indicator in clinical blood transfusion practices [1]. However, platelet storage poses significant challenges in clinical applications [2]. Under room temperature conditions, platelets can only be stored for a maximum of seven days [3]. As pathogen inactivation and detection technologies advance, the primary limitation in platelet storage has shifted toward addressing platelet storage lesions (PSL) [4].

During storage, platelets undergo a series of biochemical and morphological changes collectively referred to as PSL. These alterations include increased lactate levels, decreased pH, loss of surface membrane glycoproteins (particularly glycoprotein Ib), and a reduced aggregation response to adenosine diphosphate (ADP) and collagen [3]. These changes directly impair platelet survival and hemostatic function, significantly diminishing their physiological efficacy and potentially leading to adverse outcomes such as ineffective platelet therapy, patient toxicity, or even death [5]. Therefore, employing appropriate techniques for platelet preservation and conducting a thorough analysis of the molecular changes during storage are crucial, despite the limited extent of current research in this area [6].

The global proteome—encompassing the entire set of proteins expressed in platelets—undergoes dynamic changes during storage, reflecting shifts in protein abundance that are tightly linked to functional deterioration. Characterizing these proteomic dynamics is essential for understanding the foundational molecular alterations underlying PSL, as changes in protein levels can directly impact cellular processes. Beyond such changes in protein abundance, post-translational modifications (PTMs) serve as a key mechanism to expand the functional diversity of the proteome. PTMs precisely regulate protein activity, localization, and interactions in both space and time, enabling cells to rapidly respond to environmental changes without the need for new protein synthesis [7]. This ability is particularly critical in stored platelets, where transcriptional activity is limited, and PTMs often work in concert with proteomic changes to modulate protein function. Among the various PTMs, lysine acetylation (Kac) and lysine succinylation (Ksucc) are two modifications with significant regulatory roles.

Lysine acetylation involves the covalent attachment of an acetyl group to a lysine residue, neutralizing its positive charge and inducing conformational changes, which in turn regulate protein function, stability, and protein-protein interactions [8]. This process is tightly regulated by the dynamic balance between lysine acetyltransferases (KATs) and lysine deacetylases (KDACs), forming a complex network that responds sensitively to cellular metabolic states and energy levels [9]. Lysine acetylation (Kac) has become a crucial regulatory modification in controlling key cellular processes, including metabolism, gene expression, signal transduction, and stress responses [10]. Although acetylation in platelets has received considerable attention [11,12], the dynamic changes of lysine acetylation during platelet storage have not been thoroughly investigated. Furthermore, the identification of key acetylation sites and their biological functions remain to be fully elucidated.

Lysine succinylation, a recently identified post-translational modification, involves the covalent attachment of a succinyl group to a lysine residue [13], which causes a significant reversal of its charge from positive to negative [14,15]. The regulation of this modification depends on succinyltransferases (such as KAT2A) and de-succinylases (such as SIRT5). Moreover, the level of succinylation is directly linked to the availability of succinyl-CoA and cellular metabolism, establishing a close relationship between metabolic flux and protein regulation [16]. As such, the role of succinylation in platelets warrants further investigation. However, research into its function in platelets remains limited, and further exploration is urgently needed.

Advanced mass spectrometry-based proteomics technologies have revolutionized comprehensive PTM characterization at global scales, enabling unprecedented depth and accuracy in modification site identification and quantification [17]. These technologies also empower systematic profiling of the global proteome, allowing for the quantification of thousands of proteins to capture changes in abundance during biological processes. Tandem mass tags (TMT) [18,19] have been widely employed for quantitative proteomics and protein modification analysis [20], enabling simultaneous comparison of multiple samples with exceptional precision and reproducibility while minimizing technical variation [21,22].

Building on these advanced technologies, we conducted the first comprehensive study on global proteomic changes, alongside the dynamic changes of lysine acetylation and succinylation modifications, during platelet storage. Using proteomic and modificationomic approaches, we systematically characterized dynamic protein abundance and PTM changes across multiple storage time points and employed random forest machine learning to identify key regulatory proteins. This study provides fundamental insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying PSL and establishes a framework for developing improved platelet storage strategies with significant implications for enhancing transfusion safety and efficacy.

Materials and methods

Clinical samples and platelet preparation

Platelet samples were obtained from Shanghai Blood Center and stored under standard clinical conditions [23]. Platelet concentrates were analyzed at days 1, 3, 5, and 7 of storage for all subsequent procedures. For studies involving human subjects, approval was obtained from the Huashan Hospital Institutional Review Board (HIRB), Fudan University, Shanghai, China (Approval No.:2020-720). The study period and sample collection spanned from 2021 to 2023. Written informed consent was obtained from all participating subjects prior to their enrollment in the study, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Protein extraction and trypsin digestion

Sample debris was removed by centrifugation at 20,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C, and protein concentration was determined using the BCA Quant Kit (GE Healthcare Life Sciences, Beijing, China) according to manufacturer’s instructions. For digestion, protein solutions were reduced with 10 mM DL-dithiothreitol (DTT) (Sigma-Aldrich, Shanghai, China) for 1 h at 37 °C and alkylated with 20 mM iodoacetamide (IAA) (Sigma-Aldrich, Shanghai, China) for 45 min at room temperature in darkness. Protein samples were diluted with 100 mM tetraethylammonium tetrahydroborate (TEAB) to reduce urea concentration below 2 M. Trypsin (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) was added at a 1:50 trypsin-to-protein mass ratio for overnight digestion, followed by a second 4-hour digestion at 1:100 ratio. Approximately 100 μg protein per sample was processed for subsequent experiments.

TMT labeling and HPLC fractionation

Following trypsin digestion, peptides were desalted using Strata X C18 SPE columns (Phenomenex, Tianjin, China) and vacuum dried. Peptides were reconstituted in 0.5 M TEAB and processed according to the TMTsixplex Isobaric Label Reagent Set protocol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai, China). TMT reagent (24 μl acetonitrile per unit) was incubated with peptides for 2 h at room temperature. Labeled peptides were pooled, desalted, and dried by vacuum centrifugation.

High pH reverse-phase HPLC fractionation was performed using an Agilent 300 Extend C18 column (5 μm particles, 4.6 mm ID, 250 mm length). Peptides were separated with a gradient of 2% to 60% acetonitrile in 10 mM ammonium bicarbonate (pH 10) over 80 min into 80 fractions, which were subsequently combined into 18 fractions and vacuum dried.

Affinity enrichment for acetylated and succinylated peptides

Tryptic peptides were dissolved in NETN buffer (100 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 50 mM Tris-HCl, 0.5% NP-40, pH 8.0) and incubated with pre-washed antibody beads (PTM Biolabs, Chicago, IL, USA) at 4 °C overnight with gentle shaking. Beads were washed four times with NETN buffer and twice with ddH2O. Bound peptides were eluted with 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), combined, and vacuum dried. Peptides were cleaned using C18 ZipTip pipette tips (EMD Millipore, Shanghai, China) according to manufacturer’s instructions prior to LC-MS/MS analysis.

LC-MS/MS analysis

Peptides were dissolved in 0.1% formic acid and loaded onto a reversed-phase pre-column (Acclaim PepMap 100 C18, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Separation was performed using a reversed-phase analytical column (Acclaim PepMap RSLC, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a gradient of solvent B (0.1% formic acid in 98% acetonitrile) from 8% to 25% over 20 min, 25% to 40% over 12 min, increasing to 80% over 4 min, and maintaining at 80% for 4 min at 400 nl/min flow rate on an EASY-nLC 1000 UPLC system.

Peptides were analyzed using a Q Exactive Plus Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with nanospray ionization. Intact peptides were detected in the Orbitrap at 70,000 resolution. MS/MS was performed using normalized collision energy of 30, with ion fragments detected at 17,500 resolution. A data-dependent procedure alternated between one MS scan and 20 MS/MS scans for the top 20 precursor ions above 5E3 threshold with 15.0s dynamic exclusion. Electrospray voltage was 2.0 kV with automatic gain control preventing Orbitrap overfilling (5E4 ions for MS/MS spectra generation). MS scan range was 350–1800 m/z with fixed first mass at 100 m/z.

Database search and quality control

MS/MS data were processed using MaxQuant with integrated Andromeda search engine (v.1.5.2.8) [24]. Tandem mass spectra were searched against the SwissProt human database with reverse decoy database. Trypsin/P cleavage allowed up to 4 missing cleavages, 5 modifications per peptide, and 5 charges. Mass error tolerance was 10 ppm for precursor ions and 0.02 Da for fragment ions. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as fixed modification, while methionine oxidation and lysine acetylation/succinylation were variable modifications. False discovery rate thresholds were 1% for proteins, peptides, and modification sites. Minimum peptide length was 7 amino acids. TMT-6-plex was selected for quantification. Site localization probability threshold was >0.75.

Quality control included mass error distribution analysis and peptide length distribution assessment. Mass error distribution centered near zero with most values <0.02 Da, confirming adequate mass accuracy. Peptide length distribution (8–20 amino acids) was consistent with tryptic peptide properties, indicating satisfactory sample preparation quality.

Bioinformatics analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) annotation was derived from the UniProt-GOA database [25]. Protein IDs were converted to UniProt IDs and mapped to GO terms. Proteins not annotated in UniProt-GOA were analyzed using InterProScan software for sequence-based GO function annotation. Proteins were classified into biological process, cellular component, and molecular function categories. Two-tailed Fisher’s exact test assessed enrichment of differentially expressed proteins against all identified proteins, with corrected p-value <0.05 considered significant.

KEGG pathway annotation utilized KAAS for database description and KEGG Mapper for result mapping [26]. Two-tailed Fisher’s exact test evaluated pathway enrichment with false discovery rate (FDR) correction [27]. Pathways with corrected p-value <0.05 were considered significant.

To ensure data reliability, a ‘filter-and-retain’ approach was applied. Proteins or modification sites with missing values in more than 20% of samples across all storage time points were excluded from downstream analyses. For features with ≤20% missing values, the original valid measurements were retained without imputation, supported by high reproducibility among biological replicates (n = 3, Pearson correlation coefficient > 0.8). Proteins and modification sites showing significant temporal changes were standardized using z-score normalization. Hierarchical clustering was then performed with Euclidean distance as the dissimilarity metric and average linkage for cluster formation. The biological relevance of each cluster was confirmed through GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses. Enriched sequence motifs surrounding lysine acetylation and succinylation sites were identified using the Motif-X software [28]. Only confidently identified modification sites were included. Peptides containing ambiguous modification sites were excluded, and only MS-identified peptides with lengths between 8 and 20 amino acids were considered for analysis. For each qualified modification site, a 21-mer sequence (centered on the modification site, with 10 amino acids upstream and 10 amino acids downstream) was extracted from the full-length protein sequences in the SwissProt human protein database (https://www.expasy.org/resources/uniprotkb-swiss-prot), which also served as the background for statistical comparison. Motif-X analysis was performed using the software’s default parameters.

Random Forest analysis for core protein identification

To identify key proteins driving platelet storage lesion, a Random Forest machine learning approach was implemented in R (version 4.2.1) using the randomForest package (version 4.7.1.1). The Random Forest algorithm was selected for its ability to handle non-linear relationships, feature interactions, and high-dimensional data without overfitting [29]. Proteins commonly modified by both acetylation and succinylation across storage time points were selected through intersection analysis. Feature engineering captured temporal patterns, including fold-change trajectories and modification site dynamics for each protein. The Random Forest model employed 1,000 decision trees with bootstrap sampling and a fixed random seed (2024) to ensure reproducibility. Feature importance was evaluated using Mean Decrease Gini, supported by Mean Decrease Accuracy and 100 permutation tests. Cross-validation with stratified sampling and out-of-bag (OOB) error analysis (overall fluctuation <5%) ensured model robustness. Proteins were ranked by their contribution to storage time classification accuracy, and the top 20 proteins were selected as core regulators. Model validation included temporal holdout testing and biological pathway enrichment analysis to confirm relevance to known mechanisms of platelet storage lesion.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

To identify molecular modules associated with platelet storage time, we performed WGCNA using the R package WGCNA (version 1.73). Expression profiles of all quantified proteins, acetylation sites, and succinylation sites were preprocessed and merged. The soft-thresholding power was selected using the pickSoftThreshold function by evaluating the scale-free topology fit index (signed R2) and average connectivity across different powers. The minimum power at which the network achieved a signed R2 > 0.85 was chosen to satisfy scale-free topology criteria. Network construction and module detection were performed using the blockwiseModules function with the following key parameters: network type = ‘signed’, minimum module size = 50, and module merging/cutting height = 0.1. This step grouped genes or modification sites into co-expression modules, each assigned a unique color. Module–trait correlations were calculated by assessing the Pearson correlation coefficient between each module eigengene (ME) and platelet storage time, with associated p-values used to identify modules significantly related to storage duration.

qRT-PCR

qRT-PCR validated 4 key genes on days 1,3,5,7. RNA isolated via TRIzol (OD260/280:1.8–2.0; RIN > 8.0) was reverse-transcribed to cDNA. Reactions with SYBR Green and gene-specific primers ran on StepOnePlus (40 cycles:95 °C 15s, 60 °C 1 min). Triplicate technical/3 biological replicates. GAPDH normalized expression via 2^(-ΔΔCt) (D1 reference). One-way ANOVA with Tukey’s test (p < 0.05) analyzed temporal trends (mean ± SD).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate with three biological replicates. Differential protein expression was assessed using fold-change ≥1.3 and p-value ≤0.05 criteria. Pearson correlation coefficients evaluated reproducibility between biological replicates. Hierarchical clustering analysis used Euclidean distance with average linkage. Heat maps were generated using the ‘heatmap.2’ function from the ‘gplots’ R package. Statistical significance was determined using appropriate tests as indicated in figure legends.

For the acetyl-proteome after acetylated peptide enrichment, 1,308 Kac sites were matched on 648 identified proteins, among which 790 sites in 396 proteins were quantified. For the succinylome, 1,947 Ksucc sites in 959 proteins were identified, with 1,279 quantitative sites in 661 proteins. All the differences listed above directly reflect functional changes that gradually occurred during platelet storage. Interestingly, when D3 is compared to D1, up-regulated proteins were more numerous than down-regulated ones, while the opposite pattern was observed for Kac and Ksucc protein comparisons. Conversely, although the total down-regulated proteins were slightly more than the up-regulated ones, the up-regulated Kac and Ksucc proteins were much more numerous than the corresponding down-regulated ones in the groups D7/D1, D7/D3, and D7/D5, especially D7/D3 and D7/D5. The shift in up- and down-regulated proteins and their modifications may be related to their functional transformation during platelet storage. The biological significance of this shift was carefully analyzed to annotate the differential proteins as follows.

To further characterize changes in protein abundance during platelet storage, we selected 5 down-regulated and 5 up-regulated proteins and analyzed their expression changes (Table 3, Figure 4). We found that proteins that decreased during storage were those primarily involved in basic cellular functions such as metabolism, signal transduction, as well as other biochemical processes. This loss precisely demonstrated the process of platelet storage lesion, and the decrease of these proteins became more obvious over time. In contrast, we also identified up-regulated proteins during standard platelet storage. These were mainly involved in platelet activation, apoptosis, and senescence as we previously reported. For instance, BCL2L1 and BAD transcripts were found to be up-regulated during storage, which are largely involved in the process of platelet apoptosis through the cholecystokinin receptor signaling pathway.

Dynamic characterization of platelet storage by functional annotation and enrichment analysis

We comprehensively annotated differentially expressed proteins based on several categories, including Gene Ontology (GO), KEGG pathways, and subcellular localization. We found a wide range of functional distributions among the up- or down-regulated proteins in the comparison groups. To further investigate the differences among the desired storage time points, we screened and selected significant entries by performing functional enrichment analysis of differentially qualified proteins (Figure 5). The most significantly enriched biological processes among up-regulated proteins were humoral immune response, adaptive immune response, and complement activation in D3/D1, D5/D1, and D7/D1, which might suggest that immunity was activated during storage. Other molecular characteristics, such as rapid increases in peptidase activity, antigen binding, and transporter activity, were also noted. Conversely, many down-regulated proteins were enriched in the regulation of coagulation and hemostasis, response to bacteria, and peptide biosynthetic processes. This might indicate that platelet activity declined over storage time.

The x-axis denotes -log10(Fisher’s exact test P-value), and y-axes list enriched GO terms encompassing molecular function (MF), cellular component (CC), and biological process (BP) ontologies. Surprisingly, when comparing D7/D3 and D7/D5, there was a sudden decrease in cellular immunity and complement activation, but also an increase in cellular detoxification and xenobiotic catabolic processes. This might indicate a slight decline in immune response at the end of storage, when a large number of toxins had accumulated. For differential Kac proteins, significant increases in metabolic and catabolic processes were observed, which involved small molecules, nucleic acids, and various compounds. The most enriched cellular components were mitochondria and cell junctions. The enrichment of up-regulated Ksucc proteins mainly focused on cell junction assemblies, cytoplasmic vesicles, and metabolic processes, which was similar to the up-regulated Kac proteins. In contrast, the enrichment of down-regulated proteins was reflected in protein localization, secretion, and the cytoplasmic region.

KEGG pathway-based enrichment analysis showed significant increases in complement and coagulation cascades, Staphylococcus aureus infection, systemic lupus erythematosus, and pertussis during the first 5 days of storage, while ribosome and cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions declined (Figure 6). At the end of storage, almost all aforementioned observations decreased. However, we also observed elevations in drug and xenobiotic metabolism induced by cytochrome P450, chemical carcinogenesis, and oxidative phosphorylation. It is also important to note the large variations in differential Kac protein expressions. The up-regulated Kac proteins were enriched in metabolic pathways, focal adhesion, and the TCA cycle. On the other hand, down-regulated Kac proteins focused on platelet activation and fatty acid metabolism, which might signify a decline in stored platelet activity. The down-regulated Ksucc proteins were mainly enriched in glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and focal adhesion in D3/D1 and D5/D1; at the end of storage, focal adhesion and regulation of actin cytoskeleton were clearly enriched among the up-regulated proteins.

The analysis above suggests that platelet immune responses were activated and processed during storage, as well as complement and coagulation cascades. Cellular detoxification and cell junction functions also increased over time. Conversely, it appeared that platelet membranes and ribosomes were slightly suffering from damage. The enrichment of differential Kac and Ksucc proteins was consistent with the global protein patterns. Altogether, the enrichment analysis of global proteins, Kac proteins, and Ksucc proteins provided insights into the dynamic changes in stored platelet biology.

Dynamic clustering of differential protein expression

Based on clustering analysis, the differential proteins were divided into 12 clusters (Supplemental Fig. S1). Each cluster had specific characteristics among the groups, indicating differential patterns for protein expression in platelets during storage time. There were 9 clusters for the differential Kac proteins and 5 clusters for Ksucc proteins (Supplemental Fig. S2 and Supplemental Fig. S3).

According to the dynamic clustering, we obtained heatmaps from GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis (Figure 7). The enriched biological processes for global proteins varied among differential groups, as did the KEGG pathways, suggesting dynamic protein expression and regulation patterns during platelet storage. For example, regulation of lipid metabolic processes clearly increased from D1 to D3, and continued increase was observed until D5, but declined on the 7th day, indicating that metabolic activity was enhanced at the start due to sudden stimulation from microenvironmental changes, and then decreased to complete loss of activity ex vivo. The same pattern was applicable to other metabolic processes or transport of metabolites. Some pathways may be initiated sooner upon platelet collection and then decline gradually with complement and coagulation cascades; however, other pathways may be activated later, such as drug metabolism induced by cytochrome P450 and chemical carcinogenesis, among others. The heatmap patterns of Kac protein enrichment followed the same regularity as those of global proteins, with minor differences in the specific biological processes and pathways involved in Kac regulation (Supplemental Fig. S4). Interestingly, Ksucc protein enrichment heatmaps also contained a different set of biological processes and pathways compared to those of Kac protein enrichment (Supplemental Fig. S5). Upon comparison of heatmaps, Kac and Ksucc proteins were noted to be involved in the regulation of different biological processes and pathways with minor overlaps, indicating significant differences between the two modifications. Importantly, they also complemented the global protein expression patterns, meaning that Kac and Ksucc were indispensable for the regulation of protein functions.

To further characterize the structural context of lysine acetylation (Kac) and succinylation (Ksucc) sites identified during platelet storage, the distribution of modified sites within protein functional domains was systematically analyzed (Supplementary Fig. S7). The stacking distribution analysis revealed distinct patterns of Kac and Ksucc sites within and outside annotated protein domains (Supplementary Fig. S7A). Quantitative assessment showed that 20.7% of Kac sites and 15.6% of Ksucc sites were localized within known functional or structural domains (Supplementary Fig. S7B). Further analysis of the top 15 domains with the highest enrichment of modified sites demonstrated that Kac sites were predominantly enriched in LIM-type zinc finger domains (99 sites) and P-loop nucleoside triphosphate hydrolase domains (95 sites), whereas Ksucc sites showed the highest enrichment in adenosylhomocysteinase-like domains (94 sites) (Supplementary Fig. S7C).

Machine learning identified 20 core proteins

Through systematic multi-omics analysis of the platelet storage process, we identified lysine acetylation (Kac) and succinylation (Ksucc) modification sites. Intersection analysis revealed proteins co-modified by both post-translational modifications (Figure 8A). Machine learning screened 20 core proteins from this co-modified subset (Figure 8B). Differential modification analysis of these 20 proteins clarified dynamic changes in Kac/Ksucc levels across storage time points (D3/D1, D5/D1, D7/D1) (Figure 8C). Figure 8D presents qPCR validation results: 4 key proteins (ARAP1, COX5B, F13A1, PPIB) were selected from the 20 core proteins. Scatter plots show their Log2 (Fold Change) relative to D1 across storage days, further observing mRNA-level expression dynamics of these genes to complement multi-omics modification profiles.

To further characterize the coordinated dynamics between protein abundance and post-translational modifications during platelet storage, the correlations between protein-level changes and the corresponding Kac and Ksucc site intensities were summarized across storage time points (Supplementary Fig. S8). The distribution of correlation coefficients revealed heterogeneous relationships between protein abundance and modification dynamics, with subsets of proteins showing concordant or discordant temporal trends. In parallel, WGCNA was performed using protein abundance, Kac, Ksucc, and the combined dataset to explore module–storage time associations (Supplementary Fig. S9). In the integrated network, the brown module showed the strongest positive correlation with storage time (cor = 0.39, p = 0.02) (Supplementary Fig. S9A). No modules exhibiting statistically significant correlations with storage duration were identified in the Kac or Ksucc networks (Supplementary Fig. S9B–C). In contrast, in the protein abundance network, the grey module displayed the strongest negative correlation with storage time (cor = −0.79, p = 0.004) (Supplementary Fig. S9D).

Discussion

This study pioneers the integration of multi-omics technologies to systematically analyze the dynamic regulatory network of lysine acetylation and succinylation during platelet storage, offering a novel perspective on the molecular mechanisms underlying PSL. By tracking the modifications over 1 to 7 days of storage, we provide the first evidence that post-translational modifications act as ‘molecular timers’ for platelet function degradation. Their spatiotemporal-specific regulatory patterns are directly linked to the clinical outcomes of transfusion efficacy. This finding lays a theoretical foundation for optimizing storage strategies in the field of transfusion medicine.

In the ‘-omics’ era, it is desirable to identify and quantify the entire suite of expressed proteins and uncover the dynamic changes they undergo during a process of interest [30]. The next-generation proteomics has been greatly improved to achieve proteomic dynamics in depth, making it possible to systematically describe molecular changes in a cell and those of tissue proteomes with a high temporal resolution [31]. Recent advances in plasma proteomics have demonstrated the powerful capability of protein-based approaches in assessing organ health and predicting disease risk, as evidenced by large-scale studies using thousands of proteins from extensive biobank cohorts [31].

The TMT-labeled LC-MS/MS combined with pre-fractionation by HPLC allows for comprehensive proteome profiling with deep coverage [32]. Combined with acetylated and succinylated peptide enrichment methods, this strategy was able to determine the level of global proteomic changes, as well as expression changes of acetylome and succinylome, simultaneously [30]. The development of high-performance antibodies targeting site-specific post-translational modifications has further enhanced our ability to detect and analyze these modifications with unprecedented specificity and sensitivity [33].

To analyze the dynamics and regulations of platelets stored for 1, 3, 5, 7 days, we applied the strategy described above to achieve global proteome, acetylome, and succinylome for clinical platelets at 4 time points, respectively. In total, 3,609 proteins were identified, and among them, 3,100 proteins were quantified. 1,308 Kac sites in 648 proteins were identified with quantitative 790 sites in 396 proteins, as well as 1,947 Ksucc sites in 959 proteins with quantitative 1,279 sites in 661 proteins.

The temporal analysis of acetylation and succinylation modifications reveals a complex regulatory landscape that evolves throughout the storage period. The initial decrease in both modifications during early storage (D1-D3) followed by a dramatic increase in later stages (D5-D7) suggests a biphasic response to storage stress. This pattern likely reflects the transition from acute stress adaptation to chronic cellular dysfunction, consistent with the established timeline of PSL development [34].

The early suppression of modifications may represent an energy conservation mechanism, as both acetylation and succinylation are metabolically expensive processes requiring acetyl-CoA and succinyl-CoA, respectively. Recent discoveries of novel post-translational modifications, such as lysine vitcylation derived from vitamin C, have expanded our understanding of the diversity and complexity of protein modifications in cellular regulation [35]. This highlights the potential for discovering additional modification types that may be relevant to platelet storage biology.

The differential kinetics between acetylation and succinylation modifications provide important mechanistic insights. While acetylation showed a more gradual recovery pattern, succinylation demonstrated more pronounced fluctuations, particularly the massive increase observed at D7. This divergence suggests distinct regulatory mechanisms and functional roles for these modifications in platelet storage biology. Recent studies have demonstrated that succinylation is more directly linked to metabolic flux through the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, as succinyl-CoA availability directly influences modification levels [36].

Recent studies have reported that platelet functions, such as granule release, adhesion and aggregation could be affected by acetylation. The integration of multi-omics technologies is paving the way for a deeper understanding of platelet mechanisms, with significant implications for biomedical research and clinical applications. Platelet activation is an exceptionally rapid process characterized by various posttranslational modifications, including protein breakdown and phosphorylation, which can be thoroughly investigated using multiomics technologies and biochemical methods.

Our pathway enrichment analysis reveals extensive metabolic reprogramming during platelet storage, with particular emphasis on energy metabolism pathways. The upregulation of complement and coagulation cascades during early storage, followed by their subsequent decline, suggests an initial hyperactivation state that eventually leads to functional exhaustion. This pattern is consistent with the concept of ‘storage-induced platelet activation’ described in recent literature, where platelets undergo spontaneous activation in response to storage conditions [32].

For the global protein expression, the most significant changes were the activation of immune responses as adaptation to the microenvironment ex vivo, including humoral immune response, adaptive immune response, and lymphocyte-mediated immunity. Complement activation and protein activation cascades were also evidently stimulated. On the contrary, regulations of hemostasis, cell division, and biosynthetic processes were reduced.

The enrichment of drug metabolism and xenobiotic processing pathways at later storage time points indicates cellular stress responses and potential toxin accumulation. This finding aligns with recent observations that stored platelets accumulate metabolic byproducts and reactive oxygen species, contributing to functional deterioration [34]. The role of platelets in systemic inflammation has been increasingly recognized, with proteomic studies revealing significant differences in protein categories related to platelet granule release and cellular iron uptake in inflammatory conditions [34].

Lysine is a common target for post-translational modification, and acetylation is the most extensive pattern involved in efficient biological mechanism for protein regulation. Post-translational modifications of transcription factors significantly modulate their functionality, leading to drastic changes in transcriptional outcomes, particularly in vascular diseases where metabolic alterations are extensively studied [36].

During the process of platelet storage, the Kac proteins were participating in a wide range of biological functions and regulations, such as involvement in metabolic pathways, focal adhesion, cell junction, and platelet activation among others. Beyond acetylation, lysine succinylation has also been extensively explored, involved in glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, regulation of cell motility, as well as platelet activation. The biological changes, as well as protein regulations, manifested the transformation that platelets were undergoing to shape the dynamics of platelet biology.

The machine learning-identified core proteins likely represent key nodes in these metabolic networks, serving as potential biomarkers for storage quality assessment. Based on our high-resolution data, a systematic analysis of protein expressions and modifications upon acetylation and succinylation were followed to illustrate dynamic changes of platelet biology during the storage time.

The comprehensive characterization of PTM dynamics during platelet storage opens new avenues for improving transfusion medicine. The identification of 20 core proteins through random forest analysis provides potential targets for storage optimization strategies. These proteins likely represent critical control points in the PSL pathway, and their modulation could extend platelet storage duration or improve post-transfusion function.

Recent advances in platelet storage technology, including pathogen reduction systems and novel storage media, have focused primarily on maintaining basic cellular integrity. Our findings suggest that targeting specific post-translational modifications could provide more precise interventions. Cross-population genomic and proteomic studies have demonstrated the power of integrating multiple data types to improve risk prediction and reveal disease mechanisms, suggesting similar approaches could be applied to platelet storage optimization.

Future studies should explore the functional consequences of specific modification changes identified here. Targeted manipulation of individual modifications through genetic or pharmacological approaches could validate their causal roles in PSL development. Additionally, the development of real-time monitoring systems based on PTM biomarkers could enable dynamic assessment of platelet quality during storage.

Although evidence regarding the impact of platelet storage time on mortality or bleeding endpoints remains inconsistent, several clinical studies indicate that prolonged storage affects platelet function and post-transfusion efficacy, complementing the post-translational modification kinetics observed in our study. For example, recent clinical trials show that platelets stored for ≤3 days exhibit significantly higher corrected count increment (CCI) and platelet recovery rate (PPR) at 24 h compared with platelets stored for 4–5 days, while primary clinical outcomes such as bleeding, infection, or mortality remain unchanged [37]. This suggests that storage duration may be more sensitive for evaluating transfusion function than for predicting hard clinical endpoints.

Similarly, platelets stored at room temperature for extended periods show increased complement activation and reduced responsiveness to PAR‑1 and TXA2 receptor stimulation, reflecting functional decline in vitro, particularly during late storage [38]. Furthermore, analyses in certain patient populations, such as those undergoing cardiac surgery, indicate that storage beyond 4–7 days is associated with higher postoperative blood loss, increased reoperation rates, and elevated in-hospital mortality [39]. The kinetic patterns of Kac and Ksucc identified in our study likely mirror these functional declines, including diminished receptor responsiveness and reduced platelet activation, which correspond to changes in post-transfusion efficacy measures such as CCI and PPR. Integrating the assessment of specific post-translational modifications with functional indicators may enable the development of molecular markers for more precise evaluation of platelet quality, optimize transfusion timing, and guide interventions targeting modification pathways, thereby enhancing the clinical translational potential of platelet storage research.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. (1) Platelet samples were obtained from anonymous healthy donors at a blood center, and detailed clinical information (e.g. age, sex, lifestyle) was not available. As a result, the influence of donor variability on baseline platelet proteome and PTM profiles could not be systematically assessed. (2) Due to the retrospective design and sample characteristics, this study primarily characterizes the dynamic patterns of kac and ksucc through multi-omics and bioinformatics analyses, without directly verifying the causal roles of key modification sites or core proteins via molecular experiments, such as site-directed mutagenesis or functional recovery assays. (3) The study focused on two metabolism-related PTMs—Kac and Ksucc—and did not simultaneously examine other important modifications, such as phosphorylation or ubiquitination, which may also contribute to platelet storage lesions. Consequently, the integrated network of PTM interactions remains incomplete. (4) Although biological replicates (n = 3) were sufficient for exploratory analyses, a larger sample size would enhance statistical power and generalizability. Future studies could strengthen the molecular regulatory map of platelet storage lesions by integrating donor-matched clinical information, conducting prospective functional experiments (e.g. site-directed mutagenesis, pharmacological interventions), and incorporating multi-dimensional PTM data in more controllable systems, such as immortalized megakaryocyte lines or gene-edited animal models. Developing a biomarker-based monitoring system targeting key PTMs may also provide practical tools for assessing platelet quality and defining safe transfusion windows.

The potential for personalized platelet storage based on donor-specific PTM profiles represents an exciting frontier. Individual variations in baseline modification patterns or storage responses could influence optimal storage conditions or duration. Such personalized approaches could maximize the therapeutic efficacy of platelet transfusions while minimizing adverse effects.

Conclusion

This study presents the first comprehensive multi-omics characterization of lysine acetylation and succinylation dynamics during platelet storage, revealing critical insights into platelet storage lesion mechanisms. We identified distinct temporal patterns of post-translational modifications governing platelet function deterioration, with succinylation more extensive than acetylation. Pathway enrichment analysis reveals extensive metabolic reprogramming involving complement activation and energy metabolism. The identification of specific motif patterns provides mechanistic insights into functional specificity. Random forest machine learning identified 20 core regulatory proteins representing critical nodes in PSL development. These findings establish a robust framework for developing improved platelet storage strategies and have significant implications for enhancing transfusion safety and efficacy in clinical practice, while advancing our understanding of post-translational modification networks in anucleate cells.
