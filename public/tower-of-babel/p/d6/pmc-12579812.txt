On the future of guidelines
Authors: W Joost Wiersinga, Morten Hylander Møller, Jan J de Waele, Hallie C Prescott
Published: 2025

“GRADE is an excellent way to exhaust all the volunteers on the guideline committee but probably will not help much in putting together the final recommendations.”, exclaimed a respected intensivist during a clinical practice guideline panel meeting. The remark underscored how guideline development is often slow and resource intensive. Yet, on reflection, most people acknowledge that the benefits of the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach have been remarkable, and its influence on evidence-based medicine and daily clinical practice remains central [1]. This forward-looking comment on the evolving landscape of clinical practice guideline development highlights both challenges and opportunities, beginning with a comparison of guideline formats, and followed by emerging approaches to evidence appraisal.

Traditional-, rapid-, and living clinical practice guidelines

Traditional clinical practice guidelines, typically published every 3–5 years, are comprehensive, multidisciplinary, and address multiple topics. They systematically review the evidence base, evaluate the balance of benefits and harms, and issue transparent, graded recommendations. Strengths include credibility, broad endorsement, and structured methodology. Downsides are resource intensity, the long timeframe, and risk of becoming outdated at publication or soon after, with the added challenge of inconsistencies that sometimes arise between guidelines on the same topic issued by different entities.

Rapid practice guidelines (RPGs) emerged to address urgent, single clinical questions, providing focused guidance within weeks or months when new, potentially practice-changing evidence emerges [2]. Their key advantage is responsiveness, speeding translation from evidence to practice during public health emergencies or with emergence of new therapies. Examples include RPGs on dexmedetomidine for sedation in ICU patients [3] and on neuromuscular blockade in ARDS [4]. Drawbacks include lack of comprehensiveness and potential fragmentation: clinicians may need to consult multiple RPGs to cover related clinical problems. Furthermore, ensuring methodological rigor under time pressure is a persistent challenge.

Living guidelines represent perhaps the most transformative development in recent years. They rely on continuous evidence monitoring and active surveillance, rapid integration of new evidence, and near real-time communication of updates [5]. The COVID-19 pandemic proved their value, showing they are especially useful for rapidly evolving clinical topics [6]. Benefits include updated and current guidelines, improved usability, and narrowing of the knowledge-to-practice gap. However, they require continuous resources, sophisticated evidence surveillance, and strong collaboration [7]. Traditional guidelines often gain broad endorsement - for instance, the Surviving Sepsis Campaign is supported by over 20 societies [8] - but securing similar backing for living guidelines may be challenging.

Taken together, traditional clinical practice guidelines are comprehensive but slow, RPGs are fast but narrow, while living guidelines aim to combine timeliness with credibility but require sustained investment (Table 1). Each approach has unique strengths and limitations, and hybrid models are increasingly likely.

Evolution of clinical practice guidelines

Methods of evidence appraisal: GRADE, rapid synthesis, and AI-assisted approaches

The GRADE approach remains the most widely used standard. It structures questions in PICO format, rates certainty of evidence, and formulates recommendations that balance benefits, harms, values, and resources [1]. As David Sackett emphasized decades ago, “External evidence is only meaningful when filtered through clinical expertise and applied to individual patients” [9]. Translating evidence into practice remains the physician’s responsibility, and clear guideline purposes are essential for acceptance and impact.

Rapid evidence synthesis methods have emerged to complement or accelerate guideline development by streamlining reviews; for example, restricting eligibility criteria, purposively sampling richer studies, and limiting searches to a few prioritized databases [10]. Early involvement of knowledge users, standardized templates, and targeted quality checks can further enhance efficiency [10]. Their main advantage is speed, making them especially useful for RPGs or urgent living guideline updates. The trade-off is reduced depth, comprehensiveness and risk of missing evidence, so transparency about shortcuts and safeguards is essential to maintain credibility.

Artificial intelligence (AI) tools are beginning to reshape evidence synthesis. Large language models, for example, have been tested in drafting recommendations. A recent evaluation of ChatGPT in drafting brain abscess guidelines showed substantial overlap with expert consensus: for 17 out of 20 questions, ChatGPT was able to give recommendations on the management of patients with brain abscess, including certainty of evidence and strength of recommendation, that aligned with the official guideline [11]. However, it also produced two recommendations that directly contradicted the guideline, being associated with the imminent risk of harm to the patient [11]. This underscores AI’s promise for accelerating synthesis and drafting, but also its risks: lack of transparency (“black box” outputs), inaccuracies, and uncertain clinician acceptance. Responsible integration of AI will require human oversight, validation and regulatory frameworks [12].

Towards personalized recommendations

Most clinical guideline recommendations are based on clinical trial evidence; however, average treatment effects may not represent individual outcomes, which can be larger, smaller, or even opposite, a phenomenon known as heterogeneity of treatment effects (HTE) [13]. How to incorporate individualized treatment effects in guidelines? In a secondary analysis of two trials, Buell et al. used machine learning to predict individualized effects of lower versus higher oxygenation targets in critically ill, mechanically ventilated adults [14]. Predicted benefits varied by patient phenotype, with some improving under lower targets (e.g., acute brain injury) and others under higher targets (e.g., sepsis). Applying individualized targets instead of trial-assigned averages was estimated to reduce overall mortality by 6.4%. The next step is to prospectively test HTE-guided treatment strategies. More personalization in guidelines is a needed future direction, though the exact mechanism to do this is still being workout out [13].

At the same time, even the best evidence risks limited impact if not embedded in clinical workflows. Embedding guidelines into Electronic Health Records, including Clinical Decision Support modules, can represents an important step toward closing the “implementation gap”, which is often larger than the knowledge gap [15]. Structured, digital guideline formats will also improve discoverability and reusability.

Looking ahead

The future of guideline development must balance rigor with efficiency, comprehensiveness with timeliness, and innovation with safety. Traditional, rapid, and living guidelines each have distinct strengths, and hybrid models will likely prevail. Likewise, GRADE, rapid evidence synthesis, and AI-assisted methods are best seen as complementary. Key next steps include moving from population averages toward more personalized recommendations, integrating guidelines into digital workflows, using AI with transparency and safety, and advancing implementation pathways (Table 1).

Ultimately, the value of guidelines depends not only on methodological rigor but also on clinical relevance, accessibility, and applicability, ultimately contributing to better patient care. The next generation of guideline development should narrow the gap between evidence and practice, offering clinicians real-time, context-sensitive support, while making the process a little less exhausting and more rewarding.
