Electrochemical Transformation of Copper Sulfide Electrodes for Selective CO2‐to‐Formate Conversion
Authors: Roser Fernández‐Climent, Daniele Giusi, Matteo Miceli, Camilo A Mesa, Ana Gutiérrez‐Blanco, Junnan Li, Jesús Redondo, Frederik Schiller, Sara Barja, Veronica Celorrio, Nikolay Kornienko, Claudio Ampelli, Sixto Giménez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrecise control over the dynamic transformations that electrocatalysts undergo under operating conditions offers a powerful strategy for tailoring catalytic selectivity. Herein, the electrochemical modification of Cu2−x S‐derived catalysts to generate selective active sites for the electroreduction of CO2 to formate is investigated. Through a combination of in situ and ex situ characterization techniques, it is demonstrated that electrochemical cycling induces sulfur leaching, resulting in the formation of reduced, amorphous copper structures that exhibit enhanced selectivity toward formate production. Compared to the pristine material, the electrochemically modified catalyst achieves a twofold improvement in Faradaic efficiency, reaching values as high as 75% for CO2‐to‐formate conversion. These findings not only establish a cost‐effective and scalable platform for catalyst fabrication and activation, but also open new avenues for advancing sustainable CO2 conversion technologies toward industrial implementation.Keywords: CO2 reduction, copper sulfides, dynamic reconstructions, formate, in situ characterizations

Introduction

Carbon dioxide utilization strategies, such as electrochemical CO2 reduction (CO2RR), address the urgent need to decrease the concentration of anthropogenic CO2, while producing fuels and feedstocks.[ 1 , 2 ] This synthetic process is also appealing for industry, due to its tunable selectivity, its mild reaction conditions, and its versatility to combine with other renewable energy sources.[ 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 ] However, the highly demanding thermodynamics and slow reaction kinetics of the CO2RR hinder its scalability.[ 7 , 8 ] Many efforts to overcome these limitations and develop efficient CO2RR technologies have focused on the design of electrocatalysts with optimized productivity and selectivity for a specific CO2 reduction product.[ 9 , 10 ] Among all the materials of interest, copper and copper‐based materials are considered state‐of‐the‐art electrocatalysts for CO2RR, due to their unique ability to catalyze the reduction of CO2 into a wide range of products under mild conditions.[ 11 , 12 , 13 ]

The development of selective electrocatalysts for CO2RR is particularly challenging, due to the dynamic structural reconstruction that these materials typically undergo during the reaction.[ 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 ] Under cathodic potentials, substantial changes in the catalyst structure,[ 14 , 18 ] chemical state,[ 19 ] and morphology[ 20 , 21 ] take place, generating new active sites, which modify the activity and selectivity of the catalyst.[ 22 , 23 , 24 ] Although the research on the reconstruction behavior of CO2RR electrocatalysts has been intensified in the last years, a thorough understanding of their dynamic structural evolution under reaction conditions is lacking.[ 25 ] Attaining precise control over catalytically active sites to target specific CO2RR products is key to achieving efficient and selective CO2 to value‐added chemical conversion technologies. Recently, novel strategies to electrochemically modify the catalyst have been reported, influencing the formation of catalytic sites that can enhance its selectivity. In particular, the application of electrochemical pulses has gained popularity in Cu‐based materials and copper oxides.[ 26 ] In this context, electrochemically induced modifications in copper sulfide catalysts remain largely unexplored. Due to the various bonding modes of S atoms, sulfides exhibit diversified structures, which means that S is an attractive candidate as a modifier for electrocatalysts, enhancing both the catalytic activity and product selectivity.[ 27 , 28 ] Copper sulfides have been reported as suitable electrocatalysts for CO2 conversion to formate, which is one of the most economically feasible products from CO2RR.[ 29 , 30 ] Formate/formic acid is a relevant nonflammable and nontoxic feedstock and a valuable hydrogen storage chemical.[ 31 ] Although the specific mechanisms by which sulfur alters the catalyst selectivity for CO2RR remain unclear, it is evident that the S dissolution under cathodic polarization plays a key role in the formation of selective copper active sites to drive CO2‐to‐formate conversion.[ 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 ] An in‐depth fundamental understanding of the dynamic reconstruction, evolved active sites, and their effects on catalytic activity and selectivity is key for the rational design of efficient catalysts.

Herein, we investigate the catalytic activity and dynamic reconstructions of copper sulfide (Cu2−x S) electrocatalyst, upon different electrochemical processes. The catalyst undergoes in situ surface restructuring generating catalytically active Cu(0) sites highly selective for electrochemical CO2 to formate conversion, while inhibiting the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER). Although Cu2−x S growth on Cu foil by a facile drop‐casting technique was previously evaluated for HER, exhibiting an unprecedented durability and catalytic behavior,[ 36 ] to the best of our knowledge, this is the first report on the application of Cu2 − x S catalysts grown on Cu foil via a simple drop‐casting technique for the CO2RR. This approach not only provides a cost‐effective and scalable platform for catalyst fabrication but also opens new opportunities for advancing sustainable CO2 conversion technologies toward industrial implementation.

Results and Discussion

The copper sulfide (Cu2−x S) electrocatalyst was synthesized by drop‐casting a sodium polysulfide solution on an electropolished Cu foil, as previously reported.[ 36 ] The resulting Cu2−x S films with a highly porous reticular structure (Figure S1, Supporting Information) were evaluated to electroreduce CO2 in a CO2‐saturated 0.1 M KHCO3 electrolyte,[ 37 ] in a two‐compartment static electrochemical cell, with a three‐electrode configuration.

The selectivity of the catalyst was first investigated at different potentials: −0.4, −0.6, −0.8, and −1.0 V (vs. reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE)) for 1 h. As shown in Figure 1a, formate is the main CO2RR product with a maximum Faradaic efficiency (FEHCOO‐) of 35% at −0.8 V versus RHE, while negligible traces of methane and carbon monoxide were detected. However, significant hydrogen production was observed at all tested potentials, evidencing the strong competition with HER. After the first use, the electrode was tested over five catalytic cycles at −0.8 V versus RHE, leading to a progressive increase in FEHCOO‐ and the concomitant decrease of H2 production. This unprecedented reusability with enhanced selectivity was confirmed by reproducing the experiments with more than 20 identical samples, consistently showing the same increasing trend from an average FEHCOO‐ of 29% to a maximum of 65% by the fifth cycle as shown in Figure 1b. In contrast, when a bare electropolished copper electrode (purple bars, Figure 1b) was tested for CO2RR five times as a reference material, negligible changes in the selectivity were observed. This result highlights the role of sulfur in facilitating structural reorganization, which is directly linked to the improved selectivity observed in CO2RR.

CO2RR performance in CO2‐saturated, 0.1 m KHCO3 solution for the Cu2−x S‐derived electrocatalyst. a) FEs measured from CA measurements at different potentials. b) FEs for formate production at −0.8 V versus RHE for five consecutive catalytic cycles. Results for a reference electropolished Cu foil (EP Cu) are shown for comparison. c) FEs for formate, estimated from four CP measurements at different current densities. d) FEs after three catalytic cycles for formate at −6.5 mA cm−2 in air and N2 atmosphere, respectively.

Remarkably, among all the samples measured over five catalytic cycles at –0.8 V versus RHE, the highest FEHCOO‐ values were obtained at current densities around –6.5 mA cm−2 (Figure S2, Supporting Information). The selectivity of the catalyst was further explored by setting different galvanostatic conditions for one hour, confirming that the optimum conditions for maximum FE HCOO‐ were obtained at –6.5 mA cm−2 (Figure 1c). The relationship observed between current density and maximum FEHCOO − suggests that the electrochemical process plays an important role in catalytic performance. Further optimization of FEHCOO‐ was addressed, keeping the current density at –6.5 mA cm−2 for subsequent catalytic cycles. Notably, the selectivity is enhanced after the first electrochemical test, reaching the maximum FEHCOO‐ values of 75% in the second use, which was maintained for the third use (Figure 1d). These findings suggest more favorable control of catalyst changes through chronopotentiometric (CP) measurements. Furthermore, the stability of the activated catalyst was assessed through CP tests for 15 h (Figure S3, Supporting Information), showing that after an initial transient behavior, due to structural reorganization, the stability is comparable to the best results recently reported.[ 38 ]

To investigate the origin of this enhanced selectivity, the electrodes were structurally and compositionally characterized before and after each use. A comparison of the top‐view scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the electrocatalyst before and after the electrochemical tests shows the formation of Cu–O nanocrystalline structures (Figures S4, S5, Supporting Information), consistent with the formation of CuO/Cu2O phases when the electrode is in contact with air.[ 21 , 39 , 40 , 41 ] Postmortem X‐ray diffractograms (XRD) exhibit an intense peak at the 2θ value of 36.48°, which corresponds to the formation of CuO (PDF #892531).[ 42 ] A less intense peak at 61.4° is assigned to the formation of Cu2O (PDF #78‐2076),[ 43 ] (Figure S6, Supporting Information). This implies that sulfur leaching during the first use leads to partial oxidation of the electrode surface prior to the second use Figure S7, (Supporting Information). To investigate the influence of Cu x O as a precatalyst on the improved selectivity for CO2RR, identical electrochemical and structural measurements were carried out under inert conditions (N2 atmosphere) to minimize oxidation. However, in such conditions, the catalyst exhibits slightly enhanced FEHCOO‐ (dark green bars), compared to air exposure (light green bars, Figure 1d).

The evolution of the chemical composition of the electrodes after the experiments under N2 atmosphere was investigated by X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and X‐ray absorption near‐edge structure (XANES). To suppress the contribution of the substrate in these analyses, the electrocatalyst was carefully scraped from the copper foil, preserving the inert atmosphere conditions. Figure 2a,b shows XPS photoemission results of the Cu LMM Auger transition and the S 2p core‐level emission, respectively, for the as‐synthetized Cu2−x S material, compared to those of the catalyst after the first and second electrochemical tests. The spectra in Figure 2a indicate an evolution of the Cu(I, II) components (K.E. 916.39 eV) in the Cu LMM spectra toward reduced Cu, attributed to the increase of the Cu(0) (K.E. 918.34 eV and K.E. 921.1 eV) component after the CO2RR. In parallel, a decrease of the S 2p component takes place with the catalytic cycles (Figure 2b), revealing almost complete depletion of S after the second use. To gain deeper insight into the sulfur species present, the S 2p peaks were deconvoluted (Figure S8, Supporting Information). The fitted components show good agreement with the overall spectrum and correspond well to the two sulfide species identified. Figure 2c shows the XANES spectra of the as‐synthetized Cu2−x S material, together with those after the first and second uses, as well as Cu foil, as Cu standard. The Cu standard (red line) shows its characteristic Cu K‐edge absorption around 8983 eV with the maximum absorption normalized to 1, whereas the spectrum of the as‐prepared Cu2−x S film (black line) does not exhibit this reduced copper contribution due to the presence of oxidized copper species. After the electrochemical test (green and blue lines), the scraped electrocatalyst shows a peak around 8986 eV similar to the Cu standard peak. The derivative XANES spectra (Figure 3c, inset) confirm that the edge of the Cu(0) is the most reduced one, while the edge of the as‐synthetized Cu2−x S is the most oxidized one (shifted toward the right). From these derivative spectra, it is more evident that the reduction of the oxidized Cu species occurs after the subsequent electrochemical tests (left‐shifted peak indicated by the arrow).[ 44 , 45 ] The reduction of Cu species after electrochemistry does not result in complete conversion to Cu(0), suggesting that the electrodes show a mixture of Cu(II)/Cu(0) oxidation states, consistent with the XPS analysis. The reduction of copper species together with S leaching into the electrolyte has been previously reported on these Cu2−x S electrodes during hydrogen evolution.[ 36 ]

Inset: Derivative normalized XANES spectra showing the characteristic Cu(0) absorption peak around 8980 eV and the right‐shifted Cu(I) absorption peaks around 8986 eV.

To investigate the catalytically active species responsible for the enhanced selectivity, the as‐synthetized Cu2−x S material and the potential surface reconstruction throughout the CO2RR were investigated by operando and ex situ XRD analyses. Figure 3a shows the evolution of the collected XRD patterns, when the applied potential is progressively increased from open‐circuit potential (OCP) to –1.1 V versus RHE and returned to the OCP. At OCP conditions, characteristic phases of Cu, Cu2S, and superficial Cu2O are observed. At more negative potentials, the Cu2S and Cu2O peaks gradually disappear, while the Cu(0) peak increases in intensity and splits into smaller features, consistent with the progressive loss of long‐range crystalline order, indicative of material amorphization. Complementary ex situ XRD characterization of the catalytic electrodes was conducted after the first, second, and third use, sealing the electrode with Kapton tape, preserving the nitrogen atmosphere. The collected XRD spectra in Figure 3b show the two characteristic peaks of metallic Cu at 46.6° and at 50.7° after each use, while no relevant peaks assigned to Cu2−x S were observed. In contrast to ex situ characterization in air atmosphere (Figure S6, Supporting Information), no electrode oxidation is observed. The evolution of the oxidized Cu(I/II) species from the Cu2−x S catalyst (Figure S6, bottom, Supporting Information) toward Cu(0) after electrochemical measurements is consistent with the in situ XRD characterization. Most notably, the diffraction signals for metallic copper, particularly the Cu(111) facet, undergo progressive amorphization with the different uses in CO2RR, suggesting that surface reconstruction takes place with the catalytic cycles. The dissolution of sulfur under reaction conditions, most likely as H2S,[ 46 ] removes anionic sublattices from the original Cu–S framework. S leaching, together with the reduction of copper species to metallic copper, is driving an atomic‐scale reorganization that results in a more amorphous and porous structure. This effect has been previously observed on Cu and Cu‐derived electrocatalysts[ 21 , 47 ] and was further correlated to the catalytic selectivity of the CO2RR.[ 20 , 40 ] Roldan–Cuenya and co‐workers reported that subtle changes in well‐oriented Cu(100) and Cu(111) single‐crystal surfaces favor the CO2RR selectivity, while clean, flat, and atomically ordered surfaces are more selective for H2 production.[ 48 ] Thus, when introducing roughness and defects on the catalytic surface, significant amounts of hydrocarbons can be generated. Indeed, structural and morphological defects are key factors determining the catalytic selectivity of CO2RR toward a specific product. Figure 3c schematically represents the proposed atomic structure evolution of the catalyst over different uses.

Our results clearly suggest that consistently with previous studies,[ 46 ] under applied potential, sulfur atoms are released into solution as soluble species, such as H2S or SO3 2 −. This process removes anionic components from the Cu–S lattice, leading to disruption of the crystalline framework and partial amorphization. As sulfur is leached out, voids are generated within the structure, promoting a reorganization of the remaining copper atoms. This results in the formation of a more open, porous structure enriched in reduced copper species. The transformation produces a copper‐rich amorphous phase with increased surface area and newly exposed active sites, which can enhance selectivity for the electroreduction of CO2 to formate. This interpretation is also supported by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy measurements (Figure S9, Supporting Information). The raw impedance data were fit to a simple Randles’ circuit,[ 49 ] and series resistance (R s), charge transfer resistance (R ct), and double layer capacitance (C DL) were extracted. A comparative analysis of the as‐synthesized Cu2−x S, after 1 h of activation and following 15 h testing, reveals a clear decrease in both R S and R ct over time. This trend suggests enhanced electron transfer and lower Ohmic loss as the electrode evolves during operation. In addition, C DL increases as a function of operation time, evidencing an evolution of the material toward a more porous surface. Together, these changes in impedance parameters align well with the proposed structural transformation of the material, including sulfur leaching and partial amorphization, which lead to a more open and electrochemically active surface.

Finally, we also investigated the activation of the catalyst by performing repeated linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) prior to CO2RR, evaluating the impact on the subsequent catalytic selectivity. In previous experiments, three LSVs were systematically carried out before each use. To study the effect of LSVs on the CO2RR performance, Cu2−x S catalysts were tested after measuring 1, 3, and 5 LSVs from the OCP to –0.8 V versus RHE. Figure 4a shows a linear increase in FEHCOO‐ with the number of LSVs. The evolution of the catalyst and its chemical environment was monitored by operando Raman spectroscopy, comparing the as‐synthetized catalyst, at OCP, after applying 1–5 LSVs and during one hour at –0.8 V versus RHE. As shown in Figure 4b, after the first LSV, the peaks associated with the presence of oxides vanish, consistent with the reduction effect observed with catalytic cycles (see Figure 3a), exposing the active sites for selective CO2RR toward formate. After the fourth LSV, the formation of *CO3 2−/*COOH intermediates proceeds, which means a favorable activation of the catalyst toward formate production.[ 50 , 51 , 52 ] Collectively, our results provide different electrochemical treatments to significantly enhance the selectivity of CO2 reduction toward formate, compatible with large‐scale implementation.

CO2RR performance after several LSV before the CO2RR. a) FEs toward formate as a function of the number of LSV prior to the CP. b) In situ Raman spectra collected on the dry Cu2−x S, at OCP, from 1 up to 5 LSVs, and after 1 h CA at −0.8 V RHE.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates multiple electrochemical pathways to transform the chemical and structural properties of Cu2−x S‐derived electrocatalysts during CO2RR. Specifically, Cu2−x S‐based electrodes undergo a progressive evolution under electrochemical cycling, wherein sulfur leaching promotes the formation of a more open, partially amorphous, copper‐rich structure. This transformation enables the emergence of highly active Cu(0) sites that are not accessible when starting from metallic copper. As a result, the FE for formate production increases significantly—from 35% to 75% after initial use. These findings underscore the critical role of precatalyst design, in which a well‐defined precursor phase is intentionally converted into the active catalytic phase under operating conditions. Understanding and controlling this dynamic transformation pathway offers a strategic route for developing more efficient and robust catalytic systems based on in situ‐generated active phases.

Experimental Section

Preparation of the Catalytic Electrodes

The Cu2−x S electrocatalyst was prepared by drop‐casting a sodium polysulfide solution at 373 K and N2 atmosphere on electropolished copper foil (Goodfellow, 0.2 mm thick, 99.9%). For the polysulfide solution, NaOH (Scharlab), Na2SO4 (Alfa Aesar, 98%), and S (Sigma‐Aldrich, 99.98%) were mixed stoichiometrically with deionized water.

Electrochemical Measurements

The electrochemical performance of the electrodes was evaluated by LSV, chronoamperometry (CA), and CP using an Autolab potentiostat/galvanostat PGSTAT302 in a two‐compartment electrochemical cell (Redoxme). Aqueous Ag/AgCl (0.3 m KCl, ALS, Japan) and a Pt wire (Alfa Aesar) were used as reference and counter electrode, respectively, and a 1 cm2 geometrical area Cu2−x S film as working electrode. Reinforced Nafion 212 membrane was used between the anodic and cathodic compartments. CO2 saturated potassium bicarbonate (KHCO3) was selected as the electrolyte. All the potentials reported herein refer to the RHE and were calculated through the Nernst equation: V RHE = V Ag/AgCl + 0.199 + 0.059·pH. LSVs were performed between 0.1 and −1 V versus RHE with a scan rate of 0.01 V/s and step of 2 mV. CA measurements were carried out at a constant potential of −0.8 V versus RHE for 1 h, and CP measurements were performed at a constant current density of −6.5 mA cm−2 for 1 h.

Structural and Surface Characterization: Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

SEM measurements were performed with a JSM‐7000F JEOL FEG‐SEM system (Tokyo, Japan) equipped with an INCA 400 Oxford energy dispersive X‐ray spectroscopy (EDS) analyzer (Oxford, UK) operating at 15 kV (see Figure S1, Figure S3, Figure S4a, and Figure S4b, Supporting Information for the Cu2−x S EDS analysis).

Structural and Surface Characterization: X‐ray Diffraction

XRD were recorded using a Rigaku Miniflex 600 (Rigaku Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å) (see Figure S5, Supporting Information). For the ex situ XRD characterization after the reactions, the electrodes were sealed with Kapton tape under N2 atmosphere and measured with Kapton (see Figure 3b).

Structural and Surface Characterization: In Situ X‐ray Diffraction

In situ XRD patterns were collected on a Malvern Panalytical Empyrean 3 diffractometer with a Mo Kα radiation source (λ = 0.7093 Å) with focusing mirror optics and a GaliPIX3D detector in static 1D mode with 30 s integration time and an omega angle of 12.5°.

Structural and Surface Characterization: In Situ Raman Experiments

Raman spectroscopy was carried out using a Renishaw inVia Raman microscope. A 633 nm laser was used for all experiments with ≈5 mW intensity. The line focus method was employed to distribute the laser across a line on the sample, reducing the flux by 20–30 times and enabling a more ensemble‐type measurement by sampling a larger catalyst surface area simultaneously. Raman experiments were carried out under electrochemical conditions with Ag/AgCl reference and a glassy carbon counter electrode. The working electrode was the Cu2−x S electrode. An immersion objective was used to maximize the signal intensity. All the experiments were conducted in a custom‐built spectroelectrochemical reactor, operating in batch mode and with a 5 mL electrolyte volume. Typical acquisition times were 60 s per spectrum, and several points along the sample surface were acquired to ensure that the signal was homogeneous throughout.

Structural and Surface Characterization: X‐Ray Photoemission Spectroscopy Measurements

XPS was carried out by holding the sample at room temperature and illuminating it with monochromatized Al Kα light (hν = 1486.6 eV) from a monochromatic microfocus setup (SPECS Focus 600, spot size 500 μm). The excited photoelectrons were collected by a SPECS 150 hemispherical analyzer at emission and incidence angles of 40° and 60°, respectively. The overall experimental resolution was extracted from Fermi edge analysis of a reference gold sample and resulted in 0.4 eV. To average inhomogeneities of the electrodes, especially after the electrochemistry, different positions of the samples were characterized by XPS. The data presented here is the result of averaging multiple spectra of different positions for each sample.

The characterized as‐synthetized electrodes were gently sputtered in ultra high vacuum (UHV) to remove the oxide layers that formed upon exposure of Cu2–x S to air and the adventitious C that adsorbs. This procedure was shown not to reduce the Cu2–x S film.[ 36 ]

The samples were kept in a commercial glovebox during CO2RR under a continuous flow of N2 and then rinsed with MiliQ water to remove electrolyte residues. For the XPS analysis, the scraped catalyst was deposited on an indium substrate to avoid the Cu contribution from the Cu substrate. The postelectrochemistry electrodes were transported in a sealed N2 container and inserted in the UHV load‐lock without breaking the N2 atmosphere. High‐purity (99.999%) N2 was used to reduce unwanted oxidation from air exposure during the manipulation and transference of the samples.

Structural and Surface Characterization: X‐Ray Absorption Near‐Edge Structure (XANES)

X‐ray adsorption spectroscopy (XAS) measurements were recorded at the B18 beamline of Diamond Light Source (UK). The monochromator comprised Si(111) crystals operating in Quick extended X‐ray absorption fine structure (QEXAFS) mode. Calibration of the monochromator was carried out using a Cu foil, and X‐ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) spectra were recorded in transmission and fluorescence modes at the Cu K‐edge (8979 eV) using a Canberra 36‐element monolithic planar Ge pixel array detector.

Structural and Surface Characterization: Product Analysis

The H2 generation was monitored by gas chromatography measurements, coupling the sealed cell to an Agilent Micro‐GC gas chromatography. The FE was calculated through the relation: FE (%) = H2(exp)/H2(theo), where H2(exp) is the amount of evolved H2 in mol and H2(theo) corresponds to the theoretical H2 evolved calculated by Faraday's law: n(mol) = j H2 t/nF, where j H2 is the current density recorded during the CA measurement, t is the time in seconds, n is the number of electrons transferred in the reaction, and F is the Faraday constant, 96485.33 C mol−1.

The formate production was quantified by high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Electrolyte aliquots after each CP were collected and filtered with the appropriate filters before measurement to avoid problems of clogging. The product separation was performed on an ion‐exchange ligand‐exchange column, Agilent Hi‐Plex, and 20:80 acetonitrile:10 mm H2SO4 was used as the mobile phase. The measurements were conducted on HPLC Agilent 1260 Infinity II LC System provided with a diode array detector and a refractive index detector.
