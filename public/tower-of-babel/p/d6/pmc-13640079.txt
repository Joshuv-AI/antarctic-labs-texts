Nurses’ grief after patient loss: a cross-sectional study of coping strategies and emotional impact in a Nigerian tertiary hospital
Authors: Muhammad Awwal Ladan, Yakubu Salifu, Muna Alshammari, Bilkisu Kankia Lawal, Abdullahi Haruna Ibrahim, Muhammad Abdullahi Adam, Halima Abdul Musa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNurses frequently develop close therapeutic relationships with patients and experience significant emotional distress when patients die. Despite extensive literature on caregiver burden in palliative care, there is limited data on how nurses in resource-constrained settings cope with grief and the downstream effects on their well-being and practice. This study aimed to examine grief coping strategies, emotional responses, and perceived mental health impacts among nurses following patient loss in a tertiary specialist hospital in Northern Nigeria.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional survey between January and March 2025 at a high-volume (a high patient sort) Specialist hospital in Kano State, Nigeria. Using Yamane’s formula for finite population, 194registered nurses with at least one year of clinical experience (N = 194) were invited; all 194 (100%) completed a structured self‑administered questionnaire. Three domains were assessed using 4‑point Likert scales (1 = Strongly Disagree to 4 = Strongly Agree): (1) grief coping strategies (10 items), (2) attitudes and emotional responses after patient death (10 items), and (3) perceived impacts on mental health (10 items). We calculated descriptive statistics and mean domain scores; higher scores indicate stronger endorsement of the item or domain. Inferential statistics also explore gender differences and the years of clinical experiences across the three domains. Ethical approval was granted by the Kano State Ministry of Health Research Ethics Committee (NHREC/17/03/2018), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.ResultsA total of 194 nurses participated in the study. Emotional exhaustion, anxiety, sleep disturbances, and burnout concerns were commonly reported following patient loss. Peer support, spiritual practices, and relaxation techniques were the most frequently used coping strategies. Female nurses reported significantly higher coping strategy scores (p = 0.013), stronger emotional responses (p = 0.008), and greater mental health impacts (p = 0.003) than male nurses. No significant differences were observed across years of professional experience.ConclusionsPatient loss remains a significant source of emotional and psychological distress among nurses. While peer support and spirituality are important coping mechanisms, healthcare institutions should implement structured grief-support systems to strengthen resilience, promote psychological well-being, and sustain the delivery of compassionate, high-quality care.Keywords: Nurse grief, Coping strategies, Patient death, Emotional impact, Cross-sectional survey, Nigeria

Introduction

Nurses are on the front lines of healthcare, providing critical care and emotional support to patients and their families. The professional role they carry out tends to put them in proximity with the patients both physically and emotionally with some even having to bond beyond the hospital especially in those patients that have repeated hospitalization [1, 2]. When a patient dies, nurses may experience profound grief, which can manifest emotional distress, sleep disruption, fatigue, burnout, post-traumatic stress disorder, and reduced job satisfaction [3–6].

In this regard, Kostka, Borodzicz [2] asserts that death is generally associated with loss and those with either the physical or emotional association with the dead have to come to terms with such reality for them to be able to function properly subsequently. Due to the nature of their work, nurses’ duties require them to be alongside their patients in a professional capacity during their illness and at death. This may significantly contribute to occupational burnout among healthcare workers [2].

Furthermore, the emotional impact following loss of a patient may tend to manifests in various forms such as sleep disorders, diminished confidence in carrying out nursing activities leading to errors in both judgement and actions, guilt, anxiety and depressive symptoms, which may affect the satisfaction of other patients with the nurse’s role [1]. As Faremi, Olatubi [7] pointed out, the loss of a patient represents one of the most stressful experiences for nurses, particularly when compounded by limited staffing and inadequate resources. As such during care, particularly for patients with complex conditions, the persistent fear of losing the patient can be a significant source of burden. Nonetheless patient death during care is seen as one of the phases or outcomes of the healthcare system [8]. It is noteworthy, that nursing training often emphasizes supporting families after a loss but rarely addresses how nurses themselves are expected to cope in such situations [9, 10].

Baranauskas, Kalpokas [1] suggests that there exist both cognitive and behavioural efforts and strategies exhibited by the nurses towards coping with patient loss during care which in turn reduce the emotional severity of the circumstance. Whereas [8] suggest that self-efficacy of nurses play an important role in emotional preparedness and coping when a patient death occurs.

Existing literature predominantly from high-income settings highlights a range of coping strategies employed by nurses following patient loss, including peer support, formal debriefing, spiritual or religious practices, and self-care techniques such as relaxation and reflective writing [1, 2]. In sub-Saharan Africa, and Nigeria in particular, studies have documented the heavy emotional toll of caring for critically ill patients [7], yet none have systematically explored grief-specific coping strategies or the subsequent impact on nurses’ mental health.

The present study is informed by the Dual Process Model of Grief (Schut, 1999) and Meaning Reconstruction Theory [11]. The Dual Process Model proposes that individuals oscillate between confronting loss-oriented stressors and adapting to restoration-oriented demands. Meaning Reconstruction Theory suggests that bereaved individuals seek to reconstruct meaning following loss. These frameworks provide a useful lens for understanding how nurses navigate patient death while maintaining professional responsibilities and emotional well-being.

To address this gap, we conducted a cross-sectional survey of registered nurses in a high-volume tertiary specialist hospital in Northern Nigeria. The study aimed to: [1] characterise the coping strategies nurses use after patient death; [2] describe their emotional and attitudinal responses; and [3] assess perceived impacts on mental health.

Methods

Design and setting

This study employed a cross-sectional design, appropriate for investigating the immediate emotional responses and coping strategies of nurses following patient loss across multiple clinical units within a single tertiary specialist hospital. This method enabled the efficient collection of rich, context-specific data, offering valuable insights into prevalent patterns of grief and support needs [12, 13]. Data were collected between January and March 2025 in a 220‑bed tertiary specialist referral centre in Northern Nigeria, Kano State, Nigeria.

Participants

Registered nurses with at least one year of clinical experience and direct patient care responsibilities were eligible. Exclusion criteria included administrative staff and nurses on extended leave during the study period. A total of 194 nurses out of 380 at the time of the study were recruited to participate. These nurses were drawn from across medical, surgical, emergency and intensive care units of the hospital. These hospital wards were chosen due to the high patient presentation within them with debilitating medical or surgical conditions and emergencies compared to the other units within the hospital. Respondents were given questionnaires during shifts and researchers subsequently returned and collected the questionnaires if they were too busy to fill them at that time. All questionnaires were completed and returned representing 100% response rate.

Research instrument

A structured self-administered questionnaire was developed following a review of literature on nurse grief, bereavement, coping strategies, and occupational well-being. Draft items were reviewed by senior nursing academics with expertise in palliative care and nursing research to assess clarity, relevance, and content coverage. A pilot study involving ten nurses was conducted to evaluate comprehensibility and completion time. Minor modifications were made following participant feedback. The final instrument comprised three domains: grief coping strategies, attitudes following patient loss, and perceived impacts on mental health. Internal consistency testing demonstrated acceptable reliability across domains (Cronbach’s alpha ≥ 0.78).

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations were used to summarise participants’ socio-demographic characteristics and questionnaire responses. Composite scores were generated for each of the three study domains (coping strategies, attitudes following patient loss, and perceived mental health impacts) by calculating the means of the individual Likert-scale items within each domain. The resulting domain scores were summarised using means and standard deviations.

Independent samples t-tests were performed to examine differences in domain scores between male and female nurses, while one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess differences across categories of years of clinical experience. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance was established at p < 0.05.

Discussion

This study shows that patient death has a sustained emotional and psychological impact on nurses, affecting their wellbeing, professional confidence and capacity to continue providing compassionate care. While nurses commonly rely on peer support, spirituality and relaxation techniques to manage grief, persistent anxiety, sleep disturbance, emotional exhaustion and self-blame indicate that individual coping strategies alone are insufficient. These findings underscore the need for grief-informed organisational support, including formal debriefing, culturally sensitive psychosocial care and workplace structures that recognise the emotional labour of nursing.

Attitudes of nurses after patient loss

The attitudinal changes reported by nurses following patient loss reveal a complex interplay of emotional responses and their struggles to handle the grief. While acknowledging the emotional toll of feeling drained and, to a lesser extent, experiencing a sense of personal failure which is consistently reported in previous studies [14–16], the participants in this study reported a prevailing sentiment that patient death is an inherent aspect of their professional life and often serves as a catalyst for their professional growth through reflection. The findings further underscore the emotional labor inherent in nursing which was acknowledged in several studies [17, 18], where patient loss is both a source of professional development and a significant risk factor for emotional exhaustion among nurses as the participants reported a perceived increase in personal resilience as a result of these experiences while recognizing the potential for burnout with frequent exposure. This demands proactive strategies to foster resilience and prevent burnout within the specific cultural and professional contexts in the healthcare delivery system.

The loss of a patient can have profound emotional and professional consequences for nurses, who often form close therapeutic bonds through ongoing care. Grief may manifest as sadness, fatigue, sleep disturbance, and reduced job satisfaction, contributing to burnout and compassion fatigue. Beyond the personal toll, such experiences pose ethical challenges: nurses must balance their own emotional well-being with professional duties, maintain therapeutic presence despite distress, and navigate boundaries between empathy and over-involvement.

Despite high resilience scores, persistent emotional distress, including feelings of being “emotionally drained” and anxiety, was common. Mohamed and Mohamed (2015) similarly described nurses’ somatic manifestations of grief: fatigue, headaches, and appetite changes highlighting how unmanaged grief can translate into physical symptoms. Integrating somatic awareness and body-focused interventions (e.g., guided relaxation, mindful movement) into support programs may address these embodied dimensions of grief.

Coping strategies in nursing practice

Nurses facing the emotional reality of patient loss actively employ a range of coping mechanisms, prioritising social support within their professional circles and extending personal relationships with friends and family. They also find solace and strength in personal practices such as relaxation techniques and deeply rooted spiritual or religious rituals which is consistent with what was reported in several studies [14, 15]. Furthermore, a prevailing perception exists that their workplace offers a supportive atmosphere during these emotionally challenging times, suggesting an existing, though perhaps needing strengthening, network of care and understanding within their professional environment which is echoed by similar studies reporting consistent lack of support for nurses in times of such griefs [19].

The coping strategies identified in this study, peer support, spiritual practices, and relaxation techniques reflect adaptive responses that help nurses manage the emotional toll of patient loss while maintaining their professional functioning and psychological resilience [20, 21]. These coping strategies not only preserve the emotional well-being and dignity of nurses but also enable them to continue delivering compassionate, respectful care that upholds the dignity of deceased patients and provides comfort to their grieving families [22–26].

The absence of reported maladaptive coping mechanisms like alcohol use among nurses in this study, in contrast to findings from other studies [27], may be significantly influenced by the prevailing social context. The strong cultural and religious norms within Northern Nigeria due to predominance practice of Abrahamic religions (i.e. Islam and Christianity) shaping cultural behaviours and interactions [28] likely act as considerable constraints against the adoption of such behaviors as coping strategies for grief following patient loss.

This study found that nurses predominantly relied on peer support, spiritual practices, and relaxation techniques to cope with grief after patient loss. This aligns with Agyei et al. (2022), who reported that Ghanaian nurses also turned to colleagues and religious faith to process bereavement, noting that communal prayer and shared reflection fostered emotional relief in resource‑limited settings. Such collective coping underscores the importance of nurturing team cohesion and faith‑inclusive spaces in hospitals to buffer grief’s impact.

Perceived impact on nurses’ mental health

The experiences shared by nurses in this study underscore the profound and far-reaching consequences of patient loss, extending beyond the immediate professional sphere to deeply affect their personal lives which is similar to other findings reported in studies from various countries and regions worldwide [14, 18]. Their narratives reveal a consistent pattern of emotional distress, cognitive impairment, and behavioral changes that suggest a significant and potentially prolonged grieving process. This collective impact highlights the considerable psychological burden faced by nurses in their daily practice, emphasizing the urgent need for institutional and cultural support mechanisms to acknowledge and address the unique challenges they encounter in managing grief within the demanding healthcare environment. Similar position was reported by another study who also found nurses reporting with anxiety disorders and dread to go to work the next day following patient loss [16]. Reflective engagement with sensitive and emotionally charged research is essential, particularly where dual roles and exposure to distressing clinical narratives may have implications for researchers’ wellbeing [29].

Our findings also revealed sleep disturbances and burnout concerns in over two‑thirds of participants. Esplen, Wong, and Vachon (2022) showed that continuing education programs focusing on resilience and self‑care effectively reduced sleep disruption and burnout risk by teaching nurses to recognise early warning signs of compassion fatigue. This is consistent with a systematic review which emphasises that structured educational and supportive interventions are critical for addressing nurses’ palliative care learning needs and enhancing coping capacity in low- and middle-income country contexts [30] Offering regular workshops on sleep hygiene, boundary setting, and reflective practice could therefore enhance nurses’ restorative capacity and long‑term retention. These findings highlight that unmanaged grief can lead to sustained emotional distress and deteriorating mental health among nurses, underscoring the critical need for timely psychological support within healthcare systems [31].

Grief theoretical implications and future directions

While the Kübler-Ross model provides a foundational understanding of grief, contemporary theories offer more nuanced insights into nurses’ experiences [32]. The Dual Process Model [33] explains how nurses oscillate between confronting their grief and adapting to workplace demands. Our findings—that nurses rely heavily on peer support, spiritual coping, and relaxation techniques, yet continue to experience emotional exhaustion, sleep disturbances, and burnout fears—underscore the complexity of grief in clinical practice. The Dual Process Model [33] helps explain this tension: nurses must oscillate between loss-oriented coping (mourning a patient’s death) and restoration-oriented tasks (meeting the relentless demands of a busy ward). Simultaneously, Meaning Reconstruction Theory [11] suggests that when nurses can reframe their grief—by focusing on the comfort and dignity they provided at the end of life—they build resilience and find renewed purpose in their roles. Integrating these theoretical lenses clarifies why unstructured grief responses can undermine well‑being and why purposeful reflection promotes recovery.

In the context of palliative care and against the backdrop of growing debates on assisted dying in the UK and globally our results carry particular weight. As some jurisdictions move toward legalizing physician‑assisted death, nurses are asked not only to manage traditional bereavement but also to navigate moral and ethical complexities around hastening death. Contemporary studies show that nurses involved in assisted dying confront unique grief trajectories, balancing professional duties with personal values and societal expectations. Healthcare organisations must therefore extend beyond routine debriefings to offer ethics‑informed support groups, specialized training on end‑of‑life decision‑making, and forums for open discussion about assisted dying. Such interventions will help nurses process grief in a morally coherent framework, preserve compassionate care, and safeguard mental health in an evolving legal landscape.

Based on the findings, future research should map how grief‑coping strategies evolve over the span of a nursing career and vary across cultural settings where beliefs about death and dying differ. Technological innovations such as AI‑driven peer‑support platforms or real‑time mood‑tracking apps could complement traditional interventions, offering immediate, confidential assistance. Combining these tools with institutionally embedded programs (e.g., resilience workshops, narrative medicine sessions, and ethics rounds) will create a multilayered support ecosystem. Valuing nurses’ emotional experiences, healthcare systems can not only enhance job satisfaction and retention but also ensure the delivery of empathetic, high‑quality palliative care in an era of rapid legal and cultural change around assisted dying. This direction is also supported by evidence from participatory and synthesis-based studies which demonstrate that strengthening community engagement, death literacy, and social support systems is central to improving palliative care experiences for patients, caregivers, and health professionals in resource-limited settings [34, 35].

Conclusion

Key findings show that nurses experienced substantial grief-related distress after patient death, including anxiety, sleep disturbance, emotional exhaustion, self-blame and reduced concentration. They mainly coped through peer support, spirituality and relaxation techniques, but these strategies were not sufficient to prevent ongoing emotional burden. This study illuminates the multifaceted nature of nurses’ grief where social support, spiritual practices, and personal resilience coexist with persistent emotional exhaustion, sleep disruption, and burnout anxiety. Grounded in the Dual Process Model, our findings reveal how nurses continually oscillate between confronting the loss of patients and refocusing on clinical duties. Meaning Reconstruction Theory further clarifies that reframing grief by recognizing the dignity and comfort nurses provide at the end of life can transform loss into professional purpose and personal growth.

To translate these insights into practice, healthcare institutions must adopt systematic, theory‑informed interventions. Stigma‑free peer‑support networks, spiritually inclusive debriefings, and accessible mental health services should be integral to workplace culture. Regular resilience training and ethical debriefing sessions particularly in regions debating assisted dying, will help nurses process moral complexities and maintain compassionate care.

It is critically important to create a fostering a compassionate, grief‑literate environment to benefit both nurses and patients. Acknowledging the emotional labor inherent in nursing and providing comprehensive, sustained support enables healthcare leaders to cultivate a resilient workforce that remains engaged, empathetic, and mentally healthy. Such systemic change not only safeguards nurse well‑being but also upholds the highest quality of palliative and end‑of‑life care in an evolving global landscape.

Limitation.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design captures nurses’ experiences and perceptions at a single point in time and therefore does not allow causal relationships to be established between patient loss, coping strategies, and mental health outcomes. Second, the study was conducted in a single tertiary healthcare facility in Northern Nigeria, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to nurses working in other healthcare settings or geographical regions.

Third, data were collected using self-reported questionnaires and may therefore be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias, potentially influencing participants’ responses regarding their grief experiences and coping mechanisms. Fourth, although the questionnaire was developed through a review of relevant literature, expert review, and pilot testing, comprehensive psychometric validation was not undertaken. Consequently, the reliability and validity of the instrument should be interpreted with caution.

Finally, while inferential analysis was conducted to explore differences in grief-related experiences according to gender and years of professional experience, the study was not designed to identify predictors of grief outcomes. Future studies employing longitudinal designs, larger multi-centre samples, and fully validated instruments are recommended to enhance understanding of nurses’ grief experiences and the factors that influence coping and psychological well-being following patient loss.
