SGLT2 Inhibition Attenuates Renal Tubular Senescence by Suppressing CTRP1‐Mediated Glucotoxic Stress in Diabetic Kidney Disease
Authors: Lina Gu, Buhui Liu, Lei Zhang, Jing Zhao, Weiming He, Hong Lang, Wei Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRenal tubular senescence is a defining pathological feature of diabetic kidney disease (DKD) and a key driver of disease progression. While glucotoxic stress is recognized as a major contributor to tubular aging, the upstream regulatory mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Here, we investigated the role of C1q/TNF‐related protein 1 (CTRP1) in DKD‐associated tubular senescence and its mechanistic link to SGLT2‐mediated glucose uptake. We conducted a retrospective observational case–control analysis of human renal biopsy specimens, complemented by mechanistic experiments in NRK‐52E cells and a high‐fat diet/streptozotocin/uninephrectomy (HFD–STZ–UNx) rat model. Renal cortical tissues from DKD patients and controls were analyzed via immunohistochemistry and Western blotting. CTRP1 expression increased progressively with DKD severity and was positively correlated with the senescence markers p21 (R = 0.498) and p16 (R = 0.494). Bioinformatic analysis of CTRP1‐deficient mice revealed consistent SGLT2 downregulation, which was validated experimentally in NRK‐52E cells, where CTRP1 overexpression increased SGLT2 expression and glucose uptake, particularly under hyperglycemic conditions. In HFD/STZ/UNx‐induced DKD rats, dapagliflozin (0.1 mg/kg/day) improved metabolic abnormalities and renal dysfunction and attenuated tubular senescence. Dapagliflozin suppressed CTRP1–SGLT2 axis activation, reduced glucose uptake in renal tubular cells, and markedly decreased the proportion of CTRP1+SGLT2+ double‐positive tubular cells. These findings support the presence of a previously unrecognized CTRP1–SGLT2 signaling axis associated with glucose‐mediated tubular senescence in DKD and provide mechanistic insight into SGLT2 inhibitor‐mediated renoprotection.

Introduction

Diabetic kidney disease is the leading cause of end‐stage renal disease worldwide, affecting 40% of diabetic patients [1, 2]. Despite standard RAS blockade therapy, DKD continues to progress inexorably toward ESRD in a significant proportion of patients [3, 4]. Although research has historically focused on glomerular pathology, accumulating evidence has demonstrated that tubular injury and dysfunction are equally critical in driving disease progression [5]. Cellular senescence has emerged as a pivotal process that not only reflects tubular cell injury but also actively propagates renal fibrosis and functional decline through paracrine mechanisms [6, 7].

Cellular senescence is characterized by irreversible cell cycle arrest, which is characterized by the accumulation of p16INK4a (p16) and p21CIP1/WAF1 (p21), loss of nuclear envelope integrity (LAMINB1 downregulation), and acquisition of a senescence‐associated secretory phenotype (SASP) [8]. Senescent tubular cells secrete proinflammatory cytokines and profibrotic mediators, creating tissue microenvironments favorable for chronic inflammation and matrix deposition [9, 10]. In DKD, tubular senescent cells are strongly correlated with decreased renal function and progression to ESRD [11]. Interventions that reduce tubular senescence have demonstrated renoprotective effects in preclinical models; however, the upstream molecular events triggering tubular senescence remain incompletely understood [12].

Glucotoxicity—chronic hyperglycemia‐induced direct cellular toxicity—is recognized as a central driver of diabetic complications [13]. In proximal tubular epithelial cells, excessive glucose induces mitochondrial dysfunction, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, advanced glycation end product (AGE) accumulation, and proinflammatory signaling activation [14]. These factors converge to impair DNA repair and activate senescence programs [15]. Sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) is responsible for reabsorbing 90% of filtered glucose in the proximal tubule [16]. SGLT2 inhibitors (dapagliflozin, empagliflozin, and canagliflozin) effectively slow DKD progression and reduce albuminuria in clinical trials [17]; however, the upstream regulatory mechanisms governing SGLT2 expression in DKD remain incompletely elucidated.

C1q/TNF‐related protein 1 (CTRP1) is a member of the C1q/TNF superfamily of secreted proteins 11originally identified as adipokines [18]. CTRP1 exerts pleiotropic effects on glucose and lipid metabolism [19], vascular function, and inflammatory responses [20]. In metabolic disease, CTRP1 expression is altered in obesity, insulin resistance, and diabetes [21]; however, its expression profile and pathophysiological roles in the kidney—particularly in DKD—have received limited attention. Bioinformatic mining of publicly available transcriptomic datasets from CTRP1‐deficient mice revealed consistent and robust downregulation of SGLT2 expression in renal tubular epithelial cells across multiple independent studies [22]. Temporal expression profiling further demonstrated that SGLT2 mRNA levels are closely related to fluctuations in CTRP1 expression, suggesting a direct or indirect regulatory relationship [23].

This study was undertaken to test the hypotheses that CTRP1 functions as an upstream regulator of SGLT2 expression and that dysregulated CTRP1–SGLT2 signaling in DKD contributes to increased glucose uptake, glucotoxic stress, and tubular senescence. We characterized CTRP1 expression across DKD disease stages, correlated it with senescence markers, mechanistically evaluated CTRP1‐dependent SGLT2 upregulation in cultured cells, and validated the pathway in a progressive DKD animal model via SGLT2 inhibition. These integrated approaches suggest a previously unrecognized CTRP1–SGLT2–glucotoxicity–senescence axis that may contribute to tubulointerstitial injury in DKD.

Materials and Methods

Human Tissue Collection

This retrospective observational case–control study included all eligible adults undergoing native kidney biopsy at the Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine between March 2021 and September 2022. DKD tissues were obtained from 18 patients with biopsy‐confirmed diabetic kidney disease, whereas control tissues were retrospectively identified from three non‐DKD patients biopsied during the same period whose renal histology showed largely preserved architecture without evidence of DKD and whose serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen levels were within the normal range. The cohort comprised 23 participants (18 DKD and 5 controls). Patients were classified according to KDIGO staging criteria, with representative samples from stages G1–G5 included. Those with other primary kidney diseases, active infection, malignancy, or insufficient tissue were excluded.

Sample size was determined by the number of eligible specimens available during the study period; no formal a priori sample size or power calculation was performed, and the analysis should therefore be considered exploratory. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table S1. Clinical data, including age, eGFR, and UACR, were collected at biopsy. Tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for immunohistochemistry or snap‐frozen for Western blotting. For correlation analyses of CTRP1 with p16 and p21, cases with unavailable or unevaluable staining were excluded, yielding a final sample size of 18. Ethics approval (2022NL‐090‐01) was obtained, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Definition of Variables

The primary exposure was renal cortical CTRP1 expression, quantified as the percentage of CTRP1‐positive tubular cells in each biopsy core. Primary outcomes were tubular senescence markers, assessed as the percentages of p16‐positive and p21‐positive tubular cells. Secondary outcomes included renal function parameters (estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR], serum creatinine, and blood urea nitrogen), albuminuria (urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio), and histological markers of renal cortical fibrosis. DKD cases were staged according to kidney disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria. Age was categorized as 18–35, 36–64, or ≥ 65 years, and eGFR was stratified as 30–89 or < 30 mL/min/1.73 m 2 to reflect clinically meaningful categories of renal impairment.

Analyses were conducted using complete cases; samples with missing or unevaluable staining for a given marker were excluded from the corresponding analysis without imputation.

Immunohistochemistry and Analysis

Paraffin‐embedded sections (5 μm) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, treated with 3% H2O2, and subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). After being blocked with 10% goat serum, the sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with antibodies against CTRP1, p21, or p16 (1:100), followed by incubation with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500) for 1 h at room temperature and subsequent DAB development. Whole‐slide images were acquired via a KFBIO scanner. Each whole‐slide image contained one or more renal biopsy cores, and the percentage of cells positive for CTRP1, p21, or p16 in each biopsy core was quantified using QuPath software (version 0.6.0, RRID:SCR_018257).

Western Blotting

Tissues or cultured cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease/phosphatase inhibitors. Proteins (30–50 μg) were separated by 10% SDS–PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, blocked with 5% nonfat milk, and incubated overnight with primary antibodies (1:1000) against CTRP1, SGLT2, p21, Collagen I, α‐SMA, or GAPDH (1:5000). After HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies were added (1:5000, 1 h), the signals were detected via ECL and quantified via ImageJ (RRID:SCR_003070).

Antibodies and Reagents

The following primary antibodies were used in this study: anti‐CTRP1 (Abcam, Cat# ab25973, RRID:AB_448937), anti‐SGLT2 (mouse monoclonal, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Cat# sc‐393 350, RRID:AB_2814658), anti‐p21 (rabbit monoclonal, Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# 2947S, RRID:AB_823586), anti‐p16INK4a/CDKN2A (rabbit monoclonal, ABclonal, Cat# A11651, RRID:AB_2861619), anti‐COL1A1 (rabbit monoclonal, Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# 91144S, RRID:AB_2800169), anti‐α‐SMA (rabbit monoclonal, Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# 19245S, RRID:AB_2734735), and anti‐GAPDH (rabbit monoclonal, ABclonal, Cat# A19056, RRID:AB_2862549).

Bioinformatics Analysis

Transcriptomic datasets of renal tubular epithelial cells from CTRP1‐knockout and control mice were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (GSE127332) [22] to assess glucose transporter expression, with a focus on SGLT2. Time‐series transcriptomic data over 48 h (GSE217853) [23] were further used to quantify CTRP1 and SGLT2 mRNA levels. A generalized additive mixed‐effects model was applied to the time‐series data to characterize their expression trajectories and evaluate parallel changes over the 48‐h period.

Cell Culture and Lentiviral Transduction

NRK‐52E cells (RRID:CVCL_0468) were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. The cells were transduced with CTRP1‐overexpressing (LV‐CTRP1‐OE) or control (LV‐CTRP1‐NC) lentivirus; GFP positivity confirmed transduction. After transduction, the cells were cultured under normal‐glucose (5.5 mM) or high‐glucose (30 mM) conditions for 48 h. These conditions were selected with reference to published in vitro models of diabetic hyperglycemic stress in renal tubular epithelial cells [24, 25].

Glucose Uptake Assay

Glucose uptake was measured via a Glucose Uptake Assay Kit‐Blue (Dojindo, UP01) [26]. The cells were washed and incubated in glucose‐free DMEM (15 min) and then in probe solution (1:500, 15 min). The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was measured via flow cytometry.

Animal Model

All animal experiments were conducted at the Animal Experiment Center of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine, Xianlin Campus, Nanjing, China. Male Sprague–Dawley rats (RRID:RGD_70508, 8 weeks old, 200–250 g at baseline) were housed under controlled conditions (22°C ± 2°C, 50%–60% humidity, 12 h light/dark cycle) with free access to food and water. They were then fed a high‐fat diet (D12462), subjected to unilateral nephrectomy, and injected with streptozotocin (50 mg/kg) to establish DKD. The HFD/STZ/UNx protocol was selected because it integrates insulin resistance, hyperglycemia, and nephron loss, thereby reproducibly accelerating DKD progression and inducing metabolic and renal abnormalities that resemble progressive DKD [27, 28]. Hyperglycemic/proteinuric rats were randomized using a computer‐generated random number sequence into the following groups: Sham, DKD, DAPA, and DKD + DAPA (n = 5/group). Sample size was based on prior experience with this DKD model and practical constraints. The experimental unit was a single animal. Dapagliflozin (0.1 mg/kg/day) or saline was administered orally for 12 weeks. The dapagliflozin dose and treatment duration were selected with reference to previous experimental studies and adapted to the present DKD model [29]. Animals were monitored daily; no animals reached predefined humane endpoints or required early euthanasia. Perioperative anesthesia and analgesia were provided according to institutional protocols. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine and carried out in strict accordance with the institutional guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals (Approval No. 202308A014).

Metabolic and Renal Function Assessments

After 12 weeks, glucose tolerance tests (GTTs) and insulin tolerance tests (ITTs) were performed. Serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, total cholesterol, and triglycerides were measured.

Histopathology

Sections were stained with PAS, HE, Masson's trichrome, and SA‐β‐gal. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used to examine ultrastructural changes (Hitachi HT7800, × 7000). Histological image quantification was performed by investigators blind to group allocation.

Dual Immunofluorescence and Colocalization Analysis

The sections were incubated with primary antibodies against CTRP1 (rabbit, 1:100) and SGLT2 (mouse, 1:100), followed by Alexa Fluor‐conjugated secondary antibodies and DAPI staining. Images were acquired (Pannoramic SCAN II) and analyzed (v0.6.0, RRID:SCR_018257) for colocalization and MFI. Immunofluorescence image quantification was performed by investigators blind to group allocation.

Flow Cytometry

Renal tubular cells were isolated via enzymatic digestion. The cells were incubated with a glucose uptake probe, fixed, permeabilized, and stained with anti‐CTRP1 and anti‐SGLT2 antibodies, followed by incubation with fluorescent secondary antibodies. The signals were analyzed by flow cytometry.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous data are expressed as the mean ± SEM. Data distribution was assessed using Shapiro–Wilk normality tests, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Brown–Forsythe and Bartlett tests. When appropriate, right‐skewed variables were log10‐transformed to better meet model assumptions. In particular, serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, and serum total cholesterol and triglyceride levels were analyzed on log10‐transformed values, whereas the corresponding figures display the mean ± SEM of the untransformed data.

For two‐group comparisons, unpaired Student's t‐tests were used when normality and equal variance assumptions were reasonably satisfied. For comparisons among more than two groups, one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test was applied under the same assumptions. Glucose and insulin tolerance curves were analyzed by two‐way repeated‐measures ANOVA (factors: group and time), and the corresponding areas under the curve were compared using one‐way ANOVA. Pearson correlation was used to assess associations between continuous variables. A two‐sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Given the limited sample size of the human biopsy cohort, multivariable regression modeling was not performed, and we focused on unadjusted correlations between CTRP1, senescence markers, and clinical variables.

Results

CTRP1 Is Pathologically Upregulated in DKD and Is Positively Correlated With Renal Tubular Senescence

Immunohistochemical staining revealed stage‐dependent upregulation of CTRP1 expression across advanced DKD stages, with the highest levels observed in the G3 and G5 stages compared with those in normal controls (Figure 1A). Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant increase in the proportion of CTRP1‐positive cells in DKD patients, particularly in those with eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2, compared with normal controls (Figure 1B). CTRP1 expression also tended to be greater in older individuals, especially those aged ≥ 65 years (Figure 1C). Single‐cell correlation analysis revealed significant positive correlations between CTRP1 expression and both p21‐positive tubular cells (Pearson R = 0.498, p = 0.036) and p16‐positive tubular cells (Pearson R = 0.494, p = 0.037) (Figure 1D). Western blot analyses indicated that CTRP1 was significantly upregulated in DKD renal cortical tissues at the protein level compared with normal controls (p < 0.01; Figure 1E,F). Consistently, the senescence marker p21 was markedly increased at the protein level in DKD tissues (p < 0.01; Figure 1E,F). Parallel to these senescence‐associated changes, the fibrosis markers α‐SMA and Collagen I were also markedly increased in DKD renal cortical tissues, as evidenced by Western blot quantification (Figure 1E,F).

CTRP1 Drives Increased Glucose Uptake by Upregulating SGLT2 Expression

To elucidate the mechanism by which CTRP1 induces renal tubular senescence, bioinformatic and functional studies were conducted (Figure 2). Mining of transcriptomic datasets from CTRP1‐deficient mice revealed consistent downregulation of SGLT2 expression across independent datasets (Figure 2A). Temporal expression analysis via generalized additive mixed‐effects modeling revealed that SGLT2 expression increased and decreased in parallel with CTRP1 levels over a 48‐h monitoring period (Figure 2B). In NRK‐52E cells, CTRP1 overexpression markedly increased SGLT2 protein levels and concomitantly elevated p21, collagen I, and α‐SMA levels, particularly under high‐glucose conditions (Figure 2C,D). Validation of the glucose uptake probe confirmed that, compared with normal glucose (5.5 mM), high‐glucose treatment (30 mM) significantly increased glucose uptake (Figure 2E). Flow cytometry further demonstrated that compared with CTRP1‐overexpressing cells under normal glucose conditions, CTRP1‐overexpressing cells under high‐glucose conditions showed a pronounced rightward shift in glucose probe fluorescence and a significantly greater mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) (p < 0.001; Figure 2F).

SGLT2 Inhibition Alleviates Renal Injury, Fibrosis, and Tubular Senescence in DKD Rats

On the basis of the importance of the CTRP1–SGLT2 axis in DKD‐induced tubular senescence, SGLT2 inhibitor intervention studies were performed in a high‐fat diet/streptozotocin/unilateral nephrectomy (HFD/STZ/UNx)‐induced DKD rat model (Figure 3A). DKD rats showed altered body weight trajectories during the experimental period (Figure 3B). DKD rats developed severe hyperglycemia, glucose intolerance, and insulin resistance, all of which were significantly ameliorated by dapagliflozin treatment, as reflected by longitudinal blood glucose curves and GTT/ITT area under the curve (AUC) analyses (Figure 3C–E). The serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen levels were markedly elevated in DKD rats and significantly reduced in the dapagliflozin‐treated group (Figure 3F). DKD rats also exhibited dyslipidemia with increased total cholesterol and triglyceride levels, which were improved by dapagliflozin (Figure 3G). Histopathological evaluation revealed extensive glomerular injury, tubular damage, and interstitial fibrosis in DKD kidneys, together with strong SA‐β‐gal staining and ultrastructural abnormalities such as podocyte foot process effacement and glomerular basement membrane disruption; these changes were substantially attenuated by dapagliflozin (Figure 3H). Western blot analyses revealed that the DKD group showed markedly increased renal cortical expression of CTRP1, SGLT2, p21, collagen I, and α‐SMA, whereas dapagliflozin treatment significantly reduced the expression of these proteins (Figure 3I,J).

CTRP1 And SGLT2 Demonstrate Spatial Colocalization and Functional Coupling in Renal Proximal Tubules

Dual immunofluorescence staining revealed prominent subcellular colocalization of CTRP1 (red) and SGLT2 (green) in proximal tubular epithelial cells from all experimental groups, with colocalized signals appearing yellow in merged images (Figure 4A). Single‐cell quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensities revealed significant positive correlations between CTRP1 and SGLT2 in the Sham, DKD, DAPA, and DKD + DAPA groups (Spearman r = 0.15–0.37, p < 0.0001 for all; Figure 4B). The proportion of CTRP1+SGLT2+ double‐positive cells was markedly increased in the DKD group compared with the Sham group and was reduced in the DKD + DAPA group (p < 0.001; Figure 4C). Consistently, the CTRP1 MFI in SGLT2+ tubular cells was significantly elevated in DKD rats and decreased following treatment, whereas SGLT2 MFI showed relatively modest changes across groups (Figure 4D,E).

SGLT2 Inhibition Exerts Renoprotective Effects by Suppressing CTRP1‐Mediated Glucose Uptake

Flow cytometric analysis of renal tubular cells further revealed SGLT2‐dependent glucose uptake according to the CTRP1 expression status (Figure 5). After exclusion of debris and appropriate negative controls (Figure 5A–C), SGLT2+ tubular epithelial cells were subdivided into SGLT2+CTRP1+ (Q2) and SGLT2+CTRP1− (Q1) subpopulations (Figure 5D,E). In both the Sham and DKD groups, the SGLT2+ subpopulation displayed significantly greater glucose probe fluorescence than did the CTRP1‐negative subpopulation (Figure 5F,G). In DKD rats, overall glucose uptake in SGLT2+ tubular cells was markedly increased, as indicated by a rightward shift in the BV421‐A fluorescence histogram, whereas dapagliflozin treatment shifted the distribution leftward and significantly reduced the MFI toward Sham levels (Figure 5I,J). These data indicate that SGLT2 inhibition diminishes the CTRP1‐dependent increase in glucose uptake in renal tubular cells, thereby attenuating glucotoxic stress and cellular senescence.

Schematic Summary of the Proposed CTRP1‐SGLT2‐Glucotoxicity‐Senescence Axis in DKD

To integrate the human tissue, cell, and animal findings, a schematic model of the proposed CTRP1‐SGLT2‐glucotoxicity‐senescence‐fibrosis axis in DKD is presented in Figure 6. In this model, CTRP1 upregulation is associated with increased SGLT2 expression and enhanced tubular glucose uptake, leading to glucotoxic stress, senescence‐associated signaling, and fibrotic injury. Dapagliflozin is proposed to interrupt this pathogenic cascade by reducing SGLT2‐dependent glucose uptake, alleviating intracellular glucotoxic stress, attenuating tubular senescence, and suppressing fibrosis.

Schematic illustration of a proposed CTRP1–SGLT2–glucotoxicity–senescence–fibrosis axis and the putative mechanism of SGLT2 inhibition in DKD. Left panel: Chronic hyperglycemia activates metabolic stress pathways and is associated with increased CTRP1 and SGLT2 expression, thereby promoting an enhanced glucose reabsorption phenotype. Middle panel: Increased SGLT2 may contribute to excessive glucose influx, mitochondrial stress, and the accumulation of ROS and AGEs, with subsequent loss of nuclear envelope integrity. These changes are associated with the induction of senescence, characterized by p16/p21 accumulation and SASP activation. Right panel: SGLT2 inhibition with dapagliflozin may reduce glucose uptake, intracellular glucotoxic stress, and ROS generation, improve mitochondrial function, attenuate senescence‐associated signaling, and decrease CTRP1 expression, thereby helping to interrupt this pathogenic cycle and preserve renal function.

Discussion

This study identifies a previously uncharacterized CTRP1–SGLT2 axis that mediates glucose‐driven tubular senescence in DKD and provides mechanistic evidence for SGLT2 inhibitor‐mediated renoprotection. The present findings suggest that CTRP1 is elevated in the renal cortex of DKD patients and correlates positively with senescence markers p21. In vitro and animal data further support a possible role for CTRP1 in the regulation of SGLT2 and tubular glucose uptake. CTRP1+SGLT2+ double‐positive tubular cells were also increased in DKD, whereas their abundance was reduced after SGLT2 inhibition.

CTRP1 upregulation in DKD is associated with tubular senescence. Our analyses revealed stage‐dependent CTRP1 upregulation with increased CTRP1‐positive tubular cells observed in patients with reduced eGFR and more advanced DKD stages, suggesting that CTRP1 may participate in disease progression rather than being a passive biomarker. Strong positive correlations with p16 (R = 0.494) and p21 (R = 0.498) support a close association between CTRP1 and tubular senescence in human DKD [12, 30]. These correlations are mechanistically significant because p16 and p21 are canonical CDK inhibitors that promote cell cycle arrest in response to DNA damage and metabolic stress. Concurrent elevation of CTRP1, p16, and p21 with elevated SA‐β‐gal activity strongly indicates that CTRP1 functions in conjunction with the senescence program.

An age‐related increase in CTRP1‐positive tubular cells was also observed suggesting a potential link between CTRP1 upregulation and renal aging‐related injury in DKD [31]. Hyperglycemia and metabolic stress may synergize with intrinsic aging processes [32, 33]. Aging is associated with altered adipokine secretion [34], chronic inflammation [35], and decreased autophagy [36]—all potentially potentiate CTRP1‐mediated tubular injury. These findings suggest that elderly DKD patients may benefit from aggressive therapeutic strategies targeting the CTRP1–SGLT2 axis or combination therapies addressing metabolic dysregulation and the senescence burden.

CTRP1 is an upstream SGLT2 regulator. Bioinformatic mining of CTRP1‐deficient mouse datasets [22] revealed consistent SGLT2 downregulation, which was validated in NRK‐52E cells, where CTRP1 overexpression markedly increased SGLT2 protein and glucose uptake, particularly under hyperglycemic conditions. This hyperglycemia‐dependent amplification may reflect positive feedback whereby high glucose induces CTRP1, driving SGLT2 upregulation and further increasing intracellular glucose, perpetuating glucotoxic stress.

The underlying molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood. CTRP1 may indirectly modulate SLC5A2 transcription by influencing upstream transcription factors such as HNF1α, FOXO3a, or NF‐κB [37, 38]. Alternatively, CTRP1 may modulate SGLT2 mRNA stability through microRNAs or influence protein trafficking/degradation. CTRP1 can also act through metabolic intermediates linking nutrient sensing to epigenetic remodeling [39]. Dual immunofluorescence and flow cytometry demonstrated substantial CTRP1–SGLT2 spatial colocalization; CTRP1 + SGLT2+ cells exhibited significantly greater glucose uptake than did CTRP1 − SGLT2+ cells, confirming that CTRP1 functionally enhances SGLT2‐mediated transport. These findings suggest functional coupling and spatial proximity, although direct molecular interaction remains to be established.

The vicious cycle of glucotoxicity and senescence. In vitro and in vivo data support a model in which the CTRP1–SGLT2 axis drives a vicious cycle of glucotoxicity and senescence [40]. Increased SGLT2 increases the level of intracellular glucose 10–15‐fold, overwhelming the mitochondrial capacity and generating ROS, AGEs, and lipid peroxides. These insults trigger DNA damage responses, activate p53/p21, and induce irreversible cell cycle arrest. LAMINB1 loss destabilizes the nuclear envelope, compromising genomic integrity. Senescent tubular cells adopt the SASP; secrete TGF‐β, collagen I, α‐SMA, IL‐6, and TNF‐α; recruit and activate myofibroblasts; and amplify inflammatory signaling [41]. Importantly, senescent cells may perpetuate CTRP1 expression through autocrine/paracrine loops [42, 43], sustaining SGLT2 upregulation and forming a positive feedback loop that accelerates tubular injury and fibrosis. This self‐amplifying pathogenic loop is consistent with emerging paradigms wherein initial injury triggers maladaptive cellular programs that become self‐sustaining [44].

SGLT2 inhibition breaks the cycle. Dapagliflozin effectively interrupts the CTRP1–SGLT2–glucotoxicity–senescence axis. In HFD/STZ/UNx DKD rats, dapagliflozin improved glycemic control, reduced the serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen levels, attenuated tubular senescence (p21), and suppressed fibrosis. Mechanistically, dapagliflozin directly inhibits SGLT2, reducing tubular glucose reabsorption 60%–70%, thereby lowering intracellular glucose and glucotoxic stress [45, 46]. Importantly, dapagliflozin secondarily reduced CTRP1 expression and decreased the number of CTRP1 + SGLT2+ double‐positive cells, suggesting that alleviating glucotoxic stress disrupted the positive feedback loop sustaining CTRP1 upregulation. These findings provide mechanistic evidence supporting the renoprotective effects of SGLT2 inhibitors in large clinical trials (CREDENCE [47], DAPA‐CKD [48], and EMPA‐KIDNEY [49]) and suggest that the suppression of tubular senescence contributes to their efficacy.

SGLT2 inhibitors exert renoprotective effects in patients with well‐controlled glycemia, indicating benefits extend beyond lowering systemic glucose. Local tubular glucose uptake reduction—independent of blood glucose—suffices to mitigate glucotoxicity, attenuate senescence, and slow fibrosis. This mechanistic insight has important implications: patients with elevated renal CTRP1 expression or high tubular senescence burden may benefit from particular SGLT2 inhibitors. Future clinical studies incorporating renal CTRP1 measurement could enable personalized risk stratification and treatment optimization.

Literature contextualization and novel contributions. Our findings complement and extend prior SGLT2 inhibitor and tubular senescence research. Previous studies have demonstrated that SGLT2 inhibitors reduce oxidative stress, inflammation, and fibrosis in DKD patients, but the regulatory mechanisms upstream of SGLT2 expression remain obscure [50]. By identifying CTRP1 as a candidate upstream regulator, we identified a missing pathophysiological link and revealed potential combination therapy targets. Inhibiting both CTRP1 (via antibodies, small‐molecule inhibitors, or siRNAs) and SGLT2 (via dapagliflozin/empagliflozin) could achieve synergistic renoprotection by simultaneously suppressing SGLT2 upregulation and blocking residual activity.

Our work contributes to the growing literature on tubular senescence in CKD. While senescence has been extensively studied in glomerular cells, tubular senescence has received less attention despite its clear disease progression. We provide direct evidence that glucotoxicity is a major tubular senescence driver in DKD and that targeting glucose uptake can attenuate senescence induction and reduce the senescent cell burden. This finding aligns with recent studies demonstrating that senolytics (dasatinib + quercetin) reduce the senescent cell burden and improve renal function in experimental CKD, suggesting combining SGLT2 inhibitors with senolytics may offer additive/synergistic benefits [51].

Our study expands the CTRP family adipokine functional repertoire. While CTRP1 has been studied primarily in terms of systemic metabolism, cardiovascular function, and adipose biology, the role of CTRP1 in kidney disease has been underexplored [19]. We demonstrated that CTRP1, previously reported mainly as a circulating biomarker [52], may also act as an active renal pathophysiologic mediator with cell‐autonomous and paracrine effects on tubular glucose handling and senescence. This opens avenues for investigating other CTRP family members (CTRP2–15) in kidney disease and their therapeutic potential.

Study limitations and future directions. While strong CTRP1–SGLT2 associations and functional evidence for CTRP1‐dependent SGLT2 upregulation have been reported, the precise molecular mechanisms involved remain incompletely defined. Future studies employing ChIP‐seq, promoter‐reporter assays, and CRISPR‐based genome editing should map CTRP1 binding sites and establish direct causal relationships. Our in vitro studies utilized NRK‐52E cells; validation in primary human proximal tubular cells and patient‐derived organoids would strengthen translational relevance. Renal cortical tissue lysate CTRP1 assessment precludes cell type‐specific resolution; scRNA‐seq and spatial transcriptomics could reveal CTRP1 expression patterns across cell types. Animal studies have employed single dapagliflozin doses and a fixed 12‐week duration; dose–response and longer‐term studies could optimize dosing and assess renoprotective durability. The human biopsy cohort was derived from a single tertiary center and comprised a modest number of DKD and non‐DKD control cases, which limits statistical power. Given the limited sample size of the human biopsy cohort, we did not perform multivariable regression modeling and focused instead on unadjusted correlations between CTRP1, senescence markers, and clinical variables. Because no formal sensitivity analyses were pre‐specified, the statistical findings from the human biopsy cohort should be regarded as hypothesis‐generating rather than confirmatory. We did not directly manipulate renal CTRP1 in vivo; tubular‐specific CTRP1 knockout mice or neutralizing antibodies would enable definitive target validation. However, whether the CTRP1–SGLT2 axis operates in other CKD forms or acute kidney injury remains unclear.

Future research should prioritize several directions. First, future studies should elucidate the upstream signals driving CTRP1 upregulation, including hypoxia‐inducible factors, NF‐κB, and oxidative stress‐responsive transcription factors, thereby identifying additional therapeutic entry points. Second, CTRP1‐targeted therapeutics, such as small‐molecule inhibitors, neutralizing antibodies, and antisense oligonucleotides, should be developed and evaluated in combination with SGLT2 inhibitors for synergistic renoprotection. Third, future studies should investigate whether CTRP1 modulates other tubular functions, including sodium reabsorption, acid–base handling, and phosphate transport. Fourth, CTRP1 should be assessed as a urinary or circulating biomarker for DKD risk stratification and treatment response prediction, thereby supporting precision medicine. Finally, this work could be extended to human kidney organoids and patient‐derived iPSC models to facilitate mechanistic dissection and human‐relevant drug screening.

Clinical implications. Our findings provide experimental evidence that suppression of tubular glucotoxic stress and senescence may contribute to the renoprotective effects of SGLT2 inhibition. However, the translational value of renal or circulating CTRP1 as a biomarker, as well as the potential benefit of CTRP1‐targeted combination strategies, requires validation in larger clinical and mechanistic studies.

Conclusion

This study supports the presence of a previously unrecognized CTRP1–SGLT2 axis associated with glucose‐mediated tubular senescence in DKD. CTRP1 was increased in our DKD cohort, particularly in patients with advanced renal dysfunction, and its expression was positively correlated with senescence markers. CTRP1 is closely associated with SGLT2 expression and glucose uptake in renal tubular cells.

CTRP1+SGLT2+ double‐positive tubular cells showed higher glucose uptake and were reduced after dapagliflozin treatment in the experimental model. In the experimental model, dapagliflozin was associated with reduced glucose uptake, attenuated tubular senescence, and improved renal structural and functional parameters. These findings provide mechanistic insight into SGLT2 inhibitor‐mediated renoprotection and highlight CTRP1 as a candidate molecule for further mechanistic and translational investigation in DKD.

Further studies should elucidate CTRP1‐dependent SGLT2‐mediated regulatory molecular mechanisms, validate CTRP1 as a clinical biomarker and therapeutic target, and explore simultaneous glucose uptake, senescence, and fibrosis‐targeting combination therapies for DKD.
