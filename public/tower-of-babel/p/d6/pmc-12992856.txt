D-serine: A Multitalented Neuromodulator in Brain Function, Systemic Homeostasis, and Disease
Authors: Jing Wang, Yujin Guo, Wenxiu Han, Hailiang Zhang, Pei Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractD-serine is an endogenous D-amino acid and a crucial co-agonist for the N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor, well known as a key regulator of synaptic plasticity, learning, and memory. This review summarizes recent advances that extend beyond this canonical role, positioning D-serine as a dynamic nexus linking brain function to systemic homeostasis. We explore its spatiotemporal dynamics in synaptic microdomains, sophisticated epigenetic and transcriptional regulation, and emerging role as a metabolic sensor linking cellular energetics to neuronal signaling. Furthermore, we highlight novel non-canonical signaling pathways, including its function as a ligand for the glutamate delta-1 receptor (GluD1) and its direct role in peripheral organs like the gut and kidney. The pathophysiological implications of disrupted D-serine homeostasis are detailed across a spectrum of CNS disorders—from schizophrenia and Alzheimer’s disease (AD) to addiction and neuropathic pain—as well as in peripheral conditions including diabetic complications and osteoporosis. Finally, we discuss the translational potential of targeting D-serine pathways through innovative therapeutic strategies—such as serine racemase (SR) modulators, D-amino acid oxidase (DAAO) inhibitors, and gene therapy—and its promise as a biomarker for personalized medicine in neurology and psychiatry.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10571-026-01696-9.Keywords: D-serine, NMDA receptor, Synaptic plasticity, Neuromodulation, Metabolic sensor, Therapeutic target

Introduction

D-serine is a D-type amino acid abundantly present in the mammalian brain. It was first identified in 1992 (Hashimoto et al. 1992), and has since become a focal point of neuroscience research. Historically considered rare and functionally insignificant compared to their L-isomers, D-amino acids are now recognized as important neuromodulators in mammals. Accumulating evidence indicates that D-serine is widely distributed in the CNS—including the cortex, hippocampus, and cerebellum—where it contributes to critical neural functions such as synaptic plasticity, learning, and memory (Wolosker et al. 2008; Zhang et al. 2008).

The physiological significance of D-serine is primarily attributed to its role as the preferential endogenous co-agonist at the glycine site of the N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor, a key mediator of excitatory synaptic transmission (Bardaweel et al. 2014). D-serine exhibits greater efficacy than glycine in activating NMDA receptors, thereby playing a pivotal role in modulating neural circuit function and cognitive processes (Mothet et al. 2000). D-serine homeostasis is maintained through a delicate balance orchestrated by its synthesis via serine racemase (SR), degradation by D-amino acid oxidase (DAAO), and regulated cellular uptake/release via specific transporters such as Na+ independent alanine-serine-cysteine 1 (ASC-1) transporter and Na+-dependent alanine-serine-cysteine-threonine (ASCT) 2 transporter (Wolosker et al. 1999b; Rosenberg et al. 2013; Pollegioni et al. 2018; Krishnan and Billups 2023). Notably, basal extracellular D-serine levels in key forebrain regions are sufficient to saturate synaptic NMDA receptor glycine sites, underscoring its tonic, permissive role in physiological signaling (Papouin et al. 2012).

Research over recent decades has revealed a dual, context-dependent nature of D-serine, capable of mediating both neuroprotection and neurotoxicity—a “Yin-Yang” characteristic influenced by its cellular source, local concentration, and the pathological environment. Under physiological conditions, neuron-derived D-serine fine-tunes NMDA receptor-dependent signaling to support synaptic plasticity and cognition (Kartvelishvily et al. 2006; Rosenberg et al. 2013). Conversely, in pathologies such as traumatic brain injury (TBI) and Alzheimer’s disease (AD), excessive glia-derived D-serine release can promote excitotoxicity and synaptic damage (Balu et al. 2019; Tapanes et al. 2022). Conversely, D-serine deficiency—due to reduced synthesis or accelerated degradation—is associated with NMDA receptor hypofunction, which also impairs synaptic plasticity, contributes to cognitive deficits, and is implicated in various psychiatric and neurological disorders (Papouin et al. 2012; Balu and Coyle 2015). This dichotomy underscores the critical need for precise homeostatic control of brain D-serine levels.

Given its central role in synaptic physiology, disrupted D-serine homeostasis is implicated in a broad spectrum of CNS disorders—from schizophrenia and depression to neurodegenerative diseases—acting as both a pathological contributor and a promising biomarker. Furthermore, emerging evidence has expanded the functional repertoire of D-serine beyond the CNS, implicating it in peripheral diseases such as diabetic retinopathy, osteoporosis, and kidney disorders. This review integrates classical knowledge with cutting-edge research, systematically examining D-serine’s fundamental biology, complex regulatory mechanisms (epigenetic, transcriptional, and metabolic), and its multifaceted roles in central and peripheral pathophysiology. We conclude with a discussion of emerging therapeutic strategies targeting the D-serine pathway and its translational potential in personalized medicine.

Synthesis, Metabolism, and Homeostasis of D-serine

Synthesis: The Central Role of SR

The biosynthesis of D-serine in the CNS is primarily catalyzed by the enzyme SR, which interconverts L-serine and D-serine (Wolosker et al. 1999a). The expression and catalytic activity of SR are governed by sophisticated, multi-layered regulatory mechanisms. Transcriptional control is a key determinant, with SR expression exhibiting distinct developmental and region-specific patterns. For instance, postnatal development in the mouse is marked by a dramatic increase in SR transcript levels in the cortex, striatum, and cerebellum, a surge that coincides temporally with rising levels of free D-serine (Wang and Zhu 2003). Beyond transcriptional regulation, SR activity is finely tuned at the post-translational level by various cellular factors. These include modulation by glutamatergic signaling, specific inflammatory cytokines, and the cellular redox status. Notably, the TGFβ1-Smad3 signaling pathway has been shown to promote the formation of a stable SR dimer specifically in microglia, thereby enhancing D-serine production (Beltrán-Castillo et al. 2020). This finding highlights that activated microglia can serve as a significant source of D-serine under neuroinflammatory conditions. Furthermore, the enzymatic activity of SR is intrinsically dependent on co-factors such as ATP and Mg²⁺, creating a direct functional link between D-serine synthesis and cellular energy status (Baumgart et al. 2007).

This biosynthetic pathway is embedded within a pivotal metabolic framework known as the “serine shuttle”. In this model, astrocytes de novo synthesize L-serine via the phosphorylated pathway, initiated by the enzyme 3-phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase (PHGDH). This astrocyte-derived L-serine is then shuttled to neurons, which constitute the primary cellular site for its subsequent conversion into D-serine by SR (Wolosker and Radzishevsky 2013; Wolosker 2018). Through the multi-layered regulation, SR functions as a key regulatory node, enabling the stringent spatiotemporal control of D-serine synthesis that is fundamental to brain homeostasis and neuromodulation.

Degradation: Compartmentalized Regulation by DAAO

The steady-state level of D-serine is equally governed by a tightly regulated degradative pathway, primarily mediated by DAAO. This flavin adenine dinucleotide-dependent enzyme catalyzes the stereospecific oxidative deamination of D-serine, yielding hydroxypyruvate and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) as byproducts (Yamanaka et al. 2012). A crucial characteristic of DAAO is its marked regional heterogeneity within the brain: it is highly expressed and active in hindbrain regions such as cerebellum and brainstem but exhibits very low activity in key forebrain areas including the hippocampus and cortex (Pollegioni et al. 2018). This heterogeneity dictates the dominant clearance mechanism for D-serine in different brain areas: enzymatic degradation prevails in the hindbrain (Gonda et al. 2022), whereas in the forebrain, where DAAO activity is minimal, clearance relies primarily on high-affinity, transporter-mediated reuptake (e.g., via ASC-1) (Sason et al. 2017). This compartmentalization results in distinct spatiotemporal dynamics and signaling durations of D-serine across different neural circuits.

The regulation of DAAO involves further complexity through its interaction with the D-amino-acid oxidase activator (DAOA)/G72 gene. Current evidence suggests this interaction is context-dependent, with studies reporting conflicting modulatory effects—ranging from activation to inhibition—likely influenced by cell type and experimental conditions, underscoring that its precise physiological role remains to be fully elucidated (Sacchi et al. 2016; Jagannath et al. 2017). Despite this mechanistic ambiguity, the genetic link between the DAAO-DAOA pathway and neuropsychiatric disease is well-established. Genetic association studies have consistently implicated variations in both DAAO and DAOA genes as risk factors for several psychiatric disorders, notably schizophrenia and bipolar disorder (BD), highlighting the potential pathophysiological importance of D-serine degradation (Chumakov et al. 2002; Mechelli et al. 2012).

Transport and Release: Determinants of Spatiotemporal Specificity

The spatiotemporal profile of D-serine signaling is primarily shaped by its distinct release and reuptake pathways, which differ between neurons and astrocytes. In neurons, D-serine is released primarily through non-vesicular, transporter-mediated mechanisms. The principal pathway involves the ASC-1 (Slc7a10) transporter, which functions bidirectionally to regulate extracellular D-serine concentrations in an activity-dependent manner (Rosenberg et al. 2013; Sason et al. 2017). This mode of release results in a temporally protracted, tonic elevation of extracellular D-serine, creating a permissive window for sustained activation of synaptic NMDA receptors.

In astrocytes, the release mechanisms are more diverse and can be context-dependent. A well-characterized pathway is through volume-regulated anion channels (VRACs), which open in response to cell swelling or physiological stimuli, allowing for the efflux of D-serine (Rosenberg et al. 2010). Additionally, under specific conditions—particularly in neuroinflammatory or pathological states—astrocytes may employ regulated vesicle exocytosis for D-serine release, contributing to localized changes in synaptic modulation (Mothet et al. 2005; Sasabe et al. 2007).

The termination of this signal and prevention of receptor desensitization depend on a coordinated reuptake system. Neuronal ASC-1 itself contributes to reuptake, while astrocytic uptake is predominantly mediated by the ASCT1/2 (Slc1a4/5) transporters (Rosenberg et al. 2013; Krishnan and Billups 2023). Furthermore, system A transporters (Slc38a1/2) function as dual-substrate carriers that dynamically fine-tune extracellular D-serine levels through competitive reuptake of glutamine and D-serine, thereby precisely modulating NMDA receptor activity and synaptic plasticity (Bodner et al. 2020).

In summary, the homeostasis of D-serine in the nervous system is precisely regulated by its synthesis, degradation, and transport systems. These processes collectively determine the spatiotemporal dynamics of D-serine distribution, forming the foundation for its physiological and pathological functions. Signaling Mechanisms of D-serine: From Canonical Co-agonism to Novel Pathways

Canonical Action: Endogenous Co-agonist for the NMDA Receptor

The most established function of D-serine is its role as an endogenous co-agonist for the glycine binding site of the NMDA receptor. Full activation of this ligand-gated ion channel requires the simultaneous binding of both glutamate at the GluN2 subunit and a co-agonist (glycine or D-serine) at the GluN1 subunit (Hansen et al. 2018). A pivotal discovery in the field was that in key forebrain regions, such as prefrontal cortex, the glycine sites of synaptic NMDA receptors are predominantly occupied by D-serine, rather than glycine, under physiological conditions (Fossat et al. 2012). Critically, the basal extracellular concentration of D-serine in these areas is within the low micromolar range, which is sufficient to saturate these sites tonically (Mothet et al. 2000). This establishes that under resting conditions, D-serine is not merely present but actively primes synaptic NMDA receptors for activation. Consequently, the dynamic, activity-dependent regulation of local D-serine availability—through its release and clearance—becomes the principal mechanism controlling receptor gating and the induction of synaptic plasticity. This function is indispensable, as evidenced by the fact that D-serine is a necessary permissive factor for the induction of long-term potentiation (LTP), a cellular correlate of learning and memory, and its experimental depletion can abolish NMDA receptor-mediated plasticity (Yang et al. 2003; Henneberger et al. 2010).

Novel Actions: Multifunctional Signaling Beyond the NMDA Receptor

Beyond its canonical role, D-serine employs diverse receptors and mechanisms to fine-tune neural circuits and exert effects in peripheral organs. Within the CNS, it can exert cell-type-specific inhibitory control. For instance, in the prefrontal cortex, D-serine differentially reduces the excitability of intratelencephalic (IT) neurons by recruiting small conductance Ca2+-activated K+ (SK) channels and suppresses burst firing in pyramidal tract (PT) neurons by slowing hyperpolarization-activated cyclic nucleotide-gated (HCN) channel kinetics (Zhang et al. 2025). This NMDA receptor -dependent, yet net inhibitory, modulation challenges its traditional view as a purely facilitatory molecule.

D-serine also independently regulates synaptic structure as an endogenous ligand for the glutamate delta-1 receptor (GluD1). By competitively disrupting the GluD1-cerebellin-1 (Cbln1) interaction, it negatively regulates excitatory synapse stabilization in the central amygdala, thereby modulating pain-related affective behaviors (Sabnis et al. 2025). This structural mechanism is entirely distinct from its canonical role as an NMDA receptor co-agonist.

The scope of D-serine’s direct signaling role extends beyond the CNS. In the enteric nervous system, D-serine synthesized and released by gut neurons acts as an excitatory neurotransmitter by directly gating unconventional GluN1/GluN3-type NMDA receptor, thereby controlling colonic motility (Osorio et al. 2023). This discovery not only establishes a physiological role for D-serine in peripheral organs but also opens new therapeutic avenues for gastrointestinal disorders linked to brain-gut axis dysregulation.

The expanding repertoire of D-serine signaling—from ionotropic co-agonism to metabotropic synaptic regulation and even direct roles in peripheral organs—underscores its functional versatility. However, this very diversity complicates its therapeutic targeting. A key unresolved question is the relative physiological weight of these non-canonical pathways compared to its core NMDA receptor function. While evidence for roles at GluD1 and GluN1/GluN3 receptors is compelling, it primarily stems from specific behavioral paradigms or recombinant systems. Determining whether these actions are complementary, dominant in niche circuits, or context-dependent emergency pathways is crucial for predicting the outcomes of global D-serine modulation. In conclusion, through both NMDA receptor-dependent and -independent mechanisms, D-serine plays key roles in inhibitory microcircuit modulation, synaptic structure stabilization, and peripheral organ function. This diversity underlies its fine-tuned regulation and complex manifestations in pathology but also presents challenges for targeted therapy. Multilayer Regulatory Networks of D-serine

Epigenetic and Transcriptional Regulation

Emerging evidence indicates that environmental stressors and psychoactive drug exposure can dynamically reshape the D-serine pathway through sophisticated epigenetic mechanisms, effectively encoding environmental experiences into long-lasting molecular changes. For instance, paternal cocaine exposure induces hippocampal epigenetic remodeling in male offspring, leading to increased DAAO expression via permissive histone modifications. This results in reduced D-serine levels, impaired NMDA receptor-mediated synaptic plasticity, and cognitive deficits in progeny (Wimmer et al. 2017). Similarly, in chronic social defeat stress (CSDS) models, epigenetic activation of the ASCT2 transporter gene—through histone hyper-acetylation—increases D-serine uptake, diminishing D-serine levels in the mouse hippocampus and leading to depressive-like behaviors (Wang et al. 2017a). Such epigenetic alterations may function as “molecular memories”, perpetuating metabolic dysregulation long after the initial insult.

Transcriptional regulation of D-serine is a complex process that controls its spatial and temporal availability, critically influencing numerous physiological and pathological functions. This regulation primarily targets the expression of key enzymes involved in D-serine synthesis and degradation. As previously mentioned, SR transcript levels rise postnatally in several brain regions. A critical counterpoint to this is the sharp, developmentally timed increase in DAAO activity—particularly in the cerebellum and kidney during the third postnatal week—which drives a corresponding decline in local D-serine levels (Wang and Zhu 2003), highlighting a key transcriptional (and subsequent enzymatic) switch that shapes local D-serine bioavailability. Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests that the transcriptional profiles of serine metabolic enzymes shift at the cellular level during neural maturation, coinciding with increased endogenous D-serine levels, which appears to align with the functional transition of the nervous system from a focus on neurodevelopment to neurotransmission (Suzuki et al. 2025).

Beyond intrinsic transcriptional control, systemic factors also modulate D-serine pathways. Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses in a Drosophila model of sleep deprivation revealed a gut–brain axis circuit in which sleep pressure activates γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA)-ergic signaling, upregulating SR transcription in intestinal epithelial cells. This leads to local D-serine production that feeds back to the brain while concurrently inducing intestinal oxidative stress via NMDA receptor 1 activation (Dai et al. 2019; Zheng et al. 2025). Thus, sleep deprivation acts as an environmental stressor that disrupts D-serine homeostasis through gut-mediated mechanisms, linking peripheral metabolic alterations to CNS disorders.

Collectively, these findings illustrate how D-serine regulation is orchestrated across epigenetic, transcriptional, and systemic levels. This multilayered understanding not only deepens insight into the pathophysiology of psychiatric and neurodegenerative disorders but also highlights promising avenues for therapies targeting specific regulatory nodes in the D-serine pathway.

D-serine as a Metabolic Sensor

D-serine is increasingly recognized as a pivotal molecular link between brain energy metabolism and neural circuit function, with its bioavailability being highly sensitive to cellular energy status (De Miranda et al. 2002; Zanatta et al. 2009). This role as a metabolic sensor is governed by a multi-tiered regulatory mechanism. The de novo synthesis of D-serine from L-serine by SR is intrinsically energy-dependent. Since its precursor, L-serine, is a hub metabolite derived from the glycolytic intermediate 3-phosphoglycerate, its availability is directly constrained by glycolytic flux (Yang and Vousden 2016; Cararo Lopes et al. 2025). The biosynthesis of L-serine itself occurs via the phosphorylated pathway, initiated by the enzyme PHGDH, which commits 3-phosphoglycerate to serine production (Mäntyselkä et al. 2024). This metabolic architecture places the serine synthesis pathway in direct competition with other glycolytic branches for the common substrate (Locasale 2013). Consequently, metabolic perturbations, such as mitochondrial inhibition or oxidative stress, can rapidly deplete L-serine pools, directly limiting substrate availability for SR and leading to diminished D-serine production (Diehl et al. 2019; Zheng et al. 2025). In addition to this precursor-level control, the functional linkage between D-serine synthesis and energy status is further established through SR’s dependence on key energetic cofactors, as previously discussed (Baumgart et al. 2007). This biochemical coupling explains how bioenergetic deficits, common in aging and AD, can lead to synaptic failure and NMDA receptor hypofunction via D-serine depletion, as corroborated by studies showing impaired astrocytic glycolysis reduces D-serine release and drives cognitive decline in AD models (Le Douce et al. 2020).

The function of D-serine extends beyond a simple reflection of energy status to an active metabolic rheostat that fine-tunes network stability, particularly under pathological conditions. This is exemplified in hypermetabolic states like epilepsy, where D-serine exhibits a cell-type-specific duality. While D-serine derived primarily from excitatory neurons can potentiate synaptic NMDA receptors on principal cells to exacerbate network hyperexcitability and seizure propagation (Zhang et al. 2021), it simultaneously serves as a critical an antagonist of GluN3-containing triheteromeric NMDA receptors, preventing excitotoxicity-induced neuronal death and epileptogenesis (Beesley et al. 2021). This functional antagonism likely serves as a neuroprotective feedback mechanism during pathological hyperactivity (Kaniakova et al. 2018), wherein D-serine competes with glycine at GluN1/GluN3 subunits to reduce Ca²⁺ permeability (Chatterton et al. 2002), thereby limiting excessive Ca²⁺ influx while preserving synaptic plasticity mediated by conventional GluN1/GluN2 receptors (Papouin et al. 2012). This nuanced role means D-serine can either fuel or dampen hyperexcitability depending on the cellular source, local receptor subunit composition (GluN2 vs. GluN3 predominance), and circuit context.

Intervention studies underscore the detrimental impact of disrupting this delicate balance, highlighting its bidirectional nature. D-serine deficiency, induced by SR knockout or DAAO-mediated degradation, impairs NMDA receptor-dependent synaptic plasticity and cognition (Basu et al. 2009; Papouin et al. 2012). Conversely, reducing pathologically elevated D-serine attenuates seizure severity and is neuroprotective in epilepsy models (Harai et al. 2012; Ma et al. 2019). This bidirectional relationship—whereby both deficient and excessive D-serine signaling can impair synaptic function—establishes it as a critical metabolic rheostat at the interface of cellular energetics and neuronal communication.

Collectively, these findings underscore that D-serine levels are critically influenced by cellular energy metabolism and that fluctuations in its concentration serve as a pivotal mechanism through which metabolic changes can modulate neuronal signaling and synaptic plasticity. This understanding opens promising therapeutic avenues: targeting enzymes in its metabolic pathway (e.g., PHGDH (Kartvelishvily et al. 2006), SR (Lu et al. 2024) or using D-serine supplementation (Weiser et al. 2012; Otte et al. 2013), could mitigate synaptic dysfunction in disorders of bioenergetic imbalance, from AD and epilepsy to stroke and depression. However, the therapeutic window is narrow, straddling the dual risks of NMDA receptor hypofunction and excitotoxicity. Future strategies must therefore move beyond globally increasing or decreasing D-serine levels, and instead aim for precise, context-informed modulation guided by biomarkers that reflect the underlying metabolic and receptor-state pathology.

Psychiatric Disorders

Schizophrenia, Depression, and BD

The NMDA receptor hypofunction hypothesis of schizophrenia is strongly supported by alterations in the D-serine pathway, though a causal vs. epiphenomenal role remains debated. Clinically, significantly reduced serum D-serine levels are observed in patients and correlate with the severity of cognitive impairment, suggesting at least a biomarker role, if not a driver of pathology (Hons et al. 2021). This deficiency is thought to stem from a combination of genetic and regulatory factors. Genetic association studies have consistently implicated single nucleotide polymorphisms in the genes encoding DAAO and its modulator DAOA/G72 as risk factors for schizophrenia (Chumakov et al. 2002). Furthermore, antipsychotic treatment can gradually elevate D-serine levels, and this increase parallels improvements in clinical symptoms, particularly in cognitive domains, underscoring its state-dependent and potentially modifiable nature (Yamamori et al. 2014; Panizzutti et al. 2019). Thus, while D-serine deficiency is a robust correlate of cognitive dysfunction, disentangling whether it is a primary etiological factor or a downstream consequence of broader glial/metabolic dysfunction is critical for developing primary versus symptomatic interventions.

In major depressive disorder (MDD), a similar theme of deficiency emerges. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) D-serine concentrations show a significant negative correlation with depression severity scores (Ishiwata et al. 2018). D-serine deficiency supports a broader model of NMDA receptor hypofunction within key circuits, a chronic pathological state thought to underlie the synaptic deficits and impaired neuroplasticity observed in depression (Bennett 2011; Popoli et al. 2011). The apparent paradox—that NMDA receptor hypofunction is implicated in depression, yet the NMDA receptor antagonist ketamine has rapid antidepressant effects—is resolved by considering ketamine’s cell-type-specific actions. Its therapeutic action is proposed to initiate via the preferential, transient blockade of NMDA receptors on GABAergic interneurons, leading to disinhibition of glutamatergic pyramidal neurons and a surge in glutamate release (Abdallah et al. 2015). The increased glutamate then activates postsynaptic α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid (AMPA) receptors, triggering downstream neurotrophic pathways involving brain-derived neurotrophic factor and the mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR), which ultimately help restore synaptic connectivity and function (Zanos and Gould 2018). Furthermore, specific ketamine metabolites, such as (2R,6R)-hydroxynorketamine, may contribute to sustained antidepressant effects through mechanisms that are independent of direct NMDA receptor antagonism (Zanos et al. 2016; Lumsden et al. 2019). Thus, ketamine’s success does not negate evidence for chronic NMDA receptor pathway impairment in depression; rather, it demonstrates that a precise, transient intervention can acutely correct a broader state of network dysregulation.

The role of D-serine in BD is less characterized but suggestive. Patients exhibit a distinct serine metabolomic profile with elevated D-serine and markedly reduced L-serine levels in serum compared to healthy controls (Palsson et al. 2015). Additionally, genetic variations in the DAOA locus have been associated with BD, hinting at a shared pathophysiological link with schizophrenia through the D-serine degradation pathway (Murtas et al. 2022).

Addiction

D-serine modulates drug-related neural plasticity, but its effects are profoundly brain-region and drug-specific, reflecting the complexity of addiction circuits. In opioid addiction, chronic morphine exposure induces regionally opposite changes. In forebrain areas like the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus, it upregulates SR expression and increases D-serine levels, which potentiates NMDA receptor activity and contributes to the development of tolerance (Yoshikawa et al. 2008). Conversely, in the nucleus accumbens (NAc), morphine suppresses the vesicular release of D-serine from astrocytes, reducing NMDA receptor-mediated transmission onto GABAergic neurons, an effect reversible by exogenous D-serine (Wu et al. 2017). This regional dichotomy suggests that elevating D-serine in the NAc but inhibiting it in pain/modulatory centers might be therapeutic. Cocaine addiction also displays region-specificity. In the NAc, cocaine exposure leads to downregulated SR and upregulated DAAO, resulting in a net decrease in D-serine that impairs NMDA receptor-dependent synaptic plasticity (Curcio et al. 2013). Supplementing D-serine directly into the NAc can restore plasticity and reduce cocaine-seeking behavior (Kelamangalath and Wagner 2010; Liu et al. 2016). In stark contrast, enhancing D-serine signaling in the ventral tegmental area promotes cocaine-induced behavioral sensitization, indicating a pro-addictive role in this mesolimbic node (Fernandez-Espejo et al. 2008). The involvement extends to other substances: D-serine is necessary for nicotine-induced hippocampal plasticity (Lopez-Hidalgo et al. 2012), SR-knockout mice show altered nicotine sensitivity (Yu et al. 2024)d serine can reduce compulsive alcohol intake in rodents (Seif et al. 2015). In methamphetamine models, the absence of SR prevents the development of behavioral sensitization (Horio et al. 2012).

Neurodegenerative Diseases

AD

D-serine exhibits a temporally dynamic and dual role in AD pathogenesis, a framework that reconciles conflicting historical observations of NMDA receptor function in disease models. In early or prodromal stages, bioenergetic deficits in astrocytes impair glycolysis, a process essential for L-serine and subsequent D-serine production. This results in a synaptic D-serine deficiency, contributing to NMDA receptor hypofunction, synaptic depression, and early cognitive decline (Le Douce et al. 2020). As the disease progresses, a toxic gain-of-function emerges within an increasingly neuroinflammatory environment. Activated astrocytes adopt a neurotoxic phenotype characterized by robust SR expression (Balu et al. 2019), while activated microglia also upregulate SR in response to inflammatory stimuli, forming a synergistic glial source of D-serine (Beltrán-Castillo et al. 2020). This coordinated glial response results in excessive, non-physiological release of D-serine, which drives chronic overactivation of extrasynaptic NMDA receptors. The excitotoxic insult is further amplified by microglia-derived pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β), which heighten neuronal vulnerability (Yang et al. 2005; Takeuchi et al. 2006). Consequently, in the AD brain, activated microglia contribute both directly to the excitotoxic D-serine pool and indirectly by sustaining a pro-inflammatory milieu, creating a vicious cycle that accelerates synaptic loss and neuronal damage (Leng and Edison 2021; Wang et al. 2023). This biphasic mechanism—early deficiency followed by late excess—underscores the critical need for disease-stage-specific biomarkers and warns that therapeutic interventions effective in one phase (e.g., early supplementation) could be detrimental in another (e.g., exacerbating late-stage excitotoxicity).

Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS)

Excitotoxicity mediated by glutamate receptors is a key driver of motor neuron degeneration in ALS, and D-serine is a critical contributor to this process. Post-mortem studies reveal elevated levels of D-serine in the spinal cords of ALS patients (Sasabe et al. 2007). This elevation is also observed in the plasma of living patients, suggesting its potential as a peripheral biomarker (Lee et al. 2021). In transgenic SOD1 mouse models of ALS, D-serine levels and SR expression increase progressively in spinal glial cells as the disease advances (Paul and de Belleroche 2014). The pathophysiological consequence is the potentiation of NMDA receptor-mediated toxicity to motor neurons. Importantly, reducing D-serine levels, such as through intrathecal gene therapy delivering DAAO, has been shown to attenuate glial activation, mitigate motor neuron loss, and significantly extend survival in these models, providing direct proof of concept for targeting this pathway (Wang et al. 2017b). Furthermore, specific mutations in the DAAO gene itself have been identified in familial ALS cases, genetically cementing the link between dysfunctional D-serine metabolism and the disease (Khan et al. 2023; Dave et al. 2023).

Other Neurological Conditions

Neuropathic Pain

D-serine is a key mediator of central sensitization in the spinal cord dorsal horn, a process underlying chronic neuropathic pain. Following peripheral nerve injury (e.g., chronic constriction injury), spinal astrocytes become activated and dramatically upregulate SR expression. The resulting increase in D-serine production and release potently enhances NMDA receptor activity on dorsal horn neurons, lowering their activation threshold and facilitating pain transmission, manifesting as mechanical allodynia (Choi et al. 2019). This pronociceptive role is further supported by findings that D-serine upregulates pain-related ion channels like Nav1.9 and TRPV1 in injury models (Yu et al. 2022). Consequently, interventions that degrade endogenous D-serine (using exogenous DAAO) or inhibit its synthesis (with SR inhibitors) effectively reverse or prevent pain hypersensitivity in preclinical models, validating the pathway as a promising analgesic target (Moon et al. 2015). Notably, the commonly used neuropathic pain drug pregabalin may partly exert its effect by reducing spinal D-serine content, attenuating NMDA receptor-mediated synaptic transmission in the superficial dorsal horn (Kato et al. 2017).

Ischemic Stroke

The excitotoxic cascade in stroke critically involves D-serine. During cerebral ischemia, massive glutamate release coincides with increased astrocytic D-serine release. This simultaneous elevation of both agonists leads to the pathological overactivation of synaptic NMDA receptors, triggering lethal Ca²⁺ influx and neuronal death (Li et al. 2010; Tejeda-Bayron et al. 2021). Targeting D-serine synthesis is robustly neuroprotective: pharmacological inhibition of SR or genetic deletion of SR significantly reduces infarct volume and improves outcomes in mouse models of middle cerebral artery occlusion (Mustafa et al. 2010; Watanabe et al. 2016). Interestingly, in the delayed phase after stroke, DAAO expression increases in hippocampal astrocytes, and its inhibition can mitigate delayed neuronal death, suggesting a time-dependent role for the degradation enzyme as well (Liu et al. 2022).

Epilepsy

As detailed in the metabolic sensor section, D-serine exhibits a complex, context-dependent role in hyperexcitability disorders. On one hand, neuron-derived D-serine can facilitate seizure propagation by enhancing synaptic NMDA receptor activity on principal neurons (Zhang et al. 2021). On the other hand, it can act as an endogenous antagonist at GluN1/GluN3-containing NMDA receptors, which are highly permeable to Ca²⁺ and implicated in excitotoxic death. By competing with glycine at these receptors, elevated D-serine may provide a built-in brake against excessive Ca²⁺ influx during epileptiform activity, representing a neuroprotective feedback mechanism (Kaniakova et al. 2018; Beesley et al. 2021). This duality is reflected in interventional studies where SR knockout reduces seizure susceptibility in some models (Harai et al. 2012), while reducing pathologically high D-serine also attenuates seizures in others (Ma et al. 2019).

Thus, D-serine dyshomeostasis represents a shared pathogenic mechanism across major CNS disorders. Its precise role—whether causative or secondary—varies critically depending on the cellular source, spatiotemporal context, and stage of disease, underscoring the need for carefully targeted therapeutic strategies. Peripheral System Disorders

Diabetic Retinopathy

In diabetic retinopathy, elevated D-serine levels contribute to neurovascular damage by overactivating NMDA receptors (Ozaki et al. 2018). Inhibition of SR with l-aspartic acid β-hydroxamate in diabetic mice not only ameliorated retinal dysfunction and protected retinal ganglion cells but also unexpectedly improved systemic glucose homeostasis, suggesting a potential dual benefit for diabetic complications (Jiang et al. 2025). Similarly, the loss of DAAO function has been proven to be a critical factor in diabetic retinopathy, and early enhancement of DAAO activity via gene therapy can counteract both neurodegeneration and vascular abnormalities.

Bone Diseases

In bone metabolism, the D- and L-enantiomers of serine play complementary and synergistic roles. While L-serine promotes osteoblast differentiation and bone formation, D-serine suppresses osteoclast activity and bone resorption. Administration of sericin, a precursor for both, improved bone mass in a rodent model of osteoporosis, highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting serine metabolism in bone diseases (Kim et al. 2025).

Kidney Diseases

In kidney diseases, D-serine metabolic imbalance forms a vicious cycle with pathological processes: impaired kidney function leads to reduced clearance capacity, causing abnormal systemic accumulation of D-serine (plasma levels in end-stage chronic kidney disease (CKD) patients can reach 2–3 times those in healthy individuals) (Kimura et al. 2016). The mechanisms underlying D-serine accumulation extend beyond simple reduced clearance. In experimental models of cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity, proximal tubular damage triggers induction of the neutral amino acid transporter ASC-1 in distal tubules and collecting ducts. This transporter preferentially mediates D-serine reabsorption from urine, further contributing to elevated serum levels even when DAAO activity remains intact (Suzuki et al. 2019). Accumulated D-serine exacerbates renal damage through multiple mechanisms: it promotes oxidative stress by generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) such as H2O2 during its degradation by DAAO (Zhang et al. 2012), and induces Ca2+ overload and oxidative stress through overactivation of renal NMDA receptors, leading to damage to renal tubular cells and renal dysfunction (Tseng et al. 2021). Consistent with these findings, a recent study demonstrated that patients with diabetic kidney disease (DKD) exhibit significantly elevated blood levels of D-serine and an increased D/L-serine ratio compared to non-CKD controls, underscoring the potential of blood D-serine as a characteristic biomarker for DKD (Hirakawa et al. 2025).

Translational Prospects of Targeting the D-serine Pathway

Innovative Therapeutic Strategies

Traditional approaches to modulate D-serine levels have primarily involved direct oral supplementation or inhibition of its catabolic enzyme, DAAO. In a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial, researchers demonstrated that oral administration of D-serine at a dosage of 60 mg/kg/day for 6 weeks significantly relieved clinical symptoms in patients with schizophrenia (Kantrowitz et al. 2018). Similarly, D-serine supplementation has exhibited efficacy in alleviating depressive mood among healthy volunteers, as evidenced by reduced perceived stress and enhanced cognitive performance in randomized controlled trials (Capitao et al. 2020). Moreover, elevated activity of DAAO was found in postmortem brain tissue samples from depressed patients compared to normal controls, and treatment with the DAAO inhibitor sodium benzoate exhibited antidepressant-like effects (Huang et al. 2023).

However, these conventional methods face challenges such as peripheral side effects and limited bioavailability. To overcome these limitations, several novel strategies are emerging:

Positive Allosteric Modulators of SR

Small molecules designed to enhance SR activity without overriding its physiological regulation are under preclinical investigation. For instance, NSC294149, a potential SR agonist, has been shown to be effective in improving NMDA receptor dysfunction caused by decreased D-serine levels, as evidenced by a significant improvement in the survival hazard ratio in a Drosophila model of AD (Lu et al. 2023).

ASCT1/2 Inhibitors

Compounds that selectively block astrocytic D-serine uptake are being explored for depression treatment. L-4-fluorophenylglycine (L-4FPG), an inhibitor of ASCT1/2 that modulates extracellular D-serine levels, exhibits dose-dependent enhancement of LTP in rat visual cortex slices. The antidepressant effect of L-4FPG has been demonstrated and may be attributed to the inhibition of ASCT1/2, leading to subsequent activation of AMPA receptor-mTOR signaling pathway (Sung et al. 2022).

Gene Therapy Approaches

Viral vector-mediated gene delivery enables precise manipulation of D-serine metabolism in specific cell types. For example, in the SOD1-G93A transgenic mouse model, which mimics many clinical features of ALS, intrathecal injection of an adeno-associated virus carrying the DAAO gene led to reduced D-serine levels, alleviation of motor neuron loss and glial cell activation, and a significant extension in survival time (Wang et al. 2017b). Conversely, gene therapies aiming to overexpress SR in brain are being investigated for conditions associated with D-serine deficiency (Otte et al. 2013).

Microbiome Regulation Via the Gut-Brain Axis

Emerging research identifies the gut microbiome as a key regulator of brain D-serine signaling. A study by Huang et al. (2022) demonstrated that the prebiotic stachyose alleviated stress-induced LTP and cognition impairment exclusively via the gut-brain axis. Its protective effects required intragastric administration and were abolished by antibiotics, confirming gut flora’s essential role. Mechanistically, stachyose did not alter overall D-serine levels but rescued the expression of its release proteins (ASC-1 and vesicle-associated membrane protein 2) in the hippocampus, an effect blocked by antibiotics. This provides direct evidence that precision prebiotics can modulate gut microbiota to enhance D-serine release and synaptic plasticity, offering a novel non-invasive therapeutic strategy (Huang et al. 2022).

In summary, therapeutic strategies targeting the D-serine pathway are evolving toward greater precision. The field is moving beyond broad supplementation or inhibition, aiming instead to achieve spatiotemporally controlled modulation of D-serine bioavailability through molecular, genetic, or systemic interventions. Biomarker Development

Beyond its pathophysiological roles, components of the D-serine signaling pathway are emerging as promising molecular biomarkers for diagnosing, stratifying, and monitoring therapeutic response across neuropsychiatric and systemic diseases.

Schizophrenia

In schizophrenia, the hypothesis of NMDA receptor hypofunction is directly supported by biomarker studies. Consistent clinical evidence demonstrates significantly reduced serum D-serine levels in patients compared to healthy controls, with lower levels correlating with greater impairment in executive functions (Hons et al. 2021). This deficiency is dynamic; following effective antipsychotic treatment, particularly with clozapine, D-serine levels have been observed to increase, and this elevation correlates with improvements in clinical symptoms, especially in cognitive domains (Yamamori et al. 2014; Panizzutti et al. 2019). Moving beyond the metabolite itself, the proteins governing its degradation are also informative. Elevated activity of DAAO has been found in postmortem brain tissue of individuals with schizophrenia, and its modulator, DAOA (G72), is a well-established genetic risk factor. Advanced analytical approaches, such as interpretable machine learning frameworks, are now being employed to decipher the complex, non-linear relationships between DAO/DAOA protein levels, clinical symptom severity, and cognitive function, enhancing their predictive utility for precision psychiatry (Lin et al. 2025).

Depression

In MDD, a convergence of evidence supports the biomarker potential of D-serine. Clinically, CSF D-serine concentrations demonstrate a significant negative correlation with depression severity scores (Ishiwata et al. 2018). This biochemical alteration is anchored in the pathophysiology of synaptic deficits, as proteomic analyses reveal decreased levels of key synaptic proteins in the CSF of MDD patients, consistent with glutamatergic dysfunction (Al Shweiki et al. 2020). The therapeutic relevance is underscored by clinical trials where sodium benzoate, a DAAO inhibitor that elevates endogenous D-serine, demonstrated significant antidepressant and cognitive-enhancing effects (Lin et al. 2022a).

Neurodegenerative and Cognitive Disorders

In AD, CSF D-serine levels show a robust correlation with the degree of memory impairment, positioning it as a potential diagnostic or progression biomarker (Madeira et al. 2015). While findings across studies require harmonization, potentially due to differences in disease staging or analytical techniques, the link to synaptic dysfunction remains compelling. Furthermore, serum DAAO activity exhibits a concomitant increase with the progression of cognitive decline in individuals diagnosed with mild cognitive impairment, suggesting its potential for monitoring disease trajectory and possibly predicting conversion to dementia (Lin and Lane 2022).

The translational application of these biomarkers hinges on methodological rigor. Ongoing research focuses on standardizing sensitive and specific analytical methods, such as high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), enzymatic assays, and mass spectrometry, to ensure reproducibility and clinical reliability (Courtes et al. 2025; Feng et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2026). The integration of these biomarkers with other neurophysiological indices, such as EEG biomarkers identified through deep learning (Aguilar et al. 2021), and the application of advanced computational models hold promise for developing multivariate biomarker panels for enhanced disease stratification, prediction of treatment response, and monitoring of disease progression across a spectrum of neuropsychiatric disorders (Tang et al. 2022).

Clinical Trials and Drug Repurposing

The compelling preclinical evidence linking D-serine dysregulation to disease pathophysiology has accelerated its translation into clinical investigations. Recent and ongoing trials, focusing primarily on modulating the degradation pathway through DAAO inhibition, have yielded promising, albeit nuanced, results that underscore both the potential and the complexities of targeting this system.

DAAO Inhibitors in Schizophrenia

The pursuit of DAAO inhibitors for schizophrenia represents a direct strategy to address NMDA receptor hypofunction by elevating synaptic D-serine. Recent phase II trials have provided critical insights. Luvadaxistat, a selective DAAO inhibitor, was evaluated in a randomized, double-blind study. While it did not meet its primary endpoint of improving negative symptoms, a key finding emerged: treatment with 50 mg/day significantly enhanced cognitive performance, as measured by the Brief Assessment of Cognition in Schizophrenia composite score and the Schizophrenia Cognition Rating Scale (O’Donnell et al. 2023; Murthy et al. 2024). This suggests that DAAO inhibition may have a specific, beneficial impact on the cognitive deficits of schizophrenia, which are often treatment-resistant. In contrast, the repurposed compound sodium benzoate, a non-selective DAAO inhibitor, has demonstrated broader symptomatic efficacy. A comprehensive meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials concluded that sodium benzoate significantly reduces total Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale—encompassing positive, negative, and general psychopathology subscales—and also improves cognitive function in patients with schizophrenia (Chang et al. 2025). The divergence in outcomes between luvadaxistat (focused procognitive effect) and sodium benzoate (broad symptom efficacy) may relate to differences in pharmacological properties, brain penetration, or off-target effects, highlighting the importance of compound-specific profiles.

Sodium Benzoate in Depression and AD

In MDD, an 8-week, randomized controlled trial directly compared sodium benzoate with the first-line SSRI sertraline and placebo. The results were striking: sodium benzoate demonstrated superior efficacy in reducing perceived stress and improving cognitive performance, coupled with a more favorable safety and tolerability profile (Lin et al. 2022a). This positions DAAO inhibition as a mechanistically distinct and effective strategy for late-life depression. Perhaps even more unexpectedly, sodium benzoate has shown promise in AD. A 24-week clinical trial revealed that sodium benzoate treatment (750–1000 mg/day) significantly reduced the levels of amyloid-β (Aβ) peptides in the CSF of AD patients. Furthermore, higher baseline Aβ1–42 levels predicted a greater degree of cognitive improvement following treatment, positioning this intervention not only as a potential Aβ-lowering therapy but also introducing a predictive biomarker for therapeutic response (Lin and Lane 2025).

These cumulative findings robustly validate the principle that DAAO inhibition is a viable strategy for modulating glutamatergic dysfunction across a spectrum of disorders characterized by NMDA receptor pathobiology. The excellent safety profile and oral bioavailability of compounds like sodium benzoate further support their translational viability (Lin et al. 2022b). However, the variable efficacy patterns observed across trials underscore several key considerations for future development: the need for optimal target engagement within the CNS, the critical role of patient stratification using clinical and biomarker profiles (e.g., baseline D-serine levels, Aβ status), and the importance of longer-term trials to establish enduring benefits and disease-modifying potential. The ongoing refinement of these strategies will be essential to fully realize the therapeutic promise of the D-serine pathway.

A Critical Appraisal: Evaluating the Evidence and Future Directions in D-serine Research

The field of D-serine research has expanded remarkably, transforming its role from a simple NMDA receptor co-agonist to a central node integrating metabolism, epigenetics, and neural circuit function. While these advances are compelling, a critical evaluation of the strength, consistency, and translational potential of the current evidence is essential. This section synthesizes the major recent findings, appraises the robustness and limitations of the supporting data, and identifies key unanswered questions that must guide future research.

The evidence compiled in Table 3 demonstrates several core strengths that define the current state of the field:

Depth and Innovation in Mechanistic Understanding

The research has successfully moved from correlation to mechanism. For instance, the use of metabolomics and stable isotope tracing has moved beyond noting D-serine level changes to demonstrating the direct coupling of its synthesis to glycolytic flux via the “serine shuttle” (Le Douce et al. 2020). This provides a robust, biochemically-grounded link between cellular bioenergetics and synaptic plasticity deficits.

Diversification and Specificity of Signaling Pathways

Research has moved beyond the NMDA receptor, revealing novel signaling roles. D-serine acts as an endogenous ligand for the orphan receptor GluD1, regulating synapse stability in the amygdala (Sabnis et al. 2025), and directly gates unconventional GluN1/GluN3-type NMDA receptors to control colonic motility (Osorio et al. 2023). These discoveries, though largely from preclinical models, are mechanistically novel and highlight specific, non-canonical therapeutic targets for conditions like chronic pain and gastrointestinal disorders.

Spatiotemporal and Context-Dependent Roles in Pathology

The field has progressed beyond a simplistic “more is bad, less is bad” paradigm. A prime example is the temporally dynamic, dual role of D-serine in AD: early deficiency due to astrocytic metabolic dysfunction contributes to synaptic hypofunction, while later excess from neurotoxic astrocytes drives excitotoxicity (Balu et al. 2019; Le Douce et al. 2020). This biphasic model better reflects disease progression and underscores the critical importance of intervention timing.

Substantive Progress in Translational Medicine

Clinical investigation has advanced beyond simple supplementation. Trials of DAAO inhibitors (e.g., luvadaxistat, sodium benzoate) in schizophrenia, depression, and AD, while yielding mixed outcomes, confirm that modulating this pathway can translate to clinical symptom improvement (Lin et al. 2022a; O’Donnell et al. 2023). Concurrently, the potential of D-serine and its regulatory enzymes as accessible biomarkers in serum and CSF is being validated, offering tools for patient stratification.

Limitations and Methodological Challenges

Despite the promising outlook, significant limitations and methodological issues necessitate cautious interpretation and highlight barriers to clinical translation.

The Translational Gap Between Preclinical Models and Human Disease

The vast majority of mechanistic evidence derives from transgenic rodent or Drosophila models. While invaluable, these models cannot fully recapitulate the polygenic, multifactorial nature and chronic progression of human neuropsychiatric disorders. Promising effects in animals often attenuate or become more complex in human trials (e.g., luvadaxistat’s lack of efficacy on negative symptoms but pro-cognitive effect), underscoring species differences and disease complexity.

Heterogeneity in Methodologies and Biomarker Measurement

Measured D-serine levels are highly dependent on analytical techniques (HPLC, mass spectrometry, enzymatic assays), with substantial inter-laboratory variability. Furthermore, confounding factors such as biofluid type (serum vs. plasma vs. CSF), sample handling protocols, patient medication status, circadian rhythms, and diet can significantly impact results. This lack of standardization contributes to poor comparability across studies and even contradictory findings (e.g., inconsistent reports on CSF D-serine levels in AD), hindering its establishment as a reliable biomarker.

Extreme Complexity of Cellular Sources and Contextual Actions

The “Yin-Yang” duality of D-serine is exquisitely dependent on its cellular origin, release mode, and the receptor subtypes and subcellular compartments it engages. Current technologies still lack the spatiotemporal resolution to monitor these dynamics in vivo with cell-type specificity. Consequently, many conclusions about its “deleterious” or “beneficial” roles remain correlative or based on global manipulations, lacking fine-grained causal dissection.

Nascent Stage of Peripheral Research

Although D-serine dysregulation is implicated in peripheral diseases like diabetic kidney disease and osteoporosis, the underlying mechanisms are far less understood than in the CNS. Key questions remain unanswered: Is renal D-serine accumulation a cause or consequence of declining function? What are its precise receptor targets in bone cells? This mechanistic uncertainty limits the development of targeted therapies for peripheral conditions.

The “Double-Edged Sword” Dilemma and Narrow Therapeutic Window

The optimal concentration range for D-serine is exceedingly narrow and varies by disease, stage, and brain region. This poses a major challenge for therapeutic development: how to achieve precise, spatially and temporally controlled modulation of D-serine levels without triggering systemic or off-target effects? Current strategies (e.g., oral supplementation, broad-spectrum DAAO inhibitors) largely lack this precision.

Future Directions and Integrative Approaches

Developing Novel Tools

Creating high spatiotemporal resolution fluorescent sensors or PET tracers for D-serine to enable real-time in vivo monitoring.

Advancing Human-Relevant Model Systems

Utilizing induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived neurons/glia, brain organoids, and spatial omics on postmortem human tissue to validate mechanisms directly in human systems.

Conducting Rigorously Designed Clinical Studies

Implementing longitudinal cohort studies with standardized, multi-center biospecimen collection paired with multi-omics analyses (metabolomics, proteomics, epigenomics) to definitively establish the value of D-serine pathway components as stratification biomarkers.

Engineering Precision Therapeutic Strategies

Exploring cell-specific gene therapy, brain-region-targeted nanodelivery systems, and allosteric modulators to enable “fine-tuning” rather than “broad-tuning” of the D-serine pathway.

In summary, the D-serine field is at a pivotal transition. Its strength lies in revealing unprecedented functional diversity and core pathophysiological roles. Its primary weaknesses reside in the translational pathway. Addressing these limitations is necessary to realize its full therapeutic potential.

Conclusion and Future Perspectives

D-serine has evolved from a simple NMDA receptor co-agonist to a critical nexus integrating cellular metabolism, epigenetic regulation, neural circuit function, and systemic physiology. Its “Yin-Yang” characteristics—mediating both essential synaptic plasticity and pathological excitotoxicity—underscore the paramount importance of precise homeostatic control. The recent discoveries of its non-canonical signaling roles, cell-type-specific actions, and involvement in peripheral diseases have fundamentally broadened its functional landscape and therapeutic relevance.

The translation of this complex biology into clinical benefit faces distinct challenges rooted in current evidence gaps. First, a translational disconnect exists between robust preclinical models and heterogeneous human diseases. Animal studies elucidate mechanism but cannot fully replicate the polygenic and prolonged nature of human psychiatric or neurodegenerative disorders. Second, methodological heterogeneity in measuring D-serine and related enzymes across laboratories complicates the validation of reliable biomarkers. Third, and most critically, the therapeutic precision deficit: the field still lacks tools to achieve cell-type, receptor-subtype, or circuit-specific modulation of D-serine signaling, which is essential given its context-dependent actions.

Therefore, future research must pivot towards integrative and precision-oriented approaches: (1) Developing novel in vivo imaging tools (e.g., PET tracers) to visualize D-serine dynamics in real time; (2) Leveraging human cellular models (iPSC-derived neurons/glia, organoids) to validate targets and mechanisms in a human genetic context; (3) Conducting rigorously designed clinical studies with standardized biomarker protocols and patient stratification based on D-serine pathway phenotypes; (4) Engineering next-generation therapeutics, such as cell-specific gene regulators or spatially targeted delivery systems, to achieve the necessary precision.

In conclusion, while the pathophysiological centrality of D-serine is firmly established, its journey from a compelling molecular target to a mainstay of therapy requires navigating its inherent biological duality. By embracing its complexity and developing the sophisticated tools needed to measure and modulate it with precision, the full therapeutic potential of this multitalented neuromodulator in neurology and psychiatry can be unlocked.
