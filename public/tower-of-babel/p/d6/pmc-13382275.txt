Structure–function insights into the digestive fate of angiotensin-converting enzyme-inhibitory peptides from microwave-treated corn gluten meal
Authors: Ali Hamzeh, Wasana Wongngam, Sittiruk Roytrakul, Sasitorn Aueviriyavit, Ratjika Wongwanakul, Fu Tian, Jirawat Yongsawatdigul, Rotimi E Aluko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCorn gluten meal (CGM) is an underutilized byproduct with rigid structure, which limits efficient peptide release during enzymatic hydrolysis. While microwave pretreatment can disrupt the rigid CGM matrix and enhance peptide release, this study investigated how downstream gastrointestinal modification and transport behavior ultimately influence the functional fate of the enzymatically liberated peptide sequences. Enzymatic hydrolysis of microwave-pretreated CGM led to a 3.3-fold increase in peptide yield while maintaining angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE)-inhibitory activity at a level comparable to that of the untreated CGM hydrolysate. From this digest, the purified peptide DVPSADAPAAAV (DV-12) exhibited potent in vitro ACE inhibition (IC₅₀ = 57 μM). Simulated gastrointestinal digestion partially hydrolyzed DV-12 to DVPSADAPAAA (DA-11), a minor structural modification that was overestimated by in silico digestion models yet resulted in a substantial reduction in ACE-inhibitory potency. Kinetic analysis and molecular dynamics simulations revealed that this truncation fundamentally altered peptide–ACE interactions: DV-12 (Kᵢ = 8 μM) maintained more stable coordination near the ACE Zn2+ catalytic center, whereas DA-11 displayed substantially weaker binding (Kᵢ = 1.06 mM). In Caco-2 monolayers, both peptides crossed the epithelial barrier at low rates, with apparent permeability coefficients of 9.96 × 10−8 and 8.23 × 10−8 cm·s−1 for DV-12 and DA-11, respectively, while unabsorbed peptide fractions, particularly DV-12, concurrently reduced intracellular reactive oxygen species. These findings demonstrated that increasing peptide yield alone does not guarantee preserved downstream functionality, as subtle structural modifications occurring during digestion and transport toward absorption could markedly alter peptide bioactivity and should therefore be considered in the development of functional food ingredients.Keywords: Bioactive peptides, Molecular dynamics, Caco-2 permeability, Gastrointestinal digestion

Introduction

Hypertension is a major global health challenge and a leading risk factor for cardiovascular mortality. This is driving continued interest in food-derived angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE)-inhibitory peptides as biocompatible functional ingredients for hypertension management (Chanajon et al., 2025). Numerous food-derived peptides have been reported to exhibit ACE-inhibitory activity and in vivo antihypertensive effects (Daza-Rodríguez et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2017). However, the generation of potent ACE-inhibitory peptides from plant proteins remains challenging because enzymatic proteolysis is strongly influenced by the structural organization and physicochemical properties of the parent protein matrix. Corn gluten meal (CGM), which is rich in the prolamin zein, represents a particularly recalcitrant substrate due to its highly hydrophobic and compact protein architecture (Wahl et al., 2025). In particular, the peripheral localization of γ-zein within protein bodies, together with extensive hydrophobic interactions and disulfide cross-linking, limit protease accessibility and restrict hydrolysis efficiency (Lee & Hamaker, 2006). As a result, potentially bioactive peptide sequences may remain embedded within the protein matrix and inaccessible during conventional enzymatic hydrolysis.

Microwave treatment has been explored as a processing approach to improve enzymatic hydrolysis efficiency and peptide release from food proteins (Hall & Liceaga, 2020; Reddy et al., 2013; Urbizo-Reyes et al., 2019). While it is often associated with enhanced proteolysis efficiency, its biochemical impact on downstream peptide functionality remains highly erratic, occasionally triggering losses in bioactivity (Uluko et al., 2013). This variability highlights an incomplete understanding of how microwave-induced hydrolysis influences protease cleavage patterns and consequently affects the amino acid sequence, composition, and structural characteristics of the resulting peptides, which may in turn influence their binding affinity to ACE. This is particularly important in CGM, which possesses an unusually high density of hydrophobic amino acids.

However, peptide generation alone is not the ultimate objective, as the generated peptides can undergo substantial modification by gastrointestinal proteases and brush-border membrane peptidases. Current in silico digestion algorithms primarily evaluate sequence survival based on linear primary structures and generally do not explicitly consider the potential influence of parent protein conformation or steric shielding on enzyme–substrate accessibility (Wang et al., 2022). Consequently, empirical validation remains important for assessing peptide stability during gastrointestinal digestion before absorption.

Molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations have increasingly been employed to investigate peptide–enzyme interactions beyond conventional activity measurements. While docking predicts potential binding modes and intermolecular interactions, MD simulations provide additional insights into conformational stability, dynamic peptide behavior, substrate displacement, and metal coordination during enzyme inhibition. These approaches are particularly relevant for ACE because its catalytic activity depends strongly on the structural organization surrounding the Zn2+ catalytic center (Masuyer et al., 2015). Integrating experimental digestion studies with molecular simulations may therefore provide mechanistic insights into how digestion-induced structural changes influence ACE-inhibitory activity at the molecular level.

Rather than serving solely as a screening study of an unexplored substrate, this study used CGM as a model system to investigate how microwave pretreatment influences peptide yield and how subsequent gastrointestinal digestion and intestinal transport affect the bioactivity and bioavailability of a potent ACE-inhibitory peptide isolated from the microwave-treated CGM hydrolysate. Molecular simulations were further employed to elucidate how structural modifications occurring throughout the peptide journey toward absorption influence ACE-inhibitory activity.

Materials and methods

Materials

CGM was supplied by Friendship Corn Starch Co. Ltd. (Samutprakan, Thailand). Neutrase® (EC 3.4.24; 0.8 AU/g), produced from Bacillus amyloliquefaciens, was obtained from Novozymes (Novo Nordisk A/S, Bagsværd, Denmark). HPLC-grade acetonitrile (ACN), trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), penicillin, streptomycin, l-glutamine, Hank's balanced salt solution (HBSS) and 2′7′-dichlorodihydro-fluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE), N-[3-(2-furyl)acryloyl]-Phe-Gly-Gly (FAPGG), 3-(4,5-dimethyl-2-thiazolyl)-2,5-diphenyl-2H-tetrazolium bromide (MTT), fetal bovine serum (FBS), non-essential amino acids, Triton X-100, bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 2,4,6-trinitrobenzenesulfonic acid solution (TNBS, 5% w/v) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). The human colorectal adenocarcinoma epithelial cell line Caco-2 was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was purchased from Gibco (New York, NY, USA). All remaining chemicals and reagents were of analytical grade.

Microwave treatment and hydrolysate preparation

CGM was sieved through a 60-mesh screen prior to the addition of deionized water (1:2 w/v). The mixture was subjected to microwave treatment in a microwave reactor (Ethos one, Milestone, Sorisole, Italy) at the power of 1450 W for 30 s at 200 °C. Temperature was monitored using a fiber-optic temperature probe placed in a reference vessel, and the measured temperature was used by the instrument's automatic feedback control system to regulate the microwave power and maintain the programmed temperature for all vessels throughout the treatment. The microwave conditions (power, treatment time, and temperature) were selected as suitable processing conditions for this study. These conditions consistently produced a high degree of hydrolysis without causing damage to the sample.

Subsequently, the mixture was adjusted to pH 7 using 1 M NaOH prior to enzyme addition. The enzyme (Neutrase) was added at 4% (w/w) of the substrate protein content, which was based on preliminary experiments evaluating the effect of enzyme addition level on the degree of hydrolysis. The mixture was incubated at 55 °C, in accordance with the manufacturer, and hydrolysis was allowed to proceed for 4 h. The reaction was terminated by heating at 95 °C in a water bath for 20 min. The hydrolysate prepared from microwave-pretreated CGM (Mw-CGMH) was collected by centrifugation at 10,000 ×g for 15 min and stored at −20 °C.

Peptide content determination

Peptide content was estimated based on α-amino group content using the TNBS method (Adler-Nissen, 1979) with slight modifications. Triplicate aliquots (10 μL) were mixed with 100 μL of sodium phosphate buffer (0.2125 M, pH 8.2) in a 96-well microtiter plate. Then, 50 μL of 0.05% (w/v) TNBS was added. Absorbance was measured at 420 nm using a microplate reader (Varioskan LUX, Thermo Scientific, Vantaa, Finland) at 2-min intervals for 30 min at 45 °C. L-leucine (0–10.0 mM) was used as the standard.

Peptide purification

PuriFlash C18 AQ reverse-phase HPLC (RP-HPLC) chromatography

The Mw-CGMH was purified using a PuriFlash C18-AQ F0012 flash column (15 μm, Interchim, Montlugon Cedex, France) using a 2-mL loop to load the sample. Elution was performed at room temperature at a flow rate of 10 mL/min. The column was equilibrated with 1 column volume (CV) of mobile phase A (0.1% TFA in DI water). The elution gradient was started with 0–2% of mobile phase B (0.1% TFA in ACN) for 5 CVs, 2–10%B for 10 CVs, 10–20%B for 10 CVs, 20–30%B for 5 CVs and 30–100% B for 3 CVs. The column was cleaned after each run with 100% B using 3 CVs. The eluate was monitored at 214 nm and collected in 15-mL fractions. Yield (%) was calculated based on the α-amino group content of each fraction compared to the Mw-CGMH. Fractions showing the highest ACE inhibition were pooled, lyophilized and subjected to further analysis.

Preparative high-pressure liquid chromatography (prep-HPLC)

The lyophilized powder collected from the PuriFlash peptide column was resuspended in deionized water and loaded onto a preparative PF5C18AQ-250/212 RP-HPLC column (5 μm, 21.2 × 250 mm, Interchim, Montluçon Cedex, France). Elution was performed at room temperature at a flow rate of 16 mL/min. The column was equilibrated using 1 CV of DI water (mobile phase C). The sample was eluted with a linear gradient of mobile phase A (0.1% TFA in DI water), B (0.1% TFA in ACN), and C as follows: A-B-C (0:0:100, v/v) to A-B-C (100:0:0, v/v) for 2 CVs, A-B-C (80:20:0, v/v) for 3 CVs, A-B-C (60:35:5, v/v) for 7 CVs, A-B-C (0:100:0, v/v) for 1 CV, and finally back to A-B-C (0:0:100, v/v) for 3 CVs. The absorbance was monitored at 214 nm, and 10-mL fractions were collected. Fractions showing the highest ACE inhibition was selected for peptide identification.

Peptide sequencing

Amino acid sequence of peptides in the most active fraction prepared from Prep-HPLC was determined using the Ultimate 3000 LC System (Dionex UltiMate 3000 UHPLC, Thermo Scientific, Germering, Germany) with a Nano-captive spray ion source coupled to a Hybrid quadrupole Q-TOF impact IITM (Bruker, Daltonics, Germany). Peptide mass analysis was performed in positive ion mode using ESI-MS/MS, and separation was achieved on a nanocolumn (Acclaim PepMap 100 C18, 3 μm, 100 Å, 75 μm i.d. × 150 mm, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Elution was carried out using a gradient of mobile phase A (0.1% formic acid in water) and a 0–70% linear gradient of mobile phase B (80% ACN in water with 0.1% formic acid). Peptides were eluted at a flow rate of 300 nL/min for 13 min.

De novo amino acid sequencing was performed using PepNovo (http://proteomics.ucsd.edu/LiveSearch/). Sequences with de novo score > 60% were selected. The peptide sequences were subsequently aligned against Zea mays (taxid: 4577) proteins. (http://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) to confirm their likely origin from corn. Based on these criteria, nine peptides were chosen for further evaluation of ACE inhibitory activity.

Peptide synthesis, in silico and in vitro GI digestion

Based on PepNovo scores and sequence similarity to CGM proteins, nine out of 48 peptides identified by LC-MS/MS were selected and chemically synthesized using solid-phase peptide synthesis (GL Biochem Ltd., Shanghai, China). Peptides were purified using an HPLC column to 95% purity. Molecular mass of synthetic peptides was verified by the manufacturer using liquid chromatography coupled to a mass spectrometer (LC-MS/ESI). The hydrophobicity and net charge of the peptides were calculated using online tools, including the Peptide Analyzing Tool provided by ThermoFisher Scientific (https://www.thermofisher.com/) and PepCalc. (https://pepcalc.com/), respectively.

The potent peptide was subjected to GI digestion using both in silico and in vitro methods. The in silico digestion was carried out by pepsin, trypsin, chymotrypsin and pancreatic elastase using the BIOPEP program (http://www.uwm.edu.pl/ biochemia/index.php/ pl/biopep). The in vitro GI digestion was performed according to the method described by (Xu et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2019) with some modifications. Briefly, a synthetic peptide at the concentration of 2 mg/mL (prepared in ultra-pure water) was added pepsin (2% w/w, pH 2) at 37 °C for 2 h. Thereafter, the mixture was raised to pH 7.5 prior to addition of pancreatin (2% w/w). The digestion proceeded for another 4 h and subsequently terminated by heating in a 95 °C water bath. The reaction without sample was considered as a control. The digesta was filtered using a 0.22-μm membrane syringe filter and the filtrate subjected to amino acid sequence determination using LC-MS/MS as described in 2.5.

The ACE inhibition was determined as previously described (Wongngam et al., 2023). Briefly, a mixture of 10 μL ACE solution (final activity 0.1 U/mL) and 20 μL peptide sample was added to a 96-well microtiter plate and incubated at room temperature for 5 min. A 70 μL of 0.5 mM FAPGG, dissolved in 50 mM Tris–HCl buffer containing 0.3 mM NaCl, pH 7.5, was added to the reaction mixture. A decrease in absorbance was monitored at 340 nm using a microplate reader (Varioskan LUX, Thermo Scientific, Vantaa, Finland) at 2-min intervals for 30 min at 37 °C. Cellular experiments using Caco-2 cell line

Cell maintenance and cytotoxicity

Caco-2 cells were seeded in 24-well plates (Corning Life Sciences, Tewksbury, MA, USA) at a density of 25 × 103 cells/cm2 and maintained for 21 days in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin, 1% non-essential amino acids, and 2 mM l-glutamine. The culture medium was refreshed every other day. For the cytotoxicity assay, 100 μL of peptide dissolved in HBSS at concentrations ranging from 1 to 4 mg/mL was added to the cells and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C. HBSS without peptide and 0.1% Triton X-100 served as the negative and positive controls, respectively. After treatment, the cells were washed with PBS and incubated with MTT solution (1 mg/mL) for 3 h at 37 °C. Formazan crystals were then extracted by adding isopropanol and shaking the plates for 30 min at room temperature. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader. Caco-2 cell monolayer permeability assay

The permeability of the peptides was evaluated following our previously described method (Chanajon et al., 2025). Cells were prepared as described above. A 100 μL aliquot of peptide (at its maximum non-toxic concentration) containing 100 μM lucifer yellow in HBSS was added to the apical compartment, while 600 μL of HBSS was added to the basal compartment. Cellular antioxidant assay

The Caco-2 cells at the concentration of 5 × 104 were seeded on a 96-well plate and incubated in an CO2 incubator at 37 °C for 24 h. Cells were then incubated with DCFH-DA for 30 min, washed with HBSS buffer, and subsequently treated with DA-11 or DV-12 for 2 h. Besides the antioxidant activity of the peptides under normal condition, activity was assessed in H2O2– induced damage cells, in which the cells were pre-incubated with 1 mM H2O2, washed with HBSS and added with samples including DA-11, DV-12 or NAC (positive control). The cells added with HBSS were used as a control in both methods. Fluorescence intensity was then measured at excitation and emission wavelengths of 485/535 nm to quantify liberated DCF and the relative activity compared to control was reported.

Kinetics of ACE inhibition

The kinetics of ACE and inhibition were evaluated at various concentrations of substrates, FAPGG, in the presence of various concentrations of the potent peptides. The mode of ACE inhibition was determined from Lineweaver-Burk plots, while enzyme-inhibitor dissociation constant (Ki) were estimated using GraphPad Prism version 5.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA. Kinetic experiments were conducted in two independent biological replications, each comprising triplicate measurements. The results were used for comparative evaluation of inhibition mechanisms and determination of inhibition constants.

Docking analysis

The peptide structures were generated using PyMol 2.5 and the three-dimensional structure of ACE (PDB:1UZF) was obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (http://www.rcsb.org). The structure of FAPGG was obtained from the PubChem database. The docking model was selected based on the inhibition pattern obtained from kinetic analysis. Because the peptide exhibited uncompetitive/mixed-type inhibition (from 2.9), docking simulations were performed using the ACE–FAPGG complex. This model was then used as the receptor for peptide docking. ACE was prepared by removing water molecules and co-crystal ligands before the docking analysis. Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina with its empirical free-energy scoring function (Trott & Olson, 2010). The grid box was defined with dimensions of x = 56, y = 70, and z = 56 to cover the catalytic groove and substrate-binding region surrounding the catalytic Zn2+ site. Other parameters were set to default values. For each peptide, the docking pose with the lowest binding energy and reasonable placement within the ACE catalytic/substrate-binding region was selected as the initial structure for subsequent MD simulations.

Molecular dynamics simulation

GROMACS version 2024.1 was used for the MD simulations using AMBER99SB-ILDN force filed (Lindorff-Larsen et al., 2010). The catalytic Zn2+ ion was retained from the ACE structure and treated using the standard non-bonded Zn2+ parameters in GROMACS with the AMBER99SB-ILDN force field, without additional bonded coordination restraints during production MD simulations. The same Zn2+ treatment was used for all ACE–FAPGG–peptide systems. The simulation box size was optimized to ensure that the distance between each protein atom and the box boundary exceeded 1.0 nm. The TIP3P water model was used to solvate the protein–ligand complex. To achieve electrical neutrality, water molecules were replaced with Cl− and Na+ ions. Energy optimization of 5.0 × 104 steps was performed following the steepest descent method to remove unfavorable contacts. Then, first-phase equilibration was performed with the constant number of particles, volume, and temperature (NVT) ensemble at 300 K for 100 ps to stabilize system temperature, and the second-phase equilibration was simulated with the constant number of particles, pressure, and temperature (NPT) ensemble at 300 K for 100 ps to stabilize the system temperature, followed by second-phase equilibration under the constant number of particles, pressure, and temperature (NPT) ensemble at 1 bar for 100 ps. Solvated protein ligand complexes were then subjected to 100 ns production MD simulations. Temperature and pressure were controlled using V-rescale and Parrinello-Rahman methods, respectively. For all analyses of root mean square deviation (RMSD) and distances, only the equilibrated part of the trajectories (20–100 ns) was used.

Statistical analyses

All experiments were conducted using at least three independent biological replications unless otherwise stated. Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical significance was evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan's multiple range test at p < 0.05 using SPSS version 17.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results and discussion

Microwave-pretreated CGM hydrolysis

Microwave pretreatment significantly enhanced the enzymatic hydrolysis of CGM, resulting in a 3.3-fold increase in peptide yield (11.5 mg Leu/mL vs. 3.5 mg Leu/mL for the control; p < 0.05). Notably, this treatment did not compromise the bioactivity of the hydrolysates, as ACE-inhibitory activity remained stable at approximately 64% for both pretreated and untreated samples at a concentration of 0.1 mg/mL (p > 0.05). This suggests that microwave treatment primarily improved accessibility of the proteins to enzymatic hydrolysis but did not alter the intrinsic bioactivity of released peptides. The enhanced yield may be attributed to microwave-induced structural modification of CGM proteins, which may improve enzyme accessibility to susceptible peptide bonds and hydrolysis efficiency, consistent with previous findings for plant proteins subjected to microwave processing (Reddy et al., 2013; Sun et al., 2020). Gazikalović et al. (2021) demonstrated that microwave pretreatment of wheat gluten at 100 °C for 1 min altered protein secondary structure and increased the accessibility of Alcalase cleavage sites. Consequently, microwave-pretreated samples exhibited a significantly higher hydrolysis rate than untreated controls. Although structural characterization was not performed in the present study, a similar mechanism may explain enhanced hydrolysis efficiency.

Neutrase was confirmed as the most effective enzyme for generating ACE-inhibitory peptides from CGM, consistent with its preference for hydrophobic residues such as Leu, Pro, and Val—amino acids frequently associated with ACE inhibition. The retention of ACE-inhibitory activity despite increased peptide yield highlights microwave pretreatment as a promising strategy for improving processing efficiency without compromising bioactivity.

Peptide fractionation

RP-HPLC fractionation of the hydrolysate yielded four peptide fractions (F1–F4), among which F3 exhibited the strongest ACE-inhibitory activity (IC₅₀ = 9.2 μg/mL) and a yield of 32.9% (Fig. S1A, Table 1). This corresponded to a 2.5-fold increase in potency compared with the crude hydrolysate. Subsequent purification by preparative RP-HPLC further enhanced bioactivity, yielding fraction H1 with an IC₅₀ of 1.3 μg/mL, approximately 16-fold more potent than the crude hydrolysate (Fig. S1B).

Notably, both the activity and yield of the purified fraction exceeded those previously obtained from non-microwave-treated CGM using similar purification strategies (Wongngam et al., 2023), highlighting the effectiveness of microwave pretreatment in enhancing peptide recovery and functionality. Compared with peptides derived from other plant sources, such as Camellia oleifera or wheat gliadin, the present peptides exhibited superior ACE-inhibitory potency, underscoring the value of CGM as a bioactive peptide source (Thewissen et al., 2011; Yao et al., 2019).

Peptides identification and structure-activity relationship

As shown in Table 2, nine peptides were identified from the most active fraction, together with their net charge and hydrophobicity values. They exhibited moderate to strong ACE-inhibitory activity (IC₅₀: 57–615 μM). The presence of Leu, Val, and Pro at N-terminus of some peptides is consistent with the hydrolytic specificity of neutrase for peptide bonds that involve hydrophobic amino acids. Among them, DVPSADAPAAAV (DV-12) demonstrated the highest potency (IC₅₀ = 57 μM). Structure–activity analysis revealed that peptide bioactivity was influenced by hydrophobicity, charge distribution, and terminal amino acid composition. DV-12 possessed moderate hydrophobicity and a negatively charged N-terminus, features known to enhance ACE binding. Although highly hydrophobic peptides often display strong ACE inhibition, excessive hydrophobicity can reduce efficacy because of the stronger peptide-peptide interactions at the expense of peptide-ACE interactions, explaining the lower activity of NLLQDLSC and LTDAAPSQFD despite their higher hydrophobicity. The presence of Asp at the N-terminus of DV-12 appears particularly important, as acidic residues are known to coordinate with the Zn2+ ion at the ACE active site. Similar interactions have been reported for other potent ACE-inhibitory peptides such as DLLGCS (Wongngam et al., 2023). Although other Mw-CGM derived peptides, namely, LTDAAPSQFD, DCTPGPPEP and DQQYE possess the same net charge as DV-12, their lower hydrophobicity may account for the reduced ACE-inhibitory potency. These findings indicate that optimal ACE inhibition depends on a balance among hydrophobicity, charge distribution, and sequence length rather than on any single structural feature.

In vitro gastrointestinal digestion of DVPSADAPAAAV and comparison with in silico digestion prediction

In vitro gastrointestinal digestion of DV-12 produced peptide fragments that differed substantially from in silico predictions. While in silico digestion suggested extensive cleavage into dipeptides including DV, PA, PS and DA, experimental digestion revealed the persistence of the parent peptide and the formation of larger fragments, particularly DVPSADAPAAA (DA-11) with lower ACE-inhibitory activity (Fig. S2). This discrepancy provides an example of the challenges in relying solely on theoretical cleavage-site predictions generated by in silico digestion tools, which do not account for enzyme kinetics, substrate accessibility, peptide conformation, or steric hindrance. Proteolysis in physical in vitro or in vivo systems is dynamically governed by enzyme kinetics, where substrate concentration, enzyme accessibility, and competitive interactions among co-existing digestion products influence actual cleavage efficiency. Moreover, in silico models cannot account for peptide aggregation phenomena. In the case of DV-12, the large hydrophobic face formed by the APAAAV motif may promote spontaneous peptide self-association, which can sterically hinder protease access to remaining cleavage sites and thereby limit further degradation despite theoretically favorable cleavage predictions (García-Mora et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2019). Such a variation has been reported on the digestion of bovine milk proteins including αs1-casein, αs2-casein, β-casein, k-casein, β-lactoglobulin and α-lactalbumin (Barati et al., 2020). The resistance of DV-12 to complete digestion can be attributed to the presence of proline residues, which restrict backbone flexibility and reduce susceptibility to proteolytic cleavage (Wang et al., 2025). Similar resistance has been reported for other proline-rich bioactive peptides. These findings highlight the importance of experimentally validating in silico digestion predictions when assessing the digestive fate of individual peptides.

Enzyme inhibition kinetics

Kinetic analysis revealed that DV-12 acts as an uncompetitive inhibitor (Fig. 1A), while DA-11 exhibits mixed-type inhibition (Fig. 1B). Consistent with its superior ACE-inhibitory activity, DV-12 displayed a markedly lower inhibition constant (Kᵢ = 8 μM) than the DA-11 (1.06 mM). Such a correlation was observed for the peptides IER and IGK (Memarpoor-Yazdi et al., 2020), and TF, LY and RALP (He et al., 2014).

Lineweaver–Burk plots illustrating uncompetitive and mixed-type inhibition of DV-12 and DA-11 toward ACE. Ki is the enzyme-inhibitor dissociation constant.

Although lower Ki values generally correlate with stronger inhibition, discrepancies between kinetic constants and IC₅₀ values have been reported for other bioactive peptides. Zarei et al. (2022) reported the higher ACE inhibition for GGIF with IC₅₀ of 1.27 mM compared with GIFE (18 mM), but the former had a higher Ki value of 92 mM compared with 3.0 mM for the latter. The peptide CS derived from CGM, which exhibited higher ACE-inhibitory activity, showed a slightly higher Ki values (12.5 μM) compared with DLLGCS (Ki = 11.4 μM) (Wongngam et al., 2023). This reflects the complexity of enzyme–peptide interactions, where binding orientation, conformational changes, and multi-site interactions influence inhibitory behavior.

Molecular docking

Despite the marked difference in experimental ACE-inhibitory activity, docking predicted comparable binding interactions and binding energies for DV-12 (−10.39 kcal mol−1) and DA-11 (−9.82 kcal mol−1) (Fig. S3). This discrepancy indicates that static docking alone was insufficient to explain the observed bioactivity differences, consistent with previous reports where peptides with comparable docking scores exhibited substantially different inhibitory potencies (Hatakenaka et al., 2023). Visualization of the 80-ns structures in PyMOL, including the S1, S2, and S1′ subsites, showed that neither peptide is fully buried within a single pocket; instead, both align along the surface of the active groove and interact with only part of the S1/S2 residues. Both peptides exhibited contact occupancies close to 1 in the S2 region (Fig. S3), indicating that S2 engagement is a common feature of DA-11 and DV-12 and does not by itself explain their difference in IC₅₀. Therefore, interactions between the peptides and ACE were further investigated using MD simulations.

Molecular dynamics analysis of DA-11 and DV-12 binding to ACE

Conformational stability of inhibitory peptides

The structural stability of ACE during the simulations was first evaluated using backbone RMSD (Fig. 2A). For all systems—ES (enzyme–substrate–Zn), DA-11, and DV-12—the RMSD rapidly increased during the initial equilibration phase and then plateaued, remaining stable at approximately 0.16–0.20 nm throughout the 20–100 ns production window. No systematic drift or large conformational deviation was observed, indicating that the overall fold of ACE remained well preserved in all systems (Lu et al., 2021). Therefore, subsequent analyses were performed using the equilibrated trajectories from 20 to 100 ns.

Root mean square deviation (RMSD) profiles for (A) ACE backbone, (B) FAPGG substrate, and (C) inhibitory peptides. (A) All ACE complexes reached equilibrium within the initial 20 ns of simulation. (B) Substrate stability was maintained in ES (enzyme–substrate–Zn) and DV-12 systems, while DA-11 induced progressive destabilization after 60 ns. (C) DV-12 displayed consistent conformational stability (0.23–0.30 nm), whereas DA-11 underwent a significant rearrangement (0.35–0.40 nm) after 40 ns.

The RMSD profiles of FAPGG (Fig. 2B) showed substrate behavior dependent on the inhibitory peptide. In the ES system, RMSD increased initially and then stabilized at ∼0.25 nm, indicating stable binding within the active site. In the DV-12 system, RMSD values were consistently higher than ES but remained moderate, suggesting sustained displacement without major destabilization. In contrast, the DA-11 system showed a time-dependent effect: RMSD was similar to ES during the first ∼40 ns, increased moderately between 40 and 60 ns, and rose markedly after ∼60 ns with large fluctuations at the end, indicating progressive substrate destabilization. Overall, DV-12 induced early and sustained substrate displacement, whereas DA-11 exhibited a delayed and less stable effect.

When simulation was carried in the presence of individual peptides and ACE (Fig. 2C), DV-12 maintained low RMSD values (∼0.23–0.30 nm), indicating stable binding. In contrast, DA-11 showed two-phase behavior: low RMSD (∼0.20–0.25 nm) during the first 40 ns, followed by an increase to ∼0.35–0.40 nm, reflecting conformational rearrangement. This shift coincided with the rise in FAPGG RMSD, suggesting that DA-11 fluctuations may reduce substrate stability.

Spatial organization of the active site

In ACE, the catalytic Zn2+ ion is essential for enzymatic activity, as it polarizes the scissile peptide bond and activates a zinc-bound water molecule that attacks this bond (Masuyer et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2013). Therefore, the spatial relationship among Zn2+, the substrate FAPGG, and the inhibitory peptides is directly relevant to their inhibitory mechanism.

Representative snapshots at 80 ns (Fig. 3A–C) show that in the ES system, FAPGG remains close to Zn2+ within the catalytic groove. In the DA-11 system, the substrate is slightly shifted from Zn2+, whereas in the DV-12 system, FAPGG is more substantially displaced, with Asp6 of DV-12 occupying the metal vicinity. The distance between Zn2+ and the carbonyl oxygen of the scissile bond (FAP_CO) (Fig. 4A), reflecting catalytic accessibility (Gunalan et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2021), averaged 0.75 ± 0.07 nm in ES, indicating stable positioning near Zn2+. This distance increased to 1.19 ± 0.08 nm in DA-11—consistent with the elevated FAPGG RMSD after 60 ns (Fig. 3B)—and further to 1.52 ± 0.05 nm in DV-12. The greater and sustained separation in DV-12 indicates a stronger and more persistent steric and electrostatic barrier that limits substrate access to the catalytic metal compared with DA-11.

(B) ACE–DA-11–FAPGG complex at the beginning (0 ns) and end (80 ns) of the simulation. (C) ACE–DV-12–FAPGG complex at the beginning (0 ns) and end (80 ns) of the simulation.

Distance distributions obtained from MD trajectories. (A) Zn2+–FAPGG carbonyl oxygen distance (Zn–FAP_CO). (B) Zn2+–Asp6 distance. Density plots were generated from the final 80 ns of simulation.

To further clarify the inhibitory mechanism, the distance between Zn2+ and the Asp6 side chain of each peptide was analyzed (Fig. 4B). In DV-12, this distance showed a sharp peak at 0.49 ± 0.05 nm, indicating stable coordination of Asp6 with Zn2+ and effective occupation of the metal site (Natesh et al., 2003). In contrast, DA-11 displayed a larger and broader distribution (0.93 ± 0.05 nm), reflecting weak and transient interaction. The MD simulations reveal distinct inhibitory mechanisms for DA-11 and DV-12. DV-12 exerts a dual action by stably coordinating Zn2+ via Asp6 and persistently displacing the substrate from the catalytic center, thereby disrupting the geometry required for catalysis and accounting for its lower IC₅₀ and stronger ACE-inhibitory activity. In contrast, DA-11 only partially altered substrate positioning and did not establish stable metal coordination, allowing intermittent substrate access and resulting in weaker ACE inhibition. Overall, these findings provide a molecular-level explanation for the differing inhibitory potencies of DA-11 and DV-12 and highlight the importance of sustained metal coordination in effective ACE inhibition.

It should be noted that MD-derived structural interpretations may depend on the selected force field, Zn2+ parameters, and simulation settings. In this study, all systems were simulated using the same AMBER99SB-ILDN-based protocol, and the mechanistic discussion was based on relative differences between DA-11 and DV-12 rather than absolute quantitative predictions. Therefore, the present MD results should be interpreted as a comparative structural explanation consistent with the experimental IC₅₀ values. Further validation using alternative simulation protocols would be useful to assess the generality of the proposed mechanism.

Intestinal transport across Caco-2 monolayers

TEER values before transport ranged from 431 to 478 Ω·cm2, confirming the formation of a well-differentiated and intact Caco-2 monolayer. Following the 4 h transport experiments, TEER values decreased to 254–280 Ω·cm2 (Table S1). Despite this reduction, the post-transport TEER values remained above the commonly accepted threshold of 150 Ω·cm2 for maintaining monolayer integrity, indicating that the epithelial barrier remained functional throughout the permeability assay (Fredlund et al., 2017; Srinivasan et al., 2015). In addition, Lucifer yellow leakage remained below 2% after 4 h of treatment (0.37% for the control, 0.84% for DA-11, and 0.73% for DV-12), further confirming the integrity of the Caco-2 monolayer (Debebe et al., 2012). Similar decreases in TEER have been reported following transport studies and are generally attributed to prolonged incubation, routine handling during sampling, and reversible changes in ion flux and paracellular permeability rather than irreversible damage to the cell monolayer (Mukherjee et al., 2004). Collectively, these findings indicate that the transport experiment did not compromise the integrity of the Caco-2 monolayer.

Both DV-12 and DA-11 were able to permeate the Caco-2 monolayer (Fig. 5A and B), with apparent permeability coefficients of 9.96 × 10−8 and 8.23 × 10−8 cm·s−1, respectively (Fig. 5C). Transport occurred primarily within the first 2 h, although partial degradation was observed over 4 h (Apical 4 h; Fig. 5A and B). Peptide transportation across epithelial cells can occur via several mechanisms, such as transcytosis, which facilitates permeation of larger peptides. Additionally, peptide properties, including hydrophobicity and the presence of polar residues, can influence transport efficiency (Amigo & Hernández-Ledesma, 2020). The slightly higher permeability of DV-12 compared to DA-11, might have to do with the higher hydrophobicity of the former induced by Val. Peptides showed various Papp through Caco-2 cell monolayer. Xu et al. (2017) reported that the long chain peptide YWDHNNPQIR permeated the Caco-2 cell monolayer through intracellular transcytosis pathway with a Papp of 6.6 × 10−7 cm.s−1. A Papp of 2.5 × 10−7 cm.s−1 was reported for RKQLQGVN (Fernández-Tomé et al., 2018). In addition, two well-known antihypertensive peptides, VPP and IPP, have been reported to permeate through Caco-2 cell monolayer with the Papp of 0.5 and 1.0 × 10−8 cm.s−1, respectively (Foltz et al., 2008). DV-12 exhibited slightly higher permeability, likely due to its greater hydrophobicity, which facilitates membrane interaction and transcellular transport. The detection of intact peptides after transport demonstrates that epithelial permeation is possible under the experimental conditions. However, the relatively low permeability suggests limited intestinal transport, and these results should be interpreted as qualitative evidence of absorption potential.

Transport of peptides across Caco-2 cell monolayers. (A–B) LC–MS chromatograms of apical and basolateral samples. (C) Apparent permeability coefficients (Papp). Values are expressed as mean ± SD (n = 3). Bars with different letters have significantly different (p < 0.05) mean values.

Although the Caco-2 monolayer is a useful qualitative model for intestinal permeability screening, it cannot accurately predict in vivo peptide absorption because of several physiological limitations. Conventional Caco-2 systems lack the mucus layer and functional M-cells involved in peptide transcytosis, which may alter the uptake behavior of long or highly charged peptides and consequently affect their actual bioavailability (de Jesus da Silva et al., 2025). In addition, these models possess excessively tight paracellular junctions and static pH conditions that do not reflect the dynamic gastrointestinal environment (Awortwe et al., 2014). They also express lower levels of brush-border peptidases and transporter networks than native intestinal tissue, potentially underestimating peptide degradation, efflux, and first-pass metabolism during absorption (Jochems et al., 2018). Therefore, the present permeability results should be interpreted as relative indicators of epithelial transport behavior rather than direct predictors of human oral absorption, and further in vivo studies are required to confirm peptide bioavailability.

Cellular antioxidant activity

As proportions of the peptides DV-12 and DA-11 stay intact in apical (about 60% and 58% after 4 h, respectively), their cellular antioxidant activity in Caco-2 was assessed to determine their potential multifunctionality. Unabsorbed peptides may confer local benefits, such as protecting intestinal cells from oxidative damage, stimulating mucus secretion to support mucosal integrity, and positively influencing gut microbial composition in ways that promote a favorable oxidative status (Bao & Wu, 2021; Okagu & Udenigwe, 2022).

The reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation in normal Caco-2 cells (Fig. 6A) along with H2O2–induced damaged ones (Fig. 6B) upon the exposure to either of the peptides was monitored. In the normal cells, a 43.2 and 41.2% reduction in ROS was observed in cells treated with DA-11 and DV-12, respectively (p > 0.05; Fig. 6A). Whereas only DV-12 exhibited notable antioxidant activity in H2O2–induced damaged cell, reducing ROS levels to approximately 25% relative to the control (Fig. 6B). DV-12 was more effective than 3 mM N-acetylcysteine (NAC) in ROS restrain. The two peptides have similar sequences and are different only at the C-terminus. Results of this study indicate the importance of the C-terminus residue, in which the presence of Val produces a more active peptide. Val at the C-terminus of DV-12 increased the proportion of a bulky amino acid in the peptide structure, which has been reported to enhance antioxidant activity, along with peptide hydrophobicity (Ketnawa et al., 2018). Zou et al. (2016) reported that Val contributes to an enhanced hydrophobic environment for peptide. A peptide with higher hydrophobicity has more affinity to lipid based cellular interface, resulting in more effective activity in disrupting ROS (Hu et al., 2023). In addition to its ACE-inhibitory activity, DV-12 exhibited intracellular ROS-reducing activity in Caco-2 cells. Since oxidative stress is associated with reduced nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability, future studies may investigate whether the antioxidant activity of DV-12 influences NO bioavailability and contributes to its antihypertensive potential.

Conclusion

Microwave-assisted processing effectively enhanced the production of angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE)-inhibitory peptides from corn gluten meal hydrolysates. However, this study demonstrated that increased peptide yield alone is insufficient to fully realize the value of this abundant agro-industrial by-product. By integrating experimental digestion, cellular transport studies, enzyme kinetics, and molecular simulations, the results showed that although DVPSADAPAAAV (DV-12), the most potent ACE-inhibitory peptide identified (IC₅₀ = 57 μM), exhibited partial resistance to gastrointestinal digestion, even a subtle structural modification resulting in the formation of DVPSADAPAAA (DA-11) markedly reduced its inhibitory potency. Molecular simulations suggested that this reduction was associated with weaker interactions near the ACE catalytic site. In addition, both DV-12 and DA-11 exhibited low permeability across Caco-2 monolayers, indicating potential limitations in intestinal absorption. Collectively, these findings support the potential of corn gluten meal as a source of bioactive peptides while highlighting the importance of preserving peptide structural integrity throughout digestion and absorption. Future studies should focus on strategies to enhance peptide stability and bioavailability and on validating their biological efficacy as well as safety in appropriate in vivo models before their application as functional food ingredients can be established.
