Authors: Xin Yang, Jiao Zheng, Dan Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The replacement of farmland by native hygro-plants is increasingly common globally within the context of wetland ecosystem restoration. Understanding the long-term effects of this replacement on the abundance and persistence of soil organic carbon (SOC) in mountain marshes is important for soil carbon management. Introduction

Wetlands cover 4 ~ 6% of the total land area, but account for 20 ~ 30% of the total carbon storage of terrestrial ecosystems1,2. Being characterized by permanent water accumulation and limited decomposition of plant residues, wetland soils are rich in particulate organic matter2–4. In organic wetland soils, the proportion of particulate organic carbon (POC) constituting soil organic carbon (SOC) has been shown to be higher than that of mineral-associated organic carbon (MAOC)2,5,6. However, with the recent intensification of global warming and irresponsible modification of land-use, the “carbon sink” function of wetlands may be weakened by the end of this century, and these ecosystems may even be converted into sources of atmospheric carbon dioxide3. Thus, wetland soils are the focus of current conservation and restoration efforts to help offset rising carbon emissions7–10.

Most of the plants growing in marshes are annual herbaceous plants, which easily decompose after withering, and the stability of organic carbon in the produced detritus is poor2,11. In marsh wetlands, carbon is mainly stored in the soil, with more than 95% of this element being stored in the SOC pool1. Compared with natural marshes, the drainage of the marshes will increase the loss of SOC12. An estimated 2.91 million ha of marshlands that were converted into croplands in northeastern China resulted in SOC losses of ~ 240 Tg13. In many cases, organic carbon stocks, that have been slowly accumulating over centuries to thousands of years, can be lost in a matter of days or decades10,14. While an increasing number of studies have focused on SOC stocks, their stability and sequestration rates in salt marshes9,15,16, less is known about the replacement of farmland by native hygro-plants on SOC sequestration in the reclaimed plateau mountain marshes.

Mountain marshes, which are mostly distributed in mountainous areas, are characterized by a scattered distribution and small area, and are difficult to identify via remote sensing images11. In China, the subalpine plateau mountain marshes are usually herbaceous marshes, and the moss plants usually occupy the dominant position or co-grow with the herbaceous vascular plants. The very low decomposition rate of sphagnum moss made it account for about 50.0% of the world’s soil carbon storage11, and it has been regarded as a flagship species of carbon sequestration. However, the intensification of human activities, reduces wetland areas and causes resource degradation, making the ecosystem increasingly fragile, with some marsh wetlands being even at risk of disappearance11. Changes in wetland extent (e.g. in terms of vegetation cover, hydrology, and land use patterns) may alter organic carbon abundance and persistence in mountain marshes across different regions11.Thus, it is necessary to clarify the long-term effects of replacing rice paddies with Sphagnum palustre L. (S. palustre) on the size and stability of SOC pools in the reclaimed plateau mountain marshes.

The storage of SOC in reclaimed wetlands has been shown to be substantially affected by the restoration time at specific sites14,17,18. In this study, we used sampling data collected during a restoration period (at 0, 2, 10, and 20 years) in which rice paddies were replaced by S. palustre (at 0, 2, 10, and 20 years) in Dushan Duliu River wetland, Guizhou Province, China. We hypothesize the variations in SOC were associated with soil moisture content (SMC). The present study seeks to answer: (1) How do SOC and MAOC vary under the long-term effects of replacing rice paddies with S. palustre? (2) What is the relationship of SOC and related physicochemical variables in the long-term effects of replacing rice paddies with S. palustre?

Discussion

SOC concentrations after restoration

SOC concentrations in the marsh wetlands were related to the balance of organic carbon inputs, SOC decomposition/transformation, and environmental loss4,8,19. Compared to the abundant nitrogen habitats, the higher accumulations of SOC in cold–humid regions were related to the poor nutrient status and low temperature conditions5. SOC concentration was significantly greater in the natural Sphagnum locations than the rehabilitated sites and rice paddies (Fig. 2h). Similar to the distribution of SOC in the alpine ecosystems5 and Sphagnum wetlands6, the proportions of labile organic carbon in total SOC were expectedly higher than of MAOC in total SOC of this case (Fig. 3c). The mean MAOC/SOC in this case is smaller than that in alpine and boreal wetlands11 probably because of the difference in the precipitation inputs, evapotranspiration outputs and temperature variations. When the capacity of soil to store MAOC approaches the saturation point, this fraction cannot continue to accumulate20. Evidently, clay was negatively correlated with SOC (Fig. 3d) and the proportions of MAOC in SOC decreased over the restoration period (Fig. 3c), the increasing in the unstable organic carbon/SOC ratio was attributed to larger fresh SOC inputs and limited outputs (e.g. microbial activities and erosion loss). The increasing labile organic carbon especially the non-lignin compounds may inhibit microbial and extracellular processing for the moister soils21 and it is reflected that the restored wetlands have much lower SOC decomposition rates and/or erosion compared with the degraded wetlands22. The abundance of SOC, included labile organic carbon and MAOC, increased with the number of years since the start of restoration17,22–24 and the rate of SOC accumulation in the later stage of restoration was lower than that during the first several years17,18,22. This suggested that the time required to restore the SOC storage to the level observed in natural wetlands may vary with the degree of habitat modification17.

Key factors affecting SOC accumulation

Limited water availability tends to inhibit Sphagnum growth and accelerate SOC loss. SMC was positively correlated with SOC4,25 and increased with the restoration time23,26. Sphagnum paludiculture facilitated the accumulation of Sphagnum-derived SOC in agricultural fields27. Compared with the drained sites, the waterlogged habitats exhibited a higher SOC content28, and the long-term drainage consistently elevated the loss of SOC in Sphagnum habitats12. In Sphagnum wetlands, the more amount of plant residues entering the soils contributed to the elevated SOC, improved soil water holding capacity29,30 and consequently increased SMC29. The appropriate enhancement of soil moisture contributed to increasing the number of microbes and their activities31, thus accelerating the accumulation of microbial residues in SOC32. The priming effect of SOC decreased with increasing soil moisture content33. High water content promoted the formation of soil aggregates increased in the marsh wetland during the restoration period3, and the stabilizing aggregates favored the accumulation of coarse particulate organic carbon. The elevated soil moisture content created an anaerobic condition21, limited water permeability, and reduced SOC mineralization31,34, a greater accumulation of SOC was mainly comprised of labile organic carbon for the marsh wetlands over the restoration process.

The increase in soil moisture was negatively affected by pH (Fig. 2a-b). Compared with the drained sites, soil pH, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\text{F}\text{e}}_{\text{o}}$$\end{document}/\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\text{F}\text{e}}_{\text{d}}$$\end{document} and SOC were relatively higher in the waterlogged sites35. The decrease in soil pH observed in the marsh during the restoration period suggested an increase in metal cations and Al/humic acid complexes36. Soil pH was negatively correlated with MAOC (R2 = 0.78) at the 0.05 significance level, which meant that the reduction of base cations constrained the formation of organic-metal complexes37. When the content of organic carbon is high, this element is decomposed by microorganisms. This results in an increasing production of organic acids and carbon dioxide, with the latter dissolving in water and forming carbonic acid36. Studies have shown that a high soil pH can destabilize soil macro-aggregates, which reduces the protection of SOC versus biodegradation38. The relatively higher soil pH values may stimulate the activity of soil microorganisms39, while the relatively lower soil pH decreased the solubility of SOC and provided limited substrates for microbial growth, thus reducing the diversity of aerobic microorganisms and their decomposition rates8. In this study, soil pH tended to decrease with the restored ages (Fig. 2b) and pH was negatively correlated with SOC (Fig. 3d), the decrease in soil pH inhibited the mineralization of SOC (especially labile organic carbon) and limited SOC outputs8.

The organic functional groups of Fe-OC were mainly (1) aromatic structures detected in mangrove sediments49 and permafrost marsh soils25, (2) carboxyl, phenolic hydroxyl and aromatic compounds found in the rhizosphere soil of two wetland plants50. Materials and methods

Study site and soil sampling

The Dushan Duliu River wetland (25°47’58"~26°01’06” N, 107°33’58"~107°43’53” E), a typical representative of the middle mountainous marsh wetland in southwestern China, has a total area of approximately 3907 hm2. It is the source of the Duliu River, the second largest tributary of the Xijiang River system in the Pearl River Basin.The region has a monsoon humid climate, which is typical of the central subtropical plateau, with an average annual precipitation of 1570 mm and annual average temperature of 11.0 ~ 17.9 °C11,52. The water recharge consists mianly of surface runoff, atmospheric precipitation and groundwater, and the area is permanently filled with water11. The water holding capacity of the moss marshes in the area is four times that of wet meadows52. Due to population expansion, rapid economic development, the historical drainage and transformation of wetlands, as well as the over-excavation of S. palustre in the area, the ecological functionations of the wetland has been significantly reduced52.

In 2013, the Duliu River wetland provincial Nature Reserve was established in order to primarily protect the original ecological peat moss swamp, forest resources and ecological environment, which was indeed achieved. Rice paddies in Dushan River wetlands have been replaced by S. palustre with its economic effects in recent years53 and S. palustre can be harvested twice every 2 ~ 3 years without removing the topsoil52,53. Here, we collected topsoil samples (0 ~ 20 cm) at four time points during the restoration of rice paddies to Sphagnum palustre wetlands (at 0, 2, 10, and 20 years) in August 2023. One natural sphagnum wetland (NW) growing in hills without any artificial disturbance was also selected for comparison (Fig. 5). At each habitat, seven sample plots were taken. At each plot, a composite sample comprised four random sub-samples and all topsoils were collected using a Russian Corer after removing all plant debris.

Data analysis

One-way ANOVA were used to compare the differences in soil characteristics among the samples collected from the reconverted rice paddies at different time points during the restoration period. Both pearson correlation and principal component analysis were utilized to examine the relationships between SOC and related physicochemical variables.
