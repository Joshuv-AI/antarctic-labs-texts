Repeated-sprint exercise delays gastric emptying independently of hypoxia and heat exposure
Authors: Keiichi Yamaguchi, Daichi Sumi, Nanako Hayashi, Kazushige Goto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract[Purpose] The present study examined the effects of repeated-sprint exercise in normoxia, hypoxia, and combined heat and hypoxia on the gastric emptying rate.[Methods] Eleven physically active males completed four experimental trials, consisting of a resting control (REST) and three exercise trials; three sets of 5 × 6-s maximal sprints in normoxia (NOR; 23℃, FiO2: 20.9%), hypoxia (HYP; 23℃, FiO2: 14.5%), and combined heat and hypoxia (HH; 35℃, FiO2: 14.5%) in a randomized order. Post-exercise gastric emptying rate was assessed by the 13C-sodium acetate breath test.[Results] The 13C excretion was significantly lower in the exercise trials (NOR, HYP, and HH) than in the REST during 10-35 min after exercise (p < 0.05). Time to peak 13C excretion was significantly delayed in all exercise trials (NOR, HYP, and HH) compared with the REST (p < 0.05), with no difference among the exercise trials. The magnitude of the gastric emptying delay was correlated with power output and blood glucose during the exercise.[Conclusion] Repeated-sprint exercise significantly delayed gastric emptying, while additional hypoxic or heat stress did not exacerbate the delay, suggesting that hypoxic or heat stress can be applied during repeated-sprint training without negative impacts on gastric emptying.Keywords: Cycling, Heat stress, Altitude, Environment, Combined stressors, Repeated-sprint

INTRODUCTION

Gastrointestinal function, evaluated by indicators such as the gastric emptying (GE) rate, plays an essential role in nutrient digestion and absorption. Acute exercise affects the GE rate, and it depends on the exercise mode, intensity, and duration [1]. High-intensity exercise (> 70% of maximal oxygen uptake [ V˙O2max]) delays GE, while low-intensity exercise (< 40% V˙O2max) accelerates GE [1]. One potential mechanism for delayed GE is gastrointestinal ischemia caused by blood flow redistribution to working muscles [1]. Delayed GE could suppress appetite [1], and slow the appearance of blood glucose and amino acids [2], potentially impairing recovery from fatiguing exercise in athletes.

Team and racket sports athletes are often required to perform repeated bouts of short maximal sprints (≤ 30 s) interspersed with incomplete recoveries, known as repeated-sprint exercise [3]. Repeated-sprint training in hypoxia (RSH) has recently attracted attention [4], and the combined effects of RSH with heat stress have also been investigated [5]. The effectiveness of RSH on repeated-sprint ability is widely accepted [6]; however, its adverse effects (e.g., delayed GE) have not been fully elucidated. To our knowledge, no study has examined effects of typical repeated-sprint exercise on GE. However, augmented GE delay was observed during 60 min of high-intensity intermittent sprinting (18 × 30 s sprints at 100% V˙O2max interspersed by cycling at 60% or 70% V˙O2max) compared with the same duration of continuous cycling at 66% V˙O2max [7]. It appears that repeated-sprint exercise would markedly delay GE because of its supra-maximal intensity. Moreover, the situation might be exacerbated in hypoxia or heat. During exercise in hypoxia, skeletal muscle blood flow is increased by compensatory vasodilation mediated through enhanced nitric oxide (NO) production [8]. In addition, NO-induced vasodilation is exercise intensity-dependent [8] and mainly appears in the fast-twitch fibers [9]. Therefore, supra-maximal exercise in hypoxia (i.e., RSH) would markedly increase skeletal muscle blood flow, potentially promoting stomach and gastrointestinal ischemia. Furthermore, heat stress also elevates blood flow in both skeletal muscle and skin [10,11], which may further attenuate GE.

Indeed, a previous study demonstrated that 60 min of continuous running (70% of maximal running velocity) in hypoxia (inspired oxygen fraction [FiO2] 14.5%) delayed GE compared with the same exercise in normoxia [12]. Heat exposure (35℃) during 60 min of interval-type cycling (4 min repetitions at 80% V˙O2max and 2 min repetitions at 40% V˙O2max) also delayed GE compared with a thermoneutral environment [13]. However, the effects of adding hypoxic or heat stress during repeated-sprint exercise on GE rate remain unclear.

The present study examined the effects of repeated-sprint exercise in normoxia, hypoxia, and combined heat and hypoxia on the GE rate. We hypothesized that repeated-sprint exercise would delay GE, and this delay would be exacerbated in hypoxia and combined heat and hypoxia.

METHODS

Participants

Eleven physically active males participated (mean ± standard error [SE] age 19.4 ± 0.4 years, height 173.0 ± 2.3 cm, weight 72.8 ± 2.0 kg). None of the participants had undergone heat acclimation or hypoxic training for ≥ 6 months before the experiments. Intense exercise, caffeine, and alcohol intake were prohibited for at least 24 h before each trial. Informed consent was obtained after explaining the experimental procedure. Sample size was determined by a power analysis using G*Power (version 3.1.9.7). The analysis indicated that a sample size of 11 would be sufficient to detect a difference (α = 0.05, β = 0.20, expected effect size for repeated-measures ANOVA [η2] = 0.14). The present study was approved by the Ethics Committee for Human Experiments of Ritsumeikan University (BKC-IRB-2018-069) and conducted in accordance with the principles expressed in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Experimental Overview

The experimental protocol and performance outcomes were previously reported.5 Briefly, the participants completed repeated cycle sprints under three environmental conditions; control (NOR; 23℃, 50% relative humidity [rH], FiO2: 20.9%), normobaric hypoxia (HYP; 23℃, 50% rH, FiO2:14.5%), and combined heat and normobaric hypoxia (HH; 35℃, 50% rH, FiO2:14.5%), followed by an assessment of the GE rate. These trials were conducted with a single-blind, randomized, cross-over design. They also underwent a resting trial (REST; measuring only the GE rate without exercise in the same environment as the NOR) on another day (Figure 1). Since our primary focus was to investigate the effect of RSH and further determine the additive effect of combining heat stress with RSH, we did not include a heat-only trial. Each trial was separated by ≥ 1 week and conducted in an environmental chamber (FCC-5000S; Fuji Medical Science, Chiba, Japan).

Protocol overview.

Exercise Protocol

Participants arrived at 8:00 a.m. following an overnight fast. After resting for 10 min in a thermoneutral/normoxic environment, the participants entered the chamber (for each environment) and rested for 5 min. Then, the participants started warm-up exercises (5 min cycling [90 W, 90 rpm], 4 × 3-s submaximal sprints [50, 60, 70, and 80% of perceived maximal effort], and two 6-s maximal sprints) on an electromagnetically braked cycle ergometer (Power Max VⅢ; Konami, Tokyo, Japan). After a 5-min rest, they performed repeated cycle sprints (three sets of 5 × 6-s maximal sprints interspersed with 30-s active recoveries, and 5-min passive rests between sets). The pedaling load was set at 7.5% of the individual’s body weight. After completing the exercise, participants rested in a supine position for 90 min in a thermoneutral/normoxic environment for GE assessment.

Measurement of Gastric Emptying Rate

The GE rate was assessed using the 13C-sodium acetate breath test [14]. A baseline breath sample was collected using a 1.3 L sampling bag (Otsuka Pharmaceutical, Tokyo, Japan) before entering the chamber. Five min after completing the exercise, the subject drank 100 mg of 13C-labeled sodium acetate (13C: 99%; Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, Andover, MA, USA) in 100 mL of water. Breath samples were then collected every 5 min during the 90-min post-exercise rest period. The 13CO2/12CO2 ratio in breath samples was analyzed using an infrared spectrometer (POC one; Otsuka Pharmaceutical, Tokyo, Japan). The CO2 production per body surface area (BSA) was assumed to be 300 mmol/m2/h, and the BSA was estimated using the following equation, where B is body weight (kg) and H is body height (cm).

BSA = (B0.425 × H0.725) × 0.007184

The time to peak 13C excretion/h (Tmax) was calculated [14]; this is strongly correlated with the GE rate measured using scintigraphy [15]. The Tmax in each exercise trial (NOR, HYP, and HH) was also expressed as the change from the value in REST (ΔTmax), indicating the magnitude of exercise-induced GE delay.

Statistical Analysis

All data are expressed as the mean ± SE. Two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to assess the interaction (condition × time) and main effects of condition and time on the time-course changes in 13C excretion using SPSS statistics ver. 27.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). One-way repeated-measures ANOVA was used to assess the main effect of condition on Tmax. Then, the Tukey post hoc test was applied for multiple comparisons. The Pearson correlation coefficient was used to examine the relationship between ΔTmax and other reported performance (peak and mean power outputs), physiological (tissue saturation index, muscle blood flow, rectal and skin temperature, heart rate, arterial oxygen saturation, and blood glucose and lactate concentration), and perceptual (ratings of perceived difficulty breathing and lower limb discomfort) variables [5]. The maximal value over three exercise sets was used for the analyses of the peak power output, and the averaged values were used for all other variables. Statistical significance was set as p < 0.05.

RESULTS

No significant differences were observed among conditions for peak (NOR: 900 ± 24 W; HYP: 901 ± 24 W; HH: 909 ± 25 W) or mean power outputs (NOR: 692 ± 18 W; HYP: 679 ± 17 W; HH: 685 ± 20 W). Sprint-by-sprint data was already presented in Yamaguchi et al. (2021) [5].

Figure 2 shows the time-course changes in 13C excretion and Tmax in each trial. During 10-35 min after exercise, 13C excretion was significantly lower in the exercise trials (NOR, HYP, and HH) than in the REST (p < 0.05). However, no significant difference among trials was observed thereafter. Tmax was significantly delayed in the exercise trials (NOR, HYP, and HH) compared with the REST (all p < 0.05), with no difference among the exercise trials.

DISCUSSION

The main finding was that repeated-sprint exercise delayed GE regardless of FiO2 or ambient temperature. Notably, the magnitude of the GE delay was correlated with the power output and blood glucose concentration during the exercise.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the GE response to repeated-sprint exercise. As expected, GE was delayed following repeated-sprint exercise compared with baseline (REST), in line with the previous finding that higher exercise intensities led to greater delays in GE [1]. However, the absence of differences in GE rates among exercise trials (NOR, HYP, and HH) is inconsistent with previous studies reporting delayed GE following submaximal exercise in hypoxia, as compared to in normoxia [12], and in the heat, as compared to a thermoneutral environment [13]. Hypoxic and heat stresses during exercise both increase blood flow to working muscles [8,10], which can exacerbate gastrointestinal ischemia and consequently delay GE. However, in repeated-sprint exercise, supra-maximal intensity itself may strongly affect muscle blood flow and GE, potentially masking any additional impact of supplemented environmental stressors. In fact, our experiment demonstrated that muscle blood flow was similar among the three exercise trials at the end of exercise, as previously reported.5 Moreover, absolute Tmax in the present study (56.4-60.9 min) was substantially longer than values reported in previous studies showing delayed GE after submaximal exercise in hypoxia (45.5 min) [12] and in the heat (~33 min) [13] using the same GE assessment method. Therefore, the effects of environmental stressors on GE would become more apparent when lower intensity was used. These results suggest that hypoxic or heat exposure during repeated-sprint exercise does not further impair GE beyond the exercise itself.

ΔTmax was positively correlated with the peak and mean power outputs during the exercise, but not with heart rate or the post-exercise blood lactate concentration. This suggests that the absolute intensity (i.e., mechanical load) of repeated-sprint exercise plays an important role in subsequent GE. A plausible explanation is that a greater mechanical load causes a larger blood flow redistribution to the working muscles, which may promote gastrointestinal ischemia. Greater power output indicates higher muscle activity and directly impacts blood flow redistribution to skeletal muscle [16]. In contrast, heart rate and blood lactate reflect systemic metabolic stress resulting from power production, but they do not directly affect blood flow redistribution. Therefore, Tmax was determined primarily by mechanical load rather than by metabolic stress. However, is it speculated that the severity of environmental stressors and the exercise protocol (e.g., sprint duration, work-to-rest ratios) influence GE, since these factors critically affect power output during repeated-sprint exercise. ΔTmax was also positively correlated with the blood glucose concentration during the exercise in the present study. Elevated blood glucose levels have been shown to suppress ghrelin secretion, which is known as appetite hormone [17]. The GE following repeated-sprint exercise would be modulated by multiple factors, including autonomic nervous activity, hormone responses, blood glucose, and splanchnic blood flow [18-20]. Further research should clarify the mechanisms underlying delayed GE following repeated-sprint exercise.

Since repeated-sprint exercise markedly delays GE, coaches and athletes involved in team and racket sports are encouraged to develop nutritional and recovery strategies for this type of training. Additionally, hypoxic or heat stress did not exacerbate the exercise-induced delay in GE, suggesting that these environmental stressors can be applied during supra-maximal sprint training without negative impacts on GE.

In conclusion, repeated-sprint exercise significantly delayed GE, while additional hypoxic or heat stress did not exacerbate the GE delay.
