Novel insights into the imaging of axial spondyloarthritis
Authors: Xenofon Baraliakos, Brikena Lalazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) is a chronic immune-mediated disease primarily affecting the sacroiliac joints and spine. Imaging plays a central role in the diagnosis, classification, and disease monitoring of axSpA. In this manuscript, recent developments in imaging that enhance both early detection and the understanding of disease progression are presented and discussed. Key topics include the role of conventional radiographs and their accuracy in the identification of axSpA-related imaging findings, the role of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans or other advanced imaging techniques in identifying inflammatory and structural lesions, and the significance of fat lesions as a marker of postinflammatory changes with the potential to predict new bone formation. Emerging applications of quantitative imaging and artificial intelligence in image analysis are also highlighted. Recent advances in imaging have significantly transformed the diagnostic landscape of axSpA. These insights support the integration of imaging as a biomarker for personalised treatment strategies.

ADVANCES IN IMAGING TECHNIQUES

Early diagnosis and disease monitoring in axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) are challenging due to its variable clinical presentation and the limitations of conventional imaging modalities. Traditional radiography, although long considered the standard tool for diagnosis, has no sensitivity in detecting inflammatory changes. Subsequently, their use leads to delayed diagnosis and treatment initiation, which is particularly important in the disease’s early stages, before radiographic damage becomes apparent.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans have revolutionised axSpA management by enabling the detection of both active inflammation (such as bone marrow oedema [BME]) and chronic structural changes (including erosions and fat lesions). MRI's ability to identify these changes early, often before they are visible on radiographs or computed tomography (CT) scans, makes it indispensable for early diagnosis. However, MRI scan findings must be interpreted carefully, particularly in physically active individuals, where changes may occur in anatomical regions not typically associated with axSpA, such as the posterior ilium or upper sacrum. Fat lesions, detected on T1-weighted MRI scans, are of particular interest as they represent postinflammatory changes and have been associated with future structural progression and the formation of syndesmophytes [4]. The volumetric interpolated breath-hold examination MRI (VIBE-MRI) sequence scan, known for its superior sensitivity compared with the T1-weighted MRI scan, is especially effective in identifying erosive damage in the sacroiliac joint (SIJ), particularly in younger patients [5]. This is due to the VIBE-MRI scan’s ability to detect early structural changes in cartilage before they affect the underlying bone. In contrast, a CT scan is superior in visualising bone changes, making it the modality of choice for advanced erosive damage [3].

Low-dose CT (LDCT) scans are increasingly being used due to their high spatial resolution, which can detect subtle structural changes with reduced radiation exposure. Studies have shown that an LDCT scan can identify lesions not visible on radiographs or MRI scans, making it an essential tool for comprehensive assessment [6].

EMERGING TECHNIQUES IN IMAGING

Innovations such as synthetic MRI and dual-energy CT scans are further enhancing the diagnostic landscape. Synthetic MRI scans allow for the simultaneous acquisition of multiple contrast-weighted images, offering a potential alternative to CT scans for specific diagnostic purposes [7]. Dual-energy CT scan, on the other hand, has proven useful for detecting BME and cartilage damage, adding another layer of diagnostic precision in clinical practice Figure 2 [8]. CELLULAR INSIGHTS RELATED TO IMAGING

The understanding of axSpA’s immunopathology is key to informing treatment decisions, particularly at the cellular level. Histopathological studies have shown a distinct immune cell profile in patients with axSpA, with increased inflammatory cell infiltration compared with degenerative spinal diseases [9]. This knowledge was confirmed in novel cellular imaging techniques that compared the glycosaminoglycan (GAG) depletion and disease-related clinical and imaging features between patients with early axSpA and healthy volunteers [10]. The presence of interleukin 17 (IL-17)-producing T cells and tumour necrosis factor α (TNFα) messenger RNA (mRNA) in inflamed tissues underscores the immune-driven nature of the disease [11,12] (see Fig 3), supporting the therapeutic targeting of these pathways with biologics such as IL-17 inhibitors, TNF inhibitors, and janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors.Figure 3Increased level of IL-17-producing cells in the spine of patients with axSpA [11]. axSpA, axial spondyloarthritis; IL-17, interleukin 17.Figure 3 dummy alt text

Recent advancements in molecular imaging techniques, such as the fibroblast-activating protein inhibitor (FAPI) positron emission tomography (PET) scan, have allowed for the in vivo visualisation of fibroblast activity, which plays a critical role in tissue remodelling and inflammation. FAPI signal intensity correlates with clinical disease activity and MRI scan findings, making it a potential biomarker for diagnosis and monitoring Figure 4 [13]. Additionally, hybrid imaging techniques such as PET/MRI scan, which combine metabolic and structural assessments, are gaining traction [16]. Tracers such as fluorine-18 sodium fluoride ([¹⁸F]NaF) and fluorine-18 fluorodeoxyglucose ( [¹⁸F]FDG) allow for simultaneous evaluation of bone remodelling and inflammation, offering a powerful tool for early disease modification and treatment monitoring [17].

Some of these imaging techniques are not yet part of routine clinical care and are mainly being assessed in research and clinical trials.

ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE AND FUTURE PERSPECTIVES

Artificial intelligence (AI) in imaging analysis holds promise for pattern recognition and automated classification. Initial applications have shown the potential to enhance diagnostic accuracy and patient stratification. AI tools may assist in longitudinal monitoring and provide decision support for clinicians [18] (see Fig 6). CONCLUSION

Imaging plays a crucial role as a biomarker of axSpA, and the recent advancements highlight the expanding range of its diagnostic potential. From the MRI scan’s ability to detect early inflammatory and structural changes to the rise of synthetic MRI scans, dual-energy CT scans, and advanced molecular imaging techniques, these innovations mark a major leap forward in diagnostic precision. However, a key challenge persists: the gap between early detection and the reliability of imaging assessments. Although these advancements hold great promise, early diagnosis remains difficult, particularly before structural damage becomes apparent. To address this, future imaging research must continue to evolve beyond traditional methods, incorporating new approaches that further refine early detection and enhance diagnostic accuracy. As the role of imaging continues to grow, so too will its potential to inform personalised treatment strategies, ultimately leading to better patient outcomes.
