Eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4F: functional properties and physiological role
Authors: Ekaterina Shuvalova, Walaa Al Sheikh, Alexey Shuvalov, Ilya M Terenin, Elena Alkalaeva
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSuccessful translation requires multiple protein factors with diverse specific activities. Initiation of translation is regarded as the most tightly regulated step of this process, controlled by numerous factors. Among these is the eukaryotic initiation factor 4F (eIF4F), a protein complex composed of three subunits: the cap-binding protein eIF4E, the RNA helicase eIF4A, and the scaffolding protein eIF4G. While significant progress has been made in elucidating the structure and functions of eIF4F, several key questions remain unresolved concerning its mechanistic contributions to translational control. In this review, we discuss the roles of eIF4F in translation and its regulatory functions under diverse cellular conditions. Dysregulation of eIF4F is frequently implicated in a wide range of physiological abnormalities, confirming its critical role in the cell. We also review recent studies that position eIF4F as a global cellular regulator and expand our understanding of its role.

Introduction

Translation converts genetic information from messenger RNA (mRNA) into the corresponding amino acid sequence of a protein. This process can be broadly divided into four stages: initiation, elongation, termination, and recycling. Eukaryotic translation is predominantly regulated at the initiation stage through eukaryotic initiation factors (eIFs), establishing initiation as the rate-limiting step. This review focuses on eIF4F, a key mediator of cap-dependent translation initiation [1, 2].

The eIF4F heterotrimeric complex, first described in 1983, comprises three subunits: the cap-binding protein eIF4E, the DEAD-box RNA helicase eIF4A, and the scaffolding protein eIF4G [3, 4]. The complex recruits mRNA via eIF4E’s binding to the 5′ cap structure, while eIF4A unwinds secondary structures near the 5′ cap to facilitate cap recognition by eIF4E and enables the subsequent ribosomal scanning [5, 6]. eIF4G serves as a central adaptor, bridging eIF4F to the 43S pre-initiation complex (PIC) through its interaction with eIF3. By simultaneously binding eIF4A and eIF4E, eIF4G coordinates mRNA unwinding and recruitment, functioning as an essential platform for translation initiation [7–17]. Additionally, eIF4G interacts with poly(A) binding protein (PABP), enabling circularization of mRNA through 5′–3′ end convergence, a closed-loop configuration that significantly enhances translational efficiency compared to linear mRNA [18–20].

Several fundamental questions regarding eIF4F function remain unresolved. First, the mechanism of scanning long 5′ untranslated regions (UTRs) is not completely understood: whether the eIF4F complex remains bound to the 5′ cap during ribosomal movment along the entire 5’UTR or dissociates during scanning? Second, with approximately half of mammalian mRNAs containing upstream open reading frames (uORFs) [21–23], how does eIF4F regulate the switch between leaky scanning and uORF translation followed by reinitiation? Third, the mechanism of initiation within closed-loop structures is debated, particularly whether 5′UTR scanning with secondary structure unwinding occurs in this context (reviewed in [24]).

Recent studies have significantly advanced our understanding of eIF4F’s regulatory roles. Accordingly, this review first outlines eIF4F composition, then discusses its general functions in translation, and finally analyzes its interactions with proteins that facilitate closed-loop formation and efficient protein synthesis.

Current evidence strongly supports that eIF4F serves as a global regulator for cellular mechanisms associated with protein synthesis. eIF4F activity significantly influences cell viability and development. Upregulation of eIF4F components has been observed in various cancers, prompting extensive research into the distinct roles of individual initiation factors in cancer cell proliferation and survival [25–27]. Moreover, the eIF4F translational control pathways are essential for neuronal signaling, modulating long-term synaptic plasticity and memory formation, while dysregulation of eIF4F is linked to multiple neurological disorders (reviewed in [25]). Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms governed by eIF4F holds dual significance: it advances fundamental understanding of translation process and holds substantial potential for the development of novel therapeutic strategies targeting diverse pathologies. The second part of this review consequently focuses on the physiological roles of eIF4F and current insights into its regulation under stress and disease conditions.

Components of the eIF4F complex and their isoforms

Stoichiometry of the eIF4F complex

The eIF4F complex in mammals demonstrates dynamic stoichiometric variability. Recent quantitative proteomics data reveals the following cellular abundances: eIF4A1 at ∼6.1 × 106 copies/cell, eIF4G1 at ∼8.8 × 10⁵ copies/cell, and eIF4E at ∼6.5 × 10⁵ copies/cell in HEK 293T cell line [28]. This analysis confirms that eIF4A is significantly more abundant than the other core components, being ∼7-fold more abundant than eIF4G1 and ∼9-fold more abundant than eIF4E. Consequently, the assembly of the eIF4F complex is likely limited by the availability of eIF4E or eIF4G, rather than eIF4A, and this limiting nature can vary depending on the cellular context.

In yeast, the stoichiometry of eIF4F subunits diverges: eIF4A is most abundant (present in multiple copies per ribosome), followed by eIF4E, while eIF4G is less abundant (substoichiometric relative to ribosomes) [29]. Consistent with these observations, mass spectrometry data confirm organism- and cell line-specific variations in eIF4F stoichiometry (Table 1) [30, 31]. A common feature across systems is the excess of free eIF4A over eIF4F-incorporated pools, reflecting its independent functions and dynamic exchange [4, 32].

eIF4G structure and function

eIF4G and its homologs are present in all eukaryotes, as they are required for the translation of capped mRNAs. Several eIF4G isoforms have been identified across various taxonomic groups and characterized in mammalian, plant, insect, and yeast cells [33–41]. Phylogenetic analysis of the eIF4G central domain—which binds eIF4A and eIF3—suggests that the specialized functions of diverse eIF4G variants may involve a conserved mechanism of translational control [42]. For example, in yeast, two eIF4G isoforms sharing 53% identity have been identified [36]. In plants, specific isoforms, eIF(iso)4G1 and eIF(iso)4G2, have also been found. Although these isoforms are highly divergent, they perform similar functions [43].

Structure and domain organization of eIF4G

Human eIF4G, also referred to as eIF4G1, is a 175-kDa polypeptide that migrates as a 220-kDa protein in SDS-PAGE, hence its older name, p220 [44–46]. It contains several domains involved in mRNA attachment to the ribosome and translation regulation (Fig. 1A). In contrast to the human protein, yeast eIF4G is only 107 kDa and migrates at ∼150 kDa [47]. Although the crystal structure of the full-length protein has not yet been resolved, several studies have reported the structures of individual domains or separate regions, including the middle and C-terminal parts [9, 32, 48, 49].

The N-terminal region of the protein has been experimentally demonstrated to be predominantly disordered, functioning as a docking site for RNA and the protein partners [50]. This region plays a pivotal role in cap-dependent translation initiation and mRNA closed-loop structure formation, as it interacts with multiple binding partners (Fig. 1A). The N-terminus of eIF4G (residues 27–129) contains binding site for SLIP1 [51], which interacts with the 3′-terminal hairpin of histone mRNAs [51]. Downstream of SLIP1-binding site lies the PAM1 motif (residues 166–200), which binds the RRM2 domain of PABP [52]. Following the PAM1 motif and the long, likely unstructured linker, the eIF4E-binding domain (4E-BD) is located. This domain includes the conserved YxxxxLΦ motif (where x represents any amino acid and Φ is a hydrophobic residue) [53, 54]. Despite being intrinsically disordered, this region adopts a structured conformation upon binding eIF4E [53, 55]. Additionally, eIF4G interacts with eIF1 through its N-terminal region, as confirmed in yeast [56, 57]. The binding site on eIF1 has been mapped to residues K58 and K109 [58]. Although the exact eIF4G-binding surface for eIF1 remains unknown, studies suggest that eIF1 and eIF4E bind eIF4G in a mutually exclusive manner, possibly competing for the same site [58]. The 4E-BD is followed by an unstructured linker that is susceptible to cleavage by several viral proteases [15, 59–61]. Residues 682–720 of eIF4G also comprise its RNA-binding domain (eIF4G-RBD) [36, 62, 63].

The middle segment of eIF4G contains the canonical MIF4G domain (Fig. 1A). While different studies have reported varying boundaries for this domain [14, 63–67], structural data from PDB 8J7R define it as spanning residues 722–949. The MIF4G domain belongs to the HEAT repeat family [named after Huntingtin, elongation factor 3 (EF3), protein phosphatase 2A (PP2A), and the yeast PI3-like kinase TOR1]. This domain consists of ten α-helices organized into five antiparallel tandem repeats. Downstream of the MIF4G domain lies the eIF3-binding site, a ∼100 a.a. region [15, 16, 68]. The MIF4G domain is highly conserved between yeast and human eIF4G, and mutations in its conserved residues can impair or abolish eIF4A binding [69].

Following the MIF4G domain, the 155-residue unstructured linker separates it from downstream domains [70]. Structural studies reveal that the C-terminus of eIF4G consists of two additional HEAT domains—MA3 (residues 1235–1426) and W2 (residues 1438–1593). These domains are connected by a short 13-residue linker (Fig. 1A) [70] and exhibit atypical HEAT domain architecture, characterized by unique helical coil arrangements. Key functional regions of the C-terminus include: a second eIF4A-binding site (residues 884 to 1404) within the MA3 domain, present in human eIF4G but absent in yeast [49, 65, 69–73], and two conserved AA-box motifs (aromatic/acidic) in the W2 domain that mediate interaction with mitogen-activated protein kinase-interacting kinases 1 and 2 (MNK1/2) [64, 70, 74].

Function of eIF4G domains

eIF4G, the central scaffolding protein of the eIF4F complex, plays essential roles in translation initiation (both cap-dependent and cap-independent mechanisms) and closed-loop mRNA structure formation. Moreover, it participates in the regulation of nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD) and reinitiation after uORF translation. As a molecular scaffold, eIF4G coordinates the assembly of eIF4F components and interaction with other translation factors to ensure efficient initiation.

The functions of eIF4G depend on its domains and their interactions with other translation-related factors. As was mentioned above, the N-terminal domain of eIF4G is responsible for binding PABP, eIF4E and eIF1 [18, 52, 58, 75]. eIF4G–PABP interaction is allosterically regulated through binding of the mRNA poly(A) tail to PABP [76]. The affinity of eIF4E for the cap increases as a result of its interaction with the 4E-BD of eIF4G. PABP interaction with eIF4G and mRNA circularization additionally increases the affinity of eIF4E for the cap structure [77–84].

In humans, the binding of eIF4E and eIF1 to eIF4G is proposed to be mutually exclusive. It has been demonstrated that the relative requirement for eIF4E–eIF4G versus eIF1–eIF4G interaction depends on the 5′UTR length and the start codon context. A model has been proposed in which the transition of the 43S ribosome from cap-binding to scanning mode involves switching of eIF4G’s binding from eIF4E to eIF1 [58].

In higher eukaryotes, the interaction between the middle domain of eIF4G and eIF3 is crucial for cap-dependent translation initiation. It facilitates the initial recruitment of ribosomes to mRNA for subsequent scanning [15, 16, 64], wich is necessary even for mRNAs with very short 5′UTRs [13, 17]. This mechanism is not conserved in yeast, where eIF4G lacks the requisite binding domain and eIF3 lacks the specific subunits found in higher eukaryotes [56, 85].

Recent cryo-EM structures of the 48S translational initiation complex reveal that eIF4A, when associated with the MIF4G domain of eIF4G, interacts with subunits eIF3k, eIF3l, and eIF3e near the ES6S region of the 40S ribosomal subunit [9]. This region serves as the binding and unwinding site for mRNA secondary structures. This structural analysis corroborates previous biochemical data that identified eIF3 subunits interacting with eIF4G domains [12, 86, 87]. In mammals, eIF4G binds eIF3 through the linker region between its MIF4G and MA3 domains. The segment spanning residues 1011–1051 primarily interacts with eIF3c and eIF3b, while residues 1052–1104 crosslink to eIF3e [12, 86]. Both interaction regions are essential for efficient mRNA recruitment to the ribosome and translational activation.

Additionally, recent work by Villa and Fraser (2024) demonstrated that the eIF4G-RBD can directly bind the 40S ribosomal subunit independently of other initiation factors [88]. Using native gel electrophoresis and fluorescence polarization assays, they identified this long-suspected but previously uncharacterized direct interaction between eIF4G and the 40S subunit. These findings establish that eIF4G engages with the 40S subunit through dual mechanisms: directly via its eIF4G-RBD domain and indirectly through eIF3-mediated binding.

The interaction between eIF4G and eIF4A represents a crucial regulatory node in translation initiation. Mammalian eIF4G contains two eIF4A-binding sites within its MIF4G and MA3 domains [65, 69], which enhance the helicase activity of eIF4A through binding to its N-terminal region [72, 89]. This interaction significantly boosts the RNA-stimulated ATPase activity of eIF4A, primarily by stabilizing the binding of RNA to eIF4A. This stabilization facilitates ATP hydrolysis, overcoming eIF4A’s intrinsic limitation of rapid RNA dissociation that otherwise occurs before product formation [72, 90].

The W2 domain of eIF4G specifically interacts with MNK1/2 through binding of the kinases' N-terminal disordered regions, positioning these kinases to modify translation initiation components [74, 91, 92].

Functional specializations of eIF4G isoforms eIF4G2 and eIF4G3

eIF4G (eIF4G1 or isoform 1) serves as the canonical member of the eIF4G protein family. Mammalian cells express two additional functional isoforms – eIF4G2 and eIF4G3 – that also participate in translation [93, 94]. Orthologous eIF4G isoforms have been characterized across diverse eukaryotic species, including well-studied examples in yeast and plants [36, 37, 43].

eIF4G2 (also known as DAP5, Nat1, and p97), a 97 kDa paralog of eIF4G, retains conserved MIF4G (residues 78–309), MA3 (residues 545–658), and W2 (residues 824–907) domains but lacks both canonical eIF4E- and PABP-binding sites [95, 96] (Fig. 1B). Structural analyses reveal that the MIF4G domain forms a right-handed solenoid structure comprising five HEAT repeats [97–99]. Despite sharing only 39% amino acid sequence identity with the MIF4G domain of eIF4G, it exhibits 59% similarity and maintains significant structural conservation [99]. Notably, the MIF4G domain of eIF4G2 possesses a distinctive positively charged surface that has been implicated in RNA binding [99].

The MIF4G domain of eIF4G2 directly interacts with eIF4A and eIF3 [95, 100, 101], while its MA3 domain shows no affinity for eIF4A [69]. The structure of the eIF4A-MIF4G(eIF4G2) complex has been resolved [99], but the eIF4G2–eIF3 interface remains uncharacterized. Notably, the linker region between MIF4G and MA3 domains—critical for eIF3 binding in eIF4G— is poorly conserved in eIF4G2 [114]. The W2 domain of eIF4G2 binds the β subunit of eIF2 through a glutamate residue (E862) that is substituted by lysine in eIF4G, which prevents eIF4G binding to this protein [98, 102]. Mutational studies confirm this interaction is functionally important: the eIF4G2 mutant E862K, defective in eIF2 binding, impairs translation of particular mRNAs [103]. Despite the structural homology between the W2 domains of the two isoforms, previously reported interactions between eIF4G2 and MNK1 [91] were not replicated in a subsequent study, consistent with eIF4G2’s inability to scaffold eIF4E-MNK1 complexes due to its lack of an eIF4E-binding motif [104].

eIF4G2 was initially thought to be a competitive inhibitor of eIF4G [95]; however, this was later proven incorrect. The depletion of eIF4G2 generally leads to reduced cell viability and decreased protein synthesis levels, although it does not significantly impact cells under standard culturing conditions [96, 105–107]. It was thus suggested that eIF4G2’s involvement in translation may be more significant under apoptosis, cellular differentiation, or cell cycle arrest—particularly when the eIF4G is cleaved or otherwise depleted—thereby suppressing cap-dependent translation. Recent research has also revealed the potential functional roles of eIF4G2 in canonical cap-dependent initiation, which will be discussed below.

As previously mentioned, eIF4G2 contains only one binding site for eIF4A. Although the amino acid residues involved in eIF4A binding are highly conserved between eIF4G and eIF4G2, the interaction with the MIF4G domain of eIF4G2 exhibits a significantly higher dissociation constant than the equivalent interaction with eIF4G [99]. eIF4G possesses a second eIF4A binding site within its MA3 domain; however, despite the high structural similarity between their MA3 domains, the MA3 domain of eIF4G2 is unable to bind eIF4A [69, 97]. The function of this domain remains unknown. Collectively these differences explain why eIF4G2 stimulates the helicase activity of eIF4A with only 50% of the efficiency of eIF4G [99].

Unlike eIF4G2, eIF4G3 (also referred to as eIF4GII), was long thought to be merely a functional copy of eIF4G [38]. Human eIF4G3 has a molecular weight of ∼176 kDa, though its observed electrophoretic mobility ranges from 195 to 250 kDa. It shares 46% amino acid identity with eIF4G and possesses all the domains found in eIF4G [38]. Homology is particularly high in central and C-terminal regions (up to 83%), while the N-terminal regions are more divergent [108]. The structural analysis of the MIF4G domain (residues 745–1003) of eIF4G3 has been performed (Fig. 1C) [67] revealing a crescent shape formed by five antiparallel α helical pairs.

As a functional replacement for eIF4G, eIF4G3 directly interacts with eIF4E, eIF4A, eIF3, and PABP [38, 52]. It is hypothesized that eIF4G3 binds to eIF4E2 (which cannot interact with eIF4G) under hypoxic conditions, indicating the existence of diverse alternative translation initiation complexes [109].

eIF4A structure and function

Human eIF4A is a 46 kDa DEAD-box ATP-dependent RNA helicase. Like all DEAD-box proteins, it contains a conserved helicase core composed of two RecA-like domains (Fig. 2). These domains contain conserved motifs responsible for ATP binding and hydrolysis, RNA binding, and RNA unwinding [110]. The helicase core is flanked at the N-terminus by ∼50 amino acids. The RecA-like domains are connected via flexible central hinge regions that allow them to alternate between open, half-open, and closed conformations [73, 90]. The RecA-like domains of eIF4A function as a nucleotide-dependent molecular switch, modulating their affinity for RNA. In the absence of nucleotides, the DEAD-box helicase core adopts an open conformation characterized by the separation of the two RecA-like domains and the absence of interdomain contacts. The binding of RNA and ATP triggers a conformational transition from the flexible open state to a more rigid closed state. In this closed conformation, an intricate network of interactions forms among the conserved motifs, the bound RNA, and ATP [111–113].

The activity of DEAD-box helicases like eIF4A is governed by open/closed conformational transitions. eIF4G significantly enhances eIF4A’s helicase function, and structural studies have proposed models to explain this regulation. These models are underpinned by eIF4A’s conserved structure, which includes nine characteristic motifs (Q, I, Ia, Ib, II, III, IV, V, and VI) [114]. Nucleotides bind within the interdomain cleft, with adenine recognized by a conserved glutamine within the Q-motif [115]. While motifs I, II, and III are crucial for RNA-dependent ATPase activity, other motif VI (HRIGRGGR) mediates both RNA binding and ATP hydrolysis [116, 117]. The initial “soft clamp” model proposed that eIF4G stabilizes eIF4A in its active, closed conformation by simultaneously interacting with both RecA domains [118]. Subsequent research refined this understanding, demonstrating that eIF4G binding first induces a ‘half-open’ conformation in eIF4A. Through this engagement, eIF4G facilitates the dynamic transition of eIF4A between ‘half-open’ and ‘closed’ states, ultimately enhancing helicase activity by accelerating the rate of phosphate release [73].

There are two major isoforms of eIF4A in mammals: eIF4A1 (eIF4AI) and eIF4A2 (eIF4AII), both participating in translation [119]. While human eIF4A1 and eIF4A2 share 90% amino acid identity and can substitute for one another within the eIF4F complex [120], their functional equivalence remains debated. Although they freely exchange in vitro, subtle functional differences may exist despite their sequence similarity [121].

The two proteins are differentially expressed depending on the cellular state. For instance, during growth arrest in murine cell lines, the mRNA level of eIF4A2 increases threefold, while the mRNA level of eIF4A1 decreases by 10%–20% [122]. The eIF4A2 isoform may play a critical role in protein synthesis in both nondividing and dormant cells. As mentioned above, in mammalian cells, eIF4G contains two potential eIF4A binding sites [15, 69]. However, yeast eIF4G has only a single eIF4A binding site [71, 123]. Notably, only one molecule of either eIF4A1 or eIF4A2 is able to bind to eIF4G in human embryonic kidney cells [124]. This observation suggests that eIF4A does not simultaneously occupy both binding sites on eIF4G or that the C-terminal domain of eIF4G, which is responsible for eIF4A binding, plays a modulatory role. Consistent with this, structural analyses of eIF4A complexes with MIF4G or MA3 domains indicate that binding is mutually exclusive.

eIF4G pull-down assays show eIF4A1 is more abundant than eIF4A2 in the eIF4F complex [68, 124]. Notably, studies demonstrate that eIF4A2 prevents CNOT7 (a component of the CCR4–NOT complex) from deadenylating mRNAs. eIF4A2-bound mRNAs exhibit longer poly(A) tails than DDX6-bound mRNAs, indicating eIF4A2 protects poly(A) tails from CCR4–NOT [125]. Furthermore, mRNAs bound by eIF4A2 (or both isoforms) are enriched in microRNA (miRNA) sites compared to mRNAs associated with eIF4A1 alone. These data support a specific role for eIF4A2 in miRNA-mediated translational repression, while eIF4A1 is excluded from this regulatory pathway [126].

The third isoform of eIF4A, eIF4A3 (eIF4AIII), is an exon junction complex component that functions in NMD rather than in translation [127]. Despite 65%–70% sequence identity with eIF4A1 and eIF4A2, eIF4A3 shows minimal eIF4G interaction and cannot substitute the helicase activity of eIF4A1 [128].

While all three eIF4A isoforms exist in vertebrates, Caenorhabditis elegans (nematodes) and Drosophila (insects) possess only eIF4A1 and eIF4A3 homologs and lack eIF4A2. Arabidopsis thaliana (plants) has eIF4A1 and eIF4A2 homologs but lacks eIF4A3. Saccharomyces cerevisiae possesses two eIF4A paralogs (Tif1/Tif2), along with the unrelated helicase Ded1p, which functions within the eIF4F complex [129]. Consequently, all eukaryotes have at least one eIF4A ortholog for canonical translation.

As previously mentioned, the primary function of eIF4A involves unwinding secondary structures in 5′UTRs, with its helicase activity being significantly enhanced through interactions with eIF4G and cofactors such as eIF4B and eIF4H. eIF4A exhibits inherently weak helicase activity by itself. However, the combined action of eIF4A, eIF4B, and eIF4G results in a 100-fold stimulation compared to the basal activity of eIF4A [130–133]. Among these regulators, human eIF4B, a ∼69 kDa protein capable of forming dimers/oligomers, plays a particularly important role. Although lacking an autonomous catalytic activity, eIF4B substantially enhances RNA binding, ATPase and helicase functions of eIF4A [3, 131, 134, 135]. Structurally eIF4B contains an RNA recognition motif, an RNA binding domain, and a DRYG domain, which have been linked to mRNA binding, self-association, interaction with eIF3, and the stimulation of eIF4A helicase activity [136–138] (Fig. 3A). Mechanistically, several models have been proposed to explain eIF4B’s function. The initial “Brownian Ratchet” hypothesis suggests that eIF4B ensures unidirectional mRNA scanning by preventing backward ribosomal movement [139]. Supporting evidence emerged with the discovery that eIF4B interacts with both eIF3a and the 18S rRNA [138], suggesting an alternative model where eIF4B establishes additional connections between the mRNA and the 40S ribosomal subunit to facilitate scanning. Recent structural insights from cryo-EM analysis of the 48S complex have clarified eIF4B’s specific role, demonstrating that it interacts with and regulates the second eIF4A molecule at the ribosomal mRNA entry site rather than the eIF4F-bound eIF4A, thereby directly participating in 5′UTR scanning [32].

In addition to eIF4B, eIF4H also modulates eIF4A activity. As a small protein with a molecular weight of ∼28 kDa, eIF4H enhances the RNA helicase activity of eIF4A [133, 140]. The eIF4H protein features an RRM homologous to that of eIF4B (Fig. 3B). It is proposed that eIF4H operates through protein–protein interactions, potentially stabilizing conformational changes in eIF4A during RNA binding, ATP hydrolysis, and RNA duplex unwinding [66, 131, 140–143]. While eIF4H stimulates eIF4A’s RNA helicase activity weaker than eIF4B, it forms stable complexes with eIF4A [14], suggesting context-dependent regulation through differential binding to eIF4B or eIF4H [130, 144]. Both proteins may stabilize single-stranded RNA during scanning by preventing reannealing [131].

eIF4E structure and function

Human eIF4E, a 25-kDa protein, is present in cells both in free form and as a component of the eIF4F complex [145]. eIF4E is found in both the cytoplasm and nucleus [146]. In the cytoplasm, eIF4E, as part of the eIF4F complex, recognizes and binds mRNA 5′ cap thereby facilitating ribosome attachment. Structural studies reveal that eIF4E adopts a palm-like shape, featuring distinct concave and convex regions that correspond to the cap and eIF4G binding sites, respectively [147, 148] (Fig. 4). In the nucleus, eIF4E forms nuclear bodies and participates in the nucleocytoplasmic export of a subset of mRNAs, many of which encode growth-promoting proteins such as cyclin D1, murine double minute 2 (MDM2), and Nijmegen breakage syndrome 1 (NBS1), and others involved in RNA capping, processing, and splicing [146, 149–156].

In mammals, the eIF4E family comprises three members: eIF4E1, eIF4E2, and eIF4E3, each exhibiting distinct functional characteristics. In other organisms, the eIF4E repertoire can be significantly broader. For instance, Drosophila melanogaster has seven eIF4E genes [157], while S. cerevisiae encodes only one [158]. In plant genomes, at least three eIF4E isoforms have been found: the canonical eIF4E1, eIF4E2, and flowering plant-specific eIF(iso)4E [159]. Although all eIF4Es can bind the mRNA cap, their protein–protein interaction patterns differ markedly. Specifically, eIF4E1 interacts with both eIF4G and eIF4E-binding proteins (4E-BPs, translation repressors; see below). In contrast, eIF4E2 binds 4E-BPs but not eIF4G. And vice versa, eIF4E3 binds eIF4G but not 4E-BP. Each member of the eIF4E family regulates the recruitment of mRNAs to the ribosome under distinct cellular conditions [158].

Due to its inability to interact with eIF4G, eIF4E2 (also known as 4EHP or 4E-LP) is implicated in unconventional mechanisms of translation initiation or, more commonly, thought to repress translation (for review, see [160]). For example, under hypoxic conditions, the eIF4E2-RBM4-HIF2α complex has been proposed to form at the 3′UTRs of specific mRNAs, driving their translation through an unexplored mechanism [161], probably through the binding of eIF4E2 to eIF4G3 [109]. More extensively characterized is the participation of the eIF4E2-GYGIF2 complex in translation repression through multiple pathways, including NMD [162, 163], miRNA-mediated silencing [164, 165], and ribosome-associated quality control [166, 167]. Notably, SARS-CoV2 promotes this eIF4E2 activity to inhibit translation of certain host mRNAs during infection [168].

eIF4E3 is an atypical cap-binding protein. Its cap-binding pocket is composed of tyrosine and cysteine instead of the canonical aromatic residues, resulting in a 10–40-fold lower cap (m7GTP) affinity than eIF4E1 [169]. The expression of eIF4E3 mRNA is generally low. However, its inability to bind 4E-BPs renders the protein insensitive to mTOR-mediated regulation. Therefore, eIF4E3 is believed to be predominantly active during stress conditions, where it can effectively bind the pool of eIF4G/eIF4G3 released from eIF4E1 by 4E-BPs [94]. eIF4E3 is not merely a backup cap-binding protein, as it seems to have a distinct pool of the preferred mRNA-targets compared to eIF4E1 [94, 170].

The distribution of eIF4E extends far beyond ribosome-containing compartments; it localizes to the nucleus, processing bodies (P-bodies), RNP granules, and stress granules (SGs), where eIF4E does not collocate with the ribosomes [171]. In yeast, P-bodies can accumulate eIF4E and eIF4G at low concentrations under glucose deprivation conditions and at elevated levels in stationary phase [172]. These mRNP complexes include polyadenylated mRNAs, Pab1p (the yeast homolog of PABP), eIF4E, and eIF4G2. Under arsenite and heat stress conditions, eIF4E2 localizes to P-bodies and is redirected to SGs exclusively during heat shock. In contrast, eIF4E3 localizes to SGs but not to P-bodies under both heat shock and arsenite stress conditions [173]. These findings indicate that eIF4E functions in both ribosome-dependent and ribosome-independent manners in the cytosol, with both activities relying on its cap-binding functionality.

The role of eIF4F in translation initiation

The role of eIF4F in cap-dependent translation initiation

During cap-dependent translation initiation, two parallel processes converge to form the 48S initiation complex. First, the 5′ mRNA cap is recognized by eIF4F through its eIF4E subunit [75, 174, 175]. Simultaneously, the small ribosomal 40S subunit assembles with initiation factors eIF1, eIF1A, eIF3, eIF5, and the eIF2·GTP·Met-tRNAiMet ternary complex to form the 43S PIC (Fig. 5A) [2, 68, 176–178]. The 43S PIC is then recruited to the mRNA through eIF3’s interaction with eIF4G, after the unwinding of secondary structures near the 5′ end by eIF4A with assistance from eIF4B. The resulting 48S complex subsequently scans the 5′UTR to locate the start codon [2, 68, 178]. In higher eukaryotes, scanning of highly structured mRNAs is further facilitated by DHX29, an additional helicase bound directly to the 40S subunit [179, 180]. Following start codon recognition, eIFs dissociate and eIF5B-GTP facilitates the joining of the 60S ribosomal subunit to form the 80S ribosome, initiating the elongation phase of translation [181–183].

While the scanning of 5’UTRs is facilitated by eIF4F, certain aspects of the mechanism remain elusive. A key question is whether eIF4F remains bound to the cap while the ribosome moves along the 5′UTR. This is important because the start codon can be located hundreds of nucleotides downstream. If eIF4F remains tethered to both the cap and the ribosome, only one ribosome can scan the mRNA at a time. This could slow down the process of translation initiation, particularly if the 5′UTR is very long.

Two models have been proposed to explain how eIF4F functions during scanning: (i) the tethered model proposed that eIF4F remains bound to the cap while the ribosome moves along the 5′UTR, (ii) the untethered model suggests eIF4F dissociates from the cap after the ribosome initiates scanning. Recent structural and biochemical studies have provided new insights into this process (reviewed in [178]). Cryo-EM structures of the 48S preinitiation complexes have revealed that eIF4F components are present on scanning ribosomes [9, 184]. The structural analysis conducted by Querido et al. demonstrated the highly probable involvement of eIF4E, supporting the tethered model [9]. Other experiments, such as selective 40S footprinting, show that most ribosomes remain cap-bound, with eIF3B, eIF4G, and eIF4E persisting until it has passed ∼12 codons during elongation [185].

However, some data also support the untethered model. For example, eIF4G can switch from the eIF4G–eIF4E complex to the eIF4G–eIF1 complex [58]. Another study using a reconstituted translation system demonstrated that eIF4E dissociates from the 5′ cap during the initial attachment of mRNA to the 43S PIC. This release prevents steric hindrance as the mRNA enters the ribosome’s binding channel and allows the 5′ end to participate in subsequent rounds of initiation [28]. In addition, eIF4A can freely exchange within the eIF4F complex [4, 121, 186, 187]. Experiments utilizing nonhydrolysable ATP analogues have revealed that both the intact eIF4F complex and “free” eIF4A/4B play crucial roles in facilitating efficient scanning and regulating the small ribosomal subunit to achieve an “open” conformation of the mRNA entry channel. The authors further suggested that eIF4A within the eIF4F-ribosome complex may function not only as an RNA helicase but also as a regulatory protein that modulates alternating high and low affinity states of scanning ribosomal complexes for mRNA, thereby ensuring the unidirectional movement of the ribosome in the 5′–3′ direction [188].

The role of eIF4A at the 40S subunit’s mRNA entry channel was initially indicated by yeast biochemical studies, which demonstrated its ability to enhance mRNA recruitment irrespective of 5′UTR structural complexity. This localization was later confirmed in mammalian systems by proximity labeling, which positioned eIF4A and its associated factors near the mRNA entry site of the 43S PIC [189–191]. A recent study in the human 48S complex provided definitive visual evidence, revealing a second eIF4A molecule specifically at the mRNA entry site, distinct from the eIF4F-bound eIF4A at the exit site [32, 191]. This entry-site eIF4A is strategically positioned to unwind secondary structures as mRNA enters the channel, directly explaining how structural barriers are resolved during scanning [32]. These findings support a model of cooperative action between the two eIF4A helicases to ensure unidirectional scanning. The structural data further suggest that eIF4E and the 5′ cap are released from the complex during this process confirming previous studies.

Collectively, these findings support a model in which eIF4F remains labilely associated with the 5′ cap during scanning. This dynamic, rather than stable, association enables the complex to perform its essential functions—including ribosome capture, coordination of protein–protein and protein–ribosome interactions, and facilitation of scanning—while allowing for the flexible exchange of components. Therefore, it is unlikely that eIF4F dissociates from the cap as an intact complex. The precise mechanism coordinating scanning with cap binding, however, remains unresolved. Further studies employing techniques such as time-resolved cryo-EM or single-molecule fluorescence will be useful to elucidate these molecular details.

During 5′UTR scanning, the 48S complex frequently encounters uORFs. Present in approximately half of mammalian mRNAs, uORFs function as key decision points for the scanning ribosome [21, 192, 193]. The finding that eIF4F components, including eIF4E and eIF4G, can remain associated with the ribosome during scanning [11, 185, 194] provides a crucial framework for understanding these decision mechanisms. This persistent association suggests that eIF4F is not merely a recruitment factor but also a potential regulator of the scanning ribosome’s behavior at uORFs. When a ribosome encounters a uORF start codon, it can either bypass it via “leaky scanning” or initiate translation. Following uORF translation and termination, the ribosome may resume scanning and reinitiate at a downstream start codon, a process facilitated by factors like eIF3 [195, 196].

The isoform eIF4G2 plays a distinct role in this context. While the canonical eIF4F complex is involved in initial ribosome attachment and scanning, eIF4G2 appears to be critical for supporting scanning and reinitiation after uORF translation [197–200]. While eIF4G2 can substitute for eIF4G in scanning, it cannot facilitate initial ribosome attachment [201], suggesting unique and nonredundant functions of different eIF4G isoforms in uORF regulation.

Despite these advances, the precise mechanistic contribution of eIF4F to uORF translation remains incompletely resolved. Fundamental questions persist regarding how eIF4F components coordinate with other initiation factors to regulate the decision points between leaky scanning, uORF translation, and reinitiation at downstream start codons.

The role of eIF4F in cap-independent translation initiation

In eukaryotes, cap-dependent translation initiation is the predominant mechanism for protein synthesis, utilizing the majority of the cellular translation machinery (Fig. 5A) [68, 202, 203]. However, during infection, cellular stress, or disease, cap-independent translation may become more prevalent (Fig. 5B). Despite extensive research on this topic, the mechanisms underlying cap-independent translation of cellular mRNAs remain poorly understood, though they are thought to be highly diverse. Cap-independent translation can occur via internal ribosome entry sites (IRESs) [204, 205], cap-independent translation enhancers (CITEs) [206, 207], m6A methylation in 3′ and 5′UTRs [208, 209], and possibly other yet-to-be-identified mechanisms [210, 211]. The eIF4F complex frequently plays a role in cap-independent initiation, either through direct or indirect binding to various IRESs and CITEs.

IRESs are specific nucleotide sequences within mRNAs that serve as recruitment sites for ribosomes, enabling translation initiation at internal positions without requiring a cap or a vacant 5′ terminus [212, 213]. IRESs are commonly classified into four types based on structural similarities and the mechanisms they use for ribosome attachment and factor recruitment, though more refined classifications have been proposed [204, 205, 214]. Type I and II IRESs require nearly the entire set of translation factors, except for eIF4E, whose function in recruiting eIF4G is instead performed by structural elements of the IRES [215]. Additionally, type I and II IRESs require specific proteins known as IRES trans-activating factors [216–218]. Viruses that possess such IRESs mediate proteolytic cleavage of eIF4G, thereby removing the eIF4E binding site, or exploit host pathways of eIF4E repression [219], thereby inhibiting cap-dependent translation [214]. Viral type III IRES requires a minimal set of initiation factors (eIF2, eIF3, eIF5, and eIF5B) [220, 221], and type IV IRES does not require initiation factors at all. More importantly, however, IRESs not only bind ribosomes and/or initiation factors but also facilitate the accommodation of mRNA into the ribosome’s mRNA-binding channel independently of the mRNA’s 5’ terminus.

CITEs also bind components of the host translation machinery but require a free 5′ end of the mRNA to mediate translation initiation. CITEs promote the assembly of the PIC, which is followed by conventional scanning to locate the start codon. Thus, viral CITEs function as cap analogues and recruit eIF4F to drive viral RNA translation when cap-dependent translation is suppressed [204, 206]. Often, eIF4G or eIF4G2 is required for the functioning of cellular cap-independent translation elements [222, 223].

Translation initiation can be stimulated by specific methylation of adenines, either in the 3′UTR of mRNA near stop codons or in the 5′UTR. m6A in mRNA 5′ regions binds eIF3 directly [224], while m6A in 3′ regions is recognized by YTHDF1, which interacts with eIF3 to promote cap-independent translation [225]. It is assumed that m6A-facilitated translation is resistant to eIF4E inactivation. The m6A-stimulated translation normally coexists with cap-dependent translation, and both mechanisms can operate simultaneously [226].

Interestingly, the poly(A) tail may enhance translation of uncapped mRNAs containing various IRESs [227]. For mRNAs containing type I and II picornavirus IRESs, the stimulation of translation by the poly(A) tail is dependent on intact eIF4G and PABP [228, 229].

Additional activities of eIF4F in translation

A recent study showed that the translation rate increases after the first round of translation on capped and polyadenylated mRNA [230]. This phenomenon is speculated to occur because the closed-loop structure is established in a translation-dependent manner. Several hypotheses have been proposed regarding the function of the closed-loop mRNA structure (see the review [24]).

One hypothesis suggests that at least some translated mRNA molecules undergo circularization, bringing their 5′ and 3′ ends into close proximity. This circularization is thought to be mediated by protein–protein interactions between two key translation factors: eIF4G, which binds to eIF4E at the cap structure at the 5′ end of mRNA, and PABP, which associates with the poly(A) tail at the 3′ end [231, 232].

Another hypothesis suggests that the close proximity of the 5′ and 3′ ends results from the intrinsic ability of mRNAs to form structures and promote an end-to-end configuration. The evolutionarily conserved eIF4G–PABP interaction primarily reinforces or detects this proximity rather than serving as the driving force behind mRNA circularization [233]. It should also be mentioned that recent single-molecule experiments suggest a revision and refinement of this model. Using single-molecule fluorescence in situ hybridization to measure the 5′–3′ distances of single mRNAs in untreated and translationally inhibited cells, researchers found that the ends of translating mRNAs were farther apart than those of nontranslating mRNAs [234]. Furthermore, studies in fixed cells and in living cells have shown that the mRNA becomes more extended as more ribosomes are loaded, indicating that the translational machinery is responsible for the extension [235, 236]. These data suggest that during translation, the mRNA either remains linear or adopts a closed-loop conformation only transiently, indicating a dynamic structural state [233, 237, 238].

Nevertheless, multiple studies confirm the physiological relevance of the closed-loop model (see review [24]). Atomic force microscopy analysis has shown that, in the presence of eukaryotic eIF4E, PABP, and eIF4G, mRNA is stabilized in a circularized conformation [18]. The absence of these proteins prevents the formation of this structural configuration. It has also been found that eIF4F, which is attached to the 5′ cap, frequently crosslinks with the 3′ terminal sequences of mRNA, while PABP crosslinks with the 5′ end [19]. These observations suggest that eIF4F is positioned in close proximity to the 3′ end of the mRNA, whereas PABP is similarly located near the 5′ end. It is possible that circularized mRNA becomes stabilized after the first round of translation. The formation of a closed-loop structure may subsequently facilitate post-termination ribosome recycling and the reinitiation of translation on the same mRNA (Figure 5C).

The interaction between PABP and eIF4F synergistically promotes translation in vitro [19, 83, 239–241]. In the absence of poly(A), eIF4G and PABP interact only weakly, suggesting that PABP binding to the poly(A) tail is essential for translation activation [76]. Multiple connections between eIF4E, PABP, eIF4G, eIF3, and poly(A)-binding protein-interacting protein 1 (PAIP1) can stabilize the mRNA in its circularized state. Among these interactions, the association of PABP and eIF4F is particularly critical, as it facilitates the co-stimulation of translation. However, the exact molecular mechanisms governing eIF4F activation during the assembly of the closed-loop mRNA structure remain poorly understood.

Due to the formation of a closed-loop structure during protein synthesis, the spatial proximity of the 5′ and 3′ ends of mRNA is maintained, ensuring coordination between the initiation process at the start codon and the termination process at the stop codon. A recent study has provided initial evidence indicating that eIF4F is directly involved in stimulating release factors [242]. Using a reconstituted mammalian translation system, this work showed that eIF4F, comprising eIF4G, eIF4E, and eIF4A, enhances translation termination by facilitating the loading of eRF1 into the ribosomal A site and stimulating the GTPase activity of eRF3. It is important to note that the role of eIF4F in termination is a newly discovered function, and its broader mechanistic details and physiological relevance across different cell types and conditions warrant further investigation.

Additionally, recent research has revealed a direct role for eIF4G in modulating NMD, challenging the simplistic view that NMD is incompatible with eIF4F-dependent translation. Evidence now indicates that human mRNAs undergoing eIF4F-dependent translation can still be subjected to NMD. One study demonstrated that under conditions of prolonged translation inhibition, during which the exchange of cap-binding complex (CBC) for eIF4F occurred, NMD-sensitive mRNAs retained their competence for this process [243]. Furthermore, inhibitors of eIF4E-dependent translation stabilized mRNAs susceptible to NMD in cap-dependent translation, leading to the conclusion that eIF4F-dependent translation can maintain NMD competence in human cells, allowing for potential activation of NMD on previously translated mRNAs [243].

The mechanistic link between eIF4G and NMD suppression has been elucidated using tethering assays. Two groups used an MS2 tethering approach to show direct involvement in this process of not only PABP, but also its partner, eIF4G. One study showed that PABP, associated with the inserted MS2 binding sites between the stop codon and the long 3′UTR, specifically inhibits NMD-mediated mRNA degradation [244]. Contrary to the current model, PABP mutants unable to interact with eRF3 still effectively suppress NMD. These authors also found that the interaction of PABP with eIF4G, which mediates mRNA circularization, is required for the inhibition of NMD by PABP and that the recruitment of eRF3 or eIF4G near the premature stop codon (PTC) counteracts NMD [244].

Another group, in a study by Joncourt et al., showed that eIF4G can suppress NMD through two mechanisms [245]. The first mechanism involves the interaction between the N-terminus of eIF4G and PABP, while the second mechanism is mediated by the association of the core domain of eIF4G with eIF3. Both mechanisms independently enable tethered eIF4G to inhibit NMD [245].

In summary, eIF4F emerges as a crucial regulator that bridges translation initiation with mRNA quality control. Through its role in closed-loop formation via eIF4G–PABP interaction and its direct involvement in modulating the termination/NMD decision, eIF4F plays a central role in coordinating translation efficiency with mRNA stability.

Regulation of eIF4F activity

The regulation of eIF4F is multifaceted and involves numerous proteins and post-translational modifications (Fig. 6). In the previous chapters, we outlined translation factors that either stimulate or modulate the function of eIF4F in translation. Here, we describe additional regulators that influence the essential role of eIF4F, through either protein–protein interactions or post-translational modifications. These key regulators include eIF4E-binding protein 1 (4E-BP1), tumor suppressor programmed cell death protein 4 (PDCD4), PAIP1, and several kinases, phosphatases, and proteases. The variety of interactions underscores the intricate nature of eIF4F regulation, indicating that its activity is tightly controlled through a network of protein–protein interactions and post-translational modifications. This regulatory complexity potentially enables fine-tuned control of global protein synthesis rates.

The key regulators of eIF4F complex.

Proteins that disrupt formation and function of eIF4F

PDCD4

PDCD4 is involved in many cellular processes, including transcription, translation, apoptosis, and modulation of various signal transduction pathways [246]. It consists of two MA3 domains, each capable of binding one eIF4A molecule [247, 248]. Under physiological conditions, however, the PDCD4–eIF4A complex has a 1:1 stoichiometry [249]. Another group studying the crystal structure of proteins observed a protein ratio of 1:2 [247]. Through its MA3 domains, which mimic those of eIF4G, PDCD4 inhibits the binding of eIF4A to the C-terminal region of eIF4G but does not interfere with its binding to the MIF4G domain [250]. By sequestering eIF4A, PDCD4 inhibits translation of mRNAs with structured 5′UTRs, that require higher eIF4A activity [248, 250]. Additionally, PDCD4 can directly bind to the 40S ribosome through its N-terminal domain; this extension into the mRNA entry channel thereby prevents mRNA accommodation and/or scanning [251, 252]. Under normal cellular conditions, eIF4A is far more abundant than PDCD4, with ratios of ∼10:1 (eIF4A:PDCD4) in HeLa cells [253] and 20:1 in HEK293 cells [30]. This stoichiometry suggests that PDCD4 is unlikely to be a major regulator of eIF4F assembly under normal conditions. However, under stress conditions or after specific signals, PDCD4 expression can be upregulated [254, 255]. The increased PDCD4 concentration may enhance its capacity to bind and inhibit eIF4A, potentially interfering with eIF4F complex formation and repressing the helicase activity of eIF4A during translation initiation, thereby contributing to the suppression of global translation.

4E-BPs

eIF4E-binding proteins are a class of proteins that competitively displace eIF4G from eIF4E, thereby inhibiting cap-dependent translation through the disruption of the eIF4F complex [53, 148, 256]. The binding stoichiometry of the eIF4E:4E-BP1 complex is 1:1 [257]. In cells, the relative concentrations of these proteins vary—ranging from 1:1 to 2:1 and 5:1 in HeLa cells, and 1:1 in HEK293 cells [30, 31, 253]. The levels of eIF4E and 4E-BP proteins are regulated by a homeostatic mechanism ensuring translation remains at levels compatible with cell survival. For example, when eIF4E levels are reduced, hypophosphorylated 4E-BP1 is rapidly ubiquitinated and degraded [150]. In mammalian species, three members of this protein family have been identified: 4E-BP1, 4E-BP2, and 4E-BP3. The strength of their interactions with eIF4E is regulated by the phosphorylation of at least seven sites, particularly under conditions of hypophosphorylation. Upon hypophosphorylation, 4E-BPs sequester eIF4E, thereby preventing its interaction with eIF4G and subsequently inhibiting translation. In contrast, under hyperphosphorylated conditions, 4E-BPs are unable to bind to eIF4E, enabling its release and facilitating its involvement in the initiation of protein translation [258, 259]. Certain viruses can influence the activity of eIF4E by targeting the phosphorylation status of 4E-BP. Specifically, picornaviruses are known to induce the dephosphorylation of 4E-BP, which subsequently impedes protein synthesis. Additionally, the interaction of 4E-BP with eIF4E results in disruption of the closed-loop structure and repression of translation [260, 261].

PAIP1

PAIP1 is an important regulator of translation, sharing about 40% sequence homology with the central and C-terminal regions of eIF4G [262]. Similar to eIF4G, it interacts with PABP, as well as eIF3 and eIF4A. The interaction between PAIP1 and eIF3, mediated by eIF3g, plays a pivotal role in activating translation initiation [263]. Despite high homology, eIF4G and PAIP1 bind distinct eIF3 subunits, this enables formation of a ternary PAIP1–eIF3–eIF4G complex. These findings suggest that PAIP1 may stabilize the closed-loop structure formed through the interaction of eIF4F and PABP [262–264]. While both eIF4G and PAIP1 seem to interact with the RRM1-2 region of PABP [52, 265], it remains unclear whether these interactions are mutually exclusive.

Musashi

Musashi proteins constitute a family of neural RNA-binding proteins that play crucial roles in regulating gene expression. One of its members, Musashi1, simultaneously binds the 3′UTR of its mRNA targets and PABP to displace eIF4G from the latter [266]. In oocytes, progesterone treatment significantly alters the interaction patterns of Musashi, transforming the protein into an activator of target mRNA translation in a PABP-dependent manner [267].

Phosphorylation of eIF4F

eIF4F complex activity is coordinately regulated by phosphorylation through mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) (Fig. 7) and MNK signaling pathways, which target multiple components including eIF4G, eIF4E, and inhibitory factors.

The diagram illustrates mTOR-mediated regulation of eIF4F–mRNA binding. The availability of eIF4E and eIF4A is controlled by the phosphorylation of 4E-BP and PDCD4, respectively. Hypophosphorylated 4E-BP and PDCD4 bind eIF4E and eIF4A, blocking their interaction with eIF4G and preventing cap-dependent initiation. mTOR-dependent phosphorylation triggers dissociation of 4E-BP and PDCD4 from eIF4E and eIF4A, enabling these factors to interact with eIF4G. Phosphorylated 4E-BP and PDCD4 are subsequently dephosphorylated by protein phosphatases, allowing reassociation with eIF4E and eIF4A.

eIF4G is phosphorylated by several kinases with distinct functional consequences. mTOR, activated by the PI3K–protein kinase B (PKB or Akt) signaling in response to growth factors and cellular energy status [268–270], phosphorylates eIF4G to modulate bulk translation. Protein kinase CβII (PKCβII) in complex with ribosomal receptor for activated C kinase (RACK1) phosphorylates eIF4G at serine1093 within the 48S initiation complex, potentially promoting the dissociation/recycling of eIF4F [271]. Conversely, cyclin dependent kinase 1 (Cdk1) phosphorylates eIF4G at serine1232 enhancing the interaction between eIF4G and eIF4A while reducing mRNA affinity, thereby suppressing translation initiation [272].

MNK kinases, which bind the C-terminus of eIF4G [91] primarily regulate eIF4E phosphorylation. MNK1 phosphorylates eIF4E at Ser209, while MNK2 exhibits a dual regulatory role: weakly phosphorylating eIF4E while negatively regulating eIF4G Ser1108 phosphorylation and p70S6K activation [273]. This phosphorylation reduces m7G-cap affinity to eIF4E [86] potentially through steric clash with mRNA phosphate groups and may facilitate eIF4E release after PIC formation for recycling [110]. MNK-mediated phosphorylation preferentially enhances translation of mRNAs containing both cap and structured 5′UTRs [274]. eIF4E dephosphorylation activates the NF-kB signaling and type I interferon production [275] though the precise mechanisms governing Ser209 phosphorylation effects remain incompletely understood [276–280].

A reciprocal feedback loop integrates mTOR and MNK signaling: mTOR complex 1 (mTORC1) phosphorylates MNK2 at Ser74, reducing its kinase activity and eIF4G binding, while MNK2 inhibition enhances eIF4E phosphorylation and upregulates mTORC1 signaling [281].

mTOR also phosphorylates inhibitory 4E-BPs, releasing active eIF4E for cap-complex assembly [258, 282]. 4E-BP1 contains at least seven phosphorylation sites, four mTOR-regulated [283] with phosphorylation states modulated by growth factors (enhanced) and hypoxia (reduced) [284]. The phosphatase PPM1G counterbalances this by dephosphorylating 4E-BP1, with PPM1G depletion increasing cap-dependent translation, cell size, and protein content [285, 286].

Additional regulation occurs through PDCD4, whose stability is controlled by mTOR-dependent phosphorylation. S6K1 phosphorylates PDCD4 at Ser74, promoting proteasomal degradation [287], while RSK-mediated phosphorylation at Ser76 achieves similar outcomes through the MAPK cascade [288]. Phorbol ester treatment in hepatoma cells also destabilizes PDCD4 via PKC-mediated phosphorylation [289]. Both S6K1 and RSK phosphorylate eIF4B at Ser4²², though this modification does not directly affect eIF4A activity [290].

Physiological relevance of eIF4F

eIF4F acts as a central regulator of translation initiation, with its dysregulation implicated in various pathologies including cellular stress responses (apoptosis and viral infections), cancer, and neurological disorders.

eIF4F regulation during cell stress

During apoptosis, eIF4G isoforms undergo caspase-mediated cleavage leading to translation inhibition (Table 2). Caspase 3 cleaves eIF4G at two sites between 532–533 (downstream of DLLD) and 1176–1177 (downstream of DRLD) amino acid residues generating p120 and p150 fragments that retain binding capacity for eIF4E, eIF4A, eIF3, and 40S ribosomes but lose PABP interaction, disrupting closed-loop formation [291, 292]. eIF4G3 undergoes similar cleavage at five sites, producing potentially competitive inhibitory fragments [293].

eIF4G2 is cleaved by caspase 3 to generate the p86 fragment, which exhibits complex regulatory roles [294, 295]. While initially proposed to stimulate proapoptotic protein expression via IRES-mediated translation [198, 296] subsequent evidence indicates anti-apoptotic functions, as eIF4G2 depletion induces mitotic arrest and increases caspase activity [98]. Both p97 and p86 eIF4G2 isoforms attenuate cisplatin-induced apoptosis [297], and eIF4G2 demonstrates reduced proteasomal degradation compared to eIF4G, supporting cell survival under oxidative stress [298].

Viruses employ diverse strategies targeting eIF4F components to manipulate host translation (Table 2). Multiple viruses including rhinoviruses, enteroviruses, aphthoviruses, and caliciviruses, induce proteolysis of eIF4G, preferentially cleaving the eIF4G–eIF4E complex rather than monomeric eIF4G [299–304]. Picornaviruses utilize 2A proteases while aphthoviruses employ Lpro proteases for this cleavage, generating inhibitory p100 fragments that lack eIF4E-binding capacity [60, 305–307]. eIF4G3 is also cleaved during viral infections (Table 2). Poliovirus infection demonstrates sequential cleavage kinetics where eIF4G degradation precedes eIF4G3 cleavage, with the latter coinciding with complete host translation shutdown [308].

The human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) protease cleaves eIF4G to generate a p50 fragment containing MIF4G domain (Table 2) that retains translation capacity, though its precise viral function remains unclear [309, 310]. Furthermore, the host factor eIF4A2 has been identified as critical for efficient HIV-1 replication [311]. The 3C protease of the foot-and-mouth disease virus (FMDV) targets a different component of the translation machinery, specifically cleaving the eIF4A1 isoform, while eIF4A2 remains unaffected by this viral protease [312, 313]. Collectively, the fact that eIF4A2 is either exploited by HIV or selectively spared by FMDV suggests that this specific isoform is likely indispensable for viral replication, underscoring its critical importance as a host factor.

Viral dependency on eIF4F components varies significantly. Hepatitis E virus requires all three components of eIF4F [324], while arenaviruses (JUNV, TCRV, PICV) require only eIF4A and eIF4G [325]. Human cytomegalovirus (HCMV) exhibits stage-dependent eIF4F requirement, with early-but not late-infection protein synthesis requiring functional eIF4F [326]. Similarly, many other viral mRNAs become independent of eIF2 and eIF4F for translation in late infection stages though the underlying mechanisms remain unclear (reviewed in [204]). Rotavirus demonstrates complex eIF4F interactions where NSP3 stabilizes eIF4E–eIF4G interaction, to promote viral translation [327, 328], yet eIF4F component knockdown increases viral replication, suggesting host restriction functions [329].

eIF4F regulation under cancer development

Dysregulation of protein synthesis is a hallmark of cancer, impacting cell growth, proliferation, and genome stability. Malignant cells subvert normal translational control mechanisms to support oncogenic programs, making components of the initiation machinery promising therapeutic targets (reviewed in [330, 331]).

eIF4G dysregulation contributes significantly to tumor progression. Its overexpression reprograms protein synthesis in inflammatory breast cancer toward IRES-dependent translation and correlates with increased invasiveness in lung and breast cancers [332–334]. In nasopharyngeal carcinoma, eIF4G serves as a predictive biomarker [335], while in hepatocellular carcinoma, its interaction with HSP70 promotes proliferation and inhibits cell death [336].

eIF4E oncogenic activity extends beyond simple overexpression. While eIF4E elevation promotes diverse malignancies in transgenic mice and transforms fibroblasts [337, 338], its phosphorylation status critically determines oncogenic potential. Cells expressing nonphosphorylatable eIF4E resist transformation and suppress prostate carcinogenesis in vivo [339–341].

4E-BPs exhibit context-dependent roles in tumorigenesis. Although typically downregulated in cancers, 4E-BP1 overexpression paradoxically enhances angiogenesis in hypoxic breast cancer via eIF4G-dependent VEGF translation [342]. Reduced 4E-BP1 expression creates dependency on eIF4F in prostate tumors [343], while hypophosphorylated 4E-BP1 suppresses metastasis and improves survival in lymphoma models [344, 345].

Isoform-specific functions emerge across cancer types. eIF4A2 displays tissue-specific duality, correlating with favorable prognosis in breast and lung cancers but promoting invasion in colorectal cancer, where its downregulation reduces MYC transcription and sensitizes to chemotherapy [346]. eIF4G2 deficiency drives hepatic tumorigenesis, with its mRNA undergoing APOBEC-1-mediated editing that introduces PTCs [347].

Therapeutic targeting of eIF4F components shows considerable promise. Approaches include eIF4E suppression using antisense oligonucleotides or shRNA [348, 349], eIF4A inhibition with compounds like silvestrol [350–354], mTOR pathway inhibition [355, 356], and MNK inhibitors that induce apoptosis and suppress metastasis [357]. In pancreatic cancer, eIF4A inhibition with rocaglates suppresses tumor growth and alters glutamine metabolism, suggesting combination strategies with metabolic inhibitors (432).

eIF4F in neurological diseases and developmental disorders

The eIF4F complex plays critical roles in neuronal development and function. Vertebrate-conserved neural microexons in eIF4G and eIF4G3 regulate synaptic protein translation and cognitive performance in mice [358]. eIF4G in neuropathology demonstrates a protective role. Following ischemic brain injury, eIF4G degradation correlates with reduced protein synthesis and neuronal death. Confirming this role, eIF4G overexpression enhances protein synthesis and confers neuroprotection, while its silencing exacerbates ischemic damage [359]. In Parkinson’s disease, eIF4G mutations disrupt eIF4E binding through structural alterations in the eIF3/eIF4A interaction domain, despite only directly impairing eIF3 binding [360].

Developmental consequences of eIF4G dysfunction extend across species. RNAi-mediated eIF4G knockdown in C. elegans reduces fecundity, impairs offspring growth through intestinal defects [361]. Loss-of-function mutants of eIF4G also increase germ cell apoptosis via altered mRNA translation [362]. Similarly, eIF4G2 deficiency causes embryonic lethality in Drosophila, zebrafish [363, 364], and mice and developmental failure in human ES cells [100, 365, 366].

Synaptic and behavioral regulation involves precise eIF4F control. In mouse models with silent synapses, 4E-BP removal rescued dendritic growth independently of synaptic transmission [367]. eIF4G overexpression increased striatal translation, while inhibiting eIF4E–eIF4G interaction with 4EGI-1 reversed autistic-like behaviors in 4E-BP2 knockout and eIF4E-overexpressing mice [368]. Fragile X syndrome patients exhibit elevated eIF4E phosphorylation [369], and FXS mouse models show that targeting MNK1 or eIF4E phosphorylation alleviates multiple pathological phenotypes, including translational, structural, and behavioral deficits [370].

Conclusion and outlook

In summary, this review has observed various roles of the eIF4F complex beyond its canonical function in cap-dependent translation initiation. It is evident that eIF4F serves as a critical node in cellular physiology, integrating signals from diverse pathways to regulate gene expression in response to stress, during viral infection, and in diseases such as cancer and neurological disorders. The dysregulation of eIF4F components is not merely a consequence but a driver of pathogenesis, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target.

Looking forward, several key challenges and directions emerge. First, the dynamics of eIF4F during the initiation process require clarification. A central unresolved question is whether the complex remains cap-associated throughout ribosomal scanning of long 5′UTRs or undergoes cyclic dissociation and reassociation. Second, the mechanistic details of how different eIF4G isoforms regulate translation reinitiation after uORFs remain largely uncharacterized. Finally, there is a growing need to understand how initiation and termination are coordinated within the closed-loop structure. Recent evidence suggests a direct role of eIF4F in coupling these processes, but the mechanistic underpinnings of this coordination are still obscure.

Addressing these questions will not only refine our fundamental understanding of translation but also open new avenues for therapeutic intervention. The future of eIF4F research lies in dissecting its isoform-specific functions, its role in integrating translational control with cellular homeostasis, and ultimately, in translating these insights into novel strategies for treating human diseases.
