Opportunities and Approaches to Optimising Advanced Cholangiocarcinoma Outcomes in the Era of Targeted Therapies: A Narrative Review
Authors: Teresa Macarulla, Cindy Neuzillet, Gerald W Prager, Lorenza Rimassa, John Bridgewater
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCholangiocarcinoma (CCA) represents a diverse group of malignancies. It is often identified at a late stage after the opportunity for curable resection has passed. An international educational meeting on CCA was held in Barcelona in September 2024. The meeting described the challenges with obtaining accurate epidemiological estimates for CCA because of misdiagnosis and marked regional differences, reflecting the prevalence of socioeconomic risk factors. Early-stage CCA is usually asymptomatic, and when symptoms appear, they are nonspecific. Diagnosis and treatment planning should involve a multidisciplinary team (MDT) from the outset. The European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) guidelines recommend molecular testing using next-generation sequencing when advanced disease is diagnosed. Biopsy sampling is technically challenging; if insufficient quality tissue is collected for molecular testing, liquid biopsy can be used. If the tumour is unresectable, the recommended first-line treatment is cisplatin + gemcitabine + durvalumab a programmed cell death ligand 1 [PD-L1] inhibitor or pembrolizumab a programmed cell death protein 1 [PD-1] inhibitor. The development of targeted therapies has led to these treatments being recommended as second- or third-line therapy for patients with actionable gene alterations, while 5-fluorouracil-based chemotherapy is recommended for those without. Robust data support the use of ivosidenib in patients with IDH1 mutations (phase 3), and phase 2 trials showed efficacy of pemigatinib and futibatinib for patients with FGFR2 gene fusions, trastuzumab deruxtecan and zanidatamab for patients with HER2 overexpression/amplification, and dabrafenib + trametinib for patients with BRAFV600E mutations. It is hoped that wider dissemination of the content from this meeting will improve outcomes of patients with CCA by encouraging earlier referral, increasing the use of early molecular testing, an MDT approach, and maximising the use of targeted therapies. Continued efforts to raise awareness, implement education outreach opportunities, and involve patient advocacy groups are encouraged to improve CCA outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40487-025-00370-2.Keywords: Biliary tract cancer, Cholangiocarcinoma, Molecular testing, Targeted therapy

Key Summary Points

Cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) is a malignancy that is often identified at a late stage after the opportunity for curable resection has passed.The resulting unmet needs in the diagnosis and management of CCA were discussed, based on presentations by CCA experts at the EXACT meeting.Epidemiology, presentation, treatment options, recent advances, and ongoing research in molecular biomarkers and targeted therapies that have the potential to positively influence CCA outcomes were highlighted.Awareness of the importance of education and outreach opportunities, early referral of patients, the engagement of a multidisciplinary team, molecular profiling and the use of targeted therapies will guide future improvements in CCA outcomes.

Introduction

Cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) is the second most common primary liver malignancy after hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and the incidence is increasing globally [1]. CCA represents a diverse group of malignancies, classified by their location within the liver and biliary tract [1]. CCA comprised 63.4% of all reported biliary tract cancers (BTCs) in the United Kingdom (UK) between 2001 and 2018, followed by gallbladder cancer (23.1%) and ampulla of Vater cancer (13.5%) [2].

CCA is potentially curable if diagnosed early enough to allow for curative surgery. However, early diagnosis relies on patients being referred for specialist assessment, and there are several barriers against early referral. There is no consensus about which individuals should be screened for CCA [3], and the nonspecific symptoms of CCA do not necessarily alert a physician to cancer as a probable diagnosis. Cancer Alliance data in the UK indicate that approximately half of all patients with CCA are referred after an emergency presentation, and about 34% from general practitioners (GPs), with about two-thirds of the GP referrals (20% of the total patient cohort) as standard referrals and only one-third (14% of the total) as urgent referrals associated with a high suspicion of cancer [2, 4]. Factors associated with emergency presentation were age > 84 years, female sex, comorbidities and regions of higher deprivation [4].

Late presentation of patients with CCA was primarily due to the lack of obvious early symptoms, insufficient screening populations to target, and the absence of accurate diagnostic screening tests to deliver coordinated and expedited care [4, 5]. Therefore, approximately 70% of patients with CCA are diagnosed at a late stage when the tumour is unresectable [1]. Moreover, they are frequently misdiagnosed at initial presentation (in 34% of patients according to one survey [6]), with some cases of unknown primary origin [7]. Late diagnosis, poor response to chemotherapy, limited treatment options, underlying comorbidities/frailty and complications (jaundice, malnutrition, infection) [8] lead to poor outcomes, with a median overall survival (mOS) of < 12 months and a 5-year survival rate of ≤ 10% at the advanced stage of disease [9, 10]. A substantial proportion of patients with CCA are diagnosed so late that they receive no treatment other than palliative care; there is very limited survival in these cases (median < 6 months) [8]. In the data from the Cancer Alliances in the UK, about half of the patients with CCA diagnosed between 2014 and 2017 received only best supportive care (BSC) and only 12.4% underwent potentially curative surgery [11]. The French PMSI database study reported that 63% of patients received BSC and 14% of patients underwent surgery [5].

As part of an educational initiative called the Expert eXchange meeting in acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) and CCA on Targeted therapy (EXACT), a meeting of 30 oncologists from 16 countries was held in Barcelona, Spain on September 12, 2024. EXACT is an industry-supported initiative in which oncologists from different countries meet to review evidence related to AML or CCA, through panel discussions and an integrated review of real-world approaches to difficult-to-manage cases. The content of each EXACT meeting is then disseminated locally by attendees at a central meeting.

The EXACT program was designed to incorporate several features proven to be successful in oncology education: (1) the ‘hub-and-spoke’ model of central education disseminated locally, allowing for local educators to tailor the content for specific audiences based on regional needs, practices and treatment availability [12]; (2) inclusion of case-based learning to simulate real-world clinical challenges and stimulate critical thinking [13–15]; and (3) promoting and providing international and local networking and mentoring opportunities [14, 15].

This review describes the key presentations and discussions at the September 2024 meeting in relation to the epidemiology, presentation and management of CCA. The aim of the meeting was to highlight unmet needs in the diagnosis and management of CCA, as well as recent advances and ongoing research in molecular biomarkers and targeted therapies that have the potential to positively influence CCA outcomes. Clinical case discussions are not included in this review but form part of the educational program that is being disseminated locally.

Methods

The content of this article is based on presentations by the speakers (the authors of this article) at the EXACT meeting. The authors selected the references to reflect the best evidence on the topics that were discussed. This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Epidemiology of CCA

Although CCA is a rare cancer, the incidence has been increasing globally over the last several decades, even among patients < 50 years of age, particularly for intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA) [1, 2]. The increasing incidence of CCA is likely related to better diagnosis and a growing prevalence of factors that may cause liver damage, such as metabolic syndrome, diabetes, alcohol consumption, viral hepatic infections and emergent risk factors such as the environment (not yet elucidated) [1].

While the reported incidence of CCA varies between 0.3 and 6.0 individuals per 100,000 per year in most countries [1], the true incidence is likely to be at the higher end of this range. The exceptions are the CCA ‘hotspots’ in eastern Asia (South Korea, China and Thailand) [1], where the incidence is in excess of 6.0 per 100,000 per year, driven by endemic liver fluke infestations [16]. In Europe, North America, Australasia and Japan, the incidence is estimated to be between 1.0 and 4.0 cases per 100,000 [1]. However, the true incidence of CCA is difficult to ascertain because of complexities associated with coding CCA correctly using International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes, which hampers accurate epidemiological estimates [1, 17, 18]. Referral to the recent revisions of the ICD codes (version 11) for CCA may help to resolve these issues.

A comprehensive analysis of age-standardised rates of CCA incidence and mortality from Cancer Alliance data in the UK between 2001 and 2018 demonstrated regional differences in the prevalence and incidence of CCA, even within a country [1, 2]. The incidence of iCCA was higher in areas of greater socioeconomic deprivation, with similar trends apparent in the incidence of extrahepatic CCA (eCCA) in recent years [2]. Net overall survival (OS) increased over time but remained consistently and significantly better in areas with less deprivation when compared with the most deprived areas [2].

Presentation and Diagnosis

As described earlier, CCA is usually asymptomatic in the early stages. A confirmed diagnosis at an early stage usually follows an incidental finding during a workup for other conditions, surveillance of patients with chronic liver disease (differential diagnosis with HCC), or when the patient presents with tumour-related symptoms [1]. eCCA is often associated with jaundice and other signs of biliary tract obstruction, whereas symptoms of iCCA tend to be vague and nonspecific, including asthenia/malaise, abdominal pain, nausea, anorexia and weight loss [19]. Symptom severity is related to tumour load in the liver [20].

Once a patient has been referred, the workup for diagnosis of CCA recommended by the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) involves blood tests and imaging, with biopsies of suspicious masses for histological confirmation, together with a biopsy of the non-tumoural liver to look for underlying liver disease (Table 1) [18, 21]. Multidisciplinary team (MDT) collaboration, preferably by a team that specialises in CCA, is recommended from the earliest stages of CCA management [18], including diagnosis. This is because biopsy collection of hepatobiliary tumour samples can be challenging, particularly for lesions in the biliary tree, and insufficient or poor-quality tissue may hamper histopathological and molecular assessment [22, 23]. Careful planning and coordination by the MDT will optimise biopsy collection, pathological assessment and treatment planning (Fig. 1) [22]. The MDT should include a medical oncologist, interventional radiologist, pathologist and surgeon, as well as a gastroenterologist experienced in interventional endoscopy [22]. The MDT should collaborate at all points throughout the patient’s care, and not only at diagnosis and treatment planning [24].

Biopsy Sampling

Obtaining a biopsy sample from lesions in the biliary tree can be technically challenging, with a high sample failure rate that most commonly occurs as a result of insufficient tissue being collected during biopsy, particularly for eCCA [23]. Challenges arise from the choice of the imaging modality, difficulties accessing the tumour because of its location, tissue acquisition method (combination of brushing and endobiliary biopsy for eCCA, choice of needle gauge, choice of core needle biopsy versus fine needle aspiration, cholangioscopy, endoscopic ultrasound), and suboptimal tumour cell yield because of high fibrotic or stromal tissue content in the sample [22]. For example, in the approach to molecular diagnosis of perihilar CCA (pCCA), where surgical resection is often not feasible, biopsies are complex or risky because of anatomical constraints (mainly due to occlusion by hilar vessels) and physicians are often left with brushings of very low yields for molecular analyses [25]. Depending on local practices, the tissue sample should be separated into individual cassettes for diagnostic and biomarker testing and, whenever possible, undergo rapid on-site evaluation [22].

Histological and Molecular Assessment

Differential diagnosis with liver cancer of unknown primary cause involves immunohistochemistry (IHC) panel tests, including CK7, CK20, CDX2/SATB2, TTF1, GATA3 in women and (optional) C-reactive protein [26]. In Europe, targeted therapies are available for a range of genetic alterations relevant in CCA, including those affecting isocitrate dehydrogenase 1 IDH1, fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR), B-Raf proto-oncogene, serine/threonine kinase (BRAF) and neurotrophic tyrosine receptor kinase (NTRK), as well as mismatch repair deficiency (dMMR)/high microsatellite instability (MSI-H) [18].

An estimated 45–47% of patients with CCA have potentially actionable genetic alterations [27, 28]. In patients with iCCA, the most common genetic alterations are IDH1 mutations (13–20% of cases) and FGFR2 gene fusions (approx. 11%), whereas human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) overexpression/amplifications are most common in patients with pCCA or distal CCA and gallbladder adenocarcinoma (Fig. 2) [29–31]. Therefore, major European guidelines recommend molecular testing for relevant genetic alterations [18, 32].

If biopsy sampling fails to collect sufficient tumour tissue for molecular testing, repeat tumour biopsy is indicated, although liquid biopsies are a reasonable option for collecting samples of cell-free circulating DNA for molecular testing in patients who are unwilling to undergo a repeat liver biopsy or if tumour biopsy is not feasible [33]. Clear structures should be put in place to ensure meticulous handling of small biopsy samples and their proper fixation in formalin [34]. It is also essential to ensure clear lines of communication between physicians and pathologists, to provide relevant clinical information and improve diagnostic success [34].

Liquid biopsies have a lower failure rate compared with tumour tissue sampling and are minimally invasive and immediately accessible [23]. A drawback is that the result of liquid biopsies can be negative if the patient is under active treatment. Liquid biopsy should therefore be performed at tumour progression to obtain a better yield. Liquid biopsies are not validated for routine practice, particularly as a result of insufficient sensitivity for detecting fusions, amplifications and large deletions.

Several methods can be used for molecular testing, including DNA/RNA sequencing, polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based single-gene IHC and fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH) [35]. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) is the preferred method to determine DNA (mutations/amplifications) and RNA (fusions/rearrangements) sequencing, through two different assays [35]. NGS is the most sensitive and only method suitable for testing all of the targetable genetic alterations in CCA, and does not require fresh or frozen tissue because it can be performed on formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded tumour specimens [18]. The longer turnaround time for multigene NGS testing of tissue samples highlights the importance of early testing at first diagnosis of advanced or metastatic disease. Performing NGS testing at disease progression can add delays to second-line treatment, particularly if a new sample is required. For patients with CCA, both RNA and DNA sequencing is recommended because limiting the NGS analysis to only DNA may miss other potentially targetable gene fusions [36].

The ESMO Precision Medicine Working Group recommends using multigene NGS panels for tissue or liquid biopsy testing and molecular testing during first-line therapy [21, 37]. Testing is also recommended for patients with KRAS G12C mutations, RET fusions and high tumour mutation burdens [29].

Reimbursement for molecular testing varies between countries. In Europe, many patients access molecular testing through clinical trials, because reimbursed molecular profiling is not routinely available for patients with CCA. However, this is changing in many countries [22, 23] where national programs have been launched to provide broader access to molecular screening [38, 39].

Another barrier to NGS testing is awareness of its importance in CCA. Most patients with CCA are initially seen at peripheral centres. The lack of familiarity with CCA diagnostic and treatment recommendations means that NGS is not routinely undertaken outside of academic oncology centres; however, this may vary between countries and centres. Greater education, awareness and advocacy about the importance of NGS testing in CCA are needed to broaden patient access to this important diagnostic tool.

Treatment Options

A minority of patients with CCA present with early disease that can be surgically resected. For most patients, the cornerstone of treatment is systemic therapy. However, data suggest that 50–65% of patients do not receive any treatment [5, 11, 40], and two-thirds of treated patients receive only first-line therapy [41].

Supportive care is particularly important in CCA, not only for patients who have exhausted all other treatment options but as early as possible in the cancer journey, whatever the tumour stage. The goals of supportive care are to ameliorate tumour- and treatment-related symptoms (pain, jaundice, anxiety), maintain nutritional and functional status and optimise health-related quality of life [18, 42]. Measures to prevent malnutrition and loss of lean body mass may increase the likelihood that a patient will be fit enough to undergo systemic treatment.

Although evidence is limited, patients with iCCA may be candidates for locoregional therapy in cases of exclusive or predominant liver disease, such as ablation or arterially directed therapies (e.g. transarterial embolisation [TACE] with drug-eluting beads or selective internal radiation therapy/radioembolisation) [43].

ESMO 2024 guidelines recommend cisplatin plus gemcitabine plus durvalumab or pembrolizumab as first-line systemic therapy for locally advanced or metastatic disease (Fig. 3) [21]. Targeted therapy is recommended as preferential second-line or third-line therapy in patients with inactionable alterations (40%), and 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) plus oxaliplatin chemotherapy for those without inactionable alterations (Fig. 3). However, the approval process for targeted therapies is often protracted, and patient access to targeted treatments is controlled by local reimbursement policies which differ considerably between countries [44]. Therefore, some of the recommended therapies are unavailable or financially inaccessible for many patients [44, 45].

First-Line Chemotherapy

The first-line chemotherapy backbone for CCA is cisplatin plus gemcitabine, which was established in 2010 with publication of the ABC-02 phase 3 study comparing the combination with gemcitabine alone [46] and the phase 2 BT-22 study in Japan with similar results [47]. Pooled analysis of the data from these studies showed that cisplatin plus gemcitabine was associated with an improvement in OS of 35% (hazard ratio [HR] 0.65, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.54–0.78, p < 0.001) and in progression-free survival (PFS) of 36% (HR 0.64, 95% CI 0.53–0.76, p < 0.001) compared with gemcitabine alone [48]. The survival benefits of the combination were not associated with a significant excess of adverse events (AEs) compared with gemcitabine alone [46]. The OS and PFS outcomes were consistent in the ABC-02 and BT-22 studies, despite differences in the number of treatment cycles [46–48]. Overall survival in patients on triplet chemotherapy regimens with FOLFIRINOX (leucovorin calcium [folinic acid], fluorouracil, irinotecan hydrochloride and oxaliplatin) or gemcitabine plus cisplatin plus nanoparticle albumin-bound paclitaxel (NabP) were not superior to those reported with gemcitabine plus cisplatin in phase 3 trials [49, 50], with the exception being the addition of S-1 to gemcitabine plus cisplatin in Asian participants [49].

It is important to remember that the combination regimen used for CCA uses a relatively low dose of cisplatin (25 mg/m2 on days 1 and 8 of each cycle), which can be administered in an outpatient setting, typically without the need for prolonged intravenous hydration and fluid balance monitoring after administration. Therefore, concerns about cisplatin-related toxicity and inconvenience are usually not sufficient to justify omitting or replacing cisplatin (e.g. by oxaliplatin) during chemotherapy for CCA. However, oxaliplatin may be used as an alternative to cisplatin in patients with renal dysfunction or hearing impairment [18], but there are limited data supporting the use of oxaliplatin plus gemcitabine. Therefore, the oxaliplatin plus gemcitabine combination should not be used in preference to cisplatin plus gemcitabine, unless there is a contraindication for cisplatin.

In recent years, the standard first-line regimen was revised to include immunotherapy, based on the results of the phase 3 TOPAZ-1 [51] and KEYNOTE-966 [52] trials. In TOPAZ-1, all patients received cisplatin plus gemcitabine for eight cycles and were randomized to additional placebo or durvalumab, continued beyond the duration of chemotherapy, until unacceptable toxicity or progression [51]. The addition of durvalumab to chemotherapy improved OS and PFS compared with placebo (HR for OS was 0.76, 95% CI 0.64–0.91, p = 0.021 and HR for PFS was 0.75, 95% CI 0.63–0.89, p = 0.001; Table 2) [51, 53–55].

The design of the KEYNOTE-966 study was similar to that of the TOPAZ-1 study, except that patients in KEYNOTE-966 were randomised to pembrolizumab or placebo, participants could receive maintenance therapy with gemcitabine and pembrolizumab after completing eight cycles of chemotherapy, and the maximum duration of pembrolizumab was 2 years [52]. Overall, 41% of patients received maintenance gemcitabine (43% of the pembrolizumab group and 39% of the placebo group, representing more than three-quarters of patients who reached 6 months of treatment). The results of KEYNOTE-966 were consistent with those of TOPAZ-1 showing an improvement in OS with the addition of immunotherapy (Table 2) [52, 56]. The consistency of findings in the two studies add weight to the argument that maintenance gemcitabine therapy may not be beneficial beyond the eight cycles of first-line chemotherapy.

The ESMO guidelines recommend both cisplatin plus gemcitabine plus durvalumab and cisplatin plus gemcitabine plus pembrolizumab as category 1 recommendations in this setting [21].

Second-Line Treatment Options

Chemotherapy

Randomised trials of second-line therapies are limited. The ABC-06 study compared 5-FU/leucovorin plus oxaliplatin (FOLFOX) in association with active symptom control (ASC) versus ASC alone [57]. Although OS was significantly better in the FOLFOX plus ASC group than the ASC alone group (median 6.2 vs 5.3 months; HR 0.69, 95% CI 0.50–0.97, p = 0.031), most patients died within 12 months (12-month OS rate of 25.9% in the combination arm vs 11.4% in the ASC alone group) and only 5% of patients had an objective response to the combination [57].

A randomised, open-label phase 2b study in South Korea (NIFTY) compared a combination of liposomal irinotecan plus 5-FU/leucovorin with 5-FU/leucovorin [58, 59]. In the final analysis, both OS and PFS were significantly longer in the group receiving liposomal irinotecan (mOS of 8.6 vs 5.3 months, HR 0.68, 95% CI 0.48–0.95, p = 0.02 and median PFS [mPFS] of 4.2 vs 1.7 months, HR 0.61, 95% CI 0.44–0.86, p = 0.004) [58]. However, these results were not replicated in the AIO NALIRICC randomised phase 2 study in Germany reporting a mPFS of 2.6 months (95% CI 1.7–3.6) in the group receiving liposomal irinotecan and 2.3 months (95% CI 1.6–3.4) in the control group [60]. Despite the differences in PFS between these studies, a recent pooled analysis of individual patient data determined that the mPFS was significantly higher with liposomal irinotecan versus placebo (3.6 vs 1.8 months, HR 0.65, p < 0.001); significant objective response rate (ORR) benefits were also reported (17.5% vs 2.8%, p < 0.001) [61]. The NIFTY and AIO NALIRICC studies found differences in the biology of CCA between these populations [58, 59].

Based on currently available data, the preferred second-line chemotherapy regimen is FOLFOX for patients without targetable alterations [18].

Targeted Therapies

As described earlier, patients with CCA may have a range of alterations that may be suitable drug targets, so molecular analysis is an important strategy to identify potentially beneficial therapies. For patients with actionable mutations, the ESMO guidelines recommend targeted therapy as second-line treatment after progression on chemo-immunotherapy [18, 21]. Novel targeted therapy options are undergoing continuous development.

IDH1 Inhibitors

The IDH family of enzymes are essential components of cellular metabolism [62]. Cancer cells that acquire IDH1 or IDH2 mutations produce defective detoxification mechanisms, DNA damage and genome instability [62]. IDH1 mutations are present in about 14–20% of patients with iCCA and 0.8% of patients with eCCA, although the prevalence of IDH1 mutations in iCCA ranges from 4.5% to 55.6% in individual studies and is higher in non-Asian than Asian individuals (16.5% vs 8.8%; p < 0.001) [29–31]. Although an IDH1 mutation is generally not considered to be prognostic [31], some data suggest that this mutation can be a negative prognostic factor for patients receiving second-line treatment [63].

Ivosidenib is an IDH1 inhibitor that showed promising efficacy in a phase 1 study in 168 patients with solid tumours and IDH1 mutations, of whom 73 had iCCA [64]. Overall, 5% of the iCCA cohort had an objective response and 56% had stable disease (SD); the mPFS was 3.8 months and mOS 13.8 months in this heavily pretreated population [64]. The maximal effect was seen at a dose of 500 mg, so this dose was chosen for further evaluation in a phase 3 study [64].

In the phase 3 ClarIDHy study, 185 patients with pretreated (second- or third-line) mutant IDH1 iCCA were randomised 2:1 to ivosidenib 500 mg or placebo until disease progression or unacceptable toxicity [65]. Most patients (92.4%) had metastatic iCCA [65]. Treatment with ivosidenib almost doubled the PFS compared with placebo; mPFS was 2.7 months in the ivosidenib group compared with 1.4 months in the placebo group (HR 0.37, 95% CI 0.25–0.54, p < 0.0001; Fig. 4) [65]. The outcome benefit in favour of ivosidenib was seen consistently across all patient subgroups, irrespective of sex, number of previous lines of therapy or metastatic versus advanced disease [65]. mOS in the intent-to-treat population was 10.8 months (95% CI 7.7–17.6) in the ivosidenib arm compared with 9.7 months (95% CI 4.8–12.1) in the placebo arm (HR 0.7, 95% CI 0.4–1.1, p = 0.06). However, a high proportion (70.5%) of patients in the placebo arm crossed over to the ivosidenib arm, which introduces bias into the survival analysis, so a rank-preserving structural failure time (RPSFT) model, predefined in the statistical design of the study, was used to adjust for crossover [10]. After adjustment, the mOS in the placebo group was 5.1 months and the HR for OS was statistically significant (0.49, 95% CI 0.34–0.70, p < 0.001) [10]. As seen in the phase 1 study, the ORR was relatively low (2%) in the ivosidenib group, but approximately 50% of patients had SD, resulting in a disease control rate (DCR) of 53.2% in the ivosidenib group [65]. In the placebo group, no patient had a response and 17 had SD for a DCR of 27.9%. Patients in the ivosidenib group were also significantly less likely to have a clinically meaningful decrease in quality of life [65].

A post hoc analysis of the ClarIDHy study was conducted comparing outcomes in the 26 patients who received ivosidenib for ≥ 1 year (including those who initially received placebo and then crossed over to ivosidenib) with those who received ivosidenib for < 1 year [66]. Patients who received ivosidenib for ≥ 1 year had prolonged PFS compared with those receiving shorter treatment (median 16.6 vs 1.8 months), with 68% of patients free from progression at 1 year; the PFS rate in the long-term responder group was 69.2% at 12 months and 24.9% at 24 months. In addition, most of the long-term responders were still alive after 2 years, with a 2-year OS rate of 92.1% in this group compared with 8.6% in the group who stopped ivosidenib within 1 year. The long responder group included 20 patients with SD and six patients with a partial response, all six of whom had been randomised to ivosidenib initially. Tumour shrinkage was noted in 25/26 of the long responders (96%) [66].

These data shed light on the pattern of response to ivosidenib. In particular, the data provide an informative overall picture of ivosidenib efficacy at 24 months, where the prolonged PFS rates demonstrate the long-term pattern of response to ivosidenib [66]. In addition, the HR versus placebo and 12- or 24-month PFS rates provide a more informative picture of ivosidenib efficacy than the median PFS, further demonstrating the efficacy of ivosidenib. The Kaplan–Meier curves for PFS in the ClarIDHy study (Fig. 4) showed separation after about 2 months of treatment [65], demonstrating that the mPFS in the overall cohort was relatively short and influenced by patients who did not respond. On the other hand, some patients obtained a durable response with prolonged disease control and survival [66]. Therefore, the HR versus placebo and 12- or 24-month PFS rates provide a more informative picture of ivosidenib efficacy than the mPFS.

Ivosidenib was generally well tolerated in the ClarIDHy study; the most common AEs in the ivosidenib group were grade 1–2 diarrhoea, nausea and fatigue, which were also present in the placebo group, albeit at a lower incidence [10]. The most common grade ≥ 3 AE was ascites, which developed in 9% of patients in the ivosidenib group and 7% in the placebo group [10]. Prolonged QT interval, which was an AE of special interest, was seen in 10% of ivosidenib recipients and 3% of placebo recipients [10]. Prolongation of the QT interval is a sign of a disturbance in the ventricular action potential, and may lead to ventricular arrhythmias [67], thus changes to the QT interval during ivosidenib should be managed promptly (Supplementary Fig. S1) [68]. There were no grade 5 AEs; 4% of patients required a dose reduction or interruption due to AEs (vs 0% in the placebo group), and 7% permanently discontinued ivosidenib (vs 8% in the placebo group) due to toxicity [10].

A confirmatory phase 3b, open-label study called ProvIDHe (NCT05876754) is being conducted to gain more insight into the safety and efficacy of ivosidenib in patients with CCA. In a preliminary report from this study, 196/223 patients (88%) on ivosidenib treatment experienced at least one AE, most commonly diarrhoea, nausea, tiredness/weakness, loss of appetite and heart rhythm changes (QT prolongation) [69]. Serious AEs were reported in approximately 31% of patients; almost one-third of patients temporarily discontinued ivosidenib because of AEs. No new safety concerns were identified [69]. A phase 2 study with ivosidenib is also underway in Japanese patients with IDH1-mutated CCA (NCT06081829). A phase 1b/2 trial (NCT06501625) is investigating the safety, tolerability and preliminary activity of ivosidenib in combination with durvalumab and gemcitabine plus cisplatin as first-line therapy in participants with an IDH1 mutation.

FGFR Inhibitors

Several FGFR inhibitors have been investigated as second-line or later therapy in patients with CCA in phase 2 single-arm open-label studies (Table 3) [70, 71]. Pemigatinib, an ATP-competitive reversible inhibitor of FGFR-1, -2 and -3, was investigated in the FIGHT-202 study [65]. Most patients (108/147) had fusions or rearrangements in FGFR2. Of these, the ORR was 40.0%, with 37 patients having a confirmed partial response and 3 having a confirmed complete response; the median duration of response (mDOR) was 9.1 months [72]. Based on the data from the FIGHT-202 study, pemigatinib was approved in Europe and the USA for the second-line treatment of advanced or metastatic CCA in patients with FGFR2 fusions or rearrangements [73, 74]. The mOS was 17.5 months (95% CI 14.4–2.9) and mPFS was 7.0 months (95% CI 6.1–19.5) [72].

Futibatinib is an irreversible inhibitor of FGFR-1, -2, -3 and -4 available in the USA and EU [75, 76] that is associated with a lower risk of developing resistance compared with pemigatinib because of its distinct binding site (non-ATP competitive) and irreversibility of binding [77]. The phase 2 study with futibatinib included only patients with FGFR fusions or rearrangements (n = 103), of whom 43/103 had a response (ORR of 42%; Table 3) [70]. The mDOR was ≥ 6 months in 31/43 patients (72%) and ≥ 12 months in 6 (14%). The mDOR was 9.7 months, mOS was 21.7 months and mPFS was 9.0 months [70].

The most common AEs with all the FGFR inhibitors are hyperphosphataemia, stomatitis and toxicity of the eyes, skin and nails [70, 71, 78].

Other FGFR inhibitors (such as tinengotinib) are under development [79] that have activity for the mutations that arise in acquired resistance, opening the way for the sequential use of FGFR2 inhibitors. Lirafugratinib is also being developed as a novel medicine for patients with FGFR2-driven CCA [80].

HER2 Inhibitors

HER2 amplification or overexpression is present in approximately 5–9% of CCAs (intra- or extrahepatic) [35]. The phase 2 MyPathway basket study included 39 patients with advanced or metastatic BTCs that had HER2 amplification, overexpression or both [81]. Patients received pertuzumab and trastuzumab until disease progression or unacceptable toxicity. Overall, nine patients had partial response for an ORR of 23% (95% CI 11–39), and 11 had SD and a DCR of 51% (95% CI 35–68) [81]. The mPFS tended to be longer in the group with eCCA versus iCCA (6.8 months [95% CI 1.3–13.5] vs 2.6 months [95% CI 1.0–5.3]), as did the mOS (8.0 months [95% CI 2.0–not estimable] vs 3.9 months [95% CI 1.2–8.1]; Table 3) [81]. Despite the 2023 ESMO recommendation for combination therapy with trastuzumab plus pertuzumab to treat CCA, this combination is no longer included in the ESMO Guideline for CCA and is currently approved only for use in breast cancer in both the USA and Europe [82, 83].

In November 2024, the FDA approved zanidatamab, a bispecific antibody directed against HER2, for previously treated patients with unresectable or metastatic HER2-positive BTC [84]. The approval was based on data from the HERIZON-BTC-01 study (Table 3), which included 87 patients with BTC [85]. Zanidatamab was associated with a 41.3% ORR in the cohort of patients with HER2-positive disease (n = 80); interestingly, none of the seven HER2-negative patients responded. The mPFS was 5.5 months (95% CI 3.7–7.2) in the HER2-positive cohort and 1.9 months (95% CI 1.2–not estimable) in the HER2-negative cohort. The mOS was 15.5 months (95% CI 10.4–18.5) in HER2-positive patients [86]. Although zanidatamab is not yet approved by the European Medicines Agency (EMA), in April 2025, the Committee for Medicinal Products for Human Use (CHMP) recommended the granting of its conditional marketing authorisation [87].

The DESTINY-PanTumor02 phase 2 trial, which evaluated trastuzumab deruxtecan in pretreated patients with HER2-expressing solid tumours, found clinically meaningful survival benefits in patients with BTC and safety outcomes that were consistent with those observed in other types of cancer (Table 3) [88]. Among the 41 patients with BTC, the ORR was 22.0% (95% CI 10.6–37.6). The 2024 ESMO guideline update includes treatment options for patients with BTC and HER2 overexpression [21].

BRAF Inhibitors

Approximately 5% of patients with iCCA have BRAFV600E mutations [35]. The Rare Oncology Agonist Research (ROAR) study, a single-arm, open-label phase 2 study, was undertaken in patients with progressive cancers associated with BRAFV600E mutations, of whom 43 had BTC and 39 of these (91%) had iCCA (Table 3) [89, 90]. Patients received the BRAF inhibitor dabrafenib in combination with the mitogen-activated extracellular signal-regulated kinase kinase (MEK) inhibitor trametinib, to limit the risk of resistance development. In the ROAR cohort with BTC, the combination of dabrafenib plus trametinib was associated with an ORR of 47% (95% CI 31–62) and a mDOR of 9 months (95% CI 6–14). The mOS was 14 months (95% CI 10–33), with 6-month, 12-month and 24-month OS rates of 84%, 56% and 36%, respectively [89, 90]. In the USA, dabrafenib in combination with trametinib can be used for CCA because it has a tumour-agnostic indication for patients with BRAFV600E mutations whose cancer has progressed after prior treatment and who have no satisfactory alternative treatment options [91]. However, in Europe, the approved indications for dabrafenib do not include CCA [92].

PD-1 Inhibitors

The KEYNOTE-158 study was designed to investigate the efficacy of the PD-1 inhibitor pembrolizumab in patients with unresectable and/or metastatic mutation-susceptible tumours (n = 233), and included 22 patients with CCA, not previously treated with immunotherapy (Table 3) [93]. The ORR was 40.9% (95% CI 20.7–63.6) in patients with CCA with MSI-H status. The mPFS in the CCA group was 4.2 months (95% CI 2.1–not reached) and OS was 24.3 months (95% CI 6.5–not reached) [93]. The proportion of patients with grade 3–5 AEs with pembrolizumab in KEYNOTE-158 (14.6%) was notably lower than in KEYNOTE-966 (71.5%) [93].

KEYNOTE-158 was conducted before the development of durvalumab plus pembrolizumab in the first-line setting. Now that patients routinely receive anti-PD1/PDL1 in combination with chemotherapy as first-line treatment, there is no longer a rationale to treat them with second-line single-agent immune checkpoint inhibitors. However, second-line treatment with single-agent immune checkpoint inhibitors is approved for patients with MSI-H tumours.

TRK Inhibitors

A very small proportion of patients with CCA (< 1%) have NTRK fusions [35], which may be targeted by tropomyosin receptor kinase (TRK) inhibitors. The TRK inhibitor larotrectinib is tumour-agnostic in patients with solid tumours of any type associated with NTRK fusions, progressive disease and no other satisfactory treatment options [94, 95]. A phase 2 study of larotrectinib in 55 patients (n = 2 with CCA) with solid tumours and NTRK1, NRTK2 or NTRK3 fusions who had progressed on previous systemic therapy reported an ORR of 75% (95% CI 61–85) in the overall study cohort; one of the two patients with CCA had a response (Table 3). The overall responses were durable, with 71% of responders having an ongoing response and 55% of the whole cohort being progression-free after 12 months [96]. Similarly, entrectinib has subsequently received tumour-agnostic approval in Europe [97], based on phase 2 basket trials [98–100]; European approval of repotrectinib was pending at the time of writing [101].

Conclusions

There are multiple potential points in the natural history of CCA at which interventions can enhance outcomes. The first is at the earliest stages of symptom development, when suspicion of CCA should prompt, as should a referral for appropriate assessment by a specialist. The second is at diagnosis, when obtaining an adequate tissue sample for biopsy and molecular testing for actionable mutations are important. The treatment landscape for CCA is rapidly evolving, and there are a growing number of targeted therapies developed for patients with actionable mutations. The hub meeting facilitated discussion, raised awareness and highlighted the importance of education and outreach opportunities in CCA, involvement of patient advocacy groups, early referral of patients, the engagement of MDTs in diagnosis and treatment planning, molecular profiling and the use of targeted therapies where possible to optimise patient outcomes. Future research on new or emerging targets (e.g. gene deletions on tumour suppressor genes) or new immunotherapy strategies is warranted.
