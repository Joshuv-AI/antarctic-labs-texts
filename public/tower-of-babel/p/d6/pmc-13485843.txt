Association between the atherogenic index of plasma and outcomes in patients with severe aortic stenosis who undergo transcatheter aortic valve replacement
Authors: Hongde Li, Weiya Li, Ruisi Tang, Shiqin Peng, Yuan Feng, Yong Peng, Jiafu Wei, Wenqi Hu, Xi Chen, Yiming Li, Mao Chen, CARRY II Study Collaborators
Published: 2026

To the Editor: Dysregulated lipid metabolism is currently recognized as a central pathophysiological mechanism underlying degenerative aortic stenosis.[1,2] Although bioprosthetic valve replacement effectively addresses the disease process of the native valve—characterized by stiffening, fibrosis, and calcification—it does not address the persistent dysregulation of lipid metabolism. This persistent upstream disease process may continue to influence patient outcomes after transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR). In fact, lipid-related biomarkers have been widely validated as predictors of cardiovascular disease incidence and prognosis.[345] Therefore, preoperative lipid metabolism status should be evaluated in TAVR recipients. However, the relationship between lipid metabolism and prognosis in patients with TAVR remains poorly understood.

The atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), which accounts for serum triglyceride (TG) and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) levels, is calculated as log10(TG/HDL-C).[6] AIP is considered one of the most powerful predictors of atherogenic dyslipidemia and has been closely associated with various cardiometabolic disorders, including cardiovascular disease, diabetes mellitus, metabolic syndrome, and hypertension.[7] Thus, the aim of this study was to investigate the prognostic value of AIP in patients undergoing TAVR, with the use of long‑term follow‑up data from both a retrospective single‑center discovery cohort and a prospective multicenter validation cohort.

This mixed-design ambispective study comprised a single-center retrospective analysis (West China Hospital cohort) and an analysis of data from the China Aortic Valve Transcatheter Replacement Registry II (CARRY II cohort), a multicenter prospective registry. Detailed screening processes are provided in Supplementary Figures 1 and 2, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C939, respectively.

The retrospective component of this study was approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (Approval No. 2023-076). The prospective cohort was approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (Approval No. 2022-1003). Written informed consent to participate was obtained from all patients for both analyses. The study complied with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

The primary endpoint was all-cause mortality. Secondary endpoints included cardiovascular death, all-cause readmission, and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), such as cardiovascular death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal stroke, and the need for unplanned cardiovascular hospitalization. Follow-up visits were scheduled for 1, 3, 6, and 12 months after TAVR and annually thereafter. Adverse events were identified by local investigators and adjudicated by an independent committee whose members were unaware of AIP values, according to the Valve Academic Research Consortium 3 criteria.[8]

Detailed descriptions of data collection and measurement and statistical analysis are provided in the Supplementary Methods, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C939.

Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C939 lists detailed baseline characteristics for both cohorts. Supplementary Figure 3, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C939 depicts the histogram distribution of pre-TAVR AIPs with an overlaid fitted curve. The data on pre-TAVR computed tomographic angiography characteristics are listed in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C939.

Data from the 1597 patients in the West China Hospital cohort who underwent TAVR were analyzed. Patients were stratified into three groups according to their AIPs: group 1 (AIP <−0.20), group 2 (−0.20 ≤AIP ≤−0.01), and group 3 (AIP >−0.01). The patients’ mean age was 73.0 ± 7.7 years, and 58.42% (933/1597) of the cohort were men. Similarly, the 488 patients in the CARRY II cohort, who underwent TAVR and had complete AIP measurements, were also stratified according to AIP: group 1 (AIP <−0.09), group 2 (−0.09 ≤AIP ≤0.09), and group 3 (AIP >0.09). The patients’ mean age was 71.6 ± 7.2 years, and 53.89% (263/488) were men.

For the West China Hospital cohort, the mean follow-up period was 2.0 years. The 7-year cumulative incidence of all-cause mortality differed substantially across AIP tertiles, reaching 46.4% in group 3, 25.4% in group 2, and 10.5% in group 1 [log-rank P <0.001; Figure 1A]. Cardiovascular mortality occurred in a parallel pattern, with incidences of 24.7%, 12.5%, and 1.4%, respectively [P <0.001; Supplementary Figure 4, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C939]. In accordance with these findings, readmission occurred more frequently in group 3 (69.5%) than in group 2 (57.4%) and group 1 [50.5%; P <0.001; Figure 1B]. Paralleling this trend, the cumulative incidence of MACE was the highest in group 3 (59.0%), followed by group 2 (48.8%) and group 1 [40.0%; P <0.001; Figure 1C].

For the CARRY II cohort, the mean follow-up period was 1.4 years. All-cause mortality increased in a stepwise manner with higher AIP, with cumulative incidences of 11.1% in group 3, 4.5% in group 2, and 3.1% in group 1 (P = 0.019). Readmission rates were significantly higher among patients in group 3 (12.3%) than in the other two groups (P = 0.004). However, we observed no significant differences for MACE [P = 0.705; Figure 1D–F].

In the Cox proportional hazards analysis, univariable results are listed in Supplementary Tables 6 and 7, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C939, respectively. According to the fully adjusted multivariable Cox model, in which AIP was analyzed as a continuous variable [Supplementary Table 8, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C939], each 1-SD increase in AIP was independently associated with elevated risks of all-cause mortality (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 2.12; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.73–2.61; P <0.001), need for readmission (aHR, 1.27; 95% CI, 1.13–1.45; P <0.001), cardiovascular mortality (aHR, 1.72; 95% CI, 1.21–2.45; P = 0.003), and MACE (aHR, 1.68; 95% CI, 1.43–1.98; P <0.001). The fully adjusted regression model for the CARRY II cohort [Supplementary Table 9, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C939] revealed that each 1-SD increase in AIP was significantly associated with increased risk of all-cause mortality (aHR, 1.61; 95% CI, 1.06–2.45; P = 0.026) and need for readmission (aHR, 1.54; 95% CI, 1.13–2.09; P = 0.006) but not with MACE (P = 0.330).

Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed that higher preoperative AIP was generally associated with an increased risk of adverse outcomes. In the West China Hospital cohort [Supplementary Figure 5, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C939], we observed a significant nonlinear association between preoperative AIP and MACE (Pfor nonlinearity <0.05), whereas the relationships between preoperative AIP and all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, and readmission were consistent with a linear trend (P >0.05). In the CARRY II cohort [Supplementary Figure 6, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C939], we detected no significant nonlinearity for any outcomes.

In the West China Hospital cohort, Supplementary Figure 7, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C939 shows that the areas under the receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) curves (AUCs) for AIP were 0.70 (95% CI, 0.65–0.74; P <0.001) in predicting all‑cause mortality, 0.73 (95% CI, 0.68–0.78; P <0.001) in predicting cardiovascular mortality, 0.56 (95% CI, 0.53–0.59; P <0.001) in predicting need for readmission, and 0.63 (95% CI, 0.59–0.66; P <0.001) in predicting MACE. Meanwhile, the ROC curves in Supplementary Figure 8, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C939 for the CARRY II cohort demonstrate AUCs of 0.63 (95% CI, 0.54–0.72; P = 0.016) in predicting all‑cause mortality and 0.62 (95% CI, 0.54–0.70; P = 0.005) in predicting need for readmission.

We assessed the incremental predictive value of AIP by using a multivariable Cox regression model, evaluated primarily with Harrell’s C-statistic, net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) metrics. The basic model includes all variables entered into the multivariable analysis. In the West China Hospital cohort [Supplementary Table 10, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C939], the inclusion of AIP in the basic model provided statistically significant incremental predictive values for all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, need for readmission, and MACE. In the CARRY II cohort [Supplementary Table 11, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C939], the inclusion of AIP in the basic model provided a statistically significant incremental predictive value only for all-cause mortality and need for readmission.

To reduce potential biases resulting from the retrospective design, we performed sensitivity analyses of data from the West China Hospital cohort. First, we excluded patients with an estimated glomerular filtration rate of <30 mL·min−1·1.73 m−2 and those with a diagnosis of cancer to assess the effect of these exclusions on survival analysis. The postexclusion cumulative hazard curves [Supplementary Figure 9, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C939] were similar to those obtained without these exclusions. Second, we conducted a 30-day landmark analysis to ensure that short-term survival did not skew long-term results. Findings from the landmark analysis [Supplementary Figure 10, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C939] were consistent with the primary outcomes, which underscores the robustness and reliability of this study’s conclusions. To assess for potential selection bias, we compared the baseline characteristics between the included and excluded patients in the West China Hospital cohort [Supplementary Table 12, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C939].

The relevance of AIP in patients undergoing TAVR is further discussed in the Supplementary Discussion, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C939.
