Evidence‐based guideline for clinical practice in the diagnosis, treatment, management, and prevention of recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers in China

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWheezing is a common respiratory symptom in infants and toddlers, and recurrent wheezing is a significant respiratory disorder affecting this age group. Given the multifactorial etiology of recurrent wheezing, clinical practice lacks standardized diagnostic and therapeutic protocols. Recent years have witnessed substantial progress in clinical research and therapeutic advancements, yielding robust evidence to inform clinical decision‐making for recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers. The guideline is developed by integrating the latest domestic and international clinical consensus statements, evidence‐based guidelines, and relevant research findings. Meanwhile, this guideline comprehensively takes into account the actual situation in China and the opinions of Chinese experts. This guideline aims to provide clinicians with standardized protocols to enhance diagnostic accuracy and therapeutic efficacy in managing infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing.Keywords: Diagnosis, Evidence‐based guideline, Infant and toddler, Management, Prevention, Recurrent wheezing, Treatment

INTRODUCTION

Wheezing is a common respiratory symptom in children. Due to the characteristics of respiratory tract anatomy, physiology, and immune function, infants and toddlers are especially prone to recurrent wheezing. Furthermore, the diagnosis and management of recurrent wheezing are particularly challenging due to its multifactorial etiology, nonspecific symptomatology, and lack of reliable diagnostic tools in this age group compared to school‐age children. Therefore, it is necessary to develop practical, evidence‐based clinical guidelines to aid pediatricians at all levels in the standardized diagnosis, treatment, and management, as well as prevention, of recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers. For this purpose, the Committee of Pediatrics, China Medical Education Association, convened a panel of experts, who voted to establish a consensus and put forward the following recommendations.

GUIDELINE DEVELOPMENT METHODS

The development of this guideline is based on the World Health Organization (WHO) Handbook for Guideline Development and the Guiding Principles for Developing/Revising Clinical Practice Guidelines in China (2022 Edition). It follows the Appraisal of Guidelines for Research and Evaluation II (AGREE II), AGREE in China (AGREE‐China), and Reporting Items for Practice Guidelines in Healthcare.

Guideline working group

The guideline established a steering committee, an expert group, an external review group, and a secretariat. These groups consist of experts and researchers from China in fields including pediatric respiratory medicine, allergology, and methodology, ensuring representation in terms of discipline, geographic region, and gender. There were five members in the steering committee, 20 in the expert group, five in the external review group, and three in the secretariat. All members declared no conflicts of interest.

Formulation of clinical recommendations

A systematic search and review of published literature in the field of childhood wheezing was conducted. Based on surveys and interviews with clinicians, preliminary clinical recommendations were discussed in steering committee and expert group meetings, which were held both online and offline. Ultimately, 31 clinical recommendations were selected by the expert group through a modified Delphi process.

Evidence search

Based on the Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome principle, search strategies including subject headings and free‐text terms were developed and systematically executed. The search was limited to publications up to June 30, 2025, and to the English and Chinese languages.

The English databases included MEDLINE, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science. The Chinese databases included China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang Data, VIP (China Science and Technology Journal Database), and China Biomedical Literature Database. The main types of evidence included were systematic reviews, meta‐analyses, randomized controlled trials, cohort studies, case‐control studies, case series reports, recommendations, consensus statements, and guidelines.

Guideline websites included the WHO official site, the Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) official site, and the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence official site.

Evidence selection and data extraction

Evidence related to wheezing in infants and toddlers was included, encompassing recommendations, consensus statements, guidelines, systematic reviews, and original research. The screening and information extraction for each piece of literature were independently performed by two individuals. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third party.

Evidence quality assessment, grading, and recommendation formulation

The retrieved evidence was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation methodology. Recommendations were formulated based on voting by the expert panel. A proposed recommendation was finalized if it achieved agreement from over 90% of the experts. Proposed recommendations with agreement from less than 75% of the experts were not adopted. For recommendations with agreement between 75% and 90%, further literature evidence was retrieved, and a revote was conducted (Table S1). After the secretariat revised the recommendations based on feedback, the guideline was finalized following review and approval by the steering committee.

Guideline writing and external review

After finalization, the guideline was submitted to the external review group for full‐text evaluation. The expert group and secretariat revised and refined the guideline based on the feedback received, resulting in the final version.

DEFINITION, CLASSIFICATION, AND SEVRITY GRADING OF RECURRENT WHEZZING IN INFANTS AND TODDLERS

Definition of recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers

Wheezing is a continuous, coarse sound produced during breathing when airflow passes through a narrowed airway, creating turbulent flow that vibrates the airway wall. In theory, any disease that narrows the airway can cause wheezing. Narrowing at the level of the larynx or upper airway produces an inspiratory‐predominant sound, known as stridor. Narrowing of the lower airway generates an expiratory‐predominant sound, also called wheezing or whistling. If the sound is audible during both inspiration and expiration, it is described as biphasic wheezing. In this guideline, recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers refers to expiratory‐predominant wheezes that occur repeatedly. In clinical practice, parents often mistake ‘tachypnea’, ‘snoring’, or ‘gurgling sputum sounds in the throat’ for ‘wheezing’. Therefore, clinicians must distinguish among these different ‘wheezing’ presentations and, when necessary, ask parents to record the sound or video of the suspected ‘wheezing’ episodes.

Currently, there is no universally accepted definition for recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers. In this guideline, infants and toddlers are defined as those aged 29 days to 3 years, and recurrent wheezing is defined as three or more wheezing episodes, each separated by an asymptomatic interval of at least 7 days. 1 , 2 The 2025 update of the GINA defines recurrent wheezing in children under 5 years of age as at least two acute wheezing episodes in the past 12 months. 3 In addition to recurrent wheezing, wheezing that persists without remission for more than 4 weeks is termed persistent wheezing. Because the etiologies of ‘recurrent versus persistent wheezing’ and of ‘expiratory wheeze versus inspiratory stridor’ differ, the diagnosis and management of persistent wheezing and inspiratory stridor are not detailed in this guideline.

Classification of recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers

To better guide clinicians in the diagnosis, treatment, and management of recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers, several classifications have been proposed on the basis of the clinical characteristics of wheezing episodes, age at onset and subsequent course, and the presence or absence of atopy.

Classification based on the manifestation of symptom episodes

Episodic viral wheezing: The triggers for episodes are mostly respiratory viral infections. Wheezing symptoms resolve completely when the infection is controlled, and no symptoms are observed between the episodes. This is generally diagnosed as “acute asthmatic bronchitis” in China.

Multiple trigger wheezing: Besides being triggered by respiratory viral infections, wheezing can also be induced by exercise, crying, laughing, allergen exposure, and environmental smoke exposure. Symptoms may occur even between episodes. This condition is commonly observed in patients with infantile asthma.

Unclassified wheezing: Wheezing that does not fit the characteristics of the two aforementioned types. It is primarily caused by underlying respiratory diseases, such as bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) and airway malformations. Classification based on symptom episodes has practical significance for guiding clinical treatment. However, evidence suggests that these phenotypes are often not fixed; wheezing phenotypes can transform over time, which has certain limitations in predicting the prognosis of wheezing. 4

Classification based on age at onset and subsequent course

This classification includes transient early wheezing, early‐onset persistent wheezing, and late‐onset wheezing. Transient early wheezing typically has its onset before 3 years of age, markedly decreases after 3 years, and resolves by 6 years. It is more common in preterm infants and children exposed to parental smoking. Early‐onset persistent wheezing also typically has its onset before 3 years of age and persists beyond 6 years, mainly presenting as wheezing triggered by acute viral respiratory infections. Late‐onset wheezing typically has its onset after 3 years and persists beyond 6 years, which falls outside the scope of this guideline. It is important to note that this type of classification is retrospective and cannot be used prospectively to guide clinical treatment, but it holds value for assessing the prognosis of recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers.

Classification based on the presence of atopy

This classification includes allergic wheezing and non‐allergic wheezing. Most cases of allergic wheezing develop into allergic asthma. Non‐allergic wheezing is mostly induced by respiratory infections, with most cases resolving by school age. 5 The 2025 GINA notes that a history of allergic disease or family history is a high‐risk factor for asthma, but is not specific. It is no longer recommended to use atopy as a clue for diagnosing asthma in children aged 5 years and under. 3

Severity grading of recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers

Based on the severity of symptoms during a wheezing episode, recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers can be classified as mild or severe (Table 1). 6 Based on the frequency of wheezing episodes, it can be classified as frequent or intermittent. Frequent episodes are defined as three or more wheezing episodes per quarter, while intermittent episodes are defined as two or fewer episodes per quarter. 2

DIAGNOSIS OF RECURRENT WHEEZING IN INFANTS AND TODDLERS

Etiology of recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers

The etiology of recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers is complex and can be caused by both respiratory and non‐respiratory system diseases (Table 2). Common causes include virus‐induced wheezing (acute asthmatic bronchitis), infantile asthma, and protracted bacterial bronchitis (PBB). Less common causes include BPD, bronchiolitis obliterans (BO), airway developmental abnormalities, and other non‐respiratory system diseases.

Etiology and clinical features of recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers

Key points in clinical information for recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers

Due to the complex etiology and non‐specific symptoms of recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers, it is especially important to establish an accurate diagnosis and differential diagnosis through detailed history taking, comprehensive physical examination, and necessary ancillary tests.

Key points in history taking

In the diagnostic process for recurrent wheezing, clinical history taking is crucial and often provides key information. The clinical history should focus on: age at first wheezing episode; triggers for wheezing onset or exacerbation; rhythmicity of wheezing episodes; severity of wheezing episodes; associated symptoms; relationship between wheezing and feeding; relationship between wheezing and body position; response to medication; presence of other systemic symptoms; past medical history; and personal and family history, particularly key information regarding allergies and asthma (Table 3).

Questions for history‐taking in infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing and clinical diagnostic implications

Key points in physical examination

It is essential to distinguish whether wheezing is expiratory or inspiratory. Inspiratory stridor is often associated with upper airway diseases. Pay attention to mental status, respiratory rate, heart rate, signs of hypoxia, and whether wheezing is diminished or absent to determine the severity of the episode. Asymmetric breath sounds may suggest unilateral airway obstruction. The presence of fixed fine crackles suggests possible pneumonia. Note any extrapulmonary signs such as clubbing, hepatosplenomegaly, growth retardation, or dextrocardia, which may indicate a systemic disease‐causing wheezing, warranting further investigation.

History and physical exam findings suggesting uncommon or severe diseases causing recurrent wheezing

The following findings in the history and physical examination should raise suspicion for uncommon or serious underlying conditions: growth failure, onset in the neonatal period or early infancy, poor response to asthma medications, vomiting accompanied by respiratory symptoms, wheezing episodes unrelated to common triggers, significant pulmonary or cardiac signs on examination, clubbing of fingers/toes, and severe hypoxemia.

Commonly used laboratory and ancillary tests for recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers

Eosinophil count

It is recommended to perform a blood eosinophil (EOS) count in infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing (Strong recommendation).

Elevated blood EOS is a marker of Type 2 inflammatory and are associated with the diagnosis of allergic asthma and the likelihood of asthma development in the future, as well as increased frequency of acute exacerbations in preschool children with wheezing (Evidence level: B). 7 Elevated blood EOS can also serve as a biomarker for a positive response to inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) therapy, helping to identify children with recurrent wheezing who are likely to respond well to ICS maintenance treatment (Evidence level: B). 8 Therefore, it is necessary to perform a blood EOS count in infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing. In China, elevated peripheral blood EOS is currently defined as an absolute count >0.5 × 109/L. 9

Allergen testing

It is recommended to perform allergen testing in infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing (Strong recommendation).

Allergen testing includes in vivo allergen tests (skin prick test) and in vitro tests (specific IgE [sIgE]). A positive allergen test result indicates sensitization to a specific allergen. Early sensitization to inhaled allergens (dust mites, animal dander, mold, etc.) is a high‐risk factor for wheezing in infants and toddlers, and for predicting the development of asthma (Evidence level: B). 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 Sensitization to inhaled allergens can also assist in determining the effectiveness of ICS therapy. 14 Food allergy is not directly correlated with asthma development, but early food allergy in infants and toddlers significantly increases the risk of developing asthma in childhood (Evidence level: C). 15 , 16 Therefore, early allergen testing aids in the management, treatment, and prognosis assessment of infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing.

Chest imaging

It is recommended to routinely perform chest X‐ray imaging in infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing (Strong recommendation). If chest X‐ray findings do not adequately explain the wheezing symptoms or if the response to standardized pharmacological treatment is inadequate, chest computed tomography (CT) is suggested (Weak recommendation).

International guidelines, including GINA, indicate that chest X‐ray examination is helpful for the exclusionary diagnosis of recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers. 3 If considering other causes of recurrent wheezing, such as tracheobronchial anomalies, vascular anomalies, endogenous or exogenous tracheobronchial foreign body, tuberculosis, or interstitial lung disease, chest X‐ray may be insufficient for diagnosis, and chest CT is required. Chinese experts recommend chest CT at an appropriate time for children with difficult‐to‐diagnose wheezing etiology or poor symptom control after treatment (Evidence level: D). 17 When clinical suspicion exists that recurrent wheezing is caused by tracheal compression due to vascular anomalies (e.g., pulmonary artery sling, aberrant left subclavian artery), 18 , 19 contrast‐enhanced chest CT angiography is required (Evidence level: C).

Pulmonary function tests

Pulmonary function tests are recommended for infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing (Weak recommendation).

Infants and toddlers with wheezing may exhibit early pulmonary function impairment. 20 , 21 Some infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing already have permanent pulmonary function impairment by 6 years of age (Evidence level: A). 22 Internationally, the raised volume rapid thoracoabdominal compression technique is primarily used to obtain full forced expiratory flow‐volume curves. In China, tidal breathing pulmonary function is commonly used for this age group. Literature reports that bronchial dilation tests using tidal breathing pulmonary function can also help determine the presence of reversible airflow limitation in infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing. 23 Therefore, performing pulmonary function tests on infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing is helpful for diagnosis and prognosis assessment. It should be noted that pulmonary function testing in children under 3 years is limited by age, with poorer cooperation and accuracy. Its clinical value is lower than in older children and must be interpreted cautiously in conjunction with overall clinical findings.

Fractional exhaled nitric oxide measurement

Fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) measurement is recommended for infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing suspected of having asthma (Strong recommendation).

Exhaled nitric oxide levels correlate with eosinophilic airway inflammation. 24 It is divided into fractional concentration of nasally exhaled nitric oxide (FnNO) and FeNO. FnNO can be used for screening and evaluation of allergic rhinitis, sinusitis, and primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD). Foreign studies indicate that FeNO levels are higher in wheezing infants and toddlers compared to non‐wheezing children, and higher in those sensitized to inhaled allergens compared to non‐sensitized individuals (Evidence level: C). 24 FeNO ≥30 ppb has a sensitivity of 77% and specificity of 94% for predicting wheezing still present at 3 years of age. FeNO's sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value for predicting recurrent wheezing and acute exacerbations are superior to bronchodilator response and the asthma predictive index (API) (Evidence level: B). 25 Therefore, FeNO measurement has a significant reference value for the early diagnosis of asthma in infants and toddlers. FeNO measurement in this age group is generally performed offline. Due to difficulties in exhaled gas collection, its clinical application is not yet widespread in China.

Respiratory pathogen testing

Testing for respiratory viruses is recommended during acute exacerbations of recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers (Weak recommendation). When recurrent wheezing is suspected to be associated with bacterial, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, or Chlamydia pneumoniae infections, bacterial culture of respiratory secretions or nucleic acid testing for atypical pathogens is recommended (Weak recommendation). Respiratory viral infections are the primary trigger for acute exacerbations of recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers, most commonly respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) and rhinovirus (RV), 26 as well as human metapneumovirus (hMPV), influenza virus, parainfluenza virus, and others. Respiratory virus testing helps differentiate virus‐induced wheezing from wheezing due to other causes and holds some value in predicting the risk of asthma development in infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing. In children with conditions such as PBB, bronchiectasis, PCD, cystic fibrosis (CF), and primary immunodeficiency disease (PID), acute wheezing exacerbations are often due to bacterial infections in the airways. Sputum or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) culture can identify the causative pathogen and guide antibiotic therapy. Therefore, bacterial culture of respiratory secretions is recommended in such cases.

24‐h esophageal pH monitoring

24‐h esophageal pH monitoring is recommended for infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing when gastroesophageal reflux (GER) is suspected (Weak recommendation).

GER is associated with chronic cough and recurrent wheezing. Studies indicate that approximately 20% of infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing have GER, with a possibly higher incidence in infants under 1 year (Evidence level: C). 27 The positive rate of 24‐h esophageal pH monitoring is significantly higher in infants and toddlers with asthma‐like symptoms (wheezing, cough) compared to asymptomatic children, and clinical symptoms significantly improve after acid suppression therapy (Evidence level: D). 28 Therefore, 24‐h esophageal pH monitoring is suggested for infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing who have a poor response to conventional asthma medications and in whom GER cannot be ruled out.

Bronchoscopy

Bronchoscopy is recommended for infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing that is refractory to medical treatment (Weak recommendation).

Bronchoscopy aids in the differential diagnosis of recurrent wheezing etiology. Among infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing refractory to bronchodilators or corticosteroids, over 50% have airway anatomical abnormalities such as tracheomalacia and/or bronchomalacia, or tracheal compression. 29 Positive rates for bacterial culture (including Moraxella catarrhalis, Haemophilus influenzae, Staphylococcus aureus, etc.), viral detection, and detection of lipid‐laden macrophages (suggesting aspiration) in BALF are significantly increased (Evidence level: B). 30 , 31 Therefore, bronchoscopy is necessary for infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing refractory to standard medical treatment, when airway abnormalities such as malacia, stenosis, or compression are suspected.

Swallowing function assessment

Swallowing function assessment is recommended for infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing who have high‐risk factors for swallowing dysfunction (Weak recommendation).

Swallowing dysfunction or incoordination of sucking, swallowing, and breathing increases the risk of aspiration in infants and toddlers (especially under 1 year), leading to recurrent lower respiratory tract infections and wheezing. 32 High‐risk factors for swallowing dysfunction include cerebral palsy, epileptic encephalopathy, neuromuscular diseases, chromosomal disorders, etc. Assessment methods suitable for this age group include clinical evaluation, modified barium swallow study, videofluoroscopic swallow study, and fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing. Studies show that in infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing diagnosed with swallowing dysfunction via videofluoroscopic swallow study, multi‐method rehabilitation interventions improved swallowing function and correspondingly improved or resolved respiratory symptoms (Evidence level: D). 33 Additionally, increasing food viscosity can also improve respiratory and gastrointestinal symptoms in infants and toddlers with swallowing dysfunction (Evidence level: D). 34 Therefore, swallowing function assessment and intervention are recommended for infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing and high‐risk factors for swallowing dysfunction to reduce recurrent lower respiratory tract infections and wheezing due to aspiration.

Genetic testing

Genetic screening can be selectively considered for infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing when a monogenic disorder is suspected (Weak recommendation).

Clinically, rare monogenic disorders such as PCD, CF, and PID often lack typical clinical manifestations in infancy and early childhood and may primarily present with recurrent or persistent cough and wheezing. When clinical presentation is atypical and diagnosis is difficult, genetic testing is an important diagnostic tool. It must be emphasized that a negative genetic test does not completely rule out the diagnosis. If a novel mutation is detected, its pathogenicity needs to be judged in conjunction with clinical findings. 35 For children with recurrent wheezing, screening for multiple asthma susceptibility gene loci (e.g., specific variants in toll‐like receptor [TLR] genes such as TLR4, G protein‐coupled receptor genes, tumor necrosis factor‐alpha, and interleukin‐13) 36 may improve the accuracy of predicting childhood asthma, but the correlation between these loci and asthma requires further clinical validation. Therefore, routine genetic testing is not recommended for infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing.

Etiological diagnosis of recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers

The most common causes of recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers are virus‐induced wheezing (acute asthmatic bronchitis), infantile asthma, and PBB. 37 For children whose clinical presentation is not consistent with common wheezing disorders, particularly those with poor response to conventional pharmacologic therapy, severe illness, or special medical history and physical signs, it is essential to strongly consider the possibility of other disorders, including uncommon or even rare conditions, as the cause of recurrent wheezing. Corresponding investigations should be performed to establish a definitive diagnosis.

Virus‐induced wheezing/acute asthmatic bronchitis

This condition primarily affects infants under 2 years of age and is an acute lower respiratory tract infection caused by viruses such as RSV, RV, and parainfluenza virus. Symptoms may include fever, nasal congestion, rhinorrhea, cough, wheezing, and tachypnea. Auscultation may reveal wheezing and crackles. Chest X‐ray may show increased and disorganized lung markings in both lungs, possibly with hyperinflation. The course is generally <10 days, with 2–3 episodes per year. Most children experience a significant reduction in episodes after 3 years of age.

Infantile asthma

The Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Optimal Management of Asthma in Children (2025) 6 recommend that for children under 6 years of age with recurrent wheezing, diagnosis should primarily rely on clinical clues, judged comprehensively based on asthma risk factors, severity, response to anti‐asthma treatment, atopic genetic background, and respiratory pathogens. The following clinical features highly suggest asthma, and standardized asthma treatment can be initiated: 1) Recurrent wheezing and cough often triggered by acute respiratory infections, exercise, laughter or crying, exposure to inhaled allergens, cold air, or smoke, or occurring without obvious respiratory infection; 2) After resolution of acute wheezing symptoms, a diagnostic trial of low‐dose ICS (with as‐needed short‐acting beta2 agonist [SABA]) for 8–12 weeks leads to significant clinical improvement, with recurrence or worsening upon discontinuation; 3) Presence of other allergic diseases, or a first‐ or second‐degree relative with asthma history, or sensitization to inhaled allergens; 4) Exclusion of other diseases causing wheezing, cough, shortness of breath, or chest tightness. The 2025 GINA further specifies that the diagnosis of asthma in children aged 5 years and under requires meeting all three of the following criteria: 1) At least two acute wheezing episodes in the past 12 months, or at least one acute wheezing episode accompanied by asthma‐like symptoms between episodes (e.g., episodic dry cough, with symptoms worsening during sleep or after laughing, crying, or physical activity); 2) No likely alternative cause of the respiratory symptoms; and 3) Timely clinical response to asthma treatment. If one or more of the above criteria are not met, a provisional diagnosis of “suspected asthma” is made, and asthma treatment should be considered, with regular assessment of the response to medication and/or symptom changes over time.

PBB

PBB in infants and toddlers primarily presents as a persistent wet cough and recurrent wheezing. Its diagnosis relies on meeting both clinical and microbiological criteria. The clinical criteria require a wet cough persisting for more than 4 weeks, which shows significant improvement after over 2 weeks of antibiotic therapy, with other causes of recurrent wheezing excluded. The microbiological diagnostic criteria include a wet cough lasting more than 4 weeks, evidence of lower respiratory tract infection demonstrated by a positive bacterial culture from sputum or BALF with a colony count of at least 104 CFU/mL, and significant improvement in cough following more than 2 weeks of antibiotic therapy. 38

TREATMENT OF RECURRENT WHEEZING IN INFANTS AND TODDLERS

In most cases, the etiology of recurrent wheezing can be identified through detailed history taking, physical examination, and relevant diagnostic investigations. For children with a clear etiology, standardized treatment according to disease‐specific guidelines and consensus is recommended, along with regular follow‐up and management. For children in whom the cause of wheezing cannot be immediately determined, symptomatic relief treatment is provided during acute exacerbations, followed by regular follow‐up and assessment. If wheezing episodes are frequent or severe, in addition to symptomatic relief during acute exacerbations, low‐dose ICS maintenance therapy for 2–3 months during remission periods is recommended, with regular follow‐up assessments.

Treatment for acute exacerbations of recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers

ICS

The use of ICS is recommended for infants and toddlers with severe acute wheezing exacerbations or suspected asthma attacks (Strong recommendation).

For mild viral‐induced wheezing episodes, even high‐dose ICS show no significant difference compared to placebo in terms of symptom duration, duration of bronchodilator use, requirement for oral corticosteroids (OCS) therapy, or hospital admission (Evidence level: A). 39 For severe viral‐induced wheezing episodes, the ICS group had significantly lower wheezing symptom scores than the placebo group, with the placebo group experiencing more frequent severe cough and severe wheezing (Evidence level: A). 40 Furthermore, the proportion of children requiring rescue systemic corticosteroids was significantly lower in the ICS group compared to the placebo group (Evidence level: B). 41 In children at high risk for asthma experiencing acute exacerbations, the ICS group had a higher proportion of episode‐free days and less need for systemic corticosteroids compared to the placebo group (Evidence level: A). 42 A systematic review on ICS use during acute wheezing also indicated that for virus‐induced wheezing, ICS does not prevent the development of asthma. Their use should therefore be guided by the child's atopic status, with ICS reserved for those at high risk of asthma. 43 Therefore, ICS are recommended for infants and toddlers with severe wheezing exacerbations or suspected asthma episodes. Dosage, frequency, and duration should be adjusted based on disease severity and symptom improvement.

Bronchodilators

The use of SABA is recommended for acute wheezing exacerbations in infants and toddlers (Strong recommendation).

SABA may help improve symptoms in infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing caused by RV or RSV infection, onset outside RSV epidemic months, or those with atopic dermatitis or a first‐degree family history of asthma (Evidence level: C). 44 Inhaled SABA during acute wheezing exacerbations can improve respiratory rate, symptom scores, and oxygen saturation, but shows no significant benefit on hospital admission or wheezing recurrence (Evidence level: A). 45 Studies have confirmed that the vast majority of acute wheezing exacerbations in infants and toddlers require inhaled SABA treatment (Evidence level: A). 46 Therefore, SABA is recommended for acute exacerbations of recurrent wheezing in this population. Albuterol aerosol or nebulizer solution is suggested, with frequency adjusted based on symptoms. For moderate or severe acute exacerbations, administering SABA every 20 min for up to three doses followed by re‐assessment of severity may be considered. 3 For children unable to receive nebulized therapy, oral SABA (e.g., procaterol, albuterol tablets/orodispersible tablets) can be given. When using nebulized albuterol, combination with ICS and/or inhaled ipratropium bromide may be considered based on clinical needs, potentially aiding faster control of airway inflammation and relief of wheezing symptoms.

Leukotriene receptor antagonists

Leukotriene receptor antagonists (LTRA) can be administered to infants and toddlers during acute wheezing exacerbations (Weak recommendation).

Acute asthma attacks or respiratory viral infections can lead to elevated levels of cysteinyl leukotrienes in the airway. As anti‐inflammatory agents, LTRAs reduce airway inflammation and have some bronchodilatory properties. A study on moderate‐to‐severe preschool wheezing found that, compared to placebo, LTRA showed no significant effect on symptom‐free days, OCS use, healthcare utilization, quality of life, or hospitalization in children regardless of API positivity, but it significantly improved activity scores and symptom scores during wheezing episodes (Evidence level: B). 47 Some studies suggest that LTRA is effective in alleviating virus‐induced or asthma‐triggered wheezing symptoms in preschool children (Evidence level: C). 48 The 2025 GINA recommends LTRA as one of the initial home management options for acute asthma exacerbations in preschool children. 3 Therefore, LTRA is recommended to alleviate symptoms during acute wheezing exacerbations in infants and toddlers. However, parents/caregivers should be informed about the potential adverse effects of LTRA on sleep, behavior, and mental health. 49 It is also important to note that the current use of LTRAs in China is approved for children over 1 year old; use in infants under 1 year is off‐label.

Systemic corticosteroids

Systemic corticosteroids are not recommended for mild acute wheezing exacerbations; they may be considered for severe acute exacerbations (Weak recommendation).

Most research on systemic corticosteroids for recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers involves OCS, with limited evidence for intravenous use. For virus‐induced wheezing (acute asthmatic bronchitis) in infants and toddlers, OCS in children with an atopic constitution shows no improvement in symptom relief, rescue success rates, or hospital admission rates (Evidence level: A). 50 In most cases, OCS is ineffective for treating acute exacerbations of recurrent wheezing, with no significant difference between the OCS and placebo groups regarding hospitalization, length of hospital stay, or rehospitalization within the following month (Evidence level: B). 51 However, for children with a history of asthma, OCS may have a potentially clinically relevant impact on hospital length of stay. 52 OCS treatment may be considered for severe wheezing exacerbations that are highly suggestive of asthma or unresponsive to bronchodilators (Evidence level: B). 53

Antimicrobial agents

Routine use of antimicrobial agents is not recommended for acute exacerbations of recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers (Strong recommendation). Antimicrobial therapy is recommended when conditions such as PBB or coexisting respiratory bacterial/atypical pathogen infections are suspected (Strong recommendation).

Respiratory viral infections are the most common triggers for acute exacerbations of recurrent wheezing; therefore, routine antibiotic treatment is not needed. 54 Antibiotic therapy is recommended when a diagnosis such as PBB, PCD, or CF is considered, and wheezing is caused by a respiratory bacterial infection. If wheezing is confirmed to be caused by atypical pathogens like M. pneumoniae or C. pneumoniae, macrolide antibiotics may be added. Some studies suggest that azithromycin can shorten symptom duration during acute exacerbations in infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing (Evidence level: A). 55 However, a recent meta‐analysis indicates limited evidence supporting the use of macrolides during acute exacerbations in children with recurrent wheezing (Evidence level: A). 56

Treatment during remission periods of recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers

ICS maintenance therapy

Low‐dose ICS maintenance therapy is recommended during remission periods for infants and toddlers diagnosed with asthma, those experiencing severe wheezing exacerbations, or those with frequent wheezing episodes (Weak recommendation).

There is no unified protocol for ICS maintenance therapy during remission periods for recurrent wheezing in this population. 1) For children diagnosed with asthma, follow guideline‐recommended ICS maintenance regimens. 2) For children suspected of having infantile asthma, daily ICS can reduce wheezing episodes. 57 A 2‐year randomized, double‐blind, multicenter study showed that daily inhaled fluticasone significantly reduced wheezing episodes in high‐risk toddlers, especially those with aeroallergen sensitization. 58 3) Children with severe wheezing exacerbations are advised to receive low‐dose ICS maintenance therapy. The first efficacy assessment should be conducted after 8–12 weeks of treatment. Therapy can be discontinued after at least 3 months of symptom control or after 3 months of absence of symptoms. 59 4) For children with frequent wheezing episodes, high‐dose ICS can be used for 7–10 days at the onset of a respiratory infection, followed by low‐dose ICS maintenance therapy for 2–3 months (Evidence level: B), 4 , 60 which can be stopped if asymptomatic. It is important to note that frequent use of high‐dose ICS in infants and toddlers may increase the risk of adverse events, necessitating careful identification of indications (Evidence level: B). 59

LTRA

LTRA maintenance therapy can be administered during remission periods in children with asthma or suspected asthma (Weak recommendation).

The 2025 GINA states that, compared with placebo, LTRAs can moderately reduce symptoms and need for OCS in preschool children whose asthma symptoms are not well controlled or who have experienced one or more severe exacerbations in the previous year. 3 However, LTRA does not reduce the number of severe acute exacerbations requiring systemic corticosteroid treatment (Evidence level: A). 61 For preschool children with asthma or recurrent wheezing, daily ICS is more effective than regular LTRA monotherapy in improving symptom control and reducing exacerbations (Evidence Level: A). 62 , 63 Another study involving over 3000 children with recurrent wheezing showed that maintenance therapy with LTRA was ineffective in reducing virus‐induced wheezing episodes (Evidence level: A). 61 Physicians should weigh the benefits and risks before prescribing LTRA and explain its potential adverse effects on sleep and behavior to parents/caregivers. 64

MANAGEMENT OF RECURRENT WHEEZING IN INFANTS AND TODDLERS

Recurrent wheezing episodes in infants and toddlers are detrimental to their growth, development, physical and mental health; adversely affect long‐term pulmonary function; and increase the health‐economic burden on families and society. For all infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing, regardless of etiology, it is recommended to regularly assess and manage type 2 inflammatory diseases, environmental factors, and nutritional status, and to establish a long‐term follow‐up plan.

Type 2 inflammatory disease

Assessment and management of type 2 inflammatory disease is recommended for infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing (Strong recommendation).

Type 2 inflammation is an immune response triggered by allergens or harmful substances that stimulate innate and adaptive immunity. It is characterized by elevated type 2 cytokines, EOS, total IgE (tIgE), and/or sIgE. 49 , 65 This inflammation is the common pathophysiological mechanism underlying various allergic diseases, and a variety of Type 2 inflammatory diseases often exist simultaneously. 66 Type 2 inflammatory diseases in infants and toddlers include atopic dermatitis, bronchial asthma, allergic rhinitis, and food allergy. 67 Research on childhood asthma phenotypes indicates that type 2 inflammation can originate early in life and persist lifelong. 68 The main biomarkers for type 2 inflammation include blood or sputum EOS, FeNO, tIgE, and sIgE. 65 Clinically, the presence of Type 2 inflammation is determined by whether these biomarkers are elevated (Evidence level: A). Although standardized criteria for assessing type 2 inflammation in this age group are not yet established, Type 2 inflammatory diseases in infancy and toddlers increase the risk of long‐term asthma in children, and asthma severity correlates with the severity of the type 2 inflammatory disease (Evidence level: B). 69 Therefore, assessing and managing type 2 inflammatory diseases is crucial for effectively controlling wheezing symptoms and evaluating long‐term prognosis in this population.

Environmental assessment

Regular environmental assessment and management are recommended for infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing (Strong recommendation).

Results from multiple prospective cohort and retrospective case‐control studies confirm that exposure to inhaled allergens, home living environment, and passive smoking are risk factors for recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers (Evidence level: B). 70 , 71 , 72 Addressing these risk factors through appropriate measures can reduce the risk of recurrent wheezing. Recommended preventive measures include: 1) Reducing exposure to environmental aeroallergens, especially house dust mites, by measures such as frequent bedding changes, washing bedding and clothes in hot water (≥55°C), vacuuming, avoiding carpets and filled furniture/toys, minimizing indoor ornaments, using high‐efficiency particulate air filters in the child's bedroom, and ensuring adequate ventilation, etc; 2) Improving living conditions by avoiding excessively lumid environments, regular dehumidification, improving ventilation, and preventing/removing mold (Evidence level: D); 73 , 74 , 75 3) Avoiding contact with pet for infants and toddlers allergic to animal dandruff. (Evidence level: D); 76 , 77 , 78 4) Encouraging parents to quit smoking and avoiding exposure to smoke‐filled environments (Evidence Level: B). 71 , 72

Nutritional assessment

Regular nutritional assessment and management are recommended for infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing (Strong recommendation).

Research indicates that the prevalence of asthma is significantly higher in children with obesity, and the risk of asthma correlates with the degree of obesity. 79 Furthermore, children diagnosed with asthma at 3–4 years of age have nearly twice the risk of developing obesity by 8 years of age compared to their peers. 80 As previously mentioned, early food allergy increases the risk of developing asthma. 15 Research shows that approximately 45% of children with asthma have food sensitization, and sensitized children have higher hospitalization rates due to asthma exacerbations. 81 Conversely, children with food allergies have a higher prevalence of underweight status (Evidence level: B). 82 Additionally, some underlying causes of recurrent wheezing—such as BO, interstitial lung disease, GER, congenital heart disease, PCD, PID, CF, and other diseases—will lead to growth impairment due to high basal energy expenditure, increased energy consumption from recurrent infections, or malabsorption. Adequate caloric and energy support is essential for normal development and immune function in these cases. Therefore, regular nutritional assessment and management are recommended for infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing, tailored to the underlying etiology.

Long‐term follow‐up plan

Developing a long‐term management and follow‐up plan is recommended for infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing (Strong recommendation).

1) For children diagnosed with asthma, asthma self‐management education should be provided to their family members and caregivers. This includes basic knowledge about the disease (e.g., pathogenesis, common triggers, and influencing factors), training on correct inhalation technique, emphasizing the importance of medication adherence, and providing a printed version of asthma action plan. 3 2) For children with suspected asthma or other causes of recurrent wheezing, follow‐up every 1–2 months to assess wheezing episodes, medication use, living environment, and its management; provide health education on wheezing and allergic diseases to parents and children every 2–3 months; and conduct pulmonary function monitoring every 3–6 months, while following up on the control of co‐existing type 2 inflammatory diseases and monitoring relevant laboratory indicators. Given the instability of recurrent wheezing phenotypes in this age group, phenotypes may change over time (Evidence level: B). 83 Clinical management should be adjusted promptly based on potential evolution (Evidence level: A). 84 A meta‐analysis on the management of infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing showed that providing parents and children with repeated, long‐term health education can increase symptom‐free days and improve the quality of life. 85 Considering the impact of early recurrent wheezing on pulmonary function at 6 years of age (Evidence level: A), 69 , 74 , 83 regular pulmonary function follow‐up and pulmonary rehabilitation exercises are beneficial for the recovery of infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing.

PREVENTION OF RECURRENT WHEEZING IN INFANTS AND TODDLERS

Currently, there is a lack of effective pharmacological preventive measures against recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers. Immunomodulators can serve as adjunctive therapy for wheezing triggered by respiratory infections, while the preventive roles of vitamin D and probiotics lack substantial evidence from clinical studies.

Immunomodulators

Immunomodulators can be used as adjunctive therapy for recurrent wheezing triggered by respiratory infections in infants and toddlers (Weak recommendation).

Respiratory infections are the primary trigger for acute exacerbations of recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers. Commonly used immunomodulators in children include microbial preparations, synthetic chemicals, and biological agents. They work by enhancing immune function and promoting immune balance to reduce respiratory infections, thereby playing a positive preventive role against wheezing. 86 , 87 Bacterial lysates can correct the Th1/Th2 lymphocyte imbalance, induce broad immune responses, promote the maturation of the child's immune system, 88 reduce susceptibility to various respiratory pathogens, 89 and decrease the number of wheezing episodes, symptom duration, and symptom medication scores (Evidence level: B). 90 They can be used for primary prevention of wheezing in infants (Evidence level: D). 91 As an adjunct to standard therapy, bacterial lysates in children with wheezing are associated with lower overall treatment costs, greater gains, good tolerability, and few adverse effects (Evidence level: D). 92 Other immunomodulators, such as pidotimod and transfer factor, may be beneficial in reducing recurrent wheezing by potentially treating and preventing recurrent respiratory infections, but more high‐quality clinical studies are needed to confirm the efficacy. 93

Vitamin D

Vitamin D supplementation is not recommended as an adjunctive therapy for preventing wheezing in infants and toddlers (Weak recommendation).

Research on the preventive effect of vitamin D supplementation for recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers is limited, and the results are inconsistent. Some studies suggest that maintaining 25‐hydroxyvitamin D levels above 30 ng/mL during pregnancy may reduce the probability of asthma or recurrent wheezing in offspring at 3 years of age (Evidence level: B). 94 However, other randomized controlled trials comparing standard‐dose and high‐dose vitamin D supplementation during pregnancy failed to obtain similar results regarding its preventive effect on childhood wheezing (Evidence level: B). 95 , 96 Therefore, there is insufficient evidence to support vitamin D as an adjunctive therapy for preventing recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers.

Probiotics

Probiotics are not recommended as an adjunctive therapy for preventing recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers (Weak recommendation).

Animal studies demonstrate that probiotics such as Lactobacillus and Lactobacillus rhamnosus have significant immunomodulatory effects. 97 , 98 However, no evidence supports a protective association between perinatal probiotic use and physician‐diagnosed asthma or childhood wheezing (Evidence level: A). 99 Supplementing infants and toddlers with probiotics, especially mixed strains, can reduce the relative risk of eczema but shows no significant preventive effect against wheezing, asthma, or rhinoconjunctivitis (Evidence Level: A). 100 Due to substantial variations in probiotic products regarding strain types, combinations, and dosages, their efficacy is often difficult to assess. Thus, insufficient evidence supports the use of probiotics as adjunctive therapy for preventing recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers.

LTRAs

LTRAs are not routinely recommended for prophylactic therapy of recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers (Strong recommendation).

Currently, insufficient evidence supports LTRAs as prophylactic therapy for recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers (Evidence level: B). 101 Compared to daily ICS, LTRAs are not effective in improving symptom control or reducing the number of acute wheezing exacerbations in preschool children with asthma or recurrent wheezing (Evidence level: B). 62 However, LTRAs can alleviate symptoms of allergic rhinitis. The 2016 Allergic Rhinitis and its Impact on Asthma guidelines recommend LTRAs for the prevention of children with allergic rhinitis and concomitant asthma (Evidence level: B). 102 Therefore, LTRAs are not recommended for routine preventive treatment of recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers, but may be considered for prophylactic use in cases complicated by allergic rhinitis.

Antihistamines

Antihistamines are not recommended for prophylactic therapy of recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers (Strong recommendation).

Research on the preventive effect of antihistamines on recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers is scarce. Some simple case‐control studies suggest that preventive oral administration of ketotifen or loratadine for 2–3 months may reduce wheezing episodes (Evidence level: D). 73 Long‐term use of cetirizine in children allergic to dust mites can reduce symptoms related to allergic rhinitis and wheezing and decrease the use of ICS, bronchodilators, and antibiotics (Evidence level: C). 85 However, literature from the past decade exploring antihistamines as preventive drugs for acute exacerbations of recurrent wheezing is limited and of poor quality. Therefore, insufficient evidence supports the use of antihistamines as prophylactic therapy for recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers.

PROGNOSIS OF RECURRENT WHEEZING IN INFANTS AND TODDLERS

Generally, the prognosis of recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers is determined by its etiology. However, the prognosis is easily influenced by multiple factors such as age, genetics, environment, pathogens, and allergies. Various triggers or etiologies can accumulate in the same child or appear sequentially over time. Therefore, the prognosis of recurrent wheezing carries uncertainty, and reliable indicators or tools for predicting long‐term outcomes are lacking.

Wheezing phenotypes

Wheezing phenotypes have predictive value for the prognosis of recurrent wheezing but have limitations (Weak recommendation).

Previously, there has been a tendency to classify recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers into episodic viral wheezing (EVW) and multiple trigger wheezing (MTW), as this classification aided in predicting the prognosis of wheezing in this population. In infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing who do not have an atopic constitution, the symptoms mostly resolve by school age. 10 Viral infections can cause severe wheezing episodes, and their frequency may correlate with asthma. 78 However, wheezing phenotypes in infants and toddlers can change significantly within a year (Evidence level: C). 79 Although EVW is considered self‐limiting, and MTW carries the potential to develop into asthma, their phenotypic classification can still change over time. This is because the triggers for both conditions overlap, and as children age, their classification can be influenced by a variety of individual factors, including nutrition, environment, infections, and immune status. Clinical classification of wheezing has a lag in guiding clinical management and limited value for prognosis prediction.

Asthma risk factors

Assessing asthma risk factors aids in predicting the prognosis of recurrent wheezing (Weak recommendation).

Asthma is a leading cause of wheezing in infants and toddlers, and its onset frequently occurs before 3 years of age. Studies show that about one‐third of infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing develop asthma by school age. 103 High‐risk factors for asthma development include parental history of asthma, atopic dermatitis, sensitization to inhaled allergens, allergic rhinitis, wheezing unrelated to respiratory infections, peripheral blood EOS >4%, and allergies to milk, eggs, or peanuts (Evidence level: B). 83 Early sensitization to multiple allergens is an important risk factor for asthma development (Evidence level: C). 104 Respiratory infections with RSV and/or RV, prematurity, and severe wheezing episodes are also risk factors. 105 Therefore, assessing asthma risk factors in infants and toddlers with recurrent wheezing is necessary.

Asthma prediction tools

Combining multiple asthma prediction methods aids in the early identification of asthma in infants and toddlers (Strong recommendation). Diagnosing asthma in preschool children has been challenging, prompting exploration of prediction tools or diagnostic models by experts worldwide. Three commonly used international methods for predicting the development of asthma in children with wheezing are as follows: 1) the API and modified API (mAPI); 84 , 106 2) the asthma prediction tool, which assesses the probability of developing asthma five years later in infants and toddlers who have experienced wheezing between 1 and 3 years of age; 107 3) the clinical asthma prediction score (range 0–55), developed using data from the Prevention and Incidence of Asthma and Mite Allergy (PIAMA) birth cohort, predicts the risk of asthma at 7–8 years of age. 108 Due to the poor predictive accuracy and significant variability in sensitivity and positive predictive value of these three methods, 109 their combined use is more helpful for early identification of asthma. Domestic research has also proposed a prediction model for preschool asthma 109 : cumulative wheezing episodes ≥4 (3 points), presence of reversible airflow limitation (3 points), presence of allergic rhinitis and/or atopic dermatitis (1 point), first‐degree relative with allergy history (1 point), positive in vivo or in vitro allergen test (1 point). A score ≥4 points can diagnose asthma, but more reliable prospective clinical studies are needed for confirmation (Evidence level: C).

Respiratory viral infections

RSV and RV infections increase the risk of asthma in school‐age children (Weak recommendation).

Extensive research indicates that viral infections can trigger recurrent wheezing, especially in atopic children. This is related to factors such as damage to the respiratory mucosal barrier following infection, activation of inflammatory responses, development of airway hyperresponsiveness, and bronchospasm, which are also important triggers for asthma exacerbations. Virus detection rates can be as high as 85%–95% in children with recurrent wheezing or asthma exacerbations. 27 RSV and RV infections carry the highest risk for subsequent asthma development in infants and toddlers, with RV posing a relatively higher risk (Evidence level: C). 110 , 111 A birth cohort study found a 90% virus detection rate in children with wheezing within the first 3 years of life. RSV, RV, or co‐infection with both were associated with an increased risk of asthma at 6 years of age. 112 RV‐induced bronchiolitis is more likely than RSV‐induced bronchiolitis to lead to recurrent wheezing (Evidence level: B) 113 and increases the risk of rehospitalization. 114

Long‐term pulmonary function

Recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers can impair long‐term pulmonary function (Strong recommendation).

Multiple cohort studies indicate that infants and toddlers with early recurrent wheezing exhibit persistent pulmonary function impairment by 6 years of age (Evidence level: A). 23 Regardless of subsequent asthma development, early recurrent wheezing can cause airway obstruction, leading to lower pulmonary function values at 16 years of age compared to healthy controls (Evidence level: A). 115 Adolescents and adults with a history of recurrent wheezing before age 6 have, on average, an approximately 10% lower FEV1 and a 5% lower FEV1/FVC ratio than their peers without such a history. This impairment may be established by 4 years of age or earlier (Evidence level: A). 5 Different wheezing phenotypes affect pulmonary function to varying degrees, with persistent wheezing having the most significant impact (Evidence level: B). 84 The PIAMA cohort study demonstrated that infants and toddlers with different wheezing phenotypes had lower FEV1 levels at 8 years than those who seldom or never wheezed, especially those with persistent wheezing. 116 Infants and toddlers with MTW can show significant pulmonary function abnormalities at 4–6 years, whereas those with EVW are less affected (Evidence level: C). 117

Despite the complex and diverse etiology, lack of symptom specificity, and diagnostic challenges of recurrent wheezing in infants and toddlers, the cause can be identified through detailed history taking, physical examination, necessary ancillary tests, and regular follow‐up assessments. Corresponding etiology‐specific and symptomatic treatment and prevention can then be implemented. Since the pathogenesis of recurrent wheezing is not fully understood, triggers are diverse, and there is a long‐term risk of asthma development and pulmonary function impairment, the importance of long‐term management and follow‐up plans for this population is particularly emphasized. Future research should focus on the type 2 inflammatory mechanisms in recurrent wheezing, risk factors, and interventions for asthma development, long‐term pulmonary function impact and outcomes, and the clinical efficacy evaluation of RSV monoclonal antibodies and vaccines for preventing and treating infection‐induced recurrent wheezing.
