Hierarchical lineage tracing reveals diverse pathways of cytarabine resistance
Authors: Rachel Saxe, Hannah Stuart, Abigail C. Marshall, Fahiima Abdullahi, Zoë Chen, Francesco Emiliani, Aaron McKenna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cancer cells adapt to treatment, leading to the emergence of clones that are more aggressive and resistant to anti-cancer therapies. We have a limited understanding of resistance mechanisms as we lack technologies to map cancer evolution under the selective pressure of treatment. To address this, we present a hierarchical, dynamic lineage-tracing method, FLARE (Following Lineage Adaptation and Resistance Evolution). We use FLARE to track the progression of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) cell lines treated with Cytarabine (AraC), a front-line treatment in AML, both in vitro and in vivo. We map distinct cellular lineages in both murine and human AML cell lines that are predisposed to AraC resistance. Using FLARE, we identify treatment-naïve populations responsible for seeding resistance that are characterized by upregulation of stemness markers and a cell adhesion-associated AraC-resistant lineage signature. We find that expression of this signature in pediatric AML is associated with the expansion of HSC-like malignant cells at relapse and significantly shorter overall survival. These findings underscore the role of pre-existing lineage states in driving relapse and establish FLARE as a platform for uncovering the evolving, heritable transcriptional programs that underlie tumor evolution.

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a heterogeneous hematological malignancy that has served as a model of clonal evolution in response to treatment1–4. In recent years, targeted treatments and intensified chemotherapy regimens have led to improved outcomes for many patients5–7. From the advent of FLT3 inhibitors to the introduction of venetoclax to standard chemotherapy, there is an emerging landscape of new treatment options for AML patients6,8,9. Despite this, survival rates remain low, especially for patients without well-characterized cytogenetic alterations and mutations5–7,10. Disease heterogeneity, high relapse rates, and propensity for drug resistance are still pressing issues11,12. This challenge is amplified in pediatric patients where well-characterized alterations, such as those in the NPM1, IDH1/2, and DNMT3A genes are less frequent7,10.

Upon diagnosis, most adult and pediatric patients, regardless of disease classification, are treated with an induction regimen including Cytarabine (AraC) and an anthracycline13. While induction therapy achieves complete remission in up to 80% of patients, defined as fewer than 5% blast cells in the bone marrow14, most patients will relapse15. Furthermore, relapsed AML tends to be less responsive to chemotherapy, with hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT) being the primary curative option15. Fewer than half of patients will survive 2 years post-salvage HSCT, with leukemia-free survival around 20%16. The high rate of recurrence and limited treatment options for relapsed disease underscore the importance of understanding AML treatment failure.

AML subtypes and risk categories have long been primarily defined by cytogenetics, mutation status, and structural genomic variations. With recent advances in sequencing, transcriptomic profiling has begun to uncover leukemic disease states, dependencies, and prognostic signatures17–19. Such discoveries pave the way for novel targeted treatment strategies for disease subtypes that lack well-defined mutational profiles. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) of matched pre- and post-treatment bone marrow samples shows a dramatic change following AraC treatment2. However, it remains unclear how resistance to AraC evolves in many AML patients. Are AraC-resistant subclones pre-existing, or does resistance emerge de novo in rare subpopulations? Do resistant phenotypes pass through multiple intermediate states or emerge in a punctuated evolutionary step20?

Many studies have investigated the origins of chemoresistant cell populations in various cancer types, including AML21–25. Previous efforts have characterized pre-existing, stem-like populations at diagnosis that drive relapse in AML26. Yan et al. identified a subpopulation of TP53-mutated AML cells in treatment-naïve samples believed to be the source of future AraC-resistant populations21. Shaffer et al. concluded that rare subpopulations of melanoma cells could transiently establish resistant phenotypes upon treatment23. These studies highlight that treatment failure and resistance are driven by diverse disease, drug, and likely, patient-specific mechanisms. Characterizing the breadth of resistant phenotypes in cancer will allow for better treatment monitoring and response prediction.

CRISPR lineage recording has been previously used to characterize clonal evolution during tumorigenesis and metastasis; however, the effect of treatment has yet to be explored27,28.

We set out to develop a cellular lineage tracing system to record the evolutionary steps of AraC treatment resistance in AML. Here, we present FLARE (Following Lineage Adaptation and Resistance Evolution), a multi-tiered lineage tracing approach, which we use to identify clonal patterns of drug response and expansion in vitro and in vivo. Using advances in CRISPR lineage tracing, we track the evolution of cell fate independently of cell state, recording the relationship among cells in small, integrated recorders that can be used to recover cellular lineage trees28,29.

Here, we use FLARE to trace multiple heritable, lineage-specific gene modules associated with AraC resistance. We identified a lineage-dependent upregulation of immunoproteasome subunits, with further upregulation leading to improved expansion in vivo. FLARE lineage patterns also show a highly heritable gene signature in stem marker-expressing treatment-naïve AML cells, leading to lineage branch survival and expansion under AraC exposure. This signature can distinguish AraC-resistant populations prior to the onset of treatment. In primary pediatric AML samples, signature upregulation correlates with the expansion of HSC-like malignant cells. Furthermore, heightened expression of this signature at diagnosis is predictive of significantly shorter overall survival. Together, these results support the utility of FLARE lineage recording in identifying heritable patterns of drug resistance.

Results

Hierarchical lineage recording in AML

Building on our previous macsGESTALT lineage tracing28, we created a hierarchical system that captures lineage information at three levels (Fig. 1A, B). At the top level, FLARE encodes founder populations using a unique combination of static tags accompanying the lineage recorders integrated into the genome. Within each labeled founder population, individual clones are divided into sub-populations with a second round of unique identifiers that are integrated with the Cas9 cassette. Cas9 integration begins the dynamic lineage recording, creating insertions and deletions (indels) within the lineage recorders, capturing sub-clonal lineage information. This second round of tagging also reduces homoplasy between sub-clones within a founder, enhancing lineage recording fidelity (Supplementary Fig. 1A). We then use computational methods to generate a phylogenetic tree of cell relationships within founder and clonal populations from these indels. We first engineered FLARE in the C1498 cell line, a syngeneic mouse model of AML compatible with transplantation into immune-competent C57BL/6 mice. Despite being long-passaged, C1498 cells are heterogeneous, making them an optimal model for investigating the diversity of clonal treatment responses30. Subclones derived from C1498 cells exhibit similar myeloblast/monoblast mixture phenotypes, but have diverse genomic profiles30. Furthermore, subclones displayed varying levels of immunogenicity in vivo30. We established 11 founder populations from individually isolated cells through successive engineering rounds (Supplementary Fig. 1B, C). Sequencing revealed a unique set of founder tags for each population, varying from 19 to 33 recorder integrations (Supplementary Fig. 1D). These isolated founders displayed a wide range of growth kinetics, suggesting sustained phenotypic heterogeneity through FLARE engineering (Supplementary Fig. 1E). Whole genome sequencing failed to identify founder population-specific AML driver mutations that account for this heterogeneity. We did detect homozygous Trp53 missense mutations predicted to be deleterious and shared by all C1498 cells sequenced (Supplementary Fig. 1F). There are currently no approved targeted therapies for TP53-mutated AML, a subtype characterized by complex karyotypes, poorer outcomes, and high rates of chemoresistance21,31–33.

Characterizing lineage recording

Next, we set out to test the lineage recording capacity of our engineered cells. We activated CRISPR lineage recording by integrating the Cas9 construct into the genome (Fig. 1A, B). After electroporation and sorting, we sampled Cas9-positive C1498-FLARE cell populations at days 13, 44, 64, and 87 and sequenced the lineage recorders to characterize Cas9 editing (Fig. 1C). Recording proportions suggest the bulk of lineage recorder editing occurs between day 13 (22.61% of recorders contain ≥1 edit) and 44 (99.97% ≥ 1 edit). Editing rates remained high through at least day 87 (85.93% ≥ 1 edit), with fluctuations in editing patterns attributable to population drift. Editing patterns were comparable across lineage recorder target sites, with most edits spanning multiple target sites, and a smaller proportion affecting individual targets (Fig. 1D). Approximately 15% of sequenced lineage recorder targets remained unedited, indicating our ability to trace lineage over at least 87 days without saturation.

We further characterized the response of individual recorder integrations with a single founder group, designated as founder 1 (F1), with 19 unique integrations. All integrations were active, displaying diverse editing outcomes (Fig. 1E). For all founder populations assessed, individual recording events tended to be large, spanning 3.1 targets on average, and each recorder contained an average of 2.6 recording events. While large deletions can lead to loss of lineage information, we were still able to reliably reconstruct lineage trees with an average depth of 5.6 nodes over relevant timescales. We attribute this to this system’s high lineage recording capacity, with engineered cells containing between 152 and 240 CRISPR target sites per cell.

Clonal treatment response and tree generation in vitro and in vivo

Confident in our ability to record and recover cellular lineage information, we set out to characterize C1498-FLARE population dynamics in response to the chemotherapy AraC, both in vitro and in vivo (Fig. 2A). By day 43 of in vitro AraC treatment, we observed two distinct growth phenotypes. Our resistance 1 (murine R1, mR1) population survived treatment but displayed minimal proliferation, and cell numbers were too low at the endpoint for a repeat ED50 determination. In contrast, our resistance 2 (mR2) population displayed rapid growth and a 10-fold higher ED50 than control cell populations (Supplementary Fig. 2A). We sequenced 27,237 single cells from the control (mC), mR1, and mR2 populations. Treated cells clustered into distinct groups, predominantly separated by population phenotype (Fig. 2B). We successfully captured founder tags and lineage recorders from 100% of single cells and clone tags from 31.9%. After filtering, quality control, and lineage assignment, 25,489 single cells were assigned to founder groups, of which 5468 were successfully assigned to clones. Founder tag sequencing revealed in vitro fitness differences: in the untreated mC population, founder 1 (F1) and founder 6 (F6) dominated (43% and 57% of cells, respectively), and few founder 11 (F11) cells were detected (<0.5% of cells) (Supplementary Fig. 2B, C). F1 and F6 control cells clustered separately, suggesting distinct founder-specific transcriptomes (Supplementary Fig. 2B, C). In contrast, treated mR1 and mR2 populations were primarily composed of F1 with few F6 (<0.7%) and zero F11 cells. F5 was outcompeted in all conditions (Fig. 2C).

We performed differential gene expression analysis between founder groups, focusing on F1, F6, and F11 in the control setting. F1 lineages were characterized by high expression of Nkain3, Wnt6, and Chl1 (Supplementary Data 1). In contrast, F6 lineages were characterized by high expression of Sox2ot, Tnni1, and Tnnt1 (Supplementary Data 2). GSEA results indicated enrichment of multiple Hallmark pathways in F6 compared to F1, including MYC targets V1, oxidative phosphorylation, and MTORC1 signaling (Supplementary Fig. 2E, F). Enrichment of these oncogenic pathways likely contributed to the success of F6 in the absence of treatment. F11 cells shared features with both F1 and F6 cells, such as enrichment of MYC targets V1 and diminished oxidative phosphorylation, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2E–G).

Since these pathways have well-established roles in chemoresistance, we expected F6 to display a survival advantage under AraC treatment34–37. Unexpectedly, F6 was almost fully outcompeted by F1 in the presence of AraC (Fig. 2C). Within F1 populations, a distinct and divergent selection of specific clones was observed in AraC-treated and control cultures (Fig. 2D). The highest proportion of mR1 cells belonged to clones F1.1 and F1.2, while the highest proportion of mR2 cells belonged to F1.4. The outgrowth of distinct clones in treated replicates suggests clone-specific responses that contribute to population growth dynamics.

In parallel to our in vitro treatment assay, we injected immune-competent C57BL/6 KH238 mice with 25k cells from the pooled founder starting population to induce leukemia with lineage tracing capacity (Fig. 2A). Mice were then treated with AraC at a dose comparable to one round of 7 + 3 induction chemotherapy21. We used bulk lineage sequencing to measure founder and clone dynamics from blood draws of circulating leukemia cells. On day 13 prior to treatment, almost all circulating leukemia cells across mice belonged to F1 (Fig. 2E). Interestingly, subsequent blood draws showed an expansion of the F6 lineage, outcompeting other founder populations in vivo in all but one mouse by endpoint. A selective sweep was nearly complete in treated mouse M1 (>99.9% F6 by day 43), treated mouse M2 (100% F6 by day 67), and untreated mouse M4 (>99.9% F6 by day 57). Untreated mouse M3’s leukemic burden was composed of >77.5% F1 by day 43, and this increased to >85.9% on day 50 when M3 was euthanized early due to symptomatic disease. This observed F6 dominance suggests leukemic lineages can have a competitive edge in vivo that is not reflected in vitro, and is in line with previous studies showing diverse lethality rates of C1498 sub-populations upon in vivo injection30.

We then focused on the within-founder clone tag sequences seen in blood samples collected from mice throughout AML growth and expansion. Circulating leukemic cells from mouse M1 displayed sustained clonal diversity split amongst F6.1, F6.2, F6.3, F6.4, and F6.5 (Fig. 2F). These F6 clones had comparable fitness through engraftment, AraC treatment, and post-treatment outgrowth. This is indicative of a lineage-intrinsic survival advantage shared between F6 clones. In contrast, blood samples from M3 were primarily dominated by F1’s clone F1.2 by day 43 (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Blood samples from M2 and M4 did not contain enough overlap with our known clone tags to infer population dynamics, likely representing diversity not captured in our scRNA-seq at endpoint.

To further examine the lineage patterns of the F6-intrinsic in vivo survival advantage, we sequenced 10,473 and 6344 cells from the blood and bone marrow, respectively, of treated mouse M1, which displayed sustained F6 clonal diversity. Non-leukemic cells clustered by cell type, and leukemic cells were identified by founder and clone tags (Supplementary Fig. 3B, C). Of the leukemic cells, 6 and 6053 belonged to founders F1 and F6, respectively. Clone groups were assigned to 2002 single cells, 702 of which belonged to clone F6.1. The remaining clone assignments were split between F6.2, F6.3, F6.4, and F6.5 (Supplementary Fig. 3D). We also detected low frequencies of cells belonging to F6.6 and F6.8. These scRNA-seq results matched the population detected in bulk sequencing (Fig. 2F), confirming our ability to reconstruct founder and clone patterns from limited samples, such as blood draws, using FLARE lineage tracing.

We then created phylogenetic trees for F1 and F6 using our in vitro and in vivo scRNA-seq data (Fig. 2G). AraC-treated F1 cells clustered based on resistance phenotypes, exhibiting clone-specific adaptations to in vitro treatment. In contrast, cells from mouse M1 post-AraC treatment were distributed relatively evenly between F6 clones, demonstrating founder-wide, clone-agnostic leukemic adaptation in vivo.

To identify global, heritable gene expression patterns associated with the development of AraC resistance and in vivo expansion, we conducted lineage autocorrelation analysis on founder trees using PATH (Fig. 2H)39. PATH highlighted genes previously identified by differential gene expression analysis as highly cross-correlated with mR1 and/or mR2 cell identities in the F1 tree (Supplementary Fig. 4A). These genes include Olfm3, a gene associated with anoikis resistance in AML cells40, and Cd7, the expression of which is a prognostic risk factor in AML41–43. Both genes were significantly upregulated in mR1 cells. Genes such as Adprh and Mctp1, associated with the F1.3 clone branch, were also strongly heritable but expressed primarily in a particular cluster of control cells (referred to as the Adprhhigh cluster).

Heritable genes over the F6 tree cross-correlated with the identity of cells collected from mouse M1 included Gpx1, expression of which is correlated with anti-leukemic immune response suppression in AML patients44, and Fth1, expression of which is associated with poor prognosis in pediatric patients with non-M3 AML45 (Supplementary Fig. 4B). In vivo lineage data support an F6-wide advantage in AML engraftment in an immune-competent model, potentially due to the shared upregulation of these phylogenetically autocorrelated genes. Future experiments with extended AraC exposure are needed to determine if this lineage-wide adaptation also leads to chemoresistance.

We then set out to identify F1 lineage patterns associated with the development of AraC resistance. Within our in vitro data, we observed a small subset of treatment-naïve F1 cells clustering with mR1 or mR2 cells (Supplementary Fig. 4C, D). To trace the lineage of resistance, we focused on clone F1.2, which produced the largest clonal phylogenetic tree. Within control F1.2 cells, we observed both mR1 and control cluster cells that were significantly anticorrelated, indicating divergent evolution along the lineage (Supplementary Fig. 4E).

We then used PATH to determine phylogenetic state transition probabilities (Supplementary Fig. 5A, B). The control cluster state was highly stable, and mR1-clustering cells were most likely to emerge from the Adprhhigh treatment-naïve cluster. The mR1-clustering cells were most likely to transition to control-clustering cells; however, this transition probability was significantly lower than expected, suggesting a stable transcriptional fate. F1.2 cells surviving AraC treatment had higher phylogenetic correlation with mR1-clustering treatment-naïve lineage branches compared to control cluster treatment-naïve populations (Supplementary Fig. 5C). This suggests that mR1-clustering, treatment-naïve cells had pre-existing tolerance to AraC and seeded chemoresistance.

Clonal treatment response and tree generation in human AML cells

To further validate the phylogenetic and transcriptomic patterns of AraC response we engineered FLARE lineage recording the human HL60 FAB-M2 line46. Similarly to Trp53-mutated C1498 cells, HL60 cells are also p53 null47. We seeded 500 HL60-FLARE founder cells, expanded, and split them into parallel cultures (Fig. 3A). Our resulting HL60-FLARE cells clustered in distinct groups based on AraC treatment in vitro (Fig. 3B). Differential expression analysis of resistance 1 (human R1, hR1) cells uncovered the upregulation of genes shared with mR1 C1498 F1 cells, such as VIM, PSD3, CRIP1, and S100A4 (Supplementary Data 3). ScRNA-seq of founder and clone tags was used to cluster cells into 15 surviving founder populations (H1–H15), each composed of 1–14 clones. All 15 HL60 founders were detected in the experimental starting population and the control sample at endpoint (Fig. 3C, D). Untreated endpoint cells were most likely to descend from H2, while both AraC-treated samples consisted primarily of cells belonging to H1. Both AraC-resistant samples maintained diversity within H1 on the clonal level, evenly split between clones H1.1, H1.2, H1.3, H1.4, and H1.5 (Fig. 3E). The success of H1 clones in both treated samples suggests a repeatable, lineage-intrinsic propensity for treatment resistance prior to the onset of treatment. We combined clonal trees to create founder-level lineage trees, and these founder-level trees were then merged to form the HL60 population tree (Fig. 3F). PATH analysis on the population tree revealed many highly heritable genes, both with and without AraC, including genes previously implicated in AML prognosis and treatment response, such as MPO and ELANE48 (Fig. 3G).

As we observed in our C1498-FLARE data, a subset of treatment-naïve cells clustered with the AraC-resistant populations (Supplementary Fig. 6B). Most of these resistant-clustering cells belonged to H1, further supporting H1’s lineage intrinsic propensity for surviving AraC exposure (Supplementary Data 5).

Identification of heritable transcriptional programs along leukemic lineages

To better understand broad transcriptomic changes over AraC-induced clonal evolution, we generated lineage-dependent gene modules for C1498-FLARE F1 and F6 trees, as well as the HL60-FLARE population tree using Hotspot (Fig. 4A–C, Supplementary Data 6 and 7)49. We identified the C1498-FLARE mR1 module, enriched along F1 lineages dominant in the mR1 population (Fig. 4A). The mouse M1 in vivo module was the most heritable module across the F6 phylogenetic tree, exhibiting significant enrichment in cells collected from mouse M1, representing a putative in vivo survival mechanism (Fig. 4B). To assess the potential relevance of these modules in patient populations, we turned to the TARGET-AML pediatric patient cohort. We calculated hazard ratios for these modules, along with modules heritable along other leukemic lineages, using multivariate Cox proportional hazard models (Supplementary Data 8). The three aforementioned modules were associated with significantly increased hazard (F1 mR1 module HR = 1.97, adjusted p = 1.69 × 10−5; F6 in vivo module HR = 1.29, adjusted p = 0.0031; HL60 hR1/hR2 module HR = 2.44, adjusted p = 1.8 × 10−7. Confidence intervals can be found in Supplementary Data 8). These results highlight the utility of FLARE lineage tracing in identifying heritable and clinically relevant leukemic states and treatment responses. Furthermore, Jaccard indices revealed significant similarity between several HL60 and C1498 modules, suggesting shared mechanisms across our two experimental lines (Supplementary Fig. 6C, D).

Pro-leukemic transcriptional programs associated with in vivo immune escape

To investigate resistance pathways shared by our FLARE models, we focused on the overlap between the HL60-FLARE hR1/hR2 resistance module and C1498-FLARE F6 in vivo module (Supplementary Data 9). These two modules share the immunoproteasome genes PSME1, PSME2, PSMB8, and PSMB9, which are involved in antigen processing and presentation for MHC class I-mediated immune response. PSMB8 and PSMB9 encode two of the three beta core subunits of the 20S immunoproteasome core complex50. In a variation of the immunoproteasome, the 20S core can complex with the 11S (or PA28αβ) regulatory cap, the primary subunits of which are encoded by the PSME1 (PA28a) and PSME2 (PA28b) genes51. There is evidence that peptide fragments produced by the 20S/11S immunoproteasome may be less conducive to MHC class I presentation and lead to tumor cell immune escape52–54. Oxidative stress, a known result of AraC exposure, leads to immunoproteasome subunit upregulation, which may be why we detected upregulation of these genes in HL60-FLARE cells treated in vitro55,56.

In our C1498-FLARE model, we identified universal upregulation of 11S regulatory cap genes Psme1 and Psme2 in vitro in the F6 lineage compared to F1 (Supplementary Data 2). Additionally, Psmb8, Psme1, and Psme2 were significantly upregulated in F6 clones in vivo compared to their in vitro counterparts, in proportion to the clone success in vivo (Supplementary Fig. 7A). F6 clones F6.1-F6.5 upregulated Psme2 and Psmb8 in the in vivo setting, representing a founder-specific enrichment. Expression of Psme1 differentiated F6 clones further; significant in vivo upregulation was unique to clones F6.1, F6.2, and F6.4, the three most successful clones in the blood (Supplementary Fig. 7A). This founder-specific survival benefit in vivo is in line with previous studies which found C1498 sub-clones exhibit differential immunogenicity, with certain sub-clones leading to immune escape in vivo30. Additionally, expression of 11S cap genes PSME1 and PSME2 is associated with significantly increased hazard in the TARGET-AML cohort (HR 1.15, adjusted p = 6.82 × 10−5. Confidence intervals can be found in Supplementary Data 8).

Pro-leukemic transcriptional programs associated with pre-existing AraC-resistant lineages

To further explore the shared mechanisms of resistance, we focused on the overlap between the HL60-FLARE hR1/hR2 resistance module and the C1498-FLARE F1 mR1 resistance module (Supplementary Data 10), the most heritable modules enriched in treatment conditions. The HL60-FLARE hR1/hR2 resistance module was enriched in all AraC-treated cells (Fig. 4C), while the C1498-FLARE mR1 resistance module was associated with the mR1 population (Fig. 4A). These modules shared 9 genes: ITSN1, VIM, CRIP1, S100A11, ANXA2, MYL6, S100A4, PSD3, and DOCK10 (Supplementary Data 10). This set of genes is highly active in regulating cell adhesion and maintaining and remodeling the cytoskeleton. Such pathways are involved in the epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), a form of transdifferentiation implicated in cancer cell migration, invasion, and drug resistance57. While EMT is less well studied in hematological malignancies than solid tumors, there is increasing evidence of its relevance in AML and other blood cancers58–62. There was no significant correlation between module expression and any of the tested EMT gene sets, suggesting that the C1498 mR1 and HL60 hR1/hR2 resistance modules describe cell adhesion processes that are largely independent of canonical EMT (Supplementary Fig. 7B).

We examined related pathways in pre-experiment and control H1 cells to identify whether heritable upregulation of these genes in leukemic lineages prior to treatment was associated with the generation of AraC resistance. Sixty-five genes significantly upregulated in treatment-naïve H1 cells were also found in the HL60 hR1/hR2 resistance module, constituting a lineage-intrinsic resistant state referred to as the resistant lineage signature (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Data 11). Expression of the resistant lineage signature clearly distinguished H1 cells in the treatment-naïve setting and could therefore be used to predict lineage-specific resistance (Fig. 5B). As expected, many resistant lineage signature genes are involved in cell motility and adhesion, including NCAM163, PRICKLE164, BMP265, CPNE866, and NAV367. Many genes are also involved in the maintenance and proliferation of hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), HSC-like blasts, and leukemia stem cells (LSCs), including MEIS168, HOXA969, MEF2C70,71, and IRF872,73. We next hypothesized that this altered regulation of adhesion-related pathways could be related to leukemic stem cell-like properties. Adhesion molecules are vital for the HSC niche and for maintaining self-renewal capacity75. Furthermore, deregulation of adhesion pathways has been implicated in chemotherapy resistance in AML74,76. We set out to characterize expression patterns of genes associated with HSC-like leukemic cells in our HL60-FLARE populations. The majority of tested HSC-like and LSC markers, including CD9377,78, CD9979, PTPRC79 and IGF2BP280,81, show distinct upregulation in resistant populations (Fig. 5C). We also see upregulation of the same markers in treatment-naive H1 cells compared to all other founders, suggesting the H1 cells display HSC-like qualities before AraC exposure (Supplementary Fig. 7C). This upregulation is most pronounced in the starting cell population and appears to diminish through time in untreated cultures (Supplementary Fig. 7C). From this, we concluded that lineages with HSC-like gene expression are selected for upon AraC treatment, but not in the absence of treatment. These results suggest that expression of the resistant lineage signature in treatment-naive leukemic cells is rare, but predictive of diminished AraC sensitivity. Rare signature expressing lineages displaying stem-like features and de-regulated cell adhesion survive AraC exposure and expand to create an AraC-resistant population.

Resistant lineage signature genes MEIS1, IRF8, HOXA9, and MEF2C are considered key leukemogenic transcription factors (TFs) in KMT2A-rearranged AML, where gene translocations lead to aberrant HOX regulation and dysfunctional differentiation69,71,72,82. As expected, malignant cells from patients with KMT2A (also referred to as MLL) rearrangements expressed significantly higher levels of the resistant lineage signature. This was true across all malignant cell types except common lymphoid progenitors and progenitor cells (Supplementary Fig. 8B–J). We validated this pattern in a second pediatric AML scRNAseq dataset83 as well as in the TARGET-AML bulk RNAseq cohort. We again found enrichment of the resistant lineage signature in KMT2A-rearranged samples (Supplementary Fig. 8K, L).

Consistent with the potential role of the resistant signature in HSC-like malignant cells, we found that the mean expression of all malignant cells per patient was closely correlated with expression in HSC-like cells (Supplementary Fig. 9A). When looking at all malignant cells, patients with higher signature expression had higher proportions of HSC-like malignant cells at relapse and greater increases in HSC-like fractions compared to diagnosis (Supplementary Fig. 9B, C). Within HSC-like malignant populations, we found that the mean resistant lineage signature expression at diagnosis was positively correlated with the number of HSC-like cells per 100 malignant cells at relapse, as well as the change in the rate of HSC-like blasts from diagnosis (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Fig. 9D). This suggests that resistant lineage signature expression is associated with increased survival and expansion of HSC-like malignant cells with de-regulated adhesion pathways. These correlations remained significant upon removing MEIS1, HOXA9, MEF2C, and IRF8 from the signature, suggesting the resistant lineage signature phenotype is not driven exclusively by the known driver TFs (Supplementary Fig. 9E, F). Furthermore, there is minimal correlation between expression of individual signature genes, indicating the remaining signature genes are regulated independently and not simply part of the leukemogenic TF transcription network (Supplementary Fig. 9G).

Lastly, we investigated the role of the resistant lineage signature in patient outcome in the TARGET-AML pediatric cohort. We found that increased signature expression is associated with significantly higher hazard in these patients, independent of clinical features such as cytogenetic categorization and FLT3 status (HR 2.14, p < 0.001, Fig. 6C). Expression of the resistant lineage signature is also associated with a significantly higher hazard compared to random gene signatures (Supplementary Fig. 9H)84. This increased hazard remained when we removed the known leukemic driver TF genes (MEIS1, HOXA9, MEF2C, and IRF8) (Supplementary Fig. 9I). Patients with high expression of the resistant lineage signature also have significantly shorter overall survival, with a median survival of approximately 24 months (Fig. 6D). Together, these results show that expression of the resistant lineage signature is negatively associated with survival outcomes in pediatric AML patients.

Discussion

We developed FLARE, a CRISPR-based dynamic lineage tracing system capable of tracking founder, clone, and subclone lineage in tandem with single-cell transcriptomics in response to chemotherapy. We demonstrated that our hierarchical approach captures cancer transcriptional evolution over an extended treatment, including both clonal evolution and the selection of pre-existing cellular states. Using FLARE, we uncovered (1) the upregulation of immunoproteasome 11S regulatory cap subunits contributing to clone-specific in vivo fitness and (2) heritable patterns of cell adhesion and stemness-associated AraC-tolerance in leukemic lineages. Chemotherapy resistance likely arises from the selection of pre-existing resistant populations and the reprogramming of previously sensitive cells. Similar to Yan et al.21, our results support that AraC chemoresistance can originate from the selection of pre-existing resistant populations.

With our C1498-FLARE model, we observed divergent selection in vitro and in vivo. In vitro, there was clear selection of the F1 lineage with diverse clonal outcomes in treated populations. In contrast, the F6 lineage became dominant over F1 in vivo in all but one mouse. These divergent results are likely the result of the added selective pressure in vivo due to the murine immune system. The F1 and F6 lineages differ significantly in their transcriptomic profiles, including in the expression of immunoproteasome genes. F6 further upregulated various immunoproteasome subunits, including those comprising the 11S regulatory cap, in vivo. While the exact pro-leukemic mechanism of the 11S cap is still unclear, there are data to suggest that this alternative cap leads to the generation of fewer peptides conducive to MHC class I presentation and can interfere with tumor-specific epitope production53,54,85. Leukemic lineages upregulating 11S cap genes are potentially better able to evade immune detection. In TARGET-AML primary pediatric AML samples, expression of the 11S regulatory cap is associated with significantly increased hazard. Further investigation and functional analysis are warranted to determine the role of the 11S cap in AML.

Our HL60-FLARE model displayed clear founder-level selection, with H1 dominance in all treated populations. Within the H1 founder, we observed minimal clone-specific selection or transcriptomic adaptation, supporting the conclusion that H1 harbored a founder-wide resistance phenotype prior to AraC exposure. From the H1 population, we identified 65 genes that were upregulated relative to other founders. This signature differentiated H1 cells prior to AraC exposure and was further upregulated along the H1 lineage through the generation of an AraC-resistant population. As in the upregulated pathways in the mR1 population, many signature genes are involved in cell adhesion and motility.

Aberrant regulation of adhesion-related pathways has been implicated in AML, primarily in the interactions between AML blasts and bone marrow stromal cells through integrins and adhesion molecules86–88. Furthermore, AML cells have been found to hijack the stem cell niche, leading to LSC retention in the bone marrow through treatment74,89,90. In line with these previous findings, our resistant lineage signature also comprises many genes known to be enriched in HSCs, HSC-like leukemic cells, and LSCs. These genes include HOXA9 and MEIS1, overexpression of which in HSCs leads to expansion and leukemic development91. These stem-like blasts likely benefit from deregulation of adhesion-related pathways, allowing them to persist through AraC treatment. Using pediatric AML scRNA-seq data, we find that upregulation of the resistant lineage signature in HSC-like malignant cells is significantly correlated with increased proportion and expansion of HSC-like cells at relapse. This suggests that expression of the signature lends a survival advantage through AraC treatment in HSC-like malignant lineages. In TARGET-AML primary pediatric AML samples, expression of the resistant lineage signature is associated with significantly increased hazard and shorter overall survival.

We identified the resistant lineage signature from in vitro-treated AML cells in the absence of the bone marrow niche, suggesting adhesion to other blasts and/or culture dish surfaces can contribute to the adhesion-mediated resistance phenotype. Previous studies have proposed a role for homotypic adhesion (HSC-to-HSC interactions) in HSC and mesenchymal stem cell regulation75,92,93. In fact, resistant lineage signature gene NCAM1 has been found to play a role in homotypic adhesion94–96.

HOXA9 and MEIS1, along with MEF2C and IRF8, are primarily implicated in leukemogenesis and malignant HSC-like maintenance of KMT2A-rearranged AML82,97. We detected higher signature expression in KMT2A-rearranged patient samples across datasets (Supplementary Fig. 8J–L). Interestingly, the HL60 cell line, with which we identified the signature, does not contain any KMT2A translocation97–99. Furthermore, the increased hazard associated with resistant lineage signature upregulation is independent of disease cytogenetics (Fig. 6A). This suggests that while the resistant lineage signature may be most prominent in the KMT2A-rearranged setting, it is associated with AML treatment resistance regardless of KMT2A status.

We also assessed the prognostic impact of the 11S cap and resistant lineage signatures in adult patients using the Beat AML100 (subset for patient age >29) and TCGA LAML101 cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 10). While some analyses in these patient cohorts reached statistical significance, suggesting a potential role for our signatures, the trends were minimal compared to the TARGET-AML data. This may be due to the larger cohort size of TARGET-AML, which allowed for more statistical power. It may also suggest that specific resistance mechanisms are associated with age at diagnosis, which is unsurprising given pediatric and adult AML are considered molecularly distinct102. The increased predictive value in pediatric patients may also be related to the HL60 cell line having been isolated from a 36-year old patient, significantly younger than the average adult patient103.

While FLARE has advantages over published CRISPR-based lineage tracing systems, it also has limitations to be addressed in future iterations. The most significant involves the creation of double-stranded breaks in the DNA, leading to cell stress104. These double-stranded breaks can also lead to the loss of lineage information, including previous editing events. Furthermore, edited lineage recorder transcripts range from 155 to 264 base pairs in length, which can lead to amplification and sequencing biases. We incorporated sequencing PCR UMIs in library preparation to minimize such biases, though this ultimately can limit our sensitivity in capturing every cell. Alternative lineage recording platforms, such as base and prime editing, also offer solutions to many of these limitations105–107.

In the future, FLARE and similar lineage tracing methods can be applied to dissect the transcriptional evolution of other tumor types under selective treatment pressure. Given the ability to sample tumor evolution from tagged cell populations in circulating blood, this can be multiplexed to explore many more initial cell states and treatment spaces. Moreover, this approach can be combined with mouse lineage-tracing models such as DARLIN108 or the MARC1109 mouse to capture the interplay between engrafted tumor and immune cell populations in the context of checkpoint inhibitors and other immunotherapies.

Methods

Ethics statement

This study complied with all relevant ethical regulations. All experimental protocols were approved by and performed in accordance with the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Dartmouth College.

Cell lines and drugs

The murine C1498 cell line (Cat # TIB-49) was purchased from ATCC in 2021. The HL60 AML cell line was a gift from the Lau lab at Dartmouth College. C1498 cells were cultured in DMEM, supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 μ/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. HL60 cells were cultured in Iscove’s Modified Dulbecco’s Medium, supplemented with 20% FBS, 100 μ/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. All cell lines were expanded, aliquoted, and cryopreserved in culture media plus 5% DMSO and stored in liquid nitrogen upon receival. Active cell cultures were stored at 37 °C with 5% CO2. All cultured lines were regularly checked for the presence of mycoplasma using the Universal Mycoplasma Detection Kit (ATCC, 30-1012 K). Cytarabine (AraC) powder was purchased from Selleck Chemicals (Cat # S1648). AraC was resuspended in PBS fresh for each in vitro and in vivo treatment.

Mice

KH2/iCas9 mice (Jackson Laboratory strain #029415) were a gift of the Huang lab at Dartmouth College. All mice were maintained in pathogen-free housing at the Center for Comparative Medicine and Research in Dartmouth Hitchcock Medical Center. To attenuate the immune response to Cas9, all injected KH2/iCas9 were exposed to doxycycline during in utero development through irradiated feed containing 625ppm doxycycline (ScottPharma, Inc., Marlborough, MA).

Molecular cloning

GESTALT lineage tracing system engineering

All digested vectors and fragments and PCR products were purified with the Monarch Gel Purification Kit (Cat #T1120S) or the Zymo Clean and Concentrate-5 Kit (Cat #D4003). All Gibson assembly reactions were conducted with NEB HiFi DNA Assembly 2x Master Mix (Cat #E2621S), following the ssDNA HiFi assembly protocol. Golden Gate assemblies were conducted following the Rice University Esp3I protocol. Chemical bacterial transformations were done with DH5alpha competent cells following the NEB Transformation Protocol with a 2-h recovery at 37 °C. Electroporated transformations were conducted with NEB Electrocompetent E. coli and were electroporated with the following conditions: 2 kV, 200O hms, 25 μFd. All bacterial cultures were grown in a 37 °C shaker at 220–290 rpm overnight. Vectors were purified using either the Qiagen Miniprep Kit or the Zymo Midiprep II Kit. Correct cloning of all final constructs was confirmed via Circuit-seq110.

FLARE-miniV10-10x-UMI

Eight Cas9 target sequences were designed for high efficiency (>69% Doench) and low off target scores. The eight-target array was purchased as a gene block from IDT, with upstream Esp3I sites for founder tag insertion and NdeI + BamHI sites for cloning purposes. The target gBlock and pLARRY-EGFP vector (Addgene #140025) were digested with NdeI+BamHI and ligated together with Quick Ligase (Cat #M2200s). The resulting construct is referred to as pLARRY-targets.

The PB-CMV-MCS-EF1-puro vector (Systems Bio cat #PB510B-1) was digested with SpeI and HincII, removing the puromycin resistance gene, and then circularized with a Gibson assembly reaction with a short single-stranded joining oligo. The resulting construct is referred to as miniPB.

The FLARE-miniV10-10x vector was then constructed by amplifying EGFP and the target array from pLARRY-targets. The PCR primers used KpnI and EcoRI sites. The amplified EGFP+target array and miniPB were digested with KpnI and EcoRI, gel purified, and ligated together using T7 ligase. The 10x capture sequence 1 was incorporated via Gibson assembly. The FLARE-miniV10-10x construct is available at Addgene (Plasmid #234492).

Founder tag oligos consisting of 14 degenerate bases (the founder tag) and Esp3I sites were purchased from IDT. One cycle of PCR was used to anneal and extend the oligos. Founder tag oligos were then inserted into FLARE-miniV10 via a Golden Gate Assembly reaction; the resulting product is FLARE-miniV10-10X-UMI.

FLARE-pbV10g-neo

Individual Cas9 guides complementary to the target sequences in FLARE-miniV10 were designed and cloned into the pX330-mCherry (Addgene #98750) vector using the Zheng Lab pX330 cloning protocol. Guides, along with their individual U6 promoters, were amplified from pX330-mCherry vectors. A secondary PCR reaction added flanking Esp3I sites to each U6-guide fragment. Esp3I sites were also added to the PB-CMV-MCS-EF1-puro vector, while the puro resistance gene was removed. U6/guide sequences were joined and inserted into PB-CMV-MCS-EF1-puro via a 9-part Golden Gate Assembly reaction. The neomycin resistance gene was amplified from the hCas9 vector and inserted into the U6/guide array vector via Golden Gate Assembly. The FLARE-pbV10g-neo construct is available at Addgene (Plasmid #234493).

In earlier versions of FLARE constructs, Cas9 guides were encoded into a single construct along with the lineage recorder Cas9 target array. For reasons not yet fully understood, splitting this into two vectors (FLARE-miniV10-10X and FLARE-pbV10g-neo) led to significantly higher editing rates across targets.

FLARE-pbCas9-tag-mCherry

The miniPB construct was digested with KpnI for a Gibson Assembly insertion of a landing pad containing a NotI and a new KpnI site. The resulting vector is referred to as miniPB-LP. The pX330 construct was digested with KpnI and NotI to isolate the Cas9 transgene, which was then ligated into the landing pad miniPB-LP vector. The resulting construct is referred to as miniPB-Cas9.

The pX330-mCherry construct was digested with BamHI and HindIII for the insertion of a clone tag landing pad containing type IIs restriction sites in the 3’ UTR of the mCherry transgene using Gibson Assembly. The mCherry transgene, including the clone tag landing pad, was amplified and extended to include Gibson Assembly adapters complementary to the insertion region in miniPB-Cas9. The miniPB-Cas9 construct was then digested with NotI and the mCherry and clone tag landing pad were inserted using Quick Ligase. The resulting construct is referred to as FLARE-pbCas9-mCherry and is available at Addgene (Plasmid #234494).

Lastly, clone tag oligos with Esp3I sites were purchased from IDT. For C1498-FLARE cells, the clone tag consisted of 14 degenerate bases followed by 10x capture sequence 2. For HL60-FLARE cells, the clone tag consisted of 38 patterned bases (NNNWSNNNWSNNNWSNNNWSNNNWSNNNWSNNNWSNNN, pattern from Johnson et al.111) followed by 10x capture sequence 1. One cycle of PCR was used to anneal and extend the oligos, and the resulting product was inserted into FLARE-pbCas9-mCherry via Golden Gate Assembly. Capture sequence 2 was replaced by capture sequence 1 for HL60-FLARE cells due to the planned discontinuation of support for capture sequence 2 announced by 10x.

Cell engineering and lineage tracing system testing

We used the Neon Electroporation System to integrate all piggyBac constructs into cells to generate stable cell lines. Electroporations were carried out with the following protocols: C1498 cells were resuspended at 2 × 107 cells/mL and electroporated with 1080 V, 50 ms, and 1 pulse; HL-60 cells were resuspended at 2 × 107 cells/mL and electroporated with 1450 V, 35 ms, and 1 pulse. All electroporations were done with the Neon 100 μl tip system. Transposase:transposon ratios for piggyBac integration ranged from 1:1 to 1:8. All FACS was conducted on a BD FACSAria II or BD FACSAria Fusion. A representative example of gating strategy for isolating EGFP+ and mCherry+/EGFP+ cells can be found in Supplementary Fig. 11. For antibiotic selection cells were incubated with selection media for 1–3 weeks, and dead cells were removed from culture 2–3 times/week using a Ficoll density gradient.

GESTALT constructs were introduced to cell lines in the following order: first low passage cells were electroporated with FLARE-miniV10-10X-UMI and FLARE-pbV10g-neo. EGFP-positive and neomycin resistant cells were isolated via FACS and 1 μg/mL G418 selection. This step was repeated two to three more times, gating for higher EGFP expression during FACS in each subsequent round. For the third round of FACS for the C1498 cell line, individual cells were sorted into wells of a 96-well plate to initiate individual clonal populations.

Prior to lineage tracing experiments, engineered lineage tracing cells were electroporated with FLARE-pbCas9-tag-mCherry and mCherry+ cells were isolated via FACS. Cells were sorted into bulk populations and 96-well plates at 100 cells/well.

Lineage recorder editing levels in C1498-FLARE cells over time were calculated using single cell and bulk lineage barcode data. Day 0 editing was calculated from bulk (not a subset of founders) C1498-FLARE cells prior to FLARE-pbCas9-tag-mCherry integration; day 13 editing was calculated from this same population 13 days following integration post FACS. The remaining editing rate timepoints were measured using the cell population used in C1498-FLARE founder pool AraC treatment experiment and lineage recorder reads were subset for reads containing valid founder tags associated with founders 1, 5, 6, and 11. Day 44 editing was calculated from scRNA-seq of the founder pool subset for F1 and F6 prior to AraC treatment. Day 64 editing was calculated from scRNA-seq of one untreated flask cultured from the same population as used in day 44, and was subset for valid cells identified via GEX analysis. Day 87 was also measured from one control flask from this population - editing was calculated from single cell lineage endpoint data.

C1498-FLARE founder isolation and characterization

To screen individual C1498-FLARE founder populations for copies of FLARE-miniV10-10X-UMI integrations gDNA was collected and founder tags were amplified and prepared into sequencing libraries. Founder tag libraries for individual populations were multiplexed and sequenced on an Illumina MiniSeq with a Mid-Ouput 300-cycle kit. Founder tags for each clone were identified as having more than 4300 reads. Cells from each clone were expanded, aliquoted, and cryopreserved.

Growth curves

To begin growth curves for individual founders, cells were resuspended at 50k cells/mL and plated in T25 flasks in triplicate. From day 0–7, two 10 μl aliquots from each flask were mixed with 10 μl Trypan Blue and counted on the Luna II Cell Counter daily. Growth curve data were analyzed and plotting using the Growthcurver R package (version 0.3.1)112.

AraC dose-response curves

Cells were plated at 15,000 cells/well in a 96-well plate in media containing various AraC dosages in triplicate (0.0001 mg/ml-10mg/ml). 100 μl of CellTiter 96 AQueous One Solution (Cat #G3582) was added to each well and after incubation for 1–3 h absorbance was measured at 490 nm on a SpectraMax i3x. Absorbance from wells containing media without cells or drug were averaged to calculate background absorbance. Corrected absorbance for each well was calculated as (well absorbance - background absorbance). Corrected absorbances were then loaded into the drc R package (version 3) for generating dose-response curves, ED50 values, and EC50 values113.

Whole genome sequencing

High molecular weight (HMW) gDNA was isolated from parental C1498 cells and individually engineered clones using the Monarch HMW gDNA Kit for Cells (Cat #T3050S). HMW gDNA samples were multiplexed and prepared into a sequencing library following the Oxford Nanopore SQK-NBD114.24 kit. The library was then sequenced on a PromethION flow cell with R10.4.1 chemistry. Sequencing data were basecalled and demultiplexed using Guppy. Variants were called using PEPPER-Margin-DeepVariant, SNPs were isolated and filtered for a minimum quality of 20 and mean depth between 10 and 80. To remove population variants, the GRCm39 dbSNP was annotated using Ensembl VEP and variants with moderate or high impact were removed. The resulting dbSNP was subtracted from filtered SNPs using Bedtools (version 2.31.0)114. Remaining variants were annotated with Ensembl VEP and SIFT and intersected with datasets of known AML mutations.

AraC response and resistance experiments

C1498-FLARE founder pool treatment response and resistance

C1498-FLARE founders F1, F5, F6, and F11 were sorted by FACS on day 13 following electroporation with the FLARE-pbCas9-tag-mCherry vector. Cells were sorted into 96-well plates at 100 cells/well. In vitro AraC treatment began on day 44 (31 days post FACS). Cells were allowed to grow for a longer period following sorting due to growth rate differences between founders. On day 44, 1 well of sorted cells for each clone was pooled at equal numbers and split into four flasks for in vitro treatment with an aliquot set aside for mouse injections.

In vitro

Two flasks were maintained in normal culture media; the remaining two flasks were maintained in media containing 0.05ug/ml AraC. Flasks were maintained for 43 days to allow for the development of AraC resistance. Media was replaced 2–3 times/week and split when needed. Dose-response curves were calculated for each flask on day 15 of treatment, and a final dose-response curve was calculated for flask displaying resistance on day 36 of treatment. On day 43 of treatment, cells were prepared for MULTIseq tagging and single cell capture following MULTIseq and 10x protocols.

In vivo

Pooled C1498 clone cells were washed twice with PBS, resuspended at 250,000 cells/mL and filtered through a 40uM filter. Female mice (8–12 weeks old) were injected IV with 100 μL of cell suspension (25,000 cells/mouse). On day 14 mice were randomly assigned to treatment groups; on days 14–16 treatment mice received 100 mg/kg AraC IP and control mice received an equal volume of PBS. Blood was collected via the retro-orbital sinus on days 13, 28, 43, 50, and 57. Prior to blood draws mice were anesthetized with isoflurane. Microhematocrit capillary tubes were placed in 1.7 mL tubes containing 50 mM EDTA and then used to collect 50–200 μl of blood from the retro-orbital sinus into EDTA coated tubes. Blood samples were then kept on ice until further processing. Mice displaying symptoms of high AML burden, such as piloerection, reduced movement, and isolation were euthanized. All surviving mice were euthanized on day 67 (timed by cell injections). Upon euthanasia, whole blood was collected via cardiac puncture, and femoral bone marrow was harvested. gDNA was isolated from 20ul of whole blood, and the remaining blood was separated using a Ficoll-Paque Plus (Cytiva cat # 17144002) density gradient to isolate PBMCs. To isolate bone marrow, 18 g needles were used to puncture the bottom of 0.5 mL tubes, femur bones were placed in the 0.5 mL tubes and nested in 1.7 mL tubes, and tubes were spun at >10,000 × g for 20 s. Bone marrow pellets and PBMCs were methanol fixed and permeabilized following 10x protocols.

Human AML cell line in vitro treatment response

Engineered lineage tracing HL60-FLARE cells were sorted into 96-well plates at 100 cells/well following electroporation with FLARE-pbCas9-tag-mCherry to establish GESTALT lineage tracing populations, as described above. Sorted well populations were expanded for 23 days. On day 23 post FACS, five expanded wells were combined into an initial cell pool. The pool was then split for 10x scRNA-seq preparation, methanol fixation, cryopreservation, and in vitro AraC treatment. For in vitro AraC treatment cell pools were split into control (no AraC) and AraC conditions (0.01 μg/mL AraC). All treatment wells were cultured in duplicates. AraC solutions and media were made fresh for media changes twice per week, and wells were split as needed. Cell concentration for each well was measured once per week in duplicates using the Luna II Cell Counter. Cells were regularly cryopreserved through the experiment for later analysis. In vitro treatment continued for 89 days, after which cells were prepared for 10x scRNA-seq preparation with MULTIseq multiplexing.

Lineage library preparation and sequencing

All PCR reactions were conducted using Q5 HotStart Polymerase (NEB cat # M0494L) and Sybr Green dye. DNA was purified from PCR reactions using Omega TotalPure NGS magnetic beads with 0.8–1.5x ratios based on amplicon size. Primer sequences for lineage barcode amplification can be found in Supplementary Data 15.

Bulk DNA library preparation and sequencing

gDNA was collected from cultured cells, PBMCs, and bone marrow samples using the Qiagen DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Cat # 69504). For library preparation, lineage recorders (including founder tags and clone tags) were first tagged with sequencing UMIs prior to amplification to control for non-linear amplification and sequencing, with the exception of libraries constructed from blood collections. Blood collection libraries were treated as sensitive samples, as C1498-FLARE cells were sparse in the blood. As a result, these libraries were amplified without sequencing UMIs and initial PCRs were set up in a PCR hood to prevent contamination. Initial amplification for most libraries was split into three to eight reactions. This was done to maximize sequence diversity. For all libraries, TruSeq/Nextera indices and Illumina adapters were subsequently attached with PCR. Final sequencing libraries were quantified with the Qubit fluorometer and the NEB NextQuant Library Quant kit (Cat # E7630L). Libraries were then diluted to the appropriate loading concentration for sequencing.

Single cell library preparation and sequencing

Cells were captured for scRNA-seq following 10x guidelines for 3’ scRNA-seq with feature barcoding. For multiplexed single cell experiments, cells were first tagged following MULTIseq protocols (Millipore Sigma, LMO001)115. Gene expression and MULTIseq libraries were constructed and quantified according to 10x and MULTIseq guidelines, with one alteration: a portion of cDNA was set aside following initial cDNA amplification but prior to SPRIselect cleanup for use in FLARE lineage library construction. This was done to avoid splitting FLARE transcripts between GEX and feature barcoding fractions.

The previously set aside cDNA was purified using a 2x bead cleanup. For each sample, cDNA was split to generate a library of dynamic lineage recorders (which includes founder tags) and a library of clone tags. All single cell FLARE libraries were initially amplified in 9–16 replicate wells and then combined into 3-4 separate sub-libraries. TruSeq/Nextera indices and Illumina adapters were subsequently attached with PCR. Final sequencing libraries were quantified with the Qubit fluorometer and the NEB NextQuant Library Quant kit (Cat # E7630L). Libraries were then diluted to the appropriate loading concentration for sequencing.

Computational analysis

Sequencing data pre-processing

For bulk DNA sequencing raw data were converted to fastq format using bcl2fastq2 (v2.20.0.422) or BCL Convert (Illumina, v4.3.13). For scRNA-seq raw data were converted to fastq format using CellRanger mkfastq. Cells were then called using CellRanger count for non-multiplexed 10x runs or CellRanger multi for MULTIseq multiplexed 10x runs116.

Single cell RNA-seq analysis

Cell Ranger output filtered matrices were analyzed with Seurat (v5.1.0)117. Low quality cells were filtered out based on the number of RNA features and percentage of mitochondrial reads. To isolate singlets in non-multiplexed samples, doublets were identified and removed using the scDblFinder R package (v1.20.2)118. For multiplexed samples, MULTIseq barcode filtered matrices were added as Assay Objects to Seurat objects. Cells were demultiplexed and singlets were identified and isolated with the MULTIseqDemux() function. We additionally used singleCode119 to assess our ability to detect doublets with lineage barcode data. Although sensitivity was low, 83% of detected doublets were previously established doublets using MULTIseq tags. For PBMC and bone marrow samples cell type annotations were added using the SingleR R package (v2.8.0) with the MouseRNAseqData reference set from the celldex R package (v1.16.0)120. All single cell Seurat objects were normalized with the SCTransform R package (v0.4.1) for data visualization and integration with other objects121,122. Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using the FindMarkers() and FindAllMarkers() Seurat functions.

Gene set enrichment analysis

C1498-FLARE founder-specific gene set enrichment analysis was conducted with the fGSEA R package (v1.32.0)123. Differentially expressed genes were identified for each founder by Wilcoxon rank sum test and input into the fGSEA function with Mus musculus Hallmark gene sets from mSigDB.

Single cell lineage analysis and tree reconstruction

Founder and clone tag clustering

To map founder tags to founder tag groups (or founders) and clone tags to clone tag groups (or clones), we used a clustering strategy based on per-cell tag set similarity. Briefly, given a mapping of cells to a set of tags, a graph was constructed with cells as nodes and Jaccard similarity as edges. Jaccard similarity was calculated pairwise as the intersection between per-cell tag sets divided by the union of per-cell tag sets. Any pairs of cells with a Jaccard similarity of 0 (indicating no overlapping tags) shared no edges. Cells were assigned to clusters using the Louvain algorithm for community detection, which returned the partition that maximized modularity, with resolution = 1.

Founder assignment

To extract lineage information from scRNA-seq-captured dynamic lineage recorders, we first used the SingleCellLineage pipeline, as previously described28,29. Briefly, sequences are collapsed by 10x UMI and aligned to the reference to obtain a pattern of insertions and deletions across a target array per cell. Founder tags, located immediately upstream of the lineage recorders, were extracted per collapsed merged read. We filtered cell barcodes against the 10x feature barcoding Cell ID whitelist. Merged reads with less than 85% identity to the reference sequence were removed from the dataset. To ensure we were using high-quality barcodes to build single-cell lineage trees, we leveraged tools from the Cassiopeia pipeline124 to perform additional data cleaning. Founder tags were corrected for PCR errors via Starcode (v1.4)125 with Levenshtein distance = 1. UMIs were error corrected using Cassiopeia function error_correct_umi(). Finally, we filtered our error-corrected founder tags and target arrays using filter_molecule_table() to remove low read count UMIs and cells with too few UMIs. Additionally, this step ensures there is only one target array per 10x cell ID-founder tag combination.

Founders were assigned by the combination of founder tag integrations in cells. For C1498-FLARE founder populations, founder tag assignments were previously determined via sub-founder culturing and bulk gDNA sequencing. Therefore, if cells had reads mapping to more than one founder based on the founder tags found in its lineage data, it was assigned to a founder based on the total sum of reads and UMIs associated with a certain founder group. Only cells with 90% identity to a certain founder were kept.

Unlike the C1498-FLARE founders, HL60-FLARE founders had no ground truth mapping of founder tags to founder groups. Subsequently, we took additional steps to ensure we had a confident list of founder tags per cell and group. To remove within-cell background noise, we excluded integrations that accounted for less than 1.5% of reads per cell. This threshold was based on qualitative inspection of the distribution of integration read counts per integration per cell. The founder tags were further filtered by removing tags found in fewer than 5 cells across the dataset. The cleaned set of tags was clustered into founders as described previously (Founder and clone tag clustering). Finally, to refine the final set of founder tags defining a founder, we filtered for tags with greater than 50% identity to a single founder group. This threshold was based on visual inspection of the distribution of tag identity to founders, determined by the total number of cells with a tag in a founder divided by the total number of cells with a tag. This also ensures that each founder tag is associated with only one founder.

Clone assignment

Clone tags were extracted from sequencing reads using the SingleCellLineage pipeline. For HL60-FLARE clone tag reads, only tags matching the expected 38 bp IUPAC pattern were kept. Clone tag reads with more than one missing base within the tag sequence window were removed from the dataset, then clone tags were error corrected using Starcode with Levenshtein distance = 1 for 14 bp tags or Levenshtein distance = 2 for 38 bp tags. These clone tags were then filtered for read count >1 and number of UMIs >1. Tags with nucleotide repeats of 5 or longer were also removed (i.e., AAAAA, TTTTT, GGGGG, CCCCC). Background tags, defined as contributing to fewer than 10% of all reads in the cell, were filtered out. Finally, clone tags were discretized to founders by keeping tags with 90% identity (C1498s, 2 founders) or 50% identity (HL60s, 15 founders) to a given founder based on the total proportion of reads per clone tags. C1498-FLARE and HL60-FLARE cells were assigned to clones as previously described (Founder and clone tag clustering) given the final set of clone tags.

Building trees

Trees were reconstructed per clone using Cassiopeia’s124 greedy vanilla solver from the pattern of indels per target array (v2.0.0). The method was chosen for its scalability, given the size of our trees ranges from tens to thousands of cells. Briefly, a tree is built in a top-down fashion by splitting cells into two groups based on the most frequent mutation in the population. Cell subsets are repeatedly split until only one cell remains per subset. Clones belonging to the same founder population were combined into founder trees using a Python script to attach trees at the root, due to the hierarchical nature of the lineage tracing tag system. All founder trees were combined with the same method to generate a population tree. Cells with unedited/wildtype target arrays were removed from the analysis prior to building the trees.

PATH analysis

To identify genes that were significantly heritable in our clonal population we used PATH (v1)39. PATH takes as input a phylogenetic tree, along with either discrete annotations, such as cluster assignments, or continuous data, such as gene expression or module scores. We considered the top 3000 highly variable features per tree to identify genes with strong autocorrelations, suggesting high heritability.

Hotspot module generation

To identify informative gene expression modules based on our single-cell lineage trees and gene expression data, we leveraged the tool Hotspot (v1.1.1)49 as described in the following vignette https://hotspot.readthedocs.io/en/latest/Lineage_Tutorial.html. Briefly, we ran Hotspot on our log-normalized data via the ‘normal’ model filtered for genes in a minimum of 10 cells. Then we generated the KNN graph with 10 neighbors. Autocorrelation scores are calculated per gene to perform feature selection. These genes are then correlated to generate a gene-by-gene matrix, which is used to identify modules using a hierarchical clustering approach with min_gene_threshold = 10 to control the size of modules being joined.

Module overlap analysis

C1498-FLARE module mouse genes were converted to human orthologs for analysis. C1498-FLARE and HL60-FLARE modules were assessed for significant overlap as measured by Jaccard indices and odds ratios compared to genomic background using the GeneOverlap R package (v1.42.0)126.

Simulations

We used Counterfeiter (https://github.com/mckennalab/counterfeiter) for cell lineage simulations. To simulate the reconstructive accuracy different between known clonal populations and the corresponding blinded populations, we built 4 trees (A–D) with using the set number of generations (4–8) and the Cas9 nuclease model in Counterfeiter. These trees were either reconstructed individually (known clonal assignments) and grafted together into a master tree in sets of three (A + B + C, A + B + D, etc) or reconstructed globally using all cells samples from all three populations. Accuracy was then compared using Robinson-Foulds in the ETE3 Python package127.

Survival analysis

TARGET-AML, BEAT AML, and TCGA LAML RNAseq, clinical, and sample data were accessed through the cBioPortal and GDC. Expression datasets were split by sample type where applicable. For the TARGET-AML cohort, survival analyses were conducted on bone marrow samples taken at diagnosis, or peripheral blood samples taken at diagnosis for patients without bone marrow RNAseq data. Sample expression scores for modules and gene sets were calculated by averaging the FPKM or RPKM scores. Cox proportional hazard ratios were generated using the survival R package (v3.8-3)128. To stratify patients for Kaplan-Meier analysis, we first determined the expression score mean and standard deviation within the samples of interest. Patients with scores within one standard deviation of the mean were assigned to the Mid-expression group. Patients with scores above and below one standard deviation of the mean were assigned to the High and Low expression groups, respectively. We excluded patient samples missing tissue type information.

Single cell analysis of publicly available datasets

Data were accessed from NCBI’s GEO (Lambo et al. accession number GSE235063; Mumme et al. accession number GSE235923). The Lambo single cell data was integrated and analyzed using the Seurat sketch workflow for larger datasets117. Malignant cells and cell types were identified using the original authors’ annotations. The Mumme single cell data was integrated with Harmony and analyzed following the standard Seurat workflow. Malignant cells were identified using the original authors’ blast-identifying signature. For all datasets, gene signature scores were calculated using Seurat’s AddModuleScore() function.
