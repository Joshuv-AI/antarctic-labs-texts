Comparative effectiveness of pharmacogenomic-guided versus unguided antidepressant treatment in major depressive disorder: new insights from subgroup and cumulative meta-analyses
Authors: Yuan Zhang, Yiyuan Gao, Yazhu Zou, Yu Ye, Fugui Jiang, Zuxing Wang, Jian Qiu, Zhili Zou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractQuestionHow effective is pharmacogenomic (PGx)-guided antidepressant treatment compared with treatment-as-usual (TAU) in major depressive disorder (MDD), and how do ethnicity, disease severity and genetic panel scope influence outcomes?Study selection and analysisThis systematic review and meta-analysis comprised 13 randomised controlled trials (2013–2024) comparing PGx-guided therapy with TAU in MDD. PubMed, Ovid Embase, Ovid Medline, Ovid PsycINFO and the Cochrane Library were searched up to December 2024. Outcomes included response and remission rates at 8 and 12 weeks. Subgroup analyses examined ethnicity and MDD severity. Cumulative meta-analyses assessed gene panel size. The pooled risk ratios (RRs) with 95% CIs were calculated to estimate the overall effect.FindingsPGx-guided treatment significantly improved response rates at 8 weeks (RR 1.23, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.43) and 12 weeks (RR 1.29, 95% CI 1.17 to 1.43). Remission was significant at 8 weeks (RR 1.37, 95% CI 1.19 to 1.57) but not at 12 weeks (RR 1.56, 95% CI 0.93 to 2.61). Benefits appeared stronger in the Asian country subgroup compared with non-Asian country subgroup (interaction p=0.02), but this requires validation due to the smaller Asian country sample size. No significant subgroup differences were observed between the MDD not-specified and MDD difficult-to-treat subgroups, despite the latter demonstrating significant improvements in both response and remission rates with PGx-guided treatment compared with TAU at 8 weeks. Cumulative analyses showed effect sizes plateaued, with broader panels offering minimal incremental gains.ConclusionsPGx-guided treatment seems to offer moderate benefits for antidepressant efficacy, with potential advantages in Asian and difficult-to-treat subgroups. Genetic and ethnic variability in drug metabolism underscores the need for population-specific approaches. While multigene panels show clinical benefits plateau, suggesting cost-benefit optimisation is critical. Future research should address adverse events, the cost‐effectiveness of expanded panels, long‐term remission outcomes and treatment efficacy across more precisely stratified disease severity levels to maximise clinical relevance.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42024570014.

Background

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a mood disorder marked by persistent sadness, loss of interest and emotional and physical symptoms that severely impact daily functioning, affecting one out of five individuals in their lifetime.1 The standard of care for MDD involves a combination of evidence-based interventions, primarily pharmacotherapy and psychotherapy. Psychotherapeutic approaches, such as cognitive–behavioural therapy and interpersonal therapy, are effective in treating mild-to-moderate depression and in preventing relapse.2 Pharmacotherapy is essential for moderate-to-severe cases, offering rapid symptom relief and neurochemical balance.3 It typically begins with first-line antidepressants, including selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors and serotonin norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors, due to their broad efficacy and favourable side-effect profiles. For patients who do not respond to initial therapy or who have specific clinical indications, alternatives such as tricyclic antidepressants, monoamine oxidase inhibitors and atypical antidepressants may be considered. Despite the widespread use of antidepressants, substantial variability in treatment response remains, with 30%–40% of patients failing to achieve adequate response or remission, partly due to genetic differences impacting drug efficacy and side effects.4 This variability, coupled with a trial-and-error approach to prescribing, can result in delayed relief, prolonged suffering and an elevated suicide risk.5 These challenges highlight the urgent need for more precise, personalised treatment strategies to improve outcomes for individuals with MDD.6

Pharmacogenomics (PGx) studies how genes influence drug response.7 Initially focused on single genes like CYP2C19 and CYP2D6 affecting antidepressant metabolism, it now uses multigene panels to guide personalised treatment. This approach helps clinicians choose effective antidepressants with fewer side effects, improving outcomes and minimising trial-and-error in managing MDD.8 Despite its promise, the uptake of PGx testing in clinical settings has been slow, hindered by factors such as cost, lack of standardised guidelines and insufficient evidence about its clinical utility.9 Several meta-analyses published so far evaluating the efficacy of PGx-guided therapy for MDD consistently report moderate improvements over traditional treatment approaches.1012 For example, a 2024 meta-analysis by Santenna et al included 18 clinical trials and found that PGx-guided antidepressant selection was associated with greater efficacy in achieving remission of depressive symptoms at 8 and 12 weeks, with pooled risk ratios (RR) of 1.523 (95% CI 1.255 to 1.843) and 1.631 (95% CI 1.001 to 2.657), respectively.12 Similarly, Brown et al analysed four studies and reported patient outcomes were significantly improved for PGx-guided group compared with unguided care (symptom improvement Δ=10.08%, 95% CI 1.67 to 18.50).11 However, concerns about the generalisability and inclusivity of these findings persist. First, genetic variants in drug-metabolising enzymes, such as CYP2D6 and CYP2C19, differ significantly across ethnic groups. Meta-analyses that do not stratify by ethnicity risk overlooking population-specific effects, thereby limiting the applicability of their conclusions. Second, PGx panel composition varies widely across studies. Some panels focus on key metabolic genes (eg, CYP2D6, CYP2C19), while others include broader gene sets encompassing drug transporters (eg, ABCB1) and drug targets (eg, serotonin receptors).1315 Analyses based on panel design are necessary to determine whether broader panels offer greater clinical utility compared with more targeted approaches. Finally, the efficacy of PGx-guided treatment may differ based on the severity of MDD. Patients with moderate-to-severe or treatment-resistant depression appear to benefit more from personalised therapy than those with mild symptoms, as evidenced by the GUIDED (Genomics Used to Improve DEpression Decisions) trial.16 Stratifying by baseline severity ensures findings are relevant to specific patient subgroups.

Objective

This study uses subgroup and cumulative meta-analyses to explore how ethnicity, disease severity and genetic testing scope influence outcomes, aiming to improve PGx accuracy and relevance for diverse populations.

Study selection and analysis

This systematic review and meta-analysis was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) database (registration number: CRD42024570014) and followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses statement. The protocol is available on the following website: https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/display_record.php?RecordID=570014.

Search strategy

We searched the online databases of PubMed, Ovid Embase, Ovid Medline, Ovid PsycINFO and the Cochrane Library for randomised controlled trials (RCTs) published before December 2024. The board search terms “pharmacogenomics AND antidepressant” were used. We also searched ClinicalTrials.gov for unpublished studies by inputting the “major depressive disorder” in the “Condition/disease” section, “pharmacogenomics” in the “Other terms” section and “antidepressants” in the “Intervention/treatment” section.

Eligibility criteria

We included RCTs investigating the clinical efficacy and/or safety of PGx-guided interventions compared with the standard of care in patients with MDD. The inclusion criteria are: (1) studies enrolled participants diagnosed with MDD based on established diagnostic criteria (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) or the International Classification of Diseases (ICD)) and excluded those with significant suicide risk; severe co-occurring psychiatric disorders (eg, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia or personality disorders); cognitive impairment or unstable or serious medical conditions (eg, cancer).

Studies included participants diagnosed with MDD according to established diagnostic criteria, such as the DSM or the ICD, without significant suicide risk, severe co-occurring psychiatric disorders (eg, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia or personality disorders), cognitive impairment or unstable or serious medical conditions (eg, cancer); (2) the intervention of interest was PGx-guided antidepressant treatment, in which PGx testing (assessing variants in drug-metabolising enzymes, drug transporters or drug target receptor genes) was used to inform the prescribing of antidepressants; (3) the comparator group received treatment-as-usual (TAU) without PGx guidance; (4) studies were required to report clinical outcomes related to the severity of depressive symptoms, such as response rate and remission rate, measured by validated clinical scales such as the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D), Montgomery-Asberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS), Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) or Quick Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology (QIDS); (5) only RCTs were included. The exclusion criteria are: (1) studies did not involve PGx-guided treatment; (2) studies did not include an unguided treatment arm as a control group; (3) studies included non-human subjects; (4) studies did not report relevant clinical outcomes; (5) letters, reviews, editorials or articles published in languages other than English or Chinese.

Titles and abstracts were independently screened by two investigators, and full-text articles of eligible trials were retrieved. Disagreements regarding study inclusion were resolved through discussion with a third investigator.

Data extraction and quality assessment

The following data were extracted: name of the first author, publication date, country where the studies were conducted, trial name, diagnosis, age, ethnicity, blinding settings and female percentage of the participants, PGx test characteristics, antidepressants prescribed, follow-up duration, total number of the participants, number of the responders, number of the remitters, changes in symptom severity, reported effect size. If critical data were missing for calculating the pool effects in the meta-analysis, we contacted authors first, if no response was received, the study would be excluded from analysis. Given that the PGx-guided versus TAU trials are designed to assess superiority and include long-term follow-up, we used a modified intention-to-treat (mITT) approach for our primary analysis.17 This approach includes only those participants who were available for assessment at each follow-up visit. If mITT data were unavailable, we used per-protocol (PP) data instead. Additional analyses based on PP data were conducted to ensure robustness of our results (online supplemental figure 4).

We assessed methodological quality using the Cochrane Collaboration risk-of-bias (RoB) tool, which evaluates the randomisation process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of outcomes, selection of the reported result and other potential sources of bias.

Data extraction and quality assessment were independently accomplished by two investigators. Any disagreement was resolved by discussion with another investigator when necessary.

Statistical analysis

The ‘meta’ package in R (V.4.3.1) was used for the meta-analyses. We included the following outcomes at 8 and 12 weeks of follow-up: (a) response was defined as ≥50% decrease at week 8 in the assessment of interest (eg, HAM-D17, CGI-I or MADRS) from baseline; (b) remission was defined as having a score of ≤7 for HAM-D17, ≤2 for CGI-I, ≤5 for QIDS-C16, ≤ for MADRS and <5 for PHQ-9. HAM-D17 is the primary assessment tool used for outcome evaluation. In case HAM-D17 is not reported, CGI-I or MADRS (CGI-I over MADRS if both presented) can be used. Effect sizes for dichotomous outcomes were expressed as RRs with 95% CIs, calculated using the Mantel-Haenszel method. Differences were considered statistically significant at p<0.05. The primary outcomes of our meta-analyses are the response rate and remission rate for PGx-guided treatment versus TAU at 8 weeks of follow-up. Secondary outcomes include the response rate and remission rate at 12 weeks of follow-up.

Subgroup analyses assessed the influence of predefined modifiers, specifically geographic regions and severity of MDD. For geographic analysis, we classified studies into those conducted in Asian countries and those in non-Asian countries. Studies from Asian countries (China, South Korea and Japan) involved only Asian participants, while studies from non-Asian countries (the USA, Canada, the Netherlands, Spain and Australia) predominantly included Caucasian participants, with minority representation from Black, Latin American, Asian individuals and other ethnic groups. For the severity of MDD, it is categorised by history of at least one treatment failure or unsatisfactory response to current antidepressant therapy versus unspecified MDD severity.

Cumulative meta-analyses were conducted to determine pooled RRs and CIs, sequentially by the number of genes analysed in each study. A p value for a subgroup test of <0.1 indicates a statistically significant subgroup effect.

Heterogeneity was tested using Cochran’s Q test and I2 statistics. For the Q statistic, p<0.05 was considered statistically significant; and for the I2 statistic, heterogeneity was considered nil, low, moderate and high when I2 was 0, 0<I2≤25%, 25%<I2≤75% and I2>75%, respectively. Data from eligible studies were combined using inverse variance by a random-effects model if I2≥50% or p≤0.05; otherwise, a fixed-effects model was used. Publication bias was assessed by visual inspection of funnel plot asymmetry and through Begg’s and Egger’s tests. The trim-and-fill method developed by Duval and Tweedie was used to adjust for potential publication bias. The leave-one-out method, in which one study is omitted at a time, was used for sensitivity analyses.

Findings

Literature search

After the initial search, 7063 articles were identified. Following title and abstract screening, 2354 duplicates and 4502 irrelevant articles were removed. Consequently, 207 full-text articles were retrieved for review, of which 193 were excluded after full-text assessment. In total, 13 articles met the inclusion criteria for this study. Study characteristics

This review included 14 studies from Asia (China: two,14 18 Korea: one19), Europe (the Netherlands: one,13 Spain: one20), America (Canada: one,21 the USA: eight1516 2226) and Oceania (Australia: one27). One non-randomised trial26 was excluded, leaving 13 studies (2013–2024) with participants aged 14–80 years. Unlike traditional drug trials, physicians in PGx-guided studies are typically unblinded because they must prescribe according to PGx reports. As a result, blinding is generally restricted to patients and outcome raters, although physicians may sometimes serve as raters. Due to this characteristic, the studies can be categorised into five patient-not-blinded14 18 19 23 26 and nine patient-blinded trials.1315 16 2022 24 25 27 Four trials16 19 22 23 recruited patients with difficult-to-treat MDD (two trials included participants with at least one prior treatment failure; one focused on treatment-resistant MDD and another on patients with poor response or intolerance to current antidepressants). One trial15 recruited patients with moderate-to-severe depression, and the rest of the eight trials13 14 18 20 21 24 25 27 included patients with depressive disorder not otherwise specified. Nine trials1516 1921 23 used commercial PGx tools (GeneSight: four; Neuropharmagen: two; NeuroIDgenetix: one; CNSDose: one; GeneceptAssay: one), and the rest four trials13 14 18 22 used laboratory developed tests (LDTs). One trial13 using LDT only covered pharmacokinetic genes, and the rest of the trials141618 chose to detect both pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic genes. Follow-up durations ranged from 4 to 24 weeks. Because of the design of these RCTs, prescribers cannot be blinded since they must select antidepressants for patients based on the results of the PGx reports. By using RoB 2.0, we rated seven studies as ‘high risks’14 18 19 22 23 25 and six studies as ‘some concerns’.13 15 16 20 21 24 27 Detailed characteristics are provided in online supplemental figure 1.

Pairwise meta-analyses

Conventional meta-analyses compared the response and remission rates of PGxs-guided treatment versus TAU for antidepressants at 8 and 12 weeks. For the 8-week response rate, nine studies (2622 PGx cases, 2568 TAU controls) showed a pooled relative risk (RR) of 1.23 (95% CI 1.05 to 1.43, I²=50%), indicating a moderate benefit of PGx-guided treatment. At 12 weeks, four studies (1552 PGx cases, 1550 TAU controls) showed an RR of 1.29 (95% CI 1.17 to 1.43, I²=0%), also demonstrating a moderate benefit. For remission rates, 8-week data from eight studies (2402 PGx cases, 2360 TAU controls) showed an RR of 1.37 (95% CI 1.19 to 1.57, I²=46%), reflecting a modest improvement. However, at 12 weeks, four studies (1486 PGx cases, 1503 TAU controls) showed an RR of 1.56 (95% CI 0.93 to 2.61, I²=72%), with results considered to be insignificant due to wide CIs. Sensitivity analyses have been performed for the meta-analyses of response rate at 8 weeks and remission rate at 12 weeks due to moderate heterogeneity presented in these two analyses. The leave-one-out analysis has shown that the pooled RR (RR 1.29, 95% CI 1.17 to 1.42) was similar to that of the overall analysis after omitting the study of Perlis et al with no heterogeneity (I2=0%). The study by Perlis et al showed atypical results, which may be attributable to variations in assay design, reporting methods and an unusually high response rate observed in the TAU group.24 As for the pooled RR for the remission rate at 12 weeks, after excluding the study by Singh, the result became significant (RR 1.39, 95% CI 1.18 to 1.63), with a much lower I2 (I2=20%, online supplemental figure 3). The variability may result from the small sample size in the study by Singh (74 cases and 74 controls), which likely inflated effect sizes and increased heterogeneity, widening CIs.28 29

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore the potential effect of prespecified modifiers including region (Asian Countries and non-Asian Countries) and presence of difficult-to-treat MDD. Subgroup analysis of 8-week treatment response by geographic region revealed a statistically significant subgroup effect (p=0.02, common effect; p<0.01, random effects), indicating that region significantly modifies the comparative effectiveness of PGx-guided versus TAU approaches. While PGx-guided treatment was favoured in both Asian and non-Asian countries, the effect size was greater in the Asian countries (RR 1.51, 95% CI 1.26 to 1.81, I2=0%) than in the non-Asian countries (RR 1.17, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.30, I2=44%). However, the Asian countries included fewer trials and participants (three trials, 384 vs 387 participants) compared with the non-Asian countries (six trials, 2238 vs 2181 participants), highlighting the need for future validation. Similar findings were observed for 8-week treatment remission, with both subgroups favouring PGx-guided treatment and a larger effect in the Asian countries (RR 1.61, 95% CI 1.26 to 2.07, I2=0% vs RR 1.28, 95% CI 0.85 to 1.91, I2=58%), although the test for subgroup differences was not significant (p=0.14, common effect; p=0.21, random effects). The uneven distribution of sample sizes between subgroups further underscores the need for additional validation. Due to limited number of studies reporting response and remission rates at 12 weeks, subgroup analyses were not conducted.

We conducted subgroup analyses to examine how MDD severity influences the efficacy of PGx-guided treatment, categorising patients based on the presence of difficult-to-treat MDD. The test for subgroup differences of 8-week treatment response showed no statistically significant effect (p=0.27, common effect; p=0.31, random effects), indicating that categorising MDD as ‘difficult-to-treat’ or ‘not specified’ does not significantly modify the comparative effectiveness of PGx-guided versus TAU treatment. The MDD not specified group had fewer participants than the difficult-to-treat group (849 vs 750 and 1633 vs 1697, respectively), which may limit the power to detect subgroup differences. Additionally, there was substantial heterogeneity among trials in the MDD not specified group (I2=68%), making the treatment effect estimate for this subgroup less reliable (RR 1.15, 95% CI 0.85 to 1.57). Notably, PGx-guided treatment was favoured over TAU in the difficult-to-treat MDD group (RR 1.30, 95% CI 1.14 to 1.48, I2=0%) for response at 8 weeks, a finding consistent for remission at 8 weeks (RR 1.44, 95% CI 1.20 to 1.72, I2=0%). In contrast, the effect in the MDD not specified group was uncertain for remission at 8 weeks (RR 1.27, 95% CI 0.70 to 2.23, I2=68%) due to uneven sample sizes and high heterogeneity (MDD not specified group: 694 vs 589 and MDD difficult-to-treat group: 1708 vs 1771). The test for subgroup differences in remission at 8 weeks was also not significant (p=0.35, common effect; p=0.52, random effects). Given the limited number of articles reporting response and remission rates at 12 weeks, subgroup analyses were not conducted. The forest plots of subgroup analysis are presented in figure 3.

Cumulative meta-analyses

Cumulative meta-analyses were performed to illustrate how the effect size evolves over the number of genes included in the PGx tests. This analysis was conducted for the response rate at 8 weeks. There were nine eligible trials, and a cumulative meta-analysis using random-effects models revealed that PGx-guided treatment began showing significant effectiveness following the inclusion of the second study. The initial study, conducted by Shan et al, examined the impact of five genes: CYP2C19, CYP2D6, CYP1A2, SLC6A4 and HTR2A. Subsequently, Greden et al investigated eight genes: CYP1A2, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP3A4, CYP2B6, CYP2D6, HTR2A and SLC6A4. The overall impact of PGx-guided treatment remained modestly favourable throughout the rest of evidence. Furthermore, the cumulative analysis of remission rates at 8 weeks began showing significant improvements with the addition of the third study by Oslin et al, which examined a total of 12 genes (two idiosyncratic adverse drug reaction genes, two pharmacodynamic genes and eight pharmacokinetic genes). The only notable temporal variation in effect sizes occurred with the inclusion of study by Perlis et al, where the lower limit of 95% CIs marginally crossed the threshold of no effect (RR 1.32, 95% CI 0.98 to 1.78). Nevertheless, the overall results maintained statistical significance, indicating a modest advantage for the PGx-guided approach. The forest plots of cumulative meta-analyses are shown in figure 4.

Conclusions and clinical implications

PGx-guided therapy, which is increasingly used in psychiatric treatment, involves baseline genetic testing for variants related to drug metabolism, drug transport and drug targets that may affect antidepressant response. Test results are provided to clinicians as prescribing reports or recommendations to help guide antidepressant selection. However, questions remain about treatment efficacy, the choice of gene variants and the influence of ethnicity; therefore, we conducted a meta-analysis to address these issues.

Our pairwise meta-analyses, incorporating the most recent RCT conducted by Xu et al,14 showed that PGx-guided treatment significantly improved response rates at both 8 and 12 weeks, with pooled RRs of 1.23 (95% CI 1.05 to 1.43) and 1.29 (95% CI 1.17 to 1.43), respectively. These findings indicate a moderate yet consistent advantage of PGx-guided approaches in optimising antidepressant therapy. In contrast, the results for remission rates were inconsistent. A significant improvement in remission was observed at 8 weeks (RR 1.37, 95% CI 1.19 to 1.57), but the 12-week remission rate did not achieve statistical significance (RR 1.56, 95% CI 0.93 to 2.61), likely due to substantial heterogeneity among studies. This suggests potential limitations of PGx-guided treatment in achieving full remission, particularly over extended follow-up periods. Our findings align with recent meta-analyses, which consistently demonstrate significant benefits of PGx-guided therapy over TAU in improving 8-week response and remission rates in MDD. However, 12-week remission rates were often inconclusive due to high heterogeneity and limited data.3034

Subgroup analyses by geographic region indicated that PGx-guided treatment may be especially effective in Asian countries; however, the small sample size in this group highlights the need for further validation. At 8 weeks, the response rate showed an RR of 1.51 (95% CI 1.26 to 1.81), and the remission rate exhibited an RR of 1.61 (95% CI 1.26 to 2.07), both with no observed heterogeneity (I2=0%). In contrast, remission rates in non-Asian countries at 8 weeks were not statistically significant. Although the test for subgroup differences did not reach statistical significance for remission at 8 weeks, these findings imply that genetic and pharmacological factors might interact differently among various ethnic groups, potentially affecting the effectiveness of PGx-guided interventions. One major contributor to the variability in drug response is the difference in allele frequencies of genes encoding drug-metabolising enzymes, transporters and receptors across ethnicities. For instance, CYP2C19 and CYP2D6 are key enzymes in antidepressant metabolism, processing 15%–20% and 50%–60% of these drugs, respectively.35 36 Poor metabolisers (PMs) for CYP2C19 are more common in East Asians (15%–20%) due to loss-of-function alleles like CYP2C19*2 and *3, compared with Europeans (2%–5%) and Africans (1%–2%).37 Ultra-rapid metabolisers (UMs), linked to CYP2C19*17, are less frequent in Asians (under 5%) but more common in Europeans and Africans (15%–30%).38 39 For CYP2D6, PMs are more prevalent in Europeans (5%–10%) than Asians (1%–2%), while UMs are higher in Middle Eastern and North African populations (10%–20%).40 41 These variations in the allelic patterns of CYP2C19 and CYP2D6 across ethnicities may significantly influence the response to specific antidepressants. For example, citalopram/escitalopram and sertraline are primarily metabolised by CYP2C19, whereas paroxetine and venlafaxine are predominantly metabolised by CYP2D6.35 In addition to genetic factors, environmental influences such as diet, smoking and concomitant medications can further modify the effects of genetic polymorphisms, resulting in population-specific drug responses.4244 These findings underscore the importance of considering both genetic and environmental factors when implementing PGx-guided treatment strategies across diverse populations.

When analysing specific populations with difficult-to-treat MDD compared with those with MDD not specified, our results revealed interesting findings. For the 8-week response rate, individuals with difficult-to-treat MDD demonstrated a significant RR of 1.30 (95% CI 1.14 to 1.48), suggesting improved outcomes with PGx-guided treatment. On the contrary, the MDD not specified group did not achieve statistical significance, and heterogeneity among studies was substantial. Regarding remission rates at 8 weeks, four studies in the difficult-to-treat MDD subgroup reported a significant RR of 1.44 (95% CI 1.20 to 1.72). In contrast, the MDD not specified group, also comprising four studies, showed a non-significant RR of 1.27 (95% CI 0.70 to 2.32) with high heterogeneity. Due to limited data, subgroup analyses for 12-week outcomes were not conducted. Although the test for subgroup differences between these two groups was not statistically significant for either response or remission at 8 weeks, these findings indicate that the benefits of PGx-guided treatment are not universal across all individuals with MDD but are particularly significant in those who have experienced inadequate responses or intolerable adverse effects to antidepressants. In the broader MDD population, especially among individuals with mild-to-moderate depression, first-line antidepressants are generally effective, reducing the incremental value of PGx testing in this group.45 Additionally, in such populations, where the likelihood of remission with standard treatment is high, the cost-effectiveness of PGx-guided approaches may be limited.9 10 Therefore, in clinical practice, prioritising PGx-guided treatment for individuals with difficult-to-treat MDD could enhance its clinical utility and maximise its benefits. However, further research with larger sample sizes is warranted to confirm these results and address heterogeneity in broader MDD populations.

Cumulative meta-analyses were conducted to assess how the effect size varies with the inclusion of additional genes in PGx tests. For the response rate at 8 weeks, the pooled effect size reached statistical significance on inclusion of the second study, which analysed eight genes. The effect size remained modestly favourable towards the PGx-guided approach, with minimal variation as the number of genes increased from 8 to 26 (RR 1.23–1.30). A similar trend was observed for the remission rate at 8 weeks, where the effect size became significantly favourable for the PGx-guided treatment following the inclusion of the third study, which analysed 12 genes. The remission rate remained consistent, with RR values ranging from 1.32 to 1.44. These findings suggest that while adding more genes to PGx panels may enhance the observed effect, there appears to be a threshold beyond which further inclusion of genes does not result in significant improvements in clinical outcomes. This limited improvement may result from insufficient evidence supporting the clinical relevance of certain single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), as MDD is associated with multiple pathogenic factors, and its aetiology remains poorly understood.46 For instance, SNPs from pharmacodynamic targets and pharmacokinetic genes—such as SLC6A4 (5-HTTLPR), HTR2A (rs6311, rs6313 and rs7997012) and ABCB1 (rs1128503, rs2032582, rs1045642 and rs9282564)—are frequently included in PGx panels but often exhibit inconsistent associations with clinical outcomes.35 45 These SNPs typically have small effect sizes or population-specific relevance, and the inclusion of low-evidence SNPs in broader panels risks generating data that are difficult to interpret, ultimately limiting the clinical utility of such tests.4749 Additionally, while PGx testing using multigene panels may provide greater accuracy, the associated costs of genotyping can be prohibitively high, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.50 Unfortunately, no comparative trials have been conducted so far to assess the cost-effectiveness of large genetic panels versus smaller panels, highlighting a critical gap in the evidence base.

In summary, our meta-analysis demonstrates that PGx-guided antidepressant treatment moderately improves response rates at 8 and 12 weeks compared with standard care, although its long-term impact on remission remains uncertain due to study variability. The benefits are particularly notable among Asian populations—likely reflecting genetic differences in drug metabolism—and in patients with treatment-resistant depression. Expanding gene panels beyond 8–12 genes offers little additional benefit and may reduce clinical practicality. Further research is needed to assess the cost-effectiveness of larger panels, long-term remission outcomes and treatment’s efficacy across more finely stratified levels of disease severity. Focusing PGx testing on difficult-to-treat cases and refining gene panel selection may maximise clinical utility.

This study has several limitations. First, we did not compare adverse drug event (ADE) risks between PGx-guided and TAU groups due to inconsistent measurement methods and time points across trials. Second, our assessment of genetic panels was based on the number of genes rather than SNPs, as gene coverage more accurately reflects drug metabolism pathways. Third, we did not analyse the cost-effectiveness of PGx-guided treatment because of variability in genotyping costs and healthcare systems among countries. Future research should aim to optimise multigene panel design for maximum clinical benefit within specific economic and healthcare contexts. Additionally, some included studies informed patients of their PGx-guided treatment (patient not blinded), while others did not (patient blinded), introducing potential variability and bias. Our supplementary subgroup analyses based on whether patients were blinded or not (online supplemental figure 5) demonstrated a significant advantage of PGx-guided treatment in patient-not-blinded trials for both 8-week response and remission rates, with no detected heterogeneity, while results from patient-blinded trials were less consistent due to heterogeneity. However, the ambiguous findings and moderate heterogeneity (in the patient-blinded subgroup, I² was 50% for the 8-week response rate and 68% for the 8-week remission rate) prevent us from drawing definitive conclusions. Future trials should implement full blinding for participants, raters and prescribers—not just the first two—to minimise expectancy bias.24 Finally, due to the lack of a standardised definition for the TAU approach, treatment decisions based on physicians’ clinical judgement may introduce potential bias in outcome evaluation, as the use of non-pharmacological interventions could not be entirely excluded in these trials.
