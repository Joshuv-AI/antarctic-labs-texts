Estimation of fMRI responses related to epileptic discharges using Bayesian hierarchical modeling
Authors: Zhengchen Cai, Nicolás von Ellenrieder, Andreas Koupparis, Hui Ming Khoo, Satoru Ikemoto, Masataka Tanaka, Chifaou Abdallah, Saba Rammal, Francois Dubeau, Jean Gotman
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractSimultaneous electroencephalography–functional MRI (EEG‐fMRI) is a unique and noninvasive method for epilepsy presurgical evaluation. When selecting voxels by null‐hypothesis tests, the conventional analysis may overestimate fMRI response amplitudes related to interictal epileptic discharges (IEDs), especially when IEDs are rare. We aimed to estimate fMRI response amplitudes represented by blood oxygen level dependent (BOLD) percentage changes related to IEDs using a hierarchical model. It involves the local and distributed hemodynamic response homogeneity to regularize estimations. Bayesian inference was applied to fit the model. Eighty‐two epilepsy patients who underwent EEG‐fMRI and subsequent surgery were included in this study. A conventional voxel‐wise general linear model was compared to the hierarchical model on estimated fMRI response amplitudes and on the concordance between the highest response cluster and the surgical cavity. The voxel‐wise model overestimated fMRI responses compared to the hierarchical model, evidenced by a practically and statistically significant difference between the estimated BOLD percentage changes. Only the hierarchical model differentiated brief and long‐lasting IEDs with significantly different BOLD percentage changes. Overall, the hierarchical model outperformed the voxel‐wise model on presurgical evaluation, measured by higher prediction performance. When compared with a previous study, the hierarchical model showed higher performance metric values, but the same or lower sensitivity. Our results demonstrated the capability of the hierarchical model of providing more physiologically reasonable and more accurate estimations of fMRI response amplitudes induced by IEDs. To enhance the sensitivity of EEG‐fMRI for presurgical evaluation, it may be necessary to incorporate more appropriate spatial priors and bespoke decision strategies.Keywords: Bayesian workflow, BOLD percentage change, EEG‐fMRI, epilepsy, hierarchical model, IEDs, presurgical evaluation

INTRODUCTION

Simultaneous electroencephalography–functional magnetic resonance imaging (EEG‐fMRI) is a unique and noninvasive multimodal brain imaging technique (Ritter & Villringer, 2006; Warbrick, 2022). Several studies have demonstrated and assessed the utility of EEG‐fMRI for focal epilepsy presurgical evaluation (Ikemoto et al., 2022). In a common workflow applied in such studies, epilepsy patients are scanned with EEG‐fMRI during the resting state for around one hour. Then, interictal epileptic discharge (IED) events, such as spikes, are annotated from denoised scalp EEG signals. In order to localize the hemodynamic responses associated with IEDs, a conventional task‐related fMRI processing pipeline is followed by applying a general linear model (GLM). An IED indicator function is convolved with a hemodynamic response function (HRF) to construct the expected hemodynamic response covariate in the design matrix of the GLM. The primary response cluster is defined in the blood oxygen level dependent (BOLD) response map as the cluster, which contains the highest absolute response using the standardized effect size (e.g., t‐value or z‐score). This primary response cluster is considered the spike or IED onset zone, which is a marker of the epileptogenic zone (Khoo et al., 2018; Pittau et al., 2012). The clinical utility of EEG‐fMRI in focal epilepsy presurgical evaluation is then assessed by comparing it to the seizure onset zone identified with stereo‐EEG (Khoo, Hao, et al., 2017; Yamazoe et al., 2019) or electrocorticography (van Houdt et al., 2013), and the surgical resection cavity with a known outcome (An et al., 2013; Coan et al., 2016; Koupparis et al., 2021).

In the subsequent five paragraphs, we introduce the issues in conventional fMRI analysis within EEG‐fMRI studies and explain our rationale for the proposed solution in this study, including (1) the lack of spatial association among voxels in conventional analysis and the necessity of modeling fMRI data with a spatial prior; (2) the advantages of involving a spatial prior, especially for estimating fMRI responses related to IEDs; (3) the use of the hierarchical model to estimate more accurately the BOLD percentage change related to IEDs; (4) the application of Bayesian methodology for improving the model inference; and (5) the model comparisons to select the optimal HRF for each brain region, balancing the model fitting accuracy and generalizability.

The conventional task‐related fMRI analysis mentioned above performs a GLM fit for each voxel's BOLD signal, denoted as a massively univariate analysis. From a modeling perspective, all voxels are assumed to be independent and the a priori underlying hemodynamic response of each voxel follows the (−∞,+∞) uniform distribution (Chen et al., 2022). The fMRI response amplitude certainly has finite mean and variance. According to information theory, the least informative prior (most objective) exhibiting the maximum entropy should be a Gaussian distribution rather than a uniform distribution (Conrad, 2004). Additionally, spatial correlations of the signal do exist across voxels due to the finite sampling of BOLD signal and spatially spread hemodynamic responses (Woolrich et al., 2004). A simple and effective solution to these methodological issues is to introduce spatial smoothing in the voxel‐wise analysis, which requires an empirically determined kernel size (Mikl et al., 2008). More sophisticated approaches model the expected spatial correlation among voxels as a spatial prior in Bayesian inference, including the most widely used Gaussian Markov random field prior (Gössl et al., 2001; Smith & Fahrmeir, 2007), a spatial regularization prior using the Laplacian operator to allow data determined smoothness (Penny et al., 2005) or a stochastic partial differential equation prior considering local spatial dependence and sparsity (Mejia et al., 2020; Spencer et al., 2022). These approaches mainly consider the local homogeneity of fMRI responses. However, the brain works as a network that involves both local and distributed neuronal assemblies (Varela et al., 2001). This means that local brain activity induced by a specific stimulus co‐exists and interacts with the underlying spontaneous brain activity (Greicius et al., 2003) that drives distributed neuronal assemblies, also known as the resting‐state functional networks. Therefore, from a modeling perspective, introducing a spatial prior involving both local and distributed homogeneity would be a more reasonable choice. Specifically, such a spatial prior could be constructed using a cortical parcellation atlas, for instance, Schaefer2018 (Schaefer et al., 2018), which is generated by considering the local‐distributed homogeneity of the fMRI resting state data.

Models using the aforementioned spatial priors are categorized as hierarchical models since they take into account the homogeneity of the variables of interest at different levels (e.g., voxel, local region, and distributed network). They not only make more realistic assumptions—exchangeability rather than independence among units at each level—but are also practically useful by introducing regularizations in parameter estimations (Papaspiliopoulos et al., 2007). Hence, they prevent the estimated fMRI response from overfitting BOLD data for each voxel. This advantage may play an important role in estimating fMRI responses related to IEDs since they are observational rather than predefined experimental events as in conventional task related fMRI studies. Indeed, the occurrence of IEDs varies from patient to patient, and the low signal‐to‐noise ratio caused by the small number of IED events limits EEG‐fMRI application to IED‐active patients (Kowalczyk et al., 2020; Szaflarski, 2022).

To determine the brain response regions related to IEDs, statistical significance filtering is usually conducted after performing null hypothesis tests for all voxels (Worsley & Friston, 1995). In the massively univariate analysis, the significance threshold requires an adjustment to control the Type I error (false‐positive) after conducting the same test multiple times. Different adjustment approaches have been proposed to handle this multiple testing issue, for instance, the random field theory (Worsley et al., 1998), nonparametric permutation test (Nichols & Holmes, 2002) or cluster‐extent based thresholding (Woo et al., 2014). However, these approaches only adjust the stringency of the statistical evidence to remove potentially noisy voxels, with the estimated response amplitudes remaining the same. As the estimated response amplitude (e.g., BOLD percentage change) is recommended to be a measure of the effect of the task (Chen et al., 2017), we may also need to pay attention to the accuracy of the estimated effect itself. Indeed, the magnitude of the effect is usually overestimated in voxels that survive the significance filtering, and the overestimation factor is denoted as the Type M (Magnitude) error (Gelman & Carlin, 2014) or exaggeration ratio (estimated effect magnitude over true effect magnitude). Hierarchical models perform regularization during parameter estimation to reduce the Type M error, since individual level estimations shrink toward the corresponding group level estimation within the hierarchical structure reflecting the homogeneity of the effect. In addition, multiple testing issues are addressed inherently in such models since effects are estimated together rather than individually (Gelman et al., 2012).

Instead of estimating only the mean value and the standard error of the mean for every parameter, Bayesian inference estimates its distribution by sampling possible values from the joint distribution of all parameters in the model. This allows uncertainty to propagate across all parameters simultaneously resulting in more accurate and reliable inferences (Gelman et al., 2013; McElreath, 2020). Bayesian inference has become more accessible in recent years. For instance, an open‐source Bayesian statistical modeling and computation platform called Stan (Stan Development Team, 2020) provides the most recent implementations of the dynamic Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) sampling algorithm (Betancourt, 2017, 2019), which have been utilized in several neuroimaging studies (Cai et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2019, 2021; Dai et al., 2021; Hashemi et al., 2020).

In addition, the HRF of IED may not be necessarily the same as the canonical one commonly used in task fMRI analysis (Lu et al., 2006, 2007). To address this issue, voxel‐specific HRFs were either modeled by the canonical HRF and its temporal derivatives (Lemieux et al., 2008; Zijlmans et al., 2007) or selected using the highest absolute t‐value from four candidates HRFs peaking at 3, 5, 7, and 9 s (Bagshaw et al., 2004; Khoo, Hao, et al., 2017; Khoo et al., 2018). This approach is promising in practice but may also face the overfitting problem considering it is fully data driven, and an HRF candidate that fits better the noise might result in a higher t‐value, especially for cases with a small number of IED events. Bayesian leave‐one‐out cross‐validation (LOO‐CV; Vehtari et al., 2017) could be applied to avoid this issue by considering HRF candidates as different models and performing model comparisons to select the “best” one, which balances fitting accuracy and generalizability.

In this study, we aimed to estimate fMRI responses related to IEDs, represented by BOLD percentage changes, using a hierarchical model. It involved the local and distributed hemodynamic response homogeneity to regularize parameter estimations. The Bayesian workflow was applied to select local HRFs through model comparison, estimate the posterior distributions of all parameters simultaneously, and prevent unreliable inferences from pathological behaviors during the model fitting (Gelman et al., 2020). We processed an EEG‐fMRI data set acquired from epilepsy patients using this proposed hierarchical model and a standard voxel‐wise model, then compared their performance from the following perspectives: (1) the “Type M error” of the estimated effect, as assessed by the ratio of BOLD percentage changes between the two models; (2) the ability to differentiate BOLD percentage changes induced by brief (e.g., spike) and long‐lasting (e.g., burst) IEDs, since the latter is expected to induce stronger hemodynamic response; and (3) clinical utility in presurgical evaluation, as determined by the concordance between the cluster exhibiting the highest absolute fMRI response and the surgical cavity. The presurgical evaluation performance was also compared with the results of a previous study using the same data set (Koupparis et al., 2021).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patients population

Eighty‐two epilepsy patients at the Montreal Neurological Institute and Hospital (MNI) were included with the following inclusion/exclusion criteria: underwent EEG‐fMRI scan between 2006 and 2019, were operated and had a follow‐up of at least 1 year with postoperative brain imaging. T1‐weighted MRI (1.0 mm isotropic) was prioritized for the postoperative image, otherwise, MR sequences with slice separation smaller than 5.0 mm or CT scans were considered. Surgical outcomes were classified according to the Engel system (Engel Jr, 1993). Prior to participation, every patient signed a written informed consent (approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the MNI).

Data acquisition and processing

EEG‐fMRI scan protocol

T1‐weighted anatomical images and fMRI were scanned in a 3 T Siemens scanner (Trio before and Prisma after 2017, Germany) at the MNI. fMRI data were collected with multiple 6 min runs lasting in total 60–90 min. T2* sequence had a repetition time of 1.75 s and 5.0 mm isotropic spatial resolution until July 2008, and after that, a repetition time of 1.90 s and 3.7 mm isotropic spatial resolution. BrainAmp MR (Brain Products GmbH, Germany) was used to simultaneously record scalp EEG during the fMRI scan. There were 25 MR‐compatible electrodes including the 10–20 (reference FCz) system and additional electrodes from the 10–10 system (F9, T9, P9, F10, T10, and P10). Field maps scanned using Siemens fm2d2r sequence were also available in some patients' data. Detailed EEG‐fMRI scan protocol descriptions can be found in our previous studies (An et al., 2013; Khoo, Hao, et al., 2017; Khoo et al., 2018; Yamazoe et al., 2019).

EEG signal processing

MR and ballistocardiogram artifacts were removed using average artifact subtraction (Allen et al., 1998, 2000) in the BrainVision Analyzer (Brain Products GmbH, Germany). We used the same annotations and classifications of IED events conducted by experienced neurophysiologists from previous studies (Khoo, Hao, et al., 2017; Khoo et al., 2018; Koupparis et al., 2021). The timing and duration of every IED were used to convolve with an HRF to represent the expected hemodynamic responses related to IEDs in the design matrix of linear models.

Anatomical image processing

T1‐weighted images were processed using fMRIPrep 20.2.5 (Esteban et al., 2019, 2020) including the following steps: intensity non‐uniformity correction (ANTs 2.3.3, Avants et al., 2008; Tustison et al., 2010); brain extraction and tissue segmentation (FSL 5.0.9; Zhang et al., 2001); and brain mask refinement combining ANTs‐ and FreeSurfer‐derived gray matter (Klein et al., 2017). Schaefer2018 cortical parcellation atlas (Schaefer et al., 2018) along with the FreeSurfer subcortical segmentation (Fischl et al., 2002) were coregistered to the preprocessed patient specific preoperative T1‐weighted image. Postoperative images were coregistered to patient‐specific preoperative images. A semiautomatic process was applied to define the surgical resection cavity mask (Billardello et al., 2022) followed by manual validation, and refinement if necessary, by experienced neurophysiologists.

Functional image processing

BOLD runs were preprocessed using fMRIPrep 20.2.5. Briefly, a reference BOLD volume was defined using a median of motion corrected volumes. The field map‐based susceptibility distortions correction (Glasser et al., 2013) was performed on the reference BOLD volume if the field map is available. Otherwise, we applied the symmetric normalization approach, which first estimates the deformation field and corrects the distortions by the nonlinear registration of the reference BOLD volume to the anatomical T1‐weighted images (Treiber et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2017). The clinical relevance of distortions correction has been demonstrated by Lima Cardoso et al., 2018 and was recently recommended for general task fMRI data processing (Roopchansingh et al., 2020) to reduce the potential activation localization error. BOLD runs were then slice‐time corrected and coregistered to the anatomical T1 using the distortion‐corrected reference BOLD volume and boundary‐based registration (Greve & Fischl, 2009). Nuisance covariate time series were calculated including discrete cosine high‐pass filter with 128 s cut‐off; 24 head motion parameters with respect to the BOLD reference; framewise displacement (FD; Power et al., 2014); and global signal averaged within the brain mask and its derivative. BOLD frames exceeding an arbitrary 1 mm FD threshold or the upper bound of FD (i.e., third quantile+1.5×interquartile range) were considered as motion outliers and excluded from the analysis. Voxel‐wise BOLD signal and design matrix were high‐pass filtered by regressing out discrete cosine covariates and then concatenated across all runs assuming stationarity across runs after removing the slow drift. We also converted BOLD signal to percentage changes relative to voxel‐wise temporal mean, since BOLD data do not have a clear physical and physiological meaning and the baseline is different across participants. It is the percentage that can be compared across participants and conditions (Chen et al., 2022; Jia et al., 2020; Moses et al., 2014).

Hierarchical modeling

Local and distributed hemodynamic responses modeling

We propose a hierarchical model involving local and distributed hemodynamic responses to IEDs using the Schaefer2018 atlas and Freesurfer subcortical parcellation as the prior (Figure 1). Hence, the fMRI response in each voxel was estimated not only from its own data, but also adjusted according to the local homogeneity of responses within each region of interest (ROI) consisting in a local neighborhood of voxels. ROI‐level responses were further regularized to take into account the distributed homogeneity using the resting state networks to which they belong. Hierarchical Bayesian modeling of fMRI responses related to IEDs. Each voxel's fMRI response is estimated by its own data and regularized by two higher hierarchy levels: (1) the local region of interest (ROI) expressing the level of local homogeneity across voxels, and (2) the network expressing the level of distributed homogeneity across ROIs. This hierarchical prior corresponds to the underlying assumption of fMRI connectivity atlas (i.e., Schaefer2018 atlas): local‐distributed homogeneity among fMRI signals. In total, there were 16 networks (1 subcortical and 7 cortical networks in each hemisphere) and 316 local regions (300 cortical ROIs + 16 subcortical ROIs, nucleus accumbens cannot be shown in this view).

The above model is presented in the so‐called centered parameterization. In practice, we converted it to a completely equivalent non‐centered version in order to obtain faster and more stable model fittings, as the equations below indicate. Note that weakly informative priors are used in this model for all parameters to ensure they cover the range of reasonable values for every parameter and do not bias the results. For instance, a Normal0,2 with a constrain of ρn,r,v≤1 ensures that the absolute autocorrelation coefficient value is smaller than 1 and that any value in this range is not preferred before fitting the model with the data; a Normal0,5 covers the range of possible response amplitudes related to IEDs; and half‐normal priors ensure variance parameters are nonnegative.

We considered 300 cortical and 16 subcortical ROIs. To define the networks, cortical ROIs were grouped following the seven networks defined in Thomas Yeo et al., 2011, and subcortical ROIs were grouped into one network (Figure 1). Considering that responses related to IEDs are usually lateralized, we separated the left and right hemispheres resulting in 14 cortical and 2 subcortical networks.

This hierarchical model was compared with a voxel‐wise model in which the voxel level response, βv, was estimated only from its own data. The preprocessed fMRI data, design matrix, and weakly informative priors mentioned previously were used. Note that we additionally applied spatial smoothing on the input data using a 6 mm Gaussian kernel only for the voxel‐wise model considering that it is the standard process in the conventional analysis.

HRF selection using model comparison

We adapted the previous HRF selection process (Bagshaw et al., 2004; Khoo, Hao, et al., 2017; Khoo et al., 2018) to the model comparison perspective. We first constructed four ROI‐specific hierarchical models using four different HRFs for each ROI assuming the HRF is ROI‐specific. This ROI‐specific hierarchical model maintains the voxel–ROI hierarchical structure while omitting the network level to reduce computational costs. For instance, in a scenario where a network comprises 20 ROIs, excluding the network level results in 20×4 distinct models to be fitted. In contrast, when accounting for the network level, this count expands significantly to 420. Subsequent model comparisons would also become more complicated. Then one HRF was selected for each ROI using a model comparison approach balancing generalizability and model fitting accuracy. Models with misspecification issues or fittings dominated by a few enormously influential data points were first eliminated from the candidates. Models that provided significantly better generalizability than others were first selected, and among these, the model that exhibited the best fitting accuracy was finally chosen. This process prioritized generalizability since the clinical application is to localize the SOZ using data with limited IED samples. It could also avoid models providing suspiciously high effect sizes, which may be caused by overfitting to noisy data points. Each selected ROI‐specific HRF was also used for all the voxels of the ROI in the voxel‐wise model to ensure that the same HRF was used in both analyses.

In detail, Bayesian LOO‐CV (Vehtari et al., 2017) was conducted to assess the model generalizability and the absolute z‐score of the ROI level response was used to evaluate the model fitting accuracy. LOO‐CV applies Pareto smoothed importance sampling (PSIS) to avoid refitting each model many times. Additionally, the diagnostic of PSIS also provides an assessment of model misspecification, erroneous data points, and potential mistakes in data processing (Gabry et al., 2019). We followed the criteria suggested by Sivula et al. (2022) to eliminate problematic HRF candidates and compared the generalizability across candidates.

Model fitting and diagnostics

The objective of model fitting is to estimate all parameter values in the model when data are given. Bayesian inference estimates a probabilistic distribution of every parameter, from which summary statistics can be calculated. Intuitively, it is a numerical sampling approach that takes a specific combination of values for all parameters and computes the posterior value of the joint distribution of all parameters by applying the Bayes theorem (i.e., multiplying prior and data likelihood values calculated using this specific combination). After repeating this sampling process many times, all calculated posterior values represent the joint distribution of all parameters. This means that any given combination of parameters is assessed by how likely it is (i.e., posterior value) knowing the data. Finally, possible values of a single parameter represented by its posterior distribution can be obtained by averaging over all other parameters. We preferred Bayesian inference since estimating the joint distribution allows uncertainty, represented by possible values, to propagate among all parameters simultaneously, which usually results in more accurate and reliable inferences (Gelman et al., 2013; McElreath, 2020).

In practice, all parameters in aforenoted models were estimated simultaneously using a full Bayesian approach. The HMC algorithm (Betancourt, 2017, 2019) implemented in the Stan probabilistic programming language (Stan Development Team, 2020) was used to sample the joint posterior distribution of the model. The resulting marginal posterior distribution of each parameter contained 4000 values sampled by four randomly initialized HMC chains. Each chain consisted of 2000 samples, including a first half warm‐up phase for the adaptation of the HMC parameters. Network‐specific hierarchical models were fitted in parallel using the high‐performance computing clusters available at the Digital Research Alliance of Canada (https://alliancecan.ca/en). The model fitting results of all networks were then combined together for later model inferences. To prevent inaccurate inference caused by pathological behaviors of the modeling fitting, we monitored the diagnostic metrics of the HMC sampling recommended by Gelman et al., 2020.

Statistical inference

Statistical inference of task‐based fMRI analysis usually involves a definition of the null activation and the significance filtering of all voxels' responses compared with the null. We defined a range for null instead of the conventional absolute 0 response. It is centered around 0 and covers possible fluctuations of the background activity. This range is usually referred to as the region of practical equivalence (ROPE). Instead of defining a consistent empirical ROPE (e.g., ±0.5% BOLD change), we estimated it for each individual map using all voxels' posterior distributions (Figure 2). The 50% highest density interval (HDI) of the posterior distribution of each voxel's percentage change was first calculated. Then all voxels whose 50% HDI included 0 were selected and considered as the population of null. Finally, the ROPE was defined as the 89% credible interval of all values in the population of null to be able to cover the majority range of percentage change values. Note that the 50% interval is an arbitrary choice, and 89% is commonly used in Bayesian inference to represent a credible interval with sufficient accuracy (Kruschke, 2014).

The posterior mean of the BOLD percentage change of every voxel quantifies the fMRI response related to IEDs, and to perform significance filtering, two statistics were calculated using the posterior distribution of the IED related response of every voxel, the z‐score, and the Bayes factor (BF). The z‐score was computed as the posterior mean over the posterior standard deviation for voxels whose posterior mean was outside the ROPE. The Bayes factor, indicating the relative evidence of the posterior with respect to the prior, was computed with respect to the ROPE range and assuming the prior odd equals 1. The arbitrary statistical significance thresholds were defined as absolute z‐score >3.1 or BF >3. Connected sets of at least six voxels that passed the significance filtering were considered the region of activation (Koupparis et al., 2021).

Assessment of the estimated fMRI responses

The estimated fMRI responses related to IEDs were compared between the hierarchical model and the voxel‐wise model from two perspectives: (1) amplitude of the estimated BOLD percentage change. A Mann–Whitney U test between the maximum absolute BOLD percentage changes (i.e., max(|%|)) estimated by the two models was performed to assess whether they provided statistically different estimations. After excluding null voxels, voxel‐wise ratios of estimated BOLD percentage changes between the two models (the voxel‐wise model over the hierarchical model) were computed and the median value of these ratios was selected to represent the Type M error for each individual patient. As the two models may not always provide the same sign of the estimated response on all voxels, we did not consider voxels for which different signs of the estimated response were presented by the two models. It is important to note that the calculated Type M error in this context actually represents the ratio of two Type M errors, one for each of the two models. This approach is necessary because of the absence of ground truth response amplitude in real data. Since the hierarchical model is the denominator, if the calculated ratio exceeds 1, the voxel‐wise model may yield a higher Type M error; (2) the ability to differentiate BOLD percentage changes induced by brief (e.g., spike) and long‐lasting (e.g., burst) IEDs. Spikes were considered as brief IEDs since their duration is much shorter than one TR of the fMRI sequence, hence, it is represented by only an instantaneous event for fMRI. A Mann–Whitney U test was performed to evaluate whether these two types of IEDs exhibited statistically different BOLD percentage changes across the two models. In addition, we investigated whether the estimated maximum absolute percentage BOLD change and the Type M error were related to the number of IEDs or to the number of IED related data points using the Pearson's correlation test after a log–log transformation. Note that we investigated these two variables since the number of IEDs is a more general and practical metric of the sample size, whereas the number of IED‐related data points is the effective sample size considering the possible overlapping of fMRI responses related to different IEDs.

Clinical utility in presurgical evaluation

To assess the clinical utility of the hierarchical model in epilepsy presurgical evaluation, we conducted a similar analysis as Koupparis et al., 2021, calculating the concordance between the cluster exhibiting the highest absolute fMRI response (primary cluster) and the surgical cavity. Two groups of surgical outcome prediction were defined: (1) concordant/partially concordant if the peak response was inside the cavity, or up to 2 cm away and part of the primary cluster overlaps with the cavity, and (2) discordant/partially discordant if the peak response was more than 2 cm away from the cavity, disregarding the possible overlap between the cavity and the primary cluster or any other significant cluster. The effect was represented by either the posterior mean of the estimated BOLD percentage change or the corresponding z‐score. Accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), Matthew's correlation coefficient (MCC), and F1‐score were used to quantify the performance of the prediction. Larger values indicating better performance are preferred in all these metrics.

Robustness to choice of nuisance regressors

Previous studies have shown that different GLM design matrix setups may result in different denoising performances (Mascali et al., 2021; Parkes et al., 2018). This may influence the results of the comparison between the hierarchical and the voxel‐wise models and the clinical utility in presurgical evaluation. To evaluate the robustness of the method to such changes, we repeated the analyzes with a different set of nuisance regressors. Unless stated otherwise, the results shown in the next section correspond to the main nuisance model consisting of 24 motion regressors plus the global signal and its derivatives, concatenating the regressors of different runs. The second set of nuisance regressors consisted of six motion regressors plus the global signal, and the regressors were allowed to vary among runs.

RESULTS

Model fitting summary

Hierarchical and voxel‐wise model fittings did not show pathological behaviors. HMC chains converged well, as indicated by low R^ values, and the effective sample size of every parameter was sufficient according to the recommended criteria (Gabry et al., 2019; Vehtari et al., 2020). Overall, the model fitting time varied according to the occurrence of IEDs in each run, the CPU assigned by the high performance computing clusters, and the fMRI sequence. As an example, the run time for 1 h fMRI data with a repetition time of 1.90 s and 3.7 mm isotropic spatial resolution took 6.7 h using an AMD Rome 7532 CPU. The adapted HRF selection approach resulted in different HRFs compared to the one, which would have been selected using the highest absolute t‐value approach in 33% of the ROIs. This indicated that either an HRF model had significantly better generalizability than the one producing the highest absolute t‐value or that misspecification issues were detected (diagnostics in LOO‐CV) in one or more HRF models.

Comparison of estimated BOLD percentage changes

The voxel‐wise model overestimated fMRI responses compared to the hierarchical model. This was evident in the estimated BOLD percentage changes, which demonstrated a practically and statistically significant difference. Differentiation of brief and long‐lasting IEDs

The estimated response amplitude, for instance, max(|%|), from the hierarchical model showed a weaker association with the number of IED‐related data points, suggesting that it may be less affected by the number of observed data points regarding the estimated response amplitude, a highly desirable situation. Pearson's correlation test in log–log scale between the number of IED related data points and max(|%|) showed a negligible correlation (r = −0.09, p = 0.42, Figure 5a) in the hierarchical model, and a higher correlation for the voxel‐wise model (r = −0.28, p < 0.05; Figure 5c). fMRI responses related to brief and long‐lasting IEDs were better separated visually in the hierarchical model compared with the voxel‐wise one (Figure 5a,c). Alternative interpretation of response maps

To perform an objective interpretation of the response maps, typically the cluster containing the voxel with the maximum absolute response (t‐value, z‐value, or percentage change) is identified and used for defining the concordance with clinical variables. This is a straightforward and objective approach since the response maps can have multiple clusters of significant activation with varied response levels. In this section, we explore the results that can be obtained if the interpretation is changed to select two clusters, the activation with the highest response (positive peak) and the deactivation with the lowest response (negative peak).

The union of these two clusters was used to determine concordance with the resection cavity, that is, as long as one of these two peaks was inside the cavity, it was considered a good prediction for an Engel 1–2 case, whereas all of them had to be outside the cavity to be a good prediction for an Engel 3–4 case. We found that the sensitivity value was increased without worsening too much the specificity using this alternative criterion, and all other performance values were also increased (Table 3). For instance, accuracy increased from 0.67 to 0.70, sensitivity increased from 0.46 to 0.68, whereas specificity decreased from 0.79 to 0.71. PPV increased from 0.57 to 0.58; NPV increased from 0.71 to 0.79; MCC increased from 0.27 to 0.38; and F1‐Score increased from 0.51 to 0.63.

Robustness to nuisance regressor choice

When compared to the above Sections (3.2, 3.5), similar results were found when using 6 head motion and 1 global nuisance regressors per run instead of using 24 head motion and 2 global nuisance regressors. When comparing estimated BOLD percent changes (Figure S1), the voxel‐wise model overestimated fMRI responses compared to the hierarchical model. The median value of patient‐wise Type M error was 1.60 with an 89% HDI range of [0.87–2.50]. The hierarchical and voxel‐wise model estimated max(|%|) was 1.1 ± 0.7% and 5.5 ± 2.2%, respectively. Mann–Whitney U test between max(|%|) estimated by the two models showed a statistically significant difference of 3.7 ± 1.8%. The Type M error was also affected by the number of IEDs and the number of IED‐related data points indicated by statistically significant negative correlations (Figure S2): r = −0.29 (p < 0.05) between the number of IEDs and the Type M error, and r = −0.31 (p < 0.05) between the number of IED related data points and the Type M error. Only the hierarchical model differentiated brief and long‐lasting IED with a significant difference in max(|%|) values (Figure S3b,d): 0.7 ± 0.3% for brief versus 1.6 ± 1.2% for long‐lasting IEDs (p < 0.05) in the hierarchical model; 5.0 ± 2.0% for brief versus 5.7 ± 2.5% for long‐lasting IEDs (p = 0.19) in the voxel‐wise model. Additionally, there was a subtle correlation (r = −0.10, p = 0.40, Figure S3a) between the number of IED‐related data points and max(|%|) for the hierarchical model. However, for the voxel‐wise model, the magnitude of this correlation was increased to r = −0.25 (p < 0.05, Figure S3c).

Regarding the presurgical evaluation results, the hierarchical model outperformed the voxel‐wise model in all performance metrics (Table S1) except for a lower specificity value (0.68 versus 0.81). When compared to our previous study the hierarchical model showed the same sensitivity of 0.57, it is noteworthy that the nuisance parameters model of the previous study also consisted of six movement regressors and one global signal regressor per run. All other metrics for the hierarchical model were higher than this previous analysis. Additionally, max(|%|) also provided higher performance metric values compared to max(|%|), except for the same specificity of 0.76 (Table S2). Finally, when using two peak clusters, all performance metric values increased with a small decrease in specificity from 0.68 to 0.67 (Table S3).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we propose a hierarchical model involving regularizations using local and distributed homogeneity of hemodynamic activity to estimate fMRI responses related to IEDs, which are represented by BOLD percentage changes rather than the standardized effect using z‐score or t‐value. Although the physical and physiological basis of BOLD signal remains unclear, the percentage signal change relative to voxel‐wise temporal mean has been suggested to represent the effect value for task fMRI studies (Chen et al., 2017, 2022; Taylor et al., 2022). It is the direct output value of the parameter associated with the expected hemodynamic response covariate in the GLM model, when BOLD raw data are converted to percentage changes. The estimation accuracy of the BOLD percentage change is then crucial since the response contrast map is generated using its mean value, whereas the statistical parametric map is constructed by standardizing it into z‐score or t‐value. Then these maps are usually used to interpret fMRI responses related to a task; for instance, IEDs in our case. Therefore, we first focused on the estimation accuracy of the BOLD percentage change related to IEDs.

It is known that when an estimation is statistically significantly different from zero, the corresponding effect size (e.g., mean of the estimation) tends to be overestimated compared to the truth. Such an exaggeration ratio, denoted as the Type M error, decreases toward 1 when increasing the statistical power of the study (Gelman & Carlin, 2014). When the underlying effect size and the stringency of the statistical test are fixed, power is positively associated with the sample size. Hence, reducing the Type M error requires increasing the sample size. In a task fMRI study, sample size can be experimentally controlled by the number and duration of task events. However, EEG‐fMRI studies related to epilepsy are observational where the number and type of IED events vary across patients, which is one of the main limitations (Kowalczyk et al., 2020; Szaflarski, 2022). This motivated us to decrease the Type M error from a data analysis perspective—introducing regularizations in the GLM model using a hierarchical spatial prior. Our results illustrate that the conventional voxel‐wise model indeed overestimates the fMRI response amplitude related to IEDs compared to the hierarchical model. For instance, when comparing the maximum absolute percentage change value, the voxel‐wise model provided larger values: 5.3% versus 1.1% in the hierarchical model (Figure 3a,c). When comparing the BOLD percentage change estimated by the two models for the same voxel, Type M errors using the hierarchical model as the reference were also mostly larger than 1 across all patients (Figure 3b and Figure S1b). Note that this does not demonstrate that the hierarchical model did not overestimate or underestimate the effect size; it was simply used as a reference to calculate the exaggeration ratio since the ground truth value is unknown. Given the absence of known ground truth with patient data and our inability to formally calculate Type M errors, we also conducted a simulation study detailed in the Supporting Information. In brief, the realistic BOLD signal simulation was conducted by adding the resting state data from one patient to the noise‐free hemodynamic response time course. The latter was generated by convolving a pre‐assumed HRF with randomly jittered events. Different simulation scenarios were also considered, including 96 different numbers of events ranging from 10 to 200, and three levels of max(|%|) scales. In the simulation study, we were able to calculate the Type M errors for both models using the actual ground truth and illustrated them separately. The results are in line with our main results of the real data shown in Figures 3, 4, 5. Notably, the median Type M errors for the hierarchical model were very close to 1 (i.e., 1.05, 1.02, and 1.01) across three simulated amplitude scales (see Figure S4). Therefore, the ratio of Type M error between the voxel‐wise model and the hierarchical model, as presented in the results from real data, may be an accurate estimation of the true Type M error of the voxel‐wise model.

However, there are several reasons why it is safe to assume that the hierarchical model results are closer to the ground truth than the voxel‐wise results. First, from a theoretical point of view, regularization involved in the hierarchical model helps to reduce the Type M error, whereas the voxel‐wise model fully relies on data of each voxel. Second, response amplitudes of common tasks such as motor, visual, and working memory, typically lead to BOLD percentage changes of approximately 1.0%–3.0% in block design and of 0.2%–1.0% in event‐related design (Jia et al., 2020). IEDs are pathological events that may or may not induce larger fMRI response amplitudes than these tasks, but the maximum possible increase in BOLD signal estimated by gas manipulation tasks in the calibrated fMRI literature for 3T magnetic field strength is 5.3%–12.1% with a median of 7.3% reported in Gauthier et al. (2011). The voxel‐wise model provided more cases exhibiting abnormally high percentage values than the hierarchical model (e.g., larger than 7% in Figure 3 and Figure S1). A third reason supporting the superiority of the hierarchical model is given by the association between the Type M error and the IED sample size. It was also in line with the theoretical expectation—Type M error reduces when increasing the sample size—and additionally, the maximum absolute percentage changes estimated by the hierarchical model did not reveal a clear relationship with the IED sample size, while this relationship was more pronounced in the voxel‐wise model (Figure 5a,c and Figure S2a,c). This indicates that the hierarchical model is less affected by the sample size, that is, more robust to its variation. Finally, the hierarchical model also provides more reasonable estimations of the fMRI response induced by IEDs, as assessed by the ability to differentiate BOLD percentage changes of brief (e.g., spike) and long‐lasting (e.g., burst) IEDs illustrated in Figure 5 and Figure S3. It is expected that fMRI responses induced by long‐lasting IEDs will be higher than those induced by brief IEDs, as the former may be associated with stronger neuronal activity. Even if the neuronal activity remains the same strength in a unit of time, longer events would also result in higher response amplitude due to the accumulations during the convolution with the HRF.

Considering that hemodynamic responses to IEDs might be different from the canonical HRF, in our previous studies (Bagshaw et al., 2004; Khoo, Hao, et al., 2017; Khoo et al., 2018; Koupparis et al., 2021), voxel‐specific HRF, which provided the highest absolute t‐value was selected from four candidates (i.e., peaking at 3, 5, 7, and 9 s). This approach is promising in practice but methodologically, it might be challenged by the overfitting problem, for instance, an HRF candidate that fits better the noise might result in a higher t‐value, especially when the number of IED events is small. Besides, the highest absolute t‐value may not assure the best generalizability, especially with a small number of IEDs. Therefore, we adapted the HRF selection process from the model comparison perspective. Bayesian LOO‐CV (Vehtari et al., 2017) was used to select the “best” HRF, which balances fitting accuracy and generalizability. Our results show that among all ROIs of all patients, 33% resulted in different HRFs compared to the one that would have been selected using the highest absolute t‐value approach. They were selected for one of the following reasons: (1) significantly better generalizability; (2) model misspecification issues detected in diagnostics of LOO‐CV of other HRF candidates, and (3) the highest absolute t‐value was achieved, but diagnostics of LOO‐CV indicated that it may be dominated by a few very influential data points for other HRF candidates. Detailed explanations of these criteria can be found in Sivula et al. (2022).

Spatial prior Bayesian models have been proposed (Zhang et al., 2015) to solve similar issues in estimating task‐induced fMRI responses. We advance this idea by taking advantage of the recent progress in the Bayesian data analysis workflow. Comparing to the previous approaches, our method may include the following advantages: (1) 3D modeling instead of solving 2D slices (Gössl et al., 2001; Penny et al., 2005), which is possible due to the increase in computation power of the recent years; (2) local and distributed regularization instead of mainly sharing information locally (Mejia et al., 2020; Spencer et al., 2022); (3) distributed information was shared within networks rather than within the whole brain (Chen et al., 2022). This prior may be more consistent with our understanding of the brain organization and more reasonable from the hierarchical modeling perspective. The networks and ROIs in the atlas were indeed generated using local and distributed homogeneity of the fMRI resting state data; (4) single‐stage model fitting instead of solving the conventional voxel‐wise model first and then feeding the estimated effects into a second stage hierarchical model (Bowman et al., 2008; Sanyal & Ferreira, 2012). Considering that the key advantage of Bayesian inference is to allow uncertainty to propagate across all parameters, resulting in more accurate and reliable inferences, simultaneously fitting all parameters can be crucial; (5) prevent unreliable inferences from pathological behaviors during the model fitting. Despite the advantages of hierarchical models, parameters in such models are usually dependent on each other, creating “high curvature” areas in the joint distribution. These areas are denoted as Neal's Funnel (Neal, 2003) which might cause inaccurate model inference depending on the model setup and data quality (Betancourt & Girolami, 2015). Diagnostics of pathological behaviors during the modeling fitting provided by Stan also help to indicate the potential occurrence of these issues, hence, increasing the reliability of the estimated parameter distributions.

Regarding presurgical evaluation, the hierarchical model outperforms the voxel‐wise model as indicated by higher classification performance metrics (Table 1 and Table S1). We first opted to conduct an apple‐to‐apple comparison; hence, this voxel‐wise model involved the same processes as the hierarchical model in the following aspects: data preprocessing except for spatial smoothing required for the voxel‐wise model, HRFs for all voxels, model fitting technique and decision strategy on defining the primary response cluster. When compared with our previous study (Koupparis et al., 2021) using a voxel‐wise analysis in the fMRIstat toolbox (Worsley et al., 2002), the hierarchical model showed higher values in most performance metrics, but the same or lower sensitivity using two nuisance regressor setups. Therefore, in general, we found only a small improvement using the hierarchical model in presurgical evaluation. The voxel‐wise analysis of this current study performed worse than the previous voxel‐wise analysis using the fMRIstat toolbox, except for better specificity. One possible reason is that fMRIstat models multiple runs using a mixed effect model considering within and between run variability of the BOLD responses for the same voxel. This approach of combining the information of different runs might be superior to simply concatenating the runs, since it can reduce the influence of extremely high response amplitude estimates from runs with high within‐run variance, that is, runs for which the confidence should be low.

We also investigated the prediction performance difference when using the maximum absolute effect size (max(|%|)) and the maximum absolute standardized effect size (max(|z|)). When the Type M error was reduced using the hierarchical model, the maximum absolute effect size outperformed the maximum standardized effect size (Table 2 and Table S2). When comparing BOLD percentage change distributions of voxels exhibiting these two types of maximum effect, it was also clear that max(|z|) voxel was not necessarily the one exhibiting the highest response amplitude (Figure 8). This has been illustrated using hypothetical and real examples (Chen et al., 2017, 2022; Taylor et al., 2022), suggesting the use of the estimated response amplitude as the measure of effect size and using standardized effect size to represent the statistical evidence. With the experience gained from this study, we would like to comment on this suggestion by considering different conditions: (1) when Type M error is handled by either applying more sophisticated models such as the hierarchical model in this study, or sufficient power is assured experimentally (e.g., a high number of trials), then the estimated response amplitude may be preferred as a measure of the effect of the task, for example, determining the location of the response as the highest effect voxel; (2) if the power is limited by practical reasons (e.g., a small number of events) and Type M error is not reduced in data analysis, standardized effect size may be more appropriate as an effect size measure and for decision making, because the estimated response amplitude could be overestimated, while the standardized effect size might provide some level of correction of the overestimation, penalizing the effect amplitude by its variance.

Different nuisance parameter models may result in different denoising performances (Mascali et al., 2021; Parkes et al., 2018), and therefore different fMRI response estimations. To study the robustness of the method, we compared the results of two typical choices of nuisance regressors. While the results showed some differences in the estimated percentage values, they resulted in similar general conclusions on comparing estimated fMRI responses and presurgical evaluation performance.

Finally, an alternative decision‐making approach considering the whole response map might result in better prediction performance: studies have for instance selected the response cluster which initiated the propagation of the IED‐related responses by modeling causal relationships among all clusters on the map (Iannotti et al., 2016, 2020). In this study, by simply including both the highest positive and the highest negative response clusters, all performance metrics were improved without worsening too much the specificity (Table 3 and Table S3).

There are some limitations of our hierarchical model: (1) fMRI connectivity studies have shown the complexity of epilepsy brain networks, which may differ from those of the healthy population (An et al., 2015; Khoo, von Ellenrieder, et al., 2017; Khoo et al., 2019; Su et al., 2019). Our hierarchical model used Schaefer2018 (Schaefer et al., 2018) atlas, which was created using healthy subjects' fMRI resting state data. The atlas only acts as a spatial prior in our model rather than a hard constraint. Bayesian inference also combines this prior with patient‐specific data to overcome this issue at some level. In other words, if a certain network or ROI of a patient differs from the atlas, their data still reflect underlying homogeneity structure across locations to calibrate the final inference. However, we acknowledge that it may reduce the efficiency of the regularization in the hierarchical model. Recent cortical parcellation studies have provided a more advanced approach to individualize parcellations using few minutes subject‐specific fMRI resting state data and a prior atlas generated from a large population (Kong et al., 2021; Qiu et al., 2022). This approach also prevents parcellations from overfitting to data. Applying such a method may potentially improve our results but it was outside of the scope of this study; (2) we did not further structure all networks into a higher level such as the whole brain. This means the information was not shared across networks, which may not be correct considering that there are specific nodes acting as hub regions involved in the interaction among networks (Lee et al., 2016, 2018). Modeling such interactions in a hierarchical model is possible in theory and it is known as the cross‐classified hierarchical model in which voxels in hub regions can be considered in all corresponding networks rather than only to the network to which they belong. However, such a model is computationally expensive, especially when using Bayesian inference; (3) an even more appropriate modeling strategy is to directly consider the correlations among ROIs as the covariance matrix in a multivariate hierarchical model instead of grouping them into networks; hence, fMRI responses and the ROI level connectivity can be estimated simultaneously. This concept has been proposed by Spencer et al. (2020), but only lobe‐level correlations were considered due to current computation power limitations; (4) although the adapted four HRF process considered the variability of HRF shapes across brain regions and balanced fitting accuracy and generalizability, there are more flexible approaches that do not require predefined HRFs. For instance, the HRF deconvolution approach with regularization on the smoothness of the HRF can directly estimate the HRF from data (Chen et al., 2023; Cherkaoui et al., 2021; LeVan et al., 2010; Lu et al., 2006, 2007). This might be more effective for our application, considering that the variability of HRF shapes related to epilepsy discharges might not be fully encompassed by the four HRFs; (5) when comparing the presurgical evaluation performance with our previous study that employed a voxel‐wise approach involving a mixed‐effects model for within‐ and between‐run variability of the BOLD responses, an improvement was observed primarily in specificity. However, the performance enhancement became evident when compared to the same voxel‐wise approach implemented in this study, which did not regularize the response estimations across runs using a mixed‐effects model. We assume that such a mixed‐effects approach in the temporal domain is similarly effective as our hierarchical model in space. Therefore, the performance may be further improved by combining spatial and temporal pooling in future work, especially to account for IED trial‐to‐trial variability rather than arbitrarily modeling run‐to‐run variability.

CONCLUSION

In this study, we proposed a voxel–ROI–network hierarchical model involving the local and distributed hemodynamic response homogeneity to regularize estimations of fMRI responses related to IEDs, represented by BOLD percentage changes. Our results provided more physiologically reasonable and more accurate estimations of the fMRI response amplitude induced by IEDs compared to the usual voxel‐wise approach. Similar observations were obtained using two different nuisance regressor setups. While only small improvements were obtained in the presurgical evaluation compared to a standard method, the use of an approach incorporating local and network‐level spatial homogeneity produces more reliable estimates of the BOLD response amplitude, provides a framework for further improvements, and could be adopted in the clinical application of EEG‐fMRI to epilepsy.
