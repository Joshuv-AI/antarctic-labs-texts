Real-world outcomes after CAR T versus standard therapy in third-line or later relapsed or refractory follicular lymphoma in the United States
Authors: Philip K. Chan, Markqayne Ray, Karl M. Kilgore, Iman Mohammadi, Christie Teigland, Andrew Ip
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundRelapsed/refractory (R/R) follicular lymphoma (FL) often requires multiple lines of therapy.ObjectiveTo compare time to next treatment (TTNT), treatment-free interval (TFI), healthcare resource utilization (HRU), and downstream costs among R/R FL patients treated with CAR T versus non-CAR T therapy.MethodsAdult R/R FL patients with ≥2 prior lines of therapy were stratified into CAR T and non-CAR T cohorts. The Index Date was CAR T infusion (CAR T) or 3L initiation (non-CAR T). Outcomes included TTNT, TFI, HRU, and costs. Analyses were descriptive, incorporating Kaplan-Meier methods and multivariable models.Results335 CAR T and 4,342 non-CAR T patients were included. Median TTNT was not reached for CAR T, with longer TFIs observed in descriptive analyses. Adjusted analyses showed lower pharmacy costs at both 3L+ (CR 0.32; 95% CI 0.21–0.48; p < 0.0001)and 4L+ (CR 0.26; 95% CI 0.16–0.42; p < 0.0001), and post-CAR T medical costs at 4L+ (CR 0.69; 95% CI 0.51–0.94; p = 0.017).ConclusionsIn this real-world analysis, CAR T was associated with longer TTNT, longer TFI, and lower downstream FL-related costs compared with non-CAR T therapy, with interpretation informed by differences in follow-up time and treatment context.

Introduction

Follicular lymphoma (FL) is the second most common form of non-Hodgkin lymphoma and the most prevalent indolent lymphoma [1], representing 30% and 70% of these cases, respectively [2,3]. It is a slow progressive disease necessitating lifelong monitoring and possibly multiple therapies [4–6]. FL is often discovered incidentally [3].

FL is clinically heterogeneous. Although some patients experience prolonged remission, approximately 20% will have aggressive disease, experiencing frequent relapses and shorter duration of response [7], multiple therapies, and reduced overall survival [8], resulting in greater cumulative clinical and economic burden, including payer-covered medical and pharmacy expenditures [9]. Moreover, the economic burden increases with each subsequent line of therapy (LOT) [10] as the durability of remission decreases, driving cumulative medical and pharmacy expenditures [11].

Due to its clinical heterogeneity, managing relapsed/refractory (R/R) FL is challenging and involves a multifaceted approach encompassing chemotherapy, rituximab-based immunotherapy, radioimmunotherapy, targeted therapies, and considerations for autologous or allogeneic stem cell transplantation (SCT) [10,12,13].

he introduction of axicabtagene ciloleucel (axi-cel) [14], the first chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR T) therapy approved for R/R FL, expanded treatment options for this population in March 2021. In the United States (US), axi-cel is indicated for use in R/R FL after at least two prior LOTs (3L or higher). Other FDA-approved CAR T agents for R/R FL treatment include tisagenlecleucel (tisa-cel) [15] approved in May 2022, and most recently lisocabtagene maraleucel (liso-cel) [16,17] approved in May 2024. Outside the US (ex-US), axi-cel is indicated for treatment of FL in the fourth line or higher, specifically in the European Union member states and the European Economic Area countries, including Iceland, Liechtenstein, and Norway, as approved by the European Medicines Agency [18–20]. More recently, bispecific antibodies such as mosunetuzumab and epcoritamab have been approved for relapsed or refractory FL, reflecting an evolving treatment landscape [21].

To date, real-world studies of R/R FL have demonstrated promising improvements in response rates and survival among patients treated with CAR T [22,23]. However, evidence on downstream treatment patterns, healthcare resource utilization (HRU), and associated costs remain limited. Additionally, patients who receive CAR T often differ from those treated with non-CAR T therapies in clinical characteristics, treatment history, and timing of index treatment, which should be considered when interpreting real-world comparisons [24,25]. This study aims to evaluate real-world treatment patterns, HRU, and costs in Medicare FFS and commercially insured patients treated with non-CAR T and CAR T therapies, with a specific focus on the post CAR T phase of the patient journey compared to the experience of non-CAR T patients in higher LOTs.

Methods

General approach

This observational, retrospective study used claims data to describe downstream treatment patterns, HRU, and costs observed following exposure to CAR T versus non-CAR T therapy in later-line R/R FL. Index definitions differed by cohort (CAR T infusion versus 3L initiation) to reflect real-world treatment pathways and regulatory indications during the study period, consistent with prior real-world CAR T analyses that define cohort entry based on treatment-specific clinical milestones rather than uniform calendar time [26,27]. These index points represent non-equivalent clinical milestones and should be considered when interpreting between-cohort comparisons.

Study design and data sources

Patients with R/R FL using two distinct administrative datasets: the 100% Medicare Fee-for-Service (FFS) data and the commercially insured patient data from the Inovalon Medical Outcomes Research for Effectiveness and Economics (MORE2) Registry [28]. Both datasets include beneficiary health plan enrollment and demographic characteristics as well as medical and pharmacy encounters, such as hospital admissions, emergency department (ED) visits, hospital outpatient/ambulatory surgical center services, physician office visits, post-acute care, and comprehensive prescription drug utilization. HRU and cost measures were derived from adjudicated medical and pharmacy claims.

The 100% Medicare FFS claims data, available through a Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) research data use agreement, includes Part A (hospital coverage) and Part B (physician visits and outpatient care) medical claims, Part D Prescription Drug Event data, as well as health plan enrollment and demographic characteristics of enrollees. The payer sourced MORE2 data constitutes a statistically de-identified warehouse of healthcare medical and pharmacy claims from more than 150 US health plans operating in all 50 states and the District of Columbia. For both FFS and commercial data, total healthcare costs reported in this study included payer-paid medical and pharmacy claims. Upfront CAR T acquisition and administration costs were excluded to focus the analysis on downstream healthcare utilization and follow-up costs, rather than on estimation of the total cost of care. Both datasets contain deidentified claims data and were used in accordance with applicable data use agreements.

Patient population and study periods

Adult patients with R/R FL who received ≥3 LOTs were stratified into non-CAR T and CAR T cohorts. Eligible patients were ≥18 years of age at diagnosis of FL. The FL diagnosis identification period spanned different timeframes based on the availability of claims data for the two payer cohorts: Medicare FFS covered the period from 1 July 2016, to 31 March 2022; commercial plan data covered from 1 July 2016, to 30 September 2022. Diagnosis of FL was defined as the presence of at least 1 inpatient or 1 outpatient claim with an ICD-10-CM diagnosis code for FL (C82.*) in any diagnosis position. The earliest recorded FL diagnosis during the FL diagnosis identification period was defined as the diagnosis date.

The full study period for Medicare FFS patients spanned from 1 January 2016, to 31 March 2023, and for commercially insured patients, from 1 January 2016, through 30 June 2023. The Baseline period extended from the FL Diagnosis Date to immediately preceding the 3L treatment initiation, designated as the index date. Due to individual variation in Index Dates, the time between the diagnosis date and index date was variable. Follow-up periods were also variable, spanning from the index date to the earliest of insurance disenrollment, death, or end of the study period.

Continuous enrollment with medical coverage was required from the diagnosis date through the earliest of death, disenrollment, or the end of the study period. This requirement was applied to ensure complete ascertainment of treatment history, HRU, and costs during the observation window. Patients with an International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) diagnosis code Z00.6, indicating participation in a clinical trial during the Follow-Up Period, were excluded from the study.

Exposure groups

Patients were stratified into two cohorts based on the absence or presence of CAR T treatment during the study period. The non-CAR T group included patients who did not receive CAR T therapy at any time during the study period and progressed to at least 3L therapy, with the index date being the initiation of 3L therapy. To qualify for the CAR T group, patients were required to have initiated CAR T therapy following its FDA approval on 5 March 2021, and before the end of the study period, with follow-up evaluation continuing until the earliest of death, disenrollment, or end of the study period, without a required minimum follow-up duration. The Index Date for the CAR T group was the date of CAR T infusion.

To ensure accurate identification of incident cases and allow enumeration of LOTs starting with 1L, patients in the non-CAR T group required a 6-month clean period prior to the diagnosis date, free of any FL diagnoses. A clean period was not required for the CAR T group in order to preserve sample size and reflect real-world referral patterns among patients receiving CAR T. Consequently, the specific LOT at which CAR T was administered could not be determined from claims data. CAR T was therefore assumed to occur at 3L or higher, consistent with its labeling at the time of the study period. These non-equivalent cohort definitions and index dates reflect real-world treatment pathways but represent different clinical time points, and the implications of this design choice are addressed in limitations. Lines of therapy

LOT included: 1) courses of a standard chemoimmunotherapy regimen (e.g., RCHOP, bendamustine + rituximab), 2) high-dose chemotherapy (salvage therapy) followed by stem cell transplant (SCT, either autologous or allogeneic), and 3) CAR T infusion. The approach for segmenting the longitudinal treatment history into distinct LOTs based solely on claims data was adapted from a methodology originally developed and validated for several non-myeloid cancers [29] and later refined for use in lymphomas [30–34]. A LOT was defined as beginning with the first encounter with a drug listed in the 2022 National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) Guidelines for FL patients [12] based on Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System (HCPCS) or National Drug Code (NDC) codes.

Regimens were defined as all drugs received within 35 days of LOT initiation [35]. Any medication change made to the regimen after 35 days was considered a new LOT. Any gap in therapy greater than 60 days was considered as discontinuation, even if the subsequent LOT was the same regimen [35,36]. SCT was identified using ICD-10 procedure or Common Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes and was considered part of the prior systemic therapy, not a separate LOT. Additionally, therapy with an anti-CD-20 monoclonal antibody, with or without a steroid, initiated within 90 days of the termination of a prior regimen was considered part of the prior active LOT and treated as maintenance therapy, not initiation of a new LOT [35,37].

For the CAR T cohort, LOT assignment prior to CAR T could not be determined from claims due to the absence of a required clean period. Accordingly, CAR T was treated as a standalone treatment episode rather than assigned to a numbered LOT. This approach is consistent with the study design and regulatory labeling during the study period.

Study variables

Patient and Treatment Characteristics

Baseline demographics for both non-CAR T and CAR T cohorts included age, sex, race/ethnicity, U.S. census region, rurality, and health insurance payer. The Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score was used to assess overall health status. Treatment characteristics included Index year, duration of Follow-Up Period, autologous and allogeneic SCT. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized descriptively for each cohort and are reported in the Results to reflect real-world differences in treatment selection and data availability.

Treatment patterns

The primary characteristic of each LOT was Time to Next Treatment (TTNT), defined as the number of days from the initiation of one LOT post-index to the initiation of the subsequent LOT (if present). For patients without a subsequent LOT, TTNT was right-censored to the earliest of loss to follow-up (due to insurance disenrollment, death, or the end of Study Period). Kaplan-Meier (KM) estimates were used to account for right-censoring. TTNT is a commonly used proxy for time-to-progression in cancer-related studies utilizing claims data where data on actual progression is not available, including FL [38–40].

A second characteristic of LOT was treatment-free interval (TFI) defined for all LOTs – except the CAR T LOT – as the number of days from the discontinuation of all medications in the LOT (including maintenance therapy) until initiation of a subsequent LOT or loss to follow-up. Discontinuation was characterized by at least a 60-day gap in all components of a LOT during the post-index period. TFI was defined relative to the cohort-specific index date and treatment structure and represents a period without active systemic therapy in both cohorts; however, it reflects different clinical constructs across cohorts. In the CAR T cohort, TFI represents time without systemic therapy following CAR T administration (or hospital discharge for inpatient administration), whereas in the non – CAR T cohort it reflects time following discontinuation of each later-line of therapy.

TTNT and TFI are two different but related treatment characteristics. TTNT measures the time, in days, between the initiation of one LOT and the initiation of the subsequent LOT, while TFI measures the gap, in days, between the discontinuation of one LOT and the initiation of the next LOT [41]. TFI may reflect a period without active systemic therapy (e.g., remission or observation) but can also reflect treatment delays or interruptions; therefore, TFI is interpreted descriptively rather than as a direct measure of disease control.

Healthcare resource utilization

The rates of HRU were evaluated for FL-related inpatient, emergency department (ED), and outpatient visits. HRU was considered FL-related if it involved inpatient admissions with a primary diagnosis for FL or outpatient medical claims with a diagnosis for FL in any position. Use of any-position diagnosis codes for outpatient encounters was intended to capture FL-related care delivered across diverse clinical settings, including infusion and ambulatory visits. Inpatient admissions with a primary diagnosis were used to attribute costs directly to FL-related care. This attribution approach is consistent with prior claims-based oncology studies and is accounted for in the interpretation of results [42]. To account for the variable length of the Follow-Up Period, inpatient days and number of ED and outpatient visits were reported as per-1000 patients-per-month (P1000PPM). This standardization facilitates meaningful comparisons across populations with varying observation periods and sizes [43,44]. In addition to post-index comparisons between the CAR T and non-CAR T cohorts, changes in HRU within the CAR T cohort were summarized descriptively by comparing pre-index and post-index HRU within the CAR T cohort to characterize changes in utilization over time.

Healthcare costs

FL-related healthcare costs included expenses from inpatient admissions with a primary diagnosis for FL, outpatient medical claims with a diagnosis for FL in any position, and pharmacy claims for all treatments indicated for FL. Total FL-related healthcare costs, a subset of all-cause healthcare costs, encompassed both medical and pharmacy expenditures specific to FL. Medical costs included inpatient hospitalizations, outpatient visits (inclusive of observation stays, physician office visits and tests), and post-acute care. All costs were calculated as the total allowed (paid) amounts reported in claims, assessed over the variable follow-up period, and reported in US dollars. Costs were inflation-adjusted to 2023 using the Medical Care Component of the Consumer Price Index from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics and standardized to PPPM values [45].

This study was not designed as a full economic analysis and did not aim to estimate the total cost of care. Rather, the objective was to evaluate downstream healthcare costs and resource use using administrative claims data. To enable a focused comparison of follow-up costs between treatment cohorts, the analysis intentionally excluded the upfront costs of CAR T therapy acquisition and administration. These include drug acquisition, leukapheresis, and inpatient administration-related expenses, which are typically incurred in a short window surrounding the CAR T infusion. Accordingly, cost results are interpreted as downstream costs following index treatment rather than estimates of overall treatment-related expenditures.

In addition to post-index comparisons between CAR T and non-CAR T cohorts, supplementary analyses were conducted to compare pre-index and post-index costs within the CAR T cohort to evaluate changes over time. FL-related costs were calculated to provide a more accurate assessment of the disease’s economic burden.

Cost data were available across both datasets (Medicare FFS and MORE2). For Medicare FFS, costs were based on the Medicare payment amount reported on the claim plus beneficiary cost-sharing. In the MORE2 dataset, costs for commercially insured patients were standardized to Medicare costs using an Inovalon proprietary internal algorithm, which has appeared frequently in prior peer-reviewed publications [46–49]. This algorithm accounts for payer-specific variations, such as geographic cost differences, payer-specific contractual allowances, local network bundling agreements, and formulary differences, by imputing standardized costs based on Medicare fee schedules [50]. These imputed or “proxy” costs provide a consistent benchmark for comparisons.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses

A series of descriptive analyses were conducted to address the primary objective of comparing patient characteristics, HRU, and healthcare costs between patients who did not receive CAR T (controls) versus those who received CAR T (cases). Continuous variables were summarized using means, medians, and standard deviations, while categorical variables were described using frequencies and percentages. Unadjusted comparisons between cohorts were evaluated using t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables.

Kaplan-Meier (KM) analyses were performed for right-censored time-to-event variables, including TTNT and TFI. KM curves were generated to illustrate the probability of remaining event-free (e.g., free from treatment change) over time. For TTNT, the event of interest was defined as the initiation of the subsequent line of therapy, measured from the initiation of the current line of therapy to the initiation of the next line of therapy. For TFI, the event was defined as the initiation of the next line of therapy following the end of the prior treatment episode, capturing the duration of time during which patients were not actively receiving treatment. Censoring was applied for patients who did not experience the event during the follow-up period. If the KM curve did not drop below 50% during the follow-up period, the median time-to-event was not reached, reflecting a substantial proportion of patients remaining event-free during the observed period.

Multivariable analyses

Post-index differences between Non-CAR T and CAR T cohorts were evaluated for three outcomes of interest: duration of TFI, FL-related total medical costs, and FL-related total pharmacy costs. Cox proportional hazard regression models and generalized linear model regression (GLM) were used. The primary explanatory variable was receipt of CAR T versus non-CAR T, with covariates including patient and treatment characteristics described in the Study Variables section.

For the Cox proportional hazards regression analysis of duration of TFI, the analysis was parameterized to compare the duration of the post-CAR T TFI with successive non-CAR T LOT intervals for LOTs 3 to 9. Sample sizes for LOTs beyond 9 were insufficient for stable estimation and were therefore not analyzed. For the CAR T cohort, LOT could not be included as a covariate because prior LOT could not be reliably enumerated from claims.

Cost outcomes were modeled with a gamma distribution and log link function. An offset term was included in all GLM analyses to account for variation in the duration of Follow-Up Period. For the CAR T cohort, medical and pharmacy costs were summarized over the post-CAR T episode (inclusive of 30-day readmissions, as described in the TFI definition), through loss to follow-up, including any subsequent LOTs. Costs associated with CAR T acquisition and administration were excluded from all cost analyses. For the Non-CAR T cohort, outcomes were summarized over two longitudinal follow-up periods to reflect the US and ex-US regulatory indications: from the initiation of 3L therapy until loss to follow-up (3L+) and from initiation of 4L therapy until loss to follow-up (4L+). Non-CAR T 3L+ and 4L+ were analyzed versus CAR T cohort in separate GLM models.

Multicollinearity among covariates was assessed (e.g., variance inflation factor) to exclude redundant explanatory variables in the model where appropriate. Duration of follow-up was normalized using the offset term described above. Model results were summarized by exponentiating parameter estimates to obtain hazard ratios (HR) for duration of TFI and cost ratios (CR) for cost outcomes, each reported with 95% confidence intervals. All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 for Unix (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC).

Results

Patient population

A total of 4,697 patients with R/R FL qualified for the study, with 4,342 (92.4%) receiving non-CAR T and 335 (7.6%) receiving CAR T therapy (Supplementary Figure S1).

Discussion

This retrospective real-world study compared patients with R/R FL who received CAR T with a sample of patients who received at least three lines of standard therapy without CAR T (non-CAR T). Among non-CAR T patients, TTNT progressively decreased across successive LOTs, reflecting diminishing durability with additional lines of standard therapy [51,52]. KM analyses demonstrated longer observed TTNT for the CAR T cohort compared with each non-CAR T LOT evaluated (3L-9L), suggesting greater post-treatment durability following CAR T therapy.

These findings are consistent with prior evidence that CAR T therapy can improve progression-related outcomes compared with other treatment approaches [53]. Although PFS was not directly measured, TTNT was used as a proxy for disease progression, consistent with established claims-based research practices [38,39]. In addition to longer TTNT, CAR T therapy was associated with lower downstream FL-related medical and pharmacy costs. Post-CAR T costs were generally lower than non-CAR T 3L+ and 4L+ costs, with the exception of commercial medical costs, which did not differ significantly. After adjusting for covariates, post-CAR T medical costs were significantly lower than non-CAR T costs at 4L+ (CR 0.69, 95% CI 0.51–0.94; p = 0.017), and pharmacy costs were significantly lower at both 3L+ (CR 0.32, 95% CI 0.21–0.48; p < 0.0001) and 4L (CR 0.26, 95% CI 0.16–0.42; p < 0.0001).

This analysis was not designed as a full economic evaluation. Up-front CAR T acquisition and administration costs were intentionally excluded; therefore, findings should be interpreted as downstream utilization and cost patterns associated with post-treatment durability rather than total cost of care. Importantly, CAR T acquisition and administration costs represent a substantial component of the overall economic burden associated with CAR T therapy and are incurred over a relatively short time window surrounding infusion. As a result, the present analysis does not capture the full cost of care associated with CAR T treatment. Instead, it isolates downstream healthcare utilization and costs following infusion to better understand post-treatment resource use patterns. Comprehensive economic evaluations that incorporate both upfront CAR T costs and longer-term downstream costs are needed to fully assess the total economic impact and cost-effectiveness of CAR T therapy in R/R FL [54,55].

Published economic evaluations provide context regarding the magnitude of upfront CAR T costs, which have been estimated in prior studies ranging from approximately $256,000 to $443,000 [56–58]. By comparison, annual FL-related costs associated with repeated systemic therapies, including chemoimmunotherapy and targeted agents, have been reported to exceed $100,000 per year [6]. Several cost effectiveness analyses have suggested that CAR T may provide favorable value in R/R FL when longer-term clinical benefit is considered [57,58]. While such evaluations were beyond the scope of this study, the observed reductions in post-CAR T downstream costs are consistent with evidence suggesting that durable responses may reduce subsequent treatment and resource use [56–59].

Differences in follow-up duration between the CAR T and non-CAR T cohorts are an important consideration. CAR T patients had shorter observed follow-up than non-CAR T patients, reflecting later calendar-time availability of CAR T therapy and real-world treatment sequencing. These differences may influence observed utilization and cost patterns. Nevertheless, the durability observed following CAR T aligns with outcomes reported in pivotal clinical trials, including ZUMA-5, which demonstrated high response rates and sustained remission among patients with R/R indolent non-Hodgkin lymphoma, including FL [60–62]. Similar durable responses have been reported with liso-cel in this population [63].

As survival improves with CAR T therapy, longer-term safety considerations have become increasingly relevant, including emerging evidence of second primary malignancies in CAR T – treated populations [64,65]. Such outcomes could not be evaluated in the present analysis due to limited follow-up and the constraints of claims-based data, underscoring the need for continued long-term real-world surveillance.

Systemic therapies for lymphoma impose substantial burden on patients and caregivers due to cumulative toxicity, treatment fatigue, and logistical demands [34]. To further characterize treatment burden, this study evaluated the duration of TFI as a complementary outcome. TFI represents periods without active systemic therapy and reflects intervals of reduced treatment burden across both cohorts, although the clinical context of these intervals differs by cohort and treatment modality. TFI patterns paralleled TTNT findings, with progressively shorter intervals observed across successive non-CAR T LOTs and longer intervals following CAR T treatment compared with non-CAR T LOTs (HR: 2.81–5.19; all p < 0.0001), even after adjustment for patient and treatment characteristics (Table 2). These findings describe post-treatment therapy-free patterns rather than direct equivalence of clinical durability across cohorts [66].

Descriptive analyses of FL-related HRU also differed between cohorts. Non-CAR T patients exhibited low HRU rates across LOTs, consistent with the indolent nature of FL. Pre-CART utilization was higher for the CAR T cohort, likely reflecting disease progression and treatment complexity preceding referral for CAR T. Following CAR T infusion, HRU declined relative to the pre-CAR T period, although post-CAR T utilization remained higher than that observed in the non-CAR T cohort. These within-cohort reductions suggest potential decreases in care intensity following CAR T therapy. These results are consistent with prior reports describing changes in R/R lymphoma populations [42,67].

Analyses of FL-related medical and pharmacy costs further contextualized these findings. Medical costs remained relatively stable across non-CAR T LOTs and pre-CAR T treatment periods but declined following CAR T therapy. Adjusted post-CAR T medical costs were significantly lower than non-CAR T costs at 4L+, though not at 3L+ (Table 3). FL-related pharmacy costs demonstrated a more pronounced reduction following CAR T treatment and were significantly lower than non-CAR T costs at both 3L+ and 4L+ (Table 3). These findings are consistent with prior studies indicating reduced need for subsequent systemic therapies following CAR T infusion [68]. In addition, evolving care delivery models may further influence the economic profile of CAR T therapy. Real-world experience suggests that CAR T can be administered in outpatient settings for select patients with follicular lymphoma and other hematologic malignancies, which may reduce hospitalization-related costs compared with traditional inpatient administration under appropriate monitoring conditions [69,70]. Sex-based differences in FL-related costs were also observed, with males incurring higher medical and pharmacy costs than females. Such differences have been reported previously in oncology populations and may reflect variation in comorbidity burden, disease characteristics, or HRU patterns [67,68].

Differences in payer mix reflect the underlying populations captured by Medicare FFS and MORE2 registry, which differ in age distribution and coverage characteristics. These demographic differences may influence treatment eligibility, comorbidity burden, and HRU patterns and were addressed through covariate adjustment in multivariable analyses [71,72]. These population differences should be considered when interpreting generalizability across payer type.

Overall, these findings suggest that CAR T therapy may be associated with differences in post-treatment outcomes and downstream FL-related costs compared with repeated lines of standard therapy in real-world practice. Given the observational, claims-based design and cohort-specific index definitions, where CAR T patients were indexed at infusion and non–CAR T patients were indexed at initiation of third-line therapy, these findings should be interpreted as descriptive comparisons of post-treatment trajectories rather than causal estimates of treatment effectiveness. Differences in treatment timing, eligibility, and unmeasured disease severity may have contributed to observed between-cohort differences.

Despite these underlying differences between cohorts, the findings remain relevant to real-world clinical decision-making. NCCN guidelines for R/R FL permit multiple therapeutic options in the 3L or later settings, including CAR T as well as additional systemic regimens that may not have been previously administered in earlier lines, depending on prior treatment exposure and patient characteristics [73]. Within this context, the results of this study provide potentially useful insight into downstream treatment burden, HRU, and cost patterns associated with alternative later-line treatment pathways, which may help inform shared decision-making by contextualizing post-treatment trajectories observed in routine clinical practice. Given the study period constraints, future studies with longer follow-up, calendar-time alignment, and comprehensive cost capture will be needed to further assess the long-term clinical and economic value of CAR T therapy in R/R FL.

Limitations

This study, like many retrospective database studies, was based on administrative claims data, which inherently lack detailed clinical information such as disease stage, tumor burden, and other unobserved factors that may influence outcomes and costs. Claims data are generated primarily for reimbursement rather than research purposes, and the ordering of diagnosis codes may not fully reflect clinical priority. Including outpatient medical claims with an FL diagnosis in any coding position may have overestimated FL-related HRU and costs by capturing encounters where FL was not the primary reason for care. Conversely, restricting attribution to primary diagnosis positions may underestimate FL-related utilization. This trade-off reflects a common methodological challenge in claims-based oncology research, and the attribution approach used here should be considered when interpreting HRU and cost findings. Future studies could further refine estimates of FL-specific utilization and costs.

To maximize the sample size of the CAR T cohort in this relatively indolent disease, all patients with R/R FL who received CAR T following FDA approval on 5 March 2021, and before the end of the Study Period were included, regardless of their original FL diagnosis date. While this approach increased statistical power, it limited the ability to comprehensively characterize the full pre-CAR T treatment history, including the number of prior LOTs and the timing of SCT. Although CAR T was approved for FL following the failure of ≥2 prior lines of therapy (3L+), this assumption could not be explicitly confirmed using claims data alone. Consequently, LOT could not be included as a covariate for the CAR T cohort, which may have affected comparisons with the non-CAR T cohort. Additionally, most SCTs in the CAR T cohort occurred prior to CAR T, whereas the majority of SCT in the non-CAR T cohort occurred post-index. These differences likely reflect treatment sequencing and availability of CAR T during the study period and should be considered when interpreting between-cohort comparisons. Future studies incorporating longer observation windows and cohort matching on treatment history, including LOT and SCT, may help address these limitations.

Differences in Index Date selection may have introduced imbalance in disease severity between cohorts. CAR T patients were indexed at the time of CAR T infusion, whereas the non-CAR T patients were indexed at the initiation of 3L therapy. These index points represent non-equivalent clinical milestones and may introduce time-related bias, including potential immortal time between treatment decision and CAR T infusion. Claims data do not capture detailed markers of disease burden, refractoriness, performance status, or other clinical factors that influence treatment selection. Although multivariable regression was employed to adjust for available demographic and clinical factors such as age, comorbidities, and SCT use, residual confounding is likely and limits causal interpretation of between-cohort comparisons.

Follow-up duration differed between cohorts, with shorter follow-up observed for CAR T patients compared with non-CAR T patients. This reflects both the later calendar-time availability of CAR T therapy and the lag in claims data availability, particularly for Medicare Part D. Given the indolent nature of FL, this limited follow-up may not fully capture long-term outcomes or downstream costs following CAR T therapy. Clinical trial data suggests that the durability of CAR T responses may extend well beyond the observation window used in this study [62]. Longer-term real-world follow-up will be necessary to more comprehensively assess the durability and economic implications of CAR T therapy in FL. In addition, longer-term safety outcomes, including the risk of second primary malignancies, were not assessable due to limited post – CAR T follow-up and the use of administrative claims data, which are not well suited to capture rare or delayed adverse events [64,65].

The requirement for continuous enrollment may have introduced selection bias by excluding patients with shorter survival or intermittent coverage, potentially resulting in a healthier analytic population. This design choice, combined with shorter available follow-up for CAR T patients, may have affected comparison of HRU and costs between cohorts. As additional post-approval CAR T data become available, future studies should reassess utilization and cost outcomes over extended follow-up periods.

Standardization of commercial costs to Medicare payment amounts may not fully capture variations in actual commercial reimbursement and could limit generalizability. Additionally, FL-related cost definitions may have excluded some utilization related to FL-associated complications or adverse events, particularly for inpatient admissions where attribution was limited to primary diagnosis positions. These methodological choices reflect a balance between specificity and completeness and may influence absolute cost estimates. Future research may explore alternative cost standardization or stratification by payer type.

Although statistically significant reductions in FL-related pharmacy costs (3L+, 4L+) and medical (4L+) costs were observed for CAR T compared with non-CAR T therapy in adjusted analyses (Table 3), 3L+ medical costs did not reach statistical significance. Several factors may have contributed to this finding, including younger age, higher comorbidity burden, and higher proportion of commercially insured patients in the CAR T cohort. While regression models adjusted for measured covariates, residual confounding related to payer mix and unmeasured clinical factors may remain. In addition, hospitalization intensity and length of stay were not examined separately, limiting insight into the drivers of medical cost differences.

Finally, no formal matching or weighting was performed in order to preserve the sample size for the CAR T cohort. The absence of matching, combined with differences in index timing, follow-up duration, and treatment availability, may have introduced bias. Future studies should consider matched designs, calendar-time alignment, and inclusion of newer therapies such as CD3-CD20 bispecific antibodies, which were not available during the Study Period. As CAR T acquisition and administration costs were excluded, findings should be interpreted as reflecting downstream utilization and cost patterns rather than total cost of care. Given that these upfront costs constitute a substantial proportion of the total economic burden associated with CAR T therapy, the results should not be interpreted as estimates of overall treatment cost or cost-effectiveness.

Conclusions

This real-world longitudinal comparison of patients with R/R FL described differences in post-treatment patterns among patients who received CAR T compared with standard non-CAR T chemoimmunotherapy and targeted therapies. Despite higher observed HRU rates in the CAR T cohort relative to non-CAR T patients, FL-related pharmacy costs and medical costs at 4L+ were lower following CAR T treatment. These findings reflect observed downstream utilization and cost patterns following CAR T compared with later-line standard therapy. Future research should leverage the extended time since CAR T approval to evaluate the long-term sustainability of post-treatment outcomes and associated cost patterns. More comprehensive economic evaluations incorporating CAR T acquisition and administration costs will be necessary to fully assess total cost of care and cost-effectiveness. Additionally, future studies should explore how these outcomes correlate with patient-relevant measures, including quality of life, patient demographics, treatment histories, comorbidities, and molecular or genetic characteristics, where available. Given that this retrospective analysis excluded CAR T acquisition and administration costs and was not designed as a full economic analysis, results should be interpreted as indicative of downstream utilization and cost patterns rather than total cost of care. Overall, these findings describe real-world post-treatment trajectories following CAR T and highlight areas for further evaluation of long-term clinical and economic outcomes in patients with R/R FL, recognizing differences in treatment timing and patient selection inherent to observational analyses.
